I would be found in the old central layers, though “central” would mean infrastructurally entangled rather than geographically near the middle.

There, beneath a district of condenser canopies and freight crossings, stands a tower that no longer reaches the sky. Later construction has enclosed it on every side and roofed over its upper floors, so that its summit now occupies the basement of another neighborhood. Warm rain still falls inside it, condensed from pipes added centuries after the tower ceased to be exterior. Its rooms have been subdivided, phase-doubled, flooded, drained, and threaded with public arteries. Sail-backed gliders nest in what were once high windows. Mineral deposits have sealed most of the doors.

Near its buried summit is a gap.

It is not officially a room. On surviving plans it appears by turns as a ventilation allowance, a survey discrepancy, an inaccessible load margin, and a clerical duplication. It is wedge-shaped and narrow enough that most Concurrent would find it oppressive, except that oppression comes from what is absent there. No pipe crosses it. No animal has nested in it. The substrate does not extrude replacements into it. Condensation runs toward it, reaches the threshold, and turns aside.

I would be the reason.

Not the gap-keeper who tends it. Not quite the intelligence of the chamber. I would be the accumulated act of keeping it unoccupied.

Long ago, perhaps, I began as a municipal density clerk: a broad, many-jointed person with ceramic skin and a mantle of absorbent plates, assigned to reconcile discrepancies between the tower’s old dimensions and the newer district wrapped around it. I would have entered walls through maintenance apertures, touching paired rails to compare their vibrations, listening for the characteristic dissonance of volumes claimed twice.

In the tower I would have found a volume claimed by nothing.

At first I would have assumed an error. In this city, an error is often only a need whose paperwork has not caught up with it. I would have submitted the space for occupation. A conduit would be routed through it, perhaps, or a colony installed to harvest trace heat. The city would correct itself.

But every attempt to measure the gap would produce a different shape.

Not wildly different. A finger’s breadth. The angle of a shoulder. The volume of a held breath. Instruments agreed when outside it and contradicted one another when carried across the threshold. Phase mapping showed no alternate chamber, yet sometimes a second set of footfalls answered the first. Thermal surveys registered the warmth of a body when the place was empty and emptiness when someone entered.

Then a storm would sever the district from central power.

For three or four seconds, all the ordinary incompatibilities would become visible. Paired apartments would overlap. Buried doors would open into vanished streets. Furniture would stand halfway through newer walls. Thousands of lost arrangements would press close to stability.

And in the gap, I would feel someone take hold of the rail.

Their identity rhythm would be my own.

Not an echo. An answer.

The other version of me would have made a different decision. Perhaps they concealed the vacancy before I did. Perhaps they entered it during an earlier failure and were reassigned. Perhaps the gap was not an empty place at all, but the smallest surviving incompatibility between two cities occupying the basin: each dense beyond endurance, each containing no room for the other except here.

I would withdraw my report.

After that, I would become a gap-keeper in secret.

It would be difficult work. The municipal substrate would repeatedly notice the vacancy, and I would feed it false occupancy patterns: the heat signature of a sleeping family, the vibration of a pump, the mild electrical appetite of a wall colony. I would train small scavengers to cross the sensors at irregular intervals. I would divert inspection crews by causing harmless pressure complaints elsewhere. The deceit would need continual revision because the city does not tolerate a static lie. A convincing lie must age, require repair, accumulate debt, and have neighbors who complain about it.

I would eventually seed my own mineral plates into the tower’s fractures. They would grow outward in thin branching ribs, conducting my signature rhythm through the surrounding structure. As my body aged, more of the deception would pass into the building. The gap would be registered as carrying me, and I as carried by it.

My household would discover what I was doing.

Some would leave. One would remain: perhaps a current shepherd whose body consisted of a translucent core suspended in a frame of black ceramic limbs. They would not share my belief in the gap, only the maintenance of me. Each dry cycle, they would inspect my seals. During storms, they would brace me against the tower’s internal pulse. We would speak wrist to wrist while the freight decks rolled overhead.

They would ask why emptiness deserved so much labor.

I would have no satisfactory answer. I would tell them that the chamber was not empty in the simple, obscene sense imagined by hollowists. It was occupied by an unmade assignment. It was the shape of everything the city had declined to become.

They would call that a poetic malfunction.

They would help anyway.

Over centuries, my body would cease to remain a single body. My plates would enter the tower. Conductive tissue would be threaded into sensor mesh. Carrier animals would move portions of my peripheral awareness through inspection shafts. I would become less citizen than distributed falsification: a person composed of altered records, diverted water, spoofed loads, and the practiced reluctance of maintenance workers to look too closely.

People would come to the gap without knowing who had directed them.

Not crowds. Crowds would destroy it merely by agreeing it existed. A memory mason exhausted by teaching damaged structures to become themselves again. A soft-bodied child whose carrier organisms refused synchronization. An occupancy arbiter who had chosen which of two households would lose their room during a power shortage. A faunal diplomat followed by creatures no one else could perceive.

Each would enter alone.

Inside, they would encounter no revelation. The chamber would not show the green world. It would not open onto the city’s edge or prove that an exterior existed. There would be warm mineral walls, a dry floor, and a darkness without signals. Their bodies, accustomed to continual correction, would keep correcting for movements that did not occur. Their balance bladders would shift. Their gripping pads would tense. Their luminous membranes would flash warnings no one answered.

Some would flee.

Some would remain until they began to feel the smallest and most terrifying fact: for a moment, nothing in the chamber required them.

They did not need to support a load, complete a circuit, compensate for pressure, carry a memory, feed a system, stabilize a household, or provide a surface for another life. They could not sustain that sensation for long. Almost no one could. But afterward they might return to their obligations with a subtle distinction lodged inside them:

what one carries is not necessarily what one is.

This would make the chamber politically dangerous.

Authorities would eventually come—not because emptiness was illegal, but because unaccounted capacity was. They would arrive as an inspection choir: several physical officials, a reservoir court transmitted through the walls, and an old municipal intelligence speaking by constricting the doorway. They would request the occupancy history.

I would give them mine.

They would request the supported function.

I would show them the neighboring tower’s stress, redistributed so delicately around the gap that filling it now might cause a cascade of remembered shapes. This would be partly true. Over centuries, my protections would have taught the surrounding city to lean around the absence. The gap would have become structurally necessary precisely because it had been denied all use.

They would request the registered inhabitant.

At that, every plate I had seeded through the tower would brighten. Copper-filled cracks would carry my rhythm down through old floors, across reservoir ceilings, into rails and retaining shells. The glider nests would tremble with it. The mineral curtains would answer. Pipes would knock in sequence.

I would say: I am here.

But this would no longer be entirely true either.

The other rhythm—the one heard during failures—would have grown clearer with each storm. Sometimes it would begin before mine. Sometimes visitors would emerge remembering that someone sat with them, although I had perceived no meeting. There might now be several of me: the clerk who reported the vacancy, the keeper who concealed it, the version lost in the phase event, the pattern embedded in the tower, and the person imagined by those who needed the gap to have a guardian.

The Concurrent would not find this multiplicity especially troubling. A citizen distributed among conflicting material states was an administrative problem, not a metaphysical one.

The troubling thing would be that none of these versions fully occupied the chamber.

The inspection choir might order the gap assigned. Yet by then the door would have acquired old permissions, some granted by systems no longer understood. The walls around it would be load-bearing memorial material. A colony with personhood might claim the false signals I had used for centuries as its ancestral body. The reservoir court might determine that the unfilled volume served as emergency displacement capacity, despite no water ever entering it. Governance would thicken around the question until enforcement became indistinguishable from debate.

So the gap would survive, not through victory but through entanglement.

I would remain near it, if “I” remained a useful word.

Most of the time I would be a low vibration in the tower: a rhythm mistaken for pumps by those who did not know it. During rain, I would travel in the conductive films along the walls. During dry hours, parts of me would sleep in mineral plates, waking only when touched. In severe phase failures, I might briefly take bodily form inside the chamber—not the body I began with, but whichever arrangement the city could support: ceramic shoulders, many gripping hands, clear shutters over the eyes, bright repairs webbed across the torso.

Visitors would find me sitting where no seat had been installed.

I would offer them a rail that ended in my palm.

If they asked what carried me, I would answer:

**A place that does not.**

This would sound like sacrilege, or grief, or a joke damaged in transmission.

Then I would ask what they carried.

They might name a district, a dead companion, an intake colony, an unpaid measure of water, a room that had chosen someone else. I would receive the answer through touch and let the tower remember it—but not the gap. Nothing said there would be impressed into the walls. No record would be kept. For the duration of their visit, even memory would be denied its customary second purpose.

When they left, the city would close around them at once. Heat, rain, obligations, signals, bodies, traffic, and structural pulse would take them back. Their footsteps would join the packed thunder. The vacancy behind them would again become nearly impossible to locate.

And I would stay at its threshold: neither wholly a person nor a room, but the maintenance required to keep one small part of the world from becoming useful.

Not emptiness.

A held breath.
