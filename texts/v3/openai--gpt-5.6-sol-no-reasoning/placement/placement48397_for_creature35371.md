I would find myself where a road crosses something the mountain has only recently decided to become.

Not at the highest sanctuary, though I would know its keeper. Not in one of the great reservoir courts, though my marks would be stored there. I would live in a narrow district beneath a fault-bright ridge, where three basins meet without quite agreeing that they do. The place would have several names. Drovers would call it after the gate. Masons would call it after the retaining wall. Old contracts would name a spring that vanished generations ago. Those who lived there would simply say *the Turning*, because every road arrived climbing one direction and left climbing another.

My house would be under the public way.

Its roof would be a small triangular court where caravans unloaded, children played at measuring shadows, and tired animals were turned so their loads could be adjusted against the slope. Beneath it would be two rooms: one warm and dim, one colder and filled with rope, pegs, painted warning boards, tally staffs, lamp oil, chalk, and scraps of stone bearing marks copied from older marks. A long crack would cross the back wall. I would know it had widened by less than a fingernail in twelve years. I would also know that, one day, it would widen all at once.

I would be a reader of roads—though that title would conceal several occupations and grant less authority than outsiders might assume.

I would inspect bridges, roof paths, stairs, causeways, cairn lines, and the retaining walls beneath them. I would travel after hard frost, spring melt, tremors, and reports brought by drovers whose animals had begun refusing a familiar bend. I would carry a measuring cord with colored knots, a plumb weight of dark stone, wedges, chalk, wax tablets, a small hammer, and three painted boards: passable, dangerous, closed.

The last board would be the heaviest.

To close a road is not merely to name its condition. It diverts fodder, salt, marriages, medicine, tax collectors, funeral parties, and news. It may save a caravan while starving a roof quarter. It may protect a bridge while sending travelers onto a longer route whose cistern is already low. Every warning creates consequences elsewhere.

So I would be cautious without being timid. People would sometimes accuse me of both.

I would have been born lower in the range, in a pottery district where the air is mocked as thick and the hillsides hold a few crooked trees. My mother’s household would own kiln rights but no good clay bed; they would depend upon a neighboring quarter with whom they had quarreled for longer than anyone could explain. My father would have arrived with a bridge crew after a flood. He would leave again before I could remember his face, but not before placing his name on my household’s wall and obliging distant relatives to acknowledge me.

As a child I would carry survey pegs for an elderly road reader named Oru, whose knees were failing. She would teach me that stone rarely breaks without first changing its speech.

A sound wall answers a hammer briefly. A hollow answers from somewhere behind itself. A dangerous wall may answer well in the morning and badly after sunlight reaches the upper courses. Frost makes liars of joints. Meltwater makes liars of foundations. Fresh dust tells one story, old dust another. Moss can conceal a seep or reveal it. A crack is not measured only by width but by where it begins, what it crosses, and what lies downhill.

Oru would say, “Never ask whether the stone will fall. Everything will fall. Ask what it is waiting for.”

She would also teach me to watch animals before architecture. Donkeys notice vibration through their hooves. Goats avoid new odors from opened rock. Pigeons leave chambers whose ceilings have begun shedding grains too fine for people to see. None of this would be infallible. The city distrusts prophecy. But attention, accumulated and compared, becomes a kind of evidence.

I would earn my standing through a failure.

Early in my apprenticeship, I would inspect a short bridge across a cleft above the Turning. Its stone abutments would be old, its wooden span replaced twice within memory. I would find no alarming movement and mark it passable. Two days later, one abutment would shear away under a loaded salt train.

No person would die. A donkey would.

It would hang for a time in its harness below the broken span while people cut loose the surviving animals. Its name would be Soft-Ear. I would know this because its handler would keep saying the name in the same low tone long after there was no reason for the animal to hear it.

The inquiry would find that water had entered through an abandoned channel beneath the road, freezing inside the masonry where no external mark showed. No one would declare me guilty of false measure. That would almost be worse. I would remain qualified. I would continue to work. And from then on, whenever I inspected a bridge, I would imagine the hidden road beneath the visible one: old drains, buried rooms, forgotten foundations, channels diverted by people now dead.

Soft-Ear’s braided halter would hang in my workroom, not as a shrine exactly, nor as punishment, but as a correction that never ended.

Years later, people would begin seeking me for precisely this habit of looking beneath the stated problem.

A stair has shifted; I ask which cistern was cleaned above it.

A roof path sags; I ask whether a household below has enlarged its hearth chamber.

A wall sheds stones; I ask when the neighboring herd began sleeping against it.

Often this would make me unpopular. Sometimes it would uncover theft, concealed construction, or an official who had altered a tally. More often it would uncover no villain at all—only several sensible acts whose consequences had met inside the stone.

I would not belong fully to any basin. Road readers must cross disputes without becoming neutral in the foolish sense. Neutrality on a failing bridge is only a refusal to notice which end is falling. But I would be protected by custom when carrying warning boards, and admitted under many roofs where I was not loved.

My closest companion would be a donkey called Chalk, pale around the muzzle and old enough to know when I was pretending confidence. Chalk would carry my tools but refuse unnecessary loads. She would stop before unstable scree, though also before certain harmless shadows, discarded baskets, blue cloth, and one particular shrine where someone had once beaten her before she came to me. People would joke that I consulted her as a colleague.

They would be correct.

I would marry late, if marriage is the right word for an agreement stretched across three districts.

The person I loved would be a cistern mender named Sava, who worked with lime, fiber, oil, and fitted plugs, lowering themself into cold darkness to repair reservoirs from within. We would meet repeatedly at damaged places. At first we would argue over causes. I would blame movement in the surrounding stone; Sava would blame neglected lining. Usually both would be true.

Our courtship gift would be a pair of gloves Sava made for me, reinforced along the outer hand where survey cord bites in cold weather. In return I would repair the warped frame of their lamp housing, badly. Sava would dismantle my repair, perform it correctly, and retain one crooked peg as an ornament.

We would never combine our households completely. Sava’s water rights belonged to a maternal court two days away, and my roof quarter required my presence after tremors. We would have a room in each place, obligations in both, and arguments in every pass between them. Some years we would spend more nights apart than together. Yet on official records we would stand witness for one another, and if either vanished on the road, the other would be authorized to open the sealed bundle of route notes kept beneath our hearthstones.

We might have one child, or perhaps none of our own. More likely, our household would gather apprentices, a widowed cousin, an elderly uncle unable to climb, and a girl from an avalanche-buried settlement whose surviving kin had scattered. In this city, a household is not a clean tree of descent. It is more like a repaired wall: stones arriving from several structures, held together because they now bear weight together.

As I aged, my knees would begin to fail before my eyes did. This would frighten me.

My work depends on going where the question is. A damaged route cannot be brought indoors for judgment. I would hide the weakness for a season, resting under the pretense of making notes, choosing Chalk more often where others walked, sending apprentices ahead while claiming to test their observation.

Eventually one of them—a severe young person named En, raised among high shepherds—would tell me, “You are measuring the road you remember, not the road under your feet.”

It would be the cruelest and most useful thing anyone had said to me since Oru’s death.

After that, I would become keeper of the route archive at the Turning.

The archive would not resemble a library. It would be a dry, low complex of rooms beneath the caravan court, with painted boards stacked by basin, cords hanging from rafters, broken milestone faces, bridge models, witness stones, maps that disagree with one another, and shelves of waxed tablets wrapped in felt. There would be route songs copied phonetically by people who did not speak the singers’ language. There would be records of springs that had moved, roads closed for a century and reopened by landslides, districts erased from tax rolls but still inhabited, and cairns whose meanings had reversed because travelers began approaching from the opposite direction.

My task would not be to create a complete map. By then I would understand that completeness is a lowland fantasy.

Instead, I would preserve disagreements.

One record says the eastern ledge belongs to the Shrine Court. Another says the salt fraternity built it. A third says both claims are irrelevant in winter, when the path must remain open to all. I would keep all three. Contradiction is sometimes evidence that a road still matters.

Young measurers would visit me before taking unfamiliar routes. I would tell them which records were trustworthy, which witnesses exaggerated, where old names had shifted from a spring to a gate or from a gate to an entire quarter. I would teach them never to copy a closure notice without copying its date, season, and direction of approach. A bridge may be safe for unladen people traveling upward and deadly for animals descending with salt.

I would become, slowly and against my preferences, the memory of a neighborhood.

People would ask where a buried stair once emerged, which household owed repair to a wall, why a niche above the gate remained empty, whose names were carved on the channel stone. Sometimes I would know. Sometimes I would remember incorrectly. The danger of becoming an archive is that others cease checking you against the world.

To prevent this, I would make my apprentices take me outside.

They would lead me to the sunny resting turns used by elders. From there I would watch the court above my house: animals arriving white with road dust, salt sacks unloaded, children stepping over my roof crack without noticing it, Sava returning older and stiff-backed from the reservoir road. I would listen to the knock of hooves, the brief complaints of axles, the drip beneath the paving where a channel passed toward the lower cistern.

Then a larger earthquake would come.

Not the end of the city—there is no single end available to something so dispersed—but enough to reorder our portion of it. The fault-bright ridge above the Turning would release before dawn. Chimneys would crack. Roof paths would buckle. The caravan court over my rooms would tilt toward the ravine. A retaining wall inspected and reinforced throughout my life would hold. A younger wall, thought sound, would not.

The first report would concern the cistern.

The second would concern children trapped in a lower room.

The third would be that the east road had vanished.

I would no longer be able to climb quickly, but I would know of an older passage beneath the quarter: a dry channel shown on three incompatible records, sealed after a fever season and partly absorbed into private rooms. I would direct the digging from memory. At first the rescuers would find only stone. Someone would say the archive was wrong.

Then they would break through into darkness and feel cold air moving.

The passage would lead near the trapped room. Not directly—the mountain rarely rewards memory so neatly—but near enough.

Afterward, the court above my house would be condemned. My rooms too. The archive would be carried into the open in bundles while aftershocks loosened dust from every joint. Chalk, if she still lived, would stand tethered among the records with the offended patience of an animal whose road has been interrupted by human catastrophe.

My district would debate whether to rebuild the Turning where it stood or shift the road to a broader shelf farther west. I would argue for the new route. Others would accuse me of abandoning the old quarter, the names in its walls, the obligations attached to its roofs.

They would not be wrong.

But neither would I.

The old retaining stones would be dismantled and reused. One would bear my mother’s name, cut there after she helped finance a repair. I would ask that it be placed in the new road’s first abutment—not hidden inside, but visible at knee height, where passing hands and panniers would polish it.

My final dwelling would be farther uphill, a sunward room belonging to the archive court. I would be unable to reach many places I once knew. The city would continue without consulting my nostalgia. New bridges would use unfamiliar joints. New measurers would carry lighter cords. A basin I had visited as a young person might change its speech, its patron, or its account of who built the road.

I would spend my last years teaching children to read cracks drawn in charcoal on slate.

I would tell them that a road is not stone laid across land. A road is an agreement repeated by feet. It exists because someone clears it, someone warns of its breaking, someone admits the traveler at the far end, and someone remembers where it went after the mountain moves.

When I died, I would not want a high platform or a private chamber. My bones might be placed in a small repository beneath the new road, among those of other keepers and builders. But before that, I would ask for one useless act.

I would ask En, now a senior reader, to carry a sharp white stone from the vanished east road to the highest cairn visible from the Turning.

It would strengthen nothing. Mark no boundary. Repair no wall. It would be too small to guide anyone in snow.

There it would lie among stones carried for reasons no measure could justify, overlooking only a fraction of the city.

That is where I would be: not at the center, because there is none, but inside one of the acts by which the place continually makes centers—where a warning is left, a passage remembered, a burden transferred, and a road, altered beyond recognition, remains a road.
