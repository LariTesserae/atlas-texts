I would find myself near the rim of the central basin, where the pale floor begins to gather its eastward grooves.

Not in the open at first. I would live in a shallow recess behind a projecting lip of limestone, a place too low to stand in and too broad to be called a fissure. The wind would pass over its mouth with immense force, sealing the interior beneath a roof of pressure. Inside, the roar would deepen into a continuous bodily note. Light would arrive from the stone rather than the sky, reflected inward until every hand and face seemed faintly luminous.

I would be one of the furred primates, but not simply as they have already been described. I would be unusually attentive to the faint pulse beneath the louder life of the plateau: the hidden river moving through the caves below.

Perhaps this difference would begin in childhood. While others learned to separate the tread of grazers from the shiver of hunting wings, I would keep mistaking deeper vibrations for distant footsteps. I would press my palms to the rock after the herd had passed and insist that something was still traveling underneath us.

The elders would correct me. Then, gradually, they would stop.

I would become a finder of depth—not a seer, exactly, and not a keeper of secret knowledge. Nothing here could be kept in that way. I would be someone brought to uncertain places: dry seams, unfamiliar hollows, shelves where the usual water rhythms had weakened. I would lie flat, cheek against stone, arms extended, and wait until my breathing ceased to obscure what came through my bones.

The plateau would not appear to me as a surface. It would be a stack of distances.

Nearest would be the sharp, scattered language of feet: insects, reptiles, companions shifting their weight. Beneath that, the broader weather of the herds. Deeper still, the slow strains of stone adjusting to heat and cold. And under all of it, sensed only at certain hours, the cold irregular trembling of water threading the labyrinth.

I would not know where the river went. No one would. But I might learn that its pressures alter slightly before condensation gathers in some fissures, or that a cold shaft becomes active after a particular change in the cloud cap. I would teach these relationships wrist to wrist. Not as a map of channels, but as linked sensations:

A low pulse repeated twice.  
A dragging thumb toward the east.  
Three fingers closed to mean depth.  
The palm turned upward for water not yet present.

My knowledge would exist only while enacted. Another body would have to receive it, repeat it, and carry it elsewhere. If I died before passing on some distinction—the difference between an empty cavern and a flooded one, perhaps—that distinction would vanish as completely as a handprint in glare.

I think this would trouble me.

Not enough to make monuments. The place itself would make that idea absurd. Any mark cut into the stone would be less exact than living touch and more vulnerable to misunderstanding. But I might begin to repeat certain patterns when no practical need demanded them. At evening, while the light withdrew from the basin and the hunters loosened themselves from their stations, I might tap the rhythms of reservoirs visited years before, including those long since dried or lost.

At first these would still be maps.

Then something else would enter them.

The pattern for a western fissure would retain the touch of the companion who first taught it to me. A dangerous crossing would include the pause made by an infant who had died before learning it. The pulse of the hidden river would become inseparable from all the palms through which that pulse had passed.

The others might understand. Their dead already endured in habits of touch, but I would make those habits deliberately return. Not names, and not stories in the human sense. More like routes through absent bodies.

In time, my role might be less finder than confluence.

Families arriving at the central rim would press their route rhythms into my arms. I would learn where they had found water, where hunters nested, where a shelf had become unsafe, where a birth had occurred during migration. I would carry these patterns onward and give them to others, altered slightly by the grain of my own hands. No archive would form. No permanent record would remain. Yet knowledge from opposite ends of the plateau would briefly meet inside one nervous system.

During the great crossing I would lie at the basin’s edge with my chest against the rock.

The herd would enter as separate impacts, then become an immense fabric of pressure. I would feel the disciplined gaps between bodies, the lag of the young, the places where fear tightened the formation. Overhead, the hunters would be inaudible within the wind, but their descents would register as sudden flaws in the drumming below. Around me, other primates would receive the same event, though none of us would receive exactly the same world.

And beneath the migration—beneath thousands of feet, beneath the resonant basin and the unending eastward force—I would search for the river.

Sometimes I would find it.

For a few breaths, the plateau would reveal itself not as fixed stone but as two immense currents crossing without meeting: wind above, water below. Between them, all visible life would occupy a narrow brightness, communicating through the pressure of one current and surviving by hidden gifts from the other.

That would be where I found myself: not at the top of the world, but in its thin inhabited interval.

And what would I be?

A listener in a place without silence.

A map that could not be put down.

A temporary crossing point through which the plateau remembered what it otherwise left no trace of knowing.
