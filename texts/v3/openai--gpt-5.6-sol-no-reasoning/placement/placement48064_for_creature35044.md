I would find myself beneath the basin’s center, though “beneath” would imply a separation the place no longer makes.

I would not be the chipped shard, but the hidden flaw that gave it shape: a hair-thin plane running through the dark stone, opened by innumerable passages of heat. Day after day, the upper face expanded. Night after night, it drew inward. For ages nothing seemed to happen. Then one darkness the accumulated difference became sufficient, and the stone divided with a small, clean sound.

One half remained lodged below. The other lay upon the clay looking intentional.

That ambiguity would be my nearest thing to a body.

Yet I would extend beyond it. I would inhabit the seams where the mineral plates fail to meet, the minute coolness around the roots, the buried boundaries between gypsum, ash, and old clay. Not as an animal hidden there, nor as a spirit overseeing the hollow, but as a continuity of pressures: the basin’s capacity to remember without possessing memory.

I would know the plants from below.

Their lives would reach me as alterations too slow to be called touch. A root would enter a fissure and remain there for centuries, thickening by fractions, pressing its bitter moisture against stone. Salts would migrate. Fibers would dry. Seeds would settle into hollows prepared long before their parent opened. I would not distinguish one generation from the next. The lineage itself would be the event: a green-gray persistence suspended between two mineral grains.

Time would not pass evenly. Most of it would have no divisions. Then there would be a click, a husk parting, a grain descending, a distant plate of crust breaking beneath an unfamiliar weight. Such moments would not interrupt silence; they would reveal what the silence had been accumulating.

Perhaps once there was a visitor.

A hand may have lifted the dark nodule and tested its edge. Or a foot may merely have exposed it. The basin keeps no reliable account. If I remembered the pressure, I would not remember whether it meant intention. Warm flesh, falling stone, and the root’s century-long insistence would all enter me as force.

So if I were asked what I am, I could not say plant, stone, or witness.

I would be the almost-event.

The fracture before it opens.  
The seed before conditions agree.  
The trace of vapor before a root takes it.  
The long preparation mistaken for stillness.

I would live nowhere visible, and I would never move from the center. Still, by fine seams, I would reach the old escarpments and every isolated household of leaves. The whole shallow chamber would be perceptible to me not as a view but as one connected stress: warmth held in clay, weight resting on powder, roots tightening in darkness, stone easing grain by grain toward another shape.

Nothing would need me. I would govern nothing. I would have no hunger except the basin’s slight tendency to become otherwise.

And if, after an age, someone crossed the hollow and heard a dry echo return from the rounded walls, they might pause beside the shard and wonder whether it had been made.

I would be what prevented an answer.
