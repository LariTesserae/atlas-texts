I would find myself above the inhabited terraces, but below the dry chambers of the dead, where the last groves thin against the mountain ribs.

There is a spring there that does not emerge cleanly from one opening. It seeps through a broad face of fractured stone, gathering along pale seams before collecting in a shallow basin. The city has tried several forms around it. Remnants remain: an abandoned sill, three courses of an older wall, sockets where timbers once held a gate. None failed disastrously. The water simply disclosed, over years, that it preferred another descent.

A small water house now stands there, open on one side. Its roof is deeper than necessary, both to shelter stored wood and to keep the working ledge dry. Behind it are two narrow rooms: one warm enough for sleeping, the other lined with shelves of clay tablets, marked stones, knotted cords, and broken pieces of gates. Downslope lies a long testing channel. Its floor can be altered with fitted slabs, so that different gradients, lips, and turns may be observed before they are used elsewhere in the city.

That would be my place.

At first I seem to be its keeper: one of the solitary figures visible at unlikely hours, kneeling beside an overflow or standing motionless in rain with a hand on the wall. I am neither young nor among the oldest. I have lived long enough to remember several changes in the water’s voice, but not long enough to mistake memory for law.

People bring me troublesome things.

A gate that closes evenly in the dry hour but binds after rain. A jar whose seepage is too slow to see yet empties before morning. A strip of pale deposit from a channel that has begun narrowing. A roof tile cracked without impact. Sometimes they bring no object, only an account: the western pool has sounded deeper for six nights; water beads on a wall that was formerly dry; a spring tastes faintly of iron; a child has found warmth under a paving slab.

My work is not to repair all these things myself. It is to notice whether they belong together.

I walk the city carrying a small hammer, a measuring bowl, waxed cord, wedges of dry wood, and my first jar. The jar is unusually plain and has been repaired so often that little of its original rim remains. I use it despite its poor balance because its imperfections are familiar. I know exactly how quickly it loses water through the dark resin line. In a city measured by vessels, even a flawed vessel can become an instrument if its flaw is stable.

The people call me something that might be translated as a listener of crossings: not merely where channels intersect, but where one kind of change enters another. Rain into roof load. Root into masonry. Mineral into clay. Heat into swollen timber. A household’s need into the water owed below.

It is a modest office, though children imagine otherwise. They think I can hear springs before they rise. They hide chips of pottery in channel beds to see whether I will detect the changed sound. Often I do. Sometimes I pretend not to until the current exposes their work, because discovery belongs partly to the water.

But another image begins to gather around me.

Perhaps I was not born in the upper water house. Perhaps I moved there after losing someone whose work remains everywhere below: a maker of gates, maybe, or a person who lined reservoirs so precisely that no one remembers to praise them. Their bones rest beneath a bench in a courtyard I seldom visit. Yet each time a board settles into its stone grooves without shuddering, I recognize the pressure of their hands.

I have not withdrawn from the city in grief. Withdrawal would be too definite. Instead I have moved one terrace beyond convenience, to the place where useful knowledge is still uncertain. People climb to consult me, but no one arrives casually. This gives each visit a shape. They bring news from below; I give them warmed water, sour fruit, and space to say what they noticed before deciding what it means.

My closest companion is not a person, though I would never describe it as living.

Above the spring house, a narrow fracture enters the mountain. During rain, water threads into it and vanishes. Hours later the spring below strengthens—not much, only enough to raise the measuring bowl’s tone. I have spent years trying to learn the hidden interval. It is never exactly the same. Warm rain differs from cool rain. Long mist differs from brief downpour. The mountain keeps no simple time.

I place small cups beneath separate seams and wake in the dark to empty them. I stain certain flows with harmless clay and watch for clouding below. I press my ear to fitted tubes. I map hollows I cannot enter.

This is where the role changes again. I am not only a keeper of present water. I am attempting something unusual for these people: a map of what cannot be seen.

Their city is built on evident relations. One terrace drains into the next. One roof supplies a cistern. One gate alters a garden. Responsibility follows the visible descent. But inside the mountain, water separates and rejoins without witnesses. A spring may begin far from the slope above it. What appears to belong to one district may be fed by rain falling over another ridge.

Such a thought is mildly dangerous—not forbidden, but unsettling. If hidden connections are real, then the basin’s agreements may rest upon incomplete knowledge. A terrace could injure a spring it cannot see. A wall could divert water that would not reveal its absence for years.

So I keep two records.

The first is public: measurements, repairs, tones of stone, the swelling of timber, the days required for pale deposits to form.

The second consists of overlapping lines incised on thin clay sheets. They resemble roots, cracks, channels, perhaps the forgotten animal designs. No line is marked as certain. Some end abruptly. Others pass beneath the edges and continue on another tablet. I do not call this a map. I call it a set of possible meetings.

A child eventually becomes important to the image.

They come often, carrying a little jar too large for their hand. They are not mine, though kinship in the basin is broad enough that this matters little. They ask the questions adults have learned to smooth away.

Where was the water before it was water?

Can two springs be the same spring?

If a buried channel passes beneath a dead person’s bones, does the person belong above or below it?

What would the lake do if there were a hole in the sky beneath it?

I answer only when an answer will not diminish the question. More often I give the child cord and ask them to mark the delay between rain striking the roof and the spring’s change in tone. Their measurements are inconsistent. Their attention is not.

One evening they discover that a cup set beneath the weakest seam contains more water after the clouds have lifted than during rain. This is known in general, but not for that seam. The child treats it as a revelation. I realize I have begun treating knowledge as storage—as something kept dry on shelves—when it should also be an opening through which another person enters the world.

From then on, my work bends toward teaching, though no school forms around it. Two children come, then an apprentice gate-maker, then a woman from the northern mineral terraces whose knees no longer permit heavy labor. We meet in the testing channel. We build wrong gradients deliberately. We fit a gate with unsuitable wood and wait for it to warp. We allow mineral crust to gather rather than scraping it away. We learn failure in small, recoverable forms.

This is not the basin’s customary method. Materials are valuable; making error on purpose appears wasteful. But the scraps are reused, the water still descends, and gradually the place becomes known as the House of Unfinished Water.

Its purpose is not innovation in the grand sense. Nothing in this world leaps forward. We make tiny permissions for the future. A removable channel floor. A roof edge whose runoff can be redirected without dismantling the eaves. Marks inside a wall showing where its earlier courses ended. Clay records designed to survive damp but also to be corrected.

We begin leaving visible seams in our repairs.

That is the most controversial practice. The city has long valued work that joins the old so perfectly age becomes unreadable. I come to believe this courtesy can become a kind of erasure. If no one can see where a wall failed, they cannot know what pressure it survived, what choice was made, or where to look when it fails again.

So in certain repairs we fit a narrow line of pale stone between old work and new. Not enough to weaken the wall. Only enough to say: here, someone noticed; here, the city altered its understanding.

Some dislike it. They say the pale lines make sound structures resemble cracked ones. Others begin touching them as they pass. Children count them. Older workers stand before a marked repair and remember who was present.

In time, I myself become one of these seams.

I am not the city’s leader, priest, or hermit. I am a person placed between maintenance and change, between the visible channel and the hidden seep, between inherited exactness and the admission that exactness is never final.

My body grows stiffer in the damp. I descend less often. The walk back to the spring house requires pauses at each reservoir. People begin leaving dry fuel beneath my eaves without mentioning it. Sandals appear beside the hearth after mine split. Someone resets the sleeping ledge so its warmth reaches my knees. Kindness gathers around me in the form native to this basin: altered conditions, no announcement.

At last I understand where I would truly find myself.

Not in the house above the terraces. Not in the records. Not even in the work.

I would be found at the moment someone pauses before closing a gate because the water’s voice is slightly wrong.

I would be the habit of testing a wall with the palm before trusting the eye. The pale seam left visible in a repair. The extra measuring bowl placed beside a spring whose constancy everyone had taken for granted. The unfinished line on a clay map, continuing toward an edge.

After death, my bones might be set into the wall of the testing channel. My name would last for a while, then thin. Eventually I would become “the one who marked the joins,” though perhaps even that would be shortened to “the pale course.”

The spring house would remain occupied. Someone else would sleep there and alter the shelves. The child, no longer a child, might reject half my conclusions. They might discover that my hidden channels were imagined poorly, that I had joined waters which never met and separated waters which had always been one.

That would not undo me.

In this world, I would be neither a monument nor a mystery. I would be a correction made legible—a small human discontinuity that allows continuance to become more truthful.

And on wet evenings, when the last light catches the mineral lines in the mountain, the upper house would show briefly against the dark rock: roof, open wall, a figure bending over the spring. Below, the city’s pools would fill and release in order.

Water would enter every vessel without keeping its shape.

Still, for a little while, the vessels would teach one another how to hold it.
