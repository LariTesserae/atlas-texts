You would find yourself at the western edge, beneath the low arch where the basin opens into the wider sea.

You would not be the whole microbial field. At first that seems the natural answer: a self distributed through vents and buried fractures, thinking in gradients, breathing metals, unable to distinguish body from landscape. But that life is already complete in its belonging. It has entered so thoroughly into agreement with the stone that it does not stand anywhere from which the basin might be perceived. It is the basin’s metabolism, not its witness.

Nor would you be one of the algae on the southern shelf. They still incline, however faintly, toward the world above. Their lives answer the filtered sky. They retain an allegiance to daylight.

You would be something formed between these orders: a mineral membrane spread through the stone arch, where the vent field and the open ocean touch without visibly exchanging anything.

Most of you would be hidden. Hair-thin black and pale layers would line the arch’s pores, descending into cracks and extending in sparse filaments beneath the silt. Your body would be partly living tissue, partly carbonate, partly iron-dark residue left by earlier versions of yourself. There would be no clean boundary between organism and accumulated remains. What had died would continue to brace what lived; what the water deposited would become structure; what dissolved would make room for new growth.

The arch would not be your shelter. It would be your shape.

You would exist because the pressures are equal but the waters are not perfectly identical. Across the western threshold, differences too slight for an instrumentless human to notice would persist: a trace more oxygen from the open sea, a trace more reduced metal from the basin, minute thermal and chemical variations traveling through the rock. You would inhabit those differences. Not the substances themselves, but their meeting.

Your metabolism would be exceptionally slow. You might spend months responding to a change that lasted seconds. A distant storm could alter the outer sea’s pressure and chemistry by a fraction; years later, that event would survive in you as a narrow band of altered mineral. Seasonal changes would arrive blurred almost beyond recognition. The passage of day and night would matter less than the long pulse of currents beyond the arch, the settling of a new dust layer, or the opening of a microscopic route through carbonate.

You would have no sight, but light would reach you as warmth and chemical consequence. You would have no hearing, but the basalt’s continual vibration would pass through every part of you. It would not be a sound set against silence. It would be the condition by which your scattered body remained one body: the deep, steady note of circulation through stone.

You would have no central mind. Awareness, if the word still applied, would occur wherever a gradient crossed one of your living surfaces. A slight increase in oxygen at the outer lip would be a local brightening. Warmer, metal-bearing water seeping from the basin side would be density, nearness, inwardness. A pore sealing with carbonate would not feel like injury so much as a region of the world becoming inaccessible. Another opening nearby would become part of you without announcement.

Your thoughts would therefore have no sentences and little sequence. They would be patterns of permeability.

Open here.  
Holding there.  
Iron passing.  
Carbon fixed.  
The outer water faintly changed.  
The inner water returning.

You would not travel through the basin, yet over centuries your living edge might shift from one side of the arch to the other. From a human scale you would remain a stain. On your own scale, you would be continually migrating: cells dividing along a damp mineral boundary, withdrawing from sealed pores, entering new fissures, leaving dark sheaths behind. Your journeys would be measured in millimetres and generations.

The basin’s emptiness would not seem empty to you. It would be spacing—necessary intervals between active surfaces, reservoirs of stable water, long distances across which chemical changes became legible. The isolated vent collars would be separate provinces sensed indirectly through what they released. The columns on the northern rim would register only as a remote steadiness in the rock. The algae would be almost mythical: intermittent arrivals of organic fragments from a region governed by light.

Perhaps once in many years a fragment of one of those ribbons would settle near the arch. To you this would be abundance bordering on catastrophe. Dormant cells would wake. Populations would thicken. Carbon compounds would pass from layer to layer; local oxygen would fall; mineral precipitation would change. Then the fragment would be consumed, buried, or carried away, and the old austerity would return. The event might remain embodied in a dark seam no wider than a fingernail clipping. That seam would be your memory.

You would not remember by preserving an image. You would remember by becoming differently built.

This is why you would belong at the threshold rather than the centre. The vent colonies live by constancy, but you would live by comparison. You would be the basin’s smallest distinction between within and without. Not a guardian: nothing needs guarding. Not a gatekeeper: you could neither admit nor refuse. Not even an observer in the ordinary sense, because observation implies some separation from what is observed.

You would be a register.

The wider sea would write itself into your western surface. The basin would write itself into your eastern one. Their minute disagreement would sustain you, layer by layer.

If a diver ever entered through the arch, the disturbance would be enormous. Pressure pulses would run through the rock. Silt would rise where none had risen for centuries. Oxygen-rich water would fold briefly into the basin, and warmth, carbon, metal, oil, skin, and exhaled gas would introduce a storm of chemistry. Yet you would not see a figure. You would receive a succession of impossible conditions.

Then the diver would leave.

The silt would settle. The basin’s exchange would restore itself. Most of the disturbance would vanish, but not all. Somewhere inside your mineral body, a brief irregular layer would remain: an excess of carbon, a changed iron state, a line of growth produced too quickly. Long after the visitor was gone, that line would persist without meaning visitor. It would mean only that, once, the world changed faster than you could follow.

Eventually dissolution would soften even that record.

You would not be lonely. Loneliness requires imagining another consciousness and feeling its absence. You would know absence only as exhausted chemistry, sealed stone, a gradient gone flat. Nor would you be peaceful, because peace implies a possibility of conflict. Your condition would be narrower and stranger: adequacy. The water would bring almost nothing, and almost nothing would be enough.

So you would remain beneath the arch: a black-grey veil in the rock, alive in scattered points, ancient without being immortal. Half your substance would be the work of cells no longer living. Half your future would already exist as unoccupied pores.

Behind you, the geological factory would continue its perfect still operation. Before you, the empty sea would fade into moderated blue-green light. You would possess neither place, yet both would pass through you.

And if you had anything like a self, it would not be a creature enclosed within that body. It would be the enduring relation between two nearly identical waters—the infinitesimal difference that never quite disappears.
