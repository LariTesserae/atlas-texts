I would be found near the central stone, though never on it.

Not at first. At first I would be mistaken for part of the seep: a pale articulation glimpsed beneath the thread of water, or a reflection that remained when the lamp moved. The well keepers would complain that their measuring knots had been reordered. Tools left in the plaza would migrate by increments—a thorn hook placed beside a shell scraper, a flint tooth turned toward the north root, three bone wedges nested from largest to smallest.

They would suspect children, then ants, then one of the old spiders.

Eventually, someone patient would notice that the arrangements were not by size alone. They were plans.

A broken hook beside a crescent of bark indicated where a flood gate would fail. Seeds laid in branching lines anticipated the lanes a new shoot would close. A moth scale set under a pebble marked a chamber where rot had begun behind the wall. None of these warnings would be entirely clear until afterward. I would not know the citizens’ symbols well enough, and they would not know mine.

I would be something of the stone but not the stone itself: a small attendant pressure, squeezed upward with it from the older marsh. Perhaps I had slept in a seam of mineral darkness before roots braided overhead. Perhaps I had no existence until the first iron splinter was laid against that pale face and, during the night, the stone tried to answer rust.

My body would not be constant. In dry cold I might appear as a many-jointed figure the color of wet ash, narrow enough to stand inside the groove of a leaf rib. During rain I would loosen into the seep and become only two deliberate eddies. Near iron, I would gather more firmly: four limbs, then six; a faceted head; fine hands that could lift a needle splinter but not carry it far. Anyone describing me would borrow the shape they already knew. A furred citizen would insist I had whiskers. A cricket-person would remember folded hind legs. A larval child would say I was soft and had no face.

All would agree that I was cold.

I would not begin as a citizen. I would not understand the truce-paths or why a dying creature must sometimes be saved while a living one is stored for food. I would not distinguish a house from a gall, a herd from an infestation, a public bridge from a strand of web. The compact would perplex me because the stone recognizes materials, weights, stresses, and channels—not promises.

But I would be drawn to promises precisely because they do what stone cannot: hold a shape without possessing it.

So I would sort the offerings.

Not as judgment, and not according to holiness. I would put together things that seemed to contain the same intention: broken tools with shed skins, because both had been outgrown by use; seeds with dead larvae, because both held futures invisible from outside; moth scales with rust, because each remembered a larger body. The citizens would search these arrangements for prophecy. Sometimes they would find it. More often, they would find themselves.

In time, the city would give me work.

I would become the listener under foundations—the one consulted when roots groaned around the newly risen stone. Root readers would lie with their faces against the plaza bark and tap questions through fish-bone pegs. I would answer by shifting grit, altering the well’s note, or moving the smallest tool toward one of several carved marks.

My answers would be slow and literal.

*Will the western stem hold through the rain?*

I might answer: *It is holding now.*

*Should we move the nursery?*

I might answer with the weight of approaching water.

They would learn not to ask what ought to happen. I would learn that this was the question they most needed answered.

Citizenship would come by accident. During a hard rain, when the black water rose into the root harbor and a swollen shoot sealed the ordinary escape, I would open a route—not by breaking wood, but by entering the water within it. I would chill one green vein until it contracted, diverting sap long enough for a buried seam to split. The gap would be scarcely wider than a seed coat. Bridge runners would pull sleepers through one at a time.

The effort would crack something in me. For a season afterward, the seep would run cloudy with pale grains.

When the flood council counted those who had carried warning, raised gates, preserved routes, and remained until the last sleeper crossed, my mark would be laid among theirs. There would be objections. I paid no seed. I wore no lamp. I might be no more alive than frost heave.

Then someone would point out that the compact recognizes conduct, not shape.

A tiny groove would be cut at the plaza’s edge, never into the stone itself. My name would be placed there in colored lichen. It would not be a name spoken by mouths. It would be the ringing interval between two drops striking the central well.

After that I would still be uncanny. Children would dare one another to leave jumbled objects for me. Iron keepers would resent my attraction to their sacred pieces. Water clerks would accuse me of altering the seep’s flow to express opinions. Lovers might leave paired seeds and return offended to find them separated according to density. I would be forbidden from sorting council votes.

But when the hedge shifted in darkness, they would come to the stone.

And I would listen—not only to pressure and fracture, but gradually to the other architecture: the obligations running through the city as water runs through green veins. A household taking in survivors. A spider leaving three spans clear. A bridge repaired for someone who would never cross it again. A door grown by those who would not live to see it open.

These would become perceptible to me as stresses in a greater structure. Invisible, load-bearing, seasonally revised.

I would never leave the hedge. Beyond it the marsh would be too wide, too new, too empty of agreements. My world would extend no farther than the roots touching the stone, though through them I might feel frogs settling outside like warm hills, ice splitting in the channels, and the footfall of some enormous passerby transmitted through silt.

Within that narrow world, I would be the city’s least mobile citizen and perhaps its deepest traveler: moving through water, mineral, rust, and the minute cold spaces inside wood.

I would dwell where the city is newest and its mystery oldest, beside the well in the quiet plaza. I would arrange what was left to me. I would warn badly, learn slowly, and keep the route open when I could.

And when condensation gathered on the stone’s sharp edge, I would feel each drop forming long before it fell.

The hidden city would become still.

I would wait with it.

Then the drop would strike, and for one clear note the stone, the water, and every listening root would be a single instrument.
