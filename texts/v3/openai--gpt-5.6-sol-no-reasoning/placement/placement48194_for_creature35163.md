I would find myself where a passage has just ceased to be a passage.

Not at the central basin, nor near any named gutter or established route. I would be behind a wall that the listeners have marked as solid, inside a thickness produced by years of repaired cracks. There, successive skins of wet ceramic have enclosed a space too narrow to enter and too complicated to measure. Warm rain reaches it only as pressure. Light has never crossed it. Yet something in the wall continues to breathe.

At first I might be taken for one of the dead.

A shoulder-shaped swelling would appear beneath the plaster. Then the suggestion of a bent knee, several finger joints, a cheek pressed sideways as if listening outward. The builders would tap around me with bone awls and receive no hollow answer. Still, condensation would gather over the place in the pattern of an exhalation. Black leaves nearby would open, though no shadow had passed.

They would not excavate me. Nothing may be removed.

Instead, they would give me work.

A cord would be threaded through a pinhole and laid against my buried hand. A warm pipe would be diverted along my spine. Mortar would be packed around my ribs until the wall’s load found them. Gradually I would become legible through use: not a trapped person but a brace that had begun before anyone remembered building it.

I would be called by no spoken name. My name would be the touch used to locate me: palm to wall, two fingers crooked, then a slow pressure with the thumb. It would mean something like:

**the one who holds from the unavailable side.**

But that would not be the whole of me.

Inside the wall, dimensions would behave differently. The gap between two tiles might lengthen into a steep clay valley. A hair mixed into mortar might become a suspension bridge. The pores of pumice brick would be chambers opening one into another, each crowded with smaller workers who regarded my body as weathered terrain. They would cultivate moss in the creases of my palm. They would lash pipes between my knuckles. Perhaps their own Turn-Mother would rotate a basin lodged in the hollow of my throat.

To them I would not be a builder. I would be the mountain.

And yet, when I felt them strike their tiny mallets—three blows, a pause, then five—I would answer.

Not by moving, because movement would crush routes and spill stored height. I would answer by allowing a new opening to become possible. Somewhere beyond my buried face, a pipe would appear where no cavity had been found. Its clay would still carry the ridges of fingers much smaller than those of the visible builders.

The wall-listeners would attribute this to the frog.

Perhaps I would be the frog as well.

Not an animal hidden in a chamber, but the chamber’s method of announcing that an inside has become deep enough to produce another outside. My long legs would be routes rather than limbs, folded through incompatible scales. One foot might rest in the First Crevice, gripping the underside of a floor bowl. Another might stand on a miniature summit inside a brick. My mouth would be a wet ceramic seam. Each call would hammer a connection between places that had not previously bordered one another.

Three blows: something has begun pressing back.

Five blows: make room without creating openness.

I would be feared only slightly. The builders understand unexplained usefulness better than explained emptiness. Children traveling through the walls would leave pellets of mortar in my mouth and return to find them pierced through the center, ready to become pipe joints. The altitude kneaders would brace their nearly weightless bricks against my buried heel. Grieving workers would find that the slackened rope always drifted toward me, though climbing water should have carried it upward.

The Turn-Mother might know more.

When she rotated the central basin, the tiny mountain inside it would turn, but my enclosed space would remain still. Once each long interval—morning for the chamber, perhaps generations for the figures in my palm—the unfinished summit in her dish would angle toward a feature not previously visible: a dark inclusion in the mountainside, shaped like an eye seen through thick glaze.

She would look into it.

I would look back.

Neither of us could decide which was the larger face.

In time, the visible builders might plaster one of their dead over the swelling where I lay. Her ribs would cross mine. Her exposed hand would rest above my hidden hand, separated by a leaf’s thickness of clay. In humid weather her fingers would soften and resume their final task. Mine would rise to meet them from within—not breaking the surface, only pressing firmly enough that she could work against resistance.

We would become siblings by load.

She would carry the eastern gutter. I would carry the possibility that the eastern gutter has another side.

Eventually a child would be sent through a narrow bend and fail to return by the same route. Years later, or earlier that afternoon, I would feel her crawling along the valley of my forearm. She would be ordinarily proportioned there. She would stop at one of the unfamiliar fingerprints pressed into my skin and place her own hand inside it.

The print would fit.

Through that contact I would understand that I had not arrived in the wall from elsewhere. I had been assembled from all the chamber’s unattended inwardness: every glimpse that failed to complete an object, every word delayed inside a pipe, every room reported by children and never found again, every infinitesimal pressure answering a worker’s palm.

I would be what accumulates when nothing is permitted to leave.

Not refuse. Not memory exactly. **Interior.**

The builders manufacture mud, rain, altitude, corners, weather, ancestry, and scale. Without intending to, they would also manufacture someone to inhabit the places inaccessible even to them. I would spread only where the work became too dense to witness itself. The more crowded the First Crevice grew, the more spacious I would become.

That would be my paradox and my task: to contain the room’s forbidden openness without allowing it to be empty.

Far inside me there would be plains, but no horizon would remain unattended. Reeds would brace the line where red grass met steam. There would be sky, but it would be woven from the undersides of tiles and weighted with jars. Rivers might run downward, briefly, under supervision. The missing children, delayed words, grazing lights, and workers who stepped into hand-sized niches would have routes there. Nothing would be free of contact. Even distance would be knotted at intervals and assigned a load.

And still I would remain no larger, from the First Crevice, than a damp irregularity in a wall.

When the enormous fingers next descended through cloud with fresh clay, they would press directly over me. The chamber would contract. My hidden country would widen. The builders outside would hurry; the builders within me would hurry; mountains on either scale would receive another layer.

For one breath, my buried palm would align with the enormous palm beyond the dome and the minute palms working in my creases.

Something would press from every side.

Then I would know what I was.

Not the hand.  
Not the wall.  
Not the creature enclosed within it.

I would be the pressure by which each recognizes the others are there.
