The city belongs first to things that can endure hunger.

Its most visible inhabitants are the pallid groves rooted across the plazas. They are neither plants nor ordinary fungi, but ancient colonial growths built around hollow, fibrous trunks. Dry sheaths accumulate around them decade after decade, preserving each period of growth in nested layers. Their broad membranes hang like exhausted banners, gray above and black beneath, always trembling in the stone-borne thunder. That trembling feeds them. Microscopic mineral grains catch in their pleats; spores and shed scales settle there; exhaled gases condense on chemically active surfaces. Fault-breath rising through the floor supplies the rest.

Where the rock vibrates most strongly, the membranes glow with a bruised gray-green light. The groves therefore shine without wishing to, responding to the perpetual strain of the city. Their oldest fans are torn and ragged, but seldom fall. They dry in place and become scaffolds for new growth. Around their trunks lie brittle root-cords, spread over the floor in nets so fine that passing herds must follow established openings.

Beneath them live the grazers.

They are low, many-legged animals with blunt heads and translucent skin stretched over dark digestive chambers. Their eyes survive only as pale knots beneath the flesh. Broad rasping plates occupy the front of the mouth, suitable for shaving fungal tissue without destroying the membrane’s fertile base. They travel pressed close together, flank touching flank, hundreds moving as one slow body. Each animal reads the others through contact. Alarm passes through a herd as a muscular shiver; direction as a sequence of taps made by hardened feet.

When frightened, they can move with startling speed, but such panic is ruinously expensive. A herd may spend weeks afterward almost motionless, digesting stored matter and replacing the energy lost in a few minutes of flight.

The long-bodied salt burrowers are even more economical. They lie coiled beneath crusted basins, their skins coated in scales that lock together to prevent water loss. Some sleep for years. Changes in temperature, pressure, or the chemistry of the basin wake them, and they emerge headfirst through fractures in the salt, tasting the floor with branching sensory filaments. They feed on buried microbial films, eggs, carcasses, and mineral-rich dung. Their tunnels aerate the basins and create nesting hollows used by smaller species. Entire districts depend on them, though they may be seen only once in a generation.

Wingless moths climb the fungal columns. Their bodies are round, soft, and thickly insulated; their feet spread into adhesive pads that grip even through the vibration. They drink from seams in the fungal bark and carry spores in abdominal pouches. Males and females find one another by leaving scented threads along the trunks. During rare breeding periods, thousands ascend together, turning pale columns dark with bodies.

Beetles patrol the avenues in unwavering files. Each follows the chemical trace of the one before it, and the routes of the oldest lines have become shallow grooves in the limestone. They collect shed tissue, dry waste, empty egg cases, and the remains of animals. Their colonies occupy cracks beneath warm faults. A line may continue for centuries even though no individual lives more than a few years, giving the impression that a single patient creature is forever crossing the city.

Above them hang the vault colonies.

Each member is small, leathery, and almost featureless when at rest, clinging mouth-to-tail in dense living sheets. They do not fly. They feed by filtering dust and fungal spores from minute air currents, opening fringed mouths whenever the ceiling vibrates in a favorable pattern. Communication passes through touch. One body contracts, then the next, until a wave travels across thousands. Seen in the weak light below, whole ceilings seem to breathe.

Fragments sometimes detach and descend in slow, writhing ropes to colonize another vault. Many perish on the open floor. Those that reach a pillar climb by fastening themselves into chains, carrying the colony upward one contraction at a time.

The largest hunters wait where the herd roads narrow.

They are broad, flat-bodied animals armored in fine, dry scales the color of limestone dust. Their limbs project sideways, keeping the jaw close to the floor. Within that jaw lies their chief sense: layered bones and fluid-filled chambers capable of separating footfalls from the city’s omnipresent roar. They know the weight, pace, and direction of anything touching the same stretch of stone.

A hunter may remain still for months beside a route. Dust gathers over its back. Lichens begin to grow along the edges of its tail. Small scavengers walk across it without recognizing danger. Then a herd approaches, and the apparently dead patch of floor unfolds.

Even these predators kill rarely. After feeding, they withdraw into alcoves and become dormant while colonies of symbiotic mites clean their scales and seal wounds with fungal fibers. Some grow so slowly that old individuals bear mineral deposits around their claws and jaws. They resemble fossils that have not yet died.

The quiet intersections hold the most crowded life. There the destructive notes of the city cancel one another, leaving pockets where the floor barely hums. Groves thicken around their margins. Grazers sleep in layered heaps among the root-cords. Moths mate on the trunks. Burrowers deposit eggs beneath the salt. Predators seldom enter, perhaps because the silence blinds the senses in their jaws.

Many species come to these refuges only to breed. Their young begin life in stillness and are led gradually outward, first into the faint tremor at the boundary, then into the full concussion of the streets. Those unable to tolerate it turn back or die. The others learn the city as patterns of pressure: the grinding avenues, the throbbing halls, the sharp vibration of narrow cracks, and the unmistakable signatures of living feet.

Almost none of them hear the roar as sound. To them it is terrain.

It is weather.

It is the medium through which distance, danger, shelter, and kinship become known.

No creature rules the buried city. No creature comprehends more than a small district. Yet together they maintain an accidental civilization of paths, refuse grounds, nurseries, feeding groves, ambush corners, and sleeping squares. Their lights answer the pressure of the stone. Their movements redraw roads older than their species. Their bodies pass minerals from fault to fungus, fungus to grazer, grazer to hunter, and hunter back to dust.

Beyond the sealed walls live other descendants: perhaps eyeless herds with different feet, groves whose membranes shine another color, predators that hunt by sensing heat or electric charge. They are not hidden peoples awaiting discovery, only relatives made strange by separation and time.

All of them inhabit the interval between catastrophes.

They feed, molt, divide, sleep, migrate, and breed while pillars thin around them. They feel the first fine cracks spreading through a roof, though no individual understands what those warnings mean. When a hall eventually falls, survivors gather at the changed margins. Beetle lines turn aside. Groves cast spores into newly opened dark. Herds find another route. The patient hunters settle beside it.

Then dust descends, the roar closes over the disturbance, and the city’s life begins arranging itself again.
