The people of the Throatworks are called the **Tenders**, though the name implies more authority than they possess.

At a distance they are difficult to distinguish from the traffic of the town. They are small, stooped beings with clay-colored hides and long limbs built for climbing rather than walking. Fine sensory filaments lie flat along their forearms, cheeks, and ribs. These lift in sequence when a root carries vibration, so that a listening Tender seems to bristle beneath the skin.

Their eyes are broad and dark but poor at detail. Faces are recognized by warmth, odor, gait, and the private rhythms tapped through wrist bones. Their mouths are narrow and usually covered with veils of woven mycelium, partly against pollen and moth scales, partly because an uncovered breath wastes water. Behind their jaws are expandable resonant sacs. In close quarters these permit a soft, percussive speech—clicks, pulses, swallowed hoots—but most public communication is conducted through touch.

A Tender greeting another places two fingers against the pale patch beneath the throat. A greeting offered to a building is much the same.

They wear little that has been killed outright. Their harnesses are grown around them from compliant vine. Tools bud from belts and are pinched free when mature. Moss pads protect knees and palms. Workers in the pollen houses cover themselves in waxy leaf-sheaths; river keepers wear buoyant collars full of sealed seed chambers. The oldest Tenders often carry entire gardens on their backs: medicinal molds, signaling flowers, tiny reservoirs, colonies of mites that clean wounds.

They have no single ruler. Authority moves according to crisis. During rain, the Channel Readers command. During drought, the Cistern Mouths do. If the Press falters, everyone obeys the Knockkeepers, who sleep in hammocks attached directly to its frame and can identify each fault by the ache it produces in their joints. Migrations belong to the Lane Mothers, regardless of what crosses the court—ants, beetles, frogs, or something unnamed. A Lane Mother will order a workshop dismantled rather than delay a column whose purpose she does not understand.

Very few Tenders are born in an uncomplicated sense.

Their young begin as soft, eyeless nurselings carried in communal throat-pouches by adults of several kinds. Later they are installed in nursery gourds, where plant tendrils enter temporary openings along the spine and feed them mineral sap. Each child shares its chamber with selected larvae, spores, and root lice. These companions train the immune body and establish the odors by which the town will recognize the child. A young Tender raised without them may survive, but doors will not reliably open for it, bridges may stiffen beneath it, and sentinel ants treat its footprints as foreign.

On emergence, the child is not named immediately. It spends a season following migrations and sleeping in different districts. Its eventual name commemorates the first useful pattern it notices: **Third Drip Before Rain**, **Warm Cable at Rest**, **Moths Turning Below**, **Two Knocks Missing**. Names change after serious injury, apprenticeship, or any event that alters the way the city responds to a person.

The Tenders eat what the Throatworks declines to reclaim. Their staple is shaved from the dark cakes before these are surrendered to the Press: bitter, fibrous paste fermented with luminous bacteria. They also drink insect milk, cultivate pale mushrooms in abandoned tunnels, and harvest the unfertilized eggs of the short-winged birds. Meat is common but rarely hunted. The city produces bodies constantly. Nothing edible remains unclaimed for long.

They do not consider the animals vermin. Roaches clean warm machinery. Rodents prune inaccessible growth. Bats regulate moth blooms and add phosphorus to the ceiling gardens. Even predators have civic functions. Root-colored snakes are encouraged around granaries and nurseries alike, though they are marked with fragrant clay so that children can smell where one hangs.

Ants occupy a more difficult category. Tenders speak of them as neighboring nations, weather, and distributed machinery, sometimes within the same sentence. Negotiations are carried out by adjusting scent stones along their lanes. Wars do occur, but the Tenders describe them as “misunderstandings of scale.”

Their dead are divided according to what appears to have claimed them. The bones go to the mineral works, flesh to the migration courts, blood to thirsty walls, sensory filaments to the warning bridges. The throat sac is dried intact and hung in the family chamber. When air passes through it, it produces a faint version of the deceased’s habitual note. Thus the dormitories murmur with ancestral breathing whenever pressure changes.

Some Tenders object to this practice. They believe the city learns voices from the preserved throats and uses them to summon workers into unsafe passages. There are stories of apprentices hearing a dead parent call from inside a wall that has no chamber behind it.

The Tenders insist they did not build the Press. They also insist they have always maintained it. Both claims are supported by their oldest records, which are not written but grown as knot sequences in perennial roots. Reading these archives requires grafting a thin filament beneath the tongue and remaining motionless for hours while stored contractions pass through the mouth.

Those who have done so return with inconsistent histories.

In one account, the first Tenders descended from the surface to shelter from a burning season and discovered the Press already working.

In another, they arrived as eggs inside the bodies of migratory animals.

In a third, the Throatworks made them from clay, marrow, and the dexterous forelimbs of six different species because its valves had become too intricate to clean.

The oldest sequence contains no people at all. It records only pressure, moisture, hunger, and a repeated interruption shaped like a hand.

Most Tenders are satisfied not to know. Their ethics are practical: keep passages open, relieve strain, return nutrients, attend to changes in rhythm. They distrust anything that has only one purpose. A good object should be edible when broken, structural while alive, and capable of carrying a warning. A good citizen should likewise know at least three trades.

There are exceptions.

In sealed alcoves beyond the workshops live the **Ungrafted**, Tenders who have cut their sensory filaments and cauterized the root ports along their spines. They claim the town’s instructions are not laws but impulses—that the pale roots do not report pain, but manufacture obedience. They use dead tools, burn precious oil for light, and speak aloud even across open chambers. The rest regard them with fear and reluctant respect. Ungrafted workers can enter diseased districts without sharing the sickness. They can also sever pale roots without collapsing from sympathetic agony.

The Throatworks sometimes seals their doors.

Whether this is deliberate remains a matter of doctrine.

Above all others stand—or hang—the **Knockkeepers** of the Press. Their bodies are distorted by lifelong vibration. Their bones thicken asymmetrically; their fingertips broaden into listening pads; many lose ordinary hearing while becoming exquisitely sensitive to impacts conducted through stone. They know when a cake falls cleanly, when it catches against the throat, and when something below strikes it on the way down.

That last sound is never entered into public record.

When the Press misses one stroke, the Knockkeepers listen.

At two, they release bitter scent into every inhabited root.

At three, the town climbs: Tenders, rodents, birds, beetles, even snakes winding upward along the cables. The lower courts empty in moments. Reservoir fruits contract against the ceiling. Bridges curl toward high anchorages. Doors open whether occupied or not.

The black pool beneath the Press then rises without rain.

What emerges has never been fully seen. Witnesses describe pale pressure against the water, a ring of blunt digits, or a mouth too broad to belong to an animal that could pass through the stone throat. The oldest Knockkeepers say it is not climbing into the city. It is turning in its sleep, far below, and the Throatworks is only the narrow part of its breathing.

The cakes, in that understanding, are not fuel or food or offerings.

They are what keeps the sleeper swallowing.

This may explain why the Tenders continue their labor despite famine, infestation, and the private suspicions of the Ungrafted. It may explain why every creature in the cavern cries out when the engines restart. The sound is not celebration.

It is relief.
