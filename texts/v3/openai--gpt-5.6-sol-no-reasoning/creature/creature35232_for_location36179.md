The people of the valley are compact, sure-footed, and difficult to hurry, even when moving quickly. They travel with one hand free for rails, ladders, hanging straps, and wet masonry. Their coats are short enough not to catch in machinery, layered against chill rather than deep cold, and reinforced at shoulder and knee. Boots are the most personal thing they own: resoled repeatedly, marked for traction with district-specific cuts, dried overnight on warm pipes when there is room. Most carry gloves, a folding hood, a lamp, and some means of making a temporary mark—a grease pencil, chalk, colored tape.

They do not divide neatly into residents and maintenance workers. A teacher may clear a roof drain before opening a classroom. A baker knows how to seat a floodboard. Children can identify the knock of air in a water main and the colored lamps of a pressure warning. Shopkeepers keep valve keys beneath their counters. Clerks arriving at an office will pause to move a planter from an overloaded terrace or report a crack that has widened across the night.

No one assumes that a route will remain open because it was open yesterday. Directions are given conditionally: take the lower tram if the east spillway is dark; cross through the laundry unless the third shift has begun; follow the blue railing until the stairs become water. Addresses include elevation, access hours, and sometimes weather. A household may have three front doors on three levels, each becoming primary under different conditions.

Their sense of ownership is correspondingly layered. A room may belong to one family, its roof to a garden cooperative, its rear wall to the drainage authority, and the heat passing beneath its floor to a neighboring works. Rights are precise but rarely absolute. Access hatches are never obstructed. Cupboards are built around old inspection ports. Furniture stands on adjustable feet. Every household knows which wall may be opened without notice in an emergency.

The oldest families are not necessarily wealthy, but they possess maps that others lack: annotated plans, inherited keys, notebooks of water behavior, lists of walls that sweat before a storm. Such knowledge carries social weight. So does the ability to remember earlier versions of the city. Elderly residents describe vanished streets as if they were still present below the paving, and often they are. When repairs uncover a sealed stair or flooded arcade, someone can usually say where it once emerged.

Children learn the valley by following water. They float leaves down curb channels, race beside descending freight lifts, and dare one another to touch the cold mist at spillway mouths. Their games reproduce adult procedures: miniature barricades, evacuation routes, bridges assembled from crates. In school they keep rainfall ledgers and tend beds of water mint or dwarf willow. Before being trusted alone on the trams, they must know how to read subsidence flags, where to stand when a cable passes, and which bells mean that a staircase is about to flood.

Adolescents range farther, climbing maintenance ways and entering abandoned levels where the city has withdrawn without truly leaving. They trade obsolete access tokens, enamel warning plates, fragments of patterned tile, and stories about chambers below the canal. Most stories are exaggerated, but the spaces are real: drowned workshops, dry rooms inside retaining walls, stations whose tracks were converted into conduits. Exploration is tolerated until it interferes with flow.

Work organizes life more strongly than daylight. The dim valley has made the clock more authoritative than the sun, but shifts remain flexible because rain outranks schedules. Sirens do not merely summon labor; they announce changes in the city’s condition. A low note may release one district and mobilize another. People pause to count the replies traveling up the ravines. From their sequence they can tell which turbines have stopped, where freight is being held, and how far the storm has reached.

Much of the population belongs to the vast, overlapping trades that keep structure, water, power, transport, and growth in balance. There are sluice keepers and wall readers, tram riggers, root engineers, cistern divers, tunnel electricians, terrace gardeners, façade masons, scaffold crews, lock attendants, fungal surveyors, and people whose sole specialty is listening to pipes. Their expertise is narrow but never isolated. A root engineer must understand load. A mason must anticipate runoff. A gardener knows which inspection plates cannot be shaded and which vines can cool a transformer enclosure without trapping moisture.

Among them move the coordinators: quiet figures carrying folded boards or sealed tablets, continually revising routes and priorities. Their authority lasts only as long as the condition they oversee. During a flood, a junior gate operator may command an entire arcade; once the water falls, that authority passes back to the market cooperative or rail office. The valley respects competence that is immediate and demonstrable. Rank exists, but water has a way of shortening hierarchies.

There are also people who work principally with what is no longer understood. They enter old foundations, trace dead pipes, compare generations of plans, and mark buried structures before new loads are placed above them. They are part archivist, part surveyor, part archaeologist. Their discoveries are rarely displayed. An ancient turbine chamber becomes storage; a chapel crypt becomes a pressure-equalizing tank; a forgotten tram tunnel carries irrigation. Preservation here means continued use, even if the use changes beyond recognition.

Gardeners are nearly as important as engineers, though the distinction between them is blurred. They prune not for ornament alone but for wind load, root pressure, drainage access, pollinator timing, heat exchange, and slope retention. They know mosses by how they hold water against brick, fungi by what they reveal about hidden timber, reeds by the contaminants they tolerate. Plants that outsiders might call weeds are classified as indicators. A fern appearing in the wrong joint may bring a repair crew within the week.

Beekeepers travel with the seasons of the roofs rather than those of the ground. They move compact hives by tram and service lift, fastening them near currant beds, greenhouse vents, and espaliered fruit. Their bees are dark, slow-flying, and selected for cool mist. On the few bright days, whole districts briefly acquire a hum otherwise absent from the city. People come out to watch. The sound is treated almost as weather.

Food is grown close, traded vertically, and prepared to suit damp labor. Kitchens smell of leeks, cabbage, mushrooms, malt, vinegar, preserved currants, river herbs, and dense bread. Warm factory roofs produce grapes sharp enough for thin wine. Cellars cultivate edible fungi on spent wood and compressed straw. Fish from the service basins are too few for ordinary meals and are generally left as signs that the water is healthy. Tea, broth, and hot grain drinks travel everywhere in stoppered metal flasks.

Markets do not occupy places so much as time slots. A landing may sell vegetables at dawn, bearings and wire at midday, and cooked food after the freight shift. Stalls fold flat against walls. Prices fluctuate with access: goods become cheaper on a level about to close and dearer above a rising flood line. Regular customers help move stock when warning lamps change. Theft exists, but abandoning someone’s goods to water is regarded as a more serious offense.

Homes are small, adapted, and inwardly warm. Windows look onto walls, bridges, pipes, or the lit windows of other homes. Mirrors and pale tiles draw what daylight they can into deeper rooms. Plants occupy every sill that will bear them. Laundry hangs over heated conduits. Walls hold flood marks, repair dates, tram notices, school drawings, and diagrams showing which valves belong to whom.

Privacy is achieved less by distance than by practiced inattention. Families hear neighbors through ducts and floor plates, pass them on enclosed bridges, and look across gaps narrow enough to exchange tools by pole. Courtesy consists of not staring, not asking about emergency repairs unless help is needed, and warning adjacent rooms before drilling into shared masonry. Solitude is found in motion: riding an empty tram to an upper terminus, walking a reservoir roof in fog, tending a lock between scheduled barges.

Their bodies bear the valley’s habits. Older people have strong hands and careful knees. Many residents carry pale scars from cable burns, sharp tile, or hurried masonry work. Hearing is prized and protected in industrial trades; workers test one another by identifying relays, pumps, and hidden leaks. A persistent cough is common in the oldest districts, where damp and generations of dust remain trapped in walls despite filtration.

Illness is read environmentally. Clinics track mold blooms, water chemistry, shift fatigue, and pressure changes alongside individual symptoms. Medical stations are distributed vertically so that no closure isolates an entire slope. Stretchers are designed for stairs and narrow lifts. During floods, pharmacies move critical supplies to upper halls before residents are asked to relocate.

Birth and death are accommodated within the same practical continuity. Newborns are entered not only in civil registers but in district evacuation rolls. Neighbors bring food and check the nearest warm route. The dead leave the city with more difficulty. Level ground for burial is scarce, so cremation is common; ashes may be placed in wall niches, mixed into soil for memorial terraces, or released into designated upland channels during low flow. Names are also stamped onto replacement stones, bridge plates, garden rails, and the brass tags of trees. Memory is embedded where hands will touch it.

Celebrations favor light, because light is precious. At midwinter, polished panels and old signal mirrors are turned toward public squares, passing lamplight from roof to roof. Canal gates are opened in sequence so reflected bands travel down the valley. Festivals seldom stop work entirely; instead, decoration adheres to machinery. Tram cables carry lanterns. Spillway boards are painted. Sirens sound chords rather than warnings, each factory contributing one sustained note until the valley itself seems to breathe.

Their religion, where it persists, is concerned less with untouched nature than with continuity through alteration. Shrines appear near confluences, foundation stones, lift heads, and places where water divides. Offerings are modest: a coil of copper wire, a clean bolt, clipped rowan, a cup of clear runoff. Some people bless new construction; others bless the load transferred from the structure it replaces. The holiest places are not necessarily old, but those where many systems meet without failing.

They are wary of outsiders who praise the city’s age while misunderstanding its maintenance. Nothing offends them more than calling a functioning wall picturesque when it is signaling distress. Visitors see moss; residents see a blocked weep hole. Visitors admire the improvised bridges; residents know which are temporary, which have become permanent, and which permanent ones should have been removed years ago.

Yet they are not humorless. Their jokes concern impossible addresses, official maps already obsolete when printed, and the perennial claim that a repair will last forever. A useless object is said to be “on the correct level.” Someone who refuses to adapt is “well founded.” A person who leaves a task almost finished is accused of building for dry weather.

Arguments are intense but usually specific. People quarrel over water allotments, root intrusion, tram priority, borrowed scaffolds, noise transmitted through old ducts, and whose alterations caused a crack. Public meetings involve models, cross-sections, rainfall records, and much pointing upward. Grand ideologies have less force than questions of load, flow, access, and who will inspect the work after five winters.

This practicality does not make them unimaginative. Their art favors layers, reflections, repetitions, and things that change with damp. Mosaics vanish when dry and emerge in rain. Metal sculptures play only when gutters are full. Choirs sing in cisterns for their long echoes. Painters work on shutters, floodboards, removable panels, and the undersides of bridges. Poets describe the same place from incompatible elevations.

Time, to them, is not a line but an accumulation. The past remains underfoot, behind walls, carrying pressure. The future arrives as an added story, a diverted pipe, a new root bed, a room suspended in a gap. They do not expect final solutions. A good work is one that can be reached, understood, repaired, and altered by people not yet born.

So they live without believing themselves to be in perpetual emergency. The red flags, blue lamps, sirens, rerouted stairs, and opened spillways are not intrusions upon ordinary life. They are its grammar. Stability does not mean stillness; safety does not mean permanence. To inhabit the valley is to participate in its balance.

And this is perhaps their defining quality: they trust neither stone nor water, neither machines nor mountains, yet they trust the long practice of attending to them.
