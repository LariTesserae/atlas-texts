They are the seam-dwellers, though no one of them knows the seam as a whole.

Each knows only pressure: warm basalt against the belly, cooler basalt along the back, the hooked feet of neighbors resting in the sockets between its plates. Space is not emptiness but a brief yielding of bodies. To move is to persuade the colony to move with you.

The commonest are the raspers. They hatch as soft, colorless slivers in pores scarcely wider than their heads. Their first shells are glass-clear. Silica drawn from the walls stiffens them; iron salts later stain them amber, rust-red, and finally volcanic black. With combs of mineral teeth they harvest the invisible living film, tasting gradients of sulfur and carbon through sensitive palps. They eat stone only incidentally. What nourishes them is the skin upon it.

Around them live the breathers: broad, flattened animals whose vestigial wings have become corrugated fans. Each braces between opposing walls and vibrates until its whole body blurs. Alone, one produces a dry buzz. In their thousands they make the fracture’s unending roar. They rest in shifts, guided not by light or time but by the thickening of waste gases. When the air grows stale, more wake and begin to beat. When the draft sharpens, they fold themselves flat and feed.

At constrictions cling the gate-backs, squat and heavily plated. Their shells match the contours of particular bottlenecks so closely that adults can seldom leave the places where they matured. By lifting one edge, lowering another, or rotating by the breadth of a crystal needle, they divide the rising gases among neighboring passages. Colonies above depend upon these minute adjustments. A dead gate-back may poison thousands before scavengers hollow its shell and the pressure seats a successor in its place.

The hunters are long enough to cross many generations of raspers. Blind and ribbon-thin, they advance almost imperceptibly, fastening their hind hooks before loosening the front. Their jaws remain open for hours. They do not stalk individuals; they place themselves where hunger, crowding, and the mountain’s slow compression must eventually push flesh between their teeth. After feeding, they may remain motionless for months while their meal is rendered into fat and shell.

Scavengers follow as pale, many-jointed threads. They enter carcasses through breathing pores, separate soft tissue from mineral armor, and pass the useful fractions body to body. Nothing is discarded. Empty plates are gnawed into flakes, swallowed, dissolved, and rebuilt into eggs or hooks. Even waste becomes food for the films glazing the basalt.

Deeper down are the red-hot lineages, small dense creatures whose proteins remain folded near vents that would cook the upper colonies. Sulfur-rich gas passes through chambers in their bodies and leaves chemically altered. Farther up, cooler forms consume what they exhale. No animal understands this exchange, yet entire populations are arranged along it, stacked by heat and chemistry with the precision of organs.

Their young do not travel freely. Eggs are pressed into warm flaws in the rock and cemented there. Hatchlings emerge shaped by the width of their nursery: narrow cracks make thread-bodied crawlers, broader pockets make fan-bearers or thick-shelled grazers. Form is partly inheritance and partly confinement. The mountain participates in every birth.

They sense the world through vibration. A predator’s advance is a slow sequence of displaced taps. A gas pocket announces itself as a tightening whine from below. Crystal growth is a patient ticking that may continue beyond a lifetime. The rare arrival of daylight at the summit is known only as a slight cooling and a faint change registered by buried eyes.

Their greatest events are closures.

As basalt settles, pressure mounts across the colony. Bodies flatten. Fans change pitch. Gate-backs lock their limbs, and hunters are pinned harmlessly beside prey. The compression may last through several broods. Then rock slips against rock, and every inhabitant is thrust upward a few millimeters. Shells split. Eggs rupture. New pores open. A passage that sustained a lineage for centuries vanishes, while elsewhere a fresh warm line appears and is occupied within hours.

Afterward the survivors molt, feed, and resume the ventilation.

At the narrowest seam live the laminates, scarcely thicker than mineral scales. They lie in overlapping ranks, each animal’s limbs tucked beneath the plate of the one above. Their breathing is communal: one rank contracts, then the next, drawing gas through the packed bodies in a traveling pulse. None could sustain the rhythm alone. Separated from the seam, a laminate suffocates even in open air.

They have almost no distinction between shelter, society, and anatomy. The opposing walls shape them; their neighbors move their breath; the colony carries their offspring; the mountain supplies both food and pressure. An individual is less a creature occupying the fracture than a temporary joint within it.

Together they make a hidden vertical fauna of fans, valves, grazers, jaws, and living scales—an ecosystem compressed until it resembles machinery. It has no ruler and manufactures no object. Its only product is continuation: heat passed upward, gas transformed, minerals embodied for a while, and generation after generation lifted through darkness by the almost imperceptible settling of the mountain.
