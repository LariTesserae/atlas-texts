The Bright Sump is inhabited by things that have learned not merely to endure its changing laws, but to anticipate them.

Most numerous are the black swimmers in the deeper channels. They are small, slick creatures, seldom longer than a finger, with fins arranged around the body in a radial fringe. This lets them choose a new “down” without turning. They have no eyes because light in the Sump is too abundant and too unreliable to guide by; instead, pale seams along their flanks detect pressure and the migrating tones of the pillars. During ordinary flow they remain hidden in submerged fissures. When a channel reverses or a waterfall bends upward, they emerge together and hang within it, feeding on mineral films and translucent motes carried from the porous rock.

Their eggs are attached to the undersides of floating water surfaces. In another place this would be meaningless. Here it keeps them secure.

The long-legged crustaceans belong to the exposed margins. Their bodies are broad, white, and almost without thickness, while their jointed limbs spread around them like cracks in glaze. Each foot ends in a soft disk that tastes vibration before committing weight. They travel in widely separated files because the motion of one warns the next: a message relayed through stone rather than air. When danger approaches, they flatten, draw their limbs into natural-looking angles, and drain the faint color from their tissues. What remains resembles a flake of limestone or a scrap of mineral scale.

They graze the dry halos around the copper moss. The halos are not barren. They contain invisible films of salts and dormant spores, concentrated where the moss redirects condensation. During the chamber’s seven seconds of stillness, the crustaceans move with astonishing speed, harvesting what the turbulence normally protects. When the roar returns, they become fragments again.

The moss itself lives by refusal. Its copper-green pads hold thousands of minute gas chambers, which contract when pressure rises and keep abrasive water a hair’s breadth above the living surface. Each colony maintains its dry ring by warming the bordering stone with slow chemical reactions. Moisture evaporates there, condenses farther out, and leaves a narrow refuge from scouring. The pads grow for centuries while scarcely increasing in diameter, thickening inward instead. Some may predate the worn platforms.

The translucent reeds are more closely allied to the machinery. Their hollow stems trap wandering points of pillar-light and pass them from joint to joint. These moving lights are not decoration but stored instruction: each point corresponds to a pressure change, a tonal interval, or a direction water has previously fallen. Before a major rearrangement, the reeds read the approaching pattern through the floor and lie down along the safest line. Broken stems continue performing these movements until their captured light escapes.

The white vine on the western wall is an opportunist of anomalies. Its mist bladders inhale whenever condensation thickens, filtering traces of metal and shed organic matter. Its colorless flowers turn toward impossible shadows because those shadows mark places where light is being delayed, enclosed, or doubled. There, for a few moments, the vine can draw energy from the discrepancy between an object and its image. It blooms rarely but explosively, following paradox as ordinary plants follow the sun.

Even the Sump’s apparently bare stone has inhabitants. Threadlike colonies occupy its pores, extending through warm limestone for kilometers. They feed on mineral gradients created when water is persuaded sideways, upward, or briefly outside sequence. Much of the basin’s smell—the wet-metal closeness beneath the spray—comes from their metabolism. The copper moss, reeds, and vine are only their exposed organs, or perhaps merely species that have entered into ancient partnerships with them.

The chamber’s largest inhabitant is harder to separate from the chamber itself.

It rests below the basin in flooded cavities too narrow, by ordinary measurement, to contain it. Its body is yielding and oval, boneless except for a loose internal lattice of resonant rods. It can distribute its mass among connected pools, pressing a flank beneath one channel while breathing through twenty others. What appears from above as a broad disturbance is not the passage of the whole animal, but a shift in where it has chosen to be solid.

Its skin is clear and densely folded. Water courses through those folds rather than merely around them. As it inhales, unrelated pools bubble because each opens onto a different chamber of the same branching lung. When it rises, it gathers itself onto the three southern platforms, fitting a portion of its body to each worn oval while the rest remains beneath the floor. The abrasions there are beds made by centuries of patient weight.

It feeds during the loudest cycles. The pillar tones give suspended grit momentary heaviness, and the creature draws that weighted material through its folds, extracting microbial films, mineral salts, dead reeds, cast shells, and anything else small enough to become part of the flow. The black swimmers pass through it unharmed. Some shelter in its respiratory canals during violent reversals. The crustaceans avoid the platforms shortly before it surfaces, sensing its inhalation through their feet.

No one knows whether this being was brought to the Sump, evolved within it, or is responsible for the Sump’s present behavior. Its internal rods answer the nine pillars at frequencies below hearing. The migrating ring may be communication, restraint, or mutual regulation. When the floor flexes after the seven-second silence, it is the creature that moves—or the basin moving through it.

There may also still be a keeper.

The shelter’s warm bench is sometimes wet in the shape of a seated body, though no tracks approach it. The cracked vessel is periodically found full of clear water despite lying on its side. Fine residue accumulates on the many-armed tuning instrument as if narrow, damp fingers have adjusted it. None of the known animals could cut handholds, set ceramic markers, or understand the branching diagrams.

Yet whatever tends the place need not remain continuously present. The hoops allow dead matter to precede itself. Light collects inside closed spaces. Distances between pillars alter. A caretaker might visit from an adjacent hour rather than an adjacent chamber.

During certain bright intervals, a figure appears only in reflections: seated beneath the paper-thin roof, tall and spare, its limbs wrapped in pale coverings, its head inclined toward the tuning instrument. Looking directly into the shelter reveals an empty bench. Looking into a pool may show the figure lifting one hand. Several minutes later, the instrument sounds.

Whether this is a survivor, an echo, or a duty continuing after the death of the one who performed it is impossible to tell. The crustaceans cross beneath the reflected feet without alarm. The vine sometimes turns all its flowers toward the shelter. The great thing under the basin quiets its breathing.

Then the answering note is struck.

For seven seconds, every inhabitant becomes perceptible on its own terms. The swimmers tremble in arrested columns. The crustaceans race across the stone. Light pauses in the reeds. The vine’s bladders open. Deep below, a transparent immensity contracts around its resonant bones.

And behind the still water, where the basin seems to possess a second interior, the keeper listens for whether everything remains in tune.
