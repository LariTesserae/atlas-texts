What lives here is not yet an animal, nor anything that could know itself as separate from the water.

It lives as a black-green membrane on the youngest stone.

Each organism is less an individual than a temporary agreement among cells. They join edge to edge in thin branching fans, soft enough to fold inside a pressure wave and broad enough, when conditions permit, to harvest the diffuse daylight. There is no fixed front or back. Whatever surface faces the greater brightness becomes its skyward side; whatever touches mineral becomes root, mouth, and sense organ.

The fans do not merely cling to the shelves. They listen to them.

Through direct contact with the crystal lattice, they detect the minute tremors preceding growth, dissolution, or a change in local law. A shelf announces its intentions long before it moves: conductivity shifts along a dark vein, buoyancy flickers, sound begins repeating at an altered interval. The living films answer by tightening some branches and abandoning others. Before the mineral beneath them loses solidity, they draw themselves into protected seams or release their smallest units into the water.

Those units are not quite spores. A spore would be a durable object crossing a hostile distance. These are patterns wrapped in droplets: tiny knots of membrane, pigment, and catalytic mineral, too fragile to travel alone. They enter pressure waves and persist as rhythms within them. Hundreds of kilometers away, where a shelf rings with the proper crystalline tone, the wave sheds its living cargo. The knots unfold, test the light, and spread.

Thus one growth may occupy places that never touch. A dark fan on a warm ridge and a thread-forest beyond an empty gulf may be parts of the same dispersed life, connected not by continuous flesh but by recurring pressure. Its body is an archipelago of events.

In sheltered hollows it takes another form. Threads rise from the stone in dense, low forests, each filament clear at its base and blackening toward its tip. When currents reverse, the whole stand falls flat in a single direction. It does not struggle upright afterward. New growth simply establishes a new upwardness. If gravity tilts, the forest tilts with it. If buoyancy inverts, its anchoring cells release and its tips fasten instead. It survives by possessing almost no anatomy that cannot exchange its purpose.

Its metabolism is equally provisional. In ordinary regions it lives on light, dissolved carbon, and the energetic surfaces of newborn crystals. Near geothermal faces it darkens and feeds on chemical gradients. Where water becomes frictionless, it stops branching and contracts into smooth films that currents cannot tear. Inside loops of returning sound, it grows in concentric pulses, each echo triggering another ring of cells until the colony resembles a black target painted on pale stone.

The world’s shifting physics has entered its heredity. It carries no single, stable set of instructions. Its cells store several incomplete possibilities in layered chemical circuits, activating one according to pressure, mineral tone, temperature, and the direction in which nearby matter prefers to fall. A lineage born in upward gravity does not die when carried into downward gravity; it rearranges which surfaces bind, which pigments turn toward brightness, which internal fluids count as heavy.

This adaptability does not make it invulnerable. Most of it is erased.

Construction fronts bury entire films inside translucent rock. Dissolution fields unmake the minerals on which they depend and then unmake the cells themselves. Storm boundaries force generations into being and destroy them minutes later. Vast blooms arise across fresh plateaus, blackening thousands of square kilometers, only to vanish when the plateau loses permission to remain solid.

Nothing decays afterward. There are no scavengers, no sediment of bodies, no patient recycling by mouths. Dead membranes separate into colorless molecules and disappear into the clear water. Their only fossils are occasional discolorations sealed deep inside a shelf—and even those last only until the shelf is revised.

Yet the life here has begun to alter the creation field.

Where the films persist, they select certain mineral faces over others. Their anchoring chemistry slows dissolution by fractions of a second. Their pigments absorb light and warm the water immediately around them. Their thread-forests damp tiny currents, allowing suspended grains to settle into dark, delicate layers. Effects too slight to matter locally accumulate across immense shelves. A ridge carrying life grows differently from a bare ridge. Its crystals lock at altered angles. Its conductive veins branch around old colonies. Its pressure-song changes.

The organisms cannot design, intend, or remember as an animal might. Nevertheless, the planet’s forming laws must now form around them.

In rare stable regions, colonies thicken into structures almost substantial enough to seem like creatures. Layered fans curl inward, making hollow rosettes several meters across. Their outer membranes drink light; their inner folds trap chemically rich water. Pressure changes pass through them as slow contractions. A rosette may close before a destructive current arrives, remain sealed for years, then open when the surrounding stone sounds safe again.

From a distance such a rosette might seem to breathe.

It does not. Breath implies a world dependable enough to repeat itself. This life has no such world. Every cycle is conditional. Every structure is an answer to rules that may change before the answer is complete.

If anything greater eventually emerges here, it will not begin with pursuit, hunger, armor, or eyes. It will begin with orientation. It will need to determine what direction light inhabits, where gravity currently points, whether sound will escape or return, whether the ground is ground or merely a brief mineral phase in the water. Its first sense will be the sense that reality has changed.

For now, only the black-green films possess even that much awareness.

They lie along the bright margins of unfinished continents, opening their soft branches to a sun they cannot see. They pass through the planet as pressure-borne fragments. They vanish by the billion and reappear wherever water, stone, and light briefly agree.

They are not masters of this place, nor its witnesses.

They are the first thing the forming world must learn not to erase.
