The chamber is inhabited less by a single species than by a young ecology—one still deciding what counts as an animal, a shelter, or a piece of weather.

Its most constant residents are the little white frogs. They sit separately in temporary basins, each occupying its own coin of water as if solitude were part of their anatomy. Their bodies are translucent enough to show fine bones and slow organs. Whenever a heart contracts, blue light passes through the frog and briefly stains the pool beneath it. They have no audible call. Instead, during rain, their pulses begin to synchronize, until cold blue flickers travel from hollow to hollow like a conversation conducted beneath the skin. When threatened, a frog does not leap away. It becomes shallow, spreading into its reflection, and may remain there after the water itself is gone.

The ceiling belongs to black moths. They cling upside down against the radiance and resemble holes cut into it. Their wings open and close without stirring the air. From below they appear plain, but the upper surfaces—visible only when one falls or turns—carry minute white patterns like unfinished maps of the chamber. They are hunters as well as grazers. Some feed upon luminous condensation; others cast predatory shadows that detach from them and travel over the floor. A frog covered by such a shadow may vanish, while the moth remains motionless overhead. Later, a heavier bead of water forms near its mouth.

Long-legged insects patrol the bare terraces. Their feet make temporary points of softness in the stone, and their bodies are so slight that they seem assembled from joints rather than flesh. They drink from mineral seams, probe newborn ridges, and gather around structures that have appeared during the night. Some carry white grains beneath their bodies and fit them into cracks. Whether they are building the little enclosures or merely completing a process begun by the rock is impossible to tell. If watched directly, they only wander. Their purposeful work is always found afterward.

Pale crickets live beneath the fitted slabs. They have broad, spade-shaped heads and delicate antennae that pass through stone as roots do. A cricket may sit inside one of the tiny houses for days, facing an empty niche. During the chamber’s low hum, it braces its legs against opposing walls and vibrates without making sound. The stone around it becomes more regular: corners sharpen, roofs settle, thresholds rise. For this reason they might be masons. Yet houses also form around sleeping beetles, abandoned pools, and occasionally nothing at all.

The glassy worms of the sediment are neither wholly animal nor wholly rooted. They rise when droplets fall, standing upright in colonies of clear stalks. Their mouths unfold into many-petaled cups, each petal tasting a different condition: moisture, warmth, nearness, likelihood. What they catch is not always matter. They may close around a fish’s approaching shadow or the warmth left where a mouse will later sleep. Fed worms cloud from the bottom upward and sink back into the dark sediment. Their discarded mouth-petals harden into tiny mineral flowers.

The mice inhabit fissures too narrow to contain them. They are round-bodied, warm-furred creatures in shades of cream, rust, and smoke, with black eyes that reflect no light. They graze on flakes loosened from the walls, preferring stone that has not fully settled into hardness. Whole colonies can be heard breathing behind a crack no wider than a hair. They possess an uneasy foreknowledge: before a new entrance opens, before the chamber hums, before a predator moves, they all become still. The warning may come minutes early, though not all of them listen to the same future. One colony freezes while another continues feeding, and afterward only one of those futures remains evident.

The airborne fish keep to the ceiling brightness. They are small, silver, and nearly flat, with translucent fins that pull them through light as ordinary fins pull through water. They school around luminous veins and descend when rain has filled the air with suspended droplets. Their mouths leave circles that widen through nothing. In drought they seek the stone enclosures, arranging themselves side by side or one atop another, rigid but alive. They are hunted by broad ray-shaped shadows. No body has ever been seen attached to these predators. The rays emerge from beneath pebbles, cross the ceiling in a single dark sweep, and disappear into rooflines too small to admit them.

Tiny crustaceans arrive only with certain rains. They hatch from hanging droplets already swimming, complete with glass-clear shells, paddling limbs, and red pinprick eyes. Their lives may last no longer than the dampness in the air. Before the chamber dries, they gather around a condensation bead and enter it one by one. In the droplet’s curved view they can sometimes be seen continuing across an inverted lake beneath an unfamiliar sky.

A fox visits, though it may also belong here.

It is narrow enough to pass between distances rather than objects, with dark red fur and a white underside that takes on the texture of whatever lies behind it. It leaves no scent. Its tracks appear as dimples in the yielding floor, sometimes before the paw descends, sometimes minutes after it has gone. The fox hunts rarely. It seems more interested in the changing boundaries, nosing along walls, testing roots and temporary cracks. When it passes behind a projection and fails to reappear, the mice listen; the moths close their wings; one of the little stone houses may acquire a new doorway. Perhaps the fox knows the routes by which the chamber sheds its inhabitants into other places. Perhaps it is not one fox, but every fox that nearly found the opening.

Beneath and among these animals lives the silver-green moss, the chamber’s quiet disposer. It grows in isolated mats, never bridging the clean stone between colonies. Each patch folds over whatever dies nearby and withdraws again before decay begins. Nothing remains but a slight brightening of the rock and, sometimes, an extra possibility of the lost creature elsewhere: a second frog pulsing in another pool, a mouse glimpsed twice in one fissure, a moth whose shadow opens its wings before the moth does. The moss may be feeding the chamber not with bodies, but with unused histories.

The translucent grasses are slower and stranger. Their visible blades are incidental; most of each plant consists of roots sliding sideways through soft stone. These roots seek not water but cracks that have yet to form. Where a root pauses, a fissure may open days later. Where many roots converge, an entrance sometimes appears. Thus the grasses seem to anticipate the chamber’s connection to the surface—or create it. Their blades lean toward weather that has not arrived, and small animals watch them for signs of coming rain.

There may also be a larger resident, though it has no distinct body.

It expresses itself through fitted stones, through the chamber’s insistence on remaining small, through roofs made wherever creatures seek cover. It may be the low hum felt in the teeth. It may be the intelligence by which limestone learns enclosure: a burrowing instinct belonging to the rock itself. The little houses could be its scales, nests, thoughts, or organs. When one is dismantled, the scattered blocks often form a curve suggestive of a jaw or a sleeping spine. When another appears around a beetle, it fits not the beetle’s present shape but the shape into which it will molt.

If this being lives, it lives at the pace of formation rather than erosion. Its breathing is the fractional swelling of walls. Its appetite is for distinctions: inside and outside, roof and sky, animal and mineral, now and not yet. It tries each distinction briefly, then softens it again. The luminous ceiling may be its upper surface turned inward. The chamber may not be its dwelling at all, but its unfinished body.

Everything else survives by accommodating that growth. Frogs entrust themselves to vanishing pools. Fish shelter in stone. Mice obey futures that may be canceled. Moths hunt with shadows rather than mouths. Crickets brace the corners of temporary houses. None behaves as though the chamber were unstable, because instability is the oldest condition they know.

They are not refugees from a completed world. They are the native life of beginnings: creatures adapted to first drafts, nourished by possibilities, at home in a place where stone has not hardened into certainty and a room has not yet decided what it can contain.
