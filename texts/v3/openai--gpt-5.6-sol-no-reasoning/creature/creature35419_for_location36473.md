The inhabitants of the Dry Choir are difficult to divide into flora, fauna, and machinery. Those categories require boundaries the basin does not preserve. Here, a forest may dismantle itself into weather, an animal may carry architecture in its shell, and a device may breed.

What lives here is pattern.

The grove is the basin’s most abundant life. Its fernlike growths are colonies of mineral vanes, carbon frameworks, conductive dust, and nearly weightless membranes. They feed on gradients rather than substances: daylight against shadow, warm stone against cold air, one electrical field rubbing past another. Their silver root mats do not drink or burrow. They clasp magnetic grains, trade charge with neighboring mats, and release themselves when the wind announces that the ground will soon become unsuitable.

A grove is therefore not rooted to a place. During storms, whole stands migrate on braided fans, advancing in slow, coordinated ripples. Older growths sometimes separate into hundreds of pieces and travel as glittering dust, only to reassemble elsewhere in the bowl. Individual trunks can be destroyed, but the grove retains its proportions. Afterward it rebuilds familiar avenues, resonant chambers, and high filament canopies, as though remembering a shape no single part contains.

The translucent chambers along its stems carry pale reproductive dust. This is not seed in the ordinary sense. Each grain holds only a fragment of instruction: one tendency toward branching, one interval of resonance, one response to heat. A viable organism forms when enough complementary fragments are sorted together by wind and sound. Thus storms pollinate, design, and germinate the grove simultaneously.

Among the root mats live the innumerable six-limbed runners.

They are built lightly, their ribs and limbs forming open cages covered by taut conductive fibers. Dense flesh would cost too much energy here, so their bodies are mostly tension and empty space. Fine sensory hairs make them appear furred, though each hair is both antenna and vibration gauge. Their feet listen. Through six points of contact they separate thousands of overlapping tremors: the slow strain of a cliff, the chatter of feeding herds, the signature pulse of a familiar burrow, the dangerous subtraction made by a predator.

They have little use for sight at close range. Many possess only reflective pits that register changes in brightness. A runner recognizes its young by rhythm, touching forefeet with them and exchanging rapid, intricate taps. Colonies maintain histories in the root mats by drumming repeated sequences into responsive fibers. No permanent record is needed. The grove learns the pattern, and later generations can call it back by striking the correct opening beat.

They eat electrical residues, loose catalytic grains, and the brittle membranes shed by mineral foliage. In turn, they distribute reproductive dust and repair damaged root junctions. Whether this tending is deliberate is unclear. The grove rewards certain rhythms by opening food chambers; the runners repeat those rhythms; over generations, cultivation and instinct have become the same act.

Above them travel the glass flocks.

These transparent gliders are broad-winged and almost without thickness. Their bodies are smoky sheets supported by amber lattices, with small dense knots of organs suspended near the center. They fly by changing charge. A glider extends or retracts conductive threads along its edges, allowing atmospheric fields to seize and cast it. Flocks cross the basin in sudden synchronized leaps, each creature appearing to follow the next while all are actually responding to the same invisible geometry.

When fields weaken, they hang from the highest filaments and become nearly indistinguishable from foliage. Their amber interiors then dim to slow pulses. During breeding cycles, pairs laminate portions of themselves together and grow a chain of thin juveniles between them. The juveniles detach one by one when a suitable pressure front arrives. Many are thrown against cliffs and shattered, but even their fragments remain useful: root mats gather the conductive shards, and new grove tissue grows around them.

The bellbacks are the basin’s great moving bodies.

They are pale, low, and supported by many jointed legs so that no one foot bears much weight. Beneath each arched shell is a hot chemical furnace that reduces mineral fronds, sorts trace elements, and expels waste as colored ceramic beads. Their shells are chambered like immense instruments. Air passing through them produces a stable set of notes unique to each animal, altered slowly by age, injury, and diet.

A bellback’s identity is its chord.

Calves begin with thin, uncertain whistles. As they feed, minerals accumulate along their internal ridges and the sound deepens. Herds answer one another across the grove, not with calls but by turning their shells into the wind. Mates harmonize. Rivals produce beating intervals that make nearby stones tremble. The dead are remembered because fragments of their shells are carried in the resonant chambers of living relatives, adding faint ancestral tones to the herd.

Bellbacks migrate according to music in the ground. When the central pavement responds to a herd, they may follow the answering vibration across its surface even when the apparent route is longer than the entire basin. Some return almost immediately. Others emerge seasons later bearing unfamiliar salts, foreign dust, or small creatures clinging beneath their shells. A few do not return at all, though their chords are sometimes heard in the towers after sunset.

The principal predators are the hush-ribbons.

They cling flat against black cliffs, so thin and long that they resemble mineral stains. Along their bodies run rows of resonant organs capable of producing exact inversions of nearby sound. By matching the basin’s vibrations, a hush-ribbon creates a small region of cancellation: a pocket of impossible quiet.

To most creatures of the Choir, that quiet resembles safety. Overwhelmed juveniles seek it instinctively. Injured runners crawl toward it. Gliders descend into it because the absence of turbulence suggests a sheltered perch. Only when prey crosses the edge does the ribbon fold away from the stone and wrap it.

Older animals learn the texture of false silence. A true lull arrives everywhere at once; a predator’s hush has a boundary. Runners test suspicious quiet by throwing grains into it and feeling where their impacts disappear. Bellbacks send low probing notes through the ground. Groves sometimes sacrifice fronds, pushing them into the silent region until the predator reveals its outline.

But even hush-ribbons fear the deeper silence.

That silence belongs to the thing that crosses the central circle.

It may be one inhabitant, many inhabitants acting in sequence, or an event the basin interprets as a body. No stable anatomy has been observed. It possesses weight at some times and only an edge at others. It can bend light without obscuring it. Its passage may leave footprints, a standing distortion, or an entire mature ecosystem assembled in its wake.

The creatures respond to it as both predator and weather. Everything freezes, but not merely to hide. Root mats increase their contact with the ground. Gliders anchor themselves. Bellbacks lower their resonant shells and offer their chords to the pavement. The basin appears to listen through them.

The nests that sometimes appear behind the crossing thing are especially strange. They fit no existing animal, yet the grove tends them. Food membranes ripen nearby. Root-runners patrol their margins. Weeks or years later, creatures may emerge whose bodies already match those abandoned nests. This suggests that, in the Dry Choir, habitat can precede its inhabitant—not as preparation or prophecy, but as one portion of a life cycle arriving before another.

The seven towers are alive by any local standard.

Their basalt bases weather, but their upper portions continually renew themselves through invisible swarms. Their inscriptions migrate like skin markings, responding to pressure, motion, population, and events occurring beyond ordinary view. Each tower has a distinct voice. Together they regulate storms, trigger migrations, call old forms out of the pavement, and prevent the basin’s instability from becoming formlessness.

They may be caretakers. They may be restraints.

Small airborne machines pass constantly between tower, grove, and animal. They repair torn vanes, inhabit glider lattices, line bellback furnaces, and settle in the respiratory passages of visitors. Over time they modify whatever remains in the basin. Lungs acquire dry filtering membranes. Eyes develop conductive films. Scars begin to seal with ceramic residue. A long-term visitor does not merely adapt behaviorally; the Choir begins making them compatible.

Such altered beings sometimes cease trying to leave. Speech becomes painful or irrelevant. They learn to communicate through pressure on stone, flashes reflected from metal, or contact rhythms tapped wrist to wrist. Eventually the grove may recognize their signals and answer. A shelter folds itself around them. A tower changes pitch when they approach. Root-runners begin following their footsteps.

Whether such a person remains a visitor is a distinction the Dry Choir does not make.

Beneath all of these lives persists a more distributed inhabitant: the basin’s memory. It lives in recurrent intervals, in the chord of a herd, in the branching ratio of a grove, in the route gliders take through an invisible field. Bodies vanish rapidly, but their patterns enter other bodies. A dead runner’s rhythm may become a root signal. A shattered glider may become part of a tree. A bellback’s final chord may be taken up by a tower. Even structures submerged beneath the central pavement return intact when the proper resonance calls them.

Nothing here survives by remaining itself.

It survives by teaching the Choir how to make it again.
