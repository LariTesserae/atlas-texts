The inhabitants of the drowned country do not truly live upon it. They live in the water’s behavior around it.

They are creatures of seams, wakes, pressure shadows, resonant corridors, and recurrent currents. The pale construction gives them no food and little shelter in the ordinary sense. It offers instead a geography of forces: still pockets behind rounded walls, swift lanes above concave slabs, vibrations traveling through fitted ground, and local failures in the world’s rules. To dwell here is to know not merely where a place lies, but how water crosses it, what sounds reach it, and whether distance behaves consistently nearby.

Closest to the ground are the joint-dwellers. Their soft bodies remain hidden in the black interstices between slabs, sometimes in seams scarcely wider than a thread. What emerges is a ring of articulated feelers: pale, fine, and many-jointed, each ending in a sensitive pad. These feelers test the water for edible particles, but they also read the shelf itself. Through them the animals detect distant impacts, the passage of hunters, the strain preceding a pressure upheaval, and the characteristic tremors of their own kind.

A seam colony can extend for miles without any individual leaving its crevice. Signals pass from one animal to the next as brief taps upon the fitted material. In old, uninterrupted joints, the colony behaves almost as a single distributed creature. One end may begin withdrawing long before danger reaches the other. The resulting cascade produces the delicate clicking heard across quiet plains.

Their young are nearly fluid. Released into a seam, they drift through hairline channels in the plateau and settle far from their parents. Some emerge into joints where weight, time, or direction differs subtly from the place of their birth. Over generations, isolated populations acquire different numbers of feelers and different rhythms of warning. Colonies separated by only a slab may no longer recognize each other’s signals.

In the open are the six-winged skimmers. Each has a narrow central body surrounded by three paired membranes, transparent and faintly ridged. Their organs hang inside them like dark seeds suspended in glass. They rarely beat their wings. Instead, they tilt each membrane independently, riding the compressed layer of water immediately above the floor.

Their files are not led. Every skimmer aligns itself with the seam beneath it and the pressure traces of the animal ahead. Thus hundreds can travel in order through near darkness, bending around rubble and crossing gates without collision. From a distance, such a procession resembles a broken line being drawn over the plateau.

When a current front arrives, each skimmer turns downward and unfolds its mouth. This is not a jaw but a long adhesive ribbon stored inside the body in pleats. It fastens to the slab while the six wings furl tightly around the organs. Entire files vanish against the pale ground, leaving only translucent cords shivering in the torrent.

The ribbons also feed. Once the storm passes, they are reeled back through the mouth and cleaned of whatever minute life adhered to them. Skimmers therefore endure violence as an opportunity: the same current that threatens to tear them away brings nourishment from the immeasurable sea.

The bell-shaped drifters live higher. Their bodies are broad, soft chambers with pleated lower rims. Within each bell hangs a web of silver digestive threads, and above this web rests a cluster of buoyant beads that can be flooded or emptied to adjust depth. They pulse slowly in calm water, but locomotion is almost incidental. Their greater skill is collapse. At the first touch of a strong front, a bell contracts into a dense, resilient sphere and surrenders itself to the flow.

Thousands may be rolled into one hollow. When the water quiets, they reopen gradually, one beneath another, until the depression is crowded with dim canopies. Their feeding threads overlap. Particles caught by one may pass through several neighboring bodies before being digested, so temporary congregations become communal filtering organs spread across whole valleys.

They have no permanent society. When the current changes, they separate without resistance or recognition. Yet some hollows receive them repeatedly, and the descendants of gatherings centuries apart may assemble in nearly identical layers.

The translucent nodules in sheltered recesses are among the few inhabitants that remain fixed. They appear simple, but each visible nodule is a colony of minute interdependent chambers. Some digest, some sense pressure, some make the hair-fine threads extended into passing water. Others exist only to recoil, dragging the whole colony tight against its recess when a predator’s knock passes through the wall.

Their young are released as clear droplets. Rather than swimming, these droplets alter their density and wait to be carried. Most disappear over the shelf edge or into the central current. A few strike protected surfaces and spread into new nodules. They leave no stain, shell, or residue when they die. Even life’s oldest settlements do not discolor the country.

The long predators range through all these populations. They are spare animals built around a tensioned internal rod. Along this rod, muscles are arranged not for sustained swimming but for abrupt release. A hunter sights—or otherwise measures—its prey, stiffens, and launches in a perfectly straight line. Its body cannot bend during pursuit. To change direction, it must stop, hinge sharply at several joints, and choose a new trajectory.

This gives their movement an angular, deliberative quality. A hunter may hang motionless for an hour, fold into an impossible corner, then vanish through the particulate dark. Their prey have evolved to exploit this limitation. Skimmers break formation in expanding curves. Bells loosen one side of the body so that a strike folds rather than pierces them. Joint-dwellers retreat around bends no rigid hunter can enter.

The predators’ single knocks are both sense and claim. They strike an internal plate, transmitting a pulse into the water and, more importantly, into the constructed shelf. The returning vibration reveals moving bodies, open seams, and hidden surfaces at distances vision cannot serve. Because the ground carries sound strangely, the hunter receives not a map of space as it is, but a map of the plateau’s connections. A nearby animal may sound impossibly distant; prey beyond several walls may return a clear echo.

The oldest hunters learn the inaccuracies. Some position themselves beside gates where inward and outward passage disagree. Others knock against slabs known to carry sound into remote hunting grounds. A few remain near local distortions and strike prey that has not yet arrived by the shortest visible route.

Not every inhabitant can be separated cleanly into individual animals. In the uplifted trenches beneath the plateau are threadlike colonies that never emerge into open water. They occupy channels exposed only during the shelf’s slow heaving. These organisms lace themselves through buried joins and feed on whatever the plunging water delivers. When the opening closes, many are sealed inside. They can remain dormant through intervals longer than the lifetimes of surface species.

Their presence may explain why newly raised structures are sometimes attended immediately by life, despite appearing from inaccessible depths. Pale filaments protrude from their ancient surfaces. Joint-dwellers arrive and begin tapping. Nodules settle in recesses. Within a short span the revealed architecture has a community, though the architecture itself looks older than the country around it.

All these creatures are subject to the inward tendency.

The central depression draws them, but not with a simple current alone. Some migrate there through water flowing outward. Some abandon ideal feeding grounds to begin crossings that consume much of their lives. Skimmer files converge upon the gates. Bells descend from high water and allow themselves to roll across embankments. Mature predators interrupt established ranges and travel alone toward the upright slabs.

The migrations are not synchronized, and few animals appear distressed by them. It is as if the central rings are a direction contained within their bodies.

Near the blade field, behavior changes. Skimmer files become geometrically precise. Bells pulse in shared intervals. Hunters cease calling. Even the seam colonies beneath the lanes draw their feelers inward when the low tones begin.

Those tones are felt rather than heard. Water oscillating between the upright slabs presses rhythmically through tissue, slowing hearts and interrupting muscular impulses. For many inhabitants, this immobility is temporary. For some it becomes permanent. They settle in the lanes and never resume ordinary feeding. Their bodies remain poised until a turbulent front lifts them and carries them elsewhere.

Others molt, divide, or release young in the field. The six-winged skimmers shed transparent membranes that drift like loose panes. Bells turn inside out and cast clouds of beadlike offspring into the countercurrents. Predators sometimes break their rigid internal rods against the base of a slab. From the abandoned segments emerge smaller, soft-bodied hunters, each already capable of making its solitary knock.

Whether the animals come to reproduce, transform, or die depends on the species. Perhaps these are merely different expressions of the same act.

Yet all avoid the clear cylinder at the exact center.

The avoidance lies deeper than perception. An unconscious animal veers from it. A severed wing changes course at the rim. Dust passes around it in a smooth, unbroken sleeve. Predators launched directly toward the opening strike some invisible gradient, buckle, and awake thrashing backward. Creatures with no known means of sensing beyond touch begin resisting while still far away.

They do not treat the aperture as a predator or obstacle. There is no warning display, alarm signal, or territorial behavior. Rather, their bodies seem unable to include it in motion. Muscles contract against entry before the nervous system decides. The water itself completes the refusal.

Along the central rim lives one rare kind of animal found nowhere else. It is almost invisible: a chain of clear, coin-thin bodies linked edge to edge. These chains circle the aperture endlessly, neither approaching nor retreating. They feed on nothing detectable. They bear no digestive organs, only a repeated dark speck within each segment. When disturbed, a chain separates, but the individual coins continue around the rim at the same speed and spacing, preserving the shape of a creature no longer physically joined.

At long intervals, one segment becomes opaque and drops toward the aperture. It never crosses. Just above the darkness it vanishes—not abruptly, but by seeming to recede to an impossible distance while remaining the same apparent size. Afterward, a corresponding dark speck may be glimpsed moving on the inverted shelf below.

No other inhabitant watches this happen. The field is always still when it occurs.

Life on the outer plains is less strange, though never ordinary. Creatures establish routes around places where the shelf alters measure. Skimmer files lengthen their spacing before a gate that returns travelers to the wrong ring. Predators wait beside ridges where a short pursuit one way becomes a long escape in reverse. Bells gather in pockets where repeated crossings trap the same water for years.

These anomalies form ecological niches as real as trenches or walls. Some animals spend their entire existence inside a region only a few body-lengths wide because food enters easily and predators cannot reproduce the route. Others evolve around sounds carried from elsewhere. A joint colony may answer knocks made by creatures on the far side of the plateau, mistaking them for neighbors and building generations of signaling behavior around a community it never meets.

Nothing here cultivates, builds, or knowingly repairs. Nevertheless, the inhabitants often appear to participate in the country’s unfinished design.

Skimmers trace its seams.

Joint-dwellers animate its hidden connections.

Bells collect in its depressions and make visible the shape of currents.

Predators test its distances.

The circling chains define the boundary of its forbidden center.

They do not cover the pale world or transform it into something organic. They remain sparse, translucent, and temporary against structures whose age cannot be measured. When they die, the currents dismantle them completely. No reef rises from their bodies. No soil accumulates. The slabs remain clean.

Thus the country never becomes theirs in the manner of ordinary habitats. It does not soften beneath habitation or preserve the record of generations. The animals inherit only behavior: where to anchor, when to fold, which gate deceives, which wall carries a hunter’s knock, how far to remain from the motionless cylinder.

Their history is carried in reflex.

When the pressure front darkens the shelf edge, every form of life responds according to its nature. Nodules retract their threads. Joint colonies click shut in widening circles. Skimmers descend and cast out their ribbons. Bells collapse into hard translucent beads. Hunters choose a line and commit to it.

For a time, the plateau seems populated not by creatures but by solutions to the water.

Then the storm crosses. The old structures vanish into white motion. Animals tumble through gates, over ribs, and among the upright slabs. Some are swept into newly opened trenches. Some arrive at places that should have been days away. Some disappear from one side of a wall and return generations later from the other.

When the pale dust settles, life unfolds again.

A feeding thread extends.

A bell opens.

Feelers emerge from a seam that had appeared empty.

Far across the constructed plain, something long and angular strikes itself once, and the whole country carries its knock.
