The people of the summit call themselves the **Moss-Between**, though the name does not mean that they live among moss. It means they inhabit the narrow allowance between one living thing and another: between root and stone, thirst and cloud, the mountain’s movement and the attention beneath it.

They are small, broad-footed people, seldom standing much taller than the giant ferns’ lowest fronds. Their toes spread widely and are cushioned by fibrous pads that thicken throughout life. Even an adult crossing bare rock makes little more sound than water gathering on a leaf. Their knees and hips bend deeply, giving them a careful, yielding walk. They do not resist unstable ground; they give way with it.

Their skin ranges from ash-gray to the warm black of wet basalt. Across it grow fine sensory hairs, most densely on the soles, palms, jaw, and ribs. These hairs detect tremors through floors and walls. A sleeping person may wake because a fissure has opened three courtyards away, while remaining undisturbed by someone passing beside their bed.

Their eyes are large, dark, and protected by transparent inner lids against fog, spores, and the tiny animals that descend from the cloud-beasts. Direct sunlight pains them. On the rare clear morning, people pull woven veils over their faces and move beneath awnings, while children dare one another to look down at the dazzling lower world.

The most expressive part of the face is not the mouth but the throat. Beneath the jaw lies a field of color-changing skin that flushes in rings, bars, and mottled blooms. Conversation combines close whispers, hand signs, and these changes of color. Meaning passes through several channels at once. A green pulse can soften a refusal; a white band warns that stone is carrying vibration; a darkened throat means that the speaker’s words are ceremonial and must not be interrupted.

They have spoken names, but these are used chiefly during rain. In quiet weather, each person has a gesture-name and a bead-name. The gesture-name can be seen across a courtyard. The bead-name can be read by touch in darkness or fog. The dead lose their spoken names first, then their gestures. Their bead patterns remain tied to whatever their bones have become.

No household consists only of people.

A family may include roof crabs descended through twenty generations, a line of thumb-deer permitted to sleep beneath a particular shelf, cultivated glow-snails, and a colony of insects housed in the warm wall behind the fermentation jars. These are not pets. Some are tenants, some co-workers, some nuisances whose presence must nevertheless be negotiated. Children learn the boundaries of these relationships as carefully as they learn kinship.

A child who steals eggs from a wall lizard has not merely harmed an animal; they have offended the creature that keeps biting flies from the sleeping room. A deer that eats seedlings may be driven away, but not from the district entirely, because its dung carries fungi needed by the tubers. Roof crabs may be culled, but one breeding hollow must always remain untouched.

Builders are therefore part mason, part gardener, and part host. They know which stone holds warmth for lizards, which bark draws crabs, and what shape of crevice persuades moss to anchor without splitting a wall. After a new house is assembled, its occupants wait elsewhere. Food is left under the eaves. Water is placed in shallow cups. If small lives begin to settle there, the builders read the sequence as a judgment.

A snail is favorable. Moss is patient approval. Roof crabs indicate a house fit for children. A thumb-deer sleeping beneath the eaves is an exceptional blessing, because these animals are believed to sense deep hunters before people can.

If nothing enters, no one sleeps inside.

The most respected people are the **listeners**, who spend hours with cheek or sternum pressed to stone. They distinguish ordinary life—the drag of roots, water threading downward, the rasp of crystal growth—from the slow, wet pressure of the fissure hunters. Listeners mark unsafe cracks with pale fungus and reroute traffic around them. Their authority lasts only as long as their accuracy. A listener who mistakes the mountain too often becomes a gardener or keeper of nets.

They insist that the buried predators are not evil. The creatures are simply among the mountain’s oldest inhabitants, perhaps older than open air. Some scholars claim they digest stone and create the fissures through which the town gathers water. Others believe the creatures are sensory organs of the mountain itself. No one tests either belief eagerly.

The hunters have many local names, but no general one. To name them as a single kind would imply that an entire kind might be summoned. People say instead: **the limb beneath the western stair**, **the pale pressure under the grain terraces**, **the one that noticed the funeral of Red Moss**.

The town’s stone surgeons carry the meteoric blades. Their title comes from their work on both bodies and buildings. They cut splinters from flesh, diseased growth from hollow trees, black crystal from foundations, and trapped animals from contracting cracks. They are expected to know when repair is merciful and when a thing must be dismantled.

Because iron cannot easily be replaced, a surgeon inherits not merely a blade but the obligations attached to it. Each stroke is recorded as a notch in the handle or a bead in the blade’s cord. Some knives carry generations of debt.

Children spend their earliest years tethered loosely to adults by braided waist cords. This is less to prevent wandering than to teach shared balance. They learn to sit before they learn to stand, to catch falling objects against the body, and to become limp if startled. Games reward stillness and foresight. In one, children arrange beetles on a slab using warmth from their fingertips. In another, they cross a courtyard carrying bowls filled to the rim, while older children make silent faces intended to provoke laughter.

They do laugh, but usually into one another’s shoulders.

Only during loud hours are children permitted to shout. The first time a child raises their full voice beneath hard rain is treated as a small coming-of-age. Many are frightened by the force of themselves. Some cry. Some spend the whole storm yelling their own rain-names into the water until they are hoarse.

Marriage is less a union of possessions than of routes. The pair and their kin negotiate access to stairs, bridges, safe beds of stone, water nets, gardens, and animal corridors. Wedding garments are covered in tactile maps of the paths being joined. During the rain ceremony, the couple walks these ways while relatives alter them behind and ahead—moving a stepping stone, opening a root bridge, closing an obsolete passage—so that neither person enters the marriage by exactly the route they knew before.

Disputes are stored for rain.

In dry weeks, an offended person adds a marked pebble to the district’s grievance jar. When runoff begins, the jar is opened at the council stone. Under the concealing rush of water, accusations may be spoken at full volume. This is considered useful because anger, if forced into whispers for too long, is believed to turn inward and “teach the bones to crack.”

Judgments usually concern restitution of passage. Someone may lose use of a dry stair, be required to maintain a dangerous bridge, or surrender a favored route to another household. Exile is rare. There are few places to go, and a person cast beyond the walls may disturb migrations on which everyone depends.

Their funerals also wait for rain.

The dead are washed with collected runoff, then placed in one of the warm groves where small creatures clean the flesh. Relatives sit nearby, communicating by throat color and touch. When the bones are ready, they are divided according to aptitude rather than strict kinship. The finger bones of a skilled net-maker may become toggles on condensation frames. A traveler’s ribs may reinforce a carrying pack. A patient councilor’s vertebrae may be set beneath a meeting stone.

Skulls are seldom kept whole. To preserve the face of the dead is thought to trap them in a single expression. Instead, the skull is cut into thin resonant pieces and built into chest instruments. At festivals, descendants hold these instruments against the sternum and feel their ancestors’ music without releasing it into the air.

Their religion has no clear separation between reverence and practical caution.

The mountain is not a god, because gods are said to desire recognition. The mountain does not care whether it is recognized. It thinks by pressure, warmth, fracture, and ascent. The people regard themselves as brief thoughts occurring near its surface.

The cloud-beasts are treated differently. They move, choose, age, carry offspring and parasites, and sometimes alter their routes. Offerings left on the northern shelf are invitations to remember the peak. House models ask the beasts to recognize the town as a place where rain should fall. Shed skins represent renewal of route. Broken blades confess that every tool eventually fails. Pond water is the most costly gift: a portion of what remains when the clouds forget them.

Scholars watch the cloud-beasts from shelters woven into the ridge. They identify individuals by scars, tendril patterns, and the small forests growing on their backs. Their records are living strings of moss and fungus, each species encoding season, direction, health, and rainfall. Such records must be tended continuously. Knowledge that is not cared for literally dies.

Recently those records have become troubling.

Old beasts arrive thin, their buoyancy sacs puckered and dark. Young ones circle empty summits, shedding unfamiliar pale worms. Some carry vegetation from warmer elevations. Others pass without releasing water, as though the mountain no longer lies on the route they remember.

The townspeople respond as they respond to all instability: not by trying to make permanence, but by multiplying possibilities.

They build more nets on the eastern ridge. They exchange bead-maps with neighboring summits. They cultivate plants that drink directly from fog and breed roof crabs that can survive longer dry periods. Gardeners test soil from fallen parasites. Listeners search for deeper moisture in the newly opened cracks. Children are taught routes their parents never needed.

Meanwhile, countless other lives continue around and through them.

The hollow trees contain layered worlds: warm crown pools where glass-winged insects lay eggs; chambers of damp pith grazed by blind worms; sleeping hollows for upside-down birds; root mouths inhabited by tiny crabs that feed on whatever the wind delivers. Some trees are older than the town despite the mountain’s youth. Their seeds may have fallen from cloud-beasts, arriving already burdened with miniature ecosystems.

The six-legged pack animals are heavy-bodied browsers called **mirebacks**. Four legs bear their weight; the smaller forward pair test surfaces before each step. Their feet inflate slightly on contact, spreading pressure over stone. They hum through cavities in their chests, a sound felt more readily through a tether than heard. A frightened mireback does not bolt. It folds its legs and adheres to the ground, becoming almost impossible to move until calm.

The thumb-deer are not deer at all, though their branching feelers resemble antlers. They move in family processions, each animal placing its feet where the one before stepped. Gardeners tolerate them because they consume invasive sprouts while avoiding mature crops. They also stare fixedly at fissures several breaths before a limb emerges. No one knows what they perceive.

Roof crabs are collectors of residue: ash, shed skin, spores, crumbs, dead insects. They arrange what they gather into low circular walls within the thatch. A healthy roof whispers faintly with their labor. Sudden cessation is a warning. Families wake one another if the crabs become still.

The hanging birds beneath the ledges have almost no voice. Their throats display blue, amber, and white, with entire colonies passing waves of color from one end of a cliff to the other. People have learned some of their signals: approaching cloud-beast, winged hunter, falling parasite, clear sky. The birds also produce patterns no human understands, including slow violet ripples during certain moonless nights when something vast seems to pass above the cloud.

And beneath all of them, inside the young stone, the soft hunters continue their blind attention.

The townspeople sometimes find their translucent cilia caught on sharp crystals after a fissure closes. These threads are dried, braided, and used in the most sensitive warning lines. A single strand stretched beneath a floor can reveal footsteps two rooms away. Thus even the predators become part of the town’s survival, though no one takes such material directly from a living limb.

Nothing here is wholly enemy, wholly tool, or wholly sacred. Every creature is dangerous in one arrangement and necessary in another.

That is the deepest habit of those who live in the summit bowl: they do not ask what a thing is by itself. They ask what passes through it, what feeds beside it, what follows when it moves, and what silence it requires.

They live lightly not because they are gentle, but because weight has consequences.

They live quietly not because they lack songs, but because the stone is listening.

And when the rains finally come, the whole small city raises its stored voice at once—people shouting, tools striking, animals flashing, buildings trembling with bone music—while water races through the cracks and, for a few loud hours, the mountain cannot distinguish any single life from the storm.
