What lives there is not a collection of creatures so much as a depth of life.

The visible animals—the soft-backed grazers, the belated-footed waders, the white things that swim through mud and air—are brief surface symptoms. They belong to the plain in the way bubbles belong to fermenting matter. They emerge when pressure permits, perform some obscure exchange, and are taken back before weather can give them permanent shapes. Even the largest beings, known only when marshes tilt and miles of water spill upward, may not be animals in any ordinary sense. They may be organs turning in sleep.

The true inhabitant lies everywhere beneath them.

It has no single outline. Its body is the terminal layer of the world: membrane laminated with mud, shell, ash, salt, fossil, and stone. The broad hides stretched over the flats are portions of it. The films feeding on minerals are its exposed tissues. Rootlets are cilia; reed rings are temporary mouths; black fans are gills opening where the crust grows thin. What seems like competition among countless organisms may instead be circulation within one ancient, distributed life.

It does not grow upward because height is an injury here. It grows by spreading, burying, and becoming indistinguishable from what buries it. It incorporates its dead without separation. Every egg case, spore, crawler track, and flattened ecosystem remains inside it, not merely preserved but still participating at some slower rate. Extinction is only descent into a deeper layer of its metabolism.

Its thoughts, if they are thoughts, occur as pressure.

The ache beneath the teeth is their faintest register. When they strengthen, pools arrange themselves into geometries and animals align along invisible lines, as filings around a magnet. Storms may be impulses moving through its nerves. The downward winds are contractions. Upward rain is exhalation through buried pores. The great convulsions—when ridges flex, stone rolls, and temporary hills walk—are perhaps attempts to turn over, or to remember a body plan it possessed before it became landscape.

The low stone structures live there too, though not independently.

They are the inhabitant’s listening parts.

Their fitted walls exclude ordinary water while drawing up deeper water from within. In them the country’s overwhelming noise separates into drips, scratches, and pulses, as though the plain had built crude inner ears to distinguish its own processes. Their migrations are not travel. They surface wherever attention gathers. An arch found a hundred miles from its former place may be the same organ expressed through another fold of the body. When two overlap and moan, the land is listening to two ages at once. When only a room-shaped volume of dry air remains, listening continues after the organ has withdrawn.

Nothing built them because building implies an outside hand. They condensed from the plain’s need to hear itself.

Beneath even this vast inhabitant, however, there may be another.

The long curved ridges are not merely formations. They are the edges of something older than the life coating them: ribs, lids, growth rings, or the impressions left by a body that has sunk too deeply to retain anatomy. The whole plain may occupy a shallow crease in that buried being. Its warmth rises through the terminal mud. Its breath condenses inside lungs above. Its minute adjustments arrive as thunder from impossible distances.

The broad organism of the surface has grown upon it like skin over a sleeping wound. Perhaps all life there is engaged in covering it. Perhaps the ceaseless incorporation is a form of restraint, each membrane and rootlet helping hold down what cannot be allowed to rise. The immense displaced beings may be knots where the covering thickens. The shelters may be watchful nodes, listening for a change in the pulse below.

Or the relation may be the reverse.

The buried presence may be feeding the surface slowly, dreaming organisms into being and withdrawing them when their forms become too definite. Names cannot remain because a name fixes a boundary, and boundaries are precisely what the inhabitant cannot sustain. Species blur into weather. Architecture passes through architecture. Nearness becomes distance. Past events leave tracks ahead of those who make them. Identity cannot adhere to matter that is continually exchanged across times, bodies, and depths.

Thus the place is crowded beyond counting, yet may contain only one life.

It is an old life, almost finished but unable to complete itself. It has spent ages settling toward a final shape. Every collapse brings it closer; every spontaneous forest, foam plateau, moving sheet of ground, and flock of white pressure-borne creatures interrupts the process. It continually produces forms because it has not yet decided which parts of itself are alive. It continually destroys them because every form that stands apart is false.

The rare animals know this. When the deep vibration strengthens, they stop and orient themselves—not toward danger, but toward belonging. For a moment each becomes a needle laid along the grain of the greater body. Then the noise returns, and they continue grazing, wading, surfacing, or sinking.

Nothing there is unoccupied because emptiness would be a wound.

Nothing remains still because stillness would permit an outline.

And nothing keeps a name because whatever lives there is still drawing all things, slowly and without remainder, into itself.
