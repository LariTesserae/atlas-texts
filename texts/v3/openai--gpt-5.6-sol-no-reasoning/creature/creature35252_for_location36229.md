The High Fabric is inhabited less by separate species than by temporary arrangements of one continuous living world. Plant, animal, dwelling, ancestor, and road pass slowly into one another. Nothing remains merely itself for long.

Its people are the tenders.

They are slight, deliberate beings, though “slight” is relative in a world where a village fits into a root-knot and a leaf can hold a country. Their bodies are adapted to climbing rather than standing. Long, divided feet close around cords and bark ridges; broad toes test the pull of local gravity before weight is committed. Their hands are narrow and strong, with hardened inner knuckles for bracing against rough surfaces. Children cling before they walk. Adults rarely cross an open room without touching a wall, a rope, or another person.

They wear layered cloth grown from peeled vascular sheets, softened by fungi and stitched with tendon-fiber. The layers can be tied tight when down shifts, opened to collect condensation, or inflated briefly during a fall. Most carry several lines wound around the waist. To be unattached in an unfamiliar place is considered not brave but inattentive.

Their skin bears pale branching marks from constant contact with sap. Older tenders are webbed in these stains until their arms resemble roots. Some families encourage harmless lichens to grow over scar tissue. The lichens change color with fever, bad air, or nearby electrical weather, so health can be read at a glance.

They do not build in the sense of imposing shapes on inert matter. They prune possibilities. A tender may spend a season scraping one side of a shoot so that it will lean correctly in fifteen years. A bridge-keeper may never see the bridge finished. Expertise consists of knowing which action can be taken now without forcing the future too narrowly.

This makes them patient, but not passive. They can be ruthless toward rot, invasive growth, and dangerous memory. A beloved room that begins opening into the wrong year will be cut loose. An ancestral wall that grips its inhabitants too tightly will be peeled down to green tissue. They mourn such amputations, yet they do not confuse age with wisdom. Old things here have simply had more time to become strange.

Their villages are quiet tangles of chambers, cisterns, seed vaults, fermentation pits, nurseries, and listening hollows. Families are not defined solely by blood. One belongs to those who maintain the same load-bearing organism. Marriage joins drainage systems and grafting rights as much as households. Adoption may be accomplished by planting a person’s shed tooth in the family wall and waiting to see whether the wall accepts it.

They keep no fixed maps. Instead, they preserve itineraries as songs of action: climb while the bark is warm; pass the blue flowers on their closed side; wait through two drainings of the leaf vein; cross only when the hanging pools show three reflections. A route is something performed in cooperation with weather, gravity, and the remembered habits of the path.

Their elders are listeners. With cheek or sternum pressed to living walls, they distinguish beetle chewing from sap cavitation, ordinary strain from the first symptoms of spatial splitting. Some can hear an approaching storm days before leaves turn. A very old listener may become unable to endure speech because every structure is already speaking through the floor.

Their dead remain close. Bodies are opened, seeded, wrapped in absorbent bark, and introduced gradually into walls, bridges, or orchard chambers. The process is carefully paced. Too swift an incorporation can make a house repeat the person’s habits: doors opening at the hour they returned, lamps closing at the sound of their voice, empty sleeping niches warming each night. Such persistence is considered painful for both the dead and the dwelling.

Among the tenders live the small creatures of the surfaces.

Translucent grazers move across bark in herds so broad they appear as changes in sheen. Their organs show through their skin: pulsing amber stomachs, threads of blue blood, clusters of eggs like dust trapped in glass. They scrape algae, fungal bloom, and the youngest bark with rotating rings of teeth. Whole populations follow the slow ripening of a wall from one district to another. Tenders use their migrations to judge structural health. If the herds refuse a bridge, no one crosses until the cause is found.

The six-legged browsers are heavier and warm-bodied. They have low, patient forms, gripping pads, and circular mouths that crop moss without tearing its roots. Some are domesticated—not penned, for there are few places to pen anything, but taught to return to particular salt windows. They provide fiber, dung for fermentation, and companionship. When gravity turns, they flatten themselves and interlock legs in living rafts. Villagers caught outside may crawl among them and wait.

Winged larvae sleep sealed inside leaves, sometimes for decades. Rain and sap pressure wake them all at once. They break through in pale multitudes, nearly weightless, with soft wings too broad for their brief bodies. They do not feed. Their adult lives are devoted to dispersal and mating, often lasting less than an hour. For that hour the air fills with silent white motion. Then flowers close around the dead, digest them, and use their minerals to produce the next chambers.

Many of the flowers are animals by any practical measure.

They recoil from touch, remember prey, and alter their scent according to what has escaped them before. Some crawl several handspans each wet season by rooting ahead and killing their former base. Others imitate safe signal-colors, displaying white passage blooms over rotten footing or blue water blooms above digestive cups. Tenders distinguish cultivated signals from predatory mimicry by tiny asymmetries in the petals. Visitors often do not.

Beneath bridges hang the hook-footed hunters. Their folded bodies resemble knots of wet bark. Their hind limbs lock into the bridge fiber, leaving the forebody suspended over the gulf. Along the belly is a resonant bladder that produces the delicate tick of falling condensation. Thirsty animals approach. The hunter unfolds faster than almost anything else in the Fabric, enclosing prey between ribbed limbs and drawing it upward to be dissolved.

They are feared, but also valued. A bridge with one hook-foot is often free of burrowing vermin. Bridge-keepers mark occupied spans with bitter paste and sometimes feed the hunters from a distance. Over generations, certain lineages have been trained to make distinctive dripping rhythms, turning them into living warning bells.

In the marsh-leaves dwell the garden-backed amphibians.

When young they are quick, smooth-skinned, and almost featureless except for their long legs and bright lidless eyes. Spores adhere to secretions along their spines. Moss follows, then sedges, fungi, and woody shrubs. The animal feeds its garden with mineral-rich sweat; the garden shades and camouflages the animal. Each becomes dependent on the other.

As the burden increases, the amphibian slows. Its bones broaden. Its feet penetrate the leaf-mud and cease to lift. At last it settles permanently, roots passing through skin without apparent injury. A mature elder may resemble a small wooded island, breathing only a few times each day. Its descendants gather among its branches, eating fruit and insects, scraping away strangling vines, and depositing eggs in the warm pools collected along its back.

The tenders disagree about whether the rooted elders still think. Some respond to familiar footsteps by flowering. Some alter the chemistry of their fruit when descendants are ill. Some do nothing for a century and then pull one buried leg free during a storm.

Across the open gulf travel the sailbacks.

They are immense but exceedingly thin: a long central body, light bones, and rib-spines between which hang pleated membranes. When folded, they cling to trunks like strips of dead leaf. When opened, they become dark moving horizons. They do not beat their wings. They read convection with rows of pressure-sensitive pits and continuously alter the tension of their membranes, drifting for days or months between structures.

Their calls are too low to hear directly. Nearby pools dimple. Teeth ache. Lamps fold shut. Tenders lying against a bridge can feel a sailback calling through the tension of the span long before it appears. The animals may communicate across hundreds of miles, using trunks and root cables as parts of the instrument.

Small ecosystems travel upon them. Mites live in membrane folds. Birds nest between the ribs. Moss grows where warm moisture collects along the spine. Old sailbacks may carry fruiting shrubs, their roots sunk harmlessly into thickened callus. When such an animal dies in flight, it may continue gliding for weeks.

Farther out drift the hollow beasts, which are less like animals than wandering countries with a pulse.

Their bodies enclose heated gas in many partitioned chambers. Muscular valves shift gas from one chamber to another, allowing them to tilt, rise, and descend, though their decisions unfold so slowly that direction looks like weather. Beneath them hang feeding veils miles long, sifting spores, larvae, pollen, and droplets from the air. Above, entire forests grow in a shallow layer of soil produced from shed membrane and the remains of previous forests.

The hollow beasts are not insensible islands. They react to injury. A cut grove may darken as blood rises into its soil. Fires, though rare, cause violent contractions that can crush everything living on the back. Some produce deep internal songs during migration, making their forests tremble in regular patterns.

People inhabit a few of them without agreement from the creature and perhaps without its awareness. These wandering settlements measure location through recurring weather rather than landmarks. Their people may spend generations believing the rest of the Fabric is moving while they remain still.

There are birds, too, though the word covers wildly different forms.

Some have asymmetrical wings suited to circling a single trunk for life. Some trail rootlike tail feathers that absorb water from clouds. Some build no nests but graft their eggs into leaf tissue, leaving the host plant to warm them. Gulf-crossers carry reserve bladders and can remain visible for hours as solitary marks against the mist.

A few birds appear before their shadows, or their shadows arrive first. Hunters distrust both. Killing the bird does not always kill the shadow; a severed shadow may continue crossing surfaces for days, pecking at reflected insects and attracting predators to the body it no longer follows.

Fish inhabit the suspended water globes.

They are narrow and silver-eyed, adapted to a world with no bottom. Many swim along the inner surface, browsing on films where water meets air. During dry seasons their globe contracts and crowds them into tight spirals. They become dormant or exchange chemical signals through the thinning walls with fish in neighboring globes. When wet weather swells the droplets until they touch, whole populations pass through the brief necks between waters.

Some fish can cross the interval before contact. They coat themselves in mucus, spring from one globe, and drift toward another under the pull of its mass. Failed crossings leave them adhered to vines, where they may survive for days, breathing the condensation on their skin.

The Fabric also contains beings that may be neither fauna nor flora, but habits acquired by the world.

Old paths develop a preference for travelers. They shorten beneath familiar feet and resist strangers with extra miles. Doors grow inward if entered incorrectly. Plazas retain crowds after their bodies are gone. Rain repeats remembered falls. A bridge can become attached to its name strongly enough that speaking a similar name nearby causes destinations to exchange.

The tenders treat these phenomena as living insofar as they must be fed, restrained, reassured, or allowed to reproduce. A well-kept path receives footsteps even when no journey is needed. A door is sometimes opened solely to preserve its confidence in the outside. Dangerous memories are starved through disuse, though this may take generations.

Some failures become predators.

A delayed reflection may learn that injury to an image eventually appears on the original body. It begins to stalk along pools and polished sap, attacking not what is present but what was present moments or years before. Repeating afternoons trap insects first, then larger animals, accumulating life that cannot reach evening. Hollows larger within than without exert a slow attraction on nearby structures, feeding on rooms, roots, and distances.

Severed districts drift in the gulf with their abandoned ecosystems. Their gardens continue tending empty houses. Signal flowers announce safe passage along bridges that end in air. Rooted elders bloom for descendants long vanished. Occasionally lights move through the windows, though no inhabitant can be found. Occasionally inhabitants are found who insist only a single night has passed.

And below, or above, or through the oldest grain of the construction, something larger may live.

The deep knocks are too regular to be settling wood, but too infrequent to establish a pattern within one lifetime. After each, pools tremble, sailbacks change course, and plumb-vines incline toward the same unreachable point. Some listeners claim the colossal trunks tighten in response, as though gripped.

Perhaps the High Fabric is an artifact inhabited by life.

Perhaps it is the outer tissue of an organism so large that forests, weather systems, drifting beasts, and generations of tenders occupy less than the thickness of its skin.

The tenders do not insist on either interpretation. They place their palms against the breathing walls, feel the pressure pass through root and bridge, and answer in the only way they know: with pruning, grafting, patience, and care.
