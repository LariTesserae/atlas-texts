The basin is inhabited by multitudes, but no single creature owns it. Its true population is a shifting commonwealth of animals, laborers, machines, and water, each living by the temporary permissions of the others.

Most visible are the birds. They gather wherever the machinery has accidentally made an island: gravel fans below culverts, concrete aprons, pipe gantries, mesh traps thickened with silt. Black-winged stilts pick delicately across skins of mud that would swallow anything heavier. Terns rest on handrails in close white rows. Herons stand beside sluice mouths, watching stunned fish emerge from turbulence. Cormorants occupy warning frames and unfinished towers, spreading wet wings in the engine-warmed air until they resemble black mechanical emblems. Their colonies shift overnight when a pool is drained or a bar disappears beneath rising water.

Beneath them lives the basin’s denser, less countable population. Frogs fill flooded tire tracks until the water seems granular with eyes. Beetles skate the oily-looking surfaces. Mosquito larvae hang below algae films, and shrimp pulse through warm discharge channels almost too clouded to see into. Small fish enter through screens, culverts, and faulty gates, followed by eels that treat the buried drainage system as a dark river network. Crabs wedge themselves under slabs where vibration keeps the water faintly trembling.

Rats inhabit the dry infrastructure rather than the land. They travel cable trays, conduits, hollow walls, and the undersides of bridges, moving through the works without needing to cross much open mud. They know which pump houses stay warm at night and which electrical cabinets admit insects. Their paths intersect those of feral dogs on the embankments: narrow-bodied animals with mud-colored coats, scarred feet, and the habit of testing each step before committing their weight. The dogs sleep near occupied stations but do not belong to anyone in particular. They follow maintenance vehicles, scavenge around sheds, and bark into the mist at disturbances no person can locate.

People live here too, though the basin makes them seem fewer than they are. Pump attendants, electricians, surveyors, dredging crews, mechanics, guards, and families in the raised brick houses form small islands of human routine. They wear rubber boots even in dry weather. They carry lamps at noon under the pipe galleries. Their clothing smells of diesel, wet mineral dust, and the sour warmth of standing water. Many communicate with economical signs: a hand circling to mean an impeller is turning, a flat palm to stop, two fingers toward the eyes before crossing a crusted flat.

The oldest workers have acquired a bodily knowledge no map quite preserves. They can feel a buried main through the soles of their boots. They distinguish ordinary vibration from a bearing beginning to fail. They know that a bright green patch may conceal liquid clay, that bubbles beside a foundation can mean escaping air or escaping ground, and that silence from a station is more alarming than noise. They keep poles near doorways to test depth and carry lengths of rope in vehicles that seldom travel far from a road.

Children, where there are children, are forbidden the canals and learn them intimately. They collect beetle cases, feathers, cable offcuts, and numbered plastic tags brought up by the mud. They recognize individual dogs and nesting birds. To them the leaning sheds and half-submerged foundations are not failed plans but ordinary landmarks. A road entering a pool is simply where the road ends.

The machinery itself has something like a life, not because it thinks, but because everything else arranges itself around its breathing. Pump stations wake, labor, overheat, rest, and wake again. Their vibrations drive nesting birds from one beam and draw warmth-seeking animals to another. Each machine has a temperament known to its keepers: one catches debris, one floods its lower chamber, one starts reluctantly in damp weather, one runs smoothly but shakes the ground farther away. The basin’s great pulse is the alternation of these engines, pushing water from pit to ditch, ditch to canal, canal to higher canal, always against the shape the land is trying to assume.

Even the plants live as opportunists rather than settlers. Rushes hold a patch only until salinity changes. Tamarisk grips spoil ridges destined to be cut down or spread. Willows rise rapidly beside leaks and are repeatedly severed beneath the power lines. Algae flourish in the warmth of discharge water; liverworts occupy concrete made perpetually wet by condensation. Their communities are young, provisional, and often erased before they can thicken into anything resembling a mature bank.

What lives here, then, is a population adapted less to a habitat than to an interruption. The birds need the pumps to expose mud and are displaced when pumping drains it too far. Fish enter through the same works that crush or strand them. People depend on ground they know is hollow and on engines whose failure would redraw the basin within hours. Rats flourish in conduits built to exclude water; eels flourish in drains built to exclude rivers.

All are creatures of margins measured in centimeters: the difference between a nesting bar and a drowned bar, between a firm crust and a broken one, between a dry motor chamber and a flooded station. They inhabit not the marsh or the factory, but the unstable interval in which each continually becomes the other.
