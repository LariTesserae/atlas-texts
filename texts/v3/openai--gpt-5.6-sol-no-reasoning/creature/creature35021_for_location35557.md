The city is inhabited first by maintainers.

They wake to spirit levels mounted above their beds and bowls of water set into windowsills. Before dressing, they inspect both. A tilted bubble means the floor has changed; a slanted surface means the district has. If the two disagree, they wake the neighbors and send for a municipal measurer.

Their clothes are heavy with practical additions: snap-hooks at the waist, chalk in waxed pockets, folding rulers, little bells that ring when local downwardness alters. Most wear soles that can be reshimmed and coats stitched with the name of their home district, blood type, nearest reliable relative, and preferred direction of burial. Children learn knots before letters. They learn letters early too, because a thing written in several hands is harder for the world to retract.

Bridge keepers live in narrow houses built into the first piers. They listen through the stone with brass cups, distinguishing ordinary load from new distance. Lift operators know each cable as shepherds know animals. Pipe clerks spend their lives revising arrows. At dawn, gutter crews walk the terraces with colored flags, redirecting the night’s water before it discovers a route through a bedroom, turbine room, or public archive.

The surveyors are among the city’s most recognizable figures. They travel in teams because solitary observations are not legally real. One carries the plumb line, another the tuning forks, another the clocks packed in separate felt-lined boxes. The youngest is entrusted with the caged bird. The eldest carries the bowl of water.

They return with reports such as:

**West Foundry is nine centimeters nearer than yesterday, but only by rail.**

**Noon occurred twice below the tannery.**

**Stair 44 now descends to its former top.**

**The vacant lot behind Saint Orra’s contains weather not belonging to this year.**

Where all instruments disagree, the surveyors paint their white circles. They do not step into them again. Foxes sleep there. Mica moths hatch there in winter. Cats use them as shortcuts and sometimes emerge old, wet, or carrying the smell of districts no person has visited.

The oldest inhabitants are the foundry families of the middle ledge. They occupy tall, narrow tenements whose brickwork has been dismantled and rebuilt so often that each building contains several incompatible architectural periods. Their kitchens are warm, their windows small, and their walls crowded with duplicate records: marriages, debts, recipes, births, disappearances, the former names of streets. They keep household ledgers not from thrift but from metaphysical caution.

A meal is recorded when it is eaten.

A guest signs on arrival and departure.

A newborn is named aloud in every room.

These families say that repetition is the first mortar. Their children recite addresses as other children recite prayers:

“Third landing, Red Furnace Court, Old Ledge, beneath the eastern drum, above the market roof.”

They repeat the words whenever fog hides the way home.

Miners work not only for ore but for certainty. They descend into warm seams where metal has not settled on its final properties. Iron may be soft as wax until sung over. Copper sometimes remembers being green stone and must be persuaded through successive furnaces. There are veins of pale material that reflect structures not yet built; these are quarried under municipal seal and delivered to planning halls, where architects examine them with dread and professional interest.

Kiln workers are held in particular esteem. Clay enters their furnaces vague, damp, and prone to losing shape when unobserved. It emerges as brick, tile, cup, drain, insulator: objects committed to being themselves. Potters stamp every piece with a district mark and date. Broken ceramics are never discarded over an edge. They are crushed into roadbeds so that even failed vessels help the mountain remain.

The engine houses contain another class of custodian. These machinists tend the great governors that keep neighborhoods at compatible rates of time. They work by vibration more than sight, sleeping in bunks suspended from springs, waking when a turbine’s rhythm varies by less than an untrained person could perceive.

Long exposure changes them. Their pulses become extraordinarily regular. Their hair grows in whorls corresponding to gear ratios. Retired engine keepers make excellent witnesses because they are difficult to confuse about sequence. They may forget names and faces, but never which event occurred first.

Not everyone serves machinery.

On residential terraces, gardeners manufacture soil from ash, moss, kitchen peelings, powdered stone, animal bedding, and imported loam. A mature garden may represent six generations of accumulation. It is deeded independently of the ledge beneath it, rolled onto mats when the ground shifts, and moved by crane if necessary.

Cistern gardeners cultivate plants according to moisture and gravity. They stake roots upward or downward depending on the week. They comb blue grasses from beneath bridges while wearing inverted harnesses. They wrap the warm electrical vines in ceramic spirals to prevent them from entering substations. Their orchard cages rotate slowly, allowing each fruit to face old rock long enough to ripen.

The fruit is dense, mineral-tasting, and faintly warm. Some varieties contain a small hard core etched with contour lines. Orchard keepers save these cores in map drawers. Decades later, the lines sometimes prove to depict newly formed ledges.

Goat herders conduct their animals over roofs and gantries, ringing handbells at junctions. The goats need little guidance. They sense changes in weight through their hooves and often refuse unsafe structures hours before inspectors detect a fault. White-coated chimney goats are considered especially reliable, though they are acquisitive and will eat survey flags, laundry, paper records, and the leather straps of unattended instruments.

There are reservoir fishers, though their prey is seldom fish. They draw up translucent eels that knot themselves around magnetic objects, blind crustaceans with shells like cast iron, and soft-bodied things full of rainwater from storms that have not occurred. Reservoir frogs gather on warm pipes and call whenever gravity turns. A sudden silence among them can halt an entire freight district.

The city’s cats belong to no one, though tunnel crews claim kinship with them. They are long-bodied, low-eared, and dark enough to disappear against oiled cable. Their whiskers fan in several directions. They hunt the blind rail rodents and the mica-winged moths, but also less visible pests: stray echoes, recurring footsteps, little pockets of obsolete darkness left behind when a lamp is moved.

A tunnel cat may sit for hours staring at an empty wall. Workers know not to interrupt. Eventually it strikes, and something unseen squeals from inside the stone.

The alpine birds live in greater numbers than the citizens. Black and blue-black, with hooked feet and heat-pale eyes, they rise in spirals between districts on factory breath. They decorate their nests with wire, glass, stamped metal, and scraps of official documents. A person who has lost a permit may find it years later in a ventilation stack, used to line a clutch of speckled eggs.

Certain birds imitate the shift whistles. Factories must therefore maintain distinctive chords, changed by ordinance each year. Older birds continue sounding obsolete shifts at dawn, sending pensioners to windows and confusing the dead.

Above them circle the great gliders.

They are broad enough to darken streets. Their bodies vary: some furred, some scaled, some stretched beneath membranes translucent with vascular light. Brass plates grow half-swallowed in their shoulders. Signal lamps blink from scar tissue. A few still trail harness rings large enough to moor a house.

Handlers work from high platforms, attracting them with steam, electrical tones, and cages of insects. No one truly commands them. At most, the city negotiates. The gliders sometimes carry weather instruments or emergency lines across gaps where bridges have failed. On rare occasions one allows a passenger to be strapped beneath its breastbone.

Such passengers return silent and frost-burned. They report having looked down on unfinished horizons where rivers waited in coils for valleys to be made.

Below the shelves move the cloud fauna, too large to be seen whole.

The city names individual migrants by the portions repeatedly observed: Lantern-Rib, Nine Fins, the Pale Orchard, Bell-Spine, She-Who-Tilts-Reservoirs. Their passage is announced by inclinometer and by animal behavior. Goats kneel. Birds leave the vents. Cats flatten themselves along east-west walls. Water climbs its containers in a smooth, shining slope.

Natural philosophers argue over whether these beings swim through cloud, gravity, or the unformed substance from which both will eventually separate. Children are less cautious. They lean over guarded rails and throw offerings: copper washers, fruit, paper boats bearing their names.

Occasionally, far below, a row of organs brightens in reply.

The Office of Measurements employs specialists called comparators. Unlike surveyors, who establish what is happening, comparators decide which version of events the city will use. When clocks diverge, maps overlap, or a doorway acquires two destinations, the comparators assemble testimony. They favor accounts supported by routine.

A baker who has opened the same shop every morning for forty years can outweigh an atomic clock.

A tram route used by hundreds is preferred to a shorter route seen by three.

A widow setting two places each evening may preserve a vanished kitchen longer than a blueprint can.

This makes ordinary citizens participants in municipal reality. Their habits have structural value. They walk customary paths even when inconvenient. They greet shopkeepers by name. They maintain festivals, queues, arguments, and weekly card games because repetition lays down the civic world as surely as rails.

At public meetings, names are read aloud: streets, bridges, districts, the living, the dead, and the missing. The dead receive particular care. Each is recorded in stone, metal, paper, memory, and song. One copy remains near the place of birth, one near the place of death, and one travels continuously on a circular tram so that geography cannot close over all of them at once.

Those who vanish are not immediately declared dead. A missing person may have entered an alley whose exit has not formed, boarded a lift arriving decades later, or gone through a shared doorway into a remote district. Their apartments remain lit at dusk. Neighbors take turns dusting the rooms, winding clocks, watering plants, and speaking the absent person’s name.

A lit room resists vanishing.

The city also contains people who do not wholly agree with its project.

There are vacancy monks who live beyond the last reliable tram stop in huts designed to leave no imprint. They own no clocks, write nothing down, and change their names with every season. They believe the world must retain some uncommitted space or become rigid and sterile. Engineers accuse them of weakening the outskirts. Gardeners quietly trade seeds with them.

There are bridge prospectors who cross unfinished spans, laying temporary rail ahead of themselves and lifting it behind. They seek places visible but not yet reachable. Many return with frost on one side of their bodies and dust from unknown continents on the other. Some bring back strangers who insist they were born in the city, though none of the records contain them.

There are illegal distance merchants. They sell folded journeys in wax packets, compressed staircases, shortcuts wound onto reels, and tiny doors that open once. Their goods are unreliable. A purchased shortcut may save ten minutes every day for years, then demand all the saved time at once.

There are sky readers who study the moving lights above the upper districts. Some are stars. Some are trains. Some are windows in neighborhoods attached to a different night. The readers distinguish them by color, rhythm, and whether the light appears in bowls of water. They claim that whole populations inhabit the highest shelves, migrating with celestial bodies rather than the mountain.

These upper inhabitants descend rarely. They arrive pale from long dusk, wearing weighted veils and boots with many articulated soles. Their speech is slow and doubled by faint anticipatory echoes. They bring frost-preserved flowers, meteoric alloys, and news of bridges whose far ends attach to moons still under construction.

At the lowest machine halls live the bolters, a compact, muscular people with scarred palms and exceptional balance. Their homes cling to vertical faces beside the counterweight shafts. Furniture is fixed to walls because the rooms occasionally rotate their understanding of level. Bolter children race along maintenance ladders before they can read. Adults spend their working lives fastening the city to rock that is not yet sure it is rock.

They say the mountain moves like a sleeping animal: never quickly, never entirely still.

They know its moods through bolts. A singing bolt means growth. A hot bolt means new stone. A bolt coated overnight in fine white hair indicates that the ledge has developed a possible future and should not be heavily loaded until morning.

Still deeper are the listeners.

Few citizens meet them. They inhabit sealed galleries near the turbines, where the mountain’s rising can be heard as something between thunder and speech. They press their bodies to warm basalt and transcribe what they perceive into long numerical chants. Their reports determine where districts may be built and which engines must alter speed.

Whether they listen to geology, machinery, or the unborn world is disputed. The listeners themselves offer no distinction.

Life in the city is dangerous, but seldom panicked. Its people distrust haste. They pause at thresholds, test rails before leaning, watch puddles for directional change, and wait for echoes to finish. Courtship is deliberate. Marriage vows include agreement on what to do if the couple’s home acquires separate locations. Children are taught never to follow their own voice around a corner.

Even pleasure has a stabilizing function.

Markets recur on fixed days, their stalls arranged in inherited patterns. Musicians play rhythms synchronized with deep turbines, adding syncopation only in districts with strong foundations. Dancers wear weighted shoes and stamp named figures into public squares. Taverns keep perpetual stews whose uninterrupted existence is locally famous. In theaters, actors perform old plays without changing a line, ensuring that at least one sequence of events concludes the same way each night.

Yet the inhabitants are not merely custodians of certainty. They love the unfinished world.

They gather at rails to watch a new shelf darken into solidity. They celebrate the first dependable raincourse with lanterns and bells. Schoolchildren are taken to fresh basalt and permitted to leave handprints before its glassy skin hardens. When a foundation emerges from the ground without a planned building, architects compete to discover what structure the place is asking for.

At dusk, families illuminate their windows. Lift operators light their shaft lamps one by one. Furnace crews open vents until dull orange glows through the mist. Signalers on distant ledges exchange slow, patient sequences:

**Here.**

**Still here.**

**Here also.**

The replies cross gaps that may be distance, weather, or unfinished creation. Birds wheel through the pulses. Gliders pass like moving roofs. Deep below, something luminous turns in the cloud and the city’s gutters briefly run upward.

Then a train enters the high dark, lamps advancing beyond the final pier.

Inside sit tracklayers, surveyors, a cook, two engineers, a municipal witness, and a child entrusted with naming whatever they reach. Ahead of them there is no completed bridge, no settled destination, perhaps no opposite ledge.

They go slowly.

Behind the last carriage, steel cools into certainty. Timetables revise themselves. A station begins to gather around the promise of arrival. And in the dimness ahead, where nothing has yet consented to exist, a few yellow windows kindle in answer.
