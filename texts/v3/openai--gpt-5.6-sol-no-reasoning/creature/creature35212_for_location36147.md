What lives there is not a forest, though every part of it is vegetal. It is a commonwealth whose citizens have never finished becoming one another.

At the uppermost skin live the small, hard leaves: black-green scales overlapping by the trillion, each no larger than a fingernail, together roofing territories wider than seas. Alone, each is simple—a lens, a cup, a valve, a patch of light-eating tissue. In assembly they become meteorological organs. They taste pressure through their undersides, measure cloud charge along their margins, and turn in coordinated fields. Their synchronized tilting can darken a province or expose it to the gray day. They do not know the whole pattern. The pattern knows itself through them.

Between these scales drift the nearest things the highland has to plankton. They are loose cells, spores, hollow grains, and green motes wrapped in films of water. Some remain airborne for years, feeding on diffuse light and mist. Some are messages carrying fragments of chemistry from distant tissues. Some are reproductive proposals, viable only if they settle upon a surface willing to incorporate them. Others are weapons so patient that their effects may not appear for centuries: a minor enzyme, a change in permeability, a tendency for a rival membrane to admit too much salt.

Fine aerial tendrils hunt through this suspension. Blind and nearly transparent, they extend into every brief pocket of air, touching droplets and one another. Contact may end in withdrawal, grafting, digestion, or the exchange of living nuclei. During still weather they weave temporary nets between leaves. When wind rises, the nets dissolve into threads and are reeled back into the canopy.

Lower down are the walkers.

They begin as swollen seed bodies cradled in the forks of larger growths. When mature, they unfold several hydraulic limbs—pale columns stiffened by water pressure—and lever themselves free. Their movement is almost too slow to witness. One foot anchors; another softens the surface ahead with secreted acids; the body empties fluid from its rear tissues and pumps it forward. Over hours it inclines. Over days it advances its own length. Over a year it may cross a valley seam.

They do not wander. Buried gradients guide them: traces of compatible pollen, mineral deficiencies, subtle electrical invitations transmitted through the canopy. Some carry dormant groves folded inside them. Some are mobile grafts seeking injured vascular lines. Some are usurpers, arriving at old junctions and slowly replacing their inherited chemistry. At intervals they root and spread broad feeding sails. If conditions favor settlement, the legs thicken into trunks and the traveler becomes a place. If not, it uproots itself and continues.

No clean boundary separates these migrants from the terrain beneath them. A walker may step onto a mat genetically identical to itself and be absorbed without reproducing. Another may spend decades crossing what appears to be one plateau, only to discover—chemically—that it has never left the body that produced it.

In the rain-filled seams live the mirror skins. They are colonies of minute floating leaves, transparent from above and silver beneath. Each traps a pinprick of air. Joined together, they regulate the darkness below, opening pores for gases, closing against cold, thickening when pressure rises in the surrounding tissues. They spread across exposed water with extraordinary urgency because open water is dangerous here: unmeasured, unallocated, capable of gathering momentum.

Beneath them, in the black pools, hang long colorless plants that have abandoned light. They feed on pressure differences, dissolved minerals, and the electrical leakage of nearby roots. Their bodies resemble strings of soft bells. Each bell contracts when a vibration passes through the pool, so a distant fracture may cause an entire submerged grove to pulse in sequence. They function as both sense organs and dampers, breaking waves before those waves can cut persistent channels.

Deeper still live forests that have not seen daylight since their first leaves were buried. Their foliage is white, amber, or clear. Some leaves face downward. Some grow inside chambers filled with warm vapor. Some have become membranes stretched across cavities, trembling continuously as gases pass through them. They no longer photosynthesize; upper growth feeds them sugars in exchange for pumping, storage, computation, and structural support.

Here are the pressure trees, immense hollow columns whose walls may be hundreds of meters thick. Their sap does not merely rise. It circulates through ascending and descending vessels, compressing in muscular chambers and flashing through valves with the force of weather. Many of the highland’s deepest sounds originate in these organs. Their openings and closures are felt far away as bass pulses, and neighboring tissues adjust before the pressure wave arrives.

Around them grow sheath plants, spiral bands that prevent the columns from splitting. When a sheath reaches the limit of its tension, it ruptures with a report that travels for kilometers. Almost immediately, dormant strips beneath it inflate and take the load. What appears to be catastrophic damage is often an expected stage of maintenance.

The pale filaments threaded through every layer are neither roots nor nerves, though they perform the work of both. Some conduct ions. Some conduct heat. A few carry coherent changes of state with almost no resistance, allowing remote groves to respond as though adjacent. They branch obsessively, forming alternative pathways, then withdraw nourishment from routes that prove unreliable. In this manner the highland thinks—not in symbols or images, but in competing anatomies.

Its thoughts become structure.

A prediction of prolonged cold is a thickened membrane begun decades before the cold arrives. A memory of drought is a knot of vessels that will never again fully close. An error is a branch starved until it hollows. A successful inference becomes a living route, reinforced each time water, charge, or mineral passes through it.

There is no central mind buried below the canopy. Awareness, if the word applies, occurs as agreements among distances. One region senses weight. Another retains the pattern of an old storm. A third compares the chemistry of rising vapor with scars left by earlier seasons. Their answer is not spoken or pictured. Leaves turn. Reservoirs contract. A ridge settles several centimeters. Clouds begin to form.

Identity is equally dispersed. A single genetic lineage may occupy scattered patches across half a continent, connected only by periodic exchanges of airborne cells. Conversely, what looks like one continuous trunk may contain thousands of inherited patterns held in negotiated balance. Some tissues cooperate for millennia without merging. Others infiltrate one another until ancestry becomes undecidable.

The oldest inhabitants are not the largest. They are tiny rings of active membrane sealed inside protected knots. Many began near the first colonization of the stone below. They preserve ancient electrical biases, chemical rhythms, and molecular arrangements that younger tissues repeatedly consult. The enclosing plants grow, split, die, and are repurposed, but the rings are copied inward from chamber to chamber. They are archives without records and elders without bodies.

The largest inhabitant may be the highland itself, though calling it an inhabitant gives it too much unity. It has no single metabolism, genome, intention, or edge. It can injure itself without noticing and defend one region against another. Yet during storms, fires of charge race through its buried networks and differences briefly collapse. Pressure systems synchronize. Reservoirs beat together. Province-sized leaves alter their angles in sequence. For a few hours, the countless growths behave less like an ecology and more like one enormous reflex.

Its reproductive bodies form far beneath the visible surface.

These are folded organs kilometers across, warm from slow digestion and supplied by rivers running vertically through translucent trunks. For centuries they accumulate tissue in darkness. When an exhalation opens the continent, light strikes them, and they unfold. Their inner surfaces bear entire embryonic ecologies: roofing leaves, pumping threads, archive knots, mobile seed forms, and symbiotic films already arranged in layers. They are not seeds for individual plants. They are seeds for systems.

Most never depart. They graft themselves into the walls of the cavity and become replacement anatomy. A few are torn loose by the violence of opening and lifted into the vapor column as thin, immense sails. Whether any cross the dim lowlands and found another highland is unknown. For days after an exhalation, fragments can sometimes be seen above the cloud ceiling: green-black sheets turning slowly, carrying rain within their folds.

There are also the dead, although death here is rarely an absence.

A spent leaf remains sensitive to pressure. A hollow trunk continues to conduct vapor. Collapsed tissues become nurseries for invasive threads, which preserve useful channels while digesting everything else. Old organs are stripped of identity but not function. Their architecture persists as conduits, cisterns, braces, and warm chambers. Thus the dead inhabit the living as habits of shape.

Nothing is wasted quickly enough to become a corpse.

Even predation is a form of revision. A carnivorous cup closes over no insect, only a rival seedling or drifting clot of cells. It dissolves the intruder, reads its chemistry, and may incorporate useful sequences before disposing of the rest. Root fronts meet in darkness and test one another with minute exchanges. If neither can dominate, they form a boundary membrane and share pressure. If one finds a weakness, it advances cell by cell, leaving the conquered structure intact but changing what flows through it.

Their wars do not produce battlefields. They produce new physiology.

To a moving animal, if one could survive there, the inhabitants would seem incurious. Leaves might turn toward its heat. Tendrils would touch sweat and withdraw. Nearby vessels would tighten in response to unfamiliar weight. Yet no mouth would pursue, no eye would watch, no predator would learn its gait. The highland would register the animal as a local disturbance: warmth, salt, pressure, foreign proteins, a brief moving cavity in otherwise occupied space.

Then it would begin assigning that disturbance a use.

Films would form over abandoned footprints. Threads would enter shed skin and hair. Cups would collect breath-condensate. The path behind the traveler would seal, not out of hostility, but because emptiness is unstable here. If the traveler stopped too long, exploratory roots would gather beneath the body, measuring its weight and minerals. Dark leaves would angle inward to reclaim its heat.

The place does not distinguish sharply between visitor, nutrient, obstruction, and opportunity.

What lives there is therefore neither a population nor a singular being. It is an unbroken negotiation conducted in water, tension, light, inheritance, and time. Its inhabitants are leaves that serve as weather, roots that serve as memory, seeds that walk, dead tissues that continue their work, and ancient fragments sustained because the whole still needs the bias they carry.

They do not speak above the roar. The roar is not their speech. It is the unavoidable sound of their existence: pressure driven through vessels, surfaces multiplying, old layers yielding to new ones, the entire inhabited thickness forcing itself upward against gravity.

Their actual conversation is quieter.

It passes as a slight turn of a billion leaves.

A pulse in a buried thread.

A cup withholding one drop.

A distant grove answering by changing how the rain falls.
