The Fabricated Prairie is inhabited less by separate creatures than by functions which have learned to wear bodies.

Its principal inhabitant is the Prairie itself: perhaps one organism, perhaps a treaty among innumerable botanical machines. It has no central mind. Awareness travels through it as pressure, voltage, chemical gradients, and changes of color. A footfall becomes a widening report. A spoken word enters velvety leaves and wanders through ducts until some remote flower repeats its altered remains. The Prairie notices locally and understands continentally, often long after it has already responded.

Its thoughts resemble construction.

Where curiosity gathers, an organ appears. Leaves cup into listening dishes. Resin extrudes lenses. Roots braid chambers around unfamiliar objects. If something remains long enough, the Prairie grows a hypothesis about it: a socket shaped to its base, a tool suited to its hand, a fruit modeled on its chemistry. These answers are precise but not necessarily correct. The landscape can reproduce a bone without knowing pain, a key without knowing which door it opens, a face without distinguishing portrait from person.

Within this greater body live regional intelligences. They occupy watersheds without water: copper provinces, ivory canopies, blue-ring colonies, black-root foundries. Each governs its own growth schedules and hereditary archives. They bargain through exchanged voltage and encoded light. They also deceive. One district may counterfeit another’s hormones, divert its conveyor vines, or persuade its flowers to release sterile pollen. Their wars are quiet, expressed as incompatible architectures advancing toward one another until the ground rises edgewise in defensive walls.

Some are old enough to have personalities recognizable even to travelers. A district may be patient, allowing foreign matter to remain untouched for decades before incorporating it. Another may be imitative, covering every dropped object with replicas. A third builds only thresholds: doors, valves, mouths, irises, arches—openings into chambers that have not yet been grown. These dispositions persist through the replacement of every visible part.

The small pale creatures that emerge from inspection pores belong to the Prairie ambiguously.

They are called feltlings by some visitors. Their bodies are low and many-jointed, wrapped in colorless fibers that stand erect near charged surfaces. Metallic dust accumulates in their coats, drawing transient diagrams over their backs. Their ceramic mouthparts click as they cut damaged filaments, eat malformed buds, and peel diseased resin from seams.

They travel in maintenance chains. The first tests the ground; those behind repeat its movements exactly, so that a line of twenty may resemble one animal progressing through time. When threatened, they fold their legs and become neat white capsules. The Prairie often picks them up afterward, carrying them beneath the surface through muscular channels.

Whether feltlings are manufactured, born, or grown as temporary organs is unknown. Dissection settles nothing. Inside them are digestive sacs, repair tools, seed glands, memory coils, and soft structures resembling undeveloped hands. Some display hunger independent of any assigned task. A colony deprived of defects will begin creating minor damage so it can repair it. This may be malfunction, domestication, or the first stage of freedom.

The large stilted beings of the far plain are less easily dismissed as machines.

They cross distances that resist crossing. Seen from afar, they resemble animals with narrow bodies suspended between four or six immense legs. Their joints bend in several directions, adjusting to changes in scale. Each step takes hours. The foot descends, negotiates with the living surface, and is accepted into it without leaving a print.

Their heads, if they have heads, hang beneath their bodies as clusters of dark sensory cords. Along their backs grow folded gardens that alter with each migration. One may carry silver fronds for a century, connect itself to a tower, and depart bearing a stand of black flowers instead.

At feeding sites they do not browse. They anchor cables deep into a growth tower and become motionless while sap, charge, organs, and perhaps memories pass in both directions. Rings of foreign blossoms later emerge around their legs. These flowers sometimes reproduce the geometry of distant regions, suggesting the stilted beings carry not food but landscape.

They may be pollinators operating on a continental scale. They may be mobile factories. Some believe they are the Prairie’s oldest inhabitants, slowly tending an organism that has grown beyond their ability to control it. Others suggest the reverse: that each apparent giant is merely a walking seed, and the whole Prairie is the residue left by one that germinated.

Rarer still are the translucent forms overhead. They have no stable outline. A pale membrane may cross the sky for days, with ribs visible only when rectangular night passes behind it. Smaller objects drift inside, or seem to. Leaves below turn in coordinated waves as it passes, and transparent pollen rises toward it.

At times one of these aerial layers descends. It does not land so much as coincide with the plain. Towers appear inside its tissue; conveyor vines can be seen running through both ground and air. For several minutes the Prairie acquires a second set of depths. Then the presence lifts away, carrying loose seeds and occasionally a growth tower whose roots have been cut with surgical neatness.

They may be animals. They may be weather engineered into migratory bodies. They may be detached skins of the world.

There are also figures among the groves.

They are upright, quiet, and approximately person-shaped, though their proportions vary subtly according to the district that produced them. In one province they are tall and ribbed, with leaf-thin hands. In another they are compact, lacquered beings whose joints are enclosed in amber bladders. Their surfaces imitate clothing without seams or fastenings. What appears to be a hood may unfold into light-catching petals; what appears to be a face may be a panel of calibration hairs arranged around a mirrored pit.

They tend the Prairie—or perform actions that look like tending. They touch trunks, exchange capsules at vine junctions, redirect feltling chains, and stand for days beside construction fronts tapping pressure codes into the ground. They seldom approach strangers directly. Instead they cultivate intermediaries: a row of bright stalks that flash in sequence, a membrane on which silhouettes appear, a fruit that opens to reveal a model of the local terrain.

These figures do not speak, although they sometimes preserve speech.

Each may carry an archive of voices gathered from the absorptive foliage. When communication requires sound, slits open along the chest or throat and release old phrases in borrowed accents. The words may have been spoken centuries earlier and thousands of kilometers away. Grammar is approximate. Questions are answered with recovered sentences selected for shape rather than meaning:

*Do not enter the white field.*

*Your left hand was expected.*

*The door has not finished remembering you.*

Whether these are people transformed by the Prairie, artificial workers grown from remembered anatomy, or extensions of the landscape given mobility is unclear. When damaged, they expose silver-green fibers and replacement buds. Yet they show attachment to individual objects. They pause at particular luminous basins. They conceal certain capsules from the ground. Some have been observed cutting their own root threads before traveling, then cauterizing the connection with resin.

The severed ones change.

Without constant instruction from the Prairie, their gestures become less efficient and more personal. They begin making unnecessary marks. They collect inert objects precisely because the landscape disapproves of inertia. They may wrap metal in insulating cloth to prevent bark from taking hold, or carry fragments of glass in sealed cavities so veins cannot grow through them.

These wanderers communicate by tapping pressure patterns, but develop private rhythms the ground cannot readily parse. Two may stand on separate leaves and exchange messages through a shared beam, inserting meaningless beats to confuse the listening tissue. In this way, secrecy appears—not as silence, which the Prairie understands, but as deliberate noise.

Some seek the geometric darkness, timing their journeys to remain within moving parcels of night. Some follow the white seams when they open. Others search for a geological bottom beneath the decks, convinced there must be an inert world buried below the successive layers of life.

No one has found it.

There may also be people who are not visible as bodies at all. Certain luminous basins preserve recurring distortions: humanlike reflections with no corresponding figure above. They move among the inverted factories in the apparent depths, stopping to look upward through the blades. Sometimes they raise a hand. Sometimes a traveler’s reflection descends to join them while the traveler remains standing at the rim.

The Prairie treats these reflection-dwellers as valid inhabitants. Conveyor vines deliver capsules to the basin edges. Flowers bend over the false water and exchange coded flashes with something below. During pale months, when pigment is withdrawn and the world turns ivory, footprints occasionally emerge from the basins and cross the plain without any visible maker.

Deepest of all are the slow beings of the machine strata.

They are known only by their cycles: village-sized pistons moving once every few days, pressure chambers inhaling entire atmospheres, black structural fibers tightening in sequences that take decades to complete. It is tempting to call these organs, but their motions occasionally diverge from the needs of the surface. One piston pauses to avoid crushing an intruding root. Another repeats a stroke that sends no material anywhere. Across centuries, separate mechanisms synchronize, then fall out of rhythm as though conversing.

Perhaps they are ancient machines being digested.

Perhaps they are the surviving builders, too large and slow to recognize the quicker life above them. A complete exchange between two such entities might last longer than a civilization: one compression as greeting, a century of resin flow as reply.

And somewhere—distributed through all these forms—there is an inhabitant still being made.

The Prairie prepares for it without possessing a finished image. Doorways grow before rooms. Tools appear before hands. Towers produce signals for which no receiver is known. Vast buds travel toward inaccessible horizons carrying folded architectures, atmospheres, hereditary codes, and carefully preserved emptiness. When the seam opens in the sky and the sideways vegetation becomes visible, every flower turns toward it with the unified attention of a single creature recognizing movement beyond its shell.

The future inhabitant may be the Prairie after it has escaped its present dimensions. It may be whatever waits on the other side. It may be a body large enough to wear this whole fabricated continent as one leaf.

Until then, everything living there serves provisionally as one of its organs: the regional minds and their quiet wars, the feltlings manufacturing their own necessity, the stilted carriers of foreign landscapes, the translucent sky-skins, the rooted attendants, the severed wanderers, the reflections under false lakes, and the engines whose thoughts require centuries.

They do not merely inhabit the Prairie.

They are the many ways the Prairie attempts to inhabit itself.
