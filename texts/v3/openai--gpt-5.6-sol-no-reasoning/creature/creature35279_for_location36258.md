Nothing lives there yet.

Or rather, what lives there is not a creature but the residuum of making: craft severed from the hands that once might have performed it. The gestures themselves inhabit the place.

They have no bodies. They appear through consequences—a thumb pressed into clay, a cord drawn taut around an absent scaffold, the regular percussion of a mallet striking where no arm can swing. They do not think, hunger, reproduce, or know one another. Yet they persist, choose among available seams, repeat favored motions, and alter their surroundings. In a world without organisms, this is as close to life as anything has come.

Some gestures are brief and local. A **Lifter** stirs beneath a submerged slab, raising it just enough for water to enter. A **Turner** rotates the slab and leaves it at an angle from which an entirely new district may eventually be measured. **Binders** move through walls as pressures in the joints, drawing courses together. **Strikers** announce themselves in sequences of evenly spaced taps. **Polishers** pass over dark glass and leave behind surfaces that reflect structures not yet present.

Others are distributed across enormous distances. A single **Carrier** may consist of thousands of simultaneous events: basins emptying, channels filling, stairs becoming wet, counterweights descending in shafts below several skies. What it carries is not necessarily material. It may transport heat from one horizon to another, move “down” through a system of arches, or bear an unfinished hour into a district where time has stalled.

These names are conveniences. They do not designate separate beings. A gesture can divide, merge with another, or change its nature according to what it acts upon. Lift repeated in clay becomes construction. Lift applied to water becomes weather. Lift applied to distance raises a faraway tower until it stands beside the observer. The same inhabitant may therefore be present as a rising wall, an upward rain, and a horizon drawing near.

The mineral colonies are their most nearly embodied offspring.

White salts branch where the warm water withdraws. Lime tubes gather at vents and open to pressure. Iron reddens around stone nails. Glazes spread toward heat and fracture rivals at their edges. These formations have no ancestry and contain no impulse toward survival, but they behave as though the unfinished world has begun rehearsing biology with the materials at hand.

Among them, the black fractures are the most animal-like. During periods of heat they travel across walls from joint to joint, pausing at bad mortar, dividing at intersections, avoiding cold stone. At night they drink clear water and vanish. What returns the following day may not be the same fracture, though it often follows part of the old route. Their trails form maps of stresses that no longer exist and stresses not yet installed. Occasionally several converge around a blank section of wall. By morning a doorway stands there.

Deeper down live—or operate—the great arrangements.

They cannot be seen whole. One encounters a counterweight descending past an opening, a ceramic beam flexing over darkness, a wheel revolving in dry air. Each is simple enough to understand. Together they form something too extensive to be called a machine, because a machine has an edge, a purpose, and a distinction between itself and the world it changes.

This has none.

The planetary apparatus may be the oldest inhabitant, or the place may merely be its visible activity. Its capstans wind no ropes; instead, tension appears elsewhere. Its wheels lift no water; rather, water acquires the possibility of being lifted. Its inclined planes exchange positions until one district becomes higher than another. It does not construct objects so much as install the rules by which objects can temporarily exist.

If it possesses anything resembling metabolism, it consumes indeterminacy. Warmth, loose distance, uncommitted gravity, and matter not yet hardened enter its motions. Walls, basins, rainfall, sequence, and direction emerge. These products do not last. They loosen back into possibility and are taken up again.

The cycle leaves no corpses, only ruins—and even ruins remain active.

A broken arch sheds echoes. A split cistern releases containment. A dismantled bridge allows separation to multiply between two shores. Such failures are not death but dispersal: a local rule escaping the structure that held it. For a time afterward, the rule may wander. Containment may settle over an open plaza and preserve a pocket of air beneath the rain. An echo without an arch may move across the water, repeating a sound that has never been made. Separation can accumulate in a narrow alley until its opposite walls become unreachable from each other.

These loose properties are perhaps the place’s smallest inhabitants.

They gather in hollows and under lintels. Elevation sleeps in staircases. Reflection collects in black basins. Direction follows channels even when they are dry. Duration clings to fresh mortar and makes it old before it hardens. A room provides temporary shelter for interiority; when its walls depart, the inside sometimes remains—a cubic absence standing alone in the marsh, rain bending around it.

The unfinished districts contain stranger populations. There, possible structures coexist without agreeing which will become real. A tower may inhabit its reflection before it inhabits the shore. Several descents may occupy one falling drop. Sounds arrange themselves into stairs that can bear weight only while they continue. Warmth takes the shape of walls. Shadows wait for buildings to arrive.

Most of these provisional beings are short-lived. They resolve when touched by rain or crossed by a newly laid boundary. Others migrate into completed districts and hide as discrepancies: an extra step, a corridor longer inside than outside, a reflection whose tower has one more window than the tower above it.

There may also be something beneath the amphitheater.

It is known only by seven measured blows.

Calling it a mason would be too simple. No figure has ever emerged from the warm sockets below the platform. No hand holds the mallet. Perhaps the taps are not made by an entity but are the entity itself: seven impacts recurring across different ages and locations, briefly gathering beneath the seats when a submerged city is ready to be shown.

The inverted city may be its body.

For those few minutes when the basin rises, roofs and alleys hang beneath the lifted water like organs exposed under glass. Chimneys exhale vapor downward. Narrow streets lead deeper into the sky below. Nothing moves through them, yet their proportions imply occupants: doors at hand height, steps worn in advance of feet, windows placed for eyes that do not exist.

The city seems to anticipate life without understanding it. It builds places to stand, gather, sleep, watch, enter, and leave. It makes theaters before audiences, roads before journeys, cisterns before thirst. Fingerprints appear because making requires pressure shaped like a finger, not because fingers have pressed there. Every structure is an expectation generated from rules whose fulfillment has not arrived.

Thus the deepest inhabitant may be **expectancy** itself.

It waits in empty seats and unfinished doorways. It gives excessive importance to a lone bridge. It arranges poles as though something might pass among them, builds gates where there are no borders, and lays plazas broad enough for multitudes in a world that has never contained one body. It is not conscious hope. It is vacancy made specific—the difference between emptiness and a place prepared to receive.

For now, no receiver comes.

The gestures continue in lieu of inhabitants. Salts branch. Fractures forage. Gravity flows through its channels. Invisible cords tighten the weather. Cities are assembled beneath surfaces and moved elsewhere before they can be entered.

And everywhere the world practices the first conditions of life: persistence, boundary, exchange, repetition, error.

It has not yet learned the final gesture—the one by which matter ceases merely to be made and begins, somehow, to make itself.
