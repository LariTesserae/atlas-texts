The town itself lives there.

It is young enough that its parts have not yet agreed on a single shape. The towers are its upright organs, sounding through water rather than air. The ceramic streets are vessels and nerves. The cistern plants are soft valves, swelling to restrain a flood and collapsing when their work is done. Its writing is less a language than a changing pattern of thought: instructions passed from one empty chamber to another, revised whenever the whole organism pulses.

The mosses, ferns, and bladdered gardens belong to it in the way a skin’s flora belongs to a body. They are true plants, but unfinished ones—life admitted only in its rooted, permeable form. They drink, fold, drift, and decay without mouths or moving intermediaries. Invisible boundaries shepherd them away from doors and streets, preserving the clean channels through which the town senses rain. Nothing here hunts. Nothing mates on the wing, burrows, swarms, or carries seed from place to place. Animal life has either not yet been invented, or has been deliberately excluded.

The nine shelters imply intended inhabitants, though not human ones. Whatever they were made for would sit low and compressed, with shoulders or limbs reaching backward to the handholds and some sensitive structure resting in the shallow alcove above. There should be nine of them. The city keeps their places empty as faithfully as it keeps water moving inward.

Perhaps they have not arrived.

Perhaps they are being made.

Each night the town adds depth beneath itself, manufacturing not merely room but a direction in which something can exist. The black basin accepts rain and translates it into distance. The delayed knocks in the walls are the by-product—or the reply. Far below, beyond any chamber yet completed, nine bodies may be assembling layer by layer from vanished water, mineral lace, warmth, and pressure. The changing inscriptions may record their progress. The shelters adjust in anticipation of anatomies that are not yet settled.

Or the knocks may come from a single resident: the thing for which the entire settlement is an egg.

In that reading, the oval boundary is a shell impressed into stone. The towers regulate its growth. The streets distribute nourishment. The plants form a provisional membrane. The central basin is an umbilicus opening not downward through existing earth, but into the depth the town creates. Every storm feeds the occupant, and every new step marks its incremental descent toward birth.

It does not knock to be admitted. It knocks because, beneath the lowest manufactured layer, it is learning where its limbs will go.

So what lives there is mostly potential: a civic organism tending a garden without animals, preserving nine bodily absences around a lightless mouth. It waits beneath a world that can touch it only as rain. And farther down—always farther down because the town continually makes farther down—something is becoming capable of returning the touch.
