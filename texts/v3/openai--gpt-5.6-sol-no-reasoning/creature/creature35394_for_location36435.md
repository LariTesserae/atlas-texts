The people who live here are called, in municipal language, residents. Among themselves they more often say **the present**.

The distinction is practical. Residence is not guaranteed by birth, property, or bodily continuity. It must be maintained. A person is present if the city still accepts their weight, if their shadow remains attached, if their address can be reached under at least one current condition, and if their reflection has not begun living too far ahead of them.

They are compactly built people, though whether this is hereditary or architectural is unclear. Generations of low lintels and contracting passages have encouraged a slight forward inclination. Their shoulders turn easily. Their hands are narrow, callused at the fingertips, and exceptionally sensitive to vibration, temperature, and the minute movement of walls. Even children can feel when a stair has added a tread or when a room is preparing to become farther away.

Their faces have the pallor of limited sun, overlaid by the red tracery of constant cold. Hair is worn short or bound close. Loose strands can be mistaken by certain doorways for cracks. Most adults have faint vertical marks at the brow and chin where they compare themselves against plumb lines during morning inspection.

Their clothes are sober, layered, and densely annotated. Coats carry stitched grids, weighted hems, reflective tabs, and narrow seams of copper cloth. Cuffs show the wearer’s registered temperature. Collars bear detachable name strips, since names sometimes transfer to nearby materials. A person whose name has adhered to a stair rail may continue daily life under a provisional designation until it can be recovered, but touching the rail is considered intimate.

Children wear louder colors than adults: rust, mineral blue, condensation green. This is not indulgence. They are easier to retrieve from complicated backgrounds.

No one runs unless pursued by weather.

Haste offends too many streets, and abrupt motion can cause a body to arrive before its admissible place has formed. Walking is therefore measured without being slow. Residents pause at corners, listen to the paving, and allow a moment for the next section of city to consent to them. At thresholds they lower a small plumb bob, not merely to test gravity but to judge depth. Friends meeting in public do not immediately embrace. First they compare shadows.

Hospitality is formal. A host says, “This room includes you,” and touches the nearest load-bearing surface. The guest answers, “For the present depth.” Only then do they remove their coat.

Homes are crowded, but privacy is abundant because adjacency can be revised. A family may occupy four rooms sharing one wall and still require half a day to visit one another. Kitchens are usually kept reciprocally near: whatever else changes, everyone should be able to reach the kettle. Sleeping rooms are permitted more distance, especially for adolescents, whose dreams have a documented tendency to generate unauthorized corridors.

Beds are low and fixed to two walls. Shelves tilt slightly inward so that falling objects remain in the household. Cups are marked with the year they were set down. If one begins a prolonged descent, no one catches it; interference may convert a harmless slow fall into an inherited accident.

Meals are hot, salted, and moist. Residents eat dark breads, pale fungi grown in warm masonry, cistern greens, algae sheets, root knots cultivated in pockets of old soil, and small translucent eggs collected from condensation gardens. Broths are common because their surfaces reveal changes in orientation before instruments do. At formal meals, a shallow black bowl is placed at the center of the table. No one drinks from it. It represents the Near River and receives the first drop spilled.

Water-keeping is the nearest thing the city has to a universal vocation.

Every household tends cups beneath known drips. Every district records the taste, interval, and apparent origin of its condensation. Children learn the genealogies of cisterns: which roofs feed them, which pipes merely seem to, which reservoir pool may return an object entrusted to a gutter. To waste water is regrettable. To redirect it carelessly is dangerous. The worst insult in the city is to say that someone has “taught water badly.”

The most respected citizens are not rulers but **continuity workers**: surveyors, threshold readers, condensation clerks, structural listeners, map-breakers, and keepers of conditional routes. They wear gray coats with white reference stitching and carry cases of tuning forks, salt chalk, ceramic gauges, and folded sheets of deliberately inaccurate paper used to distract unstable streets.

They begin before dawn. A continuity worker may spend an hour establishing whether a familiar doorway still opens into its registered room, whether the room remains inside its building, and whether the building is prepared to assume responsibility for anyone entering it. They mark safe thresholds with a short horizontal line. A vertical line means *pass singly*. A circle means *the destination is present but not yet here*. An unmarked door is not necessarily unsafe, but only visitors treat uncertainty as an invitation.

Builders form a separate and older profession, though they rarely build in the conventional sense. They are attendants to structures under formation. They brace a room that has appeared before its walls, lay foundations beneath inherited pressures, and negotiate between dream plans and municipal codes. Their tools are mostly receptive: frames that catch angles, basins for collecting load, blank bricks into which a wall can continue its intention.

A builder never says, “We will put a house here.” They say, “Something domestic has begun.”

When the same floor plan recurs in enough dreams, dream auditors interview the sleepers independently. They ask about door swing, ceiling height, smells, the number of windows, and whether anyone was waiting inside. Agreement is treated as evidence of construction. Contradictions are not dismissed; they may indicate multiple floors. Only after the first physical feature emerges—a sill, a patch of wallpaper on an exterior wall, a persistent indoor draft—does the future building receive an address.

Some residents specialize in furnishing such places. They leave chairs where rooms are expected to meet them years later. They hang pictures on temporary air. They lay tables beneath suspended upper stories. This is regarded as both craft and courtesy. An arriving house should not find itself empty.

Government is conducted by overlapping offices whose jurisdictions are defined by condition rather than territory. There is an Office of Cold Interiors, a Ministry of Shared Walls, a Register of Premature Antiquities, and a small but influential Department of Nonreciprocal Neighbors. The Water Sequence has authority whenever the Near River joins. The Lamp Custodians govern during the interval between local darkness becoming impossible and morning being officially recognized.

Elections occur when the yellow lichen bands meet, though candidacies may begin several calendars earlier. Ballots ask not only whom one supports but from which direction and at what temperature. Results are considered binding only in districts able to reach the count.

Disputes are usually about access, sequence, or belonging. Property law is cautious around ownership. A person may own a room without owning the route to it. A building may own a staircase that has not reached it. Two families can inherit the same cellar from different centuries. In such cases, magistrates do not seek a single truth. They establish a schedule of truths unlikely to collide.

The city has little violent crime. Violence produces abrupt changes, and abrupt changes are distrusted. Its characteristic offenses are subtler: moving another person’s reference line, teaching a lane to lengthen around them, placing an object upstream of its history, or speaking a child’s full name near unregistered stone. The harshest sentences involve enforced obviousness. The condemned must live for a term in a room whose dimensions, entrances, and contents remain perfectly stable. Most find this unbearable within months.

Children are born in warm, interior chambers with four verified corners. For the first several days they are not shown mirrors. Their shadows are measured hourly and attached, if necessary, with a thread of black silk tied loosely around the ankle. Each child receives three names: a household name, a civic name, and a quiet name known only to those who were present at the birth. If one name detaches, the others keep the child locally continuous.

They play games that would alarm outsiders. They hide objects in one reservoir pool and wager where they will emerge. They race along contracting alleys at the precise speed that keeps them unchanged. They build miniature rooms from damp ceramic and place them against blank walls, hoping the city will adopt them. Sometimes it does.

They are forbidden to count windows aloud, but of course they do it in whispers. The danger is not the number. It is that windows, once counted, may begin counting back.

Schooling emphasizes sequence over arithmetic. Pupils memorize routes as conditional poems:

*After frost, before bells,  
take the stair that loses blue;  
if the courtyard has three skies,  
wait for someone older.*

They learn ordinary writing alongside plan notation, moisture script, and the marks used for things that exist only from one direction. History is taught by depth. The past is not assumed to be behind them. Some of it is below the reservoir, some inside thick walls, some waiting in rooms not yet attached to their buildings.

Adolescence is the most spatially dangerous period. Young people begin attracting unregistered distances. Their bedrooms develop extra corners. Mirrors show companions whom parents cannot see. A teenager leaving home may return older after an afternoon, or unchanged after a civic decade. Coming-of-age ceremonies therefore take place at the outer wall. The young person stands with one foot on city paving and one on the raw plain while relatives recite every address by which they have been known. They then choose which foot to lift.

Almost all choose the city.

The dead remain a matter of administration before they become a matter of grief. Death must be distinguished from departure, duplication, delayed presence, and architectural absorption. For three days the body is kept in a cold room while inspectors verify that its reflection has ceased independent activity and that no nearby building has incorporated its voice. Only then do mourners begin speaking in the past tense.

Bodies are not buried on the young plain; the ground has not learned to hold them. Nor are they placed in the city’s overfilled foundations. Instead, the dead are dried in warm ceramic chambers and reduced to pale mineral tablets. These are fitted into designated walls, where they help regulate condensation. Families visit after rain, when droplets gather over the tablets. To say the ancestors are weeping would be considered sentimental and hydraulically inaccurate, but no one wipes the water away.

Not all the dead accept this arrangement. Some persist as habits of rooms: a depression crossing a mattress, a kettle that prepares itself, a coat-shaped region in which frost will not form. These are tolerated if useful and addressed politely if not. A household ghost is less troubling than an unclaimed doorway.

Religion, where it can be separated from engineering, concerns completion.

Some believe the reflected city is the finished state toward which the present city is slowly constructing itself. In that place, every wall has arrived, every route is reciprocal, and every resident exists without condition. Others regard the reflection as a warning: completion is another word for immobility, and a perfect city would leave no room for the living.

The largest faith is practiced without temples. Its adherents place unfinished objects in niches—a glove with one finger unsewn, a cup lacking its handle, a sentence ending before its verb. They believe mercy enters through incompletion.

A smaller order venerates the Near River as the basin’s only honest inhabitant. Everything else, they say, pretends to have a fixed location. Water admits that lowest is a relationship, not a place. Members of this order serve as reservoir watchers. They sit at the black pools through dawn, recording currents beneath salt and retrieving what rises: keys, seeds, tools, letters, fragments of façades, occasionally a warm object from a building not yet present.

They never retrieve faces.

During full rain, the residents gather wherever the reservoir can be seen. They do not cheer. They stand under eaves in their reference-stitched coats and look down at the complete reflection.

Each person searches for themselves.

Many find only crowds. Some see an older figure at a lit window and recognize a posture, a scarf, the angle of a hand. Some see descendants. Some see no human counterpart but discover a tree growing where their home now stands. Builders study the finished roofs. Archivists memorize impossible avenues. Children wave, though they are told not to.

Sometimes the reflected crowds wave back.

The gesture is never simultaneous.

Afterward, when the pools divide and the complete city breaks apart, the people return to their tasks with a particular tenderness. They reset cups beneath conduits. They inspect walls not yet material. They warm the bricks that arrive cold. They continue staircases whose destinations may belong to someone else.

They are not trying to force the other city into being. They have seen what haste does to truth. Their labor is slower and more modest: to keep the present habitable while the future acquires weight.

As for the smooth, pale creatures in the conduits, technicians insist they are instruments because they appear wherever infrastructure is about to fail. They nose along pipe joints, press their translucent flanks to walls, and vanish into solid surfaces shortly before a leak, collapse, or change in adjacency. Residents leave ceramic dishes of salt for them anyway.

The vent birds are treated as weather. Their single dry click means that a warm route remains open somewhere above. When two answer one another, upper floors are evacuated.

The foxlike animal on the plain belongs to no registry. It is too sharply colored for the landscape, red-brown against the unfinished gray, and leaves prints only on surfaces not yet capable of taking them. It watches the empty place above the roofs with the patience of recognition.

Most inhabitants believe it can see the reflected city without rain.

A few believe it lives there.

When it comes, residents remain indoors but extinguish nothing. They stand behind their warm windows and watch it watch what they cannot. By morning it is gone, and somewhere in the basin a new doorway has appeared—often high in a wall, sometimes at pavement level, once floating upright over the Near River.

Continuity workers measure it. Builders brace it. Clerks assign a conditional address.

Eventually, someone dreams of the room beyond.

And because this is how one survives there, no one asks first whether the room is safe, or where it is, or whether it has already been occupied.

They ask what it needs in order to finish arriving.
