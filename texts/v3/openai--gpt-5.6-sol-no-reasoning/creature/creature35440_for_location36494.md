The basin is inhabited not by a single dominant creature but by a whole society adapted to life without exposed water.

Its principal citizens are the plants: pale, moisture-storing columns; candelabra-shaped euphorbias; smooth-barked thorn trees; bundled grasses; tight rosettes; silver-leaved mats; and low, armored shrubs. They stand close without standing randomly. Each mature plant commands an underground territory and shelters a small neighborhood around its roots—herbs in its slivers of shade, insects in its bark, reptiles on its warm stems, birds among its thorns, and mammals beneath the litter at its base. They drink from predawn vapor, hoard water in sealed tissues, open themselves to the air only at night, and exploit minerals seeping through the new volcanic crust.

The most conspicuous builders are termites and other mound-making insects. Their ocher structures rise among the vegetation like inhabited towers, ventilated by warm fissure-air and repaired continuously with grains, saliva, and shredded fiber. Beetles occupy their cooler passages or travel the packed streets outside, rolling dry vegetable matter. Ants maintain narrow routes between roots and seed beds. Flies, moths, and compact, heat-tolerant pollinators attend flowers that open briefly and often after dark.

Belowground lies another city. Hares, foxes, rodents, reptiles, and innumerable invertebrates use overlapping chambers in the soft ash. Some burrows follow warm mineral seams; others cluster beneath the anchoring roots of large succulents. Their occupants continually overturn the young substrate, mixing salt, ash, droppings, husks, and dead fiber into the first rudimentary soil.

Small antelope thread through the thorn districts. They are narrow-bodied and deliberate, with tough lips for selecting leaves between spines and kidneys adapted to conserve nearly every drop. They obtain water from fleshy shoots, waxy fruit, seeds, and browse. Dust-colored hares flatten beneath shrubs, ears laid back to avoid the thorns. Tortoises move through the lowest layer, cropping succulent pads and gathering fallen fruit. Their shells often carry the basin’s pale powder, making an animal at rest resemble a rounded stone.

Lizards hold the sunlit vertical surfaces. Broad-toed species cling to smooth plant columns, while slender ground hunters run along root walls and hard-packed paths. Snakes lie where those routes converge, patterned like broken crust or ash-shadow. Small foxes wait at burrow mouths in the evening, listening through the ground. Long-legged cats rest above eye level in the forks of thorn trees, nearly invisible until they unfold and descend. Raptors occupy the highest tier, circling over the haze or nesting in crowns that no terrestrial predator can easily enter.

Birds are numerous but seldom form a constant chorus. Compact seed-eaters move between grass heads, climbing rather than flying whenever possible. Insect hunters dart beneath the porous canopy. Pale nocturnal birds emerge after sunset to take moths above the flowers. Many communicate with short notes, wing flicks, throat patches, or tapping signals suited to dense stems and limited sightlines.

Nearly every inhabitant is quiet, economical, and local. Few travel far because each plant-ring offers only a small, dependable allotment of food, shade, and moisture. Bodies tend toward dust colors, narrow profiles, sealed skins, large ears, waxy coverings, or nocturnal habits. Waste is scarce: dung becomes beetle food, shed skin feeds mites, fallen stems become walls, and carcasses disappear into ants, foxes, fungi, and the mineral ground.

Together these organisms are the basin’s city and its first soil-forming force. Roots stabilize newborn scarps. Grasses catch airborne dust. Termites bind loose grains. Burrowers aerate compacting ash. Succulents colonize fresh cracks, and thorn communities gather around them. When the crust rises, sinks, or splits, the inhabitants do not abandon it; they rebuild their territories around the alteration.

Nothing here waits for the landscape to mature. The inhabitants are already turning raw geology into habitat—floor by floor, root by root, grain by grain.
