The people of the plateau are slight beside their city’s other inhabitants. They build its towers, regulate its pumps, and keep its ledgers, yet appear almost incidental among the mantled parapets and moving avenues. They have learned not to measure possession by visibility. A roof blackened with resting wings is still a roof; a boulevard occupied for three months by a migrating herd is still a boulevard. Ownership means maintaining passage, not excluding other bodies.

They are compact, broad-footed people, shaped as much by custom as ancestry for wet stone, steep stairs, and low-ceilinged works. Their skin is often marked by mineral water: pale scaling at the knuckles, rusty traces beneath nails, darkened creases where dissolved metals collect. Hair is kept short or sealed beneath waxed hoods so conduit runners cannot nest in it. Heavy coats have smooth shoulders and high collars. Their hems are clipped above the ankle, and their soles are layered from shell-fiber and resin, soft enough not to alarm vibration-sensitive animals.

Nearly everyone carries a small bell. The common ones are dull ceramic pieces tuned to carry through pipe galleries without provoking the larger creatures below. A person’s bell identifies their general purpose: a descending pair of notes for pump workers, a dry triple click for veterinary crews, a continuous low tremor for those permitted inside herd corridors. Children wear shapeless rattles until they learn the city’s acoustic etiquette. To move silently underground is not stealth but negligence.

Their senses are trained toward changes an outsider might miss. They feel pressure shifts in their teeth. They recognize a choked drain by the altered clicking of shell colonies. They distinguish safe condensation from corrosive seepage by smell and by the color it leaves on glazed brick. Conversations pause when a buried call passes beneath a room. Cups tremble; lamps swing slightly; everyone waits, counting the interval before the answering vibration. Then speech resumes.

No one knows precisely how many nonhuman species inhabit the city. Classification follows utility more often than descent. There are openers, strainers, scourers, warmers, borers, carriers, sealers, grazers, and pressure-beasts. The same creature may receive different names in different districts, especially if its role changes with age.

The pale shell-creatures in the gutters are called stitchers. Each anchors itself across a drainage groove, leaving a narrow passage between its valves. In dry weather they close into knuckled chains. When rain comes, they open one after another, filtering suspended grit and combing dangerous mineral flakes from the flow. Their colonies resemble ornamental borders laid by a meticulous mason. In fact they arrange themselves, though municipal workers guide new settlements with lines of feed paste.

The thumb-sized conduit runners are blind or nearly so. Their translucent armor reveals pulses of dark fluid inside them. They move in ceaseless circuits through dedicated wall channels, consuming grease, shed skin, spilled nutrient gel, and softer-bodied pests. Where their traffic is healthy, the walls tick gently. Silence inside a conduit is an emergency. It may mean poison, heat, flooding, or the approach of a predator too large to belong there.

Those predators roost on towers: long-winged, narrow-headed animals whose folded bodies resemble iron finials. They drop vertically into the streets, turn at impossible angles, and snatch runners from openings without touching the masonry. Their nesting platforms are part of the electrical grid. Heat from ceramic insulators incubates their leathery eggs, while their droppings—caustic and metal-rich—are collected in troughs and processed into etching compounds.

The broad gliders beneath elevated roads are gentler. They hang in overlapping ranks, six or seven limbs clasped to beams, their membranes folded into ridged fans. At dusk they release themselves and coast through the canyon districts, feeding on airborne swarms and licking condensation from cliff walls. Builders leave gaps in bridgework for their launch paths. A bridge without gliders is considered suspect: too dry, too cold, chemically fouled, or close to structural failure.

The great gray avenue animals are known collectively as cushion-foot herds. Adults stand taller than a person at the shoulder. Their six legs do not move in alternating pairs but in a rolling sequence that gives them the appearance of heavy vessels riding a swell. Their heads are low and blunt, protected by plates polished smooth by rain and contact with barriers. They graze not on vegetation but on nutrient salts painted onto designated pavements and on microbial films cultivated inside porous feed blocks.

They are calm until forced to turn sharply. For that reason streets intended for them have no corners, only long curves. They follow variations in pavement texture through the sensitive pads of their feet. A rough band means slow; glazed transverse ribs mean cross; warm ceramic indicates shelter; cold iron warns of a drop. Their routes may remain occupied for years. Businesses along them develop serving hatches above head height and retractable walkways between upper floors.

The flying swarms are not a single kind of animal but a temporary society of many kinds. Some are no larger than filings. Others have wings as wide as a hand. They gather around thermal shafts because each species benefits from the turbulence made by the rest. Dust-feeders consume mineral aerosols. Predators take dust-feeders. Heavy-bodied carriers transport larvae and symbionts between distant colonies. Together they form the slow dark columns that turn above the city.

When they settle, towers seem to change material. Cornices soften. Statues acquire trembling outlines. Whole domes become gray, brown, or black according to the dominant season. Their combined heat alters the building’s internal drafts, so heating crews track swarm density as carefully as weather. Old apartment blocks may depend on them through winter. Residents tolerate the noise and the steady fall of cast-off wings because an empty facade can become dangerously cold.

Beneath all of these are the aquifer colonies, whose status remains unresolved. Scraped from well walls, they come away in layered sheets: gray on the surface, red beneath, sometimes veined with luminous yellow where sulfur is abundant. They contract from heat, heal after cutting, and alter their chemistry when pumps approach. They do not possess recognizable organs. Some scholars call them a consortium, arguing that no individual exists. Well crews speak of them as if each aquifer were one slow animal extending through the rock.

Workers who tend the combs are stained permanently by them. Their profession is prestigious but isolating. Months are spent in warm chambers below ordinary streets, listening to machinery and to the minute tearing sound of harvest blades. They learn where a well can be scraped deeply and where only the upper film may be taken. Overharvesting does not merely reduce yield. It changes water chemistry, sickens filter colonies, drives runners from the walls, and may cause a distant district to lose its animals years later.

For this reason, the city’s true governors are less its elected councils than its hydraulic offices. Their maps combine pipe pressure, mineral content, animal migration, rainfall, heat loss, and structural strain. A proposal to open a market or repair a tramline may require predictions extending fifty years. Orders are often conditional: a gate may open after the third herd passes, provided the western cistern remains below its black mark and the deep pressure-calls continue at no more than nine-hour intervals.

Their law does not grant animals personhood, nor does it reduce them to property. It recognizes occupation. A colony established long enough acquires a claim to temperature, surface area, water chemistry, and access. A herd acquires a corridor by repeated use. People acquire their claims in much the same way. Disputes are therefore arguments over tolerances: how much noise, how much salt, how many interruptions, how narrow a passage can become before use is equivalent to obstruction.

Courts employ listeners who place metal rods against walls and floors. They testify about movement hidden inside the masonry. A colony can be represented by its sounds, its residues, the age of its attachments, and the flow records surrounding it. Important judgments may hinge on a line of calcite proving that seepage existed before a building did.

Children grow up intimate with small, manageable species. School courtyards maintain stitcher gutters, filter basins, and runner walls. Pupils learn to clear channels without crushing anchors, to recognize mating phases when colonies become irritable, and to stand still when a cushion-foot herd loses its guide texture. They also learn the oldest rule in its practical form: living matter must not choke the water, but sterile water is a sign of catastrophe.

Their games imitate pumps, sluices, and migrations. They build little cities of wet ceramic, seed them with harmless shell-creatures, then negotiate where the water should go. A child who makes the most elaborate structure often loses; success belongs to the one whose channels remain open after the animals enter.

Death follows the same logic. Bodies are wrapped in perforated mineral cloth and lowered into digestion wells assigned by district chemistry. Families visit not a grave but an outlet. Weeks or months later, a measured portion of recovered salts may be incorporated into roof glaze, lamp ceramic, feed blocks, or a household cistern lining. The custom is neither described as reincarnation nor disposal. The dead have entered circulation. To preserve a body unchanged would be to remove it from civic life.

Their religions, where they exist, are wary of purity. Clear water is valued, but perfectly empty water is feared. Stillness is associated not with peace but blockage. Sacred images tend to be pierced, porous, or articulated so that rain can pass through them. Shrines are placed beside valves and inspection chambers. Offerings are soluble: salt wafers, oils, powdered shell, slips of paper that soften and vanish.

Yet most pauses before sluices are not prayers. They are acts of attention. A worker rests a hand on the lever, listens for the pipe’s answering tone, watches nearby stitchers close, checks that no runner traffic crosses the outlet, and only then releases the water. Outsiders see ceremony because competence here is slow and formal.

The beings in the deepest reservoirs are treated with even greater caution. They have no single accepted name. Municipal records call them channel-maintaining megafauna. Pump crews call them the underbacks. In household speech they are often simply the low ones.

An underback may be several kilometers long, though no one can prove where one body ends and another begins. Their exposed surfaces look less like flesh than soaked geology: dark folds plated with mineral crust, ridges carrying pale filter colonies, cavities that open and seal over hours. They possess great muscular mouths or boring fronts capable of ingesting aquifer film and soft deposit. Behind them, channels remain wider, smoother, and newly oxygenated.

They move rarely by human standards. A single turn may take days. Their passage redistributes entire underground lakes. Air is forced through shafts ahead of them, producing warm gusts in cellars and the deep calls heard across the plateau. Some of those sounds are respiratory. Others may be communication carried through water and stone.

The city cannot command them. It can only tempt, delay, and accommodate. Nutrient slurry is released into selected caverns. Pumps create gradients of pressure and warmth. Old channels are closed so deposits will soften elsewhere. Even then an underback may ignore decades of preparation and force itself through an unfavored district, cracking reservoirs and flooding foundations while preventing a much greater sealing of the aquifer.

No living witness sees more than a fraction of one. Knowledge is assembled across generations from pressure records, fragments of shed plate, scars inside caverns, and the testimony of lift operators who glimpsed a moving surface far below. Their migrations are mapped as broad uncertainties, not lines. Neighborhoods celebrate when one passes safely beneath them, though the celebration may occur months after the first tremor and years before the final pressure change.

Not everything in the city is harmonious. Colonies overrun heat exchangers. Swarms strip insulating paste from cables. Scavengers enter homes and eat leather, glue, hair, or sleeping pets. Herds panic. Predators take children on rare occasions. Aquifer harvests fail. Industrial poisonings can sterilize kilometers of living pipework, leaving districts eerily silent.

There are exterminators, but their work is closer to controlled redirection than eradication. They alter heat, vibration, salinity, and surface texture. True poisons are a last resort because death travels with water. A toxin placed in one wall may descend through stone, enter the nutrient harvest, and return years later in feed distributed to the opposite side of the plateau.

The most despised crimes are therefore acts of careless sterilization: dumping solvents into seepage, sealing a live channel without surveying it, electrifying a colony wall, flushing concentrated waste during a migration. Such acts threaten no creature alone. They tear holes in relationships no office fully understands.

The citizens are not sentimental about this entanglement. They slaughter animals, scrape colonies, render shells, harvest eggs, and divert herds away from valuable districts. They wear glider membrane, eat fermented runner paste, and burn gas produced from deep life. What distinguishes them is not gentleness but an ingrained refusal to imagine any extraction as isolated.

Their meals are dense, sour, mineral, and oily. Gray aquifer cake is sliced thin, charred on iron plates, and dressed with salts cultivated by filter animals. Translucent eggs are steamed in cistern heat. Rendered fat is whipped with acidic water into pale foams. There are no fruits, grains, herbs, or spices. Flavor comes from fermentation, smoke, metal ions, and the secretions of specialized animals. Affluent households prize old cultures maintained in porous ceramic jars, some said to descend from vats begun centuries ago.

Markets are built in tiers. Humans occupy the middle galleries, scavengers patrol beneath slatted floors, and swarm screens collect edible bodies overhead. At closing, vendors retract everything they wish to keep. Hard-shelled cleaners emerge from wall slots and move through the aisles, consuming scraps and polishing residue from the stone. Before dawn they follow salt trails back into their pens.

Domestic life is likewise shared. Filter creatures cling inside fountain mouths. Small warming colonies grow behind kitchen stoves. Flat shell-pets sleep against cisterns and click when water chemistry changes. Wealthy houses may keep ornamental gliders in tall internal courts, bred for pale membranes or unusually resonant calls. Poorer ones cultivate runner nests for heat and waste removal, tolerating their nocturnal ticking inside the walls.

Architecture is never considered finished. A building is born raw, then altered by water and occupation. Its gutters acquire stitchers. Its warm faces attract swarms. Salt grazers round its carvings. Seepage deposits curtains over service doors. Residents add rails, collars, ramps, nesting ledges, and sacrificial blocks. After a century its living plan may differ completely from the architect’s drawing, and this is generally regarded as maturation rather than damage.

The people themselves think in similarly extended spans. Few expect to see the completion of major works. A reservoir begun by one generation may be sealed by the next, colonized by the third, filled in the fourth, and opened as housing by the seventh. Contracts name descendants, guilds, or districts rather than individuals. Promises are written against cycles of rain and migration: after two western herd returns; before the next high mineral phase; for as long as the underback continues to use the southern channel.

Their histories are recorded not only in archives but in wear. A hollow stair proves traffic. A polished rail proves the height of an extinct herd. Black manganese fans indicate a forgotten water route. Calcite over a doorway gives a minimum age for abandonment. Historians read buildings by sectioning deposits and comparing the trapped shells, hairs, wing cases, and industrial soot of different eras.

Some of the city’s former inhabitants survive only as architecture. There are channels sized for runners far larger than those now living, towers with nesting apertures no modern glider can enter, and immense worn grooves across plazas that no known herd could have made. Whether these species vanished, changed, or migrated beyond the plateau is unknown. The city preserves their accommodations long after forgetting their shapes.

At night, its human inhabitants retreat behind grilles and thick walls, but the city does not become theirs. Scavengers spread across the markets. Gliders cross between canyon bridges. Runners pulse through the conduits. Swarm layers lift and recombine above the towers. Far below, an underback leans into a narrowing cavern, and pressure rises in a thousand household pipes.

People wake to that pressure. They place their palms against damp walls. They listen to bells answering in the passages and to wings accumulating overhead. Then they open valves, feed stoves, check drains, consult migration notices, and step into streets already occupied by older, slower purposes.

They live there not as masters of a leafless world, but as one more managed circulation within it: brief, warm bodies moving between rain and stone, maintaining the openings through which everything else continues.
