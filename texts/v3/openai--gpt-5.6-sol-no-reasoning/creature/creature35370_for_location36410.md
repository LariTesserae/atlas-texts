The basin is inhabited less by separate creatures than by a continuous, finely divided metabolism.

Most of its residents are minute: clear larvae that hinge through the shallows, burrowing threads that recoil from passing shadows, pale fish moving as one reflective body, and crustaceans so numerous that the submerged rock appears to breathe. Hairlike organisms sleep in stone pores until rain wakes them to harvest dissolved metals. Eggs, spores, translucent grazers, and colonies resembling torn green tissue drift between pools. The water itself seems granular with lives too small to hold a shape for long.

The air supports another population just as dense and nearly silent. Blue needle-insects, golden discs, soot-dark flecks, and bead-bodied fliers circulate around luminous flowers. Individually they are simple, brief animals; together they display an intelligence of arrangement. Their swarms assemble into hollow spheres, spirals, and many-sided chambers, hold these forms for a few moments, then relax into disorder. Whether they communicate, navigate some invisible contour, or momentarily become a larger organism is impossible to tell.

Larger creatures survive by resembling interruptions in the landscape. Eyeless, six-legged waders fold themselves into the mottling of rock and feed through drinking pores in their ankles. Broad mud-burrowers travel beneath the marsh, betrayed only by bubbles and the slow displacement of reeds. At the change of light, nearly weightless walkers emerge on fungus-fine legs, while soft gliders skim the water and thumb-sized animals scrape luminous films from stone. Predators close without cries; prey often approach them, drawn by pressure, warmth, chemistry, or needs older than caution.

Many inhabitants are bound to the white hoops on the northern rim. Ferns anticipate arrivals through them. Amphibians fasten their eggs where distorted duration suppresses rot. Mosses occupy boundaries measured not by moisture or shade but by fractions of time. Migrating insects pass into one hoop and out through another, sometimes from elsewhere entirely—another basin, another season, perhaps another version of the same place. Thus the marsh receives a continual immigration of misplaced pollen, foreign larvae, and creatures whose origins no longer exist nearby.

The abandoned mechanisms have inhabitants of their own. Roots pass through their fields. Nests gather in their protected margins. A small reed-weaving animal lives inside the old ceramic vessel beneath the overhang, arranging its home around the permanent bead of water drawn from the air. No species treats these remnants as ruins. To them, sideways-falling droplets and impossible passages are as natural as rainfall.

Even the collapsed circle of black petals sustains a dusk community. Nocturnal moths remain above it at noon, feeding and mating in the accumulated shadow. Darkness there is not merely an absence of light but something stored and returned, a resource gathered over centuries.

Nothing rules this place. Its life is distributed across swarms, films, burrows, pores, eggs, temporary bodies, and inherited behaviors. The closest thing to a dominant creature may be the ecology itself: an ancient composite organism made from rain, polished stone, obsolete machinery, and countless replaceable lives. It has no center and no single awareness. It remembers through instinct, migration, and the timing of flowers. It feeds through every mouth in the basin.

The beings that once made the hoops are gone, but something of their activity persists without intention. Their machines have entered the food web. Their distortions have become seasons. Whatever intelligence remains here is no longer housed in persons or structures; it exists in the accommodation among all things—in insects that expect doors through space, ferns that open before an arrival, and animals born knowing where gravity will briefly fail.

What lives in the basin is therefore not a civilization, nor merely a wilderness. It is a multitude that has inherited miracles and reduced them to habitat.
