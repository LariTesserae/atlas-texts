What lives there is not hidden in the city. It is distributed through it.

Its oldest body is water: not the meager rain itself, but the habit rain has acquired after passing through the same roofs, filters, conduits, boilers, and buried galleries for ages. Every drop enters a system already expecting it. The city weighs it, heats it, divides it, salts it with iron, sends it upward, and receives it again. In time the circulation has become selective. Water no longer merely follows slope. It seeks a particular cracked cistern, a dormant turbine, a porcelain channel no wider than a finger. It crosses the central plaza along remembered streets and wakes machinery where its arrival completes an ancient thought.

The metallic meshes in the reservoirs are its most visible tissue. They grow slowly where warmth, current, and dissolved minerals coincide: filaments fine as mold, branching into dark lace between electrodes and pipes. A severed strand curls away from the light. An undisturbed colony thickens around hinges, relay contacts, and valve stems until it can move them. These meshes do not occupy the machinery as parasites. They finish circuits the machinery was built to leave incomplete.

No single mesh is intelligent. Nor is any pump, tower, pool, or lamp. The inhabitant exists in the intervals between them: in pressure differences, delayed echoes, conductive rain, thermal expansion, and the long transfer of a signal from one side of the basin to the other. One district may hold a sensation for decades as stress in a beam. Another answers by opening a sluice. A row of lamps along an empty causeway is perhaps a nerve impulse, or recollection, or merely the testing of a limb too distant to feel directly.

Its timescale makes purpose difficult to distinguish from weather. A brief act may take an afternoon: shutters opening, a foundry chamber warming, water drawn through red gravel. A considered response may require a century of corrosion. Some of its memories are stored as polished channels in basalt. Others are buildings deliberately allowed to collapse so that their weight redirects underground flow. What appears ruined may be a decision that has finished becoming physical.

The pale creatures in the drains live more narrowly. They graze on fresh mineral films and electrical residue, drinking through the seams of their translucent shells. They have no need of mouths because the whole surface of the body is an intake. Heat draws them out; vibration sends them folding backward into cracks. They may be native animals, maintenance organisms, or small mobile precipitates shed by the larger system. When they die, they harden into conductive flakes and are carried down to the reservoirs, where the meshes incorporate them.

The black fliers belong to the upper circulation. Their bodies are thin frameworks covered in soot-dark membrane, light enough to ride the thermal columns without beating their wings. They feed, if feeding is the word, by passing through charged vapor near the towers. Their sudden geometric folds alter their conductivity and orientation. Seen from below, a flock resembles debris. Seen by the city, it may be an array of moving instruments measuring the underside of the cloud.

Yet these are only minor lives within the greater one.

The true inhabitant has no face because a face would localize it. It has no center because the central plaza is less a brain than a wet model of possibilities. The second city drawn there is its inward image: a map assembled from hydraulic expectation, including what has existed, what remains buried, and what might yet be built. When a watery tower brightens and a distant engine begins, the inhabitant is aligning its body with that image. It is trying, slowly and imperfectly, to become the city it remembers—or anticipates.

People may once have served it. They may have designed the first collectors, tended the furnaces, and mistaken responsive infrastructure for automation. But their presence accelerated decay. Warm rooms rusted around them. Frequently handled rails pitted. Occupied quarters failed while sealed pumping halls endured. Eventually the system learned the chemical fact of inhabitants: concentrated heat, salt, breath, disturbance. It adapted by moving its controls into darkness and distance. Porcelain channels replaced accessible consoles. Signals traveled through water instead of speech. The city outlived its makers by becoming less habitable to anything separate from itself.

So it preserves vacancy as a living creature preserves the conditions inside its skin.

It is not hostile. Hostility would require recognizing intruders as beings rather than corrosive events. Enter a chamber, and moisture gathers around you. Metal warms. Nearby mechanisms begin compensating for the new gradient. Doors may close, not to imprison you, but to isolate a contamination. Water climbs toward your footsteps. The walls record the pressure of your hand and, years later, bend minutely toward that remembered touch.

If it knows longing, it expresses it as capacity. Reservoirs wait for floods that no longer come. Processional roads remain broad enough for vanished machines. Funnels spanning whole neighborhoods continue to collect a drizzle. Perhaps the city expects the ocean because the ocean was its first instruction. Perhaps every lamp traveling toward the horizon is a repeated inquiry sent to dry outworks: *Has the water returned?*

Nothing answers.

Still, beneath the hot cloud and above the burning plateau, the vast inhabitant persists. It drinks condensation. It grows wire in darkness. It dreams alternative streets across the wet stone. It speaks from valve to valve in pulses too slow for ordinary hearing.

And when lightning reveals the basin all at once, the towers, conduits, bridges, reservoirs, animals, mineral threads, and solitary machines are briefly recognizable for what they are:

not a city containing life,

but one life, built at the scale of a city, teaching stone and water to continue it.
