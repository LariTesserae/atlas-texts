The inhabitants are the growths.

They have no eyes, mouths, blood, or recognizable bodies. Most exist as territories rather than individuals: a dark film occupying the rainward face of a wall, a lace of pale moss along ten thousand mortar joints, a branching algal forest filling a court from paving to parapet. Their boundaries overlap. Their generations grow through one another. What appears to be a single black-green mantle may be a multitude of colonies, each expanding by fractions along the masonry, meeting its neighbors without struggle because nothing here needs to flee and almost nothing can consume.

They live slowly enough that architecture is part of their anatomy.

The broad flags are their fields. Grooves of erased inscriptions hold their spores. Cracks are nurseries, and old sluices distribute fragments of them into chambers where they have never grown before. Some cling flat to exposed surfaces, shaped by the long pressure of passing water. Others rise in ribbons and soft branches where currents are weak. In roofless upper districts they form heavy canopies that bow beneath rainfall transmitted through the surface. When a cold layer spills over a terrace, whole gardens incline together and take months to stand upright again.

Near the light are the richest inhabitants: dense olive and black-green weeds, blunt-fronded mats, and mosses that pack the seams between blocks until wall and growth seem jointly constructed. They cover drowned towers in rounded pelts. They fill the mouths of cisterns. They turn colonnades into dim groves whose trunks were cut and raised by vanished hands. Their uppermost leaves sometimes lie just beneath the rainy surface, continually disturbed by impacts from a sky they cannot enter.

No grazers cut them back. Nothing bites holes in a leaf or tears a stem for shelter. Growth ends through burial, darkness, cold, or its own accumulated weight. Mats thicken until their lower layers are starved of light and collapse into brown pulp. New layers settle upon them. Thus the colonies make the little soil that exists, feeding upon the remains of predecessors that died without ever being eaten.

In the middle depths, their forms become broad, spare, and dark. Leaves spread close against ramps and sloping roofs to gather the last diluted light. Fronds orient themselves along currents so faint that no animal would have noticed them. A colony may take centuries to cross a doorway and longer still to descend a stair. When two meet, they do not wage visible war. One shades the other; one tolerates colder stone; one adheres more firmly when water begins to move. Their contests are settled in layers thinner than soot and on timescales longer than dynasties.

Some live almost entirely within the masonry. Threads penetrate softened mortar and follow damp joints from block to block. They emerge as pale margins around bricks, disappear into a wall, and return leagues away where the same buried course reaches open water. Whether these distant patches are parts of one inhabitant or descendants of it cannot be meaningfully decided. Separation here is often only a chamber closed for a few thousand years.

Below the reach of daylight live the faintest occupants. They are colorless or nearly so: slick mineral films, powdery veils, translucent colonies clinging to lead clamps and the tannin-dark surfaces of ancient beams. They subsist on what descends—dissolved remnants, particles of decayed vegetation, minerals loosened from mortar, traces carried through a joint when two basins slowly equalize. They do not illuminate the dark or animate it with hidden motion. They endure as stains that thicken, recede, and return.

From brighter levels descend long cords of communal growth. They hang through shafts and collapsed vaults, passing through hundreds of yards of black water before fastening to lower masonry. They resemble roots but belong to no tree. The upper portions gather light; the lower portions absorb what they can from silt and stone. As cells die, neighboring cells replace them, so the cord persists while no part of it is ancient. Some bridge entire architectural ages: anchored in the mortar of an upper terrace, threaded through the broken dome beneath it, and lost among foundational buttresses below.

These cords are among the few inhabitants to connect regions otherwise isolated by darkness. When a gate shifts and begins a new current, strands tear loose and drift through the opening. Most settle where they cannot survive. A few catch upon a sill, a hinge, or the rough underside of a vault. Centuries later, a pale patch appears there. Millennia later, if the light and water permit, the patch becomes a hanging field.

The rains sustain other, humbler populations at the boundary between water and air. Low mosses occupy the crowns of retaining ridges and the blunt remnants of towers. Shallow-rooted plants spread over sodden accumulations of grit, soot, and pulverized limestone. Their stems lie flat beneath the weather. They know no dry season; their roots are submerged even when their leaves emerge. During clearer intervals they darken almost to green, and when the clouds close they return to gray-brown anonymity.

Even these surface plants do not form a terrestrial world. They are temporary extensions of the drowned one. Water saturates their beds from below, rain presses them from above, and every hollow beside them is a pool descending through stairs and rooms into the interior ocean. When an embankment settles, whole meadows slip quietly beneath the surface. Their leaves decay in place, and aquatic films inherit them.

The only inhabitants that seem to move are pieces of these stationary lives. Spores drift through arcades. Torn ribbons revolve in the nearly imperceptible circulation of reservoirs. Bladders of gas lift dead mats until they strike a ceiling and spread there like drowned cloth. Fine green dust pours through a newly opened gate and settles over a district that has been bare since its construction. Motion belongs less to organisms than to water carrying their possibilities.

There may also be colonies sealed away: growth trapped behind gates that have not moved within the lifetime of the present surface gardens, or preserved in cavities where cold water excludes decay. A shifted counterweight can expose such a chamber. Its contents do not rush out. A dark stain enters the adjoining basin, scarcely distinguishable from sediment. Years later, unfamiliar films appear along the direction of the current. They are not monsters or invaders, only old lineages returned to contact after an interval too long to measure.

None of these lives perceive the structure that contains them. Yet together they disclose it.

They trace the joints between blocks. They gather in carved depressions and make erased writing legible as darker bands. They hang from arches, outlining vaults invisible in the gloom. They reveal seepage through walls by the direction of their growth. They mark the slow paths of currents across plazas and down stairways. The masonry gives them shape, and they, by covering it, make its shape briefly apparent.

There are no keepers of the gates, no builders surviving in hidden chambers, no pale swimmers in the trenches. No intelligence waits below. The immense works have only these patient tenants: films, fronds, mats, mosses, threads, and rain-beaten plants rooted in the residue of construction.

Their history has no battles, migrations, or extinctions in the ordinary sense. It consists of surfaces becoming available and then occupied. A parapet rounds; growth spreads over the new curve. A roof falls; colonies from the upper face are buried, while the shattered underside receives its first faint settlement. A sluice opens; two floras mingle. A district sinks beyond the light; its forests darken, collapse, and become nourishment for thinner lives.

Nothing watches these changes. Nothing remembers them. But the vegetation itself is a kind of record, laid down in soft layers over the harder record of stone.

The masonry was made once by vanished hands.

The inhabitants make it again, slowly, as something covered, bound, softened, and alive.
