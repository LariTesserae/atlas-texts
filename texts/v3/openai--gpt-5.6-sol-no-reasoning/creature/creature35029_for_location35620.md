The people who live on the high tables are not numerous enough to regard the plateaus as theirs. They inhabit particular hollows, springs, and crossings. The country between remains unnamed or bears only practical names: the Shelf That Sheds Stone, the Seven Wet Steps, the Place Where Voices Return Wrong.

They are compact, sure-footed people, though not all are born so. Balance is taught before walking. Infants are carried against the chest in close-woven slings, and toddlers wear short cords fastened to an adult’s waist whenever they leave a hut. By adulthood, they move with bent knees and a low center of gravity, touching the rock whenever the wind shifts. They seldom leap. A missed foothold is less dangerous than an unexpected gust.

Their hands are broad and heavily callused, with exceptional strength in the smallest fingers. Everyone learns knots. A child’s first serious possession is a length of cord, and one of the first games is to tie it correctly while an elder jerks and twists the other end. Poor knots are not mocked. They are quietly untied and made again until the fingers understand.

Clothing lies close to the body. Loose fabric belongs to signals, not garments. People wear layered tunics of waxed fiber, narrow trousers wrapped at the calf, soft-soled shoes with rough stone grit worked into the leather, and short hooded mantles for mist. Hair is braided flat or cropped. Jewelry is pierced stone or seed threaded tightly at the throat and wrist, where it cannot catch the wind.

They are not silent, but speech is local. Inside stone rooms, in sheltered groves, or during the dusk lull, conversation is abundant. Their languages favor deep vowels and hard consonants that can be felt through a floor or wall, though no language survives the full voice of the chasms. Outdoors they use hands, shoulder taps, pressure on a shared rope, and panels of dyed cloth. Many sentences have no spoken form. They belong entirely to the body.

A grip at the elbow means *I have you*. Two taps between the shoulder blades mean *the way behind is clear*. A palm laid flat on the crown of the head means *wait for the wind to change*. Hands crossed at the wrist mean not merely danger, but *the air is lying*—mist, echo, or depth has made sight unreliable.

Names are often given twice. One is spoken and used among the household. The other is a pattern of touch by which a person can identify themselves in darkness or noise. Close relatives may know a third, private name tied as a sequence of knots. After death, the spoken name is allowed to fade, but the knot-name is added to a family cord kept dry in a wall niche. Some of these cords are longer than the houses that protect them and must be coiled in stone-lined pits.

Their settlements are usually five or six households gathered around a seep. Kinship follows water more than blood. Anyone who drinks from a spring through three dry intervals becomes answerable for it: clearing its channel, covering it against debris, rationing it when flow weakens, and guiding strangers to it if they arrive in need. Refusing water is among the few acts considered irredeemable. Wasting it is treated less as sin than as evidence that a person is dangerously unwell.

No village expects permanence. When a spring narrows, families wait through one full cycle of storms. If it fails, they remove roofs, cordage, vessels, tools, seed stores, and door lintels, leaving only the fitted lower walls. Departure is not called abandonment. They say the house has gone ahead with them, because a house is understood to be its bindings and contents, not its stone.

They cultivate no broad fields. Instead they keep hundreds of small agreements with the landscape. A rain cup may be sown with fast-flowering grain. A damp crack may hold edible bulbs. A sheltered shelf may support three fruiting shrubs and no more. Mosses are scraped from only one side of a stone. Seed is scattered in dozens of basins because nine may fail and one may flood. Their gardens look accidental to outsiders, but every plant has been placed according to remembered wind and water.

Memory is therefore their chief wealth.

Some people specialize in routes. They remember where water stood after particular storms, which handholds become slick under mist, which bridges hum safely and which fall silent before a strand parts. They are called listeners, though much of their knowledge comes through their feet. On entering unknown ground, a listener may lie flat and press cheek, sternum, and palms to the rock. The plateau’s vibration reveals cavities, loosened shelves, distant falls, and weather moving in unseen chasms.

Others tend the suspended gardens. They descend on braided ladders, carrying baskets close against the spine. Weeks may pass on a ledge barely wider than a hut, pruning reeds, gathering medicines, and wrapping ripening seed cases before a storm can take them. Each ledge develops its own lineage of keepers. Over generations, their bodies acquire small differences: unusual tolerance for cold spray, long toes, lungs practiced at breathing in wet wind. Some rarely visit the summit and speak with gestures broader than those of the plateau settlements, designed to be read across vertical distances.

The bridge people belong to no single plateau. They live near anchor points and move continually along the great ropes, inspecting abrasion, replacing waxed bindings, and splicing new fiber into ancient load-bearing strands. They claim the bridge is not an object but an animal with an exceptionally slow metabolism. It eats cord, wood, resin, and labor. It sheds pieces into the gulf. Its groans indicate health or discomfort, and experienced keepers can distinguish the two through their bare feet.

No one crosses alone. Travelers form a line joined by a secondary rope, leaving enough distance that one person’s fall does not sweep away the rest. They move during favorable winds, pausing when the bridge twists broadside to the chasm. In cloud, each traveler keeps one hand on the main cable and one on the person ahead. Progress may continue for hours without sight.

Children born near the bridge sometimes sleep to its groaning and cannot rest in still places.

The plateau people do not divide animals neatly into ordinary and monstrous. Creatures are grouped by how they relate to weather. Lizards are rain-followers. The long-winged birds are wind-resters. The unseen heavy animals at the springs are deep drinkers. Whatever moves beneath the low clouds is called a bearer, because its shadow often seems to support the cloud from below.

A few listeners believe the bearers are vast, nearly weightless creatures with translucent hides stretched over hollow bones. Others say they are mats of airborne vegetation, thousands of buoyant seed bodies tangled together and temporarily alive as one being. On rare mornings, pale filaments are found draped over high monoliths. They contract when touched by sunlight and smell of wet stone. The people burn none of them and keep none.

The three-toed animals of the ravines are known more intimately. Their paths can sometimes be followed from one persistent spring to another, and offerings of salt-rich clay are left where their tracks appear. They seem to browse at night, cutting whole fans from the giant leaves. Once in a generation, someone glimpses one: a high-backed shape furred in dark moss, with a narrow head and forelimbs folded close to its chest. The sighting is not pursued. It is announced with a whistle call followed by three periods of silence.

More ubiquitous are the small beings that emerge with temporary water. Eggs or seeds—no one agrees which—lie dormant in the dust of potholes. Within minutes of rain, translucent bodies begin swimming there. Some have fins; some have jointed legs; some are green with tiny leaves growing beneath their skin. They feed, mate, lay shining cysts against the rock, and die before the pool drains. Their entire visible existence may last an afternoon.

The people call them the hurried. Children sit beside fresh pools to watch a lifetime pass. Funerary songs are sometimes patterned after their brief cycles: awakening, feeding, joining, laying down what will continue, and becoming dry.

Death on the plateaus is treated without the fiction of possession. Bodies cannot always be recovered from chasms, and graves are difficult to cut in bedrock. The dead are wrapped in old rope and placed in sheltered fissures where roots can reach them. If no body remains, the person’s weight in stone is carried from the settlement and stacked at a lookout they loved. Over years such memorials become low cairns. Travelers shelter behind them, add a pebble, and continue.

Those taken by the wind receive a different rite. At dusk, the household releases an empty seed case bearing the dead person’s touch-name in knots. It may fall into the canyon, cross to another plateau, or rise from sight. The destination is not interpreted. What matters is that the wind, which took, is made to carry.

Their religion—if it can be separated from weathercraft—is concerned less with gods than with attention. The oldest teachings hold that the world gives warning before nearly every danger, but rarely in a form a person expects. A breaking ledge may sing. A safe rope may abruptly cease vibrating. Water may taste of iron before a spring shifts. Birds may settle on the ground before an upward gale. Disaster is therefore understood as a failure of relationship as often as a failure of knowledge.

Yet they do not blame every victim. The land is too complicated for that cruelty. At memorials they say: *There was more happening than one body could hear.*

The carved rain cups are attended by elders who know their tones. During storms, water fills the basins and produces a sequence of notes, each shaped by depth, overflow, and wind. The music indicates rainfall on the summit, but also—in ways imperfectly understood—pressure changes in distant valleys. An elder may listen and order a bridge closed, though the sky above it is clear.

The cup-lines serve another purpose during certain nights. Long after rain has ceased, one cup will occasionally begin to hum. Then another answers farther along the stone. No visible water moves between them. People extinguish lamps, place their hands on the ground, and wait.

Sometimes nothing follows.

Sometimes hammer blows come from below.

Two blows are considered an echo. Three are an answer. Four mean that everyone nearest the rim must move inland before dawn.

No one knows who first established this reckoning. It appears carved beside the oldest cup-lines in signs no living reader can fully interpret. The deserted valley may once have held a larger population devoted to these exchanges. Its bird-shaped whistles are still collected after floods expose them, but they are never taken into houses. They are placed on high stones with their beaks toward the chasms.

When the distant whistle of another settlement arrives, people stop what they are doing. Even if no formal caller is present, someone answers. A child may do it, or a traveler, or the oldest person able to breathe strongly enough. The reply carries no news, promise, or request. In that landscape, presence is sufficient information.

At night, with every cord drawn taut and the stone humming underfoot, families lie in their weighted houses and feel the weather pass through the plateau. They distinguish rain from airborne mist by the pitch of the roof. They hear bridge notes from valleys away. They know when trees on the cliff face have turned their leaves, though they cannot see them.

And sometimes, among the ordinary tumult, they hear a familiar sequence: the rhythm of a dead worker’s hammer, a caller from an empty foundation, footsteps on a bridge no one is crossing.

They do not insist these are spirits. Neither do they dismiss them as echoes.

In a country where sound may travel for a season before returning, the past is not gone. It is still moving through the stone and air, delayed by distances no one can measure, looking for a living body patient enough to receive it.
