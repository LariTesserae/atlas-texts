What lives there is not one people but an assembly, layered as densely as the structure itself.

Nearest the cloud roof are the fruit bats. They spend the day compressed into upper vaults, clinging to carved ribs and one another until architecture and colony are difficult to distinguish. Their heat warms chambers no sun reaches. Their droppings sustain pale insects, fungi, and seedlings in the accumulated debris below. At evening they unfasten by the thousands and stream through broken arches toward the fruiting canopy.

Birds divide the rest of the heights. Swifts possess the open shafts and high galleries. Pigeons fill dry recesses. Parakeets quarrel in fig crowns, hornbills patrol the balustrades, and owls sit in vacant niches as though the stone had anticipated their shapes. Weaverbirds inhabit the space between growth and water, hanging their nests from vines over cisterns where many climbing predators hesitate to follow.

Macaques rule the middle levels. They do not understand the valley as a ruin, but they understand it minutely: every warm parapet, concealed well, fruiting court, dangerous bridge, and passage with no second exit. Their territories follow stairways and arcades rather than rivers or ridgelines. Knowledge of the structure passes between generations as routes, warnings, habits, and inherited claims.

Below them, deer graze the broad courts while wild pigs turn the shallow soils that have gathered over tile. Civets travel the narrow galleries. Porcupines den beneath fallen stairs. Crabs occupy drains and follow retreating water downward. Frogs wait in buried dampness for rain to reconnect the basins. Fish persist in the deep cisterns, isolated for part of each year and briefly joined when the old channels fill.

Leopards—or other dark forest cats—move through the upper vegetation and across the bridges. They are seldom seen directly. Their true body in the valley is the silence that precedes them: birds lifting, macaques drawing close, deer staring into a colonnade that appears empty. Snakes use the sunlit cornices and abandoned watercourses, becoming almost another length of root or stain.

The smallest inhabitants are the most numerous and, in many ways, the most transformative. Ants make roads of mortar lines. Beetles wear paths through petals. Termites import soil onto stone one mouthful at a time, wrapping walls in earthen passages. Moths turn damp surfaces into trembling skin. Centipedes hunt beneath loosened tiles. Fireflies mark the lowest flooded chambers with green points, revealing, by their reflections, where the air ends.

Trees are inhabitants too, though their lives proceed on the valley’s slowest animal timescale. Figs clasp columns and gradually contain them. Roots cross floors like purposeful travelers, descend stairs, enter doorways, and seek the permanent damp of buried reservoirs. Ferns, orchids, reeds, mosses, fungi, and vines occupy narrower jurisdictions determined by shade, mineral seepage, flood height, and the brief angles of daylight.

Together these beings form no single society, yet they are bound by the vanished design. Water dictates their movements through conduits none of them made. Fruiting terraces set the routes of bats, hornbills, monkeys, pigs, and cats. Cisterns concentrate frogs, fish, insects, snakes, and hunting birds. Bridges become borders; courtyards become grazing grounds; galleries become migration corridors. The old city survives less as an object than as a pattern imposed upon countless lives.

No creature there knows that the cliffs are walls. None needs to.

To those born in the valley, the square cave, the tiled lake, the stair-shaped river, and the mountain pierced by corridors are ordinary facts of the world. The inhabitants have not reclaimed the place. They have inherited it so completely that ruin is no longer the right word for their home.
