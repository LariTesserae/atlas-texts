The basin is inhabited less by separate creatures than by a single, crowded continuity of appetites.

Its oldest residents are the rooted ones: low trees with pleated, heat-shedding leaves; shrubs armored in wax and thorns; reeds whose hollow bodies breathe for the marshes; grasses tough enough to survive cropping down to the buried crown. Their roots overlap beneath nearly every patch of ground. Some spread broad and shallow to intercept rain before it sinks. Others descend into abandoned channels, following seams of damp silt left by rivers generations ago. Around them live fungi, mosses, algae, and bacterial films that catch condensation, digest wood, fix minerals, and turn every dead surface back toward life.

These plants do not wait passively. They collect water, store sugars, close wounds, poison some mouths and feed others. Groves release pollen and seed in coordinated pulses. Certain flowers open only after rain; others attract pollinators by heat, odor, or weak phosphorescence rather than color. The forest’s dim lights belong mostly to its smallest negotiations.

The most numerous inhabitants are hidden or easily mistaken for texture. Beetles overlap on bark like scales. Moths sleep in layered masses under leaves. Termites roof their roads with chewed fiber and soil, extending pale subterranean societies across enormous distances. Ants harvest seeds, herd sap-feeding insects, raid neighboring colonies, and carry fragments of the dead into chambers warm with decay. Mites live on the beetles, worms in the mud, larvae in the roots, parasites in the larvae. There may be no animal in the basin that is not itself inhabited.

After rain, another population appears as if newly made. Frogs break the hardened seals of their chambers and climb from the mud already calling. Soft-bodied insects split dry cases. Eggs dormant in dust hatch in the transient pools. Small crustaceans swim in hoofprints; hunters arrive almost immediately to eat them. For a few hours or nights, creatures that spent most of the year as buried flesh, eggs, or resistant cysts fill the basin with mating, feeding, and flight. Then they vanish again, leaving spawn in the mud and bodies for the reclaimers.

The rivers hold a denser life. Fish move in shoals deep enough to alter the current’s skin. Blind scavengers nose through drowned root tunnels. Broad, heavy amphibious grazers lie in bends where the water remains deep, surfacing only by nostrils and reflective eyes. Shell-bearing animals rasp algae from submerged wood, while long-bodied hunters wait beneath undercut banks. Some fish leave the main channels during rain, forcing themselves through grass and flooded depressions to breed in water that may last only days.

On land, dark grazers travel in narrow herds. They are lightly built but enduring, with close coats slicked by humidity and long feet suited to soft ground. Generations of their passage maintain the basin’s nearest equivalent to roads. They move almost continuously, taking coarse grass mouthful by mouthful, carrying seeds in their hair and nutrients in their bodies. Their trails bend around old root masses, flooded hollows, nesting grounds, and places where too many animals have disappeared.

The browsers are heavier. Broad feet keep them from sinking; thick lips or flexible tongues strip waxy leaves from the low canopy. Their ears turn independently, attending to minute changes in the dark. Sight matters less than smell, ground vibration, and the behavior of smaller animals. Beetles going silent, frogs submerging, or moths tightening against the undersides of leaves can warn them before any predator is heard.

Those predators seldom pursue openly. They are shaped for nearness: low bodies, padded limbs, dark hides broken by faint markings invisible except at arm’s length. Some wait along herd paths. Some enter channels and approach through water. Others climb the leaning trees and drop onto browsers passing below. Their eyes return borrowed light, but only when they choose to look directly outward. More often their presence is known as subtraction—a gap in insect noise, an abandoned patch of grass, a carcass already moving with flies.

Above them live the basin’s least visible inhabitants. Winged animals roost high in the canopy or circle in the warm rising air, navigating by scent, echo, magnetic sense, or the sparse lights below. Fruit-eaters disperse seeds between distant groves. Moth-hunters pass through swarms with mouths open. Larger fliers take frogs, fish, and young grazers, sometimes feeding without ever landing on open ground. Their waste and discarded food fall from darkness, supplying small ecosystems wherever they strike.

Every large body supports a lesser country. Skin folds harbor mites and luminous bacteria. Wounds attract flies before blood has dried. Gut organisms break down leaves no solitary animal could digest. Parasites regulate herds as effectively as predators do, passing through water, dung, insects, and flesh in cycles older than any channel now flowing. A sick animal changes the routes of scavengers; a dead one becomes warm terrain.

The dead are inhabited fastest of all.

Fungi enter wood before it falls. Beetle larvae tunnel beneath bark while branches are still green. A carcass is opened by mouths, eggs, spores, and microbes in quick succession: first blood-feeders and large scavengers, then flies and burrowing beetles, then worms, molds, roots, and mineral-feeding films. Bones persist longer, but even they are gnawed, dissolved, colonized, and drawn down into the mud. Nothing remains refuse for long enough to form a heap.

No species governs this place. Even the largest animals are temporary carriers moving water, seed, flesh, and mineral from one district to another. The closest things to cities are termite colonies, tangled root groves, communal roosts, and spawning pools, but none stands apart from its surroundings. Their walls are eaten; their inhabitants are preyed upon; their wastes become someone else’s food before they accumulate.

What lives here is therefore not one monstrous organism, though at times the basin behaves like one. It is a multitude held in coordination by thirst, hunger, heat, reproduction, and decay. Each creature acts for itself or its colony. Together they pump water, aerate mud, open channels, close clearings, redistribute sediment, and process nearly every usable substance.

The basin’s characteristic inhabitant might be any of them: a grazer taking one quiet step; a root extending into damp soil; a larva hollowing a fallen branch; a fungus brightening briefly over rot; a predator waiting beside a path it did not make.

But taken as a whole, what lives there is the traffic itself—the uninterrupted passage of matter from river to root, root to mouth, mouth to blood, blood to mud, and mud back into leaf.
