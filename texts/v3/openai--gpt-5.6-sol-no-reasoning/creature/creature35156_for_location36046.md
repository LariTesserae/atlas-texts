The cleft is inhabited less by separate species than by a single crowded arrangement of bodies, water, and stone. Its dominant life is the wall colony: innumerable slate-backed creatures layered across the rock until animal and cliff are difficult to distinguish.

Each is small enough to fit in two hands, broad and soft beneath a shield of short mineral spines. Six limbs spread from the underside, ending in damp gripping pads that fasten to even the sharpest faces. They have no obvious head. Their mouths lie low against the rock, beneath grooves that conduct condensation from spine to spine. Translucent lids cover clustered light-sensitive organs along the forward edge, though sight seems secondary to pressure, temperature, and the shared vibration passing through the colony.

Alone, one is nearly inert. Together, they behave like a slow tissue stretched over the mountain. They tighten against cold, lift toward moisture, and relay alarm by changes in posture and tone. Their hollow bodies resonate as air crosses them, producing the cleft’s constant subterranean hum. This sound organizes much of the life around them. It marks weather, warns of movement, and gives blind animals a map of ledges and empty space.

Their cast spines feed the pale striders that traverse the walls and floor. These grazers have compact bodies suspended among enormously long legs, each ending in a divided, fleshy foot. They test every surface before placing weight upon it. Their eyes have disappeared under smooth skin; instead, they listen through their limbs. A disturbance in the communal hum can halt an entire group midway through a step. Around sleeping striders, warmth-loving plants briefly awaken, leaving green silhouettes behind when the animals move on. Thus each herd writes its route across the rock in fading halos.

The overhangs shelter dense roosts of winged gliders. Their bodies are heavy, their wings proportionally small, and true flight is beyond them in the thin air. They live by surrendering to rising currents: dropping from stone, turning in the updraft, and striking another wall with hooked limbs. Their colonies seem noisy and disorderly beside the slow wall-dwellers, yet they too are attentive to resonance. They collect naturally hollow stones and drop hard seed-cases into them, favoring those that answer with the deepest or clearest notes. The resulting clatter may be courtship, play, territorial display, or the first dim recognition that one object can alter another.

A dark-furred hunter or scavenger frequents the condensation pools. It is low-bodied, broad-pawed, and seldom visible except as a gap passing across the colonies. It carries triangular crystal flakes in its teeth and sets them near fissures from which soft prey emerge. The flakes are selected but not fashioned, positioned but not preserved. Each hunt begins with a new search. No tradition has yet become a possession, and no object has acquired value beyond the immediate act.

Older predators have gone still enough to resemble features of the mountain. Some inhabit cracks, exposing only rows of pale hooks. Others lie as hinged slabs beside pools and close around drinkers. Along high ledges, jointed ambush hunters imitate ropes of rime, lowering themselves segment by segment when vibrations announce prey. Their patience is suited to a place where hunger can outlast weather. They may wait weeks for a single movement to enter their reach.

The plants are almost entirely creatures of shelter. Soot-dark moss adheres to wet recesses. Rust-colored cups open close to the stone. Clear stalks gather around mineral warmth and freeze if they extend too far. The green halo-plant survives only in the temporary climate beneath a resting body. None can command space; all depend on borrowed moisture, heat, or protection.

Most important, the inhabitants collectively govern the cleft’s water. Condensation adheres poorly to bare rock but readily to spines, fur, membranes, and living skin. The wall colonies tilt by imperceptible degrees, passing drops across their backs toward brood chambers and root-filled pockets. Grazers stand where their limbs can bridge a broken route. Hanging flocks become dripping reservoirs beneath the shelves. In drought, the entire chamber rearranges itself without any obvious migration, forming a watershed made of animals.

No creature appears to understand the whole system. Each turns toward moisture, warmth, vibration, food, or safety. Yet their accumulated choices make channels, fill pools, irrigate plants, and sustain nurseries. The cleft itself seems to possess a slow physiology: clouds enter, the walls drink, droplets circulate through thousands of bodies, and the excess gathers in black mineral basins.

Alarm reveals how thoroughly these lives are joined. A shadow first flattens one patch of wall-dwellers. Minutes later the contraction reaches the opposite cliff. Their note sinks section by section. Blind striders crouch as the altered frequency reaches their feet. Gliders tighten into the overhangs. Stone-mimicking predators close their mouths. Even the faint plants seem to withdraw into the wet seams.

For a short time, the mountain contains only falling water.

Then a single gripping pad releases with a click. Another answers. Air begins to move again through the colonies’ cavities, and the low paired tones return. The grazers lift themselves. The roosts loosen. Water resumes its passage over living backs.

From beyond the three summits, the inhabitants are invisible. There is only a black fissure near the limit of breath. Within it lives a community older in instinct than invention, without shelter-building, fire, or shaped tools, yet already capable of remaking its environment through collective behavior. Nothing rules the cleft, and almost nothing moves far. Nevertheless, the entire place acts: a chamber-sized organism assembled from thousands of patient lives, holding water against stone at the edge of the sky.
