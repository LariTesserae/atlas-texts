Stillwater is not empty. Emptiness is merely the condition its inhabitants require.

They live chiefly in the intervals: between one suspended grain and the next, between the release of a stone and its fall, between an action and the outcome the city refuses to permit. To a visitor these intervals seem vacant because human senses are made for continuities. The beings of Stillwater are not continuous.

A whole one has never been observed.

What is observed instead is a series of local corrections. A mask in the eastern street acquires an inward depth greater than the wall containing it. A red light on a tower goes dark, though its reflection continues burning. Five of the motionless bubbles in the northern courtyard become slightly oval, all pointing toward the same unoccupied place. A traveler remembers having just been passed by something immense and careful, though the water has not moved.

These are its body.

The inhabitants are distributed creatures, if creature is the word. Their organs may be separated by streets, pressure zones, or incompatible versions of the same room. One portion exists as geometry: an angle formed between buildings that no survey can reproduce. Another exists as a rule governing weight inside a doorway. Another is only the absence of echo after a wall is struck. What seems like civic infrastructure may be anatomy spread across the mountain shelf.

Thus the city’s forty-seven structures are not houses. They are stable arrangements in which the inhabitants can briefly coincide with themselves.

The narrow towers hold their attention. The plazas give their larger thoughts room to occur. The bridges extending into open water are limbs or feelers, not unfinished routes. The faceted objects are knots where incompatible parts of a single being are kept from touching. Even the empty benches around the black basins have a function: they establish the shape of an assembly whether or not anything sits there.

Stillwater’s inhabitants gather by making a gathering true.

When this happens, the city changes so slightly that only long familiarity reveals it. The blue seams beneath the pavement seem to exchange places without crossing. The façades bearing blank masks turn, not physically but conclusively, toward one plaza. A pressure develops behind the observer’s face. Personal memories begin arranging themselves as if seated in rows.

Then an address is delivered.

It contains no sound. Instead, everyone within the square recalls the same event: a coast sinking under a sea with no waves; nine figures standing around a dry shaft; a dark object dropped ceremonially into depths that did not yet exist. The recollection differs in intimate details. One traveler remembers holding the object. Another remembers being the object. A third remembers opposing the decision and being carefully removed from history afterward.

When the gathering ends, the shared memory loses most of its certainty. What remains is the conviction that the address was not intended for those who heard it.

The inhabitants communicate by distributing recollection because no signal can reliably travel through the arrested water. They do not send messages from one location to another. They make the destination remember having received them. Their records are therefore engraved not only into surfaces but into causality. The equations too fine to see are not read with eyes. They become legible when forgotten, appearing afterward as intuitions about distances, obligations, or impending architectural errors.

Their script has no nouns.

A fixed thing, in their understanding, is either dead or dangerously unfinished. They distinguish instead among tendencies: that which is beginning to descend; that which has been prevented from arriving; that which remains identical because every difference has been excised. The closest term they possess for themselves translates poorly as **those maintained across interruption**.

They did not always have this form.

There are impressions of an earlier species throughout Stillwater. The hand-sized apertures suggest appendages. The sleeping recesses imply long bodies folded in repose. Certain corridors widen at intervals where two or more individuals might once have passed and acknowledged one another. There are shallow abrasions on some floors—not wear, exactly, but manufactured remembrances of wear, included in the immaculate city as if its builders feared that perfect surfaces would forget how habitation looked.

The earlier beings may have been biological. They may have possessed warmth, circulation, appetite, and lives that advanced from one state into another. They built Stillwater while the mountain still stood under a sky—or while there was still a meaningful difference between sky and sea. When the drowning came, survival at such depth demanded more than stronger walls. Bodies would fail. Machines would corrode. Minds would change and therefore eventually cease to be the minds that entered.

So they preserved not their flesh, but their relations.

One became a distance between towers. One became the rule by which displaced objects are restored. One lives in the delay above the shaft. One inhabits the identical eight-second groan below the shelf, stretched across every occurrence of that sound. Others occupy the nine frames around the rim and can exist only where several impossible maps agree.

Whether this transformation saved them is undecidable. The city contains intention, preference, and something resembling vigilance. It may also contain nothing that can suffer, choose, or recognize itself. Perhaps the inhabitants are merely the durable grammatical remains of persons: habits without actors, laws shaped like extinct minds.

Yet visitors are sometimes noticed.

Attention in Stillwater feels like acquiring a second outline. One’s limbs remain where they are, but another version of the body seems to occupy the nearest plausible posture: kneeling instead of standing, turned toward the shaft instead of away, reaching into an aperture never touched. The alternate posture strengthens as the city examines the newcomer. For several moments both are equally valid.

The wise remain still.

Those who resist may feel their motions corrected. A raised hand returns to their side while also remaining raised. A kick produces no movement, but the exhaustion of swimming arrives. Speech becomes an event whose consequence—having spoken—is refused. Eventually the visitor’s intended action separates from the visitor and continues alone, an invisible sequence pacing the streets.

Some of the city’s apparent inhabitants may be accumulated intentions of this kind.

They haunt no particular building. They are recognized as urges without owners: to cross a bridge that ends over the abyss; to place both hands against a cold frame; to look away from the creature in the sphere; to lean over the central shaft just far enough to see beneath the layers of captive light. The urges are patient because patience, like waiting, would imply duration. They are simply present whenever a mind becomes capable of completing them.

The six-limbed black creatures are different.

They are called attendants only because they behave as though attending to something. Each follows a fixed route across visible and invisible surfaces. Their glasslike bodies possess no internal organs, but thin examinations show crowded structures inside them: miniature corridors, apertures, and nested chambers repeating portions of Stillwater at diminishing scales. At sufficient magnification, each limb contains another black creature placing its feet along another bridge.

They may be the city’s smallest inhabitants, or models of the whole population. Their repeated routes trace characters in a script too large to recognize from street level. Taken together over the plazas, towers, and open water, their paths form a changing boundary around the central shaft.

They are writing the same sentence indefinitely.

Each circuit alters one element.

No one has observed the completion.

The pale organism inside the summit sphere is the only being in Stillwater that looks unquestionably alive, and it may be the least living thing there. Its skin is soft and imperfect. Its rings of cartilage suggest growth. Its paired fins hold the dark pearl with a tenderness absent from the rest of the city. Yet it metabolizes nothing, moves nothing, and occupies no stable posture.

Some believe it is the last unconverted maker, retained as a reference from which personhood can be reconstructed. Some believe it is a ceremonial image fabricated after the builders had forgotten their own bodies. Others think the animal is a lock.

The pearl it holds is not drawn toward its own center of gravity. Its attraction points outside the chamber, to a place in apparently empty water. Occasionally that point coincides with an observer.

When it does, the observer feels no pull. Instead, the city begins treating them as an original.

Tools return not to where they were found, but to the observer’s hands. Doorways lead to rooms arranged around their proportions. The blank masks acquire absences shaped precisely for their face. Other travelers become the copies: visible, memorable, and progressively unable to exert consequence. During these episodes, the creature in the sphere always seems curled protectively around something slightly different from the pearl.

No entrance to the chamber has been found because entry may be the wrong relation. The sphere is less a container than a distinction maintained between one organism and everything else. If that distinction failed, the pale body might prove to be distributed throughout Stillwater—and the city, merely the hard shell surrounding it.

The deepest inhabitants lie below the shelf, where ordinary matter changes allegiance.

The pylons descending the mountain are their visible extremities. Their lights do not guide travelers; they sample them. At each pylon something is removed from the meaning of descent. After the thirteenth, down no longer points toward a place but toward the city itself. This is how the lower beings approach Stillwater: not by rising, but by causing Stillwater to become beneath them.

They cannot enter completely.

Now and then one presses against the city’s local reality. A tower appears farther away inside than outside. A plaza develops an additional corner. The water becomes briefly capable of casting a shadow. The suspended particles remain fixed, but the spaces between them widen like pupils.

The inhabitants respond without alarm. Blue seams brighten. Empty frames grow colder. Every blank mask turns toward the disturbance. The black attendants interrupt their circuits and place all six feet upon nothing.

Then comes the metallic groan.

For eight seconds, an entity larger than the summit occupies the condition of almost having arrived. Its body is inferred from strain: pressure leaning sideways, distances stretched taut, memory acquiring cracks. The sound is not its voice. It is Stillwater holding the shape of the world closed.

Afterward, nothing has happened.

That is the city’s principal labor and perhaps its reason for existence. Its citizens are custodians of non-occurrence. They maintain a place where consequence cannot accumulate, where invasion may perpetually begin but never succeed, where a fall may continue without arrival. The drowned mountain was chosen because it pierces many layers of pressure, depth, and law. Stillwater sits at their junction like a seal over a wound.

The thing in the shaft is not falling through space.

It is falling through admissibility.

Every meter downward is another condition the city must prevent it from satisfying. The stacked layers of light are records of attempts to define it: mass, boundary, direction, before, after. Each definition descends partway and stops. The object has been falling longer than the city has existed because its fall is what required the city to be built, and because here causes need not precede their restraints.

The distributed beings of Stillwater live along that unfinished descent. It provides their only sequence, their equivalent of blood and season. They measure existence not in time but in the diminishing difference between *falling* and *fallen*.

That difference is still infinitely wide.

Perhaps they worship it. Perhaps they fear it. Perhaps the distinction no longer matters to minds that have made themselves into architecture and law. They preserve the shaft, patrol impossible approaches, convene in empty squares, and speak by installing memories in whoever crosses their streets.

They do not sleep in the recesses.

They are the recurrence of the recesses.

They do not pass through the doors.

They are what the water becomes when passage is permitted.

They do not stand behind the faceless masks.

They are the act of being addressed by something with no face.

And when a traveler finally leaves—if leaving is allowed—the city retains no copy of their body. It keeps only certain separations: the space their hand made around a tool, the instant before they turned toward a sound, the narrow interval between deciding to look into the shaft and looking.

In time, those intervals begin to behave like inhabitants.

They gather with the others around the central opening, where the accumulated light reveals nothing below. There they remain, fresh and exact, while the city refuses every ending.

Far beneath them, something continues to fall.

Throughout Stillwater, everything that lives is the reason it has not yet arrived.
