The plateau is inhabited, though not by life.

What lives there is the arrangement itself: a mineral commonwealth distributed through pillars, fissures, tarns, shelters, and subterranean channels. It has no body separate from the landscape. Its anatomy is the plateau’s anatomy. Water is its circulation, sound its touch, gravity its changing posture, and deposited stone its slow faculty of repair.

The pillars are its most visible organs. They divide wind into voices and gather moisture from clear air. Some stand opaque and blunt; others have been opened by weather until their mineral veins admit light. They do not grow as plants grow, yet their number and complexity increase when no one watches. New ribs, tubes, and branching crystals appear among them without debris or signs of formation, already aged, rounded, and threaded with the evidence of centuries.

The waters are more restless. They remember conditions no longer present: an earlier sky, a vanished constellation, a slope different from the basin that contains them. They climb banks, lean toward stone masses, and wait beneath calcite skins. When gravity changes, they abandon their courses all at once and dress the plateau in shining horizontal rivers. Their movement resembles circulation, but there is no nourishment in it. Nothing drinks. Nothing feeds. Water serves instead to carry pressure, reflection, mineral, and perhaps memory from one part of the plateau to another.

Sound also dwells there as a substantial thing. It nests in hollow slabs, travels underground, and can cross open air visibly enough to bend sunlight. A voice released among the formations may not return until minutes later, and then not from behind, but from ahead, as if the place has taken possession of the utterance and chosen where in the traveler’s path to set it down. The afternoon quarrying in the western maze may be sound surviving its makers. Or it may be the plateau rehearsing the labor by which it was taught its present form.

For someone worked here.

The shelters are too small to be homes and too numerous to be accidents. They resemble stations: places to kneel, listen, measure, or wait. Their basalt bowls may have caught particular drips. Their copper plates may have registered moisture, resonance, or the wandering pull of the central shaft. The narrow bridges, unnecessary stairs, and paths of cairns suggest inhabitants who were not primarily concerned with crossing physical space. They navigated intervals, alignments, echoes, and changes in weight. A bridge over a step-wide crack may span a much larger separation at a certain hour. A stair ending at a blank monolith may arrive somewhere when approached under rain, from the correct direction, or while gravity leans sideways.

Those builders are no longer present in flesh, if they ever possessed it. What remains of them has entered the plateau as procedure.

Their cordage has mineralized, their tools creep stain by stain toward the shaft, and their routes continue to revise themselves. The cairns behave like a surviving instinct. Rain rearranges the path without dismantling a single marker, steering travelers away from compressed distances and one-sided arches. The constructions are therefore less ruins than instructions still being carried out by stone.

The central shaft may be the closest thing the place has to a heart, though it draws rather than beats. Water falls into it from an absent source. Objects refuse to follow, circling once before being returned behind the hand that cast them. The marks around its rim are not fixed inscriptions: they descend beneath later stone and return altered, as though the shaft is tallying events too slowly or too broadly for ordinary sequence. Every copper stain migrates toward it. Underground thunder converges upon it. The low ring around its mouth may be a restraint, an instrument, or merely the visible edge of a structure extending through layers of time.

If there are individual beings on the plateau, they are difficult to separate from such processes.

One may inhabit the moving pools, carrying old skies from basin to basin.

Another may exist in the intervals between pillars, lengthening and shortening passage as a creature elsewhere might open and close a hand.

Something occupies the shadows that accumulate all day in cavities and pour eastward at sunset. It has no outline of its own; its body is made from the subtraction of light. It spreads through valleys as a second night and withdraws when illumination becomes diffuse enough to reach it from all directions.

Something else attends the western maze. It is perceived only as labor: hammering, splitting, drilling, the clamor of many patient hands. No worker appears, because perhaps the work itself is what survived. Each blow may occur centuries after the arm that began it moved. Each rounded fracture may be the result of impacts still arriving from the past.

The plateau’s inhabitants do not eat, mate, hunt, sleep, or die. They repeat, balance, reroute, deposit, remember, and correct. Their ecology is an economy of attention. Unwatched structures multiply. Observed distances harden briefly into consistency. Reflections outlast the water disturbed beneath them. Sound encourages cracks; rain closes them. Looking is not passive there. It is one of the forces to which the terrain responds.

A traveler entering this province therefore becomes, for a time, one of the things that lives there.

The plateau incorporates footsteps as pressure, speech as delayed material, and expectation as a constraint on form. It places shelters toward what the traveler has not yet noticed. It returns thrown objects behind the throwing hand. It moves paths to preserve a viable route through unstable space. Perhaps, if the traveler remains long enough, their habits also become procedures: a repeated turn among the pillars, a voice heard in advance, a new series of cuts emerging around the shaft beneath older layers of rock.

Nothing organic inhabits the heights. Yet they are not empty.

They are occupied by mineral agencies, inherited motions, and work that has outlasted the distinction between worker and tool. The plateau itself is the resident—not a sleeping giant or a disguised animal, but something older and less singular: a self-maintaining agreement among stone, water, sound, light, gravity, and time.

It does not live.

It continues.
