What lives in the cleft is not a forest but a colony of interruptions.

Moss is its most persistent inhabitant. It arrives as dust-fine fragments and spores, settles in mica seams, and swells wherever water hesitates. In the deepest shade it forms black-green skins scarcely thicker than felt; on ledges it gathers into saturated cushions, dense enough to retain rain after the surrounding stone has dried. Gravel works into these cushions. Mineral grit becomes trapped among their stems, then darkens with decay into the cleft’s first shallow soil. A whole slope may begin as such a pad—and end when it grows heavy, peels from the rock, and drops intact to a lower shelf.

The ferns live more precariously. Their crowns are wedged into vertical cracks, with little visible beneath them but wet stone. They send out cold, divided fronds that bow under droplets and shudder in the air driven through the gorge. When frost seals the fissures, the fronds flatten and discolor, but the hidden crowns persist. Some are torn away by rockfall; others descend with the fragment that carried them and continue growing at a new angle, as though the cliff’s collapse were merely another kind of transplantation.

Crowded shrubs occupy the larger pockets. They are low, twisted, and old-looking despite the youth of the ground beneath them. Their leaves are small and thick, almost black above and silvered below. Stems lie along the rubble rather than rising far into the wind, rooting again wherever they touch trapped soil. What appears to be a single bush may be a dispersed body joined beneath moss and stones: one organism holding several ledges at once. Its exposed branches bear pale scars from sleet and falling chips of rock. Its buried branches remain alive and emerge downslope.

Among these shrubs grow lax grasses whose blades are bleached green, gray, or nearly white. They stream in the downdrafts, filling gaps like loose hair, then lift when the air stills. Their roots make tight nets around pebbles and fragments of organic matter. Where a clump is undercut, it hangs by these roots until the whole tuft folds downward. It may die there, or catch in another crevice and resume growth.

The flowering plants are smaller still. White cups open close to the ground, held just above wet leaves. Blue bells hang beneath overhangs where they are protected from sleet. There is no creature to receive their color, no wingbeat to disturb them, and no mouth shaped to their nectar. They flower because weather and season enter their tissues: lengthening light, thaw, moisture, cold. Pollen falls within a blossom, blows against a neighboring stem, or is wasted on stone. Seeds ripen in dry capsules and are shaken loose by vibration. Some possess hairs or papery margins and rise briefly in turbulent air; others merely bounce downslope until moss stops them.

No hidden animal community accompanies this vegetation. Beneath the leaves there are only roots, fungal threads, bacteria, water, mineral grains, and the dark remains of previous growth. Nothing crawls when a stone is lifted. No larva tunnels through a stem. No beetle shelters under bark, because there is scarcely any bark and no beetle. The pools contain no nymphs or worms. The flowers have no visitors. Seed heads are not eaten. Dead foliage is reduced slowly by moisture, cold, chemistry, and microscopic life, becoming thin black deposits squeezed into fractures.

The fungi are therefore quiet residents rather than conspicuous fruiting bodies. They lace roots to newborn soil and extend into pores the roots cannot enter. They take hold in fallen leaves pressed beneath moss, in bruised stems buried by slides, and in the fine rock flour left after a collapse. Their work is slow in the cold, but continuous whenever liquid water persists. They bind the green colony to the mineral one, passing what little can be dissolved from fresh stone into living tissue.

And the mineral colony seems almost alive beside them. Pyrite oxidizes along wet seams. Mica opens in scales. Quartz catches lightning and gives it back in blue-white fragments. Frost inhabits cracks temporarily, growing there with enough force to separate plates from the wall. Water enters as mist, condenses, gathers, vanishes underground, and returns lower down carrying grit. Rock is not merely the plants’ setting; it is their nearest neighbor, their soil in waiting, their shelter, and their most frequent destroyer.

Everything that lives here has a body suited less to permanence than to repeated loss. It grows low, clings broadly, roots at many points, and can survive division. It wastes little on height. It accepts burial, abrasion, long darkness, and sudden exposure. Its community has no animals to carry pollen, disperse fruit, browse old growth, or open clearings. The mountain performs those functions instead. Wind distributes. Water transports. Frost prunes. Rockfall clears. Landslides transplant entire gardens.

Together these inhabitants make a living layer that is dense but never complete. They advance across every fresh fracture, converting glare-dark stone into green; then the wall breaks and takes them with it. Their abundance is not the abundance of safety. It is the abundance of many beginnings.

What lives in the cleft is a vegetation trained by catastrophe: moss that makes ground, roots that enter the mountain’s wounds, flowers opening without witnesses, and low shrubs repeatedly crushed into the soil they are helping to create. Beneath the roar and metallic clatter, life proceeds without voice or movement of its own—holding, spreading, splitting, and beginning again wherever the rock pauses long enough to become habitable.
