The seam is inhabited first by things that can endure interruption.

Maidenhair fern keeps to the sweating brick, each black stem carrying a small, precise ladder of leaflets. When the wall darkens at dawn, the fern opens into brightness; by afternoon it contracts toward the mortar, conserving what the stone has given it. Liverwort lies flatter still, a green skin pressed into hollows where condensation repeatedly finds the same path. Neither tries to possess the whole passage. They live by exact placement.

Purslane occupies the young ground differently. Its red stems creep over silt, and its fleshy leaves hold brief rains long after the exposed mud has crusted. Pennywort gathers around seepage in loose colonies of round leaves. Small sedges stand wherever a pool has lasted just long enough for roots to trust it. Together they make an archipelago rather than a meadow: green rooms separated by dust, shell fragments, and bare, unstable earth.

Fig seedlings are the hollow’s most numerous wagers. Their seeds arrive in refuse, droppings, and the wash from the seller’s yard. They germinate vigorously in mortar crumbs, raising tender leaves toward the narrow sky, but most encounter brick before they can deepen their roots. Some yellow. Some are dislodged by rain. Some persist for a season in cracks that seem scarcely wide enough to hold soil. The young mulberry is a rarer success, though not yet a secure one. It leans away from one wall and toward the other, arranging its leaves in the shaft of light. Its roots are beginning to make the loose floor less temporary.

The snails belong to the night face of the place. In daylight they wait beneath broken roof tiles, inside cavities in the mud brick, or among damp stems with their shells dulled by dust. After dark they climb. Their mouths rasp films of algae and fungus from the walls, following moisture upward until heat or dryness turns them back. By morning their trails cross the brickwork like fine, uncertain writing. Empty shells settle into the soil and become pale chambers for smaller creatures, then grit, then part of the ground.

Ants live by revision. No route remains correct for long. A passage safe yesterday may be flooded today; a firm bank may collapse beneath the traffic of a colony. They bridge cracks with their bodies, excavate softened mortar, carry seeds into unsuitable places, and move their brood when dampness penetrates a nest. The chalky fall of plaster is, to them, a high country of ledges and loose cliffs. Their roads climb it, divide, and disappear under its edge.

Beetles inhabit the shade beneath shards. Some graze on decaying leaves and fungal threads. Others hunt mites, springtails, and soft-bodied larvae in the damp soil. Small dark beetles push crumbs and pellets across clear patches, leaving grooves that the next bead of water erases. Under the floor’s crust live pale organisms rarely exposed: worms fine as hairs, translucent larvae, mites among grains of mortar, springtails that scatter when a tile is lifted. Much of the hollow’s population is almost indistinguishable from the processes that make soil. They eat what has fallen, and what passes through them becomes the next layer.

Spiders use the walls as opposing shores. They suspend webs across the narrowest gaps, fastening silk to fern stems, mortar knobs, and the corners of broken bricks. Condensation reveals the webs each morning, beading strands that were invisible at night. Rain ruins them repeatedly. The spiders retreat under leaves or into dry joints, then return and measure the altered space with their legs. A web rebuilt after a storm is never quite where the old one was.

The geckos live vertically. By day they flatten themselves against warm brick, mottled bodies nearly becoming the wall. Their toes find purchase on surfaces softened by age. They wait beside cracks from which flies and moths emerge, motionless except for the pulse of the throat and an occasional turn of the eye. At dusk they begin to move: a few deliberate steps, a pause, then the abrupt seizure of prey. Their eggs, when laid, are hidden deep in masonry where temperature changes slowly and rain cannot reach.

The frogs belong to the hollow only when water does. They appear after storms as though formed from the wet earth—small, brown, smooth-backed, their colors matching mud, leaf rot, and brick shadow. They sit at the edges of the newest pools, taking mosquitoes and gnats. Their lives are governed by contraction. As each pool shrinks, they retreat into root holes, wall fissures, and cavities beneath tiles, folding themselves into dampness until the next rain releases them.

Mosquito larvae inhabit the pools’ brief interior. They hang beneath the surface film and descend in quick wriggles when shadow passes overhead. Other minute swimmers arrive with dust, feet, eggs, or dormant forms already sealed in dried mud. Water striders sometimes stand on the largest pools, improbable visitors on a surface that may vanish before their young could develop. Their presence is less a settlement than an experiment.

Moths shelter under the fig and mulberry leaves, wings closed against the day. Gnats rise from wet silt in faint clouds. Flies come to the smell of fermentation and retreat toward the brightness above. Sparrows watch from the wall tops but seldom descend; the seam offers food without room for flight. Occasionally one drops in for a seed or insect, beats hard against the confined air, and leaves the vegetation shaking. Doves contribute feathers, down, and droppings from outside, sending nourishment into a place they do not themselves inhabit.

Fungi and bacteria are everywhere, though seen mostly through their effects. They soften fallen leaves, bloom as pale threads beneath tile shards, darken damp mortar, sour trapped water, and release the smell that rises after rain. Algae paint thin green and black bands along the routes of seepage. These small communities mark the duration of moisture more accurately than any channel: vivid where water returns, faint where it has failed.

No single creature rules the hollow. Its inhabitants overlap without forming one continuous world. The snail’s pasture is the gecko’s hunting wall. The frog’s pool is the mosquito’s nursery and the ant’s obstacle. A tile is shelter above, ceiling below, and eventually stone within a bank. The mulberry’s shade preserves moss while its roots split the ground that sustains it. Each life alters the conditions for another, usually by very little.

What lives here is therefore not merely a collection of species, but a community of short opportunities. Its inhabitants are specialists in edges, pauses, damp intervals, loose material, and unfinished ground. They do not require wilderness to be broad or old. They need one sweating brick, one protected underside, one puddle lasting through the night.

Together they inhabit the city’s omission. They turn leakage into water, debris into soil, cracks into refuge, and a narrow absence between walls into a living place.
