Those who live there are lean without seeming frail, compact in the way of creatures shaped by slopes and narrow passages. Their calves are corded, their hands abraded, their breathing slow and deliberate. Lowland visitors mistake this economy for solemnity. The mountain people simply do not spend breath, heat, water, iron, or words without purpose.

They walk with a slight forward inclination, as if every street were rising even when it is not. Children learn early to judge a roof edge beneath snow, the temper of a loaded animal, and whether a stone will hold weight. The elderly may move slowly, but they move with exactness, resting at known turns where walls catch the sun. Blindness is feared less than weakness of the knees. A person who can no longer climb often becomes a keeper of cistern tallies, a mender, a teacher, or the memory of a neighborhood.

Their clothing makes them broader than they are. They wear layered wool, felted coats, hide-soled boots, wrapped leggings, and hoods that can be drawn across the mouth when wind raises grit or frost. Garments are patched so thoroughly that the first cloth may disappear while the garment retains its name and ownership. Colors come from undyed fleece, soot, mineral earths, faded plant dyes, and the occasional costly thread brought from warmer country. Bright cloth is used sparingly—at a cuff, on a child’s cap, around the handle of a ritual vessel—where it can be seen without being wasted.

Almost everyone carries cord. A loop hangs from the belt beside a small knife, a cup, and some means of making or preserving fire. Travelers carry foot spikes, a felt face wrap, dried curds, and a marked water token recognized along their route. People who cross exposed country alone often wear whistles of bone or fired clay, though they use them only in distress.

There is no single people of the range. Each basin has its own speech, preferred knots, roof forms, saints or ancestors, marriage customs, and suspicions about the waterkeeping of its neighbors. Some trace descent through mothers because houses and terrace rights pass through them. Elsewhere herds follow fathers, while water shares belong to courts, guilds, or whole roof-neighborhoods. A person may hold citizenship in one basin, grazing rights in another, kin obligations in a third, and burial claims in a fourth.

Identity accumulates like architecture. Someone gives their name, household, roof quarter, home basin, and perhaps the pass from which their grandparents came. Among strangers, the most useful affiliation comes first: quarry company, caravan fraternity, bridge ward, shrine road, water council. Maps matter less than chains of obligation. A journey is described not only by distance but by whose court will admit you, whose animals know the trail, and where your family once helped repair a retaining wall.

Their rulers, where rulers exist, are constrained by practical knowledge. A prince may command guards, but cannot invent water. Reservoir keepers, channel readers, bridge crews, senior herders, midwives, masons, and those who know the old boundaries possess quieter authority. Councils meet beside cistern mouths or on broad roofs because decisions about the city are expected to occur where their consequences can be seen.

The most respected officials are often measurers. They carry rods, bowls, cords, waxed tablets, tally bones, or staffs incised with generations of marks. They know how much water disappears between a snowfield and a lower court, which wall casts a shadow at midwinter, how many animals a meadow can bear, and when a crack is merely old rather than dangerous. Their judgments are argued fiercely, but falsifying a measure is among the gravest offenses. Theft takes from a household; a false measure may injure a district for decades.

Children learn counting through shares of water and fodder. They learn geometry from terrace corners, memory from route songs, and law from roof-path disputes. Some schools are no more than a sunny stair where an elder scratches diagrams on slate. Apprenticeship begins by carrying: pegs for a surveyor, wedges for a mason, feed for a drover, messages for a reservoir court. Only later is a child trusted to cut, reckon, lead, or speak on another’s behalf.

Writing exists in several forms because no one method serves the whole range. Contracts are scratched onto thin stone or painted on prepared boards. Knotted cords record quantities and obligations. Notched staffs accompany herds and caravans so that a count cannot be altered unnoticed at either end. Important agreements are duplicated and stored in separate basins against fire, collapse, and feud. Oral witnesses remain essential. A document proves what was intended; living memory explains what the marks meant.

Their speech is adapted to distance and effort. In close quarters it is quiet and compressed, rich in gestures of chin, hand, and brow. Across ravines, shepherds use calls that carry better than ordinary words. Horn phrases and drum patterns announce only what must be known quickly. More complicated news travels on feet. Runners are admired not for speed alone but for arriving with the message unchanged.

Hospitality is strict because exposure is merciless. A traveler caught after dark is owed a place out of the wind, enough water to survive, and directions that do not knowingly endanger them. They may still be charged, questioned, watched, or turned away at dawn. In drought, hospitality narrows to law’s minimum, and the shame of such seasons lasts for generations.

Meals are hot when fuel permits and communal whenever possible. Barley appears as porridge, flat cakes, dumplings, roasted meal, and fermented drink. Beans and turnips thicken stews. Hardy greens, bitter herbs, dried mushrooms from rare wooded ravines, curds, hard cheeses, and rendered fat provide variation. Meat is common in flavor but less common in pieces: bones are simmered repeatedly, fat is apportioned carefully, and dried shavings season whole pots. Blood from slaughter is saved. Horn, sinew, hide, gut, and bone each pass into another craft.

Salt binds the range to itself. Some basins mine it; others gather crusts from drying margins or trade for it across difficult passes. Salt caravans carry not only food preservative but messages, marriage proposals, judgments, songs, disease, and fashion. Their arrival can transform a quiet court into a market overnight. The animals come in first, steaming and dusted pale, followed by drovers whose faces are darkened by glare and wind.

Herders live half inside the city and half beyond it. Their animals sleep beneath houses, cross plazas, graze abandoned terraces, and supply wool, milk, dung, transport, and status. They know the city by gates and gradients rather than districts. A lane that looks broad to a mason may be impassable to horned cattle; a stair used daily by residents may not exist in a drover’s mental map.

The herds are not treated as interchangeable stock. Old females have names and remembered temperaments. Lead animals wear wooden clappers or braided tassels. Children recognize family flocks by horn shape, ear cuts, gait, and the pattern of soot or ocher rubbed into fleece. Loss to a fall is mourned differently from slaughter. One is waste and rupture; the other, if properly done, completes an obligation.

Pack animals have their own hierarchy. Donkeys are patient, vindictive, and indispensable. Small cattle pull where footing allows. Thick-coated hybrids survive the colder roads but may be stubborn around bridges. Handlers speak to them continuously in low voices, not from sentimentality alone but because a calm animal is less likely to kill itself and others on a narrow path. Certain bridge approaches are lined with old scratches where generations of animals balked in the same place.

Wild creatures are regarded as neighboring populations rather than as a separate realm called nature. Vultures belong to the high air and to death customs. Foxes are thieves but also controllers of mice. Marmots predict weather unreliably enough to inspire jokes and reliably enough to be watched. Frogs appearing in floodwater are signs that buried life has endured another year. Choughs are notorious witnesses: many stories begin with a bird seeing what people hoped to conceal.

Dogs are few in the densest quarters, where food is too valuable and space too confined, but common among herders, guards, and caravans. They are long-legged or heavy-coated according to their work. House cats are smaller and tolerated mainly for defending grain. Pigeons are managed in towers, abandoned rooms, and niches built deliberately into walls. Their flesh is eaten, their messages occasionally trusted, and their droppings treated almost as a mineral resource.

Death is understood through the scarcity of ground. Some communities bury their dead in caves, rubble chambers, or narrow shelves beyond the walls. Others expose bodies to birds on high platforms, afterward gathering cleaned bones into family repositories. In some basins, the dead are cremated only when fuel can be spared or when disease requires it. Names are carved on structural stones, so ancestors literally remain in stairs, channels, bridge abutments, and retaining walls.

A person whose house is lost may still say, “My mother is in that wall,” meaning both memorial and claim.

Religion differs from basin to basin, but reverence gathers around continuance: springs that have not failed, passes that have reopened, foundations that survived shaking, animals that returned through snow. Sanctuaries are small because the sacred does not need spacious shelter. They occupy fissures, ledges, bridgeheads, spring mouths, and places where a road first reveals another inhabited basin.

Offerings are rarely extravagant. A twist of wool, a spoonful of meal, the first meltwater caught at dawn, a feather, a repaired object, or a stone carried uphill may suffice. To carry a useless stone a great distance is considered meaningful precisely because daily labor permits so little uselessness.

Their festivals alter the sound of the range. At the opening of reservoirs, water is released ceremonially before it is released practically. People wash faces, tools, and door lintels with a few drops. At herd return, horns answer from slope to slope. Weddings can join distant basins and therefore resemble small diplomatic congresses: relatives arrive with pack trains, route news, seed grain, wool, grievances, and witnesses.

Music favors breath that can be sustained and instruments that do not require much metal. There are frame drums, reed pipes, bone flutes, stringed instruments with hide-covered bodies, wooden clappers, and stones selected for their tone. Singing is often antiphonal. One group begins from a roof, another answers across a court or ravine, and the mountain supplies the interval. Long songs leave pauses for breathing and echoes.

Their beauty is geometric and cumulative. They ornament door lintels, loom weights, water spouts, harness, knife sheaths, and the visible ends of roof beams. Patterns repeat terraces, fault lines, horn spirals, braided channels, snowfall, migration paths, and the stepped shadow of mountains. A fine object is admired not for consuming rare material but for revealing attention: a precisely fitted joint, a balanced wall, a patch made decorative, a cup repaired so cleanly that the repair becomes its central design.

Courtship often begins in shared labor. Young people meet at markets, communal plastering, terrace repairs, shearing, pilgrimage camps, and seasonal watering. Gifts tend to be useful but intimate: a carved spindle, fitted gloves, a better boot sole, a packet of seeds, a cord dyed in a family color, a route description copied in a careful hand. To repair something without being asked can be an assertion of affection, though doing so badly may end a courtship.

Marriage negotiations concern more than two households. They may involve rights of passage, winter shelter for animals, obligations to distant elders, access to a cistern, or the maintenance of a particular stair. A wedding can settle an old dispute or create a new one. Divorce is possible in many districts, but shared masonry makes separation complicated. Rooms, roofs, stairs, walls, and water shares do not divide along emotional lines.

Feuds occur, but their forms are constrained by interdependence. Poisoning a channel, burning fodder, or sabotaging a bridge is considered an offense beyond ordinary vengeance because it endangers uninvolved people and animals. Feuding households may still be required to repair the same public roof path. Neutral specialists—midwives, channel keepers, avalanche searchers, and corpse bearers—often cross hostile boundaries under protection.

War is difficult in the mountains and therefore intimate. Large armies starve, lose animals, and become trapped by weather. Defense belongs to gate crews, slingers, archers, stone throwers, and people who know which paths can be erased quickly. Fortifications are extensions of the terrain: blocked stairs, removable bridge planks, false lanes, hidden cisterns, walls that guide intruders beneath overhangs.

Conquest rarely means occupying everything. It means controlling passes, reservoirs, winter pasture, salt, or a chain of hospitable roofs. A victorious power may collect tribute from a basin it can reach only part of the year. Rebellion begins when the snow closes behind the tax collectors.

The range also produces people who do not remain. Traders descend toward warmer lands; mercenaries sell their endurance elsewhere; pilgrims disappear over northern passes; displaced families establish new roof quarters in distant basins. Those who return bring unfamiliar fruit stones, metalwork, stories of deep rivers, and habits considered soft or extravagant. Yet even after years below, returnees often wake thirsty in the night, distrusting any abundance of water that has no keeper.

Outsiders imagine the inhabitants as uniformly austere. They are not. They gossip, gamble with marked bones, mock neighboring accents, race animals where plateaus allow, and paint rude figures in quarry shelters. Children slide on frozen channels despite prohibitions. Adolescents climb abandoned towers to avoid supervision. Market cheats shave weights. Water officials accept favors. Wealthy families enlarge private rooms beneath nominally public roofs. Every law names a temptation that exists.

The poorest live in dark lower rooms, unstable margins, quarry hollows, animal shelters, or districts where channels arrive last. Their labor is everywhere: they carry water when conduits freeze, clear night soil, drag stone, tan hides, unload caravans, and rebuild after falls. Debt may attach a household to a herd, workshop, or reservoir project for years. Some basin councils provide grain and winter fuel; others call hardship a private failure until unrest threatens the channels.

The wealthy do not necessarily display height or spaciousness. They display insulation, stored fodder, sound roofs, metal tools, access to multiple cisterns, and relations in several basins. A rich house smells faintly of wood smoke, wool, and food even at the end of winter. Its doors fit. Its animals are not prematurely slaughtered. Its residents can survive a closed pass without selling heirlooms.

Prestige also belongs to those who can mobilize help. A household known to feed repair crews, shelter stranded caravans, lend animals, and contribute stone after a collapse may possess more influence than one with greater stores. Generosity is praised, recorded, and eventually collected upon. Nothing given is entirely forgotten.

After earthquakes, social distinctions briefly loosen. Everyone listens first for water. Crews move through lanes carrying lamps, wedges, rope, and chalk. Children are counted by roof cluster; animals are released from unstable rooms; elders identify buried passages from memory. Masons mark walls safe, doubtful, or condemned. Thieves sometimes enter damaged houses, but so do strangers who dig for people they have never met.

The city’s deepest experts are those who can read change before it becomes disaster. They notice a door beginning to stick, a cistern losing water too quickly, fresh dust below a ceiling seam, birds abandoning a cliff, an animal refusing a familiar path. Such knowledge is partly taught and partly accumulated through survival. Their warnings may sound vague—“the wall has begun thinking downhill”—but no one dismisses the phrase.

In the highest settlements, people are fewer, quieter, and more transient. Shepherds, shrine keepers, hunters, fugitives, weather watchers, and quarry crews inhabit stone huts half sunk into the ground. Their faces darken under intense light; their lips split; their possessions fit into bundles. They may go weeks without hearing a wheel. Yet they remain tied to the denser city below by wool, meat, messages, kinship, and the paths they maintain.

At the lowest margins live farmers of broader terraces, potters near clay beds, charcoal burners around the surviving groves, and traders positioned for journeys out of the range. They consider themselves mountain people, though those above joke that they breathe soup-thick air and live among trees.

No inhabitant knows the whole city. A caravan master may traverse a hundred basins and still be ignorant of neighborhoods behind a familiar ridge. A scholar may compile route lists longer than a lifetime’s travel. Somewhere, every custom has an exception; every language becomes another language; every law meets an older claim.

What unites them is not government, ancestry, or belief, but practiced dependence. They live by catching what falls, storing what passes, repairing what shifts, and remembering who shares the consequence. Their civilization is less a structure imposed upon the mountains than a web of repeated acts: a channel cleared before dawn, a stranger warned away from a broken bridge, dung turned to fuel, a roof kept open as a road, an old animal trusted to lead the herd home.

Even their permanence is made of movement. People climb and descend. Animals migrate. Water vanishes into stone and reappears elsewhere. Houses settle, roads shift, families scatter, walls are dismantled into new walls. The inhabitants endure not by keeping the city unchanged, but by learning which things must flow and which must be held—then teaching that distinction, carefully, to those who come after.
