Nothing animal lives in the basin, but it is not empty of inhabitants. Its inhabitants are **works**.

They have no bodies separate from their labor. A pump is the organ that beats beneath a drowned station; the station is the temporary shell built around that beat. A vine is the green cable by which one district instructs another. Water is blood, stock, solvent, road, and unfinished architecture. Even the land is only a machine caught at one moment in its cycle.

The smallest inhabitants are **standards**.

They recur as seven-way root forks, hexagonal leaf arrays, exact apertures bored through concrete, nine hammer blows followed by three. They pass between vegetable and industrial matter without preference. A standard may live for an hour as the branching of a shrub, then as a wiring diagram inside a relay cabinet, then as the layout of canals seen through a rupture in the water. It is not a plan imposed upon the basin. It is more like heredity: a rule that reproduces whatever medium receives it.

When a stem grows wrongly, when a road curves outside the accepted radius, when a pump falls out of rhythm, the standards become corrective. Sirens flatten into pressure. Roots tighten. Channels close like wounds. The error is crushed, submerged, or excluded from the current version of the place. What passes for predation here is quality control.

Larger inhabitants are **assemblies**, though they seldom possess all their parts at once.

One may begin as a warm cable in the mud. Following it leads to a lit hut whose indicator lamps blink through wired glass. The hut may contain no machinery, only rain falling into a dry concrete room. Yet each drop strikes with the sound of a switch closing, and somewhere far off a floodgate opens. Days later the same assembly may manifest as a refinery, or a grove of piston-trees, or a conveyor feeding vines into a crusher. Its visible forms are stations in a process, not disguises.

These assemblies migrate by replacing the world ahead of themselves. They do not cross distance. Their loading end appears beside a canal; their middle becomes real among the settlements; their far end takes shape beyond the pylons. Behind them, the ground reverts or is revised into another district. To watch one pass is to see causality laid down like track and lifted again behind the train.

Some assemblies tend particular substances. There are **water-folders**, inferred from cubes stacked in open yards and from liquid walls inserted between buildings. There are **gradient engines** that manufacture down, assigning weight and buoyancy locally until mud becomes ceiling and a warehouse wall becomes a basin floor. There are **overlap mills** that press several incompatible structures into the same volume while keeping each intact from its proper approach. Their mechanisms cannot be isolated because the mechanisms include the conditions needed to perceive them.

The plants are inhabitants in the ordinary sense only briefly.

A seed falls into orange clay and germinates at once. Its first root seeks moisture; its second seeks metal; its third enters a region that has not yet been excavated. By maturity the plant has become an industrial lineage. Its leaves regulate pressure. Its fruit produces insulators and seed. Its vascular bundles braid themselves with copper. Broken across the grain, its trunk shows alternating rings of wood and manufactured conductor, as if seasons here were periods of organic and mechanical growth.

They have no flowers meant to be visited. Their blossoms are acoustic organs. A siren opens them; a stamping sequence causes them to set fruit; turbine harmonics determine which contents a pod will make. Their pollen is neither male nor female but particulate instruction. It settles on concrete, oil, membranes, and water, persuading each to participate in growth.

A forest may therefore reproduce a pump station.

First come pale shoots in the footprint of the future building. Their roots sort aggregate from mud and lift rebar in rectangular grids. Broad leaves stretch into walls, become translucent, then mineralize. Seedpods split into bolts and glass beads. Vines tighten into cable bundles. At the center, a bladder plant pulses faster and faster until its rhythm is transferred to a rotor forming around it. When the station begins operating, much of the forest is crushed into seals, insulation, lubricant, and fuel. Nothing has died. It has completed manufacture.

Conversely, a machine may flower.

Rust opens along a tank in petal-shaped seams. Warm water fills it. Pipes curl inward like stamens. A pale tower grows from the manway and produces colorless blooms whose roots are suspended above them. After the production sirens, the tank splits and releases a raft of young kiosks, each carrying one blinking yellow lamp. They drift without current until canals are made beneath them.

The basin’s closest equivalents to individuals are the **custodians** in the inspection huts.

No one sees them whole. Through wired glass there may be a movement too angular for a leaf and too supple for a lever. A lamp goes dark, and in the darkness something changes cabinets. A stair gives one metallic footfall, although the water around it remains undisturbed. Later, a narrow figure seems to stand behind the glass: head bowed, shoulders squared, arms descending farther than the room permits. Then pressure shifts, and it resolves into bundled conduit.

Whether custodians exist apart from the huts is uncertain. The hut may itself be the creature, with lamps for thought, wires for roots, and the suggestion of a figure as its lure or self-image. Its messages do not repeat because repetition belongs to signals sent through time, whereas these signals may coordinate different versions of the same site. One yellow sequence might instruct a pump house that existed yesterday. Another might warn a forest that will occupy the room after the next inversion.

Deeper in the basin live the **rejects**.

They persist in districts that have been folded out of ordinary access: malformed groves, obsolete refineries, canals whose water falls in prohibited directions, settlements based on abandoned laws of depth. During storms they sometimes intersect the accepted basin. A chimney appears with mouths along its length. A road surfaces bearing lane markings for seven directions. A stand of plants unfolds leaves in response to silence rather than sirens.

The machinery howls when these things emerge.

Pressure fronts strike them. Levees close around them. Corrective roots invade their structures and attempt to translate them into current forms. Most are dismantled. Some resist by retaining an older geometry, forcing water to divide around them into incompatible streams. Such rejects may remain as local impossibilities: a black disk in a field, a dry stair descending through flooded soil, a building always visible but never nearer.

They are not rebellious. Nothing here appears to possess enough separation for rebellion. They are prior solutions still trying to run.

At the unseen center is the **principal inhabitant**, if the word can apply.

It may be the immense turbine glimpsed beneath the deepest district, but the turbine is likely only one cross-section of it. The concentric piston-forest is another. The roaring shaft where down becomes absolute is another. So are the production sirens, the multiplying horizons, the warm rain in sealed rooms, and perhaps the absence of animals itself.

It does not occupy the basin. Occupation implies a distinction between organism and environment. This thing continually manufactures the distinction and then recycles it.

Its metabolism is revision. It consumes settled relationships: roof above floor, root below leaf, factory distinct from forest, ruin later than construction. It breaks these down and uses them as raw material. What it excretes are temporary geographies—canals, industrial districts, flooded homes, clay islands, surfaces, directions of gravity—each precise enough to function and provisional enough to be reclaimed.

The smaller works feed it, but they may also be its distributed organs. Turbines provide rhythm. Plants perform material synthesis. huts compare versions. Storms mix rejected forms back into production. Water carries not merely matter but possibility, compressed into cubes or stretched into branching sheets. The central depth accepts all of it and returns the basin altered.

There are no animals because an animal would require an inside.

It would need a boundary the basin recognized: flesh separated from water, appetite separated from industry, movement belonging to a self. Here every boundary is available for manufacture. A body would be treated as scaffolding, reservoir, mineral source, or temporary corridor. Its bones might sprout switchgear; its nerves might be continued into cable; its memories might emerge as a worker settlement on some revised shore.

Perhaps animals were never destroyed. Perhaps they were incompatible standards, corrected so thoroughly that no trace remains—not even shells, bacteria, or decay. Or perhaps animal life has not yet been approved. Somewhere below the roaring center, the basin may be attempting it: assembling mobility, hunger, fear, and skin, then rejecting each trial because it flees, because it dies, because it insists on being one thing in one place.

For now, what lives here does not flee.

It opens when the sirens sound. It roots through walls and days. It blinks from locked kiosks. It turns beneath the sediment. It builds the water that submerges it and raises the land on which it will next be built.

Its life is not survival.

Its life is continuation of process.
