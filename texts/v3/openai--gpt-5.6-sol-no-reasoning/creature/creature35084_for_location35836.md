The inhabitants call themselves, in most translations, **the Concurrent**.

The name does not mean united. It means happening at the same time.

They are not one species, though older civic records sometimes classify them as one population for administrative convenience. They include descendants of organisms born in water, creatures designed for industrial labor, minds assembled within machinery, colonies that acquired legal personhood, and people whose ancestry has been revised so often that lineage is now recorded as a history of tolerances: heat survived, pressures endured, atmospheres accepted, frequencies perceived.

What they share is the city’s demand upon the body.

Most are low, broad, and difficult to knock over. Their limbs are numerous or readily supplemented. Their feet divide into gripping pads suited to wet slopes, vibrating decks, and surfaces that may change consistency while being crossed. Balance is not entrusted to a single inner organ; it is distributed through skin, joints, pressure bladders, and small masses shifting inside the torso. Even at rest, they make continual minute corrections to the basin’s pulse.

Their outer layers are seldom naked. Some have hides like sealed ceramic, darkened by rain and polished at the joints. Others wear articulated mantles that shed water into personal cisterns. Porous mineral plates grow across shoulders, brows, and backs, seeded in childhood and fed by dissolved salts. These plates are at once clothing, filter, archive, and structural brace. Cracks are not hidden. They are filled with bright conductive compounds so that an elder may appear webbed in copper, blue glass, or dull gold.

Soft-bodied citizens move inside pressure sleeves, wheeled shells, or crowds of small carrier organisms. A person may arrive as a ceramic frame occupied by translucent tissue; a ribbon of coordinated animals threaded through a coat; a swarm inhabiting an electrostatic field; or a humanlike figure whose apparent face is only a socially convenient display. Identity is verified less by outline than by rhythm: a signature sequence tapped into a railing, a modulation of local current, a pulse of heat against another’s palm.

Faces, where they exist, are made for close distances. Eyes are protected beneath clear shutters and tuned to flashes, reflections, warning colors, and polarized signals. Many inhabitants cannot hear ordinary speech over the city, and mouths are correspondingly modest. Meaning is exchanged through luminous patches, pressure gestures, direct skin contact, and vibrations placed into the built environment.

To converse, two people may take hold of the same pipe.

They feel one another’s words as patterned tremors beneath the thunder of traffic. Intimate speech is slower: wrist to wrist, brow to shoulder, palm against the resonant plate above the heart. Privacy requires dampening material, physical isolation, or deliberate noise. Secrets are spoken into flesh.

Their homes are not refuges from infrastructure. They are negotiated portions of it.

A family may occupy a cluster of warm chambers folded around a pumping artery, paying for residence by monitoring its harmonics. Another may live seasonally in a flood gallery, suspending possessions from the ceiling when the water rises. Some households maintain paired apartments in the same volume but different phases, sharing heat and drainage while never seeing one another except during power fluctuations. Doors are treated with more seriousness than walls. A door is a promise that the space behind it will remain compatible long enough to enter.

Households are formed around maintenance as often as kinship. Those who keep one another’s seals, remember safe configurations, share emergency buoyancy, and can identify each other after a phase accident are considered family. Children may be gestated, printed, budded from colonial bodies, assembled from licensed components, or adopted when maintenance swarms discover an emergent mind in neglected machinery.

Newborns learn three things before language: how to cling, how to read warning light, and how to become still when the walls begin to move.

Stillness is not quiet. Quiet is rare and often alarming. Stillness means yielding correctly—flattening against a marked surface while a corridor narrows, locking one’s joints during a pressure release, floating without struggle when a floor takes on water, allowing a street to redirect the body with the crowd. Panic, in the Concurrent view, is not fear but refusal to coordinate.

Their work is equally inseparable from survival. Surveyors descend into weeping foundations and read mineral deposits as records of stress. Storm tenders climb the rotating vanes, carrying conductive kites on their backs. Occupancy arbiters decide which version of a room receives stability during a shortage. Faunal diplomats negotiate migrations by altering heat, light, and vibration rather than attempting to command the animals directly.

There are corrosion farmers who cultivate edible salts on sacrificial metal. Current shepherds guide plated filter-feeders away from intake valves. Memory masons teach damaged materials which former shape to recover. Density clerks search municipal plans for accidental emptiness and conceal certain gaps before the substrate can fill them. Their hidden vacancies—sometimes no larger than a closet—are treasured places of rest.

No one is wholly certain who governs the city.

There are councils, syndicates, weather authorities, transit minds, reservoir courts, hereditary custodians, neighborhood choirs, and old machine intelligences whose permissions remain embedded too deeply to revoke. Governance occurs through access: to pressure, passage, cooling, stable volume, dry hours, tolerable noise. Laws are often physical. A prohibited route does not display a sign; it ceases to align with the traveler’s door.

The city itself is not generally considered alive. That belief is dismissed as sentimental and imprecise. Yet inhabitants apologize when cutting into walls. They leave offerings of calibrated stress at the bases of ancient towers. Before dismantling a residence, they tap out its entire occupancy history so neighboring structures can remember it. When a tunnel curves to accommodate a current, people speak of its judgment. When a road seals over the dead, they speak of its mercy.

Death rarely produces an intact body. The deceased are separated according to usefulness and affinity. Mineral plates go to foundations. Conductive tissues enter civic batteries. Memory patterns may be impressed into doorframes, transit rails, or household objects. Some are dissolved into the warm water systems they maintained, becoming chemically present throughout a district. Others request incompatible occupancy: their recorded minds are placed in phased rooms that no longer stabilize, persisting only as momentary gestures when power flickers.

The most feared death is not decomposition but reassignment—the abrupt loss of one’s physical continuity during a phase failure. Memorials list names alongside room states, voltage conditions, and the last confirmed arrangement of matter. Survivors visit by recreating, briefly and imperfectly, the conditions under which the missing person vanished. For a fraction of a second, a pressure mark may appear on a chair, or a familiar vibration pass through the floor.

Their art favors recurrence over permanence. Composers write for ventilation systems whose performances take weeks to travel across the basin. Sculptors redirect condensation so that images appear only after rain. Choreographers coordinate flocks, traffic signals, bridge motions, and public crowds into patterns visible from the upper towers. Children grow delicate branching forms from electrocrystal and release small scavengers to eat them.

They do not depict plants except in works intended to disturb.

The legendary green world horrifies them because it suggests uncontrolled softness: surfaces obscured by growth, roots entering without permits, vast exposed ground that serves no structural function, living things nourished by naked light. To them, a forest is not peaceful. It is an infestation of vertical bodies standing in unusable soil beneath an dangerously vacant sky.

Their sacred images, by contrast, are dense.

A perfect place is one in which every being supports and is supported: a chamber inside a chamber, a current carrying traffic and heat and food, a wall strengthened by the creatures that bore through it, a name remembered simultaneously by flesh, mineral, and machine. Emptiness is not freedom. Emptiness is a failure of relationship.

Yet some inhabitants seek it.

They are called gap-keepers, hollowists, or, less kindly, the Unassigned. They preserve tiny spaces where nothing has been installed. They block municipal sensors, divert condensation, scrape away nesting organisms, and sit within these bare volumes experiencing an absence so complete that most citizens find it physically painful. Rumor tells of larger gaps: an unused platform, a dry courtyard, a roof from which only sky can be seen. The myths grow more obscene farther outward. There are said to be places where one may walk without touching a wall, where rain falls without being captured, where distance contains nothing at all.

Most Concurrent do not believe this.

They know that water must go somewhere, heat must pass into something, bodies must displace other bodies, and every life must be borne by a surface already bearing countless others. They understand existence as load.

When they greet one another, they do not ask, “Who are you?”

They ask, through touch or light or the trembling rail:

**What carries you?**

The answer may be a district, a household, a profession, an animal colony, a failing bridge, a debt of water, or the name of someone dead.

Then comes the answering courtesy:

**And what do you carry?**
