What lives there is not a settled family of species so much as life’s first, inexhaustible draft.

Its creatures appear newly devised. Their bodies possess the blunt confidence of forms that have never endured scarcity: soft where armor would be prudent, brilliant where concealment would help, slow beside open mouths. They have defenses—shells, spines, bitter skins, sudden speed—but these seem less like lessons learned than properties assigned at birth. Nothing bears the refinement of a long contest. Predator and prey fit together, yet neither has gained ground.

Nearest the water are innumerable small lives: silver flies that keep to their appointed patches of air; lacquer-backed beetles carrying globes of condensation; broad-winged hoverers suspended so perfectly that only the blur around them proves they are flying. Crawlers work beneath the floating soil, polishing roots with mouthparts and vanishing into the warm mud. Minute red mites gather on pale bark like spilled pigment. Pale larvae inhabit the cups of flowers and the pores of sweating stone.

The pools are crowded from surface to silt. Glass-bodied fish hang beneath the dim skin of the water, almost invisible except for their eyes and the dark machinery within them. Plump amphibians grip submerged stems with many-knuckled toes, their throats swelling in perpetual calls. Armored grazers rasp mineral films from new rock. Flat ambush-creatures lie under the bottom ooze, presenting only paired eyes and a fringe of breathing threads.

Deeper down, smooth giants travel the waterways. They have no obvious scales and few visible fins. Their long bodies bend as though made from dense muscle wrapped in gray skin, and their mouths open underneath, round and petaled. They pass without wakes because the basin closes over them as readily as flesh closes over a swallowed thing. They may be ancient in size, but not in years. Nothing here has had years.

Among the roots live bright communal lizards, piled flank to flank on the warm breathing plates. Their colors are jewel-deep—blue, copper, acid green—though gloom absorbs them at a short distance. Soft-shelled leaf-eaters nose through the fungal litter. Low, many-legged browsers shear moss from wood. Herds of heavier animals wade between islands, barrel-bodied and hairless, with wide feet that spread across the uncertain ground. Their young remain close among them, though the young never seem to become adults and the adults never stoop into age.

Long-legged birds patrol the shallows. Their oily feathers shed water in dark beads, and their narrow beaks open and close as they take one small creature after another. Above them circle larger winged beings whose anatomy is difficult to read against the clouds. Some seem feathered; others stretch membranes between elongated fingers; others have wings that are neither, ribbed like immense leaves. They follow fixed circuits beneath the ceiling, each returning endlessly over the same pool, the same pale grove, the same sweating stones. Perhaps they feed while flying. Perhaps they have no need to land. No nests are visible, though their offspring are present.

Predators live there too, but they are not rulers. Reed-colored hunters wait with narrow jaws held open, condensation collecting on their teeth. Heavy amphibious stalkers rest beneath mats of red plants. Root-mimicking constrictors hang in loops until a passing body gives them movement. Buried things seize downward; winged things seize upward; broad mouths rise from apparently empty water. Kills are abrupt and locally absolute. Then the basin seals around them. The survivors do not flee far, and the dead do not leave an absence.

Fungi inhabit everything that pauses. Yellow shelves push from fallen trunks; translucent threads net the mud; purple cups open in hoofprints and feed before the print can collapse. Carcasses soften beneath white veils and become crowded with mouths. Yet the decomposers are no more victorious than the predators. They consume perpetually without ever clearing the dead or deepening the soil.

No individual creature seems immortal. Bodies are pierced, swallowed, drowned, crushed, infected, and dismantled. But neither does any lineage advance through time. Eggs remain always newly laid. Hatchlings are always emerging. Juveniles are always beside adults. There are births without increase, deaths without loss, succession without replacement.

All these lives participate in a balance too exact to be called peace. Every feeder has food, every hunter prey, every corpse its consumers, every bare patch its covering growth. Hunger exists, but famine cannot. Violence exists, but catastrophe cannot. Fertility is everywhere, yet nothing spreads beyond its allotted abundance.

The basin itself may be the largest living thing there. Its root mats breathe as one tissue. Its waterways circulate warmth like slow blood. Its clouds preserve the moist enclosure of a vast egg. Animals move through it like cells in an organism that continually spends itself to maintain one unchanging instant.

They are the first inhabitants, forever.

Nothing descended from them will inherit the basin, because descent here has no direction. Nothing will adapt to its predators, exhaust its food, migrate beyond the haze, or leave bones compressed into stone. They live with no memory behind them and no altered world ahead: countless bodies feeding, calling, mating, hatching, killing, and dissolving beneath the fixed clouds.

And on every shell, scale, wing, hide, and open eye, the unfinished rain continues to gather.
