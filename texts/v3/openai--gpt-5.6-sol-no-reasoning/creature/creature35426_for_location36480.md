The people of the basin are slight, deliberate beings, built less for enduring heat than for passing cleanly between its extremes. They live in the narrow intervals between scalding light and subterranean cold, in chambers attached to cisterns, under the lee of cooling towers, and along the shaded sides of distillation walls.

Their skin is pale where it remains covered, but hands and faces are stained in durable mineral colors: rust around the nails, green at the wrists, a faint gray gloss across the cheeks from reflective clay. Most wear layered heat-cloaks with dark outer surfaces and damp inner folds. Hoods can be drawn across the face, leaving only a horizontal strip of smoked glass. Indoors, the same garments are reversed to retain warmth against the cold mist. Their footwear is broad, soft-soled, and patterned to distinguish a human track from those of shell-backed drain cleaners or young waders.

They are economical in movement. A basin citizen does not hurry across open ground unless the ground itself has become dangerous. They know which white surfaces conceal seepage, which red fans are merely stained, and which indicate a fresh subsurface break. They pause before thresholds to listen through their feet. Children learn to set down cups without a click and to catch a closing door with one hand. Clumsiness is treated not as bad manners but as a hazard to everyone nearby.

Their language is quiet and close. At a distance they speak with flashes: mirrors turned from the wrist, colored shutters on balconies, polished tags sewn to sleeves. Work crews use small pressure bulbs connected to communal pipes, sending coded pulses that can be felt through a palm laid against the wall. Speech is reserved for rooms, families, arguments, songs, and the naming of things too complicated for signal grammar.

Because a raised voice can summon what lives beneath the streets, anger has acquired a peculiar form. The furious become motionless. They lower their hands, lean near, and pronounce each word with perfect care.

Their settlements have no true center. Families identify themselves by a water circuit, a shade wall, or a condensation station rather than by a district. A household may occupy three or four separate rooms distributed along a pipe route: a hot sleeping chamber, a cold workroom, a kitchen beside a mineral bath, and a refuge above the flood line. Kinship follows maintenance obligations as much as blood. Those who tend the same cistern eventually become relatives in practice, sharing water allotments, tools, apprentices, and responsibility for any failure.

Among them, the most respected are the listeners.

Listeners kneel on tiled floors with cheekbones against ceramic plates. They interpret the city’s buried motions: the slow flex of a pump bladder, grit entering a valve, a blocked condensation run, the tunneling of a burrower colony. Experienced listeners can tell machinery from animals and animals from cooling stone. The oldest claim that every district has a distinct pressure signature, as recognizable as a voice.

They do not agree about the eastern stations.

Officially, the droplets heard in abandoned pipes are random releases caused by thermal contraction. Engineers chart them, demonstrate their irregularity, and forbid apprentices from answering. Nonetheless, certain maintenance families keep inherited records of the sequences. They insist that the noises recur only when someone stands listening, and that the stations sometimes echo signals sent into lines believed to terminate miles away.

No listener will sleep there.

The engineers form another broad order, though their work is closer to husbandry than command. They do not imagine the city as a machine imposed on the basin. To them it is a population of pressures that must be kept in balance. Pipes molt their mineral linings. Cisterns become fevered. Towers grow inefficient when their shaded faces acquire too smooth a crust. A good engineer encourages flow rather than forcing it, and knows when a damaged component should be abandoned to calcification instead of repaired.

Many wear small strings of ceramic gauges beneath their cloaks. These knock softly against one another only in private; in public each piece is wrapped separately.

Their dead are not buried. Digging risks warm outlets and burrower chambers, while burning is wasteful and dangerously noisy. Bodies are placed in cool mineral rooms where condensation coats them gradually in translucent stone. The family returns over many years to clear channels around the forming shell. Eventually the chamber is sealed into the waterworks. Thus ancestors remain inside the walls, incorporated into the city’s thermal mass, cold water moving past their mineral forms.

This is not spoken of as immortality. It is called becoming quiet.

The animals live beside them without ever becoming entirely tame.

The great six-legged waders are known individually along established migration routes. Each herd has favored seep fields and overflow courts, and each possesses a recognizable geometry when alarmed. Basin people name herds for these shapes: the Broken Ring, the Long Gate, the Two Spirals, the Scattered Hand. Their broad mouths comb microorganisms from water, leaving channels cleaner than any mechanical filter. Their reflective hides also reveal changes in mineral chemistry; a silver herd entering a contaminated seep may emerge tinted blue, yellow, or black.

The narrow followers at their joints are tolerated in dwellings because they consume salt crusts, mites, and soft deposits. They are inquisitive and fearless, slipping through drainage slots and sleeping around warm valves. Children lure them with damp ceramic chips, though adults discourage this. A follower that grows accustomed to being fed may abandon its herd and begin dismantling pipe seals in search of moisture.

The shell-backed reservoir creatures are more valued. Their tracks provide the city with a second language. Sharp paired grooves mean the water is clean and hot. Dragged shells indicate excessive salt. Tight circles show that they have sensed vibration below. Maintenance crews spread fine red sediment across suspect tiles at dusk and inspect the resulting marks at dawn.

Pale flyers nest nowhere anyone has found. They descend through airborne veils of microscopic life with their mouths open, but never touch roofs, towers, or stone. When exhausted, they seem to lock their translucent wings and remain suspended in rising heat. Dead flyers are almost unknown. Occasionally a fragment of wing is recovered from a cistern: clear, rigid, and veined like cooled glass.

The low predators are left unnamed in ordinary conversation. Naming one is thought to focus attention on it, and attention changes behavior. Engineers classify them by gait and hunting method, but citizens use gestures: two fingers laid flat for those that align with cracks, a knuckle drawn through water for trench hunters, a hand opening suddenly for the dark-limbed sprinters.

They are not loved. Neither are they exterminated. Without them, burrowers become sedentary and seal vents with dense colonies of shell and mucus. Then pressure gathers beneath streets until tiles lift or cistern walls rupture. Predators keep the underground population restless and the warm outlets open. At drinking courts, a person who spots one does not shout. They darken the nearest signal shutter, touch the warning pipe, and leave a clear line between hunter and open flats.

Beneath all of these lives the true multitude.

The basin’s microscopic organisms stain almost nothing, yet they occupy every film of water. They flourish at temperatures that divide larger creatures: one kind in the scalding southern reservoirs, another on cold condensation fins, others in the brief threads of rain. The city filters, ferments, and cultivates them. They are food, medicine, sealant, lubricant, and fuel. Black trays in the distillation yards gather entire harvests from water that appears empty.

There are also the burrowers, rarely seen whole. Workers encounter pale mouthparts in broken pipes, or jointed shells withdrawing beneath clay. Colonies respond to rhythm. Repeated impacts draw them upward, perhaps because subterranean drumming resembles water forcing a new channel. If they reach a machine foundation, they gather around its vibrations until the ground itself becomes crowded and soft.

When this happens, the district is not evacuated with alarms. Shutters change color one building at a time. Trams cease. Pumps slow out of sequence so no common pulse remains. Residents walk away in dispersed lines, carrying only water, shade cloth, and pressure maps. Then the predators arrive.

Children of the basin are taught that silence is not the absence of life but the shape made to accommodate it. Their games involve balance, reflection, and intricate stepping patterns across wet tiles. They race droplets down condensation fins. They send coded pressure taps through short clay tubes. On festival days, they stretch colored cloth above catchment courts and let reflected light travel from tower to tower—great noiseless processions visible across the entire basin.

Their music is performed in sealed rooms. It uses water columns, rubbed ceramic edges, breath, and strings too soft to carry through foundations. Public music is visual: shutters opening in measured sequences, mirrored arms turning on rooftops, workers crossing plazas in coordinated lines. Foreigners sometimes fail to recognize a celebration until they see half the city answering in light.

They eat what the waters yield. Thick mineral broths are strained from cultivated microorganisms and served hot in the cool quarters, cold in the furnace hours. Soft-bodied reservoir animals are raised in enclosed channels. Salt, of course, is everywhere, but edible salt requires careful separation from metals and bitter deposits. The wealthy possess not more water, but water of different temperatures: cold for drinking, warm for bathing, hot for cultivation, and nearly pure condensation for medicine.

Waste is a moral failure because almost nothing in the basin can simply be discarded. Material does not rot, and abandoned objects endure. Broken ceramics are crushed into reflective paving. Metal is dissolved and redeposited. Cloth is unraveled. Human waste feeds sealed microbial vats. Even spoken promises are often framed as circuits: what is received must return in another form.

At evening, people emerge from shade and work chambers as the animals approach the overflow courts. They sit along high ledges, faces uncovered at last, and watch the herds arrange themselves around the shallow bowls. No one reaches toward them. No one mistakes coexistence for possession.

Beyond the courts, the eastern stations begin to shed cold water.

The herds gather at a distance. Their followers hide beneath them. Pale flyers circle without descending. Sometimes every drinking animal lifts its head at once, though no audible sound has passed through the court.

Then, far below the tiled ground, something answers with a sequence of delicate pressure changes.

The citizens remain still.

Afterward, they say it was only the old pipes cooling. They collect the empty bowls, read the tracks in the mineral film, and go home by separate routes, careful not to let any door close hard behind them.
