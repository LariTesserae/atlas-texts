The summit is inhabited by custodians.

There are never more than six, though the bunkhouse contains six beds and one of them is always left untouched. The occupied mattresses shift from week to week; the empty one remains the bed nearest the downhill wall. Its blanket is folded tightly, its frame polished by condensation but never rusted. No custodian claims it. When asked, they say only that it belongs to the relief.

They are small, spare people, shaped by ladders, narrow passages, and steep ground. Their shoulders slope from moving sideways beneath conduits. Their feet turn instinctively across wet grating. They wear layered coats of waxed cloth, rubber aprons, and felted wraps around the knees and elbows. The oldest coats hanging in the bunkhouse are not relics but reserve skins, repaired so many times that little of the first garment remains.

Each custodian carries three things: a hooded lamp, a listening rod, and a packet of red chalk sealed against the damp. The rod is iron, broad at one end and narrow at the other. They press it to pipes, tanks, valve housings, and sometimes to bare stone, resting the tip against the bone just before the ear. Thus they hear what the machinery is doing beneath its noise. They can distinguish water from vapor, a failing bearing from loose scree, an empty conduit from one carrying pressure too great to measure.

They do not speak much while working. Speech is unreliable here. Water distorts it, turbines consume it, and echoes return from the wrong direction. Instead they use touches on railings, arrangements of chalk marks, and brief flashes of covered lamps. A circle means warmth where there should be cold. A broken line means a tremor has changed. Three descending strokes mean do not follow.

Their principal labor is not operating the works. The works operate themselves, or are operated from somewhere inaccessible. The custodians accompany them.

They clear roots from spillways but train other roots into filter beds. They skim old oil from pools with mats of dried moss. They grease hoists that may not lift anything for years. They count the intervals between the deep strokes and record them in ledgers whose pages buckle in the wet. Every gauge is read at dawn and dusk, even those with needles unchanged since before the oldest custodian was born.

At Second Heart, they lay a palm against the casing after each impact. At Upper Lung, they open a bronze petcock by exactly half a turn and collect the exhaled water in a black cup. They study its color, smell it, touch one drop to the underside of the wrist, and pour it—not into a drain, but onto the roots of the pale orchids.

No one drinks it.

They cultivate little except what the works seem willing to support. Mint grows in the oil drums, and a bitter herb with silver-veined leaves grows beside the electrical shed. The custodians chew this during long watches. Dwarf-tree fruit is gathered after it falls, then split and placed in settling tanks, where its dark flesh dissolves. The reason is uncertain. Asked whether the fruit feeds the filters, cleans the water, or pays for what the trees draw from the basin, the custodians give different answers.

The frogs are treated as junior inspectors. A frog sitting high in a drain mouth means the lower channel is filling. A frog facing outward means a pressure change is coming from below. If all the frogs disappear, the custodians stop whatever they are doing and move to the red-railed platforms. They do not resume work until one returns.

The moths receive less affection but greater reverence. Their pale bodies are sometimes found inside sealed cabinets, resting on live terminals without burning. A custodian who finds one does not brush it away. A small amber lamp is switched on nearby, and the panel is left open until the moth departs.

No one knows how the custodians are chosen. They arrive from the lower valleys singly, carrying little, usually after dreams of thirst, floods, or rooms breathing underground. Some are engineers. Some have never seen a gauge. The mountain strips those distinctions from them quickly. Competence here consists of attention: knowing which vibrations belong to the pump and which to the rock; knowing when a valve is stiff and when it is resisting; knowing that a familiar drip has stopped.

They age strangely. Their hair whitens early, but their faces remain smooth and wet-looking. Their nails become stained orange at the edges. After years on the summit, they develop a slight pause between inhaling and exhaling, timed unconsciously to the great pump below. In sleep, all five—or all six, if one counts the untouched bed—seem to breathe together.

They descend rarely. A custodian may spend a decade above the tree line and then appear without warning in a valley market, bewildered by dust. They buy salt, lampwick, paper, needles, and bells. They never buy water. Valley people avoid accepting coins from them because the metal is always warm.

When one leaves permanently, no farewell is made. Their coat remains on its numbered hook. Their name is crossed from the ledger, but never erased. Sometimes they die below. Sometimes they vanish into the sealed tunnel at the head of the narrow-gauge line, riding the small maintenance trolley into darkness with tools laid neatly beside them. The tunnel door is found closed afterward, though no one waiting outside hears it move.

The bell on the high catwalk belongs to them, but none admits to ringing it.

When it sounds once, every custodian stops. Listening rods are lifted. Hands leave wheels and levers. Even the routine overflow gates are allowed to run unattended. They wait through the silence between pump strokes.

Usually the next stroke arrives.

Occasionally it does not.

Then the custodians move with the only speed they ever display. Lamps open. Chalk packets tear. One runs to the Lung, another to the Kidney, two descend into the turbine vault, and the eldest goes alone to the eastern brink. There, clipped to a rail above the kilometer fall, the eldest turns a wheel concealed among roots.

What follows is not the restarting of an engine. It is more like provoking a reflex.

Pressure vessels contract. Water retreats from pools in spirals. The dwarf trees draw their leaves shut. Every frog cries once from inside the drains, a sound almost too low to hear. Far beneath the summit, something answers with a concussion that sends warm mist bursting from fissures across the lower slopes.

Afterward the delayed stroke comes, immense and slow.

On such nights the custodians set a bowl of rainwater beside the untouched bed. By morning it is empty.

Yet they may not be the deepest inhabitants of the summit.

The precision of the oldest masonry does not resemble valley work. Behind certain repairs, black blocks meet in curves like sockets or chambers, their surfaces worn smooth on the inward side. Mineral deposits found in closed pipes form layered ridges too regular to be accidental, like enamel or growth rings. The pumps themselves have replaceable housings, numbered bolts, and makers’ plates—but their shafts disappear into apertures cut through the basalt long before the steel machinery was fitted there.

The custodians say the builders discovered circulation already occurring within the mountain. They merely gave it valves.

What circulates is not wholly water. It carries heat, salts, metallic tastes, and at times a faint opacity like diluted milk. It rises through one seam and descends through another. It keeps forests green far below. It feeds springs that emerge hundreds of kilometers away. It may also carry messages, though no custodian will use that word. They speak instead of correspondences: a pressure drop followed by rain on the northern plain; a clogged filter followed by fever in a distant village; a long, uneven pulse followed by new fissures opening in the mountain’s flank.

Thus the mountain itself may be what lives there—not metaphorically, and not in any way that permits a clean distinction between organism and geology.

It is young because it is still forming. Its sharp ridges are not merely uneroded; they are unfinished. Its mineral seams are exposed tissues hardening in air. The summit basin is a damp hollow where its inner processes approach the surface, vulnerable enough to require tending. The machinery is brace, prosthesis, parasite, and life-support system at once. Perhaps it keeps the mountain alive. Perhaps it keeps the mountain asleep. Perhaps those are the same task at this stage of its growth.

The lush garden is part of that body too. Roots enter cracks the pipes cannot reach. Moss measures seepage more sensitively than gauges. Reeds draw metals from overflow pools and turn copper-colored as they accumulate them. Orchids bloom where subterranean warmth approaches the skin. Nothing grazes because the vegetation is not exactly food. It is filtration made leaf and stem.

Even the swift has a function. It comes once or twice each day, cuts through the basin, and leaves without landing. The custodians look up when it passes and note the direction in their ledgers. They believe it follows a current rising from the abyss—an exhalation too slight for instruments, carrying the mountain’s heat into the sky.

And beneath everything, lower than tunnels, shafts, and pressure vessels, there may be another resident: the thing for which the organ names were first used.

No one has seen it. There is no chamber large enough to contain it. It is inferred from delay, pressure, and response. When water is sent downward, something changes its route. When valves close, something strains. When the deep pump strikes, the vibration does not radiate evenly through the rock. It travels along branching paths, gathers beneath the basin, and returns softened, as though passing through enormous yielding walls.

The custodians do not worship it. Worship would imply distance.

They maintain its weather. They keep grit from its circulation. They listen for hesitation in its buried rhythm. They sleep above it as mites might sleep in the fold of a warm hide, except that mites are not usually entrusted with the heart.

At evening, when the amber windows shine among the ferns, the five visible custodians gather briefly outside the central pump house. They compare chalk marks on their sleeves. One checks the depth of the rain bowl. One counts the frogs. One looks toward the empty sixth bed through the bunkhouse window.

Then the pump completes another stroke.

All of them sway slightly with the mountain.

For an instant the pipes, roots, wet cliffs, human bodies, hidden water, and forests far below belong to one connected motion. Then the motion passes. The custodians separate and return to their stations, and the summit’s inhabitants—those with names, those without them, and the mountain that may contain them all—continue their slow, unfinished life.
