The people of the unfinished country are difficult to separate from its animal multitudes.

This is partly because they are small, seldom standing much higher than a crawler’s armored back, and partly because they do not gather in settlements large enough to distinguish themselves at a distance. They travel in loose companies among the herds, shelter under living shells, cling to crowded ledges, and vanish into crevices when the stone grows bright. A stranger might watch a migration for hours before noticing that some of the figures moving between the stilt-legged beasts carry bowls, hooks, folded bridges, and sleeping young.

Their bodies are low and flexible, made for corridors that can narrow without warning. They have four weight-bearing limbs and, above them, a smaller pair of deft grasping arms tucked close to the chest. The broad feet divide into many blunt, sensitive digits. Each tests heat, vibration, and the brittleness of a surface before committing weight. On reliable stone they can move quickly; across a fresh crust they advance with a wavering, many-footed delicacy, seeming almost to listen downward.

Their skin is pale and finely pleated, translucent at the throat, joints, and belly. Warm blood—or something serving its purpose—can be seen darkening and paling beneath it. Mineral dust adheres readily to this damp skin, so individuals take on the colors of the districts through which they pass: chalk-white, smoke-gray, sulfur-yellow, bruised violet, rust-red. Older people become maps of vanished passages. Hardened films accumulate in their wrinkles and around old wounds until their bodies carry little ridges of stone.

They have no eyes in the ordinary sense. Across the upper face lies a fan of thin mineralized plates, delicate as the gills of a mushroom. These register air currents, pressure changes, radiant heat, and the shifting brightness of stressed rock. The fan can spread wide in calm chambers or fold into a protected groove when rubble falls. Beneath it are paired hollows sensitive to sound, while the lower jaw rests close to the chest and receives vibrations through the skeleton.

They perceive a chamber as an intersection of tensions. A wall is not chiefly its shape but its warmth, dampness, taste, and inner murmur. Distance is measured by echoes and drafts. Approaching creatures are patterns in the floor. Stone under strain announces itself as light against their facial fans and as an ache in their teeth.

For this reason their languages are only partly airborne. They click plates in the mouth, pulse their throats, scrape tools over rock, and drum with heel or knuckle. Much of a conversation passes through whatever surface the speakers share. In loud halls they speak by touching wrist to wrist and sending tiny sequences of taps through the bones. Names are rhythms. A person’s name can be sounded on a wall, carried along a copper plate, or felt through the shell of a sleeping beast.

They do not name places permanently. A chamber is called something like *Where the Ceiling Answers in Threes*, *The Bitter Drip Beneath the Warm Wind*, or *After the Silver Swimmers, Before the Red Water*. When conditions change, so does the name. A person returning after a long absence may know every stone and still be told that the place they remember no longer exists.

Their communities form around predictive skill rather than possession. Those who can read the brightening ceilings, hear water beyond sealed rock, or interpret the sudden stillness of burrowers carry great authority. Such individuals are not always elders. Children, with their thin bones and sensitive facial fans, sometimes detect tremors adults miss. During a crossing, an entire company may obey the youngest among them without hesitation.

They keep animals, though ownership is too simple a word for the arrangements. Coin-shaped wall clingers are transferred onto portable slabs and carried as sources of vibration, food, medicine, or warning. Thread-fliers are coaxed into membrane cages; their agitation reveals changes in air chemistry. Small blind scrapers are released onto fresh deposits to thin paths through them. Some families travel beneath broad-backed crawlers for generations, cleaning their armor, harvesting parasites and secretions, and defending the luminous undersides from predators. The crawlers in turn provide warmth, shelter, and a moving island above sudden floods.

Nothing useful is allowed only one purpose. A bowl catches water, amplifies tremors, shelters dormant swimmers, and becomes a resonating instrument when inverted. A membrane cord is a tether, bridge rail, surgical ligature, or line for transmitting taps through a noisy camp. Copper plates warn of strain, summon scattered companions, and preserve routes as sequences of pitches. Thin blades are made from stone that will soften after repeated wetting, because a permanent blade is less valuable than one that can be reshaped when the world changes.

Their shelters are built quickly and left without mourning. Roof plates lean against walls rather than being fixed to them. Sleeping hollows are lined with shed armor, woven membrane, and colonies of warm coin-creatures. Entrances face not away from danger but toward several different means of escape. Every refuge has at least one deliberately weak wall, marked so it can be kicked through if the main passage seals.

They sleep in linked clusters, feet and hands touching. If one member feels an unfamiliar vibration, the whole group wakes through transmitted movement without a cry. Infants are placed at the center in flexible cradles suspended above the floor, safe from shallow floods and able to sway rather than strike stone during tremors.

Their young are soft-faced and nearly boneless in the sensory fan. For the first seasons they are carried in ventral folds or in slings against the body, where they learn the rhythms of walking before they learn to walk. Education begins as prediction. Adults press a child’s palm to a sweating wall and wait. They teach which ticking means crystal growth, which means imminent fracture, which silence belongs to a predator, and which to a chamber holding itself still before collapse.

To become adult, one must guide others through a place that is changing. The route cannot be chosen beforehand. Companions follow while the candidate reads water, animals, brightness, and stone. Success is not reaching a predetermined destination, but recognizing when the intended journey has become impossible and finding another.

Their handprints are the nearest thing they make to lasting records. A print pressed into soft limestone may include dots made by fingertips, nail cuts, embedded shell fragments, or streaks of metal. These additions identify the individual and circumstances: birth, migration, warning, debt, death, discovery of water. Rows of prints are read by touch as much as sight.

Yet they do not expect the records to endure. Preservation is considered an accident, sometimes a troubling one. A handprint that survives many generations belongs to conditions no longer present and may mislead the living. Their historians travel instead. They carry route-songs, remembered tremor patterns, accounts of vanished chambers, and genealogies tied to animal migrations. History is not what remains unchanged. It is the chain of adjustments by which someone survived.

Death comes often and is treated without surprise, but not without grief. Bodies are seldom buried; the ground cannot be trusted to keep them, and flesh left exposed draws dangerous swarms. The dead are usually carried to active mineral seeps. There they are folded into hollows and coated gradually in translucent deposit. Some become pale, distorted forms within walls before pressure splits or swallows them. Others are consumed first by scavengers, leaving only impressions.

A company may return to such a place if it remains accessible. They touch the hardened outline and sound the dead person’s rhythm against it. If the wall has vanished, the rhythm is sounded wherever the loss is remembered. Thus the dead persist not at a location but as patterns carried in living bones.

Their greatest fear is not collapse. Collapse is expected, and often survivable. What terrifies them is false stability: a silent floor that has not shifted for too long, a pool whose level does not change, a corridor whose drafts remain constant through many migrations. Such places invite dependence. Dependence invites numbers, stored goods, fixed routes, and the belief that tomorrow will resemble today. In their stories, disasters begin when people mistake delay for permanence.

Still, they are not merely fugitives from the stone. They are curious about it, and sometimes affectionate toward its transformations. They gather at a safe distance to watch new ridges rise. Children compete to predict where a fracture will run. When luminous veins flare across a ceiling, whole camps unfold their facial fans and stand in the cream-white brilliance, reading the light together.

Their music resembles the country that made them. It is played on hanging metal, hollow armor, resonant columns, water-filled bowls, and stretched membranes. Performances change whenever the cavern answers. A new drip enters the rhythm; distant herds add thunder; a cracking shelf forces the players to move without breaking the pattern. The most admired compositions are those that cannot be repeated because they include a collapse, a flood, or the opening of a chamber that no longer exists.

At times several companies converge in one of the greater halls. Thousands gather among the pale towers while traders exchange metal, membranes, dormant swimmers, hardened medicines, animal colonies, and news of the shifting world. Routes are drummed through the floor. Disputes are judged by witnesses who compare remembered vibrations. Young people join other traveling groups. Old songs are corrected. New dangers receive names.

Then the ceiling brightens.

Every facial fan turns upward. Traders stop with goods still extended. The herds fold their sensory bones. Fliers descend in pale clouds. Somewhere beneath the market, a low note enters the stone.

No bell is needed. Shelters come apart in moments. Bridges, bowls, young, and warning plates are lifted. Some companies move toward warm drafts, others toward higher shelves, others behind the broad-backed crawlers already pushing through the crowd. What seemed like a city becomes migration before the first crack opens.

Afterward, when the floor has risen and a new white ridge divides the hall, they return from every side.

They test it with their feet. They listen. They set down their portable slabs so the small clinging animals can feed.

Then someone presses a hand into the still-soft stone.
