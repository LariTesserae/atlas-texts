The plateau is inhabited less by separate organisms than by arrangements of patience.

First are the mantles: old vegetal commonwealths fitted to the stone so closely that they seem to be its living surface. Each rounded cushion is composed of innumerable shoots, none important by itself. Their outer leaves are minute, hard, and mirror-haired; beneath them lie warmer chambers where resin moves slowly through braided stems. A mantle can outlive the ledge that supports it. As the rock flakes away, the colony folds inward, digests trapped mineral grains, and lays down a dark, leathery foundation made from its own dead centuries.

Within these cushions dwell translucent hexapods no larger than seeds. Their bodies are clear enough that the black-green oils inside them show through. They rasp wax, prune fungal threads, carry spores between the mantle’s chambers, and gather into many-legged knots when the cold becomes too severe. A whole population may spend generations beneath a single stone plate, knowing the outer plateau only as changes in pressure, brightness, and vibration.

Elsewhere grow the sky-feeders.

They stand in clefts on trunks like bundles of bleached reeds fused together. By day they are narrow and sealed, their crowns clenched into pale fists. At dusk the fists loosen. Thousands of hollow vanes unfold and interlock, forming nets broad enough to tremble in the descending air. Dust adheres to charged hairs. Salts lodge in graduated sieves. Organic traces and frost molecules are drawn into wax-lined chambers. At sunrise the crown collapses around its catch, and acids circulate upward through the porous trunk. What appears to be a tree is also a filter, a stomach, and a weather instrument.

The oldest sky-feeders become communities in their own right. Silver filaments occupy the shaded hollows of their trunks. Amber bladders root in accumulated dust between their fibers. Small grazers climb into their folded crowns and remain there through daylight, protected from the killing brilliance. When an ancient trunk finally breaks, it does not rot. It frays. The wind carries its hollow fibers along the basin floor, where they snag against stone and sometimes become the first shelter of a new grove.

Across the exposed plains travel the long browsers.

They are high-legged but not massive, built with the economy of creatures that may go months without feeding. Their skins are pale and finely plated, reflecting noon glare while radiating little heat after sunset. Along their backs lie paired sails, pleated flat during the day. At dusk these rise and open into thin sensory fans. Through them the browsers taste the plateau’s moving air: the resin signature of a grove, the metallic breath of an electrical storm, the faint bitterness of overgrazed cushions hundreds of kilometers away.

They do not herd closely. A migration may consist of individuals separated by entire horizons, all following the same invisible plume. Their broad feet spread over salt crust without breaking it. Their mouths do not tear plants up; narrow combs pass over a mantle’s surface, harvesting wax, spores, and sacrificial leaf tips. By the time the animals return—perhaps years later—the colony has repaired itself.

Their young are born in the thermal shelter of wind-cut ravines. For the first season they possess no dorsal sails and follow a parent by scent at distances of only a few body lengths. Later the sails grow, darken, and become sensitive enough to detect the chemistry of remote vegetation. Adulthood begins when the young creature can cross a bare basin alone.

Above them, though seldom seen, live the escarpment gliders.

At rest they cling beneath overhangs and become indistinguishable from pale mineral sheets. Their wings are not used for beating flight. They are broad membranes stiffened by branching rods, made to enter the nocturnal rivers of cold air that pour from the heights. A glider releases its grip, falls briefly, and is carried outward in silence.

It hunts by lowering filamentous organs toward the ground. These do not listen to sound so much as to tremors passing through contiguous stone: a browser’s footfall, the rasping colony beneath a slab, the minute crackle of shells disturbed in a cushion grove. Most signals are ignored. The glider circles for hours, losing height so slowly that it appears fixed against the stars. When it strikes, it folds into a narrow pale weight. There is one scatter of pebbles, perhaps one dry clack, and then stillness.

Yet even the predator is parsimonious. It may feed only a few times in a season. Between hunts it hangs in torpor beneath a sunless ledge, its blood replaced in part by oils nearly as dark and slow as plant resin.

Other lives are easier to mistake for geology.

Violet fans spread over mineral crusts and dissolve them grain by grain. Rust-colored mats occupy the etched channels left behind. Silver threads descend from ledges, gathering electrical charge until their tips glow blue before a storm. Amber bladders pivot after the sun, swelling with gases manufactured in the light; at maturity they detach and roll across the flats, shedding spores whenever they strike stone.

There are also organisms that live entirely within rock. They inhabit salt fissures and translucent crystals, expanding during the warm hours and withdrawing at night. Their growth helps split cliffs, though no individual survives long enough to complete a fracture. Generation succeeds generation along the same microscopic fault until a slab falls away centuries later. In that sense, some of the plateau’s ravines are biological works—made without design, ownership, or any creature ever witnessing the result.

Nothing here builds because nearly everything here borrows.

A crack is a nursery. An undercut is a winter refuge. A geological seam is a road and orchard. The descending night air is both river and migration corridor. The shadow of a mountain, passing across the land, is a temporary climate in which crowns open, animals emerge, and buried resins begin to move.

Even reproduction is entrusted to the country’s existing motions. Spores wait for dust pillars. Eggs adhere beneath exfoliating stone. Seeds open only after electrical discharge has altered their skins. Some groves release their reproductive cells into the invisible pools of cold air that collect before dawn; as the air warms and rises, an entire generation is lifted away.

No species appears to command the plateau. None transforms it quickly enough to stand apart from it. Life follows the mineral grain, accepts the available shelter, and proceeds at rates scarcely distinguishable from erosion.

At night, the distinctions blur further. Resin channels shine beneath the cushions. Sky-feeder crowns pulse amber as stored energy descends through their trunks. Tiny grazers flicker under translucent leaves. Far out on the flats, the raised sails of traveling browsers show for moments as dim green crescents, then vanish when the animals turn.

Higher still, a glider crosses the stars like a loose shard of darkness.

Thus the plateau’s inhabitants reveal themselves: not as a crowded world concealed within an empty one, but as a second, slower geography laid over the first. Groves are archipelagos. Browsers are moving islands. Air-rivers carry invisible harvests. Predators drift like weather. The oldest colonies have contours, interiors, climates, and buried histories of their own.

They do not break the silence.

They give it structure.
