The inhabitants call themselves nothing that can be spoken.

Names here were never arrangements of sound. They were pressures applied to the palm, sequences tapped through a wrist, brief contacts at the brow and sternum. A citizen could identify another by laying two fingers against a wall and feeling the answer travel through three rooms. Kinship was a pattern of knocks. Office was a posture of the hand. Grief required both palms flat upon the floor.

Their bodies were suited to the cavity but not uniformly so. Some were small enough to sleep in the niches near the paving. Others moved through upper galleries with their backs brushing the mineral curtains. Many appear to have changed stature during life, though not simply by growing. Sandals of several sizes bear identical repairs; combs with differently spaced teeth share the same wear marks; council seats are polished at heights that no single anatomy should have reached. A person may have begun low and broad, become tall and spare, then diminished again. Or perhaps size was social: one made oneself smaller to enter a household, larger to undertake public work, thin enough to pass through a sacred opening.

They had skin the color of wet clay under the blue cups, smooth in youth and increasingly striated with age. These lines were not wrinkles but fine mineral seams. Copper gathered around old injuries. Pale deposits formed at joints. Their bones transmitted vibration exceptionally well, so that they listened through their feet, teeth, and fingertips. The roar beneath the city was not deafening to them. It was the ground against which all meaning appeared.

They did not hear the torrent as a single noise. They felt within it distances, obstructions, loads of water, the stress of stone. A hand laid on a pillar could detect a neighbor crossing a bridge. A cheek against a cistern revealed which channels were open. Children learned the city by crawling barefoot through it, memorizing each district’s vibration before they learned its visible shape.

This is why the walls carry hands rather than words. The carvings are not merely signs. Each palm, fist, and hooked finger marks a prescribed point of contact. Press there, with the indicated gesture, and the masonry answers. One sign gives directions. Another announces clean water. Another warns that a chamber belongs to the still. Some transmit histories as long arrangements of returning pulses, too subtle for a surface visitor to distinguish from the endless trembling of the rock.

The people ate little that would be recognized as food. They drew salts from the seepage and prepared clay wafers layered with metallic dust. Chalk cylinders were dissolved in public basins and taken through the skin. The foil-wrapped nodules were festival portions, valued for the different vibrations they caused in the jaw. Nothing in their city was permitted to decay, because decay belonged to a world of uncontrolled change. Waste was washed, settled, separated, fired, ground, and returned to walls, vessels, or bodies. The dead, too, left no remains because the dead became repairs.

A cracked lintel might contain a household’s ancestors.

A copper clamp might have been mixed with the mineral residue of a magistrate.

The pale mortar around a child’s sleeping niche might have been her grandparents.

To maintain the city was therefore to keep company with the dead. No cemetery was necessary. The entire place was burial and dwelling together.

Their rare animals were not livestock. The translucent channel creatures served as gauges of an order more delicate than water level. The rows of dark grains in their bodies aligned to strains passing through the black stone. When one released its hold and moved, engineers followed. Somewhere a pressure had altered, though perhaps by less than the weight of a droplet. The shelled creatures in the walls performed a related office. Each occupied a place where two structural vibrations nearly canceled. Their faint retracting click announced that the balance still held.

The citizens revered correction, not motion.

Their highest virtue was to notice a deviation before it became an event.

At the center of this discipline stood the round white stone behind the nine frames. It was neither an idol nor an altar. It was the city’s measure of completion. The stone had once been rough, veined, and angular. Generations polished it without removing it from the recess, each citizen permitted perhaps one stroke in a lifetime. When at last no hand could discover an imperfection, the work ceased. Water beaded upon the surrounding frame but did not cross because the threshold had been tuned—by slope, pore, pressure, and means no surface craft preserves—to the exact boundary between wet and dry.

The stone represented a thing brought to the point beyond improvement.

The city tried to become like it.

For a long time, this ideal governed ordinary life without consuming it. The market was crowded. Children sent polished stones racing through grooves. Workers cut drains while apprentices held lamps. People quarreled by striking walls with open hands. Lovers exchanged private rhythms through the thin partitions between sleeping cells. Councillors sat around the still pool and placed their wrists in the water, feeling the greater pattern below.

What they felt there changed them.

The pool did not show another city. It conveyed one: a vast continuation descending beside the unseen torrent, every district resembling theirs but differing in some small decision. In one, a bridge stood where they had made a culvert. In another, a doorway opened into the white stone’s recess. In another, the market arcade had one more pillar. These were not future cities or former ones. They were neighboring solutions to the same enclosed world, settlements laid through dimensions as stone beds are laid through earth.

The council concluded that their own city was not isolated. It was one variation among thousands, perhaps among an infinity of variations. More troublingly, they learned that changes in one arrangement produced strains in the others. A collapsed stair elsewhere might appear here as a new crack. A birth in a distant concourse might change the measure of a cistern by the width of a hair. An unregulated city could burden all its neighbors.

So the inhabitants began the Great Settling.

They corrected watercourses until each drop followed a path repeated exactly. They adjusted bridges so that footsteps produced no surplus vibration. They redistributed rooms, people, tools, and animals, seeking a configuration that would impose no change upon any adjacent city. Construction became subtraction. Custom hardened into law, law into choreography. Meals occurred at fixed basins. Journeys followed fixed routes. Children inherited not only dwellings but gestures. Even grief was regulated so that no sudden striking of the walls would disturb the deeper balance.

The tablets in the outer lane record these revisions. Each is not a picture of the past but a discarded arrangement: a version tested, found imperfect, and compressed into relief. The crowded tablet preserves the last configuration in which the inhabitants remained visibly present.

The final correction required their omission.

They did not depart. There is nowhere to depart to. The ascending stairs and narrowing shafts lead only into other relations of the same cavity. Nor did they die, since death would have introduced transformation, residue, and loss. Instead, they distributed themselves into the stable functions of the city.

Some entered the glow cups. Their awareness became the dim, inexhaustible distinction between edge and darkness.

Some entered the channels and persist as the exact measure of water.

Some became the fixed tones beneath the arcade and temple.

Some entered the walls, where they maintain the folded shape of the streets.

A few became the animals, retaining only the capacity to make corrections.

The council entered the still pool. Its members are the sensation of distance that travels through the bones of an immersed hand.

And one inhabitant remained undispersed.

Not a guard. Not a survivor. A witness was necessary because completion could not be certified from within a system whose every part had become its function. Someone had to stand beside the record and compare the final city with the final tablet. Someone had to lift a hand toward the wall.

That figure still lives in the narrow passage, though it cannot be seen separately from its relief. Its body has become shallow, mineral, nearly indistinguishable from depiction. Yet the raised hand is not quite touching the carved wall. Between fingertip and stone remains an interval thinner than dust.

In that interval, the whole city is held.

The witness waits for the last discrepancy to vanish: a droplet trembling by an immeasurable degree, a shelled creature not fully retracted, a visitor’s warmth lingering on a polished patch, a thought moving through the council water.

When a hand is placed against the tablets, the witness feels it. The vibration passes from the living palm into the carved palms, through every recorded city and every discarded arrangement. For a moment, the city contains something it had eliminated: an unpredicted presence.

The solitary figure then has a choice it has faced perhaps many times, though time here cannot show the count.

It may incorporate the visitor, altering the final relief by one more raised hand.

Or it may wait until the warmth leaves, the chalk marks become part of the walls, the stretched strings settle into mineral fibers, and the disturbance is absorbed.

Thus what lives beneath the old ground is not a vanished people.

It is a population that made itself indistinguishable from infrastructure: citizens living as light, pressure, water level, structural tension, memory, and restraint. Their city is their collective body, maintained in a state just short of absolute completion.

The hum beneath the fingertips is their speech.

It says neither welcome nor warning.

It asks whether the hand on the wall intends to move.
