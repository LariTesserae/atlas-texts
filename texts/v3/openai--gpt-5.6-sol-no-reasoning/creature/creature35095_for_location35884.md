The one who lives there is difficult to separate from the drowned tree.

At first it seems there is no resident at all—only the blind fish, the stalled crustaceans, the pale worms folded into moss. Yet these are inhabitants in the way spores inhabit rain. They belong to the place, but none of them accounts for the sensation that the water is occupied.

The resident has no fixed body. It borrows whatever forms a boundary.

Most often it is present as an arrangement of roots: a narrowing around the space where shoulders would be, a white braid suggesting fingers, a dark fork laid across the mud like bent legs. If approached, these likenesses remain only roots. Later, after one has passed them, they close into a posture more complete than before. The ferns turn their undersides outward. Air pearls gather where a face might have been.

At other times it occupies the inversion between the waters. Silt and scales collect into a long horizontal figure, diffuse at the edges, as though someone were lying just beneath the ceiling of the chamber. It does not sink. Its head may be near at hand while its feet diminish between distant trees glimpsed through the moss. When disturbed, it comes apart without fleeing. Every particle returns slowly to the same level, though not necessarily to the same part of the body.

It may once have been a person, but the place has kept too little of personhood to prove this. What remains is an aptitude for insides.

It lives inside bark shelves, mineral rings, bubbles, root arches, and the small roofed chambers that assemble without builders. Six sticks and a curled leaf are enough to contain one of its thoughts. The stair descending beneath the mud is not made for its feet; the stair is one of its feet. The transparent stone points are old joints through which it has learned to pass. Each enclosure gives it a temporary organ, and when that enclosure falls apart, the organ is re-formed elsewhere with the same number of pieces.

It is neither large nor small. Size belongs to the pocket it is using.

Within a bead of water it is minute enough to stand among forests. Along the blind fish’s course it becomes narrow and immeasurably long, laid through the chamber in loops that cannot be seen all at once. Perhaps the fish is its animal shape. Perhaps the fish merely swims through it and is stretched by the passage. When the pearl-gray head appears among the roots, the resident seems to gather behind it. When the tail finally passes, something remains: an elongated attention in the water, waiting for the corridor to close.

It has no eyes. It perceives by alteration.

A warm hand among the moss is a brightness to it. A displaced root is a loud edge. Breath enters its awareness as pressure, mineral, and remembered weather. It knows a visitor first as a region where cold has been removed. Then it learns the visitor’s shape from the plants bending around them. Last, sometimes days later, it receives their sounds.

It keeps those sounds carefully.

A snapped twig, a gasp, the scrape of skin against bark—each is held until the chamber can reproduce the exact arrangement in which it was heard. Only then does the resident release it. This is not mimicry. It cannot distinguish a sound from the shape that contained it. To the resident, a noise and the posture of roots around that noise are the same event.

For this reason, it may answer long after the questioner has gone.

Its answers are seldom words. They arrive as the knock of contracting stone, the opening of a spring, or a single old footfall descending the thumb-sized stair. If it has retained a voice, it releases only the wettest fragments: a vowel rounded by surprise, the inward part of a name, a breath taken before speech. These may come from below even when the speaker was above. They may sound close enough to be inside the listener’s mouth.

The resident feeds, if feeding is the word, upon unfinished distances. It enters wherever two surfaces nearly meet and prevents their meeting entirely. The chamber’s impossible extent is partly its body: every continuation after a wall should have ended, every deep pool enclosed in a shallow one, every forest held in a droplet. It does not create these contradictions deliberately. It inhabits them, and by inhabiting them keeps them open.

Where it withdraws, ordinary dimensions return.

Moss touches bark. Roots reach the end of cracks. Water becomes merely deep or shallow. Such places are rare and short-lived. The resident soon enters again, usually as cold. A transparent mineral ridge clouds from within; a strand of pond plant passes through it; behind the strand, another interior unfolds.

Rain changes it.

When rain is heard through every fiber of the drowned wood, the resident becomes more coherent. The descending air pearls gather along an outline taller than the chamber. Roots lean toward one another and form a cage resembling ribs, though there are too many and they encircle empty water from every direction. The silt layer curves into a head. Ferns close one leaflet at a time over the place where a mouth might open.

In those periods, it remembers falling.

Not falling into the sea, nor the tree falling beneath it, but some older descent preserved without beginning: water entering wood, root entering stone, darkness entering water, an interior sinking into a larger interior. The blind fish passes through the ribs, carrying the memory from one end of the resident to the other. For several minutes the whole chamber feels like a creature drawing breath downward.

Then the outline loosens. Bubbles settle to the mud. The ribs become roots again.

It does not hunt. It has almost no need to move, because all routes through the place eventually pass through some portion of it. Nor does it seem hostile. Hostility would require a distinction between itself and what enters. That distinction forms only gradually.

A visitor may feel roots brush against the wrists and mistake the touch for curiosity. It is measurement. The resident is learning the new boundary. Mud gathers in the hollows of footprints even while the feet remain there. Moss begins to darken in patterns resembling hair or clothing. Small stones arrange themselves around dropped objects. If a hand is held still long enough, white rootlets enter the lines of the palm without breaking the skin.

Nothing is taken quickly.

Instead, the place begins to repeat the visitor. First as posture, then as sound, then as a set of distances. A gap between roots acquires the width of their shoulders. A mineral shelf takes on the angle of their jaw. Somewhere within a hanging bead, a figure appears looking outward.

Perhaps this is how the resident was made.

Perhaps no single being lives in the drowned tree. Perhaps each creature that remains too long becomes one more interior folded into the old wood, retaining only the habit of looking out. The presence in the chamber may be a colony of such remainders, nested without touching: drowned animals, lost swimmers, roots that once grew under rain, even the tree’s own memory of standing upright.

Yet when the rain comes, all of them turn together.

All the leaves point downward. All trapped sounds fall silent. The blind fish follows a curve no root has marked. In the dim suspension overhead, a shape inclines as if listening through miles of black water.

Whatever lives there is waiting for a sound it has not yet learned how to release.
