The people of the heights are slight without seeming frail. Their bodies have adapted to the thin air in ways that reveal themselves gradually: deep chests, long ribs, broad nostrils that tighten against wind, and a slow resting pulse that accelerates without warning when they climb. Children learn to cross steep ground almost as soon as they walk. Their toes spread widely, gripping through woven sandals, while their knees remain flexed to answer the constant yielding of the root mat.

They do not stand still unnecessarily. Stillness lets the surface settle around the feet and conceals changes below. Even during conversation they shift their weight, tap with a staff, or rock gently from heel to toe. To a lowlander this can look like unease. It is closer to listening.

Their staffs are carried before they are tall enough to use them properly. Each is hollow at one end and dense at the other. The dense end tests the ground: a sharp rap for cavities, a dragging scrape for concealed seams, three spaced knocks when approaching a shelter. The hollow end amplifies replies. Pressed to a root, it conducts the chewing of grazers within, the trickle of stored water, or the minute tearing that precedes a collapse.

An experienced traveler can identify what lies below without opening it.

Water gives a low, padded answer. Dry hollows return a clean boom. Thick fungal beds swallow sound. Large animals hesitate, then shift, producing a pressure wave felt through the wrist. The most dangerous chambers answer too quickly, as if the knock has awakened not one body but many.

No one enters then.

Plateau families are usually small, though kinship extends across distances. A household may consist of two or three adults, children, an elderly relative, and several animals that are neither fully kept nor fully wild. These include broad-footed burden grazers, scale-eating crawlers permitted to clean the shelter roof, and little membrane-winged creatures that nest in gourds and emerge when the air is calm. The last are valued because they always fly toward open water. Travelers release one when lost in fog and follow its wavering course.

A family’s territory is not bounded by walls. It consists of remembered routes between moisture: this crown-cistern at dawn, that trunk-well after rain, a shaded reservoir safe only before breeding season. Rights to water are inherited, exchanged, and sometimes lent for generations. They are recorded in cords.

The memory cords are densely knotted lengths of dark fiber, supple enough to coil around the forearm. Large knots identify ridges. Fine knots mark shelters, crossings, hazards, and obligations. Texture matters as much as position: smooth bindings indicate open water, barbed ones occupied hollows, flat braided sections passages usable by herds. A skilled reader can take a cord into darkness and travel by touch through a country remembered by their grandparents.

Some cords are too long to be carried. These are kept in living archives: networks of rope trained along the interior ribs of an old shelter or beneath the eaves of a crossing house. Families gather there to recite them with their hands. No single person necessarily knows the whole account. One remembers the water sequence, another the marriages, another the disappearances, another the shapes of creatures that no longer return.

Their speech is low and economical outdoors. Long-distance shouting is useless among wing thunder, leaf organs, and the booming of the mat. At close range they speak with the mouth near the listener’s ear or place fingertips against the throat to distinguish words in heavy noise. Many common expressions combine voice and touch. Names may include a spoken portion, a hand sign, and a pattern tapped on the wrist. To know only the sound of someone’s name is to know them incompletely.

Across valleys they use reflected light. Small polished plates of shell, mica, or rare metal fragments send sequences from ridge to ridge. Messages are brief: water here; crossing damaged; swarm early; illness; strangers coming. More elaborate communication uses banners cut with holes. Held against the sky, these can be read at great distances even when their colors have faded.

At night, messages travel by vibration. People strike the major crawling trunks with padded mallets, choosing trunks known to continue into neighboring valleys. The signals are slow because each phrase must be allowed to fade before the next begins. Burrowing animals sometimes answer or disrupt them. For this reason, night messages are repeated three times: once for the living, once for the root, and once in case the root has lied.

Their clothing is layered but open. Inner garments are woven from soft shed fibers gathered beneath leaf towers after the scavengers have passed. Over these they wear crossed bands of tougher material that distribute the weight of tools and water vessels. Loose cloth catches dangerously in the upland wind, so hems are tied close around limbs. On exposed crossings, travelers fasten themselves to guide ropes with sliding waist loops.

Armor is made not for war but for falling life. Curved plates from dead grazers cover the shoulders and upper back during tower emergence. Transparent eye shields are cut from laminated wing membrane and smoked over fungal fires to reduce glare. Broad hats would be torn away, so the people wear close hoods with ridged crowns that divert falling bodies and scales from the face.

Almost everyone bears scars on the hands and lower legs. Some come from hooked vegetation, some from hidden vents, some from the sudden tightening of living bridges. Pale stippling around the eyes marks those once caught beneath an emergence. Such survivors often develop an instinctive downward tilt of the head at sunrise, even when indoors.

Food is taken from many sources but seldom stored for long. People tap sweet stem fluid into sealed gourds, scrape edible films from shaded stone, roast fungal nodules, and collect the soft-bodied young of animals that breed in cisterns. They herd broad-shelled grazers less by enclosure than by guiding them between seasonal feeding grounds. A herd recognizes particular banner patterns and will follow them across the open mat.

Meat is eaten quickly. In the warmth, nothing remains private after death. Cooking sites are elevated on woven platforms greased with bitter sap to deter scavengers. Even so, meals are crowded with small opportunists. Children are taught which may be brushed away, which may be eaten, and which signal the approach of something larger.

Salt is the most guarded substance. It is obtained from mineral skins scraped from exposed hollows, from old bones, and from the ash of certain deep-rooted plants. Salt cakes are pressed with household marks and worn in small waterproof cases against the chest. Theft of water may be forgiven under necessity. Theft of salt is remembered in the cords.

Their dead are not buried. There is no soil deep enough to receive them safely, and sealed hollows cannot be surrendered without consequence. Bodies are carried to exposed stone at the margins of active plant colonies and wrapped in a loose lattice of fiber. Scavengers come first, then root tendrils. Relatives remain nearby, striking the stone periodically so that large feeding animals do not settle unseen beneath the mourners.

By morning, the person has entered the moving earth.

The remaining hard parts are gathered only if they have significance. A tooth may become a knife, a plate a drinking scoop, a finger bone the toggle of a memory cord. Nothing is kept merely to preserve the body’s outline. To do so would be considered an attempt to hold nutrients still, an act both selfish and dangerous.

Their stories say the world below the clouds is unfinished.

Few have seen it. Those who descend the escarpments return describing dense heat, enormous waters, sharp young mountains, and forests composed of countless separate trunks. Such abundance of edges disturbs plateau people. They find lowland soil—loose, deep, and full of anonymous decay—especially unsettling. Ground that does not answer a staff seems dead to them, or worse, secretive beyond interpretation.

They prefer the old stone and the living roof.

Children are taught that the plateau rests on the back of something too ancient to wake. The rounded summits are its joints; the abandoned river stairs are places where water once combed its skin. Plants arrived later and covered it out of mercy. Animals gave the covering motion. People, youngest and least rooted, were given the task of noticing where the layers had come apart.

This duty belongs especially to the listeners.

Listeners are not rulers, though their advice can empty a ridge. They spend years learning the distinct tremors of stressed crossings, diseased mats, swelling cisterns, nesting chambers, and runoff moving under vegetation. They sleep with one hand against structural roots. Many become nearly deaf in age from exposure to the great swarms, yet this does not end their work. Hearing through bone and skin is considered the deeper skill.

Before a long crossing, a listener lies face down at its anchorage while others wait. They attend to the pull of wind, the pulse of moving animals, the slow internal clicks of fibers taking strain. Sometimes they rise and wave everyone forward. Sometimes they cut a small cord or add a brace. Sometimes they order the bridge abandoned, though it looks green and whole.

No one argues on the edge.

Not all inhabitants are human, and humans are not the most numerous or the most consequential.

The vast crawling plants are colonial beings whose apparent trunks may be older than any surviving history. Their upright towers are temporary organs, raised where light, water, and wind make them useful, abandoned when conditions shift. What appears to be a grove can therefore be one individual expressing itself in several places. It grows slowly along bare stone and quickly over the dead. Its pale gripping pads secrete acids that open the rock grain by grain, creating the mineral nourishment on which much of the plateau depends.

Its cisterns are not accidental. Leaves funnel condensation inward; overlapping sheaths exclude sunlight; inner surfaces discourage some drinkers and attract others. Small animals drown there, fungi digest them, and the plant absorbs what the water receives. Yet entire communities have adapted to these reservoirs. Blind swimmers graze the walls. Air-breathing larvae suspend themselves from the roof. Predators imitate droplets, hanging motionless until something rises.

The winged multitudes belong to several forms that share the towers in successive waves. The largest are furred, four-winged grazers that feed from leaf undersides during the day and return to communal crowns at dusk. Smaller species live among their fur, drinking oils or eating parasites. Others lay eggs directly on the great wings and hatch in flight. What the people call an emergence is therefore not a flock but a moving habitat detaching itself from a plant.

Individuals are short-lived. The colony is seasonal and persistent. Its routes follow uplifts invisible from the ground, joining distant plateaus and carrying pollen, spores, mites, and disease. A sudden change in the timing of these rivers can starve a valley. Their arrival is watched as closely as rain.

The pale aerial predators are almost all surface. Their bodies spread into flexible sheets around a light central frame, with mouths opening as slits across the leading edge. They do not chase particular prey. They shape themselves into funnels and let the swarm enter. When satiated, they become heavy and descend to warm stone, folding into ridged forms nearly indistinguishable from mineral crust.

Broad-shelled grazers travel below. Their shells are not rigid domes but arrangements of overlapping plates joined by elastic tissue, allowing the body to bend with the terrain. Wide feet distribute their weight over hollow mats. They feed by pressing their mouths flat against vegetation and pulping whole surface layers—leaf, fungus, eggs, insects, and all. Where a herd has passed, the mat appears shorn and bruised, then grows back with unusual vigor.

The oldest grazers carry gardens. Seeds lodge between their plates, along with damp debris and colonies of small cleaners. Flowering stems may rise from their backs. Some human bands recognize individuals not by the animal’s face but by this moving vegetation: the red-spored grandmother, the twin black stalks, the one with silver grass across its left flank.

The cliff dwellers are more visibly social. Long forelimbs let them swing beneath overhangs and hanging roots, while short hind limbs brace against stone. They store water in throat pouches and defend access to condensation pockets with elaborate percussion. Seed cases struck against different rocks produce distinct tones. Human musicians imitate these rhythms, but never near occupied cliffs; the animals interpret even imperfect copies as territorial challenges.

Within the root mat live the drummers. They are compact, blind, and heavily armored at the skull. Their tunnels compress the surrounding fibers into resonant walls. Communication travels farther through the mat than through air, and whole breeding fields can beat in synchrony after dark. People once mistook these rhythms for messages from buried ancestors. Some still leave offerings beside active vents, less from belief than courtesy.

There are deeper things as well.

Travelers sometimes strike a hollow and receive no echo, though the staff sinks farther than it should. The vegetation around such places is taut and unusually warm. Grazers avoid them. Winged animals passing overhead break formation without descending. Listeners mark these sites with unknotted white cords, meaning neither danger nor safety, only that the place has not answered.

Some of these marks are centuries old.

On rare nights, usually after sudden runoff, the ground beneath them moves in a single slow contraction. Water vanishes from nearby cisterns. Root towers lean inward. For several days afterward, unfamiliar plates or lengths of translucent skin may be found beside distant vents.

No one claims to know whether these belong to one animal or many. Plateau stories call them the Below-Herd, creatures that migrate inside the living terrain as grazers migrate above it. The practical teaching is simpler: if the ground drinks, leave the ridge.

Despite such dangers, the people do not understand themselves as besieged. Caution is not fear to them. It is participation. Everything inhabits something larger: insects inhabit wings, families inhabit plant-made shelters, ponds inhabit folded stems, towers inhabit the crawling colonies, and all living things inhabit the old, completed stone.

Even a person is regarded as a temporary hollow filled with breath, memory, water, and borrowed salt.

At dusk, when the flyers return and speech becomes impossible, households gather beneath their living roofs. Leaf shutters draw closed. Lamps fed with fungal oil are covered until only a dull amber remains. People place their hands on the shelter ribs and feel the arriving weight pass into the structure: millions of bodies settling in distant crowns, crossings tightening in the cooling air, grazers kneeling, hidden chambers adjusting to pressure.

The youngest children are told to distinguish these vibrations one by one.

That is the swarm.

That is the rain inside the leaves.

That is the herd lying down.

That is water passing beneath us.

That is the house growing.

And after each answer, they wait with their palms against the floor, learning the immense remainder for which there is not yet a name.
