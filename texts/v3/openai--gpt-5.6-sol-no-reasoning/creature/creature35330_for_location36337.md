The people who live in the crack are slight without seeming frail. Mass is expensive in a city where every floor is a bracket and every household must justify its load. They tend toward narrow shoulders, long hands, and compact movements. Even at rest, they stand as though sharing a ledge: feet aligned with the nearest brace, one hand free, weight never committed beyond recovery.

Children learn three things before they learn letters: how to equalize a door, how to clip into a guide cable, and how to become still when the walls make a sound.

They are pale by inheritance and by circumstance, though their pallor is seldom clean. Graphite settles into the creases of their fingers. Basalt dust shadows their eyes and works permanently beneath their nails. Old workers carry blue-gray marks where powdered stone has entered scars. Hair is kept short or bound close beneath felt caps, because loose strands catch in fans, seals, and descending machinery. Their clothes are fitted, layered, and full of loops. Nothing hangs unless it is meant to bear weight.

Almost everyone wears a small pressure dial somewhere visible. The wealthy set theirs in ceramic or silver; laborers buckle plain steel gauges to the sternum. The needle samples the air inside the innermost layer of clothing. To glance at another person’s dial is intimate but not impolite. To conceal it suggests illness, debt, or sabotage.

Their language is quiet and exact. It distinguishes between dozens of kinds of silence: the silence of a healthy bearing, of an emptied pipe, of animals listening, of stone before fracture. Ordinary speech carries little across the fault, so they have developed a second language of fingers, lamp shutters, and touches transmitted through rails. Lovers can argue by tapping opposite sides of a wall. Work crews conduct entire shifts without uncovering their mouths.

Names change with anchorage.

A citizen may be born Teren of West Face, Seventh Tendon, but become Teren Under Copper after a household migration, then Teren Three-Brace when appointed to a structural office. Old people accumulate names like abandoned addresses. The dead are remembered by their final fastening: the beam they held, the gate they closed, the cable they cut before its failure could pull down a district.

They do not bury bodies. There is nowhere soft enough, and no substance is surrendered casually. The dead are dried in low-pressure chambers. Metals, ceramics, and implanted fittings are reclaimed. Bone is fired, milled, and mixed into insulating compounds or high-grade seal paste. Families keep a small stamped tablet indicating where the material went. Thus an ancestor may remain in a bedroom wall, a lift gasket, or the white collar around a pressure valve. This is not considered desecration. To persist without function would seem far more troubling.

Their households are not built primarily around descent, but around shared air.

A household is whoever draws from the same reserve bladder and accepts liability for its leaks. It may contain siblings, spouses, apprentices, an elderly gauge-reader, two unrelated drillers, several children, and a hookcat whose official status is argued whenever rations are assessed. Joining such a household requires a pressure test. Separation is marked by uncoupling one’s breath line before witnesses.

Marriage, where practiced, is called double anchoring. Each partner promises not permanence—an absurd promise—but mutual recovery: if one fastening fails, the other will bear load until a new hold is found. Wedding gifts are clamps, insulation, lengths of certified hose, and tiny paired whistles tuned to carry through emergency masks.

They mistrust spaciousness. A large empty room produces unease; an unoccupied doorway feels irresponsible. Comfort means enclosure: padded walls close to the shoulders, a ceiling within reach, several visible exits, and the low respiration of a pipe nearby. Their sleeping boxes are often shared, not only to conserve heat but because waking alone in an unpressurized dark is among their oldest fears.

A common nightmare is the endless chamber—a place with no walls near enough to touch.

For that reason, the few who maintain the observation slits are regarded with a mixture of awe and pity. These outward watchers spend hours facing the basin’s vacant distances. They report dust fronts, intruders, unusual light, and minute changes in the far ranges. Prolonged service alters them. They begin leaving doors open. They speak louder than necessary. Some develop a longing for horizons and must be reassigned before they attempt to climb outside.

The city has rulers, though it distrusts anyone who cannot be physically reached.

Authority is distributed among the Gauge Houses, the Anchorage Guilds, the Reclamation Courts, and the great compressor crews. Laws are posted beside the mechanisms they concern. A magistrate judging water theft sits before the district recovery meter. A dispute over housing is heard beneath the bolts supporting that housing. Political claims are expected to have material equivalents: a faction demanding expansion must display cable, labor, and breathable volume sufficient to make expansion possible.

The deepest disagreement concerns the rods.

The Binder faction teaches that the mountain is fundamentally broken and that the city’s duty is to keep it from completing its collapse. Humanity, in their view, is the mountain’s brace: small, necessary, and morally justified by maintenance.

The Wedge faction believes the city survives because it keeps the fault from closing. To them the rods are not stitches but spreaders, and the city is an irritant preserving the wound in which it lives. Their opponents accuse them of secretly wanting the crack widened.

Neither faction can prove its doctrine without risking everyone.

Those who work the lowest drills are called Threaders. They are seldom seen in upper districts. Their clothing is plated against sharp rock, their hearing damaged despite layers of protection, their gestures slow from years of communicating through vibration. They return smelling of hot minerals, ceramic lubricant, and the bitter charge left by fractured stone. Some claim the Threaders know what lies beneath the visible machinery. The Threaders themselves say only that the crack continues.

At the city’s apparent bottom lives another population, less formally acknowledged: salvagers, unregistered families, fugitive debtors, and people whose households lost pressure certification. They inhabit structures lowered for dismantling and never fully dismantled—crooked neighborhoods hanging below authorized maps. They steal exhaust warmth, cultivate illicit films inside abandoned hoses, and splice themselves into municipal air with hair-thin capillaries.

Officials call them leaks.

Upper citizens call them the Unfastened, though many are fastened more intricately than anyone else. Their bridges are woven from discarded cable, their rooms swivel with the fault, and their children can cross moving scrap without looking down. They keep breeds of slate mice large enough to harness and pale hookcats with split gripping toes. During disasters, these same outlawed people often appear in sealed districts by routes no surveyor knows.

The animals are not pets in the ordinary sense. They are colleagues, gauges, thieves, food, omens, and occasionally citizens.

Slate mice are tolerated in controlled colonies because their movement predicts pressure changes. Families mark useful ones with dots of heat-fired pigment. A mouse that wakes a household before a pipe rupture may be given a name and a ration allotment. A colony that gnaws a signal line is hunted without sentiment and rendered into protein substrate.

Hookcats inspire deeper affection. Their narrow bodies are almost boneless in appearance, covered in short pale fur that darkens around heat sources. Each foot ends in two folding hooks and a soft adhesive pad. They move headfirst down walls, sleep in loops around pipes, and purr below the range of easy hearing; people feel the vibration through furniture. A hookcat chooses its own workshop. Attempts to transfer one forcibly usually end in blood and machinery mysteriously fouled with shed claw sheaths.

Old hookcats become living maps of structural trust. They remember which beams flex safely, which plates conceal voids, and which routes remain warm after pressure isolation. When one refuses a familiar crossing, work stops. When one abandons a district entirely, residents begin packing before the gauges change.

The gliders belong to no one. Children collect their cast skins and fold them into translucent ornaments. Engineers despise their nests in motors but study their ability to read compressor pulses. At certain shifts, the flock launches as one black shape, fragments, and reforms among the bridges. Citizens pause to watch despite themselves. It is the nearest thing they possess to a season.

The burden beasts of the lower works are called knucklers. Broad as tables and low enough to pass beneath pipes, they have six gripping feet, sealed nostrils, and blunt faces plated with sensory bone. They navigate by jaw-tapping and can identify familiar handlers through the return vibration of a footstep. Their minds are patient but not obedient. A knuckler will carry a furnace vessel through darkness and sparks, yet sit immovably before stone it distrusts.

Handlers sleep beside their animals in insulated stalls and learn a tactile vocabulary through the soles: danger above, hollow below, moving wall, familiar herd, pain, wait. Human tapping has entered the beasts’ language, and their rhythms have entered human engineering codes. No one agrees where one system ends and the other begins.

The city also contains quieter life. Microbial films digest mineral salts behind sealed plates, producing edible pastes with flavors described as iron, ash, chalk, smoke, and—on festival days—almost sweet. Mites live in felt insulation. Pale threadworms clean reclamation filters. Colonies of electrostatic dust bloom inside neglected switch housings and collapse when touched. Some technicians insist these colonies anticipate current changes and are alive; others classify them as a maintenance problem.

Meals are dense, dry, and carefully measured. People eat pressed wafers, hot mineral broth, cultured paste, and strips of vat-grown tissue cured until almost no fluid remains. Drinking is private. To open an ampoule casually in public would be ostentatious, like scattering coins from a bridge. Cups have locking lids and numbered seals. Children are taught to swallow without speaking.

Hospitality is expressed through air. A host raises the room pressure slightly, warms a bench, or opens a personal breathing line to a guest. The most generous invitation translates literally as: *My reserve recognizes you.*

Art is made from tension.

Musicians string wires between adjustable braces and perform by changing load rather than plucking. The resulting tones travel through balconies and arrive differently in every district. Sculptors grow crystal around stressed metal until invisible forces become pale branching forms. Dancers wear multiple safety lines and move across open shafts, their bodies tracing the permissible range of each tether.

There are no bells, but children make forbidden ones from scraps and ring them inside padded boxes.

Their stories do not begin with oceans, forests, or fields. Creation tales say the world was once a single unbroken stone without room for breath. The first living thing was a flaw. It widened itself by inhaling, and everything alive descends from that stolen interval.

Heroes are not conquerors. They are maintainers who notice the overlooked strain, return during evacuation, or hold a failing gate long enough for strangers to pass. Their villains are those who hide cracks, counterfeit gauges, waste pressure, or allow something dangerous to appear permanent.

Yet the people are not grim. Their humor is dry, technical, and merciless. A badly secured argument is said to “need another bolt.” An unreliable person is “rated for decorative load.” When a neighborhood moves unexpectedly, residents throw relocation suppers and mock the new angle of their floors. Children race homemade pneumatic toys along pipes. Workers decorate inaccessible ledges with tiny ceramic figures for the surprise of future demolition crews.

They celebrate new space.

After a major shift, when surveyors declare a chamber temporarily sound, citizens enter carrying lamps. No one calls it empty. Emptiness is dangerous. They touch the new walls, listen through rods, mark fracture lines, and begin naming what has appeared. Vendors arrive almost immediately. Someone installs a pressure tap. Someone else claims a sleeping niche. Gliders investigate the highest recesses. A hookcat walks the principal beam while everyone watches.

If it crosses, the crowd exhales.

Then the bolt crews begin, and the raw chamber becomes part of the city—not because it has been conquered, but because it has been given enough fastenings to join the living.
