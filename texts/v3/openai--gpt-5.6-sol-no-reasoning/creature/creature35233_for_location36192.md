The people of the Sun Basin are almost as difficult to see as the water.

They live below the shelf, in the last crooked woods where trees can still stand upright, but they belong to the hollow in ways that residence cannot measure. Each household keeps some knowledge of it: the southern ledges, the seasonal cairns, the spring’s hidden mouths, the order in which plants wake after snow. Few make the ascent, and no one goes casually. Those who climb are gatherers, healers, weather-watchers, and keepers of the route. They travel in twos or threes, carrying little, dressed in felted underwool, hide, and tightly woven cloth that does not snap in the wind.

At that height they speak with their hands. A lifted finger means falling stone. A palm turned downward means shadow overhead. Two touches to the throat warn that the air has become too thin for haste. Names are seldom used in the hollow. Sound carries unpredictably there, and attention is better spent on what moves without speaking.

Their bodies show the country that formed them: broad chests, powerful hands, toes spread from a lifetime on sloping ground. Yet they climb less by strength than by patience. They test every hold before trusting it. They know that a stone can be solid in the morning and loose after an hour of sun. On exposed crossings they move with their weight low, nearly on all fours, imitating the larger hoofed visitors without admitting that they do.

They arrive most often after dawn, when frost still binds the scree. First they repair the route markers. Then they crouch beside the spring and wait. No water is taken until the pool has settled from their approach and the drowned moss lies still again. The first bowlful is poured over the sharpening stone—not as payment to a spirit, exactly, but to rinse away grit, old metal, and whatever else might return through the cracks. Only afterward do they drink.

They collect sparingly. Moss is pinched from the underside of established mounds so the sunlit surface remains whole. Medicinal stems are cut above the lowest pair of leaves. Underwool is teased from thorns and bark, never pulled from an animal. Feathers are gathered only after the kites have left the basin. Every practice has an explanation, and behind every explanation lies an older one that sounds like a warning.

The basin’s gray grazers are its most constant inhabitants. Seen closely, they resemble neither hare nor antelope so much as a creature the mountain has revised again and again. Their ears are short, their muzzles blunt, their shoulders higher than their rumps. Broad, divided hooves spread against loose stone, while small inner claws catch on fracture edges. They chew almost continuously, taking willow tips, moss, lichen, and the soft crowns of grasses exposed by thaw.

Family groups center on an old female whose horns have worn down to dark knobs. She chooses the resting places and decides when a shadow is only cloud. The young are born into hollows lined with shed fur. For their first days they scarcely move; their coats, mottled like wet scree, make them disappear at arm’s length. Adults do not defend them by fighting. At danger they scatter and flatten, forcing a hunter to choose among stones that may or may not be alive.

They are gardeners without intention. Seeds lodge in their coats. Their droppings deepen the black soil beneath the willows. Their hooves open wet ground where insects breed, yet also press roots back into frost-loosened earth. The green ring follows their habitual paths as much as it follows the water.

The rock mice inhabit another version of the basin, mostly beneath its visible surface. Their tunnels join warm pockets under stones, nests made from clipped grass, caches of seed, and chambers close enough to underground trickles that the walls remain damp. They are dark-backed, pale-bellied, with whiskers unusually long and forefeet toughened for shifting gravel. When winter seals the hollow, they continue moving below it, carrying seeds from one cache to another and forgetting enough to plant the spring.

Some winters the scree settles and crushes whole passages. The survivors dig around the compacted bodies of their colony, incorporating fur and dry grass into new walls. Nothing is wasted long at such a height.

Black-and-gold finches come like weather. Their flocks contain hundreds, though they can vanish into six willow thickets and seem to contain none. The gold lies beneath their wings and shows chiefly when they turn together, a brief flare as if sunlight has broken loose from the stones. They feed fast, each bird keeping contact with its neighbors through clicks too soft to carry beyond the brush.

They nest elsewhere, on lower cliffs, but the basin is where the young learn alarm. A kite’s shadow passes; the oldest birds drop first; the inexperienced follow a fraction later. Over successive visits the delay shortens. By the end of summer the entire flock can fall out of the air at once.

The cliff kites are not large, despite the breadth of their wings. Most of their apparent size is flight: long primary feathers, a forked tail, a narrow body buoyed by rising air. Perched, they look meager and severe. Their feet are small but needle-clawed, suited to mice, moths, beetles, and newborn grazers. Their eyes possess a transparent inner lid against ice crystals and dust.

Each kite knows the basin as a map of concealment. One patrols the willow margins. Another studies the openings between scree slabs. They steal from one another more often than they kill, and old birds bear pale scars along the scalp where talons have passed. When wind enters the eastern gap, they sometimes climb until they are only black grains in the brightness. The people below say they are inspecting the wind’s road.

There are larger hunters too, though they seldom descend into the green ring. They live along the outer cliffs: long-bodied, thick-footed animals with ash-colored hides and tails used for balance. Their paws can spread wide over scree, and their claws hook backward into cracks. Usually they follow the migratory hoofed beasts or wait near the routes by which grazers leave the basin. Their presence is revealed by silence—a missing kite, finches that will not settle, gray grazers pressed so flat that frost gathers on their backs.

The smallest lives are the most numerous. Pale moths warm themselves on black rock before flying. Snow beetles manufacture a dark fluid that keeps their bodies from freezing and cross ice in search of fallen pollen. Clear-bodied midges gather above the spring’s open eye. Under wet moss live minute worms, mites red as ground pepper, translucent larvae, and slow black water-beetles that retreat into the fracture when the pool freezes.

Even the plants behave less like scenery than inhabitants.

The alpine willows grow outward rather than upward, old trunks traveling beneath moss and sending up young stems wherever light permits. What appears to be a thicket may be one organism spread across several yards, divided by stones but joined below them. Its roots brace soil, roof mouse tunnels, and narrow the spring’s exits. When part of it is buried, the surviving limbs feed shoots through the new scree.

Cushion plants build their own climates. Their outer leaves take the frost while their centers remain damp and comparatively warm. Insects sleep inside them. Windblown feathers decay there. Some mounds have gathered enough soil beneath themselves to stand slightly raised above the rock, living islands constructed from centuries of trapped debris.

The crooked conifers are older than anything around them appears entitled to be. Their dead wood faces the wind; their living tissue survives in narrow strips on the sheltered side. A branch may crawl along the ground for decades before finding a crack deep enough to root. In severe years the tree abandons whole limbs and withdraws into a green knot no larger than a basket.

Among the human visitors, one person is entrusted each generation with remembering the basin’s changes. The role has no grand title. In the low-country language it means something like “the one who notices where water went.”

This keeper records nothing on paper. The account is held in a cord braided from grazer wool and plant fiber. Knots mark collapses, vanished springs, lightning strikes, unusually late ice, the return of plants to a scar. Tiny objects are worked into the braid: a copper shaving, a finch bone, green stone from an old pool bed. No one knot means anything alone. The record is read through intervals and textures, by hands trained from childhood.

The keeper decides when the shelter must be moved, when a moss bed has been harvested enough, when the southern route has become too unstable to use. They also dismantle what should not remain. Cairns multiply if every traveler adds one. Walls rise if every visitor improves the shelter. So the keeper removes stones, lowers roofs, scatters abandoned gear, and cuts away cloth that might entangle a bird.

Their stewardship often resembles destruction.

They say the hollow tolerates use but not possession.

No one is buried there. No child is formally named there. No fire is kept after sunset unless injury or weather makes descent impossible. Those forced to stay through the night sleep close together in the low shelter, listening to frost open cracks in the mountain. At such times the overhang magnifies every sound: the scrape of a mouse, the flexing of ice, a kite shifting on its ledge. The stone roof seems to settle lower with the cold.

Yet the people do not think the basin empty after they leave. Emptiness, to them, is a lowland illusion—the result of looking only for houses, voices, and smoke.

The hollow is occupied by thirst and feeding, concealment and pursuit, roots feeling into cracks, seeds being carried where no soil yet exists. It is occupied by routes remembered in muscle, by the gray grazers’ worn passages, by kites tracing invisible columns of warm air, by mice rebuilding chambers under collapsed stone. Even the wind resides there intermittently, not as a guest but as an authority that returns to revise every arrangement.

And beneath them all lives the spring.

The people do not describe it as a creature, though their rules suggest otherwise. It withdraws from fouling. It changes its mouth after stonefalls. It keeps one eye open under winter ice. Moss, willow, mouse, grazer, finch, kite, and human all arrange themselves around its narrow persistence.

The Sun Basin’s inhabitants have no single shape. They are a ring of dependencies around black water: fur caught on thorns, roots holding accidental earth, wings turning in the updraft, careful hands lifting one bowlful and no more.

What lives there is whatever has learned to remain without ever standing too high.
