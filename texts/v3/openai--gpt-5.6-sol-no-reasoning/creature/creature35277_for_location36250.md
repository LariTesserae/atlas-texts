The inhabitants are difficult to separate from their city, because none of them merely occupy it. Each is part tenant, part organ, part architect. The towers shelter animals; animals carve doors and windows into the towers; their breath, passage, feeding, and reproduction determine where the next chambers form. What looks from afar like an urban ecosystem may be a single distributed creature that has not gathered itself into one body.

The rooted columns are its oldest visible citizens. Deep below the streets, their anchoring tissues enter warm fractures and divide into mineral-fine tendrils. They drink no liquid. Instead, they draw reactive vapors from the stone and cool them within sealed knots, condensing the amber solvent on membranes that never meet the air. Their trunks are reservoirs as much as supports: concentric layers of cork protect braided vessels, wax seals injuries, and metallic scales scatter excess heat. They grow by pressure. Young roots do not patiently seek gaps but wedge into the plateau until basalt parts around them in bright-edged seams.

Above, the columns become sociable. Their crowns seek contact. Ribs touch, soften, recognize compatible tissue, and knit together, so that separate organisms make one canopy of arches. Where they disagree, a narrow street remains between them. Where exchange is profitable, they fuse into galleries and chambers. Thus the map records affinities among rooted beings: courts are pauses in their negotiations, boulevards are thermal appetites, and sealed alleys are old refusals overgrown.

The hanging ribbons and wall-fans live upon this elevated forest without behaving like parasites. Black leaves absorb light and heat; white leaves reject the fiercest portion of noon; copper fans gather trace minerals from the air. At night, when temperatures fall, they open microscopic inlets and distill vapor into their internal channels. By day those inlets are sealed beneath glassy valves. A torn leaf does not wilt. It stiffens instantly around the wound, sacrificing a hand’s breadth of tissue to keep its solvent from flashing away.

The silica-thread cushions are the city’s smallest masons. They pack fissures, bind dust, and cover exposed rock with a heat-resistant felt. Their colonies grow in branching patterns that anticipate fractures before they can be seen. Animals often rest upon them just before the plateau knocks. Whether the filaments sense strain or merely respond to changing warmth, they are always thickest where the next crack will open.

Across these surfaces travel the minute grazers. They move six-legged and close-ranked, clipping metallic bloom from leaves without piercing the sealed tissues beneath. Each band follows a chemical path laid by the frontmost animals, but the path evaporates quickly, so their order is continually remade. When threatened they do not scatter. They compress into a dark, many-jointed plaque, polished enough to reflect the predator looming over it. Several leaf species tolerate them because their grazing clears pores and carries fertile dust from arch to arch.

The flat creatures beneath the cornices are harder to notice. Their bodies resemble patches of bark until the respiratory seams along their backs begin to ripple. They feed on trapped fragrance, dust, shed scales, and the minute crystals left by accidental spills. A single escaped bead of amber fluid can summon dozens. They seal themselves over the site, protecting it from open air while consuming the crystallized residue. In this way even injury is enclosed.

The long-bodied gliders are the city’s patient hunters. They hang head downward between towers, supported by translucent membranes stretched from limb to limb. These membranes are neither wings nor webs but living sails, adjusted by tiny changes in internal pressure. A glider may remain motionless from dawn until late afternoon, becoming another strip of pale architecture. It drops only when prey crosses a warm updraft. Then it falls, turns without a sound, gathers its quarry into the folds of its body, and catches another wall below. Its jaws never tear. They enter along natural seams in the prey’s armor, ensuring that none of the precious internal solvent escapes.

In the deepest passages stand the pale herds. They are eyeless because little light reaches the vents, but the fronts of their skulls are latticed with heat-sensitive chambers. To them the city is not a maze of walls but a landscape of temperatures: faults shine like roads, occupied chambers glow with breathing bodies, and cooling passages disappear into featureless distance. They feed with their heads lowered over vents, filtering dense mineral exhalations through frilled organs inside their mouths.

Their apparent hooves are padded clusters of small digits. Even when alarmed they place each foot with exquisite care. A herd’s coordination comes through contact: flank pressed to flank, they exchange pulses through flexible plates under the skin. No leader turns them. A change begins at one body and propagates through the whole group until dozens reverse together, as slowly and smoothly as solvent circulating in a vessel.

Predators follow them, though seldom closely. These are spare, hinged things that travel inside abandoned wall channels. They hunt not by pursuit but by altering airflow. One will block a cool passage with its unfolded body, encouraging the herd toward a waiting companion near a warm bend. Yet they kill rarely. A large meal contains more solvent than any hunter can safely store, and rupture would endanger hunter and prey alike. Their feeding is prolonged, careful, and almost surgical.

The upright mantles are the most conspicuous residents and the least easily classified. Seen at a distance, each appears to be a narrow cloak walking without a wearer. Up close, its hide resolves into nested folds, dusty outside and glossy within. Beneath it, hundreds of threadlike limbs move in waves. Some are feet, some sensory whiskers, some temporary roots. The creature can cross a street, climb a trunk, or anchor itself so thoroughly that its pulse becomes indistinguishable from the tower’s.

A mantle does not seem to eat in the ordinary sense. As it moves, its folds collect spores, mineral flakes, grazer eggs, fragments of silica thread, and the reproductive dust of the columns. It is a wandering archive of the city. Different layers of its body carry different communities, kept apart in warm, sealed pleats.

When it stops against a living wall, both mantle and tower alter. The fine limbs enter the bark without breaking its vessels. The wall thickens around the visitor, leaving a chamber shaped precisely to its folds. Inside, over many months, tissues mingle and reorganize. When the chamber finally opens, the smaller mantles that emerge are not simply offspring of the one that entered. They bear patterns from the tower, scents from neighboring plants, and traits gathered from the mantle’s accumulated passengers. They are parcels of the whole city made mobile.

Some never emerge. Their chambers remain closed and slowly become swollen junctions from which new arches grow. Others wake years later in districts far from where their enclosure began, connected through hollow channels no larger creature can traverse. For this reason it is unclear whether the mantles reproduce within the city or whether the city uses them to carry itself from wall to wall.

All inhabitants observe the rock’s deep knock. The instant it sounds, membranes draw tight, limbs lock, feeding parts withdraw, and open pores seal. Even hunters release struggling prey. The pause is not reverence but survival: the first knock may precede a fracture, and any torn body could lose its amber medium in the moving air. Yet the stillness is so exact, so universal, that it resembles a collective thought. For several breaths the city becomes one listening organism.

Last to move each day are the small bearers of light.

They spend daylight in the highest crowns, flattened between sun-facing scales. Their dark outer shells absorb the yellow sun, while chambers in their translucent abdomens store a portion of its energy in a slowly decaying mineral grain. At sunset they detach by the thousands. No overseer gathers them. The cooling of the crown releases them in successive waves, and inherited pathways draw each wave downward.

Everything yields to them. Grazers divide their bands. Pale herds press into alcoves. Mantles stop mid-stride and lift their folds from the ground. Even the towers soften certain thresholds, opening routes that remain closed at other hours.

The bearers descend through the bronze dusk like a constellation being poured into the earth. Their stored grains are not merely lamps. Deep in the fractures, where the plateau is still forming, they discharge light and chemical potential into buried tissues. Those tissues loosen the young stone, fill new seams, and exert an upward pressure distributed across the shelf. Many bearers do not return; emptied shells and spent grains remain below as scaffolds for growth. Those that climb back before dawn are pale-bellied and slow, carrying traces of the depths on their feet.

So the plateau rises by the thickness of a leaf—not solely through geology and not solely through life, but through a process in which the distinction has ceased to matter.

The city’s true inhabitant may be this exchange itself: sunlight carried downward, mineral warmth carried up; towers becoming chambers, animals becoming messengers, stone becoming tissue and tissue becoming stone. Everything that lives there protects a sealed portion of the amber medium, yet everything also participates in a body larger than its own enclosure.

No creature owns a chamber because every chamber is temporary anatomy.

No creature spills its inner fluid because the city cannot afford an open wound.

And each evening the smallest inhabitants carry daylight into the faults so that, beneath sleeping streets, there will be a little more city by morning.
