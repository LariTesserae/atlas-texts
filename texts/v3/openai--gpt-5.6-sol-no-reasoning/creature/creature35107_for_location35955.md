The beings who live here are shaped less by the stone than by the wind. Stone provides their surfaces, hollows, and boundaries; wind supplies their orientation, language, danger, and sense of distance. Nothing native to the plateau is entirely still. Even at rest, bodies stream backward, flatten downward, tremble, or lean.

The most numerous are the long-legged grazers scattered across the terraces. Seen nearby, they are stranger than their distant, spare silhouettes suggest. Their bodies hang low between four widely set limbs, distributing weight over pads divided into flexible, gripping lobes. The pads conform to flutings in the limestone and cling to shelves polished by ages of passage. Each grazer faces west into the oncoming air, with nostrils that can close to slits and small eyes recessed beneath transparent inner lids.

Their slate hides are not uniformly gray. They hold faint mineral colors—violet over the shoulders, rust along the flanks, chalk-white around the mouth—which become visible only when the animals turn away from the glare. Their dorsal quills carry much brighter signals. Ordinarily these lie in a dark, streamlined ridge, but muscles at their roots can lift them in sequences. Raised quills expose hidden bands of blue, yellow, or blood-red. A message travels visibly down the spine and then outward through the herd as neighboring animals repeat it. Alarm becomes a quick red flare. The direction of movement is given by a slanting progression from shoulder to tail. Recognition is subtler: a brief arrangement of individual quills, distinct as a face.

They graze alone but do not live alone. Each isolated figure is held within a web of visible posture and stone-borne vibration. Hoofless feet stamp questions into the ground; distant feet answer. Mothers summon young by lowering the chest against the rock and pulsing through the sternum. During migration the herd becomes a single distributed creature, its decisions passing across miles too slowly to be seen as commands and too precisely to be mistaken for coincidence.

Their young are born in sheltered fissures. For their first weeks the quills remain soft and nearly colorless, and the young learn the herd’s signs by pressing their sides against adults. Before they can cross an exposed terrace, they must learn to fall correctly—not downward, but into the wind, flattening all four limbs and allowing the current to pin them harmlessly against the stone.

Above them live the stationary hunters.

These great pale fliers have narrow bodies and wings disproportionately broad from front to back. The outer feathers divide into translucent filaments, each capable of twisting independently. They do not merely ride the wind; they fasten themselves within it. Once they have found the pressure pattern above a ledge, minute adjustments hold them in place for hours, sometimes from dawn until darkness. Their feet tuck into the breast plumage, their heads incline, and their shadows remain almost fixed below.

Their world is composed of wakes. Rock has one signature, a tarn another. A grazer lifting its head sends a soft disturbance downstream. Breath rising from a crevice leaves a warm, oscillating thread. Running produces a widening fracture in the otherwise orderly current, and the hunter folds one wing to fall through that fracture as though following a road.

They nest in cavities where the wind’s deepest notes resonate. Their chicks are nearly deaf, but the bones of their jaws and breastplates are sensitive to vibration. Before fledging, they learn the plateau as a set of pressures transmitted through the nest wall. When they finally enter the air, they already recognize the invisible stations occupied by generations before them.

The cliffs shelter gliding reptiles: small, flat-bodied animals with hooked toes and membranes stretched from wrist to flank. Their scales match the banded stone until courtship, when they flush turquoise or orange from within. Because airborne calls would be lost, they display against the mouths of fissures, where color can be seen from the opposite wall. Their glides are short and exact. They launch into the torrent, disappear sideways, and strike the next cliff with all four feet at once. Colonies occupy particular resonant cracks, and the lowest vibration of each crack passes through their bodies continuously. Removed from it, they become disoriented. They know home not by appearance but by pitch.

Beneath the exposed life is another, pale community. Condensation bowls hold blind crustaceans with long, delicate limbs and shells like cloudy glass. Beetles gather at the margins, black-backed to absorb warmth and silver-bellied to reflect it when overturned. Worm-thin scavengers enter through channels too narrow for light. Some creatures descend from ancestors that once inhabited open water; others may have risen through the subterranean river’s labyrinth. The pools are separate to the eye, yet now and then the same eyeless species appears many miles away, evidence of passages far below the accessible stone.

The translucent plants beneath overhangs are not always recognizable as plants. In damp hours they extend colorless fans from hairline seams. The fans contain reflective grains that scatter sunlight into the crack, feeding tissues recessed deeper within. During dry intervals the exposed portion contracts until it resembles a flake of mica. Tiny animals graze these fans, carrying fragments from seam to seam. Thus even the most rooted life on the plateau migrates by proxy.

The red mites hardly seem to inhabit the land at all. They spend much of their lives suspended on threads in the prevailing current. Because the wind does not veer, their journeys are not random. They launch in the west and descend in the east, generation after generation traveling the same invisible corridor. Those that land reproduce among the eastern cracks; their offspring crawl underground through cool fissures until they emerge again farther west. Their lives form a circuit through air and stone.

The plateau’s most attentive inhabitants are the furred primates of the rim.

They are compact, broad-shouldered, and low-waisted, with short legs and unusually long forearms. Dense ash-colored fur streams from their backs in a permanent eastward sweep. Their faces are dark and nearly hairless around the eyes, but the eyes themselves are protected by heavy folds and pale lashes. The palms are broad, callused, and crowded with vibration-sensitive pads. Their fingertips can distinguish an occupied cavity from an empty one through several handspans of limestone. They often sit apparently motionless with both hands laid on the ground, receiving the movements of herds, distant falls of stone, the activity around water, and the faint subterranean pulse of the river.

Their language begins in touch. Companions hold wrists, palms, shoulders, or jaws while tapping patterns with the free hand. Information is layered: location in the rhythm, direction in the orientation of the fingers, urgency in pressure, time in repetition. The location of a hidden reservoir may be taught as a sequence performed against the learner’s palm, then confirmed by placing that palm on the corresponding rock. A remembered route is not pictured from above. It is recalled as a progression of textures and vibrations: smooth shelf, hollow wall, three resonant steps, cold seam, long exposure, sheltered notch.

At greater distances they use their whole bodies. A figure on a ridge turns sideways, raises one pale palm, bows, or exposes a dyed patch of fur. They obtain pigments from mineral stains and rub them into small areas of the arms and face—not as permanent ornament, but as temporary grammar. White on the knuckles means water found. Red across one forearm warns that a fissure is occupied by predators. Blackened fingertips indicate death nearby or a route recently abandoned. Wind and skin gradually erase each sign.

They make tools but resist possession. A sharp volcanic flake is valuable only while its edge answers the hand. Once dulled, it is returned to a crack or cast into a hollow without ceremony. Individuals remember where suitable stone breaks naturally, yet they do not quarry it. They know which bones fit which shell-creatures, which sedges twist into bindings, which flat rocks shelter damp growth beneath them. A stone turned over is usually replaced after feeding, not from abstract stewardship but because the primates understand that exposed moisture vanishes and exposed growth dies.

They have no houses. Instead, families inherit knowledge of refuge: a cavity that admits three bodies, a shaded groove where infants can breathe out of the wind, a resonant chamber whose vibration masks the approach of gliders. These places are not marked. Ownership would be meaningless where survival depends on whichever shelter lies nearest when glare, cold, or aerial hunters become dangerous. Memory, rather than construction, holds their society together.

Their infants spend their first year fastened beneath an adult’s chest by hands and feet. There they learn language as vibration before they can see its gestures. Older young play by tapping false herd signals into thin shelves and waiting for an elder to correct the rhythm. They test flakes, turn stones, and imitate the posture of hunters. One common game is to crouch at the edge of an exposed strip and read, through the palms, which companion is crawling toward them from beyond a fold in the rock.

Death leaves little behind. Bodies are carried to warm, sheltered cracks where beetles and other scavengers gather. The primates return later and select no relics except, occasionally, a long bone useful for probing. Even then the association is practical rather than commemorative. The dead persist in route rhythms, water sequences, and distinctive habits of touch. A family may repeat an ancestor’s pattern for generations without preserving anything that a stranger would recognize as a name.

During the great crossings of the central basin, nearly all these lives become linked.

The grazers enter first in widely spaced ranks, their pads locking into the east-running grooves. Quill signals pass from one animal to the next until thousands of backs flicker with restrained color. Hunters occupy the air above, reading every tremor in the formation. Gliding reptiles withdraw into their fissures. Mites stream overhead. At the rim, primates press hands, forearms, and sometimes entire chests to the stone.

What reaches them is not a single pounding herd but an immense, articulate texture: each footfall distinct nearby, then merging into deeper patterns across the basin. They can feel where the formation stretches, where a young animal falters, where a hunter’s descent produces sudden disorder. The migration enters their bodies through the rock.

To an outsider, the plateau might seem to have no history because it has almost no ruins. Its inhabitants leave neither monuments nor middens, neither trails that widen into roads nor shelters that grow into settlements. Yet the place is saturated with inherited knowledge. Every herd carries routes in its coordinated spacing. Every hunter occupies an aerial station discovered long before its birth. Every primate family preserves maps that cannot survive outside living hands.

Nothing here masters the wind. Life has done something subtler: it has made the wind into memory, speech, territory, and tradition.
