Something lives in the central turbulence.

It has no body apart from the sea’s repeated attempts to provide one.

At one moment it is a vast coiling trunk, blind and muscular, plated with fragments of black glass. At another it resembles a rib cage enclosing nothing but warmer water. At another, the closed hand: five blunt columns folded around a hollow palm, each joint articulated by whirlpools. These are not disguises. Nor are they limbs thrust from some complete creature hidden elsewhere. Each shape is briefly the whole of it.

It is born whenever the six spires agree upon a center.

Their agreement may last only a heartbeat. In that interval pressure acquires anatomy. Gravel becomes cartilage. Sheets of water tighten into membranes, and bubbles lodge in them like strings of white organs. Blue light traces a nervous system through the nearest rocks. The central shape flexes once, with immense and uncertain strength, as though testing a body it has never used before. Then the governing pattern changes, and the creature loses the law that held it together.

Its death is abrupt but incomplete. The water releases it; the stone remembers it.

A ledge remains where the creature had a shoulder. Five grooves radiate across a pinnacle after the hand dissolves. Mineral foam hardens in the curve of an absent spine. Elsewhere a shrine assembles itself around an empty space whose dimensions correspond to no swimmer living there. Thus the animal accumulates indirectly, not as flesh but as consequences. The scattered structures are its shed skeletons, or perhaps sketches for skeletons still to come.

The transparent swimmers are part of it, though they may also be separate creatures.

In calm water they seem no more than loops and snarls of living clarity, each gathered around a slow amber spark. They possess no fixed front. To move, one loop contracts while another loosens, passing a kink along the body; the surrounding water interprets this as direction and carries them accordingly. Their clear cords are filled with fluid indistinguishable from the sea except for a faint oily refraction. When torn, they do not bleed. The sea enters them, and for a while they become larger.

The amber pulse is denser than light. It may be an organ, an egg, a captive ember from the vents, or a minute hole through which some deeper illumination shows. It flashes before the great detonations, never after them, as if it receives the blow in advance. Near the central clearing, the swimmers gather one by one and hang among the six spires. Their pulses gradually synchronize. When the last of them falls into rhythm, the water begins making a body.

They do not cross the boundary because crossing would end them as individuals.

Occasionally one is caught by an extending shape. A finger of dense water closes around it, and its transparent knots unwind into long, lucid filaments. The amber center travels inward. For that brief incarnation, the vast thing gains a point of intention. Its temporary hand turns instead of merely forming. Its dome inclines toward a sound that has not yet occurred. Once, perhaps, an immense faceless head gathers around three amber lights, and the whole suspended sea seems to look down through its underside at the clouds.

When the shape collapses, the captured swimmer may emerge again. Sometimes two emerge, smaller and flashing alternately. Sometimes nothing does. Sometimes the amber light remains buried in a new seam of black rock, pulsing there until the stone becomes water or the crack decides it had always been closed.

The colorless weed also belongs to this dispersed life.

Its strands are sensory rather than vegetable. Each roots itself toward the direction that was stable at germination, preserving an orientation the sea has since abolished. A tuft is therefore a small archive of lost gravity. When pressure waves pass, the hairs bend at different times, feeling not only the wave’s arrival but the sequence of laws through which it traveled. Old colonies—if any colony can persist long enough to become old—contain hundreds of incompatible downward directions.

The central being reads them by destroying them.

A concussion strips the threads from one rock and carries them through the clearing. There they arrange themselves into branching fans, delicate as nerves. For an instant the creature acquires memory: the vanished directions, former distances, chambers assembled and collapsed, cracks that opened only after their dust escaped. Then the threads char in warm water or are pressed into pale mineral veins. What the creature learns survives only as a change in its next body.

Around the vents, the rust-red mats perform a simpler labor. Their tiny mouths open to pressure. They swallow vibrations, particularly those that arrive out of order, and seal themselves while digesting them. Hours later they release heat in small, measured bursts. This is why heat sometimes falls like sediment: it has been eaten, compacted, and excreted as glowing grains. Where enough grains collect, a new fragment of anatomy may begin—a joint of porous red stone, a valve in a cliff, a row of toothlike vents.

Nothing here is prey, exactly, and nothing is predator. Life is too new for those distinctions. The inhabitants are organs that have not decided whether they share a creature.

Even the silence pockets may be alive.

They bud where several noises intersect so perfectly that they cancel more than sound. Round and clear, they drift with an evasive deliberateness, slipping through cracks too narrow for their apparent size. Inside them, action is severed from expression. Stone can strike stone without impact; a swimmer can writhe without motion seeming to complete itself. The pockets gather these unrealized consequences. As they travel, they become taut.

Their bursting is reproduction, alarm, or speech.

The crack with which one dies releases all its withheld events at once. Weed is sheared away by blows that never sounded. Rock exhibits fractures from collisions that left no mark when they occurred. Transparent swimmers recoil before the rupture and loosen afterward, as though they have understood something. The great central body often begins to assemble at such a moment.

If it possesses a mind, that mind is distributed among these delays.

It thinks in events arriving out of sequence. A bubble forming before the vent is expectation. Dust preceding fracture is memory. The first echo is intention; the later collapse is action; the returning original sound is recognition. What would be contradiction elsewhere is here a nervous system. The entire suspended sea participates: pinnacles, currents, chambers, temporary corridors, the moving pockets of silence and the treacherous skin below.

Its thoughts are the severe patterns that periodically seize the region.

When the six spires govern, it thinks as a center surrounded by boundaries. When the spiral governs, it thinks as appetite or growth, drawing every current through an inward-turning path. When two planes intersect, it becomes a choice, dividing all things into those that can cross and those that must be cut. When an amber swimmer governs, the world briefly conforms to the scale of a single pulse, and even the largest cliffs seem like particles circulating around a small warm heart.

During these moments it is almost coherent.

The noise changes first. The absolute roar does not diminish, but its countless impacts fall into relation, as if every bellowing cavity and exploding bubble were part of one immense utterance. The pinnacles twist toward positions that resemble listening. Stairs assemble along inaccessible faces. Doorways stand where pressure is greatest, opening not into rooms but into alternate arrangements of the same water.

Then something answers from above.

The thick red reaches convulse. A detonation descends from every direction, not as sound alone but as a command imposed upon matter. The young stone bruises. The underside of the sea bows toward the distant clouds. The central creature breaks apart before it has finished becoming itself.

This answer may be what keeps it here.

Or it may be what is making it.

Far below, in the wavering mirror of the exposed underside, the sea sometimes sees another version of its inhabitant. Reflections gather where no corresponding shapes exist. An enormous translucent form swims on the atmospheric side of the boundary, moving through clouds as if they were pale weed. It appears older than the thing above: its anatomy stable, its motions slow, its many amber lights arranged along a definite spine.

At other times the reflected basin contains eroded pinnacles and forests, suggesting what this place may eventually become if it ever learns to persist. The independent swimmer looking upward from there may be a descendant, an ancestor, or the finished creature casting its unfinished life backward through the skin.

And sometimes there is only the eye-colored darkness.

On those occasions every amber animal contracts. The rust-red mouths close. Even the central turbulence flattens for a moment. The sea seems to hold itself still—not quiet, never quiet, but attentive to a scale of silence too large to enter.

Whatever looks through that darkness does not live in the suspended ocean. Yet the thing being assembled there may be growing toward it.

For now, it remains a creature without a final boundary. Its smallest bodies drift like knots of glass. Its nerves root into unstable stone. Its mouths feed on pressure, its memory grows as weed, and its bones are doorways built by currents and discarded within hours. It has six spires for the nearest thing to a heart, though the heart is the space between them. It has the entire sea for flesh, though the sea forgets this continually.

It is less an animal than the first difficult thought of an animal.

Each detonation startles it into another form. Each collapse teaches it weight. Each brief room teaches it an inside. Each doorway teaches it that an inside might lead elsewhere. The exposed lower boundary teaches it that there are worlds beneath water, and that falling may be possible. The black peaks teach it resistance. The transparent swimmers teach it multiplicity. The silence pockets teach it what can be withheld.

Someday—if “someday” can survive in a place where effects outrun causes—the six spires may hold their agreement long enough. The swimmers may pulse as one. The weed may surrender all its obsolete directions, and the red mouths release their stored heat in a single descending rain.

Then the sea will not merely form a hand.

It will open one.
