The inhabitants of the plateau are not so much a collection of species as temporary expressions of one buried continuity. Plant, animal, landscape, and weather divide imperfectly here. Everything living either rises from the continental root-mass, feeds upon it, maintains it, or has learned to imitate one of its processes.

Foremost is the plateau itself: an immeasurably old colonial organism composed of intergrown lineages. Its youngest tissues form the cool silver-green surface; its dead and mineralized depths bear the weight of mountains. Light travels into it through transparent fibers. Resin moves through buried vessels. Cavities act as lungs, cisterns, resonators, and wombs. Different provinces possess distinct chemistries and rhythms, yet remain joined through deep conductive tissues. Whether this constitutes one organism, an ecosystem, or a perpetual graft-war among billions of plants is impossible to determine.

Across its surface grow widely separated crowns and towers. Needle fields hiss under pressure fronts. Mirror-leaved forms turn with the sun, casting moving blades of reflected light across the plain. Black filament plants absorb heat while exposing almost no area to the wind. Belt-groves unroll fresh photosynthetic tissue from beneath the ground and draw the exhausted layers back into digestive trenches. Piston-plants pump gases through root chambers; catalytic reefs feed on airborne hydrocarbons; wax-bearing colonies extrude clear fuels into living reservoirs. Electrical forests raise metallic spines and gather charge until their branches burn blue.

The greatest plants are the reproductive towers. Each dominates hundreds of kilometers through toxins, root competition, and electrical influence. For decades it accumulates volatile sap inside layered armor. At maturity it unfolds vanes into the upper winds, charges itself against the sky, and fires a branching discharge upward. The ensuing explosion scatters sealed capsules beyond the horizon. A tower’s death can seed a province, rupture old vascular beds, and alter local weather for years.

Many less permanent plants appear only during upheavals. When a plain swells and splits, thread forests erupt from radiant fissures. They flower, exchange cells and catalysts through the gale, then collapse within hours. Their bodies fuse into a new skin over the wound. Other plants spend centuries rooted and then detach, folding their vascular trunks into limbs or sails. These migratory forms confuse every attempt to separate flora from fauna.

The sail herds are the most conspicuous wanderers. Each animal has a compact muscular body suspended beneath a broad, mineral-stiffened membrane. Pressure fronts lift them by the thousands. They skim the plains for days, filtering spores, oil droplets, and microscopic organisms from the wind. Their membranes can change porosity and electrical charge, allowing them to climb atmospheric gradients or tack across violent currents. When calm comes, the sails pleat into long supporting limbs. The herds stand scattered over many kilometers, dark and motionless, waiting for the land to begin breathing again.

Pale six-limbed grazers travel below them. Their cutting mouths shave the thin, light-bearing skin of the plateau, opening trails of clear solvent that close almost immediately behind them. They cannot remain still: roots harden around resting feet, while chemical defenses gather beneath prolonged pressure. Their bodies are low, jointed, and plated with tissues resembling bleached wood. Instead of blood they circulate volatile resin, losing a bright aromatic mist when wounded.

Smaller followers drink from the grazers’ fresh cuts. Some protect the herds by removing parasites. Others induce deeper wounds, poison weakened animals, or plant eggs in their exposed solvent channels. Predators among them often serve several roles at once. A hunter may kill a grazer, consume only one organ, spread the victim’s reproductive spores across its territory, and seal the torn ground with secretions from its mouth. What appears to be a kill may also be pollination, surgery, and planting.

Within the plateau live more intimate creatures. Glass-bodied mites inhabit the transparent strands that conduct sunlight downward. Nearly invisible, they scrape away cloudy growth and keep the guides clear, feeding on the obstructions they remove. Their eggs act as tiny lenses. Excessive colonies steal too much light and are destroyed when the surrounding tissues flood themselves with dark resin.

Burrowing drums live in hollow roots. Broad and flattened, they strike the vessel walls in coded sequences, manipulating valves and redirecting sap toward chambers where they feed or breed. The plateau answers with contractions. Sometimes it crushes them; sometimes their drumming improves circulation enough that the surrounding root nourishes them deliberately. Entire migratory populations can be tracked only as moving rhythms beneath the feet.

Filter-beasts anchor over breathing vents. During quiet intervals they resemble dark, ribbed hills. When pressure rises below, they unfold vast mouths into the gale and inflate on the plateau’s exhalation. Internal combs strain out spores, flakes, mites, and resin aerosols. Escaping gases vibrate their tissues, producing sustained notes that may be mating calls, territorial warnings, or an unavoidable consequence of feeding. The largest sing loudly enough to be detected through the ground beyond the horizon.

Other inhabitants have abandoned hearing altogether. Stamping animals speak through the vascular floor. Long-legged hunters sense minute changes in electrical potential caused by muscle movement. Blind grazers taste distant events in the air: a tower preparing to discharge, a wounded herd, the opening of a digestive trench. Delicate auditory organs occur only in protected juvenile stages or in parasites living deep inside larger bodies.

Reproduction crosses categories. Some animals begin as hard-shelled seeds rooted into the plain. They spend years photosynthesizing before tearing themselves free and becoming mobile. Some plants produce crawling waste-buds that carry unwanted minerals away, then mature into independent scavengers. Predatory capsules impersonate fallen spores until swallowed by sail animals. Root colonies grow temporary nervous systems during storms and dissolve them afterward. Intelligence, where it exists, may therefore be seasonal, distributed, or confined to moments of crisis.

No creature truly inhabits the plateau alone. Bodies are habitats for smaller bodies; migrations open the ground for opportunistic forests; feeding redirects continental flows. Even carcasses remain active. Their bones root into the surface as scaffolds, their solvents are drawn into local circulation, and their mineral armor becomes part of the plateau’s weatherproof skin.

What lives there is a civilization without citizens and an industry without engineers: immense, self-consuming, and perpetually rebuilt. Its inhabitants do not merely endure the storms. Through breath, hunger, reproduction, and movement, they compose them.
