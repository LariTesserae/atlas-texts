The people who live in the fracture are slight without seeming frail. Their bodies have adapted less through evolution than through habit: economical movements, narrow stances, shoulders that turn before passing others on a bridge. They rarely gesture broadly. In rooms where a careless sweep of the arm might interrupt a field boundary or send an object into a runoff channel, precision has become courtesy.

They dress in fitted layers of pearl-gray, chalk, or the muted blue of freshly split stone. The cloth is thin but densely engineered, preserving a sheath of warmth close to the skin. Hard collars temper each breath before it reaches the lungs; cuffs and soles exchange heat with whatever surface they touch. Their clothes shed water instantly. After crossing a wet gallery, a resident may glitter for a moment with droplets, then stand dry while a neat ring of water gathers at their feet and is drawn away.

Faces remain uncovered in most districts. Skin tends to be pale from filtered daylight, though the city’s population comes from many settlements and bears no single ancestry. Noses and cheeks redden in the public cold. Hair is worn short, bound tightly, or sealed beneath close caps—not as fashion alone, but because loose fibers confuse sensitive surfaces and collect ice near thermal boundaries. Personal ornament is usually mineral: a sliver of black glass at the throat, a ceramic bead encoded with family records, a wire loop that changes shape with local stress.

Their tools live around them. A metallic dust rests in wrist bands, collars, or shallow implants near the fingers. When called, it gathers into probes, cutting edges, keys, lamps, splints, cups, writing surfaces, or climbing grips. When released, it dissolves again into a harmless shimmer. Children practice summoning blunt shapes long before they are trusted with sharp ones.

They are quiet people, but not solemn ones. Their voices stay low because sound travels too well, and because listening is part of survival. Everyone knows the difference between water striking ceramic and water striking newly exposed stone. Everyone can recognize the sequence of pipe knocks that precedes a pressure release. At the mountain’s deeper reports, conversation ceases at once. Heads tilt. Bare fingertips often touch the nearest wall. Only when the bridge lights fade from blue do voices resume, frequently at the exact word where they stopped.

Patience is one of their social virtues. So is responsiveness. A person who notices a fresh line of condensation and fails to report it is considered more discourteous than someone who interrupts a formal gathering. Civic responsibility begins with attention: a changed echo, an unexpected draft, a door taking a fraction too long to appear.

Children learn the mountain as if it were a moving animal whose moods can be read but never mastered. They trace stress lines in practice slabs, predict where a crack will travel, and memorize safe postures for bridges during realignment. Their earliest maps are temporary diagrams drawn in frost. These vanish as the lesson warms the surface, reinforcing a truth adults seldom need to say aloud: location is conditional.

Their games reflect this. They build matching arrangements of stone fragments on opposite sides of a room and wait for tiny seismic shifts to bring the patterns into correspondence. They race droplets through adjustable capillary mazes. They compete to cross a gallery without disturbing suspended dust. In warmer settlements such children might seem unnervingly self-controlled, until they begin laughing; then their laughter leaps down the passages, multiplied by the rock into something much larger than the group that made it.

Few citizens work at one permanent task. Most rotate through water management, structural observation, fabrication, instruction, and civic watch. Specialists exist—field geometers, fault listeners, thermal physicians, molecular cooks—but even they are expected to understand the settlement’s basic systems. No adult is wholly unable to isolate a leaking conduit or read the warning colors on a bridge.

The most respected are the structural interpreters, who study both the mountain’s movement and the invisible devices holding the fracture open. They do not command the stone. Their work is closer to negotiation. They decide which cracks should be sealed, which should be encouraged, where weight may be diverted, and when a district must move. Many spend hours seated before an apparently bare wall, feeling minute vibrations through fingers, teeth, and implanted sensors. Their records are not static maps but branching forecasts: possible mountains, possible cities, each lasting hours or years.

Water stewards form another old profession. They follow a drop from condensation to storage, from filtration to use, from use to release. Because all water eventually carries evidence of the city, they read it as an archive. Metallic traces reveal worn pipes. Heat reveals occupied rooms. Unusual molecular residues may indicate illness, damaged synthesis equipment, or the translucent organisms living in the deep filters. Some stewards quietly maintain separate lines for those uncertain forms of life, reluctant to destroy the only other persistent inhabitants the city may possess.

Food is plain in appearance but not necessarily joyless. Synthesis rooms produce dense sheets, translucent broths, mineral gels, crisp wafers, and warm pastes shaped by texture and aroma rather than by resemblance to plants or animals. Meals are often served in narrow communal chambers where each diner’s thermal envelope overlaps the next. To share heat is intimate. Families and close companions sit shoulder to shoulder, while formal guests are given distinct boundaries.

The absence of living greenery does not feel tragic to everyone born there. Many have seen forests only as recordings and find them visually excessive—too many edges, too much uncontrolled motion. Their aesthetics favor fracture, alignment, refraction, and phase change. They arrange stones whose planes briefly form a continuous surface when viewed from a precise point. They tune icicles to resonate with runoff. They compose light that travels through a room over days. Their art is often unfinished by design, completed by a shift in temperature or a movement of the mountain.

They mark time through recurrence rather than permanence. There is the arrival of the white morning band, the daily collapse of the suspended droplet ceiling, the changing sound of runoff after storms, and the seasonal relocation of cold faults. Birthdays may be recorded by the geological position of the family dwelling. Anniversaries are remembered as alignments: “the year the west bridges shortened,” or “three migrations before the iron fault opened.”

Death is handled with similar restraint. Bodies are not buried; there is no soil, and the stone is not cut merely to imitate older customs. Some are reduced in synthesis chambers and returned to civic stores as chemically anonymous material. Others are sealed into thin ceramic memorials placed in abandoned anchor sockets. These tablets contain no portrait visible to strangers. When approached by someone authorized—or loved—the pale surface warms and reproduces a voice, a pattern of footsteps, or the remembered pressure of a hand.

Families are rarely confined to a single tower. Rooms move, work rotates, and districts migrate, so kinship is maintained through permissions, shared thermal signatures, and habitual meeting times rather than neighboring addresses. A household may occupy three cells on separate anchors and assemble each evening in whichever chamber currently lies nearest stable water. Children belong partly to their immediate families and partly to the bridge-group that teaches them emergency movement.

Privacy nevertheless matters. A dwelling’s invisible door is considered an extension of the body. To reveal it without invitation is a serious violation. Inside, rooms are spare but highly personal. The wall may hold a shelf of stones collected from former districts, each labeled with where the room once stood. Light settings preserve memories of particular mornings. A resident may own only a few physical objects, but the thermal boundary knows the exact warmth of their sleep.

Strangers find them difficult to read. Their expressions can be subtle, their pauses long, and their systems reveal little unnecessarily. Yet hospitality is rigorous once granted. A visitor receives a warming collar, a guide haze, safe-water clearance, and a continuously revised route. Someone will quietly walk on the outer side of every bridge. No host mentions this precaution.

The maintenance machines are not treated as animals, though children sometimes name them. They lie flat against walls, changing color to match ceramic, glass, silver, or stone. Up close they resemble jointed patches more than creatures: many fine legs surrounding a flexible center, with tools unfolding from beneath. They test cracks, shave ice, lay capillary threads, and score rock so that future fractures follow safer paths. When several gather around a fault, they work with such coordination that they seem like one distributed body.

The uncertain organisms in the filters are smaller than grains of salt and nearly transparent. Under magnification they appear as folded skins enclosing mineral-rich water. They reproduce by laying down a second membrane inside the first, then splitting along a crystalline seam. They may be alive, manufactured, or an accidental consequence of filtration. The distinction has become politically important. Some citizens call them contamination; others call them the city’s first native species. For now, they are counted.

The occasional white bird causes disproportionate excitement. Entrance workers dim nearby surfaces so it will not strike reflections. Children watch remotely as it shelters on a warm conduit, turning its head at sounds inside the mountain. No one feeds it. Introducing food would invite dependence, waste, and organisms the city is not prepared to host. When it leaves, people track its flight until it disappears into cloud.

Most residents seldom visit the outer platform. Those who do often stand very still, unsettled by a world with no ceiling. Wind is loud to them. Distance without intervening stone can feel less like freedom than exposure. They look across the snowfields and valleys, then back toward the narrow darkness where doors, warmth, and intelligible danger wait.

From outside they leave almost no sign of themselves. Inside, however, they are continually listening, measuring, adjusting, and remembering. They live not as conquerors of the mountain, nor exactly as its guests, but as a second system threaded through its unfinished body—briefly stable, carefully warm, and always prepared to move.
