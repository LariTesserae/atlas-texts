The chief inhabitants are the forests.

They are not forests in the terrestrial sense, assembled from competing individuals, but vast slow organisms—or colonies so integrated that the distinction no longer matters. A mountainside may be clothed by what appears to be a thousand trunks, though all descend through cracks to the same buried web. Each upright column is a temporary expression of something distributed through the stone: dark green, ribbed, supple enough to bow for years without tearing. Its fans gather light, dissolved minerals, and the faint warmth leaking from young rock.

These beings measure change differently. A shadow is brief if it lasts a week. Injury may take decades to register. When a cliff fractures, roots nearest the break begin extending toward it, their tips secreting clear tissue into the new seam. Centuries later, a grove stands there. The forest has answered.

Among the trunks live the copper mats, low and densely folded. They feed partly on stone, staining pale minerals as they draw them into themselves. Their surface is soft, but their undersides leave delicate etchings in basalt. When sections die, they harden rather than decay, becoming thin metallic crusts on which new layers grow. Some cushions may contain the compressed remains of thousands of generations, each no thicker than a breath.

The long vines belong to the open water. They anchor at several summits at once and maintain themselves between them, adding length whenever the mountains drift apart. Their leaves turn independently, making each suspended strand a many-eyed instrument for measuring brightness. A change in the distant surface travels along a vine as a gradual reorientation: one leaf twists, then the next, until miles of foliage have quietly agreed where the light is.

In sheltered clefts, the clear bulb-forests husband gas. Their swollen bodies are nearly invisible except for internal pockets of silver and amber. Gas is both waste and offspring-carrier. It accumulates over years, stretching the parent tissue until a membrane finally parts. The departing globe takes with it dust-fine spores, mineral grains, and sometimes one of the black seed-shelled grazers. Most such voyagers vanish through the bright upper boundary. A few strike overhanging ridges, break, and found new translucent groves.

The small black animals spend almost their entire lives beneath leaves. They resemble wet seeds until they unfold their many filament legs. Their feeding is too slight to mark the plant: they harvest films of pollen, microbes, and mineral residue one particle at a time. Months of stillness alternate with sudden, exact migrations of a few inches. When threatened, they draw in their legs and release their hold, falling through the forest like grains of night. Some land on lower foliage. Others descend for years.

They navigate by vibration. A settling stone, a splitting root, the pulse of a distant fish—each travels through leaf and stem as a map of pressures. To these minute creatures, the apparent silence is crowded with information. They know the mountain not by its shape but by its tremors.

The pale fish of the ravines are loners. Their bodies are long, nearly transparent, with narrow skulls and no visible eyes. They hang head-up because their principal sense lies along the jaw and throat, where delicate cavities read warmth rising from mineral seams. They feed around those seams on soft ivory fans and the tiny drifting life gathered there. A slow muscular wave passes down the spine when they move. No fin disturbs the water; they seem to alter their curvature and allow the sea to carry them into a new position.

Two pale fish may inhabit neighboring gullies for centuries without meeting. When they do, they do not pair immediately. They align at a distance, head above tail, and remain that way while seasonal light comes and goes. Eventually each releases a ribbon of clear cells. The ribbons entwine, adhere to a fresh crack, and become a shared brood. The adults depart in opposite directions. The young emerge so gradually that the stone may have shifted measurably before they swim free.

Smaller life pervades everything: colorless threads inside roots, flat translucent feeders upon moss, drifting sacs that open only in violet depth. Much of it has no fixed distinction between plant and animal. It swims while young, roots when mature, and dissolves into reproductive dust at death. Some creatures spend their mobile stage inside rising gas bulbs, abandoning them just before the surface. Others live only in the resonance following a deep geological note, waking when the vibration passes and going dormant again as it fades.

And there is the larger inhabitant.

It does not dwell in caves or open passes but among whole ranges. No single view contains it. What is taken for a moving dimness may be its shadow, its body, or merely water displaced far ahead of it. Forests lean before its arrival. Pollen veils divide. The black grazers close themselves and fall.

Perhaps it is one animal. Perhaps the outer forests themselves periodically uproot and migrate, joined beneath the canopy into a continent of flesh. The few signs do not settle the matter. A pale curve sometimes appears between distant ridges and remains visible for days, too smooth to be stone and too large to judge as living. Rows of depressions have been found in copper mats, each separated from the next by a valley. Clear groves release their gas all at once after the pressure passes, filling the water with a slow upward rain.

Whatever crosses there has learned the same patience as the sea. A passage may take years. Its pulse may be mistaken for tectonic adjustment; its resting body, for a submerged range. It does not appear to hunt. Possibly it feeds on the diffuse pollen of entire forests, opening some unseen structure to strain the water. Possibly the forests grow upon it, and their collective bowing is not fear but the flexure of an animal beneath its own garden.

Nothing here expects it, just as nothing expects an arrival from beyond. Expectation would require a sharper division between event and interval than life in this place possesses.

The inhabitants live through continuities: light descending, warmth rising, stone opening, roots entering, pressure passing from peak to peak. Birth is an attachment. Travel is a loosening. Death is the gradual surrender of color until a body becomes clear enough for the world behind it to show through.

They are not waiting beneath the impossible sea.

They are becoming as slowly as the mountains.
