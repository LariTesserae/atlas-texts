They are a dry people—not desiccated, but made without any expectation of water.

Their bodies hold no blood as a wetter creature would understand it. Beneath tough, finely creased skin runs a warm suspension of waxes and mineral salts, pale when cool and amber near the organs. A wound does not drip for long. It beads, smokes faintly, and seals into a translucent scab that must later be softened and cut away. Their healers are experts in temperature: cooling an injury too quickly can make the inner fluids brittle, while too much heat lets them migrate into surrounding tissue.

They are slight, long-limbed, and broad through the hands and feet. Their toes spread against salt ledges; their fingers have hard, ridged pads capable of sensing minute tremors in walls, ropes, and pipes. Most climb with three points of contact even on level stairs, not from fear but from manners. To stand without touching the city is to make oneself momentarily deaf.

Their faces are difficult to read at a distance. The eyes are narrow, dark, and protected by clear inner lids against salt dust and volatile fumes. Nostrils close in folds. Along the jaw and throat lie vibrating membranes that receive low sounds through the air and through whatever surface the person touches. A citizen leaning their cheek against a pipe is not merely listening: they are feeling pressure, temperature, flow, and the tiny knocks of root growth against ceramic.

Their voices carry. Children learn warning calls before names, and names themselves are meant to be shouted through leaves, around corners, and across chasms. Each person has several: a close name for the household, a market name with clear vowels, and a bridge call—brief, unmistakable, chosen so it cannot be confused with the report of splitting stone. The dead retain their bridge calls. Families sometimes cry them before crossing old spans, partly in remembrance and partly because the bridge may have belonged to those voices once.

They clothe themselves in layered ribbon-fiber: loose wraps by the kilns, wax-stiffened mantles in cold descending air, veils when tending bitter gardens. Clothing is repaired until its original weave disappears beneath knots and patches. The most valued garments contain fibers from ancestral orchards or from plants that rooted in the walls of a vanished home. A household may migrate, fracture, and reform many times, yet carry the same dark strip of shade cloth for generations.

No one owns land in the usual sense. A room belongs provisionally to those who maintain its exits. A terrace belongs to those who keep its roots from lifting the neighboring pipes. A bridge belongs, by law, to those close enough to hear when its fibers begin to fail. Rights are therefore acoustic and practical. Someone may sleep beside a garden for years and still have less claim upon it than the pipe-tender three ledges below who can hear its channels choking.

Households are similarly fluid. They gather around work, warmth, and safe migration routes rather than strict descent. A child may sleep among kiln-keepers at one season, pipe-listeners at another, and an elder’s garden clan at another. Parentage matters, but competence binds more strongly. Those who share responsibility for a flame are considered kin. So are those who have waited together through a wall’s full cycle of speaking.

Their children are born soft and cool, their internal waxes not yet properly separated. For several years they must be carried between thermal levels until their bodies learn the city’s daily rise and fall. Infants sleep in woven slings hung near inhabited thresholds, where many voices and footsteps keep them calm. Silence alarms them. A quiet child is touched at once to check whether it is breathing; a quiet district is evacuated.

Play teaches vibration. Children race knots along communal ropes, drum messages through walls, and hide behind vane-groves while companions locate them by the tremor of their feet. They build tiny channels in warm dust and compete to guide drops of lamp oil without spilling them. Their most solemn game imitates civic sounding: one child strikes a slab while the others declare it sound, hollow, shifting, or dangerous. Adults pretend not to listen, though they correct mistakes afterward.

Age comes as cooling and hardening. The elderly move closer to warm pipes and kilns, but their sensitivity to vibration often sharpens as their sight dims. Many become wall-hearers, bridge judges, or keepers of the sounding slabs. They can distinguish a root pushing under pavement from salt delaminating behind plaster, or a harmless settling crack from one that will open at sunset. An elder may spend hours with both palms against a wall, exchanging occasional knocks with someone stationed on the far side.

Death is not associated with thirst or drowning, ideas the city does not possess. It is understood as cessation of response. The dead are not buried, because disturbed ground invites roots and concealed roots endanger pipes. Nor are they burned whole, because an unseen flame is an offense and a body’s vapors may poison a district. Instead, they are rendered in sealed funerary ovens under the watch of two households.

What can be safely recovered is separated. Mineral residue enters mortar. Hardened wax may be mixed into a memorial lamp. Ash is kneaded into writing tablets or applied in minute quantities to ancestral root-mats. Thus a person may become part of an arch, a page, a red light, and a garden. Their bridge call is struck once on every sounding slab their work could once reach.

They do not worship gods who live above. The upper world is too blank, too exposed, and too silent to seem inhabited. Sacredness lies in continuance: a flow given passage, a bridge answering under tension, a plant opening because generations have sung the correct work rhythm beside it.

Their closest thing to a temple is the municipal channel house, where many pipes converge without mingling. Here tenders sit among retorts and resonance jars, reading changes in pitch. Offerings are not poured out—wastefulness and uncontrolled flow are both abhorrent. Instead, a person may promise labor: ten nights clearing capillary cords, a season pruning powder-pods, or one full heat cycle listening beside an uncertain wall.

The bitter gardens inspire a different reverence. Tenders enter them masked and roped, never alone. The toxic plants there seem grotesquely expressive: membranes pulse, crowns shriek, and oily reefs rearrange themselves around seepage no one else could use. Fumes bring visions, though citizens distrust any revelation that cannot be repeated by striking a wall or testing a pipe. Bitter-garden seers are respected less for prophecy than for returning with useful metaphors. A district might decide that a fracture “has begun dreaming sideways,” and every mason will know what pattern of hidden branching is meant.

Beauty is judged by resilience made visible. A perfect new wall is less admired than an old one whose repairs show six generations of changing pressure. Fine pottery is asymmetrical so that it will settle safely when shelves shift. Songs are composed in overlapping rhythms that survive echoes and missing voices. In sculpture, polished planes are avoided; they resemble fresh breaks. Artists prefer knots, braces, seams, scars, and branching channels.

Their writing is incised into brittle wax-shell tablets. Since heat can warp them, important laws are copied at several elevations. The oldest texts survive not as whole documents but as phrases distributed through architecture: a warning impressed beside a valve, a marriage obligation under a stair, a fragment of a work song sealed into an arch. History is reconstructed by walking.

They have no cavalry, no herds, no beasts of burden, and almost no conception of another living creature choosing to follow a person. Labor is human, botanical, thermal, or gravitational. Heavy goods descend on rope cradles and rise in counterweighted stages. The inhabitants speak affectionately to useful plants, but not as pets. A plant is closer to a neighborhood: slow, demanding, productive, dangerous when neglected, and likely to outlive those who tend it.

Conflict centers on flow, access, and audibility. To divert warmth is a kind of theft. To damp a communal rope without warning is sabotage. To conceal a crack is nearly murder. Their worst criminals are not executed; they are made socially inaudible. Their calls are no longer repeated at sounding slabs, their claims on bridges lapse, and no threshold is obligated to answer them. Few endure such isolation for long. Most submit to years of dangerous maintenance in the outer chambers to regain a recognized voice.

In council, no speaker stands at the center. Representatives occupy separate ledges around a resonant shaft and strike their positions into hanging slabs before speaking. Agreement is not shown by raised hands but by repeating a rhythm until it passes through every inhabited level and returns without alteration. A law that cannot survive the city’s echoes is considered badly made.

They are cautious without being timid, loud without being quarrelsome, frugal without loving scarcity. They know that every useful substance may become poison by entering the wrong channel, that every shelter is temporary, and that a ruin can still be taught another purpose.

At sunset they climb.

Families emerge from the lower chambers carrying mats, lamps, tools, trays of fermenting wax, and sleeping children. Calls travel ahead of them. Bridges answer under their weight. High among the black vanes and pale folded leaves, they pause when the seam opens and copper light descends.

For those few minutes, they can see the shadows of the structures that usually exist only by touch, sound, and memory. They see their bridges thrown sharply across the walls. They see neighbors outlined on ledges far away. Children stretch their fingers into the beam, startled each evening that a hand can make darkness on stone.

Then the light narrows.

The people settle into their hanging gardens, touch palms to the walls beside their sleeping niches, and listen as the cooling city changes pitch around them.
