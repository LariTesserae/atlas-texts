The city is inhabited by water.

It dwells everywhere, though it owns no single shape. In the upper districts it lies open to the sky in shallow cisterns, trembling under rain. Within the walls it travels unseen, pressed through pores and hairline faults. It emerges along ledges as clear beads, gathers itself into threads, and descends from terrace to terrace. Below, it broadens into marshes and dark reservoirs, then disappears again beneath the stone. Its paths are the oldest streets, repeatedly abandoned and reclaimed.

Weather lives there too. Cloud enters the basin as a low, pale population, filling avenues and surrounding towers until only their upper ledges remain above it. Mist settles on exposed surfaces and, in the colder months, becomes a transparent rind. Frost inhabits cracks. Thaw carries grit into them. Together they work with such patience that their smallest gestures outlast generations elsewhere.

The stone itself is not inert. Basalt darkens when touched by rain; quartz seams glow from within the wet walls; iron-bearing springs leave rust-red banners down their faces. Slabs hold their places for centuries, then shift in a night and become bridges, floors, or obstructions in some lower channel. The city’s architecture is continuously occupied by the forces that made it.

Life gathers only where those forces permit.

Moss lives along the flood line, black-green and saturated, mapping the ordinary reach of water. Lichens possess the roofs and towers, their pale colonies spreading so slowly that each patch is a record of long stability. Grass keeps to seams filled with windblown grit. Dwarf willows inhabit sheltered courts, bent together beneath the prevailing wind, their roots laid sideways over stone like exposed veins. Sedges crowd the marsh margins. Reddish algae briefly stain the pools after prolonged rain, flourish, and vanish.

In protected fissures, summer produces small white flowers held close to the ground. They have little need to attract attention. Their season is short, their shelter exact, and their entire world may be a pocket of sediment no wider than a hand.

The animals are similarly slight and discreet. Gray moths sleep beneath overhangs with folded wings indistinguishable from the rock. Water beetles cross the high cisterns when the air is still, writing momentary lines on the reflected sky. Pale, narrow fish inhabit the covered waterways and deep connecting pools. They know the city from below: submerged arches, mineral ceilings, corridors through which daylight arrives as a dim green-gray stain. When they enter an uncovered channel, they appear only as a flexure in the reflected light.

Long-legged birds visit the marsh districts. They are ash-colored and spare, adapted less to concealment than to resemblance. Standing between stone pillars, they become another vertical element in the basin. They may remain motionless for hours, then place one foot into the water or utter a single low note. Their calls do not summon a chorus. Each note enters the corridors alone and is absorbed.

Larger animals live mostly beyond the city proper, on the outer slopes where vegetation is broader and passage easier. They come in without ceremony. A hoofed animal may cross a courtyard at dawn, drink from a rain-filled hollow, and leave before the mist lifts. What remains is a line of impressions in clay, each slowly filling with water until the tracks resemble small, dark pools. Thus the city often contains evidence of creatures more readily than creatures themselves.

Beneath all of these lives another, almost invisible community: organisms in mud, algae in shaded channels, larvae under sedge roots, minute feeders in seepage films. They inhabit the thin boundary where mineral becomes soil and standing water becomes marsh. Bubbles rising singly from the bottom pools are their weather, their exhaust, perhaps the only visible sign of entire populations concealed in cold sediment.

Yet no species can be called the city’s people. Nothing gathers in sufficient number to possess it. Its inhabitants are scattered and conditional, each occupying a narrow permission: a wet ledge, a sheltered crack, a deep pool, a brief season.

What truly lives there is the relationship between water, stone, cold, and time.

It has no body except the basin and no voice beyond rain, dripping ledges, settling slabs, and the rare call of a bird. It breathes by clouding and clearing. It sleeps under ice, wakes in thaw, expands through the marshes, and withdraws into aquifers. Its memory is stored in polished gutters, abandoned channels, mineral stains, drowned courtyards, and lichens on surfaces that have not moved for centuries.

The creatures dwelling within it do not animate an empty place. They are small expressions of a life already present: moss where water pauses, fish where it disappears, birds where it spreads thinly beneath the sky.

The city’s inhabitant is the city itself—not as a mind or beast, but as a vast, slow metabolism of rain and rock, forever adjusting its innumerable rooms.
