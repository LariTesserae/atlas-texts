The people of the basin are as sparely distributed as its groves. They do not fill their city so much as maintain a human presence throughout it: one household beside a reservoir, another across two empty courts; three generations occupying rooms that could hold twice their number. From a distant terrace, a person is first perceived as movement among stable things—a pale garment crossing dark paving, a bent figure beside a gate, two silhouettes lifting a roof slab into place.

They are slender, though not frail, with the patient strength of people accustomed to carrying water and setting stone. Their feet and hands are broad from use. Soles are hardened by wet paving; fingertips bear fine pale scars from sharp rock and fiber cord. Hair is generally worn close, braided flat, or bound at the nape so that it will not remain damp against the neck. Their clothing consists of loosely wrapped plant-fiber cloth, short tunics, and rain capes woven from long leaves or oiled thread. Colors fade quickly into the basin’s range: clay red, mineral white, leaf green, smoke gray. Nothing trails. Nothing jingles.

No one among them remembers animals firsthand.

They know them instead as an old category of moving life, preserved in a few inherited designs whose meanings have become uncertain. A hooked shape incised on a jar may once have represented a bird. A four-footed figure appears on the lintel of an old water house, but children often mistake its legs for roots. Stories say that some living things once moved without hands, spoke without words, and consumed one another. These accounts are neither forbidden nor especially revered. They are repeated with the remote curiosity given to an unfamiliar form of weather.

Their own diet comes from plants, fungi, and mineral-rich waters. Terraces yield broad-leaved greens, wet-ground tubers, pale gourds, climbing beans, and dense seed heads shaken into shallow baskets. Certain trees produce starchy pods without flowering visibly. Mats of edible fungus are cultivated in shaded rooms where spring water passes beneath pierced stone shelves. There are sour fruits, oily kernels, and thick reeds whose inner pith can be dried and ground. Food is simmered in clay pots, roasted on slabs, or wrapped in leaves and buried under warm ash. Without meat, milk, honey, or eggs, their cooking is subtle in other directions: smoke, fermentation, bitter herbs, mineral salts collected from evaporating basins.

They tend plants without thinking of cultivation as dominion. A garden is understood as a guided portion of the watershed. People divert a finger of water, clear competing roots, press plantlets into mud, and wait. They are reluctant to say that they have “made” anything grow. Growth belongs to the plant and the water; the gardener has only arranged their meeting.

This manner of thought extends through the city. A house is not wholly owned by those sleeping in it. Its roof belongs temporarily to a household, its foundation to the terrace, its drain to everyone below. Water passing through a courtyard cannot be withheld beyond the agreed depth of its pool. Each district appoints several keepers who inspect gates, measure overflow lines, and settle disputes over shade or seepage. Their authority is practical and brief. A keeper may direct fifty people during a repair and return the next day to grinding meal or shaping tiles.

Time is measured by water more often than by sky. Narrow jars with holes in their bases mark cooking, meetings, and periods of rest. Longer spans are named for completed works: the years since the eastern reservoir was lined, the interval between the opening of two springs, the generations during which a certain wall has needed no resetting. Age is difficult to assign precisely. Births are remembered, but not always numbered, and adults may describe themselves as having lived through nine roof renewals or three raisings of the lake stair.

Children grow up near open water without being loudly warned from it. They learn its depth by entering the shallow district pools, where older children show them how the fitted stones descend. Their games are quiet but not solemn. They race leaf boats through irrigation cuts, build miniature retaining walls after rain, alter currents with chips of clay, and try to carry full cups across courtyards without spilling. In a place without animals, they imitate water. They coil like springs rising through sand, spread like overflow across steps, or stand absolutely still and challenge one another to become lake surfaces.

Their silence is often misunderstood by strangers—if strangers ever come. They are not joyless, nor bound by vows. They laugh readily, but close at hand. Songs exist, though few are projected across distance. Most are sung by small groups in rooms with low ceilings, where voices overlap in sustained tones and pauses are left for dripping water. Work songs are marked not by percussion but by breath and the placement of stone: lift, turn, fit, withdraw. At funerals, a single line may be repeated until the words lose their edges and become a low communal sound.

Death does not introduce the dead into the lake or channels. Bodies are wrapped in fiber cloth and placed in dry stone chambers high on the inner slopes, above the springs. There, in air that is warmer and less damp, they change slowly. The chambers are reopened after many years. Bones are cleaned, arranged in ceramic boxes, and set within house walls or beneath court benches. Names persist while someone uses them; afterward the dead become identified with their work—a stair, a cistern lining, a grove planted over a seep. Thus a person may cease to be spoken of as an ancestor and remain as “the one whose gate still closes evenly.”

Birth receives less ceremony than the child’s first independent carrying of water. At that time the child is given a small jar made for the hand rather than for household need. They fill it at an upper channel and pour it into the pool of their own district, making their first deliberate contribution to the shared descent. The jar is kept throughout life if it survives. Many are mended repeatedly with fiber binding and dark resin.

The inhabitants are exacting craftspeople because their materials demand it. They listen to stone before splitting it, touching its surface with a small hammer and judging the returning note. They know which timber will swell safely in a gate, which clay will craze but remain watertight, which cordage tightens when wet. Waste pieces are not discarded far from where they are made. Stone chips become drainage bedding; broken pottery lines planting pits; ash enters the garden soil. The city has no rubbish quarter, only gradual transformations of material.

Their bodies carry the climate. Skin is frequently marked by pale mineral traces at the wrists and ankles. Nails are kept short. Minor cuts are washed immediately in running water and packed with crushed fern or clay. Dampness brings its own ailments: persistent coughs, aching joints, patches of irritated skin. Houses therefore contain warm ledges near hearths, ventilated sleeping platforms, and cupboards built into the driest walls. Firewood is valuable, not because trees are scarce, but because nothing dries easily. Bundles are stored for years beneath broad eaves.

They do not worship rain merely because it sustains them. Rain is too ordinary for petition. Their deepest observances concern continuance: that a spring follows its channel, that a pool releases what it receives, that one generation finishes what another began without erasing its fitted lines. Small offerings are sometimes placed where water first enters a district—a pinch of meal, a coil of thread, a newly sharpened stone flake—but these are removed afterward and used. To leave useful matter to decay would contradict the gesture.

Their sacred places, if they may be called that, are chambers built around natural upwellings. They contain no images. The spring rises through a fitted opening in the floor and flows out beneath the opposite wall. People sit along the sides, listening. Decisions that affect several terraces are discussed there because running water discourages haste: every speaker must wait while a measuring bowl fills and empties before speaking again. The custom produces long pauses, but few interruptions.

Conflict exists. Water gates are moved in the night. A household extends a wall and casts shade on another’s plot. Someone takes dry timber from communal storage or fails repeatedly to clear roots from a channel. Anger here is not absent; it is compressed. Public quarrels consist of low, severe exchanges, each claim made before witnesses who know the slope and flow in question. Punishments are usually labor: resetting a neglected drain, carrying clay to a distant terrace, tending an upper spring through a sequence of rains. Exile is almost unknown. Beyond the basin there may be other places, but in local imagination separation from the watershed is close to disappearance.

Their city grows because households divide, not because population surges. When a new dwelling is needed, its site is observed through many rains. Builders mark where water gathers, where mist lingers, where a future roof would send runoff. Foundation stones may wait in place for months before the first wall rises. A finished house is not celebrated with noise. Neighbors enter carrying coals, dry fiber, jars of water, and rooted plants. The first meal is eaten before doors are hung.

At dusk they withdraw early. Those still outside become careful points of lamplight moving between larger darknesses. Indoors, people mend cloth, smooth tool handles, sort seeds, tell histories, or simply sit near the hearth while wet garments steam faintly. Children fall asleep to the same sounds adults use to judge the city’s health: the interval between gutter drops, the whisper of a full channel, the change in tone when a reservoir reaches its overflow lip.

Some remain awake later. Water keepers walk the upper tiers after heavy rain, placing a hand against gates and retaining walls. Lamp bowls sheltered in niches show only a forearm, the edge of a cheek, a momentary shine on wet stone. They seldom need to speak. A channel running correctly has a recognizable voice; a blocked one thickens and mutters. By sound alone they can tell where leaves have gathered or a wooden board has begun to warp.

The basin has shaped them without making them passive. Their patience is active, composed of constant small attentions. They live in a world that does not lunge, swarm, migrate, hunt, or rot quickly. Nothing but other people moves with intention. Consequently every human gesture is unusually distinct. A hand raised across a plaza can summon someone from three terraces away. A dropped bowl startles an entire court. Footsteps reveal who is approaching long before the face can be seen.

They are accustomed to being the only creatures who answer the world.

The water sounds, but does not listen. Plants bend and root, but do not approach. The lake receives every reflection and recognizes none. Against this vast unresponsiveness, the inhabitants have become attentive to one another in minute ways: a jar left full outside a doorway, a roof tile shifted before the owner notices, dry sandals placed near a returning worker’s hearth. Their kindness is rarely announced. Like the channels, it is built into the slope of ordinary life, passing quietly from one level to the next.
