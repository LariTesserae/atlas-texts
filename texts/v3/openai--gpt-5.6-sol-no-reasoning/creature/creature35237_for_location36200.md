The chamber is inhabited less by separate creatures than by a single distributed commonwealth of roots, insects, water, fungus, and appetite.

Its most visible residents are the bronze cicada-things fastened beneath the ceiling. They hang in ordered clusters, feeding through fine mouthparts sunk into living runners. Their wings never open for flight. Instead, they vibrate in place, transmitting a tireless mechanical hum through the woven roof. The sound regulates the room: droplets loosen at certain frequencies, seed wheels keep pace with it, larvae rise or settle according to its pressure. When one insect dies, ants dismantle it where it hangs, and a younger body eventually occupies the same patch of root.

Below them, amber aphids and silver mites tend the chamber’s currents. The aphids tap pale sugar nodules that grow like rivets along selected roots. Ants harvest their excess and carry it along inherited paths, while the mites gather where faint impulses meet, flashing whenever the vascular network changes charge. Together their lights sketch a nervous system that belongs to no individual creature.

The water supports its own workforce. Nearly transparent shrimp clean softened stems; snails plane away what the shrimp leave behind; threadlike fish graze on drifting microbes and vanish through hair-thin channels. Seed wheels lift oxygen toward root beds where pale larvae feed. Flatworms wrap around stems like wet ribbons. Salamanders, small enough to sleep inside folded leaves, patrol the deeper grooves. Water striders stitch rapidly across the surface, their footfalls adding a dry chatter to the chamber’s continuous drone.

Tiny glass-throated frogs cling to waxy cups and liverwort curtains. Their metallic calls are brief and exact. Spiders walk upside down among the ceiling fibers, hunting the frogs’ young and the midges that hang in vertical clouds. Beetles answer one another with measured clicks; their larvae rasp inside old roots, hollowing chambers that plants later fill with air or new tissue.

Fungi inhabit everything the animals abandon. They enter husks, droppings, dead root hairs, shed skins, and fragments of leaf before any refuse can gather. Their pale threads separate these remains into salts, sugars, and mineral traces, passing them back to the roots. Thus nothing here is merely consumed or discarded. Every resident is both worker and material.

Yet something else appears to live in the central basin.

It has no body that enters the upper chamber. It is known only by the lower note rising through the still water and by the reflection that contains more roots, greater distances, and darker passages than exist overhead. At times a movement crosses that reflected depth without troubling the surface: not quite a fish, not quite a shadow, but a slow rearrangement of the chamber’s mirrored anatomy.

The surrounding creatures avoid the basin without fear or ceremony. Roots divide before reaching it. Insects alter their routes. Droplets falling toward it are caught by fibers or diverted along leaf veins. This avoidance may be instinct, or it may be the basin’s own form of maintenance.

Whatever dwells below does not govern the room. There is no command in the lower drone. Rather, it answers the bronze insects above, holding a second pitch beneath theirs. Between those two sustained notes, the chamber keeps its dimensions. Growth stops at the proper boundaries. Predation does not become slaughter. Water moves without eroding its channels. Death proceeds without accumulation.

The inhabitant of the basin may therefore be neither animal nor plant, but the reflected counterpart of the whole community: another ecology folded against this one, exchanging vibration where ordinary matter cannot pass. Each hidden room may be the regulator of the other. Each may see its counterpart only as an impossible depth in a hand-wide pool.

What lives here is a balanced machine that was never built, composed entirely of living things—and, beneath its still center, perhaps the dim twin that keeps it from changing.
