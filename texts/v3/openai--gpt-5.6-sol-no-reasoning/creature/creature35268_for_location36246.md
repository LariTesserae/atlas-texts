Nothing lives there in the usual sense.

The city itself is the nearest thing to an inhabitant, though it has no single body and performs none of the acts by which life is normally recognized. It does not breathe, feed, reproduce, or die. Instead, it maintains. Its roots hold the posture of architecture. Its minerals remember streets. Its water occupies impossible positions with the calm assurance of blood held in transparent veins. What seems to be vegetation is less a population of plants than one continuous green faculty expressed as moss, grove, creeper, flower, and fruit wherever those shapes are required.

The flowers need no visitors because they are not invitations. Their inward-facing ranks resemble eyes, but they do not watch. Fruit ripens only as an appearance of ripeness, then withdraws into the branch that displayed it. Leaves soften and pass through stone, emerging elsewhere without journeying between. The gardens are organs of circulation in a body whose circulation requires no motion.

If this organism has a voice, it is the roar.

The sound is not made by anything within the basin. It inhabits the place as heat and wetness do, equally present at every depth and distance. Perhaps it is the noise of all the absent activity compressed into one unvarying utterance: the waterfall that does not fall, the crowds that never arrived, the construction that never occurred, the centuries that left wear without passing. It enters teeth and ribs because those who come here briefly become its instruments, though the city itself remains unstirred.

There may be presences in the doorways, but they are not people. They are the fit between an opening and the expectation that something should cross it. Niches shaped for sleepers hold the contour of sleep without a sleeper. Plazas contain crowding without crowds. Tombs preserve the absence of the dead. Every district seems densely occupied by functions that have never needed bodies.

The fixed reflections may be another kind of resident. Pale towers stand only in water, complete and inaccessible. Buildings absent from one terrace appear in the pools of another. Shadows detach from their apparent owners and fasten themselves nearby, as though seeking more suitable forms. These images possess a quiet independence, yet they neither move nor change. They are not ghosts of what once existed. They are alternatives the city keeps visible without having to realize them.

Scale also lives there, if living can mean endlessly returning through oneself. A traveler may diminish along a narrowing street without feeling altered. A mountain may be a tower; a tower, a petrified reed; a district, the veining of a leaf growing in its own courtyard. The city contains no smallest chamber and no final outer wall. Its immense and minute forms are not copies of one another but the same form encountered at different degrees of attention.

Anyone who entered would be the basin’s only animal and its only event. Yet the place would not acknowledge the intrusion. No footprint would remain. A broken stem would already be whole while still held in the hand. Water would admit the body without rippling, and the roar would not gain a note. Hunger, sleep, injury, and aging would belong solely to the visitor. Against the city’s completion, such needs would begin to feel like local defects.

In time—if time can be said to pass there—the visitor’s memory would become less reliable than the streets. Recollections might attach themselves to neighboring courtyards as shadows attach to neighboring walls. A room never entered would seem familiar. A stair repeatedly climbed would be remembered as descending. The missing inhabitants might acquire faces borrowed from elsewhere, then lose them again. Eventually the visitor could no longer be certain whether they had discovered a bed-shaped niche or supplied the idea of sleep that shaped it.

The city would remain innocent of the confusion. It does not deceive. It simply offers every structure without choosing among meanings.

So what lives there is neither a hidden civilization nor a solitary monster. It is a complete ecology of resemblances: stone resembling intention, growth resembling labor, water resembling memory, noise resembling multitudes, and permanence resembling age. The basin is inhabited by all the things a city implies, stripped of the beings who would enact them.

It is crowded with absence.

And if the city itself can be called alive, then its life is not growth but perfect, inexhaustible self-containment: a living thing whose only action is to remain exactly what it has never needed to become.
