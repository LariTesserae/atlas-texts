The people who live beneath the salt call themselves the **Interveners**.

They do not believe the hollow belongs to them. To claim ownership would be both sacrilege and absurdity. A person might as reasonably claim to own the instant between a flint’s strike and its spark. They inhabit the chamber only in the narrowest sense: they sleep there, labor there, eat what its arrested ecology permits, and sometimes bear children in the stone shelter beneath the overhanging root. But they understand themselves chiefly as braces set against an unfinished break.

There are never many of them below. Usually six occupy the shelter: two keepers of position, two harvesters, one listener, and one novice. Others live on the salt plain in low tents built around the warm chimney. Pairs descend and ascend on a schedule measured not by the changeless world below, but by wax tablets carried from the sun. Without these, a keeper might remain for a night or for a season and feel no difference.

Long residence marks them. Their hearing dulls first, though they insist it does not disappear; rather, it becomes occupied. Even on the surface they feel the chamber’s sustained chord inside their jawbones. They speak too loudly and sleep poorly in silence. Many hang little racks of shells or metal scraps outside their tents so that the wind will make an irregular clatter. Random sound comforts them. Rhythm—the proof that one moment has yielded to another—can move an old keeper to tears.

Their bodies are lean, scarred by salt, and dusted perpetually gray. They shave their heads and coat the scalp with dark wax, because loose hair can foul the chamber’s exact arrangements. Clothing is close-wrapped: mineral-fiber bands, hide aprons, soft-soled shoes stitched without buckles. Every garment is marked with intersecting lines. These are not ornaments but positional records. A knot at the hip tells how far its wearer stood from the northern slab when a tool was taken; three white bars on a sleeve preserve the original angle of a basket’s handle. Senior keepers resemble walking diagrams.

Names below are not spoken if touch will serve. Each inhabitant has a pressure-name: a sequence tapped against knuckles, wrist, and forearm. Family names are pressed at the shoulder. Warnings are delivered against the sternum, where they can be felt through the chamber’s roar. Their language has elaborate terms for nearness, orientation, and permissible displacement, but few for duration. Instead of saying *yesterday*, they say *before the last ascent*. Instead of *soon*, *within this visit*. Death is *the final removal*.

The keepers are not themselves arrested. They age, bleed, recover, and change. This makes them the most dangerous things in the hollow.

A falling drop of sweat may land where nothing has ever lain. A forgotten flake of skin may enter an empty circle. A child’s careless foot can alter a geometry older than its people. The keepers therefore move with solemn precision, placing each foot within pale outlines chalked on the black salt. Novices spend months learning the paths before they are allowed to carry anything. They practice aboveground in full sunlight, walking between stones while elders shove, distract, and blindfold them.

Their deepest discipline is called **returning**.

To return an object is not merely to put it back. Its angle must be restored, its tension matched, its relation to every nearby body recovered. Three keepers usually perform the work: one places, one sights along cords, and one rests fingertips on the floor to feel whether the chamber’s vibration has resumed its familiar pressure. A successful return is announced by no visible change. That absence is the point. Failure may reveal itself as a stiffened strand, a silent root, an insect wing that no longer closes, or a new overtone in the endless chord.

The keepers fear new sounds more than wounds.

They harvest sparingly. From the wafer-grazers they take only mouth-scraped green tissue already separated by the animals’ fixed feeding motion. From the ivory burrowers they shave the thin mineral ribbons lifted by each repeated flex. Eggs are collected only after they have been extruded far enough to count as displaced but not far enough to have begun whatever passes for development. The resulting food is dry, sharp, and rich with salts and oils. It is softened in the mouth, never cooked. Interveners have blackened teeth, glossy gums, and an ability to taste metals with extraordinary precision.

Their children are born ordinary and noisy. Infants are kept above until they can obey a touch-command instantly. The first descent is a grave ceremony. The child crawls through the powder passage between two adults, touches the floor with both palms, and is shown the lifted foot of the three-legged animal. Every initiate asks whether it will step. Every elder answers by pressing the same sign into the child’s wrist:

**Not while held.**

What is holding it remains deliberately unspecified.

The Listener has duties unlike the others. Each watch, they cross to the transparent tree and place one hand on its trunk. The absolute silence is painful at first. After hours in the chamber, the sudden absence of vibration feels like losing the body. Within it, the Listener attends to the unfinished crack.

They are not listening for completion. If it completed, there might be no warning that could matter. They listen for difference: a widening, a roughened edge, a second fracture answering the first. On returning their hand to the roar, they record what they heard by scoring a clay tablet. Every surviving tablet bears the same mark: a single curved incision meaning **unchanged**.

Some Listeners eventually become convinced that the crack is not perfectly constant. They report minute variations: a splintering too fine for previous ears, a pause inside the breaking, a sound like breath behind a shell. Such claims divide the people above. One faction treats them as warnings. Another says prolonged silence makes the mind invent time where none exists.

No Listener serves for long.

The grove’s animals are less troubled by their condition, if condition it is. The mica-backed creatures open and close their wings with jewel-like solemnity, revealing warm organs folded beneath translucent plates. Their motion has no middle that the eye can retain. Wing closed becomes wing open, then closed again, though no gust is produced. If touched, the creature is hot and trembles violently. It does not flee. Its many legs grip the same grains of salt forever.

The blind fox-shaped sleepers may be the chamber’s only social animals. They stand nose to flank in a ring around the fissure, pale tails hanging straight down. Their chests expand, but never contract; then, without transition, they are found at the beginning of the same inhalation. Keepers sometimes sleep beside them. Through the salt floor comes a sensation of immense, shared dreaming. Several people have claimed to see through the sleepers’ senses: not the hollow as it is, but a black cleft newly opening and green life pouring into it like flame drawn into a draft.

The predator beneath the crystal fern is called the **Almost-Leaper**. Its body resembles a folded knife wrapped in translucent skin. Six hooked limbs are bunched beneath it, and along its back stand sensory blades thin enough to split amber light. The prey before it is a soft wheel of flesh balanced on dozens of delicate feet. One side of the wheel has begun to flatten in alarm.

Keepers disagree about whether the hunt repeats or has never happened even once.

The floating sacks seem least bound. They drift no measurable distance, yet their veined bodies bulge and soften as if traveling through currents unavailable to everything else. Dense gas glitters inside them. Occasionally one rotates enough to expose a puckered underside lined with black cilia. Chalk measurements insist the rotation is not real. All the same, novices are warned never to stand directly beneath one.

And then there are the things made chiefly of holes.

The keepers classify them as plants because they root into stone and take mineral vapor through fringed green margins. Yet the holes are not empty in the usual sense. Looking through one does not reveal what lies behind it from the expected angle. A hand passed into an opening emerges cold, dusted with white powder, and sometimes older by a fraction—a fingernail lengthened, a shallow cut sealed, one dark hair turned pale. For this reason the hole-plants are given the widest paths. They may be the only organisms in the grove that complete any action at all, though what they alter is carried out of sight.

Nothing so thoroughly inhabits the hollow as the arrangement itself.

The empty circles around the trunks are not vacancies but organs. The broad paths are not merely spaces between lives; they participate in that life. When a tool is left in a circle, the nearest stalk grows audibly taut, and every animal in the chamber seems to press harder against its arrested motion. Remove the tool, and the old chord returns. From this, the Interveners teach that an individual creature is only the visible border of a larger being composed equally of bodies and distances.

They call this larger being **the Poise**.

The Poise includes the seed and the arc it has not finished falling through. It includes the raised foot and the unreceived footprint. It includes the predator, its prey, and the spring that has gathered but not yet spent itself. It includes the northern slab, the dust below it, the unstruck floor, and perhaps the salt plain overhead. Whether the Interveners themselves have become part of it is a forbidden question, because any honest answer would change how they move.

Still, evidence accumulates.

A dead keeper cannot be carried up the shaft. The body grows unbearably heavy at the boundary, just as a whole animal does. It must be disassembled—not butchered, the people insist, but **distributed**. Bone is returned to tool racks, fat rendered into wax, teeth set into measuring cords, skin cured for ladder knots. Each part can leave only after being made into something that preserves a sanctioned relation. This is why the hide ladder is said to be older than those who tied it. Its knots contain their predecessors, renewed a strip at a time, while the ladder’s form remains unchanged.

Thus the chamber has inhabitants, but perhaps no visitors.

Anyone who descends alters the balance merely by arriving. Anyone who learns its paths begins carrying those paths in muscle and bone. Anyone who eats of its creatures bears away matter that can no longer be restored exactly, however meticulous the return. The Poise accommodates these small thefts by making a place for the thieves.

On the surface, the Interveners still seem human. They laugh, quarrel, marry, miscarry, tell stories, and watch weather cross the salt. But when they stand together, they unconsciously leave equal spaces between their bodies. Their children turn aside just before touching in play. Their camps form clean circles around bare patches no one remembers choosing.

And when an old keeper dies beneath the sun, the others sometimes hear—through wind, cloth, and miles of white earth—a delicate sound close beside the ear.

Something cracking.

Still not finished.
