Those who live in the basin are compact, patient people, though not necessarily small. Their compactness is social: elbows drawn in, possessions nested, tempers trained to pass through narrow places without catching. They inhabit one another’s consequences. A careless household can drown its neighbors; a diligent one may keep an entire row dry. Thus they value consideration less as kindness than as structural competence.

They are called many things by those outside—mudfolk, seep-dwellers, the sunken—but among themselves they are simply **the Settled**, a name carrying its own quiet irony. Nothing beneath them has settled completely, and few of their buildings retain the shape in which they were first made.

Their bodies show the town’s demands. Feet are broad-toed and toughened despite the damp, with pale creases from long hours in wet clay. Most wear calf-wrapped sandals woven from sedge and replaced constantly. Their hands are strong in the thumbs and fingertips: the hands of plasterers, potters, drain-clearers, and people accustomed to testing walls by touch. Fingernails hold permanent gray crescents. Knees and shoulders bear chalky patches from crawling through low ducts. Older inhabitants tend to stoop—not from submission, but from a lifetime beneath low roofs.

Skin ailments are ordinary. They rub themselves with rendered fat, powdered lichen, and bitter clay to discourage rot. A person with dry feet is remarked upon. A person who smells strongly of mildew is quietly watched, because that odor may come from a hidden seep beneath their bed rather than from their clothes.

Clothing is narrow-cut and layered: reed cloth, felted hair, patched skins, and clay-colored wraps that can be removed one at a time as they soak through. Loose hems are dangerous around sluice grooves. Children wear short tunics and bead-strung belts from which hang tiny scrapers, drain hooks, and household markers. Adults carry almost nothing that cannot survive immersion. Important agreements are pressed into fired tablets, not written on hide or fiber.

They have little use for ornament that merely shines. Their jewelry is pierced slate, smooth black cistern pebbles, shell disks, and chalk beads stained by different waters. A pale band around the wrist may signify membership in a drainage row. Green-streaked beads identify those responsible for drinking cisterns. Orange ironstone belongs to potters and kiln tenders. These distinctions are practical enough to be read at a glance during a flood.

No household is entirely private or entirely independent. The basic social body is not the family but the **fall**: all those whose roofs and channels feed the same sequence of cisterns. A fall may include blood relatives, lodgers, widows, craftspeople, adopted children, and bitter enemies. Whatever their affections, they clear gutters together. Marriages often join adjacent falls, but water rights do not automatically pass with marriage. A person may sleep in one house, cook in another, and owe maintenance to a third because runoff from their childhood roof still crosses it.

They measure kinship partly through remembered shelter. Someone who shared your dry room during a collapse may thereafter be called roof-sibling. A child nursed in a public drain vault during storm season acquires obligations to every household whose water passed overhead. The dead remain members of a fall for as long as a gutter they laid continues to function.

Age is counted less by years than by changes in the basin. Elders say, “She was born before the west bank split,” or, “He first walked in the season when the central seep tasted sweet.” Exact birthdays are difficult in a place where weather and construction blur together, but everyone remembers where a child’s first rain was caught. Newborns are washed not with central-chamber water, which may not be carried, but with the first clean runoff from the roof under which they will sleep.

Children learn hydrology before history. They are taught to distinguish clean dripping from wall seepage by sound, to smell sour cistern water, to keep fingers out of pressure cracks, and to freeze when someone calls, “Below.” Their games imitate adult work. They build miniature neighborhoods from clay fragments and pour cupfuls of water over them, arguing over which tiny house caused the flood. They float marked chips through gutters and race alongside on the roofs. They dare one another to whisper into old air pipes and listen for replies.

They are warned about the Under-Mason, but not simply frightened with it. It may punish obstruction, reward a well-cut drain, steal dropped tools, sweeten water, undermine liars, or knot testing cords merely from curiosity. Children draw it differently depending on age: first as a pale frog with many hands, later as a blind stone-eater, later still as branching lines with no body at all.

Adults do not agree either. Quarry-workers describe a soft, colorless creature broad as a mat, contracting through the pores of the stone. Cistern keepers speak of colonies of white worms that cement grit with their mouths. Potters insist the “knocks” are pressure fractures. Elders sometimes call it the town’s lowest inhabitant and leave the matter there.

Whatever they believe, they behave as though the ground can answer.

Their most respected people are **listeners**. A listener may be a builder, healer, judge, or simply someone with unusual sensitivity to vibration. They kneel with cheek or palm against floors. They tap listening bowls, compare notes between rooms, and ask sleepers what they heard before dawn. Good listeners identify hidden water by pitch and delay. Great listeners are said to know whether a wall will crack inward or outward.

They possess no grand priesthood. Authority is distributed among cistern keepers, fall elders, sluice arbiters, and those who remember previous arrangements of buried rooms. Disputes are heard beside running water whenever possible, because a visible flow makes consequences harder to disguise. The commonest offenses are diverting first runoff, silting a neighbor’s channel, concealing a seep, and shifting a boundary stone while repairing a wall.

Punishments are usually labor. An offender dredges the drain they endangered, replasters foundations, or surrenders their clean-water turn. The gravest sentence is **dry exclusion**: the person remains in town but no fall must catch water for them. Few endure it for long. Banishment is rare, because an exile takes knowledge of buried channels with them, and because sending someone away does nothing to repair what they damaged.

Their speech is soft and full of spatial terms. They say a person is “upstream of blame,” that a promise “holds clay,” that an unreliable account “runs milky.” To understand something is to “find its outlet.” A stubborn person is “roofed against rain.” A dead argument is “a filled room”—still present, perhaps still useful, but no longer open for habitation.

Silence is an active courtesy. Walls transmit murmurs, quarrels, lovemaking, sickness, and grief. People survive by hearing without possessing what they hear. Children are corrected for repeating words caught through plaster. Adults announce themselves before entering a passage beside a sleeping alcove, even if no door separates them. Privacy consists not in secrecy but in disciplined disregard.

Meals are damp, mineral, and intensely flavored to overcome their sameness. Bulbs are roasted in sealed clay pockets. Cress is chopped with sour curd or ground insects brought from outside. Mushrooms are smoked over dung embers. Algal sheets are folded around pastes of seed and chalk-salt. Meat is uncommon. The two dogs eat scraps but are valued more for their attention to the drains than for protection.

Each dog belongs nominally to the whole town. One is old and mud-brown, with a torn ear and a habit of sleeping near blocked outlets. The other is pale, long-legged, and nervous, often staring at walls seconds before a hidden trickle emerges. Their movements are watched almost as closely as listening bowls. No one admits to superstition about them, yet a household whose floor the pale dog refuses to cross will usually lift its mats before nightfall.

The translucent gutter frogs are never deliberately handled. They appear too suddenly and bruise too easily. When one clings above a doorway, those inside leave a shallow shard of clean water beneath it, though no one has seen a frog descend to drink. Children believe the frogs pass messages into the stone through their toes. Some listeners suspect their inaudible calls account for the dogs’ midnight vigilance.

Black water beetles receive more practical respect. Their presence suggests that a cistern is clean, oxygenated, and not poisoned by sour clay. Cistern keepers count them at dawn. A sudden disappearance may close a reservoir before any human detects a changed taste. Each beetle is individually noticeable, and some are recognized by scratches or missing legs. To kill one through carelessness is shameful; to find one dead is a reason to inspect every upstream roof.

Most crafts are variations of maintenance. Potters make trough sections, listening bowls, seep cups, lamp dishes, and narrow-necked settling jars. Roof fitters know how different flakes shed rain. Plasterers mix clay according to location: fatty for inner walls, gritty for channels, root-bound for foundations, chalk-heavy around cistern mouths. Drain crawlers are usually adolescents and slight adults who can enter passages others cannot. They carry shell scrapers between their teeth and cords around their ankles so they can be pulled out if a vault slumps.

Their few copper blades are not owned outright. Each has a name and a remembered chain of custodians. One may be used only for cutting reed mats; another for surgery and cord; another for scoring gutter molds. Sharpening, lending, and accounting for these blades are solemn matters. Losing one in an unlined crack is both an economic disaster and a possible offering to the Under-Mason, though no one would intentionally make such an offering.

The dead are not buried in the saturated ground outside, where bodies rise or drift, nor burned, for fuel is scarce. Instead they are dried as well as the climate allows in warm roof spaces, wrapped tightly, and placed inside rooms that can no longer be safely occupied. The doorway is narrowed, then sealed. A channel, niche, or stair may later be built across the chamber. The dead become part of the town’s load-bearing memory.

Names are not inscribed there. Instead, a thumbprint is pressed into the outer plaster with a slant indicating the deceased’s fall. If the wall is later repaired, the print is copied outward onto the new surface. Over generations some prints migrate far from the bodies they commemorate. Everyone knows this. Accuracy matters less than continuation.

Those who choose to leave fill their hand impression in the old seep chamber with pale silt. This is not regarded as death, but it is close enough that farewells are restrained. A leaver gives away all claims to first runoff and names the person responsible for their remaining channels. If they return, they add a new hand rather than reopening the old one. Thus some people are present several times upon the wall, while others who have lived there all their lives are nearly obscured by later plaster.

Newcomers are examined for usefulness, but also for weight. A large household strains roofs, food stores, and drainage. Strangers are therefore admitted slowly, usually through sponsorship by a fall. They sleep first in upper sheds, where any hidden seep beneath them can do little damage. Only after contributing through a full storm cycle may they press a hand into the central chamber.

They emerge from this initiation wet to the elbows and silent. No one applauds. Someone offers them a cloth; someone else points out which stair is least slippery. Belonging is rarely celebrated loudly. It is demonstrated afterward, when they are awakened to clear a gutter at night and rise without being asked twice.

The people do sing, though almost never in chorus. Shared rhythm is dangerous during work because it can mask the irregular sound of water. Instead they use overlapping, low melodies without a common beat. One person begins while plastering; another answers from behind a wall; a third hums through a pipe several houses away. The result resembles water moving through separate channels toward no visible meeting place.

Their stories also branch. No teller claims the only version. In one account the basin was made when the Under-Mason turned in sleep. In another, the land sank beneath the weight of an unkept promise. In a third, there was no agency at all: the flats cracked, people saw useful water, and cleverness accumulated until it became a town. All three may be told in one evening without contradiction.

On nights when the underground knocks travel inward, nearly everyone wakes. They do not rush outside. Each household lights the smallest lamp it owns, lifts mats, checks sleeping children, and taps the floor bowls. Listeners crouch at junctions. Sluice keepers loosen gates in case pressure must be released. The dogs stand rigid, noses low.

No one speaks the Under-Mason’s name while the knocks are passing. This is not exactly taboo. It is caution against mistaking a name for an explanation.

In the morning, the town gathers around the damage. The cracked wall is touched, sounded, and opened carefully. People note the fracture’s direction, the color of the moisture, the smell of the exposed clay. They argue over cause. They redistribute runoff. They carry pale stone grit to the potters. If a new spring has opened, someone kneels beside it with a cup but does not drink until the beetles have been watched, the water has settled, and its path has been traced as far as possible.

Then a channel is shaped beneath it.

A jar is placed below.

A household sign is cut into the sluice.

And by evening the new water, however strange its arrival, has acquired neighbors, duties, prohibitions, and a place in memory.
