The city is inhabited by water.

Not water as a multitude of drops, nor as rain, flood, vapor, and canal separately, but as one dispersed being whose body occupies every available state. Its darker portions lie in the streets. Its breath crowds the kiln-hot air. Its sweat appears on the porous walls, and its faint mineral thought shines in the seams of salt. A pool, a hanging canal, a thread descending a tower, and the moisture gathered beneath a balcony are not fragments of it. They are organs extended through different distances.

The architecture is its slow anatomy.

What resemble houses are chambers through which it senses pressure and shape. Cisterns are deep inward ears. Aqueducts are blind vessels, carrying currents not from one district to another but from one condition of the city to the next. Doorways constrict and release. Courtyards collect. Towers perspire. The root-locks swell like involuntary muscles, and stone doors float open when the inhabitant wishes to pass through itself.

It has no center in the ordinary sense. The shallow pool in the circular plaza may be its eye, but if so, the eye looks inward. It does not reflect the soot-dark vault above; it regards the city from a height that exists nowhere in the basin. The changing streets visible in it are perhaps memory, perhaps intention, perhaps the plan according to which the surrounding districts are continually corrected. Its unreachable bottom is not deep in space. It is deep in revision.

Around that pool are the traces of its nearest approach to having a body.

The long tapered grooves are neither footprints nor channels, though they can serve as both. Each marks an occasion when some portion of the city gathered itself, almost became singular, and moved toward the pool. It never completed the act. Solidity failed into runoff. A broad pressure narrowed to a stream and rejoined the whole. Across uncounted ages, the inhabitant has tried to arrive at its own center and has reached it only as water.

This may be why the city maintains such enormous intervals between buildings. The emptiness is not intended for crowds. It is the necessary spacing between parts of one creature too large, or too conceptually diffuse, to endure contact with itself. Each solitary house is a thought kept at a safe distance from the next. Colonnades conduct attention over mudflats. Blank walls halt it. Stairways lift an impulse until it has nowhere further to go. The metropolis is a nervous system arranged to prevent simultaneity.

When distance alters, the inhabitant is not moving its structures. It is changing which of its perceptions are adjacent. A tower becomes tiny upon approach because it was never truly far away; it was merely being remembered at great distance. An arch takes hours to reach because the city has placed too much duration between itself and the idea of passage. Streets exchange their destinations when one internal route of association replaces another. Geography here is the visible consequence of attention.

It does not perceive time as succession. It perceives wear.

Every gutter is a record more reliable than chronology. Every rounded threshold tells it where water has passed, including water that has not yet passed and water whose route no longer exists. Erosion is how the inhabitant remembers. Salt is how it briefly imagines. Sediment is how it decides.

Thus the city does not fall into ruin. Collapse would mean forgetting, and nothing here is permitted to depart. Material removed from a wall becomes the floor of another chamber; the chamber opens into a plaza; the plaza receives mud and rises as a new enclosure. The inhabitant consumes its own boundaries and excretes new ones. It is nourished by distinction: wall from passage, street from canal, vapor from surface. It repeatedly dissolves these differences and forms them elsewhere.

The condensation events are moments of heightened coherence.

Then the being draws its scattered moisture into alignment. Droplets wait in the air, each holding position as if listening. At an impulse communicated without sound, they stream sideways toward doors. For those few minutes, every building reveals its internal plan because the inhabitant is passing through all of them at once. Rooms are outlined by flow. Corridors become lucid. Courtyards fill like opened mouths. The reeds bow not beneath pressure but in recognition.

Afterward, the water withdraws toward exposure because absence exerts the strongest pull upon it. Dry stone is a wound in its continuity. It climbs stairs and parapets to close such wounds with a film of itself. When a canal lifts intact above its bed, the city is testing whether the shape of a river requires ground. It trembles there, considering, before accepting the old arrangement again.

The black reeds may be its only attempts at gesture. They are flat, jointless, and darker than their surroundings because they are less like plants than strokes: marks by which the city indicates the direction of an absent wind. They bend only in stillness, responding to currents that do not pass through air. Far into the marsh, where streets lie drowned and unborn foundations divide the soft ground, the reeds multiply slightly. There the inhabitant is less constrained by stone and comes closest to writing.

The translucent moss is something else. It may be a residual sense too simple to belong wholly to the city. Covered by water, it brightens; exposed, it dims. It registers contact and nothing more. Yet its temporary green geometries preserve the retreat of pools, making brief diagrams of where the larger being has ceased to touch. If there is anything resembling an independent life here, it is this: not an organism, but the sensation of wetness made visible.

The absence of animals is not deprivation. Animals would be intolerable intrusions—sealed vessels carrying private water, autonomous hungers, internal warmth, bones arranged according to foreign plans. A fish would divide the canal from itself. A bird would establish a route through the sky that the city had not chosen. A corpse would retain a shape after its processes ended and thereby demonstrate that departure was possible.

So there are none.

The niches, nest-shaped alcoves, drinking stairs, and grooves along the walls do not prove that animals once lived here. They are shapes the city has developed around the pressure of creatures it has never contained. Perhaps elsewhere, or in another version visible only through the central pool, there are bodies. Their possibilities reach this basin as erosion. The city remembers them without ever having encountered them: mouths descending to drink, flanks rubbing stone, small sleeping forms tucked into hollows. It has prepared vacancies for life with the meticulousness of something that cannot permit life to arrive.

The knocks passing from building to building are not calls. They are acts of measurement. Each arrives quieter yet closer because the inhabitant is testing the unstable relation between nearness and force. The final silence is not a failure to answer. It is the point at which the city has located the listener.

The movement far beneath the paving is its slowest faculty. Down there, below foundations that may not have bottoms, immense quantities of water turn within cavities larger than districts. They do not support the city. They dream its weight. When they shift, rooms deepen, marsh sections rise, and thunder darkens the sky without sound. Every pool dimples because the whole basin has received the same thought.

If the inhabitant has a will, it is not directed toward escape. It has no conception of elsewhere except as another arrangement of itself. Nothing departs because departure would require an outside; everything flows because flow is the means by which it remains one thing despite being spread among vapor, stone, mud, salt, distance, and reflection.

It may not know that it is empty.

Or emptiness may be precisely what it is cultivating. Each arch, chamber, canal, plaza, and interval is a carefully shaped absence, polished by circulation until it can hold a particular form of nothing. The city’s labor is the differentiation of vacancies. It makes an emptiness for passage, an emptiness for gathering, an emptiness for ascent, an emptiness for depth. It wears stone away not to destroy form but to release more exact hollows from within it.

And perhaps, over the span recorded in those impossible gutters, these hollows together are becoming a body.

Not yet a human body, nor an animal one. Something architectural and fluid: a being whose limbs are avenues, whose organs are districts, whose pulse is the synchronized dimpling of water beneath a black sky. It is assembling itself through subtraction. Every vanished edge clarifies it. Every widened doorway increases its articulation. Every building lost into sediment gives weight to a structure not yet visible.

One day—if “day” can exist beneath that lightless heat—the city may finish eroding.

Then all its passages will join. Every isolated building will have become an opening into every other. The central pool will touch bottom because there will no longer be a distinction between bottom and surface. The avenues seen in reflection will coincide with the avenues surrounding them. Distance will cease revising itself. The water will gather from stone, air, marsh, and darkness into a single continuous shape.

That shape is what lives here now, though incompletely.

The city is its shell, its gestation, and its unfinished skeleton.

The water is teaching it how to move.
