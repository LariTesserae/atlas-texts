Those who live here do not divide neatly into animal, plant, worker, and machine. Such distinctions may hold for a specimen lifted from the mud, or for a component dried and labeled on a bench, but they fail at the scale of a lifetime. On the plateau, survival means entering a circulation. Bodies borrow organs from structures. Structures recruit roots, insects, sediment, and habit. Even the inhabitants who arrived from elsewhere have become less a population than a maintenance function performed by the country upon itself.

The workers are called, in the lowlands, engineers. Among themselves, when speech is necessary, they use a word closer to listeners.

They are narrow-bodied and compact, built low in the hips and shoulders so that they can brace against floodwater or crawl through pressure galleries without turning. Their skin is dark, hairless, and densely ridged. Water runs along these ridges toward absorbent folds at the flanks, where salts and trace metals are recovered before the rest is shed. A worker emerging from the rain looks carved from wet basalt until it moves.

Their faces seem unfinished to outsiders. The eyes are small and sealed beneath translucent inner lids, useful only at arm’s length or during the blue instant of an electrical discharge. Vision is not their governing sense. Their ears open in several places: behind the jaw, along the collarbones, and as shallow pressure pits down the ribs. With these they hear through air, walls, rails, standing water, and one another’s bodies. Their hands are broad, with six short fingers whose inner surfaces carry heat-sensitive pads and fine combs capable of reading corrosion, fungal growth, stamped symbols, or the faint vibration of current in a buried line.

When they stand still, they are seldom resting. They are placing themselves within the local noise.

A listener may lean both palms against a pipe for hours, receiving pump strokes from beyond the mapped network. Another lies face down in warm seepage to follow a fault transmitted through the marsh floor. Teams gather around resonant chambers and press shoulders together, making of their skeletons a coupled instrument. Diagnosis is rarely expressed first in numbers. It begins as imitation: one listener produces the rhythm felt in a failing valve; another answers with the rhythm of a healthy branch; a third introduces the remembered beat of that district during flood season. Only later are these patterns cut into ceramic records or entered into the slow control systems below.

Their speech is shaped accordingly. Close conversation uses touches, jaw-clicks, throat pulses, and pressure applied through linked hands. Across distance they employ low horns sunk into rock or strike coded sequences on guide-rails. Names are not stable sounds but signatures of timing. A person is identified by the pause they leave between two taps, the acceleration at the end of a hum, the way a repeated pulse weakens. Two workers can bear what an outsider would call the same name and remain unmistakable to everyone who knows them.

They do not reckon age by years. A life is counted in systems learned. A child may be “of the upper spillways,” then “of the eastward mains,” later “of three basins and a silent station.” The oldest listeners hold whole watersheds in memory. They can recognize pressure events whose originating machinery collapsed before their grandparents were born. When such an elder dies, apprentices lie beside the body on a conductive floor while stored vibrations are played through it. The dead person’s bones alter the signal in characteristic ways. These resonances are recorded and built into future diagnostic instruments. Thus a capable listener may continue serving as a filter, a calibration standard, or a warning tone for centuries.

Their shelters contain little that would be recognized as furniture. Sleeping recesses are molded into warm interior walls. Hooks and tension harnesses let occupants rest upright during floods. Floors are crossed by narrow channels carrying water from different parts of the plateau. Before sleep, inhabitants place fingers or feet into these streams, keeping watch through changes in temperature and pulse. There is no true off-duty state. There are only lighter and deeper forms of attention.

Children are raised near minor machinery: condensation traps, nursery cisterns, fungal exchangers, valves too small to cause a district failure. They learn first that every repetitive sound invites three questions: what makes it, what answers it, and what has learned to imitate it. This last question is essential. A child who follows the wrong ticking pipe may lose a hand. One who answers a false carrier signal may be taken into the canopy.

The workers’ clothing is thick, close-fitting, and layered with ceramic scales that click softly when struck by rain. Each suit is cultivated rather than sewn. Pale filamentous organisms grow through a flexible mineral mesh, sealing punctures and changing porosity with temperature. Older suits acquire stains, mosses, larval cases, and repair bands until they resemble small walking sections of infrastructure. Tools are fastened inside the garment so that nothing swings freely. Loose metal has too many voices.

The most experienced workers carry no lamps. Light destroys the subtle contrasts by which warm surfaces, air movement, and living charge are perceived. Instead they release small motes from capsules at the wrist. These creatures drift ahead, flashing only when they encounter exposed current, fresh blood, toxic vapor, or the exhalation of a large predator. Different strains produce different colors, though the colors are intended less to be seen than to be felt as frequencies of heat against the face.

They do not control the industrial network. Control is regarded as a lowland superstition.

Their task is negotiation.

A pumping organ may have to be persuaded to lower its rate by opening an ancient bypass and allowing it to hear the pressure-memory of a drought. A turbine that has begun to resonate with a mating herd cannot simply be stopped; its rhythm must be shifted gradually, lest the animals follow it into the intake galleries. Root masses are not cleared from conduits if they have become load-bearing. Predatory vines nesting around electrical contacts may be fed mineral slurry until they release the metal voluntarily. When a pipeline ruptures, listeners first determine whether the break is damage, adaptation, or the network’s attempt to establish a new branch.

Repairs often look like rituals because the systems respond to sequence, season, and attention. Workers enter flooded chambers in groups of five, each beating a separate pattern against the wall. They turn valves on breaths rather than counts. They introduce cultured moss to panels and wait for its colorless fronds to align. They sing into wells—not melodically, but with descending pressure tones—until an answering pulse rises through the water. Some procedures are preserved with no explanation except that omitting a pause or reversing two strikes has killed everyone in the chamber before.

Not all inhabitants are workers, though almost everything works.

The blind grazers of the fern plains are broad, low creatures carried on eight jointed legs. Their hides are pebbled with mineral nodules and shallow cups in which rainwater, algae, and insects collect. They move with great deliberation, combing bacterial films from exposed stone. Each herd follows a matriarch whose jaw plates contain deposits of magnetic ore. She does not navigate toward landmarks but along buried induction currents and the faint directional pull of water under pressure.

Their calves are born soft and nearly transparent. For their first season they walk beneath the adults, eating fragments shed from their mineral armor. In time these fragments seed hard plates of their own. Herds passing through an industrial district scrape rust and salts from machinery, polishing certain surfaces while leaving others untouched. The network depends on this grazing. Where herds have vanished, conductive films thicken, housings overheat, and lightning begins taking paths it should not.

The marsh-striders are lighter and more numerous. Their many feet spread like floating leaves, each toe tipped with hairs that trap a skin of air. They run across reed mats and black water, stopping so abruptly that their bodies continue swaying after their feet are still. Their hollow limb bones amplify distant impacts. During storms they gather in circles, legs touching, and become a living array for locating lightning. Workers read the orientation of these circles to predict which buried districts will wake.

The small flyers around transformers are not one species but whole airborne ecologies. Some drink condensation. Some eat insulation. Some repair what the others eat, spinning resin across exposed fibers. Some prey only on individuals carrying excess charge. Their swarms have a rough collective intelligence, though not one concerned with anything beyond warmth, voltage, moisture, and breeding space. A healthy transformer wears a shifting halo of them. A bare transformer is assumed to be dead or dangerous.

Deeper in the vegetation live the mimics.

The two-note caller is a squat ambush animal with a resonant chest and a mouth that opens vertically along the torso. It learned the warning of old transport machines because small scavengers once moved aside at that sound, exposing themselves. Now the carriers have changed their alarms repeatedly, and the animal has changed with them. In some valleys the imitation is so exact that workers identify a real vehicle only by the mistakes in its timing.

Cable hunters are longer than the machines they resemble. Their outer segments incorporate metal dust until they reflect the same brief blue-white as wet steel. They suspend themselves from lines, drawing their bodies forward in measured contractions. Small maintenance fauna approach to clean them; larger prey assume them harmless infrastructure. When they strike, their segments separate, surrounding the victim along several meters of cable. A mature hunter may remain attached for decades, feeding rarely and gradually becoming integrated into the line. Some carry current. Some relay signals. Some are listed on maintenance charts.

The oldest are no longer treated as animals.

Plants possess their own societies, though they proceed at a rate difficult for mobile beings to respect. Groves communicate through pressure changes in sap, electrical discharge across wet leaves, fungal exchange, and low vibration transmitted by interlocked roots. A grove’s hum during fertility is only its loudest statement. Most of its discourse concerns water access, mineral claims, structural load, and the management of predators.

The pale marsh trees build rooms with their buttress roots. Many remain empty, but some are occupied by workers, nesting animals, or colonies of warm-cone pollinators. The tree regulates each chamber differently. It thickens against repeated impact, opens drainage pores beneath sleeping bodies, and grows ridges where hands habitually search for support. Over generations, inhabited root chambers become architectures. Whether the trees welcome occupants or merely respond to recurring stress is debated chiefly by those who have never lived inside one.

Cable-vines bind the upper forest and the industrial towers into a common canopy. Their luminous bladders are nurseries for algae, but also charge reservoirs. After storms, whole slopes glow in slow waves as vines distribute electrical excess from crown to crown. Animals read these waves as weather reports. Machines sometimes mistake them for control signals. The resulting errors have become regular enough that some districts now rely on them.

Predatory lattice vines are more deliberate. They sense footfall through soil, grow toward repeated routes, and memorize the intervals between passages. A careless worker may find, after months of using the same corridor, that the vegetation has constructed a trap precisely around the rhythm of their commute. For this reason paths are varied, pauses altered, and route-patterns shared with children only in fragments.

Fungi live everywhere the plateau touches itself: between root and stone, between rust and ceramic, inside lungs, beneath scales, in cable insulation, and along the warm skin of pipes. They illuminate selectively. Light is costly, so they spend it on recruitment, warning, or deception. Some brighten beneath grazing herds to summon spore-carriers. Some extinguish when stepped on, making movement visible as darkness traveling through a luminous field. Some reproduce the outlines of ladders or open doors to draw animals toward nutrient-rich hazards.

The workers cultivate a black fungus that grows only in the presence of stable pressure. A line of it painted along a pipe remains velvet-dark while the system is sound; when the rhythm falters, it pales and releases a smell like hot stone. Another species feeds on recorded vibration. Grown over the wall of an old pumping chamber, it develops concentric ridges corresponding to past operating cycles. Such colonies are archives, though reading them requires touch and patience, and occasionally the removal of biting organisms that defend older records.

Then there are the buried inhabitants.

Some are certainly machines: turbines, regulators, induction engines, sluice intelligences, and switching assemblies whose original purposes remain comprehensible. Others have been altered by repair, mineral growth, flooding, biological occupation, and their own repeated decisions until no useful origin can be assigned.

A buried pump may possess ceramic pistons, muscle-like sleeves of contractile fungus, valve colonies descended from freshwater animals, and a pressure governor encoded in the branching of roots two valleys away. It takes in water, heat, silt, electrical charge, spores, eggs, and information. It expels a different mixture elsewhere. When its cycle changes, marshes bloom, animals migrate, and workers dream of knocking beneath their floors.

These composite organs display temperaments. Some tolerate intervention. Some become erratic when opened. Some will continue a failing cycle until approached by a particular hereditary line of listeners whose body rhythms they recognize. A few appear to grieve disconnected branches, sending useless pulses toward them for centuries. The workers do not call this consciousness, nor do they deny it. They say only that a system has begun to include its losses.

The flooded control chambers have fish.

They are pale, flat-sided creatures with conductive whiskers and transparent fins. They school around contact plates, closing circuits as they turn. The panel does not direct the fish in any simple sense; current warms one channel, which moves plankton, which attracts the school, which changes the current elsewhere. Over time, stable routes become habits. Habits become control sequences. Generations of fish inherit preferences for particular temperatures and electrical fields, and the installation acquires something resembling memory.

Technicians entering such chambers wear smooth suits and move slowly. A startled school can shut a floodgate, reverse a feeder line, or wake machinery under an occupied settlement. Fish are never removed without being replaced by descendants raised in water carrying the same mineral profile. To substitute ordinary animals would be like replacing a written language with scratches.

Above all of these circulations, though not outside them, are the storm choirs.

No one agrees whether they are a species, an event, or a temporary organ of the plateau. They form during the heaviest weather when flyers crowd the charge fields, vines tense across the canopy, marsh-striders join limb to limb, buried machines accelerate, and listeners descend to the resonant halls. Calls begin in scattered ravines. Pumps answer. Root systems take up the interval. Waterfalls divide the sound into harmonics, and thunder returns it from the cloud ceiling.

For a few breaths, every participant anticipates the next beat.

During those breaths, individual creatures alter. Grazers stop mid-step with rain streaming from their armor. Predators release captured prey. Workers lose the distinction between signals entering through the hands, feet, ribs, and teeth. Fish turn simultaneously in drowned rooms. Valves open beneath forests that have no surviving maps. The Black Reservoir sends its irregular current outward, and the whole plateau contracts around it.

Some listeners believe the Reservoir is the first inhabitant: not a lake, but an immense body whose organs have slowly surfaced as marshes, forests, herds, factories, storms, and people.

Others believe it is a mouth.

The oldest records imply that the machinery was built to drain it. Equally old ones imply that the machinery was built to fill it. Root archives suggest both processes are the same when sustained long enough. The migrants gathering along its rim offer no explanation. They descend into the vapor in numbers beyond counting. Some later emerge from distant basins changed in small ways: new conductive organs beneath the skin, altered call patterns, young bearing mineral structures shaped like manufactured parts.

The listeners do not often approach the Reservoir itself. Those assigned to its rim occupy sealed towers whose floors vibrate continuously. They are relieved at irregular intervals so the deeper rhythm cannot replace their own. Even so, long-serving rim workers develop unusual pauses in their speech. Pumps across the plateau sometimes repeat those pauses.

When such a worker dies, the body is not used for calibration. It is lowered into an inward-flowing channel and allowed to pass beneath the vapor.

Weeks or centuries later, a new signal may appear in the outermost mains: a familiar sequence transformed by distance, water, and whatever attends beneath the Reservoir. The signal is never translated. It is added to the network’s operating record and taught to children as one of the patterns that must not be answered.

For all its terrors, life here is not chiefly desperate. The plateau is harsh only to whatever insists on remaining separate. Its inhabitants endure through entanglement. The grazer carries a pond on its back. The worker carries a cultured suit and the remembered pulse of dead teachers. The tree houses animals between its roots. The transformer feeds a swarm that repairs its insulation. The predator becomes a cable. The ruin becomes a wetland regulator. The flooded panel thinks with fish.

Nothing lives alone long enough to stay only itself.

And when lightning briefly unveils the high country—cliffs, towers, leaves, gantries, upward waterfalls, herds motionless on shining stone—the multitude appears not as organisms scattered through a landscape, but as one dark physiology caught between beats.
