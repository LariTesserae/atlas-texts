The city’s truest inhabitants are not animals but processes: frost, seepage, wind, roots, and the minute migrations of mineral through stone. They occupy every chamber without ever entering it. Water patrols the streets after rain, disappears into buried conduits, and returns along the lower walls. Ice takes possession in winter, completing roofs and towers the mountain itself never made, then dies violently in spring. Wind lives in the openings year-round, giving each alley and shaft its own voice.

Life survives here by becoming small, slow, and nearly indistinguishable from the place that shelters it.

Moss inhabits the lowest corridors in dense green cushions, holding whole reservoirs between its leaves. Glossy liverworts overlap across shaded floors. Ferns root in drain mouths and tremble above the dark openings as though listening to the water below. Ancient shrubs crouch in the deeper courts, their trunks twisted by decades of cold into shapes no thicker than a wrist. In the central plaza, where a little soil has gathered, brief summer brings sedges, violet bells, pale stars, and red succulent mats—a population of flowers that appears together, lives quickly, and withdraws before the frost can fully claim it.

A pale mountain vole keeps to the mossy foundations. It is seldom seen. Its existence is inferred from clipped stems, narrow passages under the cushions, and the occasional seed husk left in a dry recess. Black flies hatch from the clear basins during warm afternoons, rising in flickering columns that last only until the temperature falls. Ravens and hunting birds pass above, but the city offers them little reason to stay.

The strangest residents live in the wet passages underneath. They are soft, translucent creatures armored in ridged mineral plates drawn from the seepage. Almost colorless, they cling to the stone so closely that they resemble droplets caught in the act of crystallizing. Their movement is measured against condensation: one bead reached, consumed, and left behind; another sensed somewhere across the slab. A journey of a few handspans may occupy a season. When frost comes suddenly, they contract into cracks and enter a stillness so complete that mineral continues to accumulate over them. The oldest are scarcely separate from the walls. Their backs bear layers corresponding to many years of water, drought, and ice.

But something else can be said to live there: the arrangement itself.

It has no body apart from the mountain, yet it maintains boundaries, circulation, and a recognizable plan. Its streets shift without losing their streetness. Courts merge; alleys close; arches emerge grain by grain. Drainage reverses as if redirected. Broken pieces are lifted back toward their former sockets by ice. The central mass opens new galleries while old ones narrow shut. No single stone preserves the design, but the design persists through the replacement and rearrangement of stone.

Its metabolism is weather. It drinks cloud, exhales water through seeps, gathers salts, sheds dust, and rebuilds itself with frost. Thunder passes through it like a pulse. In winter it grows a temporary translucent anatomy; in thaw, that anatomy breaks apart and floods its buried channels. Its changes occur below the scale of attention, but now and then it makes a sound deep enough to be felt in the teeth, and a wall settles by the width of a leaf.

Nothing governs this life. There is no intelligence hidden in the citadel, no vanished builders sleeping beneath the plaza, no purpose awaiting discovery. The city’s semblance of intention exists only at the level of the whole, where countless blind actions have converged upon streets, thresholds, reservoirs, chambers, and returns.

Thus the place is empty only in the ordinary sense. It is densely occupied by creatures that pass for stains, by plants older than their size suggests, by water traveling unseen, by ice rehearsing architecture, and by a geological order that behaves almost like memory.

Its inhabitants do not live *in* the city.

They are the means by which the city continues to exist.
