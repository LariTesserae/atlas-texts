The people of the First Crevice are small, though not in any simple sense. Laid beside a bowl or ladder they seem ordinarily proportioned; seen through the wrong opening, they become minute figures laboring on a mountainside. They have no agreed measure for themselves. A hand must fit where the work requires a hand to fit. A body is whatever remains connected while passing through the chamber.

They are compact and corded from perpetual crouching. Their knees turn easily outward; their toes are long, strong, and nearly as useful as fingers. Lime has thickened their palms into pale, cracked pads, while the rest of their skin is brown-red with clay that cannot be washed away because washing only deposits another layer. Their hair is braided tightly against the scalp and threaded with reeds, wicks, gauges, seeds, and short lengths of cord. Moss often takes root in these braids. It is trimmed, watered, and harvested like any other useful growth.

Clothing is less worn than installed. Strips of waxed fiber are wrapped around the hips and shoulders, then repaired directly upon the body until a garment becomes a fitted scaffold of patches. Aprons contain cups for mortar, loops for tools, and channels that carry sweat toward stoppered gourds. Nothing shed from the body is wasted. Hair reinforces plaster. Nail parings sharpen slips of clay. Blood is mixed into sealant because it remembers warmth.

Their eyes are broad and dark, adapted less to darkness than to obstruction. They rarely see a whole object. Instead they recognize a fingertip, a patch of glaze, a reflected movement in water, and assemble these glimpses into certainty. They can identify one another by the color of steam exhaled through a pipe. Names are seldom spoken. Most names are patterns of touch: three presses to the wrist, a knuckle drawn along the thumb, a palm briefly covering the mouth.

They do speak, but the chamber makes ordinary speech unreliable. Words enter pipes and emerge altered, sometimes days later, sometimes from inside bricks not yet placed. So the builders use clicks, breath, cord-tugs, and mallet sequences. A warning is transmitted through the structure itself. A request for mortar travels as a tremor beneath the floor cups. Grief is announced by slackening one rope among thousands, and everyone knows which one.

No single builder understands the whole chamber. Such understanding would be considered a dangerous vacancy, a way of standing outside the work. Each tends a route, joint, basin, crop, pressure, or unfinished corner. Some cultivate the black-leaved vines and wake them by passing shadows overhead. Some harvest red skins from the climbing water. Some listen at sealed walls for spaces beginning on the other side. The light-bracers follow wandering beams with armfuls of reed. The altitude kneaders work blindfolded so they will not look upward when the bricks lighten in their hands.

Children are not kept apart from construction. They are carried in baskets until they can grip, then sent into passages too narrow for adults. Their first task is usually to hold a knot closed. Their second is to remember why. For years they move inside walls, feeding cords through hidden bends, returning with mineral foam on their faces and reports of rooms nobody else has entered. Some emerge younger than when they went in. Some return with unfamiliar fingerprints pressed into their skin.

A builder is considered grown when the chamber has altered around her so completely that she can no longer reach the place of her birth.

Death is treated as a difficult change of material. The dead are not removed—nothing is removed—but neither are they buried. They are folded into useful postures and plastered gradually into braces, thresholds, cistern seats, and warm sections of wall. Their mouths may become pipe openings. Their ribs support seed trays. A hand is often left exposed so that passing workers can consult its hardened palm for old alignments. In humid weather these hands sometimes soften and resume their last task.

For this reason ancestry is architectural. A builder may say, “My mother carries the eastern gutter,” or, “My grandfather is the third turn of the ladder.” Kinship follows load rather than blood. Those who support the same wall are siblings. Those fed by the same condensation are one household. Disputes are settled by exchanging tasks until each person feels the weight the other had borne.

The oldest worker at the central basin is called the Turn-Mother, though she may not be one person. Beneath her wrappings are layers of earlier hands, bound wrist over wrist around the four cords. Her visible face is smooth with steam and fissured like unfired clay. Tiny ladders have been set along one cheek. At times, figures no taller than mites climb from her collar, repair a crack near her eye, and descend again.

She alone watches the unfinished summit in the dish. The others glimpse it only in reflection. She knows that the small builders upon it repeat their gestures, but not exactly. Sometimes one raises a tool before anyone in the First Crevice has decided to do so. Sometimes a tiny figure vanishes into the miniature dome and, moments later, a newborn is found wedged behind a pipe in the larger chamber, already clutching a wet pellet of clay.

Whether the builders are constructing the mountain or being constructed by it is not a question they ask. To them, making and inhabiting are the same act. A wall is a place that has learned to hold a body; a body is a wall still deciding what it will carry.

What they fear is not collapse. Collapse creates surfaces, gradients, useful pressure, and broken material. They fear openness: an interval with no brace, no duty, no hand approaching from the other side. They fear the bare, the level, the unoccupied. Most of all they fear the open sky, whose emptiness cannot be crushed into mortar.

So when the enormous fingers descend and add fresh clay to the outer world, the builders do not worship them. They recognize them.

Every worker has seen those same creases in her own palms.

Every worker has felt, while pressing mortar into a seam, something infinitesimal press back.
