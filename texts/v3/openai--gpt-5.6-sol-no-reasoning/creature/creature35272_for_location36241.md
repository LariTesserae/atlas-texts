The orchard-keepers say the hollow has no native creatures. To be native is to remain where one begins, and nothing in the Wind Orchard does.

Even the oldest inhabitants arrive continually.

They come as dust and eggs, as sleeping spores lodged in glider sails, as seeds clenched inside the cracks of rolling stones. Some descend from the mineral haze. Others are lifted from plains so distant that their landscapes have no names here. The orchard catches them, tests them, and strips away whatever cannot endure its appetite. Those that survive learn to fasten, fold, inflate, burrow, or move.

The wind trees are the orchard’s great elders, though they are neither rooted nor silent enough to resemble ordinary trees. Each begins as a fist of gray threads gathered in the lee of a stone. The threads knot around grains of basalt and salt, making a porous bulb no wider than a thumb. For years it feeds close to the ground, opening tiny black membranes whenever dust passes. Only after accumulating enough grit does it raise a hollow shoot.

A mature wind tree is a colony braided into the likeness of one organism. Its outer fibers carry tension; its inner fibers digest. Between them run chambers lined with cilia fine as smoke. Air climbs the trunk, shedding what it carries against sticky folds. Useful particles are passed downward. Sand and indigestible fragments collect in nodules until the tree works them loose and expels them with a cough. These are the resonant knots sought by keepers: hard, convoluted masses bored through by the channels of old breath. No two sound alike.

The nodules that contain water are softer and deeper. They form around waxes, salts, and the minute traces of moisture drawn from passing air. Trunk-lizards gnaw toward them. So do certain pale grubs and, in lean seasons, the orchard-keepers. The tree seals each wound by fraying inward, but the scar always whistles. An old tree’s many voices are therefore a record of thirst, parasites, storms, and careful harvests.

The trees seem solitary only because most of their bodies are hidden. Their roots do not spread evenly through the soil. Instead they travel in braided cords between islands of vegetation, sometimes surfacing as gray loops before sinking again. When two trees touch underground, they exchange fibers. A young trunk may belong to several elders; an elder may continue moving through saplings long after its visible crown has collapsed. Keepers disagree over where one tree ends and another begins. They address the orchard by individual names anyway, since courtesy does not require certainty.

The lesser growths live in the trees’ wake.

Rope moss feeds on what the roots discard. It binds stones into nests that preserve a breath of still air inside them. Minute animals graze there: glass-backed mites, salt lice, threadworms that tie themselves into knots during storms. Fan shrubs stand on contractile stems and turn their feeding surfaces toward each new current. Their fan blades are armored on one face and absorbent on the other. When danger sounds, they slap the armored sides upward and pull their roots into a compact hook.

Bladder-vines scarcely touch the ground. Their swollen chambers contain warm gases produced by fermenting dust. In calm weather they sag around trunks and shrubs, extending thorned tendrils into every crevice. When the wind rises, they lighten themselves, tear free, and travel. A single vine may cross the hollow dozens of times before anchoring long enough to fruit. Its waxy pods are the orchard’s chief store of drinkable moisture. Bell-throats pierce them delicately. Keepers twist the necks shut and carry them like stoppered skins. Glider larvae simply crawl inside and consume the pod from within.

The crimson velvet is less clearly plant or animal. It appears as a warm red nap over stone, each hair ending in a bead of sour liquid. Beneath the visible layer lies a folded mass that digests spores, dung, shed skin, and the bodies of insects driven into it. During cold descents it ferments furiously enough to steam. Burrowers sleep under it. Bell-throats bring their young to lie against it. Keepers sometimes tuck jars into deep beds and return hours later to find their food cooked and stained pink.

If exposed to prolonged wind, the velvet withdraws grain by grain into cracks. It may emerge years later with the scent of an earlier season still held in it.

The six-legged gliders are the orchard’s most abundant travelers. Their bodies are narrow and hinged, with two walking pairs, one grasping pair, and broad sails stretched from shoulder to abdomen. Those sails begin colorless. Dust adheres to a film of oil upon them, so each flock carries a blurred map of its passage: violet from iron plains, amber from old grasslands, green from copper country, white from salt. Very old gliders become nearly opaque.

They do not steer well. They survive by accepting collision. Their forelimbs fold around whatever stops them, while their bodies flatten and distribute the blow. A newly arrived flock strikes the crowns like handfuls of thrown leaves. For a moment the branches are crowded with stunned, clinging forms. Then the gliders begin scraping food from their own sails and one another’s.

They lay eggs inside hollow trunks, where the rising breath keeps them warm. This draws the trunk-lizards.

The lizards are lean, flexible creatures with narrow skulls and claws curved to grip the fibrous inner walls. Most never see open ground. They hunt vertically through chambers too tight for a hand, locating prey by vibration. Their scales are covered with backward-pointing hairs that prevent them from being blown out when a tree inhales. When threatened, they jam themselves across the trunk and become living valves. A tree full of hunting lizards develops a stuttering note.

They are born with eyes, but the lids seal within days. Keepers prize the translucent skin shed during this sealing. Placed over the eyes, it softens glare and is said to reveal movement inside the haze. Whether this is true depends on who is selling it.

The eyeless burrowers belong to the bare ground. They are thick-bodied, blunt-snouted, and ringed with muscles that ripple independently, allowing them to brace in loose soil. At dusk they surface wherever pebbles are moving. Their mouths widen around stones far larger than their apparent throats. Inside, ridged chambers scrape away bacterial films, spores, oils, and traces of dung. The cleaned stones are coughed out with enough force to rejoin the wind channels.

Thus the burrowers cultivate the emptiness between living islands. Moss cannot easily fasten to polished stone. Seeds lose their films before they can germinate. What appears barren is an actively grazed territory, maintained by animals beneath it. A well-fed burrower may digest the same pebble hundreds of times over its life.

Bell-throats move across both kinds of ground.

They are slight animals on four long legs, with two smaller grasping limbs folded against the chest. Their hides are dust-colored and sparsely furred. Beneath each jaw hangs a pair of resonant sacs connected to chambers in the breastbone. A bell-throat does not call from the mouth. It draws air through slits along the neck and drums the sacs with internal tendons, producing notes that carry beneath the orchard’s harsher music.

Every herd maintains a chord rather than a leader. Calves begin with high, uncertain knocks and gradually settle into an open place in the collective sound. As individuals move, the rhythm tells the others how far away they are, whether they are feeding, and which direction their bodies face. In dense gloom, a herd knows its own shape by hearing it.

The predators know this.

They are seldom seen whole. Keepers call them hollow-jaws, though some insist there are several species hiding under one name. Their bodies lie low, with broad gripping feet and skin hung in loose flaps that do not snap in the wind. The throat contains paired bones capable of striking together. By altering the shape of the mouth and chest, a hollow-jaw imitates a missing herd member.

The imitation is nearly exact. What betrays it is not pitch but relationship. The false note answers too quickly, or remains unchanged while moving, or fills a place in the chord already occupied. Bell-throats recognize the impossibility before they recognize the predator. Their chorus breaks. They run in every direction, not away from the sound but away from the arrangement that allowed it.

The rest of the orchard reacts because panic is useful wind.

Gliders launch. Shrubs flatten. Vines release their hooks. Trunk-lizards wedge themselves tight. Trees reverse membranes to spill air from their crowns. Burrowers open new holes under fleeing feet. A hollow-jaw may catch one bell-throat, but the disturbance carries seeds, spores, dung, eggs, and root fragments across the basin. Predation rearranges the orchard more thoroughly than weather alone.

Among the keepers there are people adapted to hear these arrangements. They are called chord-minders, though they command nothing. A chord-minder learns each herd’s intervals, each old tree’s cluster of notes, the knocking of salt antlers, and the particular stutter made by glider nests. They can detect a cracked shelter rope by the change it makes in the wind. They can sometimes hear a hollow-jaw before the bell-throats do.

They wear layered masks of woven fiber and glider membrane. The outer layers catch grit; the inner layers are scented with crushed velvet to keep the nose from drying. Small bone whistles are stitched near the ears, not to make sound but to break dangerous resonances. Every garment closes tightly at wrist, ankle, and throat. Hair is braided against the scalp. Infants are carried in lidded baskets tethered to two adults at once.

No keeper travels alone across the basin. Bands move on shared lines clipped to bronze hooks, each person leaving enough slack to fall but not enough to be taken. They speak close to one another’s masks. Full names are reserved for shelters, caves, and the sheltered side of a tree. In open ground, a person uses a shortened name or a hand signal.

This is partly caution and partly law. Spoken names are thought to give the wind a shape it can repeat.

The dead are not buried. Soil is too thin, and a grave would not remain a grave. Bodies are wrapped in shed tree membrane and fastened high in a crown chosen by the chord-minder. Over days or months, the wrapping tears. What can be carried is carried. What catches in the tree enters its filters. Hair braids reappear in glider nests. Bone fragments polish in the ground channels. Teeth are sometimes found inside resonant knots years later.

When such a knot is recovered, it is sounded once beside the northern cliff. If its tone resembles the dead person’s voice, it is given their name and passed through the family. If it does not, no one calls the resemblance a failure. The orchard may have taken a different part.

Those lifted alive occupy a more difficult category.

A person dropped quickly from the canopy is considered returned, though they are kept silent until sunset. A person passed from tree to tree is considered claimed for as long as the crowns hold them. To pull them down during that time would be to steal from the orchard and risk loosening whatever else it has fastened. Families therefore follow beneath when they can, clipping their lines from root to root, calling only with the person’s childhood rhythm tapped on stone.

Some claimed people come down days later. They are dehydrated, scratched, and packed with dust. Many cannot bear enclosed rooms afterward. Some develop a habit of turning their faces toward currents no one else feels. A few can imitate the trees’ notes with disturbing accuracy.

Others do not return.

During evening ascent, their relatives may hear them in the trunks: a cough within a horn tone, a syllable stretched through several crowns, a familiar laugh broken into wooden pulses. Chord-minders listen but offer no verdict. The orchard produces coincidences as abundantly as spores, and grief is among the things it catches.

The keepers themselves are visitors, but repeated visiting has altered them. Their camps are temporary; their obligations are not. Each band inherits particular trees to tend, though tending mostly means restraint. They clear choking dust from apertures, cut dead fibers before they tear living ones, move fallen stone away from young root knots, and harvest only what the orchard has already loosened.

Their bronze hooks are named because bronze does not arise on the mountain. Each hook has crossed generations and lowlands, been traded, stolen, recovered, reforged, and worn thin. A lost hook is mourned like an ancestor. If the wind takes one, no keeper reaches blindly after it. They watch where it goes. Should it lodge harmlessly, it may be reclaimed. Should a vine, root, nest, or tree close around it, its name is added to the orchard’s keeping.

At the turning of the light, all distinctions become uncertain.

The gliders crowd the crowns until the trees wear wings. Bell-throat herds dissolve into moving sound. Roots lift from the soil and search for purchase. Velvet warms beneath stones. Burrowers heave through the bare lanes. Hollow-jaws call in stolen voices. Keepers flatten themselves behind hooked lines and count one another by touch.

Above them, unseen currents collide.

For a moment every organism is fastened—to rock, to root, to branch, to herd, to rope, to another body. Then the hollow draws breath.

Anything that fails to hold becomes food, traveler, message, or song.

Anything that holds does so only until the wind changes.
