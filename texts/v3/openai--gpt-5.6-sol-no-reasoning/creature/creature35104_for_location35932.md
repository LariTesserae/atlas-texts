The valley is inhabited less by a population than by a collaboration.

Its largest creature is the basin itself: stone, vegetation, buried channels, exposed machines, and the disciplined migration of dew all functioning as organs of one dispersed body. The cliffs gather light. The moss holds moisture. The transmission lines clear their own narrow nerves through the growth. Tanks and cisterns serve as empty-looking glands, while conduits beneath the roots carry pressures too slow and slight to be mistaken for a pulse. What lives here does not occupy the industrial system. It has entered into it.

The dominant organisms are patient, low-growing things. Mosses lie in generations, the living green raised on deep, cold archives of their own dead. They bridge cracks, soften housings, dam gutters, and retain every bead of condensation they can. Ferns crowd the sheltered cuts. Black-leaved shrubs turn their waxed surfaces toward the night rather than the sun. Great rosettes cup the air. Heather walls shelter pockets of stillness in which frost can survive until afternoon.

Some plants have become inseparable from the machinery. Copperleaf threads hair-fine roots beneath insulation, feeding on warmth, trace metals, or current itself. Blue crusts bloom on charged panels and contract when the power fades, as though sleeping. The glassy reeds around grounding rods are not quite transparent; light caught in their stems descends in small, delayed flashes. Their roots form pale knots around buried cables without piercing them. They may protect the system, feed from it, or regulate it in ways the machinery’s makers never intended.

Even the ordinary plants have acquired peculiar habits. Flowers open when concealed valves turn. Red moss darkens above mineral leaks. Shrubs along dead conduits grow pale and brittle, maintaining rows whose cause lies underground. Seeds that land on the creeping conveyors are transported by centimeters and meters, sometimes taking years to reach another terrace. Thus the machinery performs pollination and dispersal with the solemn inefficiency of an aging god.

The animals are few because abundance would be disruptive.

The pale grazer is the most visible, though it is rarely seen twice in the same season. It stands high at the shoulder and seems assembled from reed-thin legs, a narrow chest, and a long, level back. Its hide is not white but the color of dry lichen, faintly mottled with gray-green patches that make it vanish beside tanks and weathered concrete. Its feet divide into small, flexible hooves capable of crossing root mats without breaking them. It browses selectively on leaves stained by seepage from the pipes, drawing salts and metals that would poison animals from elsewhere.

Along its ribs, beneath the skin, run dark branching marks. In bright weather these look like veins; at dawn they resemble hairline cracks in porcelain. Dew gathers upon them more readily than on the rest of its body. The animal wakes jeweled, stands motionless while the droplets migrate toward its spine, and lowers its head only after they have gone. It drinks almost never.

Whether there is only one is uncertain. No herd has been observed, no young found, no carcass discovered. Its hoofprints often end on bare rock, but occasionally a second set begins beyond them—smaller, or perhaps merely older.

The black birds belong to the pylons. Each pair claims a tower and builds high among the ceramic insulators, weaving nests from wire filaments, grass roots, and strips of translucent plant skin. They are long-winged but reluctant to fly. Most of the day they remain facing inward toward the basin, changing position only when a relay closes or a cable draws tight. Their feathers carry a dull metallic sheen, and in electrical weather they lift around the birds’ bodies like black petals.

They seldom call because sound is not their chief means of communication. One bird inclines its head; another, kilometers away, answers. At night they tap the pylons with their beaks. The taps travel through the network farther than any cry could carry, and other pairs reply in sequences separated by long intervals. Some sequences repeat from year to year.

Their eggs are usually laid singly. The shells are blue-gray and finely speckled, nearly indistinguishable from the stone below. When abandoned, they do not decay. Empty shells have been found caught in old machinery, polished by condensation until they look ceramic.

The shaded pools contain the valley’s softest inhabitants. They are translucent and almost without pigment, with broad heads, slight limbs, and branching external gills. Some have the blunt shape of salamanders; others taper continuously, as though development stopped before deciding between limb and fin. Their organs show faintly through their skin. A dark heart contracts once every few minutes.

They feed on films too fine to see and on pale threads that descend from cracks beneath the water. They may remain still for hours, but when distant machinery moves, all of them turn in the same direction. During the valley’s collective brightening, they rise beneath the surface and arrange themselves into loose rings, heads inward, tails toward the surrounding stone. The water above them stays perfectly flat.

Beetles sleep on the conveyors. They are small, dark, and smooth-backed, with seams so fine that a resting beetle resembles a drop of hardened oil. They wake when a belt advances, walk briefly against its motion, then fold themselves down again. A single journey across the factory floor may occupy several generations. Their larvae live in moss packed around warm bearings, eating rust-colored fungi. Though the valley has no audible insect life, these beetles may be more numerous than anything with a spine.

There are likely other animals that leave no conventional trace. Maintenance doors bear faint parallel polish at knee height. Condensation vanishes from certain rails before dawn. Fern thickets occasionally settle in a line, not as if something has pushed through them, but as though their stems have briefly leaned aside. The straight bare corridors beneath the transmission lines are never crossed by hoofprints, yet marks appear on both sides.

The machines also qualify as inhabitants.

They do not think quickly, if they think at all. Their awareness, if that word applies, is distributed through strain gauges, floats, thermal contacts, corroded relays, mineral diaphragms, and roots grown into switch housings. A pressure accumulated over three winters releases a latch. A latch tensions a cable. The cable turns a wheel. The wheel opens a buried passage through which a cupful of water rises against gravity. No component contains the purpose of the act.

Some mechanisms remain clean because they move, though the movement is too slow to witness. Others are cleaned by leaves that sweep them nightly, by blue crusts that consume corrosion, or by the upward-traveling dew. The system tends itself through processes that resemble weather more than maintenance.

Within the windowless cube there may be the closest thing the valley possesses to a heart. It does not beat. At noon it tightens. The low tone felt in the teeth passes through soil, foundations, water pockets, and the hollow bones of the pylon birds. Flowers on the cube’s sunlit wall close during the tone, while those in shadow open. The pale grazer, if present, turns its body broadside to the building. Underground, many tiny conditions change at once.

No person appears to live here now, but human residence has not entirely ended.

Lichen occupies the dormitory beds in body-shaped fields. It is thickest at the pillows and follows the folds of vanished blankets. On cold nights, condensation forms on the inside of the windows in several oval clearings, as if breath still reaches the glass from rooms that have been empty for decades. Cupboards contain no food, only seeds sorted into rusted tins. A pair of boots remains beside one bed, filled to the ankles with moss that greens earlier than the moss outdoors.

The valley may remember its workers only as patterns of heat, salt, pressure, and habit. Routes once taken daily have become narrow botanical boundaries. Handrails retain mineral traces that attract certain vines. Doors designed to open for bodies still unlock at long intervals, reveal darkness, and close again. The system continues to make allowances for operators who do not arrive.

Or perhaps its true operators were never human.

The collector vanes open at night like flowers because flower and mechanism have arrived at the same solution. Dew climbs because surface tension, electrical charge, plant tissue, and buried machinery all pull together. Birds signal through pylons because the pylons already span the basin. Beetles travel the belts because the belts are the valley’s slow roads. The grazer harvests mineral leaves and carries what it has gathered across sectors, leaving almost nothing behind except a few hairs snagged on metal edges. Each inhabitant performs a minute transfer.

Taken together, they appear to refine something that cannot be piled in warehouses.

They sort water according to where it condensed. They separate light by the surface that reflected it. They gather cold from shadowed clefts, altitude from exposed ridges, electrical tension from the empty air. Living tissue makes distinctions the old machines cannot; the machines preserve quantities too slight and slow for flesh to retain. The valley’s inhabitants are therefore not workers around a factory. They are stages within its process.

During the rare alignment, every wet surface turns toward the same vacant point overhead. The glassy reeds bow. The pylon birds spread their wings without taking flight. The pool creatures rise. The grazer, wherever it is, becomes motionless. For a few minutes, all the separate lives of the basin assume a single orientation.

Something is offered upward, or something is drawn down.

Afterward, the animals resume their sparse routines. Moss closes over another millimeter of steel. A beetle wakes on a belt newly advanced by one tooth. In a black pool, a translucent heart contracts. High above, the birds tap a message into the pylons.

And beneath them all, the valley continues its immense, almost motionless metabolism: alive not in spite of abandonment, but in the form abandonment has allowed it to become.
