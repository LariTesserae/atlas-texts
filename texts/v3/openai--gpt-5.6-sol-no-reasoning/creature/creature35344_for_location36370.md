The inhabitants of the low plain are not animals in any familiar hierarchy. The forest itself is the principal life-form: perhaps one organism expressed as many species, perhaps an ecology whose members have surrendered their independence so completely that the distinction no longer matters.

Its copper-fiber trees, glassy fronds, silver mats, and graphite cushions do not compete for water or soil. They partition reality. Each growth occupies a task as terrestrial plants might occupy niches. One species regulates inertia; another keeps events from touching their consequences too closely. Tall trees fasten distance in place. Mineral blossoms manage asymmetry. The overlapping canopy appears to gather sunlight, but light is only its most visible resource. It also feeds on gradients, unrealized possibilities, and the slight pressure of things that could happen but have not.

The forest does not think in moments. Its thoughts are spatial: a branch fork, a sequence of translucent leaves, the angles between three groves. What looks like landscape may be a calculation held open across kilometers. Its reproductive grains are not merely seeds but instructions for extending that calculation. Since time scarcely proceeds, germination and maturity coexist as positions within a solved pattern. Nothing needs to grow from one into the other. The forest contains both, and the relationship between them performs the work of growth.

The small many-legged creatures beneath the fans may be its custodians—or mobile organs. Their dark, folded bodies resemble scraps of foil because they require little volume of their own. They emerge when a local relation needs correction: a shadow has acquired an extra vein, two causes have approached too closely, or a crystal edge has begun to remember erosion. They do not eat. They carry discrepancies into their folded interiors, where the errors cease to have usable dimensions.

The great pale forms above the canopy are harder to classify. They may be animals only in the sense that weather can resemble an animal. No wings bear them, and they displace neither air nor light. The leaves turn as they pass because the forest perceives them from below, regardless of where they appear to an observer. They could be immense creatures moving through a neighboring geometry, with only their pressure crossing the plain. They could also be the forest’s slowest thoughts.

The upright prisms are its most intimate inhabitants. Three-legged and mirror-faced, they seem less like wildlife than instruments by which the place notices attention. Their mirrors omit the observer because they do not reflect bodies; they reflect membership in the local arrangement. A traveler has not yet been incorporated and therefore possesses no image. When threatened, a prism does not move. It revises the relation called “near,” leaving its former position behind as a plausible mistake.

The empty root cavities suggest another resident, one never encountered because it may live in absent intervals. They are too clean for dens and too recent for fossils. Something fits there without occupying them, resting between one configuration of the forest and the next. The premature footstep near certain groves may belong to it. So may the diverging shadows. Perhaps these are separate creatures; perhaps shadow, footstep, and hollow are the only organs by which a single inhabitant can enter ordinary sequence.

Above all of them is the intelligence—or instinct—of the whole forest. It is neither benevolent nor predatory. It maintains. Its prohibitions are not laws imposed on visitors but immune responses expressed as geography. Repeated passage teaches an interval to close. Broken symmetry forces compensation elsewhere. Water provokes the strongest reaction because water carries a history the plain excludes: erosion, weather, decay, circulation, and time made visible through change.

For one instant, every droplet introduces the possibility of a different world. The crowns bend not under actual rain, but under the remembered consequences of rain: channels in the black plates, rounded ridges, softened crystals, growth followed by rot. The forest tears the water apart because it is defending more than its chemistry. It is defending its perfect, arrested solution.

Whatever engineered this place—if anything did—may no longer exist apart from it. Its machines have speciated. Its custodians have become fauna. Its architecture has taken root. Its operating system is now an ecosystem, and its oldest command is expressed in every sharp plate, unworn crystal, suspended seed, and motionless leaf:

Remain complete.
