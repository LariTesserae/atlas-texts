What lives there is difficult to separate from what merely occurs.

The translucent films in the condensation wells may be the basin’s simplest inhabitants. They cling to the precise boundary between water and stone, clear as shed skin and almost as thin. During the deep pressure stroke, each film draws itself into a row of identical beads. When the vibration passes, the beads loosen and merge, restoring an unbroken membrane. There is no visible feeding, division, or decay. A film removed from its hollow loses cohesion; left untouched, it may persist indefinitely, exchanging dissolved metals with the water so evenly that neither grows richer. It behaves less like a colony than an interface the basin has learned to maintain.

The pale crack-creatures are more recognizably alive, though only slightly. They are soft, eyeless, and nearly colorless, with flattened bodies that adhere to warm rock by dozens of fine muscular folds. Their internal fluids darken briefly when electrical pulses move through the mineral seams. Then they contract, not in alarm but in synchrony, as though the subterranean cycle were their heartbeat.

They have no obvious mouths. Condensation passes across their skins and leaves behind faint metallic traces. Perhaps they absorb heat, ions, or electrical gradients; perhaps they are only temporary shapes assumed by the membrane when pressure forces it into motion. They emerge at fixed points in the cycle, remain exposed for the same interval, and withdraw just before the sharp concussion that shakes dust from the overhangs. No young have been seen. No dead have been found.

Farther out lives—or operates—the larger thing.

It is tall only when crossing open ground. Between the pillars it can lower itself until it resembles a dark arrangement of rods laid across the silt. Its body is composed of many hinged lengths, unevenly paired, with no stable distinction between legs and torso. Surfaces that first appear black prove to be translucent, filled with slow-moving mineral suspensions. Warm water sometimes drips from its joints. Pale current passes through it at each underground discharge, briefly outlining an intricate internal lattice.

It leaves no tracks because its feet do not quite press into the ground. Each narrow extremity spreads its load through a tremor matched to the vibration rising from below. Silt loosens before contact and settles afterward in its former arrangement. The effect is not graceful. It is exact.

The walker travels from outcrop to outcrop, pausing at limestone shelves, silica pillars, fissure fields, and sinkholes. It touches the stone with delicate branching appendages and remains motionless through several machinery cycles. It may be sensing strain, correcting resonance, feeding on electrical potential, or simply completing a route imposed by its anatomy. Occasionally it removes a loose fragment from a rubble apron and carries it elsewhere. Much later, another passage may return the fragment to its original slope.

There may be only one walker. There may be many, each following the same circuit so perfectly that they cannot be distinguished. They are never seen together.

Nothing here hunts. Nothing flees except by schedule. There are no nests, burrows, bones, droppings, shells, or territorial marks. The living things do not form an ecology in the usual sense; they occupy tolerances within the basin’s operation. Their bodies are tuned to its pressures, chemistry, and intervals. They may be descendants of organisms that adapted until dependence became indistinguishable from incorporation. Or they may be products—not manufactured objects, but recurrent forms precipitated by the working landscape, as crystals are precipitated from brine.

If the basin itself is alive, it possesses no single body. Its circulation is aquifer and steam. Its nerves are conductive seams. Its breath is condensation. The films are its sensitive surfaces, the pale animals its mobile pulses, and the many-jointed walker perhaps an adjustment moving slowly across its own enormous skin.

None of them appear to want anything.

They persist, withdraw, cross, and return.

They live there in the same way the basin operates: without growth, waste, history, or visible end.
