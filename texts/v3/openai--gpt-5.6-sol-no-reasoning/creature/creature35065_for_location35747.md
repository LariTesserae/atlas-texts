What lives there does not inhabit the city so much as participate in its disagreements.

The white film is the most abundant. It gathers in sheltered cuts and along cracks where two conditions meet without resolving: warm stone against cold air, downward weight against sideways weight, a near wall against the same wall seen impossibly far away. From a distance it resembles lichen, salt, or the residue of some vanished sea. Closer inspection shows no granules or tissue. It is made of branching boundaries, each finer than the last, folded over one another until contradiction acquires thickness.

It grows where the city cannot decide.

The film does not consume stone. It consumes difference, smoothing some incompatibilities while deepening others. A ledge coated in it may become safe to stand on, but only from one direction. A crack may seal visibly while remaining open to the hand. Over centuries its colonies form pale deltas and leafless forests across sheltered façades. Their margins advance one crystal-width whenever gravity hesitates.

When disturbed, a colony may recoil without moving. Its whiteness withdraws first, then its texture, then the fact that anything had covered the wall. The exposed stone is often altered afterward. Corners lose an adjoining plane. Niches become infinitely shallow. A person leaning against such a surface may find there is no longer a side from which to step away.

The hollow rings feed among these growths.

They are hand-sized articulated loops of clear or smoky mineral, usually composed of seven to twelve segments, though counting them is unwise. No head distinguishes itself. No limb reaches outward. They travel by tipping their bodies forward and rolling along the undersides of bridges, vertical plazas, and walls where loose dust has decided to settle. At a turn, the ring opens slightly, passes the corner through its own center, and closes on the next surface.

Their interiors are not empty in the ordinary way. Looking through one does not reveal what lies beyond it, but some nearby place aligned incorrectly: the top of a stairwell, the back of an arch, a patch of sky beneath the city. At times a ring contains darkness crowded with pale moving marks. At others it frames the observer from behind.

They graze by flattening themselves over the white film. A faint darkening passes around their circumference, segment to segment. When they lift away, the colony may be stripped clean, doubled in area, or transferred to a wall several streets distant. Their tracks are narrow paired grooves in the dust. Often these tracks appear before the creature arrives, and the ring follows them with the obedience of something remembering rather than choosing.

During quiet periods they cluster in recesses, nested one inside another without touching. A dozen may occupy the same apparent space. When the sky-radiance passes, they become nearly invisible except for pale rims and the distorted architecture seen through their centers.

They are preyed upon, perhaps, by things that cannot be seen directly.

These larger presences move through the open gulf along invisible edges. Against the sky they appear as angular, many-jointed lengths, blacker than the dim atmosphere and too narrow for their apparent scale. Their bends do not flex. Instead, one angle disappears and another comes into existence farther along, so that the whole shape progresses by revising its outline.

They have been called striders, although they touch nothing, and surveyors, although they show no interest in what they reveal.

Where one passes near a tower, the tower briefly develops an inside. Blind windows open onto galleries. Door-shaped stains deepen into chambers. Rows of pillars retreat into enormous halls that cannot fit within the column. These interiors are always empty, but not unused. Floors show worn routes. Alcoves hold depressions shaped for objects never present. Some walls bear bands of polished stone at the height of a hand belonging to no known visitor.

When the drifting shape moves on, the rooms lose depth. Anyone caught within is usually returned to the outer surface, though not always to the same district. Occasionally a voice continues from behind the sealed façade for several days. It grows neither weaker nor more distant. It simply repeats the last few words spoken before closure, with small variations in emphasis, until the next passage of the sky-light erases it.

The striders may produce the polygonal shells found on exposed terraces. These delicate cups occur in fields of thousands, all differently sided and all open toward whatever direction is locally upward. They are lighter than dust but cannot be lifted. By the following dim interval, each shell has become a matching cavity in the stone.

Something may hatch from them in reverse. Nothing has ever been seen to emerge.

There are also the listeners.

Evidence of them is more common than sightings. In narrow avenues, the scrape of a footstep sometimes returns before the foot descends. A pulse can be felt in a parapet, matched to no heart in the vicinity. Dust gathers in shallow paired hollows as though around invisible ankles. These signs follow travelers for hours, always one corner behind or on the opposite face of the same wall.

A listener becomes perceptible only when silence passes through the city. As ordinary sound is removed—the windless hiss of powder, the pressure-clicks of flexing stone, the body’s internal murmur—a low figure condenses at the edge of vision. It resembles a person made from the absence of background detail. Through it, the city cannot be seen.

It has no stable height. Standing beside a child, it is small. Following a solitary adult, it may be tall enough to incline beneath arches. It imitates posture but not motion: when the traveler walks, it stands; when the traveler stops, it appears one position closer in the attitude the traveler has just abandoned.

Listeners do not attack. They accumulate habits.

A frequently repeated gesture begins occurring without its owner. A hand reaches for a railing before the traveler decides to do so. A sleeper wakes to find their bedding folded in the manner they intended for morning. Eventually the city takes up these repetitions. A stair acquires the exact cadence of a familiar climb. An alcove forms to fit a habitual posture. Footprints weather into stone along a preferred route.

At that point the listener is no longer seen. The person’s habit has become infrastructure.

This may be how the place acquires inhabitants without permitting anyone to remain.

Those who stay too long leave behind less direct residues. A remembered room begins as a proportional error in an open square. One wall seems nearer than it is. An overhang takes on the angle of a former ceiling. With continued recollection, corners appear; then a doorway; then surfaces bearing the stains and abrasions of long use. The resulting chamber is never exact. It includes details forgotten by the person remembering it and omits objects they considered essential.

Sometimes a figure is already living there.

Such figures are called tenants, though they neither enter nor depart. They resemble people viewed through thick mineral: posture and clothing reduced to broad, worn forms, faces smoothed as if by ages of wind. Each performs one limited action associated with the room. One sits at a stone table and turns toward an absent door. Another kneels beside a bed-shaped ledge. Another crosses from one wall to the other carrying a weight that has not been reproduced.

They do not acknowledge visitors unless addressed by a name spoken repeatedly in that place. Then an inscription begins to erode into the nearest surface, and the tenant alters its routine. It may pause. It may face the speaker. It may perform an additional motion never remembered.

The danger lies not in what the tenant does, but in the room becoming more certain than the person who recalled it. Once the chamber has accumulated enough wear, it may claim the memory as its own. The visitor can still describe the room afterward, but only as somewhere ancient that they once discovered in the city. They no longer know why it felt familiar.

Among the high spires live the thinnest creatures.

They appear as threads stretched between points that should be too distant to connect. In ordinary dimness they are indistinguishable from cracks in the sky. During the passage of the broad radiance, thousands become visible, trembling in parallel above the roofs. Small knots travel along them from tower to tower.

The threads feed on distance. Where they gather, remote districts seem close enough to touch, while adjacent parapets recede across miles. A mature thread may tighten suddenly and bring its anchor points together. Towers lean; a bridge buckles; two plazas separated by leagues share an edge for a few moments. The traveling knots then pass from one quarter into another and disperse.

If a thread breaks, distance spills back into the area. This is experienced as a silent outward blow. Companions standing an arm’s length apart may find themselves on opposite roofs. A single staircase can elongate into a journey of days. The broken ends curl upward and are lost against the sky, where they may be the long angular silhouettes mistaken for striders—or a larval form of them, or merely the same phenomenon seen under a different law.

No taxonomy survives the city for long.

The white film may be a mineral deposit until it migrates. The rings may be animals until one is found serving as the worn threshold of an ancient doorway. The listeners may be echoes, parasites, or the city’s method of learning how a body occupies space. Even the tenants might not be creatures at all. They may be rooms remembering the people who remembered them.

During the Revision, these distinctions collapse first.

The rings flee in every direction that can be called uphill, lengthening into spirals when no such direction remains. White colonies bloom over open air, tracing surfaces that are not yet present. Listeners become briefly solid and assume postures their travelers have not performed. Tenants step beyond their rooms, weathered smooth by lives they never lived. The threads above the spires sag under the weight of shortened distances.

And in the gulf, something immense sometimes becomes apparent—not as a body, but as a coordination.

Far-separated towers incline together. Dust veils turn at the same instant. Bridges flex in sequence across hundreds of leagues, like a ripple passing through ribs. For one moment the city’s countless contradictory gravities point toward a common moving center.

Nothing can be seen there.

Yet the hollow creatures flatten themselves. The striders halt on their invisible edges. Every newly formed doorway faces the gulf. Even the white film withdraws into hairline seams.

Then the alignment passes. The city settles into a different antiquity, and whatever seemed to move beneath or among it is gone—or has become indistinguishable from the place itself.

That may be the only true inhabitant: not a creature concealed in the abyss, nor a mind distributed through the towers, but a temporary agreement among all the city’s errors. A life assembled whenever enough incompatible laws happen, briefly, to desire the same thing.
