The people of the Unfinished Heights call themselves, when a name can survive the roar, the **Afterhearing**.

This does not mean they hear what comes after. It means that their hearing does.

A spoken word reaches them twice: first as the damaged pressure of the voice, then days or years later as a change in stone. Because of this, they seldom trust the first arrival of anything—not a sound, not a stranger, not even their own intentions. They wait to learn what an act becomes before deciding what it was.

They are human in the broad arrangement. They have two arms, two legs, upright bodies, dexterous hands. But the Heights have revised them unevenly.

Most are tall and spare, with narrow chests and heavy pelvises adapted less for climbing than for remaining attached to uncertain ground. Their feet are broad and divided by deep flexible seams. When they stand still, the soles slowly conform to the rock, gripping minute ridges that may not yet be visible. Travelers lift each foot deliberately. A careless sleeper can wake with one heel partially incorporated into a new terrace.

Their skin has the muted colors of the country: pumice-gray, bruised green, brown-black, or the pale violet of certain mineral fans. It is crossed by fine concentric lines, most densely around the ribs, throat, and skull. These are not wrinkles. They are growth rings left by powerful sounds. A person who has lived beside an ascending escarpment bears broad, widely spaced bands. One raised near a fault mouth may be patterned so finely that their skin resembles dark wood.

Their ears are small and sealed beneath folds of cartilage. Ordinary hearing would be useless here and quickly destroyed. Instead, they receive sound through the body. The jaw, sternum, spine, and hollow chambers of the pelvis conduct the mountain roar inward, where it is separated into layers.

An Afterhearing person listening intently does not turn their head. They crouch, bare one palm, and press their teeth together.

They can distinguish the slow opening of a ravine from the raising of a cliff. They recognize the thin prospective tone of land not yet present. They hear hollows inside apparently solid rock, including the strange lower spaces reached by ladders. The eldest can identify particular huts from several valleys away by the way those huts interrupt the thunder.

This bodily hearing costs them silence. Even inside hushreed-lined pits, their bones continue to murmur. Infants must be wrapped tightly in dead fan-sheets until they learn which vibrations belong to the world and which belong to themselves. Without this swaddling, they may answer the mountains’ movements with movements of their own: a hand opening whenever a crack widens, the spine arching with an uplift, the skull gradually flattening in sympathy with a distant basin.

They speak, but speech is intimate and economical. Foreheads are placed together. Fingers rest on the speaker’s throat. Many meanings are tapped directly against the wrist, jaw, or breastbone. A complicated conversation resembles two people checking one another for fractures.

Names are short pulses rather than sounds. They cannot be shouted. To give one’s name, one must permit touch.

No permanent name is given at birth. Children are called by temporary forms—Third Warmth, Reed-Bent, Sleeps-Through-Deep-Tone—until the terrain answers them. At some point, usually before adulthood, an action of theirs will coincide with a lasting change in the Heights. A ledge may appear while a child hammers a peg. A new black frond may open when they strike a bowl. An echo may return bearing the rhythm of their footsteps before they have walked the path. That event becomes the foundation of the adult name.

One person might be named **She-Who-Was-Answered-by-the-North-Face**. Another: **Three-Knocks-Before-the-Pass**.

Names can later prove mistaken. If the ledge vanishes, or if the answering echo is discovered to precede another traveler, the name is surrendered. Such people are not considered diminished. They are said to have become accurate.

### Their settlements

The apparent isolation of the huts is deceptive. The Afterhearing do not define a settlement by physical proximity. A village is a group of dwellings sharing an echo.

Ten huts scattered across hundreds of leagues may answer the same low pulse and therefore be one community. Meanwhile, two neighboring structures can belong to different settlements because the mountain places them in separate acoustic basins.

Members visit by routes that make little visual sense. They walk past a nearby hut to reach a distant wall, press through a narrow crack, descend three rungs into solid stone, and emerge beside the hut they ignored. Such routes do not remain dependable. Each is remembered as a sequence of tones, temperatures, and bodily pressures rather than turns.

A traveler’s map is a cord knotted at intervals. The spacing of knots records echo delays. Different fibers indicate the part of the body in which a tone should be felt: sternum, teeth, left knee, right palm. If the expected vibration arrives in the wrong bone, the traveler stops. The path ahead may not yet exist, or may have become a route to somewhere else.

Their huts are not regarded as finished buildings. A builder makes only the first room and leaves deliberate seams in the walls. The Heights may eventually add chambers, alcoves, false doors, stairways, or whole histories of occupation. Such additions are treated cautiously but not rejected. A newly appeared room is left untouched until the next listening interval. If the knock beneath the mountain seems to come from inside it, the doorway is sealed.

If not, the room is swept, measured, and accepted.

Objects occasionally appear with these rooms: worn bowls no one remembers carving, reed cloaks sized for absent bodies, sleeping hollows still warm, or tools made from materials the inhabitants do not possess. The Afterhearing call these **inheritances from no ancestor**. They use some of them. Others they hang outside where the roar can wear their implications away.

The smoke holes have a ceremonial purpose. Smoke does not pass through them; possibility does. When someone dies, their household places a pinch of powdered body-stone beneath the opening. If the deceased might have lived elsewhere or otherwise, the smoke hole is said to admit those unlived versions long enough to take leave.

### Birth and childhood

Pregnancy is difficult to identify at first because an unborn child does not grow continuously. It arrives in increments aligned with the deepening roar. For months there may be no visible change. Then, during a listening interval, the mother’s body acquires the next stage all at once.

Midwives are therefore listeners rather than healers. They keep one hand on the pregnant person and one on the floor, judging whether the approaching change belongs to the child, the parent, or the land beneath them.

Children are born soft-boned. Their skeletons harden by exposure to selected tones. A newborn is carried from garden to garden: first among hushreeds for the ribs, then beside silver glass-mats for the hands, then near a low steady cliff-tone for the skull. Too much resonance makes bone brittle and overdefined. Too little leaves it uncertain. Some children retain a finger that occasionally has no joints, or a shoulder whose shape varies with distance.

Adolescence begins when the person first hears an echo that has not yet been made.

They are then taught prospective listening: the dangerous art of distinguishing warning from temptation. The Heights produce both. A path may call with the sound of one’s future footsteps because it will become useful, or because the land requires those footsteps in order to exist.

The Afterhearing disagree about whether there is a difference.

### Food

They do not eat the vegetation as animals would eat plants. Much of it has no nutritive substance, and chewing living mineral growth can cause the teeth to adopt its structure.

Instead, they harvest stored vibration.

Mature hushreed bulbs contain a compact chalky mass called **thundermeal**. Gatherers cut the surrounding tubes, wait for the bulb to cease trembling, then grind it in basalt bowls. Mixed with powdered scales and warmed between flat stones, it forms dense gray cakes. These have almost no flavor, but they vibrate faintly against the tongue. Different mountain tones produce different nourishment.

Low cliff-roar strengthens muscle and bone. The sharp frequencies gathered near young cracks sustain alertness. Long valley tones induce sleep. Food from a place where echoes arrive early can produce memories of meals not yet eaten, so it is reserved for expeditions.

Black fan-plants provide thin blades that can be peeled apart into translucent layers. These are worn in the mouth until their accumulated pressure is absorbed. Glass-mats yield beads of condensed resonance at their branch points. The beads are swallowed whole. They dissolve not in the stomach but gradually throughout the skeleton.

Water is unknown as a stable substance. The inhabitants do not thirst in the ordinary way, but their bodies sometimes become too definite: skin tightens, joints lose ambiguity, old scars sharpen. To restore flexibility, they sit in beds of silver filaments that draw excess form from the body. An unattended sleeper may be drawn too far and rise unable to remember the exact number of fingers they possess.

### Clothing and tools

Their reed cloaks are thick, asymmetrical, and weighted along the hem. The weights keep a person’s outline from loosening during heavy pulses. Each cloak is braided with patterns indicating home echo, known routes, and material tolerances. A stranger can read from it whether the wearer can safely touch young stone, enter a prospective pass, or approach a forest growing against gravity.

Faces are often covered by masks of layered fan-skin. These do not protect against weather. They prevent the landscape from learning the face too precisely.

The inhabitants have noticed that repeated observation encourages imitation. A cliff watched for many years may develop a shallow human profile. Cracks reproduce gestures. Mineral fronds sometimes grow in the configuration of familiar hands. Most such mimicry is harmless, but a copied face may begin receiving its owner’s echoes, causing routes and relatives to confuse one with the other.

Tools are chosen for constancy. Old basalt is prized. So are pieces of foreign objects brought up from below after their original nature has failed. An animal bone that flowers into mineral reeds may leave behind a dense central spur. Such spurs hold their shape better than native stone and are made into awls, pegs, and listening needles.

The greatest treasures are materials that have completed all possible changes. These are black, dull, and acoustically absent. A fragment laid against the ground creates a tiny patch of true quiet.

The Afterhearing call this substance **done-stone**.

No one knows where it comes from. Small pieces are occasionally found in newly acquired rooms or at the top of stairs that cannot be climbed. Done-stone is never used for weapons. It is placed beside birthing beds, under the heads of the fevered, and in walls facing unstable distances.

### The climbers below

Every settlement maintains at least one downward ladder, though “downward” describes ritual rather than direction. Those trained to use them are called **Narrowers**, because descent makes them thinner to observers.

A Narrower travels lightly: one cord-map, one hushreed cake, one shard of done-stone, and a cloak braided with the pattern of their body. The pattern allows companions to identify them if they return with altered dimensions.

They descend when the roar drops toward its listening interval. Some return minutes later, having aged years. Others return after generations without having noticed any delay. Some bring wet pebbles, bones, feathers, rust, wood, shells, or lumps of cold translucent matter that melts and vanishes. These are understood not as relics of distant countries but as evidence of more finished worlds.

The Afterhearing are fascinated by decay. Rot, rust, erosion, and digestion seem miraculous to them: processes by which matter is permitted to conclude itself. Narrowers describe forests that feed animals, animals that feed other animals, and dead things collapsing into soil from which new life grows.

Many listeners consider this obscene.

Others consider it sacred.

A quiet dispute divides the huts. One tradition believes the Heights are a world at the beginning, slowly constructing the conditions for animals, rivers, weather, and history. Their duty is to assist, measure, and survive until the first true ecosystem arrives.

The other believes the Heights are not a beginning but an inability to finish. The mountains will continue adding ledges, rooms, plants, and distances forever, never attaining completion. To these people, the lower countries are not beneath the Heights but beyond them: worlds that escaped.

Narrowers sometimes fail to return. A few are later encountered as figures on the endless thirty-eighth stair, climbing in reed cloaks. Whether these are the missing people, prototypes of them, or shapes the stair has learned to make is not agreed upon.

Their families still greet the figures by name.

### Death

Nothing decomposes in the Heights, including the inhabitants.

After death, a body cools beyond the surrounding stone and begins to lose biological distinction. Skin becomes flexible mineral sheet. Hair separates into glass fibers. The organs simplify into nested cavities that retain characteristic tones. Over several years, the body becomes a formation known as a **person-stone**.

Families do not keep the whole formation. That would invite the hut to grow around it. Instead, they divide it along natural seams. A resonant piece of sternum may be fitted into a wall. Finger-bones become route markers. The skull is placed in a hushreed garden, where its chambers alter the feeding tone.

Thus the dead continue to influence where plants arise.

Memory is not treated as belonging solely to the living. If a new shoot appears beside a person-stone and opens in the rhythm of the deceased’s name, it may be consulted. Questions are tapped into the ground nearby. Answers, if any, take months: a change of color, a bent frond, a new crack pointing toward a path.

The dead are not said to speak. They are said to have entered the slower conversation.

### The patient figures

The figures seen above the thirty-eighth stair resemble the Afterhearing but are not accepted as kin.

They are too tall, even when distance should make them small. Their cloaks hang without responding to the roar. Each appears to carry something under one arm: a block, a bundled child, a folded ladder, or a piece of darkness. Their faces cannot be seen, because the visible side of each head is always the back.

During the listening interval, they cease climbing and turn slightly toward the knock beneath the mountains.

The Afterhearing do not approach them. They leave offerings at the first stair: powdered thundermeal, broken measuring cords, objects brought from below. By the next interval the offerings are usually unchanged. Occasionally one has become weathered.

This is one of the few ways true age enters the Heights.

A bowl left there may acquire a thousand years of wear in several days. A fresh cord may return frayed and sun-bleached. Once, a young woman placed a braid of her hair on the stair. At the next interval it had become white, then gray dust, then a shallow fossil impression.

The fossil is kept hidden.

It is the only one in the region.

### The knock

The inhabitants organize their lives around the second sound: that immense near-silence in which the Heights listen, followed by the single knock beneath every mountain.

They call it **the Builder**, though few believe it is a person.

At each recurrence, everyone who can do so lies flat against the ground. Hands are spread. Teeth are set gently to stone. No one makes an intentional sound. Children are held still, tools are wrapped, and loose cords are gathered so they cannot strike the walls.

The knock is never louder than knuckle against wood.

Yet every body feels it from below.

Some hear one blow. Some hear two closely spaced. Narrowers often hear a third from somewhere above. The dying may hear a long sequence and tap along until their hands cease.

Afterward, the community takes account of what has been added. Surveyors compare strings across gullies. Gardeners seek new shoots. Hut-keepers test walls for rooms. Listeners examine echoes for cliffs about to arrive. Names are revised where necessary.

Then they perform their only communal song.

It has no melody and cannot be heard over the roar. The singers stand far apart, sometimes on opposite sides of a valley, and strike the ground with stone mallets in an interlocking pattern. Each person contributes a single beat separated from the next by great stretches of thunder. The full composition may take days.

No individual hears the whole.

The mountain does.

Whether the song is thanks, measurement, or instruction has been forgotten. Perhaps it was never decided. But after certain performances, the newly appearing land bears marks too regular to be natural: parallel grooves, fitted hollows, walls without rooms, rooms without walls.

Once, beyond a ridge where no path had existed, the people found an immense shallow impression in the young stone.

It resembled a handprint.

Not a human hand enlarged, but a hand as yet unresolved: seven possible fingers overlapping, each ending in several alternative tips. Along the palm ran grooves matching the rhythm of their communal song.

Since then, the eldest listeners have suspected that the Heights are not merely growing.

They are being taught how.

And the Afterhearing—scattered in their solitary huts, feeding on thunder, naming themselves by consequences—may be neither witnesses nor inhabitants in the ordinary sense.

They may be the small, temporary organs by which the unfinished country listens to its own becoming.
