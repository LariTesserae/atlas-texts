Three people live inside the wave, though the chamber has begun to suggest more.

They call themselves by touch rather than sound. Each has a pattern pressed into the inside of the wrist: a hooked line, three points, a closed ring. Names spoken often would become dangerous here. The water might learn them. It might repeat a name in the knocking of stone, then build a mouth to say it.

Their bodies are compact and heavily muscled, shaped by a world in which every direction may become down. Their hands have broad palms and short fingers, excellent for gripping wet ribs of stone. Their feet are nearly as articulate as their hands. Along the sides of their torsos run pale, sealed folds that open when the chamber floods, exposing dark red breathing tissue. They can survive in water, but not indefinitely, and when submerged they bind skin bladders over their faces to keep a reserve of chamber air.

Their skin has the gray translucence of something rarely touched by direct sun. Beneath it, vessels darken and fade according to pressure. Old injuries appear as branching white seams. They own no fixed clothing, only layered wraps of kelp fiber and fish membrane, tied differently each day so the chamber will not memorize the same silhouette.

The eldest bears the hooked line.

She tends the grooves in the floor, though “tends” means as much erasing as cutting. She records each return of the hollow with a mark unlike the last: shallow, deep, forked, interrupted, or ending in a drilled pit. Her memory is better than the record. She knows which marks correspond to warm rain, which to ice-dark entrances, which to the mountain collision, and which to those occasions when the slit opened not onto weather but onto impossible stillness.

One groove she refuses to interpret. It is older than any of them, yet its edges are fresh.

The chamber has enlarged it three times.

The one marked with three points is the hunter and threshold-keeper. He spends most openings tethered near the slit, wrapped in rope, watching whatever world appears beyond. He catches shrimp from the air, cuts algae before the walls can reclaim it, and throws hooked lines into seas that may lie above, below, or sideways. He has hauled in blind silver fish, frozen weed, volcanic glass, the feather of an enormous bird, and once a length of woven cloth finer than anything their fingers can make. He keeps none of these objects unchanged. Every prize is broken, scraped, pierced, or knotted into a different form before it is brought near the shelters.

He has tried many times to kill the narrow fish in the wall. Secretly, he no longer believes it is a fish. Its visible bones rearrange themselves when he looks away, and its single eye sometimes closes before he moves, as if anticipating him.

The youngest wears the closed ring.

They were born during a turning, when the hollow had become almost entirely water. For the first minutes of their life they breathed through the side-folds, tethered beneath a shelter roof while stone, tools, and bodies streamed around them. Because no stable floor received them, the others say the chamber itself was first to hold the child.

The youngest sleeps poorly. In sleep, their fingers pulse in the rhythm of the bell-creatures. Small circular hollows have begun appearing near their bed.

They are responsible for the luminous plankton, the spore seams, the bladder cups, and the white rootless plants that swell before a pressure fall. They can predict a rearrangement earlier than the others by watching those plants. Sometimes, however, they announce a turning when none comes. On such occasions something else changes: a new rib grows under the shelf, a copied groove appears overhead, or one of the incomplete structures below becomes briefly clear.

The three do not know whether they are a family. Kinship requires a past that remains in one shape. They remember others: a broad-backed woman who vanished through an entrance into cloud; two children taken when the floor dissolved; an old man who entered the transparent wall to pursue the fish and continued walking inside it for several days. But their memories disagree about faces, ages, and sequence. In this place repetition makes things real, and so contradiction is a kind of protection. They recount their dead differently each time.

Only one account remains constant.

There was once a fourth shelter.

No fourth shelter now stands beneath the stone rib, but its lash marks remain, and during certain angles of light a shell roof can be seen reflected in the wall. The reflection contains a crouching occupant. Sometimes it copies the three inhabitants. Sometimes it moves alone.

This may be why the shelters face different directions. It may be why food is occasionally divided into four portions before anyone notices. It may also explain the extra skin bladder, carefully repaired and always kept full.

The chamber’s other lives keep their distance from them.

The clear shrimp are scarcely animals so much as startled fragments of water given legs. They tumble from the ceiling, swimming desperately through air with their many feet. The inhabitants catch them in nets and eat them raw before the chamber can reproduce their frantic shape in stone.

The algae in the seams withdraw from touch. Under dim light they extend black-green filaments across the mineral shelf, sketching branching maps that never correspond to the world outside. When the chamber turns, these filaments flatten and align. The eldest has noticed that they sometimes form the same hooked sign she carries on her wrist. She crops those patches first.

The white plants are more troubling. They have no roots, only soft sacs anchored inside cracks. As pressure falls they inflate into pale hands or lungs, taking in air that may preserve spores—or impressions. Cut open, they contain neither seed nor pulp, but tiny replicas of nearby objects suspended in clear gel: a bone wedge, a knot, a fingernail, once the shape of a sleeping child.

The long fish remains in the wall.

It is thin enough to be mistaken for a crack until its eye turns. Its skeleton has dozens of extra joints because it does not bend through water; it bends with the water, matching every deformation of the wave. When the wall stretches, it lengthens. When the hollow contracts, its ribs fold into one another like nested cages.

It watches the inhabitants not with hunger but attention.

Before collapse, it vanishes. The three have learned to follow its absence more faithfully than any other warning. Yet lately it has begun disappearing when the youngest sleeps, returning when they wake.

The bell-creatures come from wherever water and air cease to differ. Their bodies resemble transparent bells pinched into wings, with threads of blue light suspended inside them. They fly without beating and swim without stroking. On stone they pulse slowly, softening the new mineral beneath them. Each leaves a round impression that continues to deepen after departure.

The inhabitants hide when one arrives. They cover tools, untie knots, change posture, and refuse to watch its rhythm. Even so, the chamber watches.

Hours later the walls begin to pulse.

The narrow fish bends in time.

The white plants inflate and collapse.

Deep beneath the shelf, unfinished pillars flex like fingers.

Most of what lives there may therefore be one life in several attempts.

The wave has no flesh, no single boundary, no permanent anatomy. Yet it remembers pressure, outline, cadence, and contact. It practices whatever persists within it. It has practiced shelter, bone, hands, ladders, doorways, crouching bodies, and the marks by which its inhabitants count their returns. It has practiced the long fish until the fish can watch. It has practiced the bell-creatures until the whole chamber can pulse.

Now it practices the three.

Beneath the newest ledge, mineral foam has begun hardening around three narrow cavities. One has a hooked groove at the wrist. One bears three pits. The last is marked by a closed ring.

There is space beside them for a fourth.
