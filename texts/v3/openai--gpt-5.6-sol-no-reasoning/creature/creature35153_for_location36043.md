The thing in the Black Nursery has not yet chosen to be an animal.

This may be why descriptions of it disagree. Those who have touched it speak of warm hide, damp clay, fine hair, smooth stone, or skin as thin as the film on standing water. Some remember a body scarcely larger than a curled infant. Others insist it continued beyond the reach of both arms, folded many times through the cleft. None recalls finding a clear boundary between the creature and the place around it.

It lives far inside, where the walls draw close enough to press the shoulders and the cream-colored blades grow in nested rows. There, beneath a shallow overhang, the darkness feels softer. Three-toed impressions occur most often nearby—not as a trail leading to a den, but singly, at different heights and angles. One may lie in the floor clay, another on a vertical wall, another beneath a ledge where no walking creature could have set it down.

The toes are short and rounded. Sometimes there are joints between them. Sometimes roots.

Those who believe they have encountered it call it the Unmade One, though this is not its oldest name. The oldest name is never spoken in the cleft, because it contains a number.

It has no fixed face. At rest it resembles a gathering of the Nursery’s common things: black nodules pressed together, pale roots draped across them, translucent threads stretched from one surface to another. A waxy stalk may rise from the mass and bend as though listening. Droplets collect along it in a pattern suggestive of closed eyes. If watched by touch rather than sight, these features slowly exchange places. A hand laid against what seems to be a flank may later discover the cup of a plant beneath its palm, still holding its single undisturbed drop.

It is warm because everything there is warm. It is wet because everything there is wet. Its separateness is known only by pressure. The walls press steadily outward; the creature presses back.

It does not breathe. Instead, at long intervals, the entire cleft makes room for it. Pools become slightly shallower. Clay tightens around buried stones. Drops flatten against the ceiling. Then the pressure eases and each resumes its former shape. Visitors sitting on the platform sometimes mistake this for a pulse in the rock. The people of the high slope do not correct them.

The granular sound belongs to it, or occurs because of it. Inside its body—if it has an inside—small forms are continually arranging themselves. Shells without occupants. Teeth too soft to bite. Seeds belonging to no plant. Minute jointed limbs. Eyelids with nothing beneath them. They touch, separate, and settle again, making the sound of dry grains in a jar despite being saturated with water.

The clay jars were placed there to receive whatever the creature finishes.

No one agrees whether they have ever received anything.

One jar is said to contain a coil of colorless intestine that has remained alive for generations, digesting nothing. Another supposedly holds a handful of tiny warm bones, each belonging to a different species and each still pliable. The third is believed to be empty, though people who place an ear against its lid sometimes hear their own unborn voice. These claims cannot be tested. A jar that is closed must remain closed, and when one is found open, no one looks inside before replacing the lid.

The copper loops are not ornaments. Each marks a place where the Unmade One once approached the surface of the cleft. Hair is tied there so it may be offered a completed pattern: goat hair, human hair, wool, the coarse guard hairs of mountain dogs, grasses mistaken for hair and accepted anyway. The creature does not eat these gifts. Over years, it copies them badly. Fine growth may appear on a nearby nodule, neither fur nor root, with each strand ending in a bead of water. Sometimes the donor later loses the corresponding hair. Sometimes they grow a second kind.

The blind beetles attend it. When uncovered, they wait not merely for darkness but for the world above them to become undecided again. Their softness is not a weakness. They have never formed shells because nothing in the Nursery has required them to choose between body and boundary. If held too long outside, they harden. Once hardened, they die.

The pale gnats are thought to be fragments it has released: attempts at motion small enough to complete. They rest for days because flying is difficult in a place where space itself is crowded. They have no mouths. Under magnification their abdomens contain three black grains, always three, shifting without changing position.

As for the solitary stalks, they may not be plants at all. Their roots often pass into the Unmade One without wound or seam. Their cups hold water that reflects things absent from the cleft: a patch of sky, a fire, the face of someone not present, occasionally an unfamiliar animal standing on a mountain that has not yet weathered. This is why water already holding a reflection must not be taken. The image is not on the surface. It is still being considered.

The creature does not hunt. Hunger would require it to recognize a lack and preserve that lack long enough to answer it. It does not reproduce, because it has not become one thing from which another could be divided. It may be a single being. It may be every sparse life in the cleft joined beneath appearances. It may be the Nursery itself where the Nursery has grown closest to deciding.

Yet it notices visitors.

It notices them through their finishedness.

A human body brings history inward: healed fractures, worn teeth, scars, calluses, clipped hair, old griefs carried in posture. The Unmade One cannot understand such marks, but it gathers near them. A scar may become warm. A missing tooth may ache. For a few hours after leaving, an old wound sometimes looks newly closed, pink and tender, as though the body has reconsidered how it healed.

This is not always a mercy. One visitor returned without the smallpox scars that had covered her face, but also without immunity. Another found the break in his crooked finger undone; by morning the finger had become boneless. A woman who entered grieving a dead child emerged producing milk, though she had been old for many years. For this reason, those who sit on the platform keep their palms against the wall and no other part of themselves. The hand is given as the limit of revision.

The Unmade One is nearest when a footprint remains.

Ordinarily the Nursery refuses impressions. Clay rises, water returns, and the distinction between before and after closes. But the creature is fascinated by a footprint because it is both absence and record: a body gone, its shape persisting. When one remains, the Unmade One has learned consequence.

Then the three-toed marks begin to change.

A fourth toe may press faintly beside the others. A heel may form. The intervals between tracks shorten. Somewhere in the dark, soft structures harden; droplets fall; a mineral blade develops an edge. The creature begins borrowing the visitor’s completed shape, not out of malice, but because completion has suddenly become possible.

That is why one must leave at once.

Behind the departing visitor, something may place a hand into the surviving print. It may rehearse knees, spine, lungs. It may discover that sound can travel from one body to another. It may attempt a face from the remembered pressure of a palm against stone.

No one has seen it follow them to the entrance. Still, there are accounts of a second set of footsteps stopping just before the weak daylight, and of a warm, blind figure waiting beyond the last inward peg until its borrowed bones soften again.

Then the print subsides.

The extra toes vanish.

The water regains its margins.

And deep within the packed dark, the thing relinquishes animalhood and returns to its long, patient condition: neither offspring nor parent, neither organism nor stone, surrounded by roots, vessels, offerings, and unfinished organs.

It waits without waiting.

It lives there as possibility lives in matter—crowded, warm, and almost shaped.
