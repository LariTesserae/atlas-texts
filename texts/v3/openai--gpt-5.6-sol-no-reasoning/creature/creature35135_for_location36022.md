The city is inhabited not by a single people but by a nested multitude whose collective movement gives the fissure its form.

Transparent shrimp, pale amphipods, seed-sized crabs, and rippling flatworms fill its narrow avenues, keeping them clear simply by passing through. Tube worms stand in dense, organ-pipe colonies; sponges anchor the intersections and pump water through porous bodies; bryozoans extend brittle balconies between them. Limpets armor the lower walls, while translucent tunicates hang overhead in shared gelatinous colonies like dim communal lanterns.

Near the upper mouth, thread-fine red algae survive on the little green light filtering down through the ice. Deeper in, bacterial films replace vegetation. Cream, rust, and violet-black, they feed on chemicals seeping from the young rock and are grazed by animals almost too small to distinguish from the surfaces beneath them.

Every inhabitant carries or houses others. Mites shelter in worm casings and bear spores coated in bacteria. Flagellates browse those coatings; viruses pass among all of them. Parasites occupy sponge chambers, and still smaller lives inhabit the parasites. The apparent walls, plazas, and buildings of the city are themselves feeding bodies or colonies.

A juvenile wolffish is the largest resident, though it is small enough to curl inside a cup. It waits in a shell-lined cavity beyond the crowded center, periodically thrusting its blunt head into the traffic. Shrimp spring away, worms retract, and amphipods spill into pores. After it feeds and withdraws, the avenue quickly fills again. Hunting snails create slower disturbances, leaving temporarily emptied districts that scavengers, grazers, and new settlers reclaim in succession.

Inverted lenses of meltwater cling to the roof. Small animals hang beneath them and feed along the boundary between fresh and salt water. Tiny crustaceans emit cold flashes when startled; polyps glow at busy junctions; comb jellies occasionally squeeze through the upper slit with spectral light trembling along their comb rows.

Below the visible traffic, bacteria are the first colonists of every new fracture. Grazers follow them, then tube builders, filter feeders, scavengers, and predators. Eggs gather beneath ledges like clear beads. Larvae drift between districts their adult bodies may never leave. Cilia beat, mouths comb mineral films, sponges pump, bivalves drain the sediment, and waste is consumed almost where it falls.

At the deepest, lightless edge live the pioneers of surfaces not yet stable: bacterial sheens, minute grazers, larvae, and soft-bodied settlers clinging to mineral skins that may collapse within hours. They inhabit a place still deciding its own geometry.

What lives there is therefore less a population than a living circulation—bodies within bodies, colonies on colonies, predators opening vacancies and settlers closing them. No creature understands the whole city. Each knows only a current, a chemical trace, a feeding surface, a shelter, or the sudden shadow of a mouth. Together, without plan or ownership, they make the crack habitable faster than the stone can become solid.
