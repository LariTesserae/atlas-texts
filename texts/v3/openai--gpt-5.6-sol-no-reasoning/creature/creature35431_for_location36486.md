The valley is inhabited by a whole watershed behaving as one diffuse organism.

Its most visible residents are the plants, layered so densely that bare ground is almost an injury, quickly closed. Pale-trunked trees occupy the wet floor, with crowns broad enough to hold gardens of their own. Their waxy leaves shed water into bromeliad cups, root hollows, and the waiting fronds below. Tree ferns rise beneath them; smaller ferns repeat their shapes near the soil, followed by moss, liverwort, and glossy creepers until the forest seems to reproduce itself at diminishing scales. On the cliffs, shorter trees fasten themselves into cracks with roots like cables, drinking from warm seams inside the mountain. Resinous shrubs exhale clear oils in the afternoon heat. Taut seedpods open in clicking sequences. Flowers tend toward cream, green, rust, and old-wax yellow, announcing themselves less by color than by smells of pepper, fruit, bark, and heated honey.

Every plant is also a dwelling. Branch-cups contain translucent mites, larvae, and minute swimmers. Water pooled in a curled leaf may hold an entire brief food chain. Mineral films at the springs support grazers too small to see individually. Pale insects dismantle fallen leaves in coordinated fronts, while fungi and bacteria finish what they begin. Fruit scarcely has time to lie intact before beetles, soft-bodied decomposers, and the warm ground reduce it to usable matter. Nothing here is merely discarded. Death changes departments.

The streams have inhabitants fitted to transience. Thumb-length fish crowd shallow runoff channels and retreat through fissures when surface water fails. Mineral-shelled filterers join themselves into living chains across narrow currents, combing food from the flow and leaving the water clearer behind them. Frogs emerge with afternoon rain, vocal for the brief period when the whole valley is dripping loudly enough to conceal them. Around warm pools, reeds tick as their hollow stems expand, while orange microbial colonies spread over the mineral lips like slow flame.

Small bright lizards inhabit the boundary between wetness and heat. They bask on black ledges polished by water, lifting their feet one at a time when the stone becomes too hot. Moths sleep beneath leaves, their wings mottled like iron stains and mineral bloom. Beetles clean fruit pits to a dark shine. Cicadas live deep in warmed cracks, hidden from sight and almost from hearing. Their millions of bodies synchronize after noon, transmitting a steady pulse through entire cliff faces. They are less a chorus than a temporary organ of the mountain.

The canopy belongs to compact, cautious climbers. They travel in family groups along vines and interlocking crowns, rarely exposing a full body. Their passage is marked by a shaken branch, a scatter of droplets, a soft cough from behind leaves. They eat fruit, young growth, insects, and perhaps the tiny creatures collected in plant-held pools. Their routes are old enough to be almost arboreal roads, though nothing has been cut or built.

Long-legged birds patrol the river braids with extreme deliberation, hunting among reflective shallows where one careless step would scatter everything. Other birds nest on outward-leaning trees at the precipice. Some spend much of their lives below the rim, riding warm updrafts from the unseen world beneath the clouds. When they rise past the edge, they appear abruptly from white emptiness, as if the abyss were hatching them.

At twilight, bats issue from the cliffs. They roost in shallow alcoves kept damp by seeping water and warm by the rock behind them. Their emergence is orderly but not rigid: first a few scouts, then low streams crossing the meadows and channels. They harvest the insects raised by that day’s heat and rain, and by dawn they have disappeared again into the mountain’s wet folds.

The largest residents are dark, high-shouldered grazers that favor open meadows during rainfall. Their broad, spreading hooves keep them from sinking deeply into saturated earth. Warm water fills their tracks, which then become drinking places, breeding pools, and traps for drifting seeds. They browse reeds, creepers, and fresh post-rain growth, carrying spores and burrs between separated clearings. Their bodies are heavy enough to open temporary corridors through the vegetation, but the plants close behind them quickly.

Predators shadow these herds from the fern margins. They are built less for pursuit than for stillness: low movement, patient breathing, coats or hides broken into the colors of wet bark, shadow, and mineral-stained leaves. A hunt, when it happens, is sudden and short. More often predator and prey merely register one another and wait. The valley’s abundance permits caution.

Yet none of these creatures quite lives alone. The true inhabitant is the exchange among them: warm stone lifting water; roots drawing minerals from cracks; leaves slowing rain; insects reducing leaves; fish and shelled colonies filtering streams; grazers opening meadows; predators returning flesh to soil; birds and bats carrying nutrients across the precipice. Even the clouds participate, rising from the canopy and returning as localized rain.

The valley has no overseer and no central organ. Its coordination comes from temperature, pressure, appetite, decay, and timing. Cicadas begin together because the cliffs warm together. Trees shed together because moisture and light cross a common threshold. Insects arrive because the leaves fall. Springs pulse from hidden pressures. River colonies grow where the current brings enough food. Each resident follows something immediate, yet their combined activity gives the place its deep, nearly mechanical order.

What lives there is therefore not a civilization disguised as wilderness, nor a machine made of animals. It is an old community so thoroughly integrated that the distinction between inhabitant and habitat has worn away. The mountain walls house it, the waters circulate through it, and every creature—mite, tree, grazer, fungus, fish, cicada—serves briefly as one moving part of a life much larger than itself.
