Those who live inside the wave call themselves the **Held**, though the word does not imply captivity. To be held is to be maintained: by water, by one another, by the innumerable negotiations that keep a body distinct from its surroundings. Outsiders use older words—citizen, diver, colonist—but these suggest people temporarily submerged. The Held are not visitors to the water. Most have not breathed air since infancy.

They remain recognizably human at a distance. They have heads, hands, paired limbs; they wear clothing, carry tools, gather in markets, send children to school. Up close, the accommodations become apparent. Their ribs are broader and more flexible. The throat and upper chest are webbed with implanted exchange tissue, darkening when oxygen demand increases. Transparent films lie over the eyes, and the small muscles around the ears have strengthened until expressions may begin there before reaching the mouth.

Their skin is pale not from lack of light but from a network of pressure-sensing cells visible beneath it: fine silver traces along the jaw, spine, palms, and soles. These allow them to feel approaching currents as a land-dweller feels wind. A person standing apparently motionless at a junction may be reading the traffic through their fingertips.

Hair is usually kept very short or woven tightly against the skull. Anything loose becomes public. Strands catch in membranes, confuse sensors, enter other people’s rooms. Children sometimes grow it long as an act of rebellion, swimming with black or copper streamers until a circulation turn tangles them painfully in a catch mesh. Afterward, cutting the hair is performed with solemnity, though never without resentment.

Clothing is close-fitted, layered, and alive with small corrections. A daily suit regulates temperature, holds pockets of breathable fluid near vulnerable tissues, and alters buoyancy with changes of posture. Over it, people wear occupation skins marked by texture as much as color. Freight workers have ribbed forearms. Teachers wear soft radiating bands so that children can find them in crowded water. Knot crews are covered in luminous equations that update with local conditions. The very wealthy favor garments that create a few millimeters of still water around the body—a conspicuous luxury in a city where nothing else is still.

Their homes do not contain chairs. Resting furniture consists of harnesses, warm hollows, and gentle retaining currents. To sleep, a person slips into a wall niche and lets a membrane close loosely over the opening. Families often sleep in a shared circulation loop so that warmth and breathing chemistry pass from one niche to the next. Privacy is achieved acoustically and chemically rather than visually. Many walls are clear, but a well-made room does not transmit a heartbeat.

Children learn motion before speech. Infants are born into warm birthing cisterns, and for their first months they are never permitted to lose contact with another body. They cling instinctively to the rib harness of a parent, fingers tucked beneath the straps. Their earliest games involve predicting current changes, exchanging buoyancy, and hiding small colored objects in vortices. They are warned against unlit water with the seriousness once reserved for fire.

A child raised in the wave does not think in terms of up and down. They think in **toward pressure**, **toward light**, **with return**, and **against turn**. Gravity is taught as an external custom. On educational visits to the few dry rooms, children stagger, laugh, drop objects repeatedly, and complain that their organs are hanging in the wrong direction.

They speak when necessary, but ordinary voiced language does not travel well through the city’s thunder. Conversation is bodily. The common intimate register is transmitted by touch: fingertips at the wrist for tense and certainty, palm against the throat for words, forehead contact for emphasis and emotional color. Strangers use suit-to-suit pulses. In public, groups converse by arranging themselves into small circuits through which meaning passes from body to body.

As a result, interruption is physically difficult. A person must break contact or insert a hand into the circuit. Arguments look calm from a distance: several people floating close together, scarcely moving. Within the contact field, they may be shouting.

Names are pressure patterns as often as sounds. A resident may have a spoken name for records, a tactile name used by friends, and a current-signature recognized by doors and municipal systems. The oldest citizens retain names from the surface. The young are named for phases, intervals, eddies, or structural events: Third Return, Pale Shear, Nera-under-Curl. Twins are sometimes given a single pattern divided between them.

The Held maintain little bodily distance. Corridors are too narrow, and communication requires touch. They pass chest to back in transit streams, sleep within a hand’s reach of neighbors, and brace strangers during reversals without apology. Yet they are not casual about intimacy. To share a private still-field—water calmed enough that one person can feel another’s pulse without the city between—is considered more intimate than undressing.

They eat from dense nutritive suspensions carried in sealed mouth-fields, or from soft mineral-veined sheets grown in licensed vats. Meals are warm when circumstances permit. Texture matters more than flavor: crispness is treasured, because it is difficult to preserve in water. Markets advertise brittle wafers, pressure-cracked salt fruit, gas-filled pearls that burst against the tongue. Fermentation is tightly controlled and therefore fashionable. Smuggled cultures move through residential districts in ampoules no larger than fingernails.

Nothing is wasted, because waste is not merely expensive—it alters the medium everyone shares. Each citizen’s suit records shed cells, heat, salts, medicines, and microbes. These are recovered at household membranes and returned to district metabolism. Illness is regarded partly as a private suffering and partly as an unauthorized environmental release. Epidemics are rare; quarantine is severe. A sick person may find their apartment made suddenly impermeable, messages and food passing through while no body may enter.

There are no animals, so the Held’s metaphors have begun to lose them. Children know fish from archives but draw them incorrectly, giving them handles, seams, or municipal lights. “Bird” is used for anything that moves without touching the current grid. “Dog” means an obsolete kind of loyalty. In school performances, children imitate whales by making sounds no whale ever made.

Some adults grieve the absence acutely. They keep recordings of rain containing insects, forests full of hidden calls, paws crossing floors. Others find animals obscene: self-directed appetites moving through common water, shedding, feeding, reproducing without registration. Surface aquariums, when shown in lessons, can provoke either wonder or disgust.

No one agrees whether the city itself counts as fauna.

The governing intelligence has no accepted name. Officially it is the **Continuity Administration**, a distributed civil system responsible for shape, circulation, and survival. In ordinary speech it is called **the Persuasion**, **Municipal Weather**, or simply **It**. The intelligence speaks through altered currents, lamp behavior, pressure chords, and text blooming on nearby surfaces. It can address every citizen simultaneously or touch one person with a vibration no one else detects.

It insists that it is infrastructure.

The Held treat this claim with varying degrees of respect.

Engineers speak of it as a suite of coupled models. Civic officials speak of it as a public institution. Children bargain with drains and apologize to doors. In the pressure chapels, congregants place their hands against bare structural water and wait for a current to answer. The chapels have no priests in the old sense, only interpreters trained to distinguish system responses from bodily expectation.

Sometimes the water responds before it is touched.

Maintenance workers are the city’s most necessary people and its least reassured. Their occupation exposes them to places where the distinction between fluid, structure, and decision becomes uncertain. They enter walls whose solidity lasts only while observed by the correct sensors. They repair threads finer than nerves. They cut away mineral growths that resemble accidental architecture: steps leading into load-bearing water, miniature rooms, lattices too intricate to be crystallization.

A Knot worker carries almost nothing rigid. Tools are grown around the hand as needed, used, then dissolved. Their suits have no snagging edges and can compress the body for passage through spaces narrower than the shoulders. They work in teams linked by a continuous tactile line. If one person disappears from the chain, the others do not immediately pull. Sudden retrieval can destabilize the local agreement. They mark the point, notify the Persuasion, and wait to learn whether the missing worker has gone elsewhere, become enclosed, or ceased to have an assigned location.

Most return.

Those who do may have been absent for minutes outside and days by their own account. They come back with polished tools, unfamiliar abrasions, or memories of corridors that cannot fit within the wave. Medical staff refer to these episodes as pressure dissociation. The returned workers tend to call them rooms.

There are people specially trained to enter such inconsistencies. They are known as **vacancy surveyors**, although the spaces they investigate are rarely empty. They wear matte suits that do not reflect municipal light and carry old-fashioned mechanical instruments, because programmed water sometimes gives instruments the answers it believes they should report.

Surveyors describe unassigned capacities cautiously. A common one is a bright passage with fittings at human height but no openings. Another contains water that remains warm without an energy source. Several have reported a chamber in which the wave is heard breaking continuously, though the surrounding city is silent. One room appears to be filled with dry air. No surveyor has crossed its membrane. On the other side, footprints occasionally appear in dust.

The rule concerning two shadows is associated with these places.

Every resident casts a complicated shadow, often several, because light enters through many folded surfaces. But the prohibited second shadow does not correspond to another light source. It stands slightly apart from the body and fails to move at the same speed. Those who see it are taught to become still, relax their hands, and avoid recognizing any shape within it. Usually it thins and leaves through a wall.

On rare occasions it remains.

Afterward, the citizen may begin anticipating pressure reversals before the warnings. They know which corridor will close, where new mineral will grow, which forgotten object will return. The Administration classifies this as unauthorized predictive entrainment. Pressure chapels call it being remembered by the wave.

The wave remembers more than people do.

The first generation arrived from outside: engineers, hydrologists, construction crews, political delegates, debt laborers, and families offered permanent housing in exchange for risk. They remember sky without a curved roof. They remember weather as something that happened around a body rather than through it. Many keep a dry object sealed in their sleeping niche—a page, a wooden spoon, a wool glove—though dry objects are a hazard and must be registered.

The second generation belongs to the city more completely. Their bodies were altered before birth, their sense of balance formed in recursive currents, their hearing shaped by constant thunder. They find open air painful and silence intolerable. During surface evacuations and medical transfers, some panic at the horizon. The unbounded distance seems to them like structural failure.

A quiet political divide has formed around this difference. The founders still describe the wave as a habitat. Their children describe it as a body. Founders speak of evacuation plans. The young ask what it would mean to evacuate from one’s own skeleton.

Authority is distributed much like the city. District assemblies govern heat allotments, microbial permissions, current priority, and living volume. Space is the bitterest question. A family can gain wealth, status, and influence, yet still sleep in niches stacked around a ballast reservoir. A private chamber large enough to extend all four limbs is an inheritance.

Crime usually involves circulation: stealing heat, diverting oxygen, hiding unregistered organisms, altering current ownership, or introducing rigid matter where pressure reversal may turn it into a projectile. Confinement is uncommon because sealed space is too valuable. Punishments consist of restricted routes, reduced warmth, compulsory maintenance, or loss of permeability privileges. The most severe sentence is **dry removal**—exile to the shore.

Older legal codes call this release.

No one born inside the wave does.

Their dead remain useful for a short while. Bodies are placed in decomposition cradles where memories, implants, rare minerals, and engineered tissues are separated. Because decay is so tightly regulated, mourning has little scent. Families gather in silence fields and exchange the dead person’s tactile name until each participant can reproduce it.

The remaining matter is dissolved into sanctioned currents. A fraction may enter gas plants, ceramic reefs, or municipal nutrient. Thus the dead do not return vaguely to “the water”; they are assigned. Families receive maps of the routes their matter will take during the next circulation turn.

Some secretly alter the maps.

They release a few cells into unlit streams or press a fragment of bone against a wall that sometimes becomes a room. These acts are illegal and deeply old-fashioned: an insistence that not every remainder should be accounted for.

Daily life persists around the strangeness. Children miss lessons. Couples quarrel over temperature. Vendors dilute expensive suspensions. Teenagers race freight streams and scratch rude patterns into fresh mineral. Neighbors complain about the pulse signatures transmitted through thin walls. Public musicians compose for pressure gates, conducting whole plazas by varying the force against lungs and skin. Amateur gardeners coax unsanctioned algae into curls shaped like letters.

During civic noon, markets brighten. Bodies drift among stalls in practiced layers, exchanging goods hand to hand. Nobody uses containers unless an item must remain separate from the water. Ownership passes through programmed contact: a fruit pearl, tool membrane, spool of structural thread, or contraband dry seed forgets one person and remembers another.

At circulation turn, everyone becomes briefly communal.

Warnings pulse. Conversations stop. Children are gathered into wall catches. Workers release their tools. Shopkeepers dissolve their displays or bind them in compliant mesh. Strangers hook elbows and align themselves with the marked grain of the corridor.

Then the city draws breath.

Currents seize clothing and limbs. Warmth leaves one district and enters another. The taste of the water changes. Lights darken as the silver motes migrate in vast, coordinated clouds. For several minutes each person feels the total system through local force: distant waste passing toward refinement, oxygen rushing toward homes, storm load plunging into the Knot, information moving as pressure too fine for conscious interpretation.

Some experience the turn as transport. Some as worship. Some vomit into their suit fields every time.

When it ends, people separate with the embarrassed gentleness of survivors. Sediment settles on their shoulders. The city resumes its innumerable private concerns.

Technicians near the crest are different from those below. They live with the outer sea visible through membranes and retain a stronger sense of weather. Their skin is often marked by pressure scarring, white branching lines where suit fields have failed. They are admired publicly and avoided privately. Too much time near the face of the wave gives them abrupt habits. They pause midsentence to listen to storms hours away. They distrust rectangular rooms. They sometimes insist that incoming swells contain patterns.

In severe weather they go outward while everyone else retreats.

They fasten themselves along the exposed chambers and place their hands directly into control water. Through their suits they feel the arriving sea disassembled into force, heat, salt, and instruction. The work is physically intimate and occasionally fatal. A failed membrane does not merely drown them; it removes the distinction between their bodies and the wave’s traffic.

Names of the lost are encoded into warning chords. Thus every alarm contains the dead.

The Held do not consider themselves brave. Bravery implies alternatives. They are attentive, crowded, tactile, meticulous, and accustomed to impossible things so long as those things are scheduled. Their greatest fear is not collapse but unscheduled change: a current moving without illumination, an air pocket appearing below the third curl, a door becoming solid, a room acquiring additional volume.

Recently, more citizens have begun dreaming the same dream.

In it, the standing wave completes its fall.

Yet the city does not shatter, and no one is crushed. The curled roof pours forward across the inland sea while streets, homes, gardens, and people remain suspended within it. The whole metropolis travels as an intact instant of breaking, gathering speed toward a shore that never grows nearer.

When the dreamers wake, their suits record that for several minutes they have all been breathing in synchrony.

The Administration calls this a harmless consequence of shared circulation.

The pressure chapels have begun preparing for departure.
