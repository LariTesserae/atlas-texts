Those who live beneath the black shelf are mostly too small, too slow, or too temporary to disturb its emptiness.

They inhabit margins rather than the chamber itself. Liverwort crowds the rims of the cold basins, laying down dark, glossy lobes where condensation keeps the stone wet. Moss occupies the weak reach of daylight near the entrance and the bank of clay left by dissolving memorial tablets. Minute ferns anchor themselves in silted cracks. Farther from the light, fungal threads and nearly transparent films spread over damp rock, forming forests that reveal themselves only to a kneeling observer with a lamp.

Among these graze black springtails, quick as sparks of soot. Colorless worms work through the basin sediment. Other creatures—mites, pale larvae, organisms scarcely distinguishable from the films they feed upon—complete lives within territories smaller than a hand. Their seasons are not summer and winter but seepage and dryness, the arrival of a ceiling drip, the retreat of a wet edge, the collapse of a pinhead fungus.

The chamber’s nearest thing to a guardian is the cave salamander. It is rarely seen and may be absent for years. When present, it lies beneath a flat stone beside the river wall: small, cold, nearly translucent, its muted pink skin suggesting flesh healed over an ancient wound. Its stillness is so complete that condensation may gather on it. Only the slight opening of a lidless-looking eye, or the spread of one delicate foot beneath a leaf, distinguishes it from the mineral world. The visitors believe its presence means the buried river remains where it should be.

Bats sometimes sleep in the outer fissure, where the roof is low and the night air still reaches. Mice venture as far as the grit, leaving fine tracks between the windbreak and the first basin. Neither belongs to the inner hollow. They come briefly, test its poverty, and withdraw.

People live there only in the loosest sense.

They are lowlanders accustomed to cold ground, difficult water, and long distances between shelter. They arrive alone or in small parties, stooping through the crack with clay lamps cupped in their hands. Their clothes smell of smoke, wool, hide, and the weather outside. Within a few steps the river strips them of ordinary speech and makes them creatures of sight and touch.

They know the chamber with their bodies. Their feet find the pale stepping stones. Their hands seek the hanging cords and familiar cracks. Two firm pressures on the shoulder warn of bad footing. Empty palms raised before a touch to the throat ask for water. A circling lamp calls everyone back. Three eclipses of the flame announce that someone has not returned.

Here, attention replaces conversation. Faces are watched closely. A wrist caught in time matters more than a shouted warning. Promises are made brow to brow, each speaker feeling the other’s words tremble through teeth and facial bones. The gesture can resemble tenderness, confrontation, or prayer, but is simply the only way to be certain that a vow has entered another person.

No one remains permanently. Refugees may sleep behind the slab windbreak for several nights. Travelers fill skins at the basins. Families bring clay tablets bearing the names of their dead. Messengers meet, exchange objects, touch foreheads, and depart by separate routes. During storms, several people may share the hollow while remaining isolated in private islands of lamplight.

Yet generations persist there through their traces. Charred lamps sit in niches. Pegs decay beside their replacements. A bone needle waits where someone set it down and never found it again. Flood counts score the wall. Handprints survive in clay until moisture softens the ridges of the fingers. Memorial tablets lose first their letters, then their edges, and finally their identities, becoming sediment from which moss draws nourishment.

Thus the chamber’s human inhabitants gradually join its smaller ones. Names become mud. Mud supports rootlets. Fungi rise from it after seepage. Springtails graze the fungi. A salamander shelters beneath the stone beside them.

And beneath all of these lives is the river—the chamber’s absent, overwhelming inhabitant.

It cannot be seen or reached. It lives behind the farther wall as pressure, vibration, and ceaseless rearrangement. It rattles grit into bands, urges standing stones toward a lean, tilts basin water against one rim, and passes through the bones of anyone who rests on the floor. Its moods last hours or seasons. Its body changes course in passages no lamp can enter.

The people say the hollow remembers weight rather than words because the river keeps the only record that endures there. It receives every footstep as a momentary pressure and every abandoned object as a burden to be shifted. The living come and go; the dead lose their names; even the salamander may disappear.

The river remains, sealed in darkness, slowly drawing the whole chamber toward itself.
