The hollow is inhabited less by a single creature than by a dispersed, many-bodied organism assembled from everything that favors an edge.

Its visible skin is moss: dense blue-green cushions pressed flat by cold spray, threaded with liverworts as fine as veins. Beneath that skin spread rust-red bacterial films, slick and easily torn, consuming what the mineral water brings and knitting themselves together again downstream. Black manganese scales and orange iron belts mingle with them until it is difficult to say where life ends and the chamber’s slow manufacture of stone begins.

The smallest inhabitants move across these surfaces like circulating cells. Springtails crowd the rims of new ice, turning clear edges black. Translucent amphipods comb the bacterial sheets and retreat into fissures at the first tremor. Silver larvae flatten themselves beneath stones in the strongest current. Red mites climb deliberately through the moss, while minute snails inscribe pale spirals in the mineral collars around the vents. White worms inhabit the pool’s narrow throat, ordinarily hidden beneath the water, twisting in sudden exposure whenever the pressure below fails.

Even the lone reed is plural. Aboveground it appears as one bone-white shoot, frail and misplaced in the dusk. Below, its rhizome travels through warm mud, testing fissures and sending up temporary stems wherever heat permits. Frost kills each new shoot, but not the buried body. It waits, branches, and tries again.

Larger lives enter as brief disturbances. The water shrew comes glittering with condensation and turns the runnels empty with its hunger. Its whiskers read every current; its narrow body vanishes into gaps that seem too small to contain it. The pale frog lies dormant in the peat until a warm pulse reaches it, then unfolds and feeds among the flies. Long-legged flies descend through the green light, circling reflections as if mistaking them for exits. Occasionally the buried channel delivers a trout into the black pool—a sudden muscular brightness in a chamber otherwise ruled by invertebrates. It feeds ravenously, but it does not belong for long. The same pressure that brought it will retrieve it or throw it onto stone.

None of these creatures possesses the hollow. Occupancy passes among them with each pulse. When the shrew hunts, the water appears lifeless. When the frog wakes, the air clears. When the shaft fires, wings beat, larvae recoil, worms knot themselves deeper, and springtails scatter across breaking ice. Then the disturbance subsides and the multitudes return to their narrow territories.

What lives here has learned not to occupy the center. Life keeps to interfaces: the underside of stone, the meniscus of a pool, the seam where peat meets basalt, the transient border between warm seepage and ice. Bodies are small, flat, filamentous, compressible, or hidden. They survive by yielding passage to water and stone.

Taken together, the inhabitants resemble the organs and attendants of the chamber’s buried pump. Bacterial films catch its chemical outflow. Snails scrape its deposits. Amphipods and larvae transfer its fertility into flesh. Shrew, frog, fly, and stranded fish harvest that flesh in turn. Roots open channels; moss retains spray; decay darkens the water and feeds what cannot live in the light.

The chamber itself may be the largest thing living there—not alive in the ordinary sense, but breathing, feeding, secreting mineral, changing its apertures, and convulsing under pressure. Its creatures inhabit it as mites might inhabit a vast cold animal. Yet they also complete it. Without them it would only circulate water and make stone. With them, every hiss and flood becomes nourishment, alarm, migration, birth, and pursuit.

Thus what lives beneath the newly frozen earth is a community perpetually interrupted and perpetually returning: soft life coating hard beginnings, countless bodies pressed into the margins of a machine that no one built.
