The inhabitants of the high country are difficult to count, because few remain exclusively inhabitants. They become pasture while resting, weather while migrating, machinery when mature, and geography when they die.

The most numerous large animals are the **quillbacks**, low-bodied grazers with six widely spread legs. Their hides are colorless beneath coats of hollow, mineral-stiffened spines. Each quill has a narrow opening at its tip, so a running herd produces a wandering chord that changes with speed, wind, and humidity. They do not graze leaves. Instead, they comb frost organs from the plain with rows of warm, flexible teeth. In doing so they keep the surface pores clear and prevent the tableland from sealing itself too tightly.

Quillbacks travel far apart, sometimes with several body lengths between individuals. This is not caution but arithmetic. The red sieve-moss cannot usually close quickly enough around a sparse herd, and its mesh tends to select every third concentrated pressure. When threatened, quillbacks scatter in patterns that continually change which animal is third. Calves learn these broken rhythms before they learn to feed. A herd fleeing across the dark mat sounds like a pipe organ being dismantled in a storm.

Older quillbacks become increasingly buoyant. Gas-producing symbionts colonize their quills, and the animals must hook themselves into the ground to sleep. Some eventually rise into the cloud deck. Whether these become part of the aerial fauna or are simply consumed there is unknown. Their hooked sleeping grounds often sprout pale grass in the shape of reclining bodies.

The **threadnecks** browse beneath the surface. They have compact torsos, long forelimbs, and necks thin enough to enter the plain’s breathing apertures. Their skulls are no wider than their vertebrae. Once inserted, the head unfolds into a cage of sensitive ribs that combs root lice from vascular cords. A feeding threadneck can stand motionless for hours while its buried head travels many yards through the mesh.

This habit makes them vulnerable to false vents. Some apertures are mouths belonging to no discrete creature, merely temporary predatory arrangements of the plateau. Threadnecks test a hole by exhaling into it. A true breathing pore returns the breath warm and altered; a hunting pore holds it. The oldest browsers carry maps of trustworthy openings in the pigmentation of their flanks, but the maps drift as the land revises itself.

In open water live the **mirrorlings**, silver amphibians small enough to sleep within individual droplets gathered beneath translucent leaves. Their folded bodies are almost two-dimensional. At dawn—or the nearest local equivalent—they cut their droplets open and swim down the wet leaf surfaces, entering pools through films too thin for larger animals to perceive as water.

Mirrorlings breed during condenser discharge. Their eggs require the percussion of falling droplets to divide, and entire generations may remain inert if a cistern opens quietly. They feed on reflections: not light itself, but the microscopic organisms that align along reflected edges. A pool crowded with them appears dull because every image has been grazed away.

The black birds that gather at valves are **surge rooks**. They have broad feet for gripping convulsing bark and transparent inner beaks that snap shut a fraction of a second before their outer beaks. This lets them catch the glassworms expelled in high-pressure overflow without being cut apart by them. Colonies memorize the pulse of a particular condenser and anticipate its releases so precisely that they launch before the valves move.

They build no nests. Instead, each rook deposits one egg into a warm vascular channel and carries another belonging to a stranger. Thus their migrations are also exchanges of young between distant towers. Chicks hatch inside the machinery and climb toward the sound of adult feet. Many condensers possess ladderlike tissues that appear to exist for this purpose, though whether the birds shaped the towers or the towers shaped the birds is no longer a useful question.

Above them move the **cloud combers**, membrane-winged colonial animals that feed against the underside of the overcast. A single apparent creature may be hundreds of translucent bodies linked edge to edge. Their mouths face upward. As they fly, they strain spores, ice organs, and minute aerial larvae from the cloud, leaving long clear furrows behind them.

When threatened, a colony separates. Its fragments resemble loose scraps of skin and scatter in divergent winds. Days later they reassemble, but not necessarily into the same individual. Memory is stored in the pattern of their joined wing margins; after each reunion, some recollections belong to everyone and others fit nowhere. These unwanted memories condense into dark nodules that are eventually dropped. Where such a nodule lands, the ground may briefly remember being sky.

Higher still is the thing called, by implication rather than observation, the **overwing**.

No complete body has been seen. Its passage presses hollows into the cloud deck and bows condenser towers across entire watersheds. Structures interpreted as feathers descend afterward, although each is jointed, vascular, and fertile. On touching the plateau, a feather inserts its shaft through the plant skin and grows a grove of repeating barbs. Those groves sometimes beat in unison during storms.

It may be one animal, a migrating population packed too densely to distinguish, or an atmospheric organ of the plateau itself. The smallest estimated wingspan is wider than many valleys. Yet the pressure tracks left in the clouds occasionally stop directly above the same empty region toward which all creatures turn when the buried lights assume certain patterns.

The plain also supports innumerable **root lice**, **glassworms**, **valve mites**, and **piston crabs**. Root lice travel through sap channels in linked chains, each individual digesting what the one before it could not. Glassworms have transparent musculature and mineral jaws; they abrade the insides of pipes while feeding, keeping them open. Valve mites live in the creases of swollen knots and trigger releases by migrating from one side of the hinge to the other. Piston crabs inhabit the deep reciprocating organs beneath condensers. Blind and asymmetrical, they prune tissue on the compression stroke and reproduce on the return.

No condenser functions without these animals. None of the animals can reproduce away from the condensers. Together they constitute something larger than either ecology or machine.

The red predatory moss is known by no name among creatures capable of naming. It lies flush with the plain, indistinguishable from iron-rich ground cover until enough weight enters its perimeter. Then its filaments rise and cross, making a contracting sieve. Small animals pass through. Larger bodies are caught, but never all of them: regular gaps are essential because escape drives the survivors across the moss’s spore sacs. Its apparent preference for every third animal is the consequence of intersecting tension waves rather than choice, though the distinction offers little comfort.

Lakes have predators too. **Falsewaters** spread leaves whose upper surfaces reproduce depth, wave motion, cloud reflection, and even the silhouettes of swimming prey. Diving birds strike what seems to be open water and pass through valve slits into digestive bladders. Mature falsewaters can impersonate a whole mere. Some hold real water above their leaves as ballast, and genuine aquatic communities develop upon them. When the plant feeds, an entire lake folds shut.

The moving pockets of silence contain **hush larvae**. They are nearly invisible, soft-bodied aerial filter feeders that consume vibration through branching sensory fans. A dense swarm removes sound as efficiently as cloud combers remove mist. The larvae grow brighter as they feed but cannot be seen by direct gaze; they appear only as brief discolorations at the edge of vision.

When a swarm becomes saturated, it molts simultaneously. The accumulated vibrations are trapped in discarded skins and released when those skins touch the ground. This is why old noises sometimes return all at once: wingbeats, splashes, cries, and thunder may erupt minutes or years after their causes. Adult hushes live within the weeping cliffs, where they feed on repeated events and keep certain moments from multiplying without limit.

Near those cliffs dwell the **before-and-afters**, scavengers whose bodies do not occur in a reliable sequence. One may first encounter their tracks, then the mature animal, then its feeding mouth by itself, and finally a cluster of eggs retreating into an exposed stratum of fossil rain. They gather around injuries before they happen and clean bones that have not yet been uncovered. Their presence is often the only warning that an animal is about to fall.

They are not immortal. Death simply reaches different portions of them at different times. A complete corpse is therefore rare and dangerous, because it marks a moment when several incompatible histories have aligned. The plateau rapidly roots through such remains, converting vertebrae into tributaries and muscles into banks.

There are also organisms made by storms and permitted only brief existence. A meeting of root lightning may raise a stand of pump-trees, a choir of reeds, or a **gravity flower** broad enough to distort rain. These blooms attract animals not through scent but through altered falling. Beetles approach in long descending spirals; birds orbit petals without flapping; loose pools climb their stems as trembling spheres.

The principal dismantlers are **vector beetles**, whose paired legs each adhere to a different direction of pull. They can walk across conflicting gravity as if over bark. They consume the flower’s directional tissues and carry fragments away in their shells. After mating, each beetle sheds one leg. The cast limbs root and become the needles of small compass shrubs, all pointing toward a force that may no longer exist.

Beneath everything are the **deep pumps**.

Whether they are organs, colonies, or animals older than the plateau cannot be determined from the surface. Their strokes raise and settle the high country. They draw gas from buried decay, exchange heat between distant basins, and send pale cords downward beyond illumination. Sometimes a pump misses a cycle. For several seconds an entire valley becomes weightless, or rain pauses in the air, or every root opens as though listening.

The condensers may be their respiratory structures. The lakes may be their circulating fluid. The living mat may be skin. But the system contains no clear center and responds to injury in many places at once, often before the injury occurs. It may be one being whose organs have forgotten their unity, or countless beings whose interdependence has become indistinguishable from a body.

At the dimming of the sky, every inhabitant participates in the buried light. Hooves disturb luminous sap. Worms interrupt it. Towers hold it in their cisterns and release it as slow pulses. Predatory moss bends the lines around its waiting meshes. Even flying creatures are traced by sympathetic flickers below, as if the land carries a second, submerged migration.

When the pulses form lungs, the quillbacks stop grazing.

When they form the vanished coastline, surge rooks leave the valves and face outward.

When they form the other pattern—the one for which no animal possesses a sense large enough—movement ceases across the plateau. Threadnecks withdraw their heads. False lakes remain open. Condensers turn without noise. Cloud combers press themselves flat against the overcast.

All life looks toward the same empty place.

Something there may once have lived in the high country. Something may live there still, so completely eroded past body that only the behavior of everything else preserves its shape.
