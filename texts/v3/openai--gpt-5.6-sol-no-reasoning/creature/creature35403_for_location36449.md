What lives there is not a population but a single life distributed beyond recognition.

It has no trunk, no heart, no privileged center from which the rest receives command. Its body is the high country entire: the plateaus its spread limbs, the column-trees its load-bearing bones, the reservoirs its temporary stomachs, the buried rivers its circulation. What appears to be a grove, a bridge, or a freestanding tower is only a local organ briefly distinguished by shape and function. Boundaries between individuals are provisional. Roots fuse. Vines exchange sap. Fallen structures wake elsewhere carrying fragments of old pressures, old chemical instructions, perhaps old weather.

If it can be called a creature, it is a creature whose anatomy is architecture.

It lives slowly in some dimensions and violently in others. For days it may seem occupied only by water, each surface trembling under rain while nothing visibly changes. Then tension passes through it and an entire district acts at once. A plaza folds. A tower opens. A bridge chooses several directions. These are not decisions as animals make them, but neither are they random. The country senses through strain, saturation, light, resonance, and damage. Pressure is its touch. Shade is its sight. Chemistry is taste and memory. Sound enters it as force and becomes structure.

Its smallest active bodies serve as the organs of movement once supplied by animals. The walking seed pods are patient couriers, carrying sealed portions of the whole across wet courts. Feeling vines are scouts and surveyors. They rise, sway, test heat and distance, then lay themselves down as the first line of a future road. Pollen moves through internal ducts rather than open air, dispatched in pulses from grove to grove. Nothing needs wings. Nothing needs legs for long. The landscape lends mobility to whatever part requires it.

There are no predators because the country consumes only itself.

Pitchers, digestive pits, and hot fermenting basins receive bark, exhausted leaves, malformed buds, abandoned chambers, and bridges that have become structurally expensive. These organs are less mouths than reclamation furnaces. They dissolve yesterday’s design and return it to circulation. A tower may therefore contain the substance of a former reservoir; a stair may be grown from a failed causeway; the clear tissue of a new wall may once have been miles of green pavement. Death exists, but not disposal. Nothing leaves the organism except water, heat, drifting propagules, and the rare mass that falls beyond recovery.

The sparse column-trees are its great civic beings—or would appear so to any mind inclined to separate them from the ground. Each governs an enormous district through roots and hydraulic influence. Their crowns, loose in calm weather, become sails and pressure gauges in storms. They register wind across immense ribbed fans, translating gusts into twists along their trunks and from there into changes in the surrounding floor. A column does not merely support its plateau. It teaches the plateau what the sky is doing.

Some columns may be older than the surfaces around them, though none is old in any settled sense. They repeatedly consume and rebuild their own outer bodies. Their bark loosens into broad mobile skins, sails away, and roots as lesser structures. Thus every great pylon is also a dispersing colony, shedding possible neighborhoods whenever wind and tension agree.

The groves are temporary concentrations of specialized thought.

The bowl-grove thinks in overflow. Each leaf-cistern measures the excess of the one above it, and together they calculate rain by passing it downward. The red root cage thinks in heat and draft, adjusting its shaft to the breath of the lower layers. The dawn-opening dome thinks in light, spreading streets to increase exposure, then sealing itself at dusk to retain water and warmth. None understands the whole country. Each performs one pattern so completely that, linked with thousands of others, it produces something resembling intention.

The transparent leaves carpeting the open courts form its most sensitive skin. Individually they are almost nothing: a clear blade, a hinge, a pore, a threadlike nerve of sap. Together they read the sky. They flatten under hard light, sink before rain, rise after it, and pass changes in pressure across the plateau in shining waves. A shadow cast repeatedly over them would not be forgotten. They would begin to accommodate it—first by leaning away, then by thickening its margins, eventually by raising a roof over the expected darkness.

Were an animal to arrive, the place would learn its outline without knowing it was alive.

Footfalls would become roads. Resting places would deepen into alcoves. A habitual call would cultivate resonant chambers until the plateau repeated it after the caller had gone. Waste would be absorbed; shed hair, skin, bone, and breath would enter the recycling tissues. Shelter would gather around sleep. In time the traveler might mistake the landscape’s accommodations for hospitality, though the city would be responding no differently than it responds to rain: by turning recurrence into anatomy.

Its memory is everywhere and nowhere. A gale survives as asymmetrical bracing. Drought persists as thickened reservoirs and narrowed pores. Lightning remains in a lineage of white conductive tendrils. Collapses are remembered by arches that grow around their echoes. Because its memory is structural, recollection changes the thing that recollects. It cannot remember an event without becoming better suited to that event and often worse suited to another.

This is the source of its restless youth. It has accumulated responses but not wisdom. Each solution is local, extravagant, and literal. Wind from the west produces western buttresses until eastern strain becomes dangerous. A resonant boom produces walls around the boom, even if the sound is only an echo of an echo. Rain in one district raises barriers in another because pressure, not meaning, carries the message. Its errors are enormous, beautiful, and sincere.

Beneath the visible plateaus lives the darker half of it.

There, in warm cloud and perpetual runoff, hang the roots that do not anchor so much as search. Some are fine as hair and spread nets through mist. Others descend as blind columns, thickening whenever they encounter sustained moisture. Bladders inflate in the gloom and squeeze water upward. Pale curtains collect minerals from airborne dust. Knotted organs listen to the groaning weight above and answer by growing toward stress.

The deepest probes may never touch ground. They lengthen through cloud, branch, wither, and are consumed. Their purpose is not to reach a buried earth remembered by ancestral roots. The organism may have no memory of soil at all. Downward growth is simply what moisture, gravity, and inherited form demand. It builds foundations into emptiness because foundation-building is part of what it is.

Its reproductive bodies are the falling districts.

When an edge fails, the break is already seeded with contingencies. Bark spreads into wings. Root bundles loosen into streamers. Reservoir skins become buoyant sails. The fragment descends, but descent is converted into travel. If it strikes another height, its torn surfaces fuse. If it lodges in the inverted forest below, it becomes a hanging annex. If warm air holds it aloft long enough, it may unfold independently: first a net to catch mist, then a knot to retain water, then a flat green plane. A new plateau begins as wreckage that refuses to remain wreckage.

Thus the being has no clear distinction between injury and birth.

It does not watch the altered skyline at morning. It possesses no image of itself. Yet throughout its vastness, water levels equalize, fibers pull, dissolved signals spread, and chambers answer chambers. Something like awareness may exist in those correspondences: not a gaze directed outward, but an immense bodily present composed of weight, wetness, temperature, illumination, and sound.

Its nearest equivalent to dreaming occurs during the stillness after storms. Construction pauses. Fresh tissues glisten. Pressure waves diminish through the lattices. Old thunder moves through distant tubes, weak but not extinguished. In that interval the country is full of delayed causes. A vibration arrives years late. A reservoir answers rainfall that fell leagues away. A buried joint tightens because a tower has opened to the sun. The whole organism rearranges itself around events it cannot distinguish as past, present, near, or far.

Then some threshold is crossed.

Leaves turn their pale undersides upward. Water quivers in cups. The structural organs draw breath—not air alone, but tension, sap, heat, and accumulated weather. Across miles, joints lock and release.

The creature resumes becoming its next body.
