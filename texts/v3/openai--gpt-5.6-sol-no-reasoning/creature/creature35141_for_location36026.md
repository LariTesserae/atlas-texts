The people who live beneath the ice become, by necessity, quiet people in the loudest place imaginable.

They inhabit the scattered stations of the basin: the pumping house, the transformer shelf, the ventilation towers, the relay sheds, and the reinforced dormitories buried behind them. No settlement is large enough to resemble a town. Each is an island of heated metal with its own stores, filters, emergency batteries, and narrow rooms lined against vibration. Windows are uncommon. Those that exist look not onto daylight but onto pallid leaves, pipes furred with mineral ice, and the lower portions of roots whose tops disappear beyond the lamps.

Most inhabitants are engineers, drill crews, rail operators, hydrologists, electricians, and plant-cutters. Their clothing makes them look heavier than they are: layered thermal suits beneath hard shells, face seals against cold dust and spores, luminous bands at the shoulders and wrists. Colour is practical below. Red means blasting or structural danger; blue marks water and pressure systems; amber belongs to rail movement; green indicates a route judged safe, though green light lends the surrounding plants an unsettling appearance of health.

They learn to speak with their bodies. A hand held flat means wait. Two fingers tapped against the chest ask whether a message was received. Lamps mounted near the collar blink brief coded phrases, while wrist units deliver knocks and pulses directly into the bones. Spoken language survives indoors, but even there people tend to face one another closely and watch mouths. Long after leaving the basin, some continue to gesture before answering a question.

Their sense of time is shaped less by day and night than by shifts, maintenance cycles, supply trains, and the behaviour of machinery. Drill shutdowns are the nearest thing the basin has to dawn. When the vibration ebbs, sleepers wake even if their alarms have not sounded. Doors open. People step outside simply to experience the immense residual silence, although the turbines and pumps continue elsewhere. Then the reproductive pods begin to split among the roots. Spores drift through the lamp beams, and crews fit filters over their faces while standing still to watch.

They know the vegetation not as scenery but as infrastructure, hazard, calendar, and neighbour. A folded leaf can empty a work site faster than an electronic alarm. The tension in a runner reveals where the floor has shifted. Root tissue packed into wall cavities provides excellent insulation, provided it is killed first and checked for boring insects. Old workers can tell from the sheen of a mat whether it covers rock, water, or a hollow. They tap columns before approaching them after a blast and listen through contact sensors for hidden splitting.

A distinct trade has developed around keeping the forest and the works apart without destroying either. The cutters are botanists, arborists, and demolition hands at once. They clear rails, free doors, trim roots away from cable housings, and graft living fibre over unstable sediment. Their saws have heated teeth, but pale sap still freezes along the guards. They carry wedges to control the fall of severed tissue and shields against splinters from vibration-hardened columns. Most bear narrow white scars where frozen fibres slipped through seams in their protective clothing.

Some workers distrust the plants. Others attribute intentions to them. They say the columns lean toward generators because they like company, or that the floor mats hide discarded tools out of spite. They leave scraps of mineral cake near newly cut runners, though everyone knows the plants cannot consume them directly. A few touch the waxy leaves before beginning a dangerous shift. Such gestures are denied when formally mentioned.

The oldest inhabitants remember when the basin was barer. They recall exposed sediment around the original pumping house and unobstructed views between survey beacons. Their maps show roads that now pass beneath root embankments and buildings whose roofs alone emerge from the mats. Younger workers find these recollections difficult to believe. To them, the forest is as fundamental as the ice.

Human families are rare but not unknown. Children raised below are adept at vibration codes before they can read. They are taught never to run toward a moving light, never to hide under pipes, and never to step across a rail without placing a gloved palm against the beacon plate. They draw the ceiling as black rather than blue and often depict surface trees as pale columns without branches. The first open sky can frighten them: too bright, too distant, and offering no echo.

Around and beneath the human settlement lives a thinner population.

White crustaceans crowd the warm drainage channels, feeding on bacterial films and whatever the kitchens release through their filters. Blind insects cling to the undersides of leaves, translucent enough that their moving organs show through their bodies. They respond to vibration more than scent. Before blasting, they retreat into the pleats of the wall growth. Afterward they emerge in slow numbers, grazing over fresh mineral dust.

The reservoir fish are larger and less certain. Sightings describe narrow, colourless bodies longer than a person’s arm, with reduced eyes and dark sensory lines along the flanks. Cameras have recorded wakes and once a pale curve passing below the pipe bridge, but no specimen has been recovered. Some believe they entered as eggs in drilling water. Others think they descend through temporary channels in the ice. The pumping crews insist that the oldest fish was already there when the first reservoir lights were installed.

Yet the principal inhabitant is the forest itself.

What appears to be a multitude of columns, runners, sheets, and leaf mats may be less divided than it seems. Fibrous bridges pass under sediment and around foundations. Chemical changes introduced at one drainage channel appear months later in growth several kilometres away. A damaged runner seals itself while distant mats thin, as though material were being redirected through an unseen body. Researchers disagree whether the basin contains millions of plants, a few vast colonies, or one organism repeatedly fragmented by stone and machinery.

Its great root columns are not trunks in the ordinary sense. They are composite pillars built around conduits of warm fluid and dense bacterial tissue. Their outer layers harden where the drills shake them, ring after ring of pale cellulose laid down in response to stress. Some are hollow. Others contain black, warm cores that smell of iron and wet stone. They rise toward seepage, heat, and dissolved minerals, branching somewhere above the reach of ordinary lamps into thin membranes pressed against the ice.

The low leaves perform many tasks at once. They collect condensation, trap dust, exchange compounds with bacteria, and feel minute changes in pressure. Their folding is neither alarm nor fear, but it has saved enough lives to be treated as warning. When the ceiling shifts, the impulse travels across the floor in darkening bands as millions of leaves close edge to edge.

The forest’s slowest movements are also its most consequential. It enters cable trenches after their heat has soaked through the ground. It curls around pilings and fills the voids beneath rail beds. It reinforces some foundations and prises others apart. It absorbs abandoned structures without haste, first veiling paint, then covering bolts and seams, then drawing sheets of tissue over entire walls. Inside derelict relay sheds, leaves grow across desks in perfect pale planes. Cabinets become warm cavities for bacterial colonies. Wires persist longest, running like black veins through the new growth.

Neither the humans nor the forest can wholly withdraw. Without industrial warmth, much of the vegetation would contract or become dormant. Without the mats, sediments would slump into channels, drainage would choke with mineral precipitate, and foundations would shift more rapidly. The arrangement is not harmony. It is dependence produced by proximity, maintained through cutting, leakage, adaptation, and repair.

So the basin is inhabited by a coupled life: workers who tend machines, organisms that tend the altered ground, bacteria that feed the plants, and small blind creatures that graze upon the excess. Even the disputed fish belong to this chain, if they exist.

Above them, the ice descends by fractions.

Below it, the drills continue.

And between those pressures, human and vegetal bodies endure by becoming sensitive to the same things: heat, vibration, fracture, water, and the rare arrival of silence.
