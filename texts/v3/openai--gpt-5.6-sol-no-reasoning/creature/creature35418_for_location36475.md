The city itself lives there.

Not as a single creature neatly contained by the basin, but as a colony whose buried membranes, mineral shells, exhausted chambers, and advancing edges together form an organism. Its living tissue threads the porous rock below the streets, drawing energy from migrating ions and the difference between subterranean warmth and the cold atmosphere. The visible city is what this life leaves around itself: walls where filaments converge, towers over reproductive knots, avenues along stress fractures, and plazas over ground already consumed.

It inhabits its own remains. As its active margins migrate, hardened chambers are abandoned, split open, hollowed, or reclaimed. Thus the colony’s body is partly alive tissue and partly architecture: a shifting accumulation of casts, scars, husks, and mineral excretions. Its growth requires emptiness. When construction becomes too dense, it starves foundations until entire districts collapse and new open courts appear. It also refuses sealed interiors, breaking every closed chamber back into contact with the atmosphere.

Upon this greater life grow the gray blades. Rooted in wall faces, they stand as bundles of hollow, chambered needles. They feed at dusk on traces of reactive gas, sealing their pores by morning beneath another transparent mineral layer. They are almost geological in patience, but reproduce catastrophically, whole clusters splintering into electrostatically mobile shards.

At the feet of towers live black rosettes. These do not seek light. They read strain in the city’s shell, rotating their scales toward fractures before they occur. They are its dark weather vanes, responding to pressures still invisible in the stone.

Inside the hollow walls move ash-pale crystal grazers, each no longer than a fingernail. Their bodies contain little that could be called fluid: they are articulated arrangements of wax, salt, and conductive thread. They scrape new mineral from growing surfaces and walk in measured bursts, emerging into the streets when the green radiance rises from below. In procession they look less like animals than loose fragments of metal answering a magnetic field.

The basin is also visited—or inhabited—by a solitary tall grazer. It has no apparent head and carries its skeleton outside itself: a narrow cage of black rods bound by flexible white joints. By vibrating beside the gray blades, it draws out whatever lives within them, leaving their forms intact but empty. It passes through openings too narrow for its visible frame and sometimes vanishes behind structures incapable of concealing it. Whether there is one such animal or many widely separated individuals is unclear. Its circuit may predate every street now standing.

Farther out, other vertical forms punctuate the salt-field. They may be young colonies, dormant propagules, or reproductive spires shed by this one. If so, what lives in the basin is only a local body of something dispersed across the plain: an archipelago of buried organisms, each slowly raising an empty city above itself.

Nothing here inhabits buildings in the ordinary sense. The buildings are metabolism made visible. The streets are separations the colony needs in order to continue. The rooms are temporary organs opened to the poisonous air. Even vacancy is alive with function.

What lives there is a city that must forever build, abandon, fracture, and unbuild itself—and the few dry creatures adapted to feed upon its unfinished body.
