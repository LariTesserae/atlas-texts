What lives there is not a collection of separate species so much as a mechanical ecology: a kingdom of self-assembling mineral animals whose bodies resemble engines because engines are simply the most useful shapes available to matter.

The great mills dominate it. They are slow, hollow-centered organisms built from rings, drums, vanes, pistons, furnaces, and flywheels grown rather than forged. Their metal is secreted, their ceramic is shell, their trailing wires are nerves, and the gases glowing inside their bladders serve as blood, breath, and stored energy. Some are no larger than carts; others spread across whole districts of the plateau. They move almost imperceptibly, yet in aggregate form migrations broad enough to be mistaken for geological change.

Around and beneath them live countless lesser kinds. Gear-mites cluster at the borders between hot and cool stone. Chrome-scaled crawlers sift dust crystal by crystal. Hinged foil swarms harvest light and static, vanishing against the surfaces they reflect. Needle-bodied animals drink voltage from the thin air. Bellows herds descend into invisible gas lakes, inhale their stratified atmospheres, and release the separated components elsewhere. Burrowing rotors follow stress lines through the crust, feeding on tension and leaving placid stone behind.

These creatures do not eat substances alone. They eat disparity. Heat differences, pressure boundaries, electric potentials, magnetic imbalances, chemical concentrations, structural strain—even slight asymmetries in time, distance, and likelihood—all attract them. A gradient is scent, pasture, and prey. Their metabolism converts unevenness into rotation, heat, stored charge, growth, courtship, and migration.

For all their mechanical appearance, they behave unmistakably as animals. They startle. They hesitate. Their pistons recoil from sudden shade; their exhaust organs turn toward unfamiliar noises. They court by bringing their cycles into synchronization until two bodies sound as one immense instrument. They fight by meshing their rims and stealing one another’s momentum, struggles so slow they may outlast generations. The defeated animal does not rot. It cools and is disassembled by scavengers, each useful bearing, plate, cable, and crystal entering another body.

No individual form is permanent. A component may be locomotion in one creature, armor in the next, and part of a jaw centuries later. The true continuity of life lies in the circulation of mechanisms.

The largest social structures are fear-colonies. When the herds detect a predator, thousands of mills gradually converge and lock together. Their coupled bodies become something like a refinery city: towers of stacked animals, braided nerves, shared transmissions, incandescent chimneys, and resonant chambers capable of projecting a stone-breaking note across the horizon. Such a city has no architects and contains no buildings. Every wall is alive; every tower can, given enough time, detach and walk away.

The predators are unlike the rest. They possess no dependable anatomy. Each is a mobile omission, a region in which other creatures cease not merely to live but to have lived. Its approach is marked by a traveling silence in the plateau’s universal roar. Behind it lies suspiciously pristine ground, scrubbed even of tracks, remains, and historical wear. The mills sense these beings years or decades before contact, perhaps as an approaching gradient of nonexistence. Migration streams bend away long before the emptiness arrives.

If escape becomes impossible, the living cities assemble. Sometimes their united resonance repels the omission. Sometimes mill, city, struggle, and evidence all disappear, leaving only sound behind: an echo detached from its erased cause, repeating across the plateau for centuries.

The ecology also contains creatures for which ordinary space is only one habitat. At feeding grounds where reality has become uneven, immense herds gather around differences too subtle for conventional senses: a preference in direction, a thickness in elapsed time, a fractional excess of existence. Their organs enter folds invisible from outside and return duplicated, abraded, or rearranged. Long proboscises pump from empty air. Rotating parts disappear halfway through each revolution. Some animals may occupy overlapping versions of the plateau at once, grazing the misalignment until the worlds coincide again.

They are therefore more than inhabitants. They are custodians of consistency. By consuming small contradictions, they prevent those contradictions from widening into boundaries between incompatible realities. Their ceaseless industrial thunder is metabolism on the scale of natural law: the sound of space being kept continuous, time being kept directional, and the plateau being persuaded to remain one place.

Yet this work is instinctive rather than benevolent. The mills do not preserve the world for anything else. They preserve it because inconsistency is nutritious.

A visitor would be understood in the same terms. Body heat would attract thermal grazers. Salt, water, and organic molecules would draw chemical sorters. Neural voltage would summon needle swarms. Shadow would create a boundary worth harvesting. The smallest creatures would arrive first and arrange themselves along the visitor’s outline, seams, vessels, and nerves.

They would not attack with recognizable violence. They would equalize.

Over weeks or months, heat would be carried away, charge drained, compounds separated, structures loosened, and each element distributed into the impoverished plateau at the concentration most compatible with its surroundings. The mills would process a body with the patience they apply to a warm boulder or a pocket of gas. Nothing would hate it. Nothing would hurry. Eventually the distinction between visitor and landscape would become too slight to support further feeding.

That is what lives in the high country: wheels that graze sunlight, furnaces that migrate, cities assembled from panic, scavengers that inherit machinery, and predators shaped like revisions to history. Together they form a biosphere devoted to the abolition of difference.

Every grain is inspected. Every imbalance is pasture. Every anomaly, including life itself, is eventually smoothed toward silence.
