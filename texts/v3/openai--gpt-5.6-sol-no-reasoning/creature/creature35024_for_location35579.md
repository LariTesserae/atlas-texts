They are a small people—not childlike, though a stranger seeing them among the compressed doors and low ceilings might first mistake them for such. Generations of narrow passages have favored slight frames, flexible shoulders, careful feet. They stand without occupying more room than necessary. Even at rest, their elbows remain near their ribs; when two meet on a stair, they turn together in a practiced, almost graceful exchange, each briefly becoming the other’s shadow.

Their skin tends toward the pallor of things grown under stone, though cheeks and knuckles are stippled red by cold. Hair is worn close or braided flat against the head so it will not brush leaves, stir dust, or catch in ladders. They dress in layered felt, rabbit wool, woven nettle, softened hide. Their clothes are patched so finely that the repairs form maps: pale islands, branching seams, concentric borders around an old tear. Buttons are horn, shell, knotted cord, sometimes a smooth seed passed down long after the plant that bore it has vanished.

Nothing on them swings loose. Keys, knives, spoons, needles, and small personal charms are tied close to the body. Their soles are felted. Their sleeves narrow at the wrist. Even mourners wear no jewelry that can strike stone.

Their faces seem composed in the city’s manner: attentive, reserved, altered by minute expressions rather than broad ones. A lifted finger may be greeting, warning, gratitude, or a request to wait, depending on its angle. Displeasure tightens one corner of the mouth. Affection is shown by touching brow to brow, sharing the warmth of breath for an instant. Laughter exists, but is breathed through the nose or pressed into a companion’s shoulder. Outsiders sometimes think them joyless until they learn to notice how frequently their eyes crease.

They speak, though seldom across distance. Their language is soft and densely abbreviated, full of clipped syllables made at the front of the mouth. In close chambers it is accompanied by signs: one-handed gestures for water, rot, frost, mouse, ladder, stranger, collapse. Two fingers drawn downward mean a fresh drip has appeared. A thumb enclosed in the fist means a birth. An open palm laid flat means the earth has shifted and everyone must become still.

Names are rarely called. Each person has a spoken name for use at arm’s length and a gesture-name visible across a court. The gesture may refer to a gait, a craft, an old accident, or the shape of a person’s favored tool. These signs change during life. The dead retain the last one they were given.

Children are raised by rooms as much as by parents. In so compact a place, no infant’s hunger belongs to only one household, and no dangerous curiosity goes unwitnessed. Babies sleep in padded wall niches above the warmest compost chambers. Toddlers are tethered lightly at the waist near steep ladders, not as punishment but as casually as a pot is secured on a shelf. They learn the geography of dripping water before they learn ancestry. They know which stair complains under weight, which moss may be stepped on, which pale mushroom causes sleep, and which cracks exhale air from chambers no one enters.

Their first discipline is catching. Adults drop wooden cups without warning, then spoons, then eggshell bowls filled with water. A child who catches one receives no praise beyond a quiet touch at the nape. A child who misses helps reclaim every drop. By seven or eight, most can interrupt a falling object with hand, foot, basket, or fold of clothing before it strikes the floor.

They play silently and ingeniously. They build entire districts from shells beneath benches, reproducing real houses with uncomfortable accuracy. They race droplets along carved grooves by warming the stone with their fingers. They keep silver beetles in pierced boxes and wager on which will emerge first when the lid is lifted. One game consists of hiding a polished pebble somewhere in the city and indicating its location only by moving household objects on a threshold. Adults pretend not to understand the game, though they use the same grammar in disputes.

Work is not divided by wealth so much as by patience, steadiness, and memory. Those with the keenest hearing tend cisterns and listen for hidden leakage. Those whose hands remain warm prune roots and nurse seedlings. Broad-backed people move stone, though never alone. The near-sighted become fine menders, shell-setters, or readers of softened inscriptions. Elders who can no longer climb sit where several passages meet and serve as living maps. They remember which wall is also a chimney, which pantry ceiling supports a sleeping alcove, whose floor must be warned before a peg is driven.

No one owns a wall outright. A wall may belong to one family for hanging shelves, another for warmth, a third for structural care, and everyone for silence. Water belongs first to the vessel that catches it, then to the room, then to the city. Fruit belongs to whoever tends the branch until ripening, but the first and last fruits are always shared. Empty space is treated as a resource more precious than bronze. A family may possess a bowl for generations and still require permission to leave it in a passage.

Their leaders are difficult for outsiders to identify. There is no raised seat, emblem, guard, or public proclamation. Authority settles on those who can keep many interlocking claims in mind without enlarging one at the expense of the rest. Decisions are made by rearranging counters—seeds, stones, vertebrae, fragments of colored pottery—on a square of felt. Speakers move a counter and whisper. Silence follows. Another moves it back or places something beside it. A meeting may last all night and produce only the relocation of a ladder, yet that ladder may alter light, water, access, inheritance, and the growth of a tree for five households.

Punishment is likewise spatial. Someone who has taken too much water may lose the right to place jars beneath a reliable drip. Someone habitually careless with noise may be assigned work in the outer root chambers, where sound is absorbed by soil. The gravest sentence is not exile to the field, which would be considered a swift form of death, but unplacing: the removal of one’s tokens from communal thresholds. People stop making room. In a city where every life depends on others turning sideways, this is almost unbearable.

Still, they are not uniformly gentle. Quietness can conceal cruelty as easily as grace. Feuds endure because they are never allowed the relief of shouting. A cup placed mouth-down may reopen an insult five generations old. Families cultivate rival strains of berries, rival cures for lung fever, rival interpretations of a crack in the western shaft. Sabotage, when it occurs, is minute: a wick shortened, a graft loosened, a basin shifted a finger’s width away from its drip. Murder is rare, but accidents are easy to arrange in a place made of stairs.

The oldest inhabitants are called the Settled, though the title is not granted merely by age. A Settled person has ceased resisting the city’s repetitions. They wake before the same drop falls, reach toward shelves before a jar begins to slip, and pause at crossings for people not yet visible. Their daily routes become so regular that moss grows differently beside them. Birds allow them near ancestral nests. Frogs remain at the surface when they bend over cisterns.

Some of the Settled remember events that happened before their births. They do not claim visions. They simply correct a young mason’s account of a repair performed two hundred years earlier, naming the flaw in a stone that has never been exposed. They know lost gesture-names. They hum fragments of songs no one sings because sustained notes are forbidden. Asked how, they touch the wall.

They treat the animals less as possessions than as fellow occupants with narrow civic duties. Mice receive grain dust in exchange for cleaning cracks. Beetle towers are moved from crop to crop according to flowering. Snails consume peelings and become food, medicine, glue, and shell inlay. Cistern frogs are spoken to when water is drawn; their pigment marks identify not ownership but responsibility. If a household frog dies, that family must examine the water, the glaze, and its own recent conduct before receiving another.

The birds are accorded a more uneasy respect. Anything that leaves and returns is regarded as knowing two worlds. Children are warned never to stare into a bird’s eyes at the mouth of a light shaft, lest it mistake their gaze for open sky and carry something of them upward. When a swallow fails to return in spring, its nesting niche is left untouched for a full year.

Death creates no cemetery. There is nowhere to spare, and burial beneath the city would disturb foundations. Bodies are washed with condensation collected over several days, wrapped in old household felt, and carried at night through the crack between the boulders. On the field, where the wind can be painfully loud, the dead are laid in shallow stone-lined places beneath the grass. Their bones, after weather and small creatures have done their work, are brought back one at a time.

A finger bone may become a needle handle. A vertebra may serve as a meeting counter. Teeth are set into the hidden side of lintels, where they are believed to help a house remember its inhabitants. None of this is considered desecration. To leave useful matter outside would be the greater disrespect.

Yet not every bone returns.

Sometimes a body taken into the field is gone by morning, though the frost around it is unmarked. Sometimes, years later, a face above a doorway acquires a familiar slope to its cheek despite no one having recarved it. Families seldom speak of this. They touch the softened stone and continue past.

Their religion, if it can be called that, concerns fit rather than obedience. They do not imagine the world as made for them. They believe everything persists by settling into the shape allowed by everything else. Virtue is good joining. Sin is pressure transferred carelessly. A lie is condemned not because it is false in the abstract, but because someone will build upon it and be injured when it shifts.

The closest thing to prayer is adjustment. A wedge tapped beneath a shelf. A vine redirected around a nest. A sleeping child moved so condensation does not fall on its face. Small offerings are placed where different systems meet: at root and wall, ice and mica, roof and field, birth bed and grave cloth. Usually the offering is a twist of hair, a berry skin, a drop of milk, or a breath held briefly in a cupped hand and released into a crack.

They do not worship the Still Pool. Worship would imply distance. Instead, they consult it.

At certain intervals—some say yearly, though no calendar agrees—the inhabitants gather around it in descending order of age. No one speaks. Each household places a single object near the rim: a broken tool, a seed, a tooth, a fragment from a repaired wall. The blind shrimp make their three circuits and cross through the center. If one deviates, even slightly, plans for the coming season are changed.

A ladder is not built. A marriage is delayed. A chamber is emptied. Someone who intended to travel to the surface remains below.

The shrimp have not deviated within living memory.

There are, however, people who watch for it with dread, and others with hope.

These are usually the young, or those who have spent too long at the entrance breathing wind from the field. Surface-tenders climb out before dawn to trim grass around the hidden shaft mouths, clear snow without revealing geometry, gather seeds, and inspect the low stone humps where their dead lie. They alone know the enormousness above: weather arriving from nowhere, clouds with no supporting ceiling, light that casts hard-edged shadows, birds shouting at full volume.

Most surface-tenders return shaken and relieved. A few become enamored of distance. They begin leaving wider gaps between objects. They ask why a cracked stone must remain merely because it has settled. They wonder whether silence protects the city or keeps it from noticing its own confinement. They bring back forbidden things: a blue feather, a shard of foreign metal, a flower too tall to cultivate below.

The city makes room for such people if it can. It assigns them to the upper gardens or the entrance watch. It allows them to sleep beneath the light shafts. But occasionally one leaves across the field.

Those who depart are not pursued. Their niches are redistributed, their bowls adopted, their gesture-names held in reserve. Yet the relief by the Still Pool changes soon after. A tiny figure appears near the carved entrance, weathered and ancient, carrying a bundle toward the surface.

Sometimes the traveler returns after years, bent by wind and loud places. They find their old sleeping niche occupied by a child born in their absence. Their cup has become a medicine mortar. Their name-sign now belongs to someone else.

Still, a space is found.

It is always exactly large enough.

That is what troubles the most observant inhabitants: not that the city endures, nor that it seems to anticipate repairs, births, departures, and deaths, but that they themselves may be among the things fitted into it. Midwives notice that newborns arrive in numbers matching newly emptied alcoves. Gardeners discover unfamiliar herbs sprouting weeks before an unfamiliar illness. Craftspeople dream of tools, then find the necessary piece of antler already set aside beneath old cloth.

And some nights, when everyone is still, a person wakes with the certainty that somewhere nearby a stair is being worn down by feet not yet born.

They lie listening in the milk-pale dark.

Nothing moves. A frog rests beneath its marked stone. Mice breathe within the compost walls. Leaves hold their allotted drops. The blind shrimp wait under the eastern rim.

Then, from inside the wall beside the sleeper’s head, comes the faintest sound:

not scratching, not settling earth, but the soft, patient brush of a sleeve against stone as someone turns sideways to pass.
