The beings here are small, still, and easily mistaken for stains.

They live where the fired plain admits a little water: in hairline cracks, beneath lifted edges, around shaded pools, and deep inside the immense ceramic vaults. Most are photosynthetic colonies rather than plants in any familiar sense. Gray-green skins lie dormant against the tile for years, contracted to brittle films. Rain wakes them within moments. They swell into translucent lobes, deepen in color, and spread across the temporary sheets of water until evaporation folds them shut again.

Among these mats rise sparse threadlike growths and wax-coated cups. Fine acidic filaments secure them to microscopic flaws without making soil. Their pale inner bladders hoard condensation, while fans no broader than a hand open during the cool morning and seal themselves before noon. After exceptional storms they produce short-lived stalks and release dust-fine spores directly into the wind. They have no flowers, fruit, nectar, or scent meant for visitors. Their lives anticipate only heat, rupture, rain, and long dormancy.

The plain’s deepest life is less visible and far larger in effect. Mineral-binding colonies inhabit the warm buried cavities, feeding on dissolved compounds carried down after storms. They generate ceramic slurry and draw it upward through capillary seams. At night the substance wells onto the surface as dark glaze; by daylight it hardens. In this way the colonies close fractures, cushion shifting plates, and redistribute material around buckled surfaces. No individual directs the work. Collectively, however, they behave like the secretory tissue of an immeasurable organism whose shell has become the world.

There are no animals—not even insects, worms, or unseen scavengers. Nothing grazes the mats, pollinates the stalks, digs into the joints, or makes tracks beside the warm pools. What moves is moved by wind or water. What spreads does so by spores, fragmentation, pressure, and flood. Death does not summon feeders; dead growth dries, mineralizes, and becomes another thin contribution to the self-mending floor.

Whatever originally secreted the plain is gone. Its surviving inhabitants do not remember it, resemble it, or consciously continue its purpose. Yet they occupy the systems it left behind and perpetuate some fragment of its ancient metabolism. They wake when the world is wet, close themselves when it burns, and labor invisibly below. In their aggregate life, the empty construction still sweats, heals, and very slowly grows.
