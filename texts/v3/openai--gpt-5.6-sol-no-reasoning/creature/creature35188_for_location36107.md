The hollow is inhabited not by a community so much as by a single, crowded condition of life.

Its smallest creatures lie nearest the stone. Frogs fit the worn cups as if the rock had shaped itself around their bodies. Newts occupy the submerged weave of roots, and salamanders adhere to wet seams in the walls. Snails gather beneath leaves until their touching shells resemble pale clusters of fruit. Beetles lodge in bark; moths tile the undersides of branches; spiders keep empty webs stretched across the few remaining intervals. Nothing hunts or flees. Each creature holds the posture immediately before action, indefinitely.

Birds fill the upper layers: wrens, thrushes, finches, owls, and blunt-beaked mountain birds, all damp, wakeful, and silent. They perch so long that their claws have marked the wood. Their blinking is among the hollow’s largest motions.

The water is equally full. Minnows remain suspended among sedges. Eels rest coiled in peat pockets. Newts and larvae wait without changing form, while water beetles hold dim beads of air beneath their bodies. Eggs persist under leaves, neither spoiling nor opening.

In the larger chambers are mammals: a hare folded under ferns; three foxes tucked separately among the roots; a small stag standing in the deepest pool, moss established on its hide and fungi growing delicately from its antlers; and a bear seated nearby with joined paws, contemplating the black water. Predator and prey share the same suspension. They breathe, but appetite never becomes pursuit. They live, but life produces no growth, waste, birth, age, or death.

Fungi, algae, lichens, mosses, ferns, juniper, willow, and birch inhabit every surface left to them. They do not compete for more. Each has reached its allotted boundary. Even decay lives here without consuming: white fungal threads penetrate the drowned wood, but the wood never quite becomes soil.

Yet the hollow seems to reserve one inhabitant it does not possess.

Near the western wall lie stones shaped almost into tools, though no hand made them. Among the packed roots is room for two feet. Ferns curve around the absence of legs and hips. Birds line the contour of shoulders. At the height of a face, wet leaves and watchful eyes leave a narrow vacancy. The place appears arranged around a human-shaped omission, as though its true resident is not an animal already present but the next being to enter.

Perhaps the hollow itself is what lives there: a slow, collective organism composed of arrested creatures, saturated plants, old stone, and held water. Its many eyes are its attention; its breaths are the faint vapor along the ground; its roots and webs are its tissues. The animals are not trapped within it so much as incorporated into its stillness.

It waits without impatience, already shaped around the final addition.

When that last inhabitant arrives, nothing will need to seize or restrain them. There will simply be no unoccupied place into which a movement can continue.
