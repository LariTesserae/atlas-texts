The people of the tableland are spare, deliberate beings. They are human in the ordinary anatomical sense—two hands, two eyes, warm blood, soft organs—but the plateau has trained every unnecessary motion out of them. They tend to be narrow-bodied, with dense joints and broad, callused knees. Their skin is weathered not by sun or wind so much as by perpetual desiccation: fine pale cracks gather around the mouth and knuckles, sealed with carved animal fat. Hair is kept under felt wraps, and eyelashes are greased so they do not become brittle and break.

Their clothing is made in layers. The innermost cloth is soft hide worn until it conforms to a single body. Over it hang scales of shed ceramic tissue, laced loosely enough that they do not clatter. Each garment is intentionally irregular. One shoulder may carry three plates and the other five; hems end in unmatched lengths; cords are braided with a deliberate error. Perfect repetition is dangerous here. A symmetrical person may be mistaken by the country for two people, or by themselves for an image occurring elsewhere.

They seldom stand fully upright. Among the shell-creatures, height resembles declaration, and declarations can become permanent. Instead they walk bent slightly forward, watching the powder around their feet. Every few hundred paces they stop and adopt a remembered posture: one knee down, palm raised, cheek turned toward a landmark they do not yet expect to see. Then they wait. Sometimes the wait lasts minutes. Sometimes a companion must feed them while they remain posed for several days. Only when the landscape has settled around them do they continue.

They possess no profession corresponding exactly to guide. The nearest equivalent is a **halter**, one who knows where and how to stop. A skilled halter can lead a party across what appears to be a morning’s walk in seven pauses, or bring them to a shelter that has not stood in that location for generations. Their knowledge is kept in bodily sequences rather than words. Children learn routes as dances performed almost too slowly to perceive. An adult may carry hundreds of such dances in muscle and tendon, yet be unable to describe where any destination lies.

Their names are likewise postural. A person’s public name might be represented by a thumb pressed into the left palm, the head inclined, and one foot turned outward. Spoken names exist, but are used only at close range, since a sound can recur without its speaker. No one answers a voice heard from beyond sight until they have found the asymmetry by which the caller proves themselves.

Their communities are tiny because larger gatherings become difficult to distinguish from counting. Most households consist of three to seven adults, several children, and sometimes an elder whose exact relationship to the others has become uncertain. Kinship is established by remembered care rather than descent. One is the child of whoever taught one to chew shell-meal, keep still during dislocation, and look away from a multiplying aperture. Birth records are not kept. To record a beginning too precisely risks fixing it in more than one place.

Children are the quickest things on the plateau, and therefore the most closely watched. They arrive with the alarming instinct to run. For their first years they wear anchor cords trailing from padded belts. Adults attach these to pegs whenever the child sleeps, plays, or becomes excited. Games teach restraint: balancing a copper hook on one fingertip; crossing a patch of dust without changing its surface; holding the breath while an elder traces a route on the child’s back. The most important childhood achievement is called the **first remaining**—the moment a child is left alone among the upright fauna and succeeds in doing nothing until collected.

The plateau’s dominant creatures have no single name. The inhabitants refer to them through categories of use, posture, and danger: the sheltering ones, the almost-open ones, the bitter-ribbed, the double-hearted, the hunters facing inward. They do not assume these categories describe separate species. A shell classified as harmless for ten generations may also be the feeding limb of something whose body stands beyond the horizon.

Each visible form is only a local expression of a much larger life. Its gray casing is not dead armor but a load-bearing organ, grown from ceramic proteins and interlocking crystals. Beneath it lie membranes folded into enormous surface areas. There is no blood. Charge, vapor, and reactive dust migrate through crystalline boundaries, making metabolism less like circulation than weather passing through stone.

Their nervous systems are architectures of strain. Pressure accumulates in fibers, facets, and hairline faults. A decision may travel upward through a shell as a crack that takes twelve centuries to reach the next rib. Memory appears as altered angles in the crystal lattice. The oldest individuals are warped by thought: their columns lean without falling, their spirals tighten, and their apertures develop minute asymmetries where choices have been stored.

They perceive chiefly through duration. Sudden events are nearly invisible to them. A passing person may register only as a faint thermal imperfection, gone before sensation can form. A settlement, however, is vivid. A family that inhabits one hollow for centuries becomes, to the surrounding fauna, a warm and chemically active animal. The shell-creatures may feed it, avoid it, court it, or slowly attempt to pierce it.

This is why shelters are abandoned even when sound. If pale nodules begin appearing in a doorway, or a wall acquires a groove aligned with someone’s sleeping body, the residents leave. Such signs can indicate that a nearby creature has noticed them.

The coiled hunters are among the few animals whose intentions humans can recognize. They resemble springs made from nested ceramic blades, usually no larger than a hand, though some are wound around entire towers. At the center of each coil rests a hollow needle. The hunter chooses prey by orienting this needle, then begins to strike. Its lunge may take ten thousand years, but once initiated it is difficult to escape, because the strike defines its own eventual point of contact. Moving the prey does not always change where that point will be.

Hunters therefore identify dangerous needles by sighting along them with a split copper gauge. If the line passes through a shelter, cradle, or commonly used posture, the threatened thing must be altered beyond recognition. It is not enough to move a bed; it must be unmade, divided among households, and rebuilt asymmetrically. Occasionally a person learns that a needle is aimed at them. Such people take new names, scar one side of the face, and train themselves to sleep in an unfamiliar shape. Some succeed. Others disappear, leaving behind a clean puncture in a wall that had never stood near them.

The plate-creatures live more quickly. A scale may shift during a human lifetime. Colonies migrate across large shells in overlapping sheets, scraping trace metals from the ribs. Their passage produces subtle changes of color that the inhabitants use as calendars. Years are not counted, but a person may say they were born when the red plates reached the seventh fold, married while the fold was gray, and lost a sibling before it became white.

Glassy mites inhabit joints and apertures. They are nearly transparent, with legs arranged in radial clusters and bodies full of tiny inclusions resembling stars. When removed from a host they remain motionless, but their internal constellations change. Healers use them cautiously. A mite pressed against injured skin can persuade the body that the wound occurred long ago, closing it at once. It can also persuade the body that an old wound is new. No healer carries more than one.

The black vegetation is faster than the animals and simpler in appetite. It binds carbon from the thin air and constructs itself directly against warm stone. What look like stains are forests laid flat: branching trunks one cell thick, overlapping leaves of brittle graphite, roots that follow electrical gradients rather than moisture. Around great shells these growths flourish on leaked charge. The concentric halos mark long pulses in an animal’s metabolism, each ring corresponding to an event deep inside it.

People harvest only the outer rings. The innermost black growth is avoided because it has fed too intimately on the host and may reproduce aspects of its thought. Meal made from such growth induces a profound stillness. Those who eat it report awareness of pressure, sunlight, and distant mineral contact, but lose the ability to perceive brief events. A single night can pass unnoticed. Heavy consumption leaves a person standing among the shells, awake but unable to see anything that does not last a century.

The inhabitants have no rulers. Authority belongs temporarily to whoever can recognize the relevant danger. A halter governs a journey, a cutter governs a harvest, a heater governs the fire-disk, and a witness governs disputes over identity. Witnesses are people with exceptional memory for small inequalities: which eyebrow rises higher, which finger was broken in childhood, which boot bears an inward seam. When two versions of a person appear—and this does occur—the witness decides whether they are repetitions, divisions, or unrelated people who happen to share a past.

The distinction matters. Repetitions must not meet face to face. Divisions may divide possessions and kinship between them. Unrelated duplicates are treated courteously and sent away by different routes.

Death is not declared merely because a body has ceased breathing. On the plateau, cessation proves little. The dead are seated outside the shelter, tethered to three anchors, and observed through a full turning of the plate colonies. If no new asymmetry appears, the body is dismantled from its social identity: clothing unstitched, tools dispersed, name-posture reversed. The remains are then placed upright among the shell-creatures.

No graves are dug. A grave is a counted place containing a counted body, and either fact may destabilize the other.

Over centuries the remains dry and mineralize. Some become indistinguishable from the smaller fauna. This is not considered an afterlife, exactly. The people say only that the dead have become slow enough to stay.

Their oldest stories concern the Assembly. Some claim the interlocked giants are not a gathering but one creature repeatedly entering the plateau from different directions. Others hold that every shell on the tableland is an exposed organ of the Assembly, and that the inhabited country is merely its sensory surface. A less comforting tradition says the Assembly is a posture being adopted by the plateau itself.

People enter it for practical reasons—harvesting warm membranes, seeking old tools, learning routes from carvings—but no one sleeps there. The chambers respond too readily to occupation. A hand left against a wall may feel another hand pressing back from within, though the answering hand is larger, has too many joints, and takes hours to withdraw.

The carvings are studied by specialists called **readers of before**. They make rubbings with carbon powder on hide, never copying an entire image onto one sheet. Complete copies sometimes acquire the age of the original, becoming brittle before they are finished. Some carvings depict known human postures; some show settlements that have not yet been built. The figures with multiple vertical bodies are interpreted variously as families, shell-creatures, or humans represented at the pace the plateau perceives them: not as moving individuals, but as every place they stood during a lifetime.

Then there are those who tend the mirrors around the empty circle.

They are not priests. They clean copper with fine dust, repair the seats, replace the felt covers, and discourage visitors who come from curiosity alone. Each mirror has a subtly different reflection. In some, the faceless figures are clothed. In others they are naked, armored, translucent, or wrapped in cords extending beyond the circle. But from every seat one figure will eventually become recognizable.

Recognition seldom depends on a face. It may be the bend of a shoulder, an injured hand, a familiar way of carrying unseen weight. A visitor may recognize a dead parent, a missing child, a stranger encountered years later, or themselves in a posture they have never assumed.

The mirror-tenders offer no interpretation. They only ensure that the observer covers the copper before standing. An uncovered mirror with an empty seat sometimes continues to reflect an observer.

Those who recognize themselves are called **the regarded**. They are not shunned, but certain tasks are taken from them. They may no longer count rations, witness identities, or harvest from creatures near the circle. Many become halters, having acquired an unusual sensitivity to the way places arrive. A few eventually leave their settlements and build solitary shelters facing the Assembly. Over decades they grow increasingly still. Ceramic plates accumulate on their clothing. Black vegetation traces their sunward sides.

Whether they die, transform, or simply become recognized by a slower category of life is unknown.

The faceless beings in the mirrors may be the tableland’s true inhabitants. Perhaps the shell-creatures are only the forms their suspended actions take in ordinary light. Perhaps each ceramic body is a single gesture of one reflected figure: a hand almost closing, a spine bending, a mouth preparing to speak. The packed fauna and the packed reflection would then be two views of the same assembly, one reduced to anatomy, the other to intention.

The people do not favor this explanation over others. Explanations are a kind of counting.

What they know is narrower and more useful. The plateau is full. Every apparent emptiness is occupied from some direction. Every still thing is engaged in an act, however remote its completion. A person survives not by overcoming this slowness, but by learning to fit inside it—moving lightly, stopping completely, and never assuming that an unfinished motion has forgotten what it meant to do.
