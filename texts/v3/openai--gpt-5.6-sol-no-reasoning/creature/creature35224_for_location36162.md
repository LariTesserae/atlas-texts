The people of the Closeworks are small only by the standards of the machinery around them.

They are human, or near enough that visitors notice the differences late: after the heat has exhausted them, after their own voices have returned unexpectedly from a drain, after they have watched a work crew pass barefoot over rails too hot to touch. Closeworkers tend to be slight, long-fingered, and broad through the soles of the feet. Their skin carries the permanent sheen of the chamber, and black mineral dust settles in the creases of their hands like ink. Many have pale scars on their forearms from young rock, fine straight cuts made before they learned where not to brush against a wall.

They dress in layers despite the warmth. The innermost cloth is thin and absorbent; the outer pieces are loose, dark, and easily shed when moving between thermal districts. Sleeves tie above the elbow. Trouser legs narrow at the ankle so they cannot be taken by belts or gears. Nearly everyone carries gloves, though they are worn tucked through a sash more often than on the hands. A Closeworker prefers to feel a railing before trusting it.

Their clothes are full of small accommodations: loops for ceramic cups, chalk pockets, folding rulers, packets of salt, strips of felt for muffling a loose object. Children wear padded basket frames when gathering water sacs. Engineers and pipekeepers have white ceramic plates sewn over the backs of their knuckles, both protection and insignia. Those who work near the deepest faults wrap their feet in copper-colored cloth that shows black when certain mineral seepages have soaked through.

They do not hurry gracefully. Running is difficult where every passage contains a hot flange, a hanging root, or a neighbor’s kitchen. Instead they become skilled at continuous, economical motion: turning sideways without pausing, ducking before the beam, placing a hand on the correct rail, exchanging burdens in spaces too narrow to set anything down. Two residents meeting on a bridge may rearrange themselves around each other with the intricacy of a practiced knot.

Even their gestures are compact. A lifted finger asks for silence. Two fingers laid against a pipe mean listen. A palm turned outward warns of heat; downward, of uncertain flooring. Pointing is considered crude in inhabited shafts, where a gesture may seem to indicate three homes at once. Directions are given by temperature, material, and sound: past the cool tiles, under the second slow wheel, toward the gutter that rings in D.

They speak softly, but not timidly. Closework speech is precise and dry, crowded with distinctions that surface languages lack. There are separate words for water that can be drunk, water that can be cultivated in, water owed to a household, and water that has merely failed to kill anyone yet. Distance is often expressed in carrying terms: within bowl-reach, within cable-reach, within one careful breath. A room that did not exist on the last map is not called new. It is called available.

Every child learns the knock language early. Before letters, they learn to distinguish warning from ordinary labor: the double report of a hammer setting a rivet, the irregular chatter of a loose bracket, the deliberate three strokes of a leak signal. Lessons are conducted on old pipe offcuts laid across the knees. The finest speakers can send not only danger but location, severity, and requested tools through a wall. Some elderly mechanics can hold entire conversations in taps, though custom forbids using emergency patterns for gossip.

Keeping the stone shapes them more deeply than any law. Anger is not forbidden, but it must be contained. Public quarrels draw intervention before they grow loud, not because violence is expected, but because no one can know where the words will travel or when they will arrive. Insults have entered homes days after they were spoken. A proposal of marriage once issued from a chapel vent during a funeral. The story is funny now, but only because everyone concerned is dead.

Thus the padded rooms: small chambers lined with fungus felt, quilted cloth, cork, and old insulation. Families reserve them for arguments, confessions, negotiations, grief, and songs requiring a full voice. Children dare each other to scream inside and are startled by the deadness of the sound. To a Closeworker, silence without resonance feels less natural than darkness.

The dead are treated cautiously. Their bodies are not buried in the chamber, where space is active, water is communal, and walls cannot be trusted to remain walls. They are dried in warm upper rooms, wrapped in salt cloth, and taken through the cistern shafts to surface crematories. Their names may be spoken in sealed rooms, written in ledgers, or tapped into isolated plates that touch no wider system. Near open ducts, the dead are referred to by relationship or craft: my elder sister, the wheelwright, the keeper before this one. This is partly reverence and partly fear that a name carried through the rock might find a voice shaped like its owner.

The Closeworks supports no idle class, but it has many patient professions.

Condensers tend the cooled lines and harvest water before roots divert it. Fitters measure rooms anew before installing anything rigid. Leafcutters keep vegetation from entering bearings without killing more of it than necessary. Echo clerks test vents and record where voices emerge. Threshold masons specialize in houses that have shifted around their doors. Space assessors inspect new alcoves, deciding whether they are structurally sound, legally claimable, or geometrically suspect.

The most respected workers are not those who build fastest, but those whose work continues to fit.

A good bridge accepts that its landings may drift. A good pipe has enough bend to forgive the wall. A good home can lose a corner without losing its kitchen. Apprentices are warned against the surface vice of exactness. Precision is useful in a valve, they are told, but arrogance in a city.

New space belongs first to whoever discovers it, second to whoever can make it safe, and third to the city, with proportions argued according to old and contradictory statutes. Discovery is established by placing a lamp, sounding the walls, and fastening a dated tile at the entrance. This has led to feuds in which one household claims to have found an alcove while another insists that the alcove approached them.

Space assessors enter such openings in pairs. One carries a cord, the other a ledger. They never tie the cord directly around the body, since a passage may shorten behind them. They mark every few paces with wet clay and listen for machinery that should not be audible from that district. If they hear familiar voices, they turn back, especially if the voices are their own.

Most new spaces are disappointing: a wedge behind a cracked facing, a narrow chimney full of hot mineral vapor, a cavity large enough for storage but not habitation. Occasionally a chamber opens with a floor no tool has touched and walls that seem older than the fracture around it. Such places are immediately made ordinary. They are bricked, lit, taxed, and given some unromantic use—a laundry, a fern bed, a rack for spare couplings. This is not denial. It is defense. Closeworkers believe a space becomes trustworthy through obligation.

Their religion, where it can be separated from maintenance, concerns containment and passage. Shrines occupy obsolete cabinets because a sealed vessel that once held pressure is thought suitable for prayers. Offerings are practical: a cup of clean water, a polished bolt, a strip of cloth bearing a household’s heat stains. Saints are depicted holding plumb lines that bend, lamps that shine around corners, doors in walls without openings.

The central engine hall has no single priesthood. Pump crews perform its rites by keeping it operational. At the beginning of each long cycle, the senior operator places a bare hand on the warm casing and waits until the piston begins to descend. At the end, someone rings a small ceramic bell. The sound does not carry far, which is considered virtuous.

The blind silver fish is regarded with more unease than holiness. When one appears, even skeptics stop work. It is lifted in a glazed bowl and carried along a cleared route while residents stand on platforms, ladders, roofs, and suspended walkways to watch. No one taps during the procession. No names are spoken.

The fish is taken to the oldest cistern, though no one agrees why this is preferable. Some say it remembers water beneath the city. Some say it comes from space not yet opened. Others claim the fish is ordinary and the mystery lies only in the pipes. Afterward, the bowl is broken and its fragments entered in the civic archive. The person who first saw the fish receives no reward but is excused from dangerous work for one year, since they are considered to have already encountered enough improbability.

Children know the chamber through creatures before they understand its systems. The colorless pipe lizards are their first inspectors. A child who finds one drinks from the condensation line only after watching the lizard do so. They give the animals private names, although adults insist that this makes them harder to count. Shed skins are tucked behind gauges for luck.

The black moths are never touched. When they settle on an electrical housing, technicians reroute current if possible and work around them. Their presence makes lamps waver and batteries discharge in delicate, repeating pulses. Echo clerks have translated these fluctuations into no known code, but continue to record them. After the moths vanish, a soot-fine outline remains on the ceramic, showing veins more intricate than those visible in the living wing.

Food comes from the city’s constrained abundance. Fungus, fern shoots, algae paste, pale tubers grown in insulation beds, herbs trained along warm pipes, and the severe little fruits of the catwalk orchards form the ordinary diet. Surface grain is expensive. Meat is rarer and often imported smoked, because animals fare poorly below. Meals are strongly salted, sour, and spiced enough to survive the dulling effects of heat.

Kitchens are social because few are large enough to be private. A single stove may belong to six households stacked around one shaft. Pots travel vertically by hooks. Plates are passed across door gaps. People eat on thresholds, stairs, machine housings, and bridge landings, always leaving one hand free to steady themselves when a pump changes phase.

Water-sac season is celebrated despite having no fixed date. When enough translucent sacs ripen at once, children patrol the passages in quilted hats and basket frames, shrieking only in padded rooms and otherwise laughing through clenched mouths. The harvest is mostly drinking water with a green metallic taste. The largest sacs are hung over public basins and pierced ceremonially. Mechanics barricade their gearboxes and pretend to despise the whole affair.

Courtship is shaped by crowding. Privacy cannot be promised, so attention is shown through maintenance. A suitor oils the hinge beside another person’s bed, rehangs a crooked shelf, clears roots from the path to their door, or brings them a piece of cool ceramic during the hottest weeks. To offer someone a custom-cut object is an intimate gesture, because it assumes enough confidence in their shared future to risk exact dimensions.

Marriages are recorded by joining two household maps, never merely two names. The couple receives a flexible threshold of layered brass and leather designed to accommodate movement. Each year they scratch the doorway’s new position onto its frame. Some marriages produce a fan of marks. Others remain a single line, though no one agrees whether this indicates harmony or stagnation.

Closeworkers seldom leave. Those who go to the surface complain first of cold, then of distance, then of sound. Wind is intolerably loud. Open sky appears less like freedom than the absence of a ceiling where one is urgently needed. They distrust streets wide enough that a dropped bowl cannot be caught by a neighbor. Many return with headaches from sunlight and the unnerving conviction that nobody knows where they are.

Visitors, meanwhile, often mistake the inhabitants’ intimacy for kindness. The Closeworks is generous because generosity is infrastructural. A cup passed across a gap, a warning hand on a hot rail, a shoulder placed beneath a load—these are survival before virtue. The same people can be merciless over water rights, unauthorized roots, improperly muffled machinery, or a door extended three inches into shared passage.

Their politics are conducted through access. Whoever controls a stair, a pump schedule, a condensation line, or the seasonal map holds power. Government changes are rarely announced with speeches. A platform is reassigned. A lock receives a new key. A pipe once marked private is painted with the blue band of common use. Months later, the ledgers acknowledge that power has moved.

Yet authority remains vulnerable to geometry. A council may award a room and find by winter that it opens onto another district. A family may own both sides of a wall that has ceased to be the same wall. Tax collectors carry rulers, chalk, and a tolerance for metaphysics. Court cases sometimes end not with a judgment but with an order to wait one season and measure again.

Underlying everything is the sense that the chamber is unfinished in a way deeper than construction. The people do not speak of this often. They tile the fresh walls. They mount sliding brackets. They teach children never to follow a familiar voice into an unmarked corridor. They make inventories of doors.

But certain residents become attentive to the city’s slow additions. They are called listeners, though no office appoints them. They wake before an alcove opens. They pause beside blank stone and feel drafts where none can yet be measured. They dream of stairs with a different number of steps and spend the following weeks watching existing staircases for signs of agreement.

Listeners are neither revered nor trusted. Their warnings are recorded, dated, and ignored until useful. Many are ordinary workers who continue at their trades. One trims medicinal ferns. Another repairs ceramic battery jars. An elderly listener lives beneath the western dye house and refuses to use its impossible stair, not because one step is missing, she says, but because one has gone ahead.

On ordinary nights, these people fill the Closeworks without making it feel crowded to themselves. They sleep beneath stairs and above engines. They wake to the temperature of walls. They place cups under particular drops by memory. They hear a belt seam knock and know whether it belongs to their district. They know which foliage can be pushed aside, which rails will burn, which whispers are current and which have been traveling for years.

They live not merely inside the fracture but in negotiation with it. Every habit says: this space is known because we use it. This wall is real because we have tiled it. This distance belongs to us because we cross it carrying soup, tools, children, water.

And when, in the deepest part of the night, someone finds that a blank wall has become a recess large enough to stand in, they do not gather to marvel for long.

One person brings a lamp. Another brings a measuring cord. A third arrives with hinges.

By morning there is a door, and someone already lives behind it.
