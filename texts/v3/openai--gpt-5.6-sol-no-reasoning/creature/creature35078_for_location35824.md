The things that live there are not plants except by analogy. They are **lithophytes**: organisms whose mature body is only the visible, temporary portion of a geological metabolism.

Each stands as a solitary column of pleated vanes around an absence it cannot enter. What resembles a trunk is a stack of conductive mineral tissues, continually dismantled at its lower end and rebuilt above. Nothing anchors it. Its hovering filaments do not support its weight; they negotiate with the inaccessible center, maintaining a separation as exact as a law. The organism is held upright by charge, pressure, and geometry.

They have no individuals in the ordinary sense. The eleven—or nine, or fourteen—columns are phases of one distributed life. Each performs a different operation, and what passes among them is not food but unfinished matter. One purifies what another rejects. One stores structural errors in coils of metallic ribbon. One produces seedlike beads that never germinate, but sink into the newly made wall and become future ore. The oldest does not command the others. It keeps time.

Their apparent ages are also functions. The smallest columns are not necessarily young, nor the tallest old. A plant may spend ten thousand years as a low crown while carrying out a slow separation of isotopes, then rise several meters in a century when the mountain requires a particular stratum. Another may shed all its vanes and persist as a violet ring over the floor, neither dead nor dormant, until some stress thousands of years hence calls for its chemistry.

What they consume is impossibility.

From the center they draw substances that cannot survive consistent space: metals with contradictory weights, heat that flows toward heat, powders composed of absent grains. Taken raw, these would undo the chamber. The lithophytes admit them in increments smaller than causality can notice. Within their fronds, branching electric paths force each contradiction through successive compromises. Blue iron loses its color first, then its impossible valence, then the memory of having possessed either. Negative ash is paired with ordinary vapor until both agree to become sulfur. Crystals with premature facets are broken into angles that Euclidean stone can accommodate.

The broad leaves are where this correction occurs. Their pleats are not merely surfaces but sequences: every fold imposes another condition on matter. By the outer edge, the impossible has been made mundane. Waste products emerge as basaltic glass, quartz, nickel, and heat. To any later observer, the result will seem natural because naturalness is precisely what the organisms manufacture.

They reproduce rarely, and not by making more of themselves inside the pocket. At intervals measured in mountain-building events, one column forms a compact knot of layered crystal beneath its crown. The knot contains no embryo. It contains a tendency: a slight preference for emptiness to organize itself in this particular way. During the communal hum, the knot is projected outward and buried in rock that does not yet exist. It may remain there forever. If pressures, temperatures, and spatial failures align elsewhere, it unfolds—not into a plant, but into another sealed interval. Only afterward do the columns appear within it, as if they had always been waiting.

Thus their offspring are rooms.

They do not perceive light, though they turn toward it. They perceive correspondence. The sourceless illumination gives the chamber an image of a future exterior sun, and the vanes align themselves with that eventual fact. Each minute adjustment helps reconcile the buried pocket with the world that will surround it. Their clean shadows are measurements sent backward from daylight. When the angle changes every few centuries, the lithophytes learn that the mountain has advanced into another portion of its eventual history.

Nor are they entirely silent. The ticking of an expanding leaf is structural speech: one column announcing that a tolerance has been met. Static moving along a frond carries the state of distant strata. The deep communal note is both metabolism and consensus. For eleven minutes the separate bodies cease to approximate separateness. Their circuits close through the inaccessible center, and the pocket becomes a single organ whose leaves, atmosphere, floor, and dimensions all participate.

In that state, the being has no nameable shape. The columns are its valves. The shadows are its indexing marks. The room is its cell membrane. The mountain growing around it is at once secretion, shell, and body.

It may possess something like awareness, but not of objects. It knows stresses before they occur, because it installs the faults that will relieve them. It knows vanished seas as mineral requirements, though those seas have not yet formed. It knows future erosion as a pattern of subtraction and compensates for valleys millions of years before rain begins cutting them. Its attention is spread so thinly through time that a century registers as a flicker of chemical uncertainty.

The individual columns know less. They know charge, composition, alignment, readiness. A damaged vane does not produce pain, but an unfinished gradient that the whole grove feels and resolves. Death is not the cessation of a column. Death would be an unprocessed remainder: something impossible escaping into finished stone.

For now, none has escaped.

The lithophytes continue their slow correction beneath the young summit. They refine paradox into mineral, duration into strata, and inaccessible space into height. They are not inhabitants of the mountain, because the mountain is their accumulated product. Nor are they its makers in any deliberate sense. They are the living process by which the mountain becomes capable of having existed.

When the chamber is finally approached from outside, they may perish in oxygen and weather. They may fold themselves into the walls. They may complete the last missing volume and leave no interval to discover.

But if they remain long enough to see true sunlight, they may turn their vanes toward it without surprise. They have been following its future position since before the mountain had a sky.
