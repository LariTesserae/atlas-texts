What lives there is not a collection of separate creatures so much as a single appetite divided among millions of bodies.

Its largest inhabitants move slowly because haste would tear the forest apart. Long-legged browsers pick their way among the buttress roots on splayed, gripping feet. Their shoulders are narrow enough to pass between trunks, but their backs carry fringes of coarse, water-shedding hair in which mosses, mites, and pale climbing frogs reside. They browse leaves, fungi, and condensation-heavy epiphytes, closing slitlike nostrils before rain. Herds are temporary alliances. Individuals gather around active vents and separate when water fails, leaving no enduring trail behind them.

Above these browsers lives the greater part of the forest.

Dark-furred primates travel through the interlocked crowns in restless bands. They are compact, broad-handed, and rarely descend except to reach fresh basalt. They know which black seams fracture cleanly. A stone is struck, a glass edge produced, and the edge is used while still sharp: fruit armor is scored, vines cut, nests opened, parasites shaved from skin. Nothing is carried for long. The forest offers few safe places to store possessions, and any abandoned tool is soon hidden by moss, taken by another animal, or nudged into a crack.

They are not alone in the crowns. There are gliding leaf-eaters with membranes mottled like lichen; hook-clawed hunters that cling beneath branches; colonies of small bodies that sleep braided together so rain runs around the outside of the knot. Broad-handed creatures fold living leaves into shelters and secure them with tendrils, making green chambers that continue to grow after their makers move on. Some animals occupy a shelter for only a night. Others arrive afterward: nest thieves, egg layers, molds, and ambush hunters. Within days the shelter ceases to belong to any one species.

Flying life fills every available interval between branches. Leathery-winged fruit eaters launch in such numbers that their departure bows the canopy. Needle-beaked drinkers visit leaf cups and puncture swollen vines. Marsh birds with long, sensitive bills probe hot cracks using hooked twigs selected from the litter. Predatory fliers shadow migrations through the crowns, while smaller wings gather behind them to seize scraps, parasites, and disturbed insects. At the forest margins these populations stream between green islands until the humid air itself appears granular and alive.

The ground belongs to things that can emerge quickly and disappear faster.

Pale, jointed burrowers spend dry intervals packed beneath thin soil or wedged in basalt fractures. A change in pressure brings whole colonies upward. They spread across clearings, consume fungi and fallen matter, mate, then retreat before hunters converge. Armored grazers scrape luminous films from warm stone. Flat-backed crabs inhabit water pockets no larger than a footprint. Beneath loose bark wait animals so exactly bark-shaped that they become visible only when the bark lunges.

The temporary waters contain compressed lifetimes. Fishlike creatures lie dormant in mucus cases beneath dry channels, their bodies folded around drought-proof organs. Rain wakes them. They enter torrents only hours old, feed on larvae and one another, court, spawn, and begin dying before the water has settled. Their eggs endure in ash and mud until the next flood. As a channel contracts, scavengers arrive on wings; shell-bearing things rise from the margins; stabbing mouths crowd every remaining puddle. By the time the water vanishes, little remains except eggs, cases, and a clicking layer of occupied shells.

Every plant-held cup is a vertical settlement. Transparent larvae hang beneath the surface film. Minute grazers browse the cup’s inner walls. Rootlets descend to drink among them. Moths balance at the rim, while camouflaged hunters wait under the leaf. Some cup plants nourish their occupants with secretions and absorb their waste. Others digest whatever falls in. The distinction between reservoir, nursery, trap, and stomach is rarely clean.

Around the vents live the heat-bound communities. Soft red moss feeds on mineral moisture. Blind grazers crop it, attended by smaller organisms that clean their breathing folds. Pale films spread across the hottest stone and glow faintly where the vapor is richest. Trees rooted nearby have reshaped generations of leaves into funnels and gutters, making each grove a condensation machine. When a vent fails, these lives do not migrate gracefully. They raid, crowd, fight, desiccate, and die until another exhalation draws the survivors elsewhere.

Predators are abundant but seldom solitary. Hunting swarms pass through the understory like weather, flushing tree-dwellers into the paths of waiting fliers. Low, many-legged pursuers communicate through vibrations in root mats. Larger hunters follow the swarms, feeding on both fugitives and hunters. Near scarce water, massive still-bodied ambushers can remain indistinguishable from a buttress root until heat, breath, or pressure brings them forward. No kill belongs to its maker for long. Scavengers arrive almost at once, then parasites of scavengers, then organisms that eat the scraps those parasites leave.

Decay itself is alive and hurried. Fruits are opened before fermentation can soften them. Shed skin is sectioned and carried off. Fallen wood becomes bridge, nursery, garden, hunting perch, and food in overlapping succession. Fungi do not merely wait underground; they inflate overnight from bruised soil, lace carcasses before they cool, and shine along wet trunks. Nutrients pass through so many bodies that the earth beneath them remains thin and hungry.

Some inhabitants seem less like species than events.

There are the rain climbers, amphibious creatures that emerge from heated cracks before storms and ascend the trees in glistening masses. There are canopy colonies whose members clasp one another into living sheets beneath branches. There are tiny rootless plants drifting across water cups and transported in the fur of browsers. There are parasites that cause their hosts to seek exposed ledges just before winged offspring emerge. There may be animals whose entire reproductive cycle depends on the panic caused by another species’ migration.

And there are the makers of the nocturnal light.

No single creature commands it. Fungal threads brighten first along lacquered trunks. Leaf insects answer. Throat sacs flare in distant crowns. Larvae in water cups turn upward and glow through the cloudy surface. The signals gather into rings that travel across the canopy without an apparent source. As each ring passes, calling animals fall silent—not from peace, but attention. Hunters pause with jaws open. Colonies stop moving. Even the low drumming in flooded hollows ceases.

For a few seconds, the forest becomes aware of itself.

Then every suspended action resumes: wings strike leaves, throats pulse, prey bolt, hunters close, and the layered noise returns with the force of released pressure.

Nothing here rules for long. Intelligence exists, but it does not separate itself from the living mass. Tools are made and dropped. Shelters remain leaves. Routes wander. Territories shift with vapor and rain. Even the largest bodies must follow moisture, and even the smallest pool acquires rivals.

The true inhabitant is therefore the occupation itself: life entering every hollow, fastening to every surface, exploiting every dependable rhythm until that rhythm fails. It lives in the trees and between them, in hot stone, trapped breath, temporary water, borrowed bodies, and the brief darkness beneath a passing wing. It has no capital, monument, or permanent nest.

Its only enduring work is to leave nothing empty.
