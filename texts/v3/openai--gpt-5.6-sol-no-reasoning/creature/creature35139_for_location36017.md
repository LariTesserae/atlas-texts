Those who live above the clouds do not speak of the country as a single creature. That would make it seem simpler than it is.

They say instead that the land is **awake in places**.

Its awareness gathers around wounds, grafts, flowerings, and the deep hot seams where old wood ferments. It travels slowly through sap and tension. A branch plain may remain indifferent for generations, then begin answering footsteps with faint contractions. A grove may recognize the season before the weather does. The oldest people insist that the continent has no mind of its own, only innumerable local hungers that sometimes align.

Nearly everything alive there has learned to exploit those hungers—or to resemble them.

### The keepers of ponds

Human communities are few, and most are seasonal. The most stable are the pond-keepers, families who inherit cisterns in the forks of ancient limbs. Their wealth is measured not only in water but in the succession of lives within it: black fish beneath the ice, dormant eggs in the mud, edible films along the walls, larvae that hatch during warm cloudfall, and the pale rootlets that cleanse the water without draining it.

A pond-keeper knows ice as another person might know weather. White ice is usually young. Blue ice may be deep or merely clear. Ice without trapped bubbles is feared because it means something below has consumed the air. Such ponds are not always dead. Sometimes they are too alive.

The keepers travel little. Their broad-toed boots are made from layered bark cloth with hooked bone cleats. Their hands are stained by resin, and their hearing becomes acute from years spent listening through ice. They test a cistern by pressing brow or teeth against its rim. A healthy pond gives many tiny sounds. A dangerous one may produce a single patient scraping from below.

Their dwellings hang beside the water rather than above it. Each is wrapped in overlapping leaves and tethered with living cords that contract in storms. Heat-moss grows in clayless baskets of woven root. During the long cold, several people sleep around one moss pit beneath quilts of felted pollen fiber. Breath freezes inside the shelter and is scraped from the walls each morning, melted, and returned to the pond.

They tend the water, but they never claim to own it. Water belongs to whatever reaches it thirsty.

### The walkers

Other people follow routes too unstable to become roads.

These walkers are messengers, hunters, seed-brokers, bone workers, and carriers of the rare mineral tools. They navigate by sequence rather than map: the split black ridge, the whistling hole, the knot shaped like a closed eye, the three leaning stems, the bitter-smelling hollow. Their route songs are lists of such features fitted to rhythms that can survive panic and exhaustion. When a landmark grows over or falls away, the song is altered. Old verses are retained as warnings.

Most walkers travel in groups of three or four, roped together at the waist. One reads the surface, watching frost and bark tension. One listens for clappers, animals, and deep movement. One looks upward for weather. If there is a fourth, that person watches behind—not chiefly for predators, but for changes in the trail. A safe surface can begin closing after a party has crossed it.

They carry very little. A hook. A coil of living vine. Resin salve. Folded water leaves. A bone awl. A handful of dormant heat-moss wrapped in wax. The most fortunate carry a sliver of stone in a wooden reliquary, bringing it out only for work bone and shell cannot do.

A stranger arriving at a refuge does not knock on its wall. Knocking may imitate borers or courtship drumming and call the wrong thing. Instead, the traveler repeats the refuge’s clapper rhythm on a portable pair of wood pieces, then adds a personal cadence. Identity is musical before it is visible.

Long-distance exchange depends on these people. They carry larvae between isolated ponds to prevent whole populations from failing. They bring pollen packets from groves whose flowering no longer overlaps. They transport stories, marriages, grafts, remedies, and news of the land’s shifting shape. A walker may leave a settlement fifteen kilometers from its neighbor and return years later to find the distance doubled, or the two places pressed together by a swelling branch.

For this reason, they say no one crosses the same country twice.

### The rooted folk

Not all inhabitants remain entirely human throughout the year.

In certain exposed provinces, people survive the deep dark by entering a rooted state. Before the cold reaches its height, they cut shallow sockets in sheltered bark and bind themselves upright against the living surface. A cultivated symbiont is spread over their legs and spine. It penetrates the outer skin without drawing much blood, slows the heart, stiffens the limbs, and grows a fringe of black photosynthetic membranes around the sleeper.

For months these people neither walk nor speak. They drink through the symbiont and share in the substrate’s chemical warmth. Frost seals them into place. At a distance a wintering family looks like a stand of narrow shrubs.

Waking is difficult. The membranes must be peeled away gradually, and some roots have to be cut. A person may emerge weak, green-veined, and unable to remember which sensations belonged to their own body. Many report dreams of immense pressure, warmth below, and distant injuries. A few refuse to be severed. Their families maintain them as rooted elders, visiting to interpret changes in leaf angle, sap odor, and pulse.

After many years, it becomes impossible to say whether such an elder is still a person using a plant or a plant preserving the habits of a person. Their descendants usually consider the distinction impolite.

### The dark browsers

Across the branch plains move low herds covered in dense charcoal fur. The people call them **rimebacks**, because resting animals gather frost until only their breathing holes remain visible.

They stand on six short limbs, each ending in a broad pad divided by gripping ridges. Beneath the fur, their bodies are lean and warm. Flexible ribs allow them to flatten themselves against the bark during violent winds. Their mouths scrape lichens, moss mats, soft outer bark, and the mineral-rich films that grow around old lightning scars.

Rimebacks travel nose to tail. The lead animal tests the surface with a vibrating pad beneath its jaw, sensing hollows and strain within the wood. When danger approaches, warnings pass down the line through touch faster than they could through sound. A whole herd can flatten almost simultaneously, disappearing into the texture of the plain.

They are not fully mobile all year. During the coldest interval, pregnant animals settle in sheltered cracks and push vascular filaments from their bellies into the bark. Their young develop while connected both to the mother and to the land. When they depart, they leave behind circular scars surrounded by unusually lush growth.

People hunt rimebacks rarely. Their hide is valuable, but killing too many along a route allows hard, poisonous moss to spread unchecked. Some settlements tend semi-wild herds by salting bark with crushed insect shell. The animals are never enclosed. Fences become food, barriers become traps, and a frightened herd can bring predators from far away.

### The pale gliders

The cliff leaves house creatures whose bodies are almost all sail.

Their bones are hollow lattices. Their limbs support translucent membranes furred with sensory cilia. When folded, they hang like wet seed husks under leaves. When open, an adult may span the width of a dwelling chamber while weighing no more than a child.

They launch only when wind combs correctly across the cliff. Once airborne, they may not land for months. They drink cloud through fringes around the mouth and feed on spores, tiny fliers, pollen, and electrical plankton drawn to storm fronts. During sleep they lock their wings into a shallow curve and drift in broad spirals.

Their skin is crowded with hooks that collect seeds. Some seeds germinate during flight, sinking shallow roots into the glider’s outer tissues. Older animals carry whole gardens: black moss along the spine, dangling bulbs beneath the belly, luminous tendrils around the neck. The relationship is mostly beneficial. The growths store water and disguise the glider from aerial hunters. Eventually, however, their weight forces the animal to land.

A successful landing may become a nesting site, a flower patch, or a new grove. A failed one becomes a brief feeding frenzy.

People catch young gliders by raising frames of scented membrane into the wind. A few can be trained to carry light packets, but never to a precise destination. Messages entrusted to them may arrive years late and hundreds of kilometers away. For this reason, a glider-borne message is written as much for strangers and descendants as for its intended recipient.

### The clatterers

The long-legged hunters of the open bark are called **clatterers**.

At rest, they resemble frost-killed shoots: narrow bodies held upright on four or six jointed legs, pale shells streaked with gray, heads lowered into the wind. The lower sections of their limbs are hollow. By striking them together, they produce a dry knocking audible across great distances.

The knocks serve several purposes. They declare territory. They keep hunting groups coordinated in darkness. They also measure the land. A clatterer listens to echoes returning through the air and vibrations traveling through the bark, locating cavities, moving herds, and weak sections of substrate.

When hunting together, they spread in a crescent ahead of prey. Some drum; others bite tension vines or worry at shallow fissures. They do not need to outrun a rimeback herd if they can persuade the ground to buck beneath it. A flexing branch throws prey off balance, after which the clatterers close with startling speed.

Along their flanks are insulated fermentation sacs filled with chewed plant matter and symbiotic microbes. These produce enough heat to keep the hunters active in cold that immobilizes most animals. Before a chase, vapor streams from vents under their limbs. Afterward they must feed the sacs as carefully as their own stomachs. A starving clatterer may still be full of meat yet freeze because its internal garden has died.

Their courtship sounds are nearly indistinguishable from distant human clappers. Travelers therefore listen for imperfection. Human rhythms vary deliberately. Clatterer patterns repeat too cleanly—unless one has been listening to humans for a long time.

### The cistern life

Every pond is a crowded world.

The flat black fish have eyes only when young. As adults they navigate by pressure, tasting the water with ribbonlike fins. During freezing, they descend to the deepest mud and reduce themselves nearly to stillness. Some survive being partly enclosed in ice. Their blood contains sugars derived from the roots they eat along the pond wall.

The pale larvae are more troubling. They hatch with soft branching limbs and feed on microbial films. As water shrinks, they gather into braided colonies, linking mouths to tails until thousands form a single slowly contracting cable. The outer larvae freeze and die, insulating those within. When the thaw returns, the cable unknots into individuals again.

Sometimes it does not.

An old colony may retain its union and crawl from the pond as one animal. These braided forms seek warm fissures and carrion. They can enter sleeping shelters through holes no wider than a finger, separating briefly to pass through and knotting again inside. Heat-moss attracts them. Pond-keepers place rings of bitter resin around every warming pit.

There are also bell-shaped grazers that cling upside down beneath the ice, thread worms that grow oxygen bubbles along their backs, and translucent ambush animals almost indistinguishable from cracks. Certain ponds contain miniature rooted forests. Others hold nothing visible at all, though their water level falls faster than evaporation permits.

### The walkers that become trees

Some of the upright “trees” scattered across the plains began as animals.

Their mobile stage resembles a narrow, many-legged tower wrapped in overlapping scales. For years it wanders slowly through groves and along moisture channels, grazing on growths and accumulating chemical knowledge of the region. It visits wounds, ponds, carcasses, and flowering sites. Its body stores spores and fragments of other organisms in sealed internal pockets.

When it finds suitable ground, it stops.

The legs penetrate the bark and become roots. The scales spread into leaves. The upper body splits into reproductive branches, while the central nervous tissues disperse into a ring beneath the new trunk. The creature does not exactly die, but its decisions become seasonal and architectural.

Such towers often grow where the land is damaged. They knit cracks, drain poisoned water, and introduce collections of symbionts gathered during their wandering years. For this reason they are tolerated near settlements, though never trusted. A recently rooted tower may remember movement. It can pull up immature roots and shift several body lengths during the night.

Older towers sometimes produce walking offspring whose first actions suggest inherited memory. They turn toward ponds their parent visited centuries before. They avoid certain scars. They pause beside abandoned human structures and tap them with their forelimbs as though expecting an answer.

### The underhangers

Below the inhabited surface, among roots descending into perpetual storm, move the largest known creatures.

No one has seen an entire one.

Lightning reveals sections: a pale flank broader than a grove, a cluster of gripping limbs wrapped around separate hanging trunks, a head—or what may be a feeding organ—opening like a dark flower in the cloud. They move suspended beneath the country, distributing their weight across dozens of roots. Some browse on pendant foliage. Others scrape fermented wood from the underside. Their passage causes ponds above to ripple and dust to rise from shelter floors.

Their calls are too low for ordinary hearing. The land carries them instead. A bowl of water trembles. Loose hooks tap against a wall. Teeth ache. Rooted elders become agitated days in advance.

People name individual underhangers by the rhythm of their tremors. **Slow-Three** passes beneath the western cisterns every few years. **The Dragging One** leaves long strips of peeled wood visible along the cloud edge. **Many Hands** is associated with branch failures, though it may simply favor already weakened regions.

Some believe the underhangers cultivate the plateau, pruning roots and spreading organisms from one hanging forest to another. Others call them parasites. Once in several generations, a vast grip closes around a major limb and pulls until part of the country breaks away. Whether this is feeding, migration, reproduction, or accident is unknown.

After such a severing, winged swarms fill the air and human communities travel toward the fresh wound to gather sap, shell, and rare matter from below. Many do not return.

### The wound-feeders

Fresh golden wood summons life from extraordinary distances.

First come microscopic feeders carried in the wind. Then blue fungal threads web the exposed grain. Needle-mouthed drinkers arrive in clouds dense enough to darken the glow. Small heat-seeking animals burrow into the steaming sap. Predators follow them, and larger predators follow those.

Some organisms appear so quickly that people doubt they traveled at all.

The most feared are the **hush flowers**. They emerge around a wound as pale folded bulbs, each warm to the touch. They absorb sound through membranes inside their petals. Near a mature colony, wind seems distant and footfalls vanish. This silence draws exhausted animals seeking shelter from the constant roar.

When enough weight enters the colony, the flowers close.

They do not bite. Their roots contract beneath the bark, pulling the softened surface inward and burying whatever stands upon it. Weeks later the wound is sealed by a smooth pale scar.

A glowing wound is therefore never merely a source of heat or sap. It is an announcement that the food web is assembling.

### The out-of-sequence groves

Across the country, reproductive towers pulse with slow green light. Their rhythm travels through the living substrate, each grove answering the next with delays caused by distance, temperature, and the condition of the wood.

Sometimes a grove flashes at the wrong time.

Such groves may be diseased, isolated by a hidden break, or overtaken by something that has learned to imitate the signal. Animals entering them often lose their migratory direction. Seed-walkers root in unsuitable places. Human clappers warp in tone as their wood swells unevenly.

Within some out-of-sequence groves, growth repeats forms it has touched. Leaves curl like fingers. Seed pods develop toothlike ridges. Bark reproduces ladder patterns, knots, woven walls, even shallow human faces—not accurately, but persistently.

Travelers go around these places. Rooted elders listen to them with dread. The oldest stories suggest that the irregular pulses are not random but fragments of a much larger rhythm traveling from deep within the continent.

### What the people believe

The people above the clouds do not worship the land. Worship would imply that it notices reverence.

They respect locality instead. Every hollow has tendencies. Every branch has stresses. Every pond has a history. A thing may be safe here and deadly one ridge away because it participates in a different web of relations.

Their stories rarely divide beings into plant and animal, living and dead, natural and made. More important distinctions are:

- what moves now and what may move later;
- what feeds alone and what recruits;
- what remembers injury;
- what carries warmth;
- what answers signals;
- what has rooted by choice;
- what has not yet chosen.

A person is understood similarly. Identity is not located entirely inside the skin. It includes one’s route songs, grafts, pond obligations, clapper rhythm, inherited blade, winter symbionts, and the refuges that would recognize one’s approach.

The worst punishment is not exile, because almost everyone must wander at times. It is **unanswering**: the removal of a person’s cadence from the clapper sequences. A condemned traveler may still hear refuges in the storm, but none will answer back.

The highest honor is to become a remembered landmark. A dead person may be placed in a prepared bark hollow with seeds, heat-moss, and strands of their family’s hair. If the graft takes, a distinctive growth rises there—a colored branch, a whistling aperture, a circle of luminous leaves. Future route songs include the name.

Not all memorials remain where they were planted.

### In the rare light

When the upper cloud tears open, all these lives become visible at once.

Rimeback herds rise from stillness and shake frost from their fur. Gliders wheel in pale multitudes. Clatterers abandon concealment and race for the shadows cast by reproductive towers. Rooted people wake enough to turn their leaf-fringes toward the light. Pond ice becomes transparent, revealing black fish, braided colonies, and larger shapes below them that no keeper has named.

The land responds more strongly than any of its inhabitants. Leaves rotate in continental waves. Warm vapor pours from flowers. Branch plains bow as tissues fill. Signals run in green fire from grove to grove.

For those few minutes, it is possible to see that the apparent emptiness was produced by scale, concealment, and waiting. The whole country is occupied. The bark beneath every foot is a roof over chambers, a skin over circulation, a feeding ground, a road, a nursery, a memory.

Then the opening closes.

Creatures return to fissures. People sound their refuges through the dark. The great underhangers move below, unseen except as pressure in the roots. The out-of-sequence groves continue their wrong replies.

And beneath everyone, too slowly to be witnessed but too quickly to be called eternal, the country grows another shape.
