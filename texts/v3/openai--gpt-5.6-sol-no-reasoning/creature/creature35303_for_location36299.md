No animal lives in the hollow. Its inhabitants are slower things: organisms whose lives can be mistaken for stains, dust, or changes in the stone itself.

The most established are the lichens. They occupy the limestone in thin, faded colonies, circular only where the surface permits, elsewhere following pores and hairline fractures. Their bodies are scarcely raised above the rock. In dry weather they contract into pallid crusts, gray, cream, and muted green; when rain darkens the walls, they soften and become briefly more visible. They cross without distinction from ancient bedrock onto the stones set by human hands. To them, wall and outcrop are the same mineral country.

Beneath the shelves are low mats of moss or mosslike growth, dense enough to hold condensation but too spare to make a carpet. They live by patience. Clear droplets gather above them, fall, and disappear among tightly folded leaves. The mats brighten for a while after rain, then close down again, keeping what little moisture they can in the shaded seams.

The wiry grasses along the channel live as separate individuals. Each has claimed a narrow depth of soil and exhausted the ground immediately around itself, leaving a bare ring that preserves its distance from the next tuft. Their roots reach into old silt packed between stones. Aboveground they seem nearly dead—stiff blades, dulled green, dry tips—but they remain responsive to the basin’s small cycles. Water passes; the blades loosen slightly. The hollow dries; they draw tight again. Their rare contact makes the basin’s only sound of living movement.

At the deepest seep, the broad-leaved cluster is the place’s largest and most conspicuous resident. Its leaves spread close to the ground, dusty on top and cooler beneath, with browned margins recording earlier dry intervals. It does not flourish, exactly, but neither is it failing. Its roots possess the one dependable thread of moisture in the hollow, and that modest advantage is enough to give it a heavier form than anything else growing there.

Smaller lives persist beyond easy sight: films of algae in damp pores, fungal threads within the silt, bacteria inhabiting the transient wet line of the channel and the undersides of stones. When the handspan pools appear, these organisms wake into brief activity. They do not break the surface or cloud the water. As the pools vanish, they retreat into spores, membranes, and minute protected pockets, waiting through warmth and dryness for the basin to repeat itself.

Together they form a community with almost no traffic in it. Nothing grazes the leaves, carries seed, pollinates flowers, turns the soil, or feeds upon another visible body. The plants spread, if they spread at all, by root, fragment, spore, or the chance movement of rain. Growth is measured less by seasons than by the gradual occupation of a crack.

The ruins are inhabited in precisely the same manner as the natural stone. Lichen enters their exposed faces; fine roots test their joints; microbial films follow the rain grooves. No creature shelters inside the walls, and no former builder remains except as the arrangement of the blocks. What lives there has accepted the construction without preserving its purpose.

Thus the hollow’s inhabitants are not a hidden population waiting to emerge. They are the pale crust on the limestone, the gray-green mats under the ledges, the isolated grasses, the broad leaves at the seep, and the invisible life that briefly quickens wherever water lingers. They do not fill the emptiness. Their way of living depends upon it: each presence kept apart, each rooted to one small allowance of shade, mineral, and recurring water.
