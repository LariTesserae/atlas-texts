The chief inhabitant of the manufactured marsh is not any single planted species. It is the water moving through them all.

It lives everywhere at once, though never in one shape for long. In the high mesh it is a trembling skin of condensation. In the perforated gantry it waits under pressure, divided into measured rain. It fattens moss, darkens brick, enters the mineral paste, rises through stems, clouds the glass, and collects in the minute cups of bolts. Beneath the terraces it is drawn through pumps like blood through a mechanical heart. Released above, it wanders.

The designers intended a circuit, but the water is learning a territory. It presses experimentally at seals. It discovers which joints are imperfect, which channels are fractionally lower, which screw heads can shelter a grain of silt. It pauses where no reservoir was planned and makes one from persistence. Already it has begun to write the marsh’s first illegible history: a pale mineral lip below a nozzle, a dark fan on concrete, a green stain widening around a drain.

The plants live by intercepting this restless passage. Taro holds shallow basins in its leaves. Sedges split currents around their stems. Ferns inhabit the dripping vertical faces, while vines occupy the uncertain border between trellis and thicket. None has forgotten its cartridge or trough, but many have ceased to respect it. Their roots cross plumbing boundaries, drink from maintenance spaces, and enter one another’s beds. They are young, vigorous, and slightly misplaced—as though the marsh, assembling itself from a plan, has begun choosing different components.

Moss is its most patient resident. It covers the distinction between structure and ground without arguing that the distinction is gone. Under its green nap remain bolts, seams, ceramic veins, inspection numbers. Liverworts spread beside it in glossy, primitive sheets. Together they soften the facility by fractions, preparing surfaces for later arrivals.

Among those arrivals are the small uninvited creatures.

The copper midges rise where the warmed mud exhales, hanging in loose constellations under pipes. They seem almost generated by the mist: brief flecks of metal-colored life, vanishing whenever the air shifts. Minute snails move along one shaded channel, leaving clear tracks through films of algae. No one knows whether their eggs came in moss, substrate, reed plugs, or on a worker’s wet boot. They have no official place in the system, yet they graze it as though it had been built for them.

The black bird belongs differently. It does not reside so much as visit from the immense bright air. Landing on the rain gantry, it grips the drilled metal with dark feet and drinks from the hollows around the bolts. Its feathers gather droplets. Its head turns toward every pump vibration and hidden sluice. For the length of its stay, it makes all the terraces appear older and less deliberate. The pipes become branches; the basins, pools; the parapet, a cliff edge. Then it falls outward and is gone, carrying perhaps a seed, a snail’s egg, or only a mouthful of manufactured rain.

Deeper within the marsh live the least certain inhabitants.

They appear at dusk, when the pumps reverse and nutrient water begins to rise through the porous tiles. Translucent larvae emerge downstream of tanks said to be sealed. They are nearly clear except for their inner thread of darkness and the geometric marks arranged along their bodies. These marks are too regular to look like ordinary pigmentation: dots, slots, and small dark intervals recalling the perforation pattern of the irrigation pipes.

In still water the larvae curl like scraps of wet filament. When a pump shudders through the floor, they straighten. Some press themselves against filter mesh, aligned with the current. Others enter openings barely wider than their heads and vanish into the ceramic arteries.

Perhaps they came hidden in imported moss. Perhaps their markings merely resemble the machinery that sorts and carries them. But workers who clean the clarifying chambers have noticed stranger correspondences. When a row of irrigation holes clogs, larvae downstream sometimes bear an interruption in the same place along their marked flanks. When a nozzle plate is replaced, the next dusk’s brood may carry the new pattern. No one has established whether they imitate the pipes, whether the pipes select among them, or whether both are impressions of some repeated rhythm in the circulating water.

They may be less an animal population than a kind of soft record.

By night, while the terraces drain, the larvae pass through roots, charcoal, sand, and revolving screens. Most should be trapped. Yet they return uphill. They appear in lamp housings, valve collars, and the thin luminous seep above the mud beds. Their bodies flex when hidden chains move behind the walls. In the moment before a sluice opens, they gather at its mouth.

Thus the marsh’s inhabitants divide loosely into two kinds: those placed there to make the system work, and those produced by the system’s working. But the distinction is already failing. Roots alter drainage. Snails clean algae from sensors. Midges pollinate flowers intended only as indicators of humidity. The bird brings material across the parapet. Water remakes every prepared surface, and the marked larvae travel through the whole apparatus as if reading it from within.

Something larger may be forming from these exchanges—not a creature with one body, but an organism distributed across terraces. Pumps give it a pulse. Reservoirs lend it changing organs. Pipes serve as vessels until roots enter and complicate them. Plants become lungs, filters, shade, and appetite. The larvae may be nerves, or symptoms, or merely one more accidental script. Its skin is concrete, leaf, mist, glass, and the warm mud manufactured below.

It is very young. It has not yet learned the final shape of itself.

For now, it lives by circulation. It drinks its own weather, dismantles and rebuilds its own ground, and sends the same water endlessly through different identities. Each cycle leaves a minute remainder: sediment behind a screw, rust at an improbable seam, a root in the wrong tank, a shell no inventory can account for.

These are the traces of what lives there most fully: a small constructed world beginning to behave as though it had been born.
