The plateau’s true inhabitant is the plateau itself.

It is not an organism in the clean, bounded sense. It has no skin except the shifting limits of its vegetation, no organs that keep to a single function, no center from which commands descend. Rather, it is a congress of growths so anciently interdependent that individuality has become temporary. Trees, fungi, mosses, reservoir plants, buried rivers, and the slow populations of the soil compose one distributed life. Creatures move through it as blood cells might move through a body, carrying spores, minerals, heat, and injury from one district to another.

Its thoughts, if they can be called thoughts, take centuries to complete.

Rain is its most immediate sensation. Every drop strikes a thousand receptive surfaces before entering the dark below. Pressure passes through floating marshes; chemistry travels through root sap; distress moves along fungal films as changes in taste and conductivity. A storm crossing the eastern reaches becomes a broad agitation in the living weave. Years later, water-storage chambers thicken in the west. Decades after that, certain groves lower their canopies and begin making shade-loving seeds. The response is so delayed and distributed that cause and answer may be mistaken for separate events.

The beings best adapted to this place do not oppose its slowness. They borrow it.

Broad-backed marsh grazers are less like wandering herds than small mobile islands. Their hides are divided by deep vascular grooves in which moss, liverwort, mites, and seedlings establish miniature communities. Older animals can be recognized by the succession of plants upon them. Saplings rise from the shoulders of the most ancient; their roots enter folds beside the spine and drink salts from the blood without quite becoming parasites. When one of these animals rests, its body settles gradually into the trembling mat until only the ridged back remains visible. Birds—or the local things that fulfill something like the role of birds—may nest there for years, unaware that the ground beneath them breathes.

The grazers feed with wide, fringed mouths, combing the upper growth without severing its deeper weave. Their teeth are soft combs of mineralized horn, continually replaced and stained black by tannins. They follow no fixed routes. Instead, they are drawn toward subtle warmth generated where new root mats are fermenting. A herd may take thirty years to cross a basin. Where they pass, the vegetation returns changed: cropped plants branch more densely; spores carried in the animals’ grooves establish foreign patches; shallow wallows become clear pools.

Their young are born almost weightless, buoyed by sacs along the ribs. For their first seasons they walk atop leaves that would not support an adult’s foot. The mother does not call to them. She releases a faint chemical trace into the water, and the roots around her briefly alter their taste. The young follow that gradient through the marsh.

In the canopy live the long-limbed walkers. Their bodies are narrow and furred with short, dark filaments that trap condensation. Each hand bears several opposed digits and a pad of living adhesive, not sticky but densely folded, able to conform to wet bark. They seldom leap. A missed branch would mean not simply a fall but a descent through successive worlds: crown ponds, epiphyte thickets, branch caverns, and finally the drowned dusk below. They move by testing every support, sometimes waiting until the wood’s slow flex reveals whether it is rooted, suspended, or rotten within.

They eat translucent leaves, canopy fruit, soft fungi, and the eggs of pond animals. Their faces are small and inward-folding, protected from constant moisture by overlapping membranes. Eyes are large but not especially keen; in mist, vision is of limited use. More important are the sensitive soles of the hands and feet. Through them, the walkers read tremors in the communal wood: the distant collapse of a limb, the approach of a heavy grazer beneath the forest, the tapping courtship of another walker kilometers away.

Their calls enter the trees rather than the air. An individual braces itself across two branches and contracts a chamber in its chest, sending a low pulse into the fused timber. The message is not a word or song so much as a signature: weight, health, sex, direction, perhaps mood. The forest modifies it as it travels. Hollow trunks deepen it; saturated wood smears it; root junctions divide it. By the time it reaches another animal, the signal also contains an account of the country between them.

Some walkers spend their whole lives exchanging such pulses without meeting.

The small inhabitants are beyond counting. There are glass-bodied grazers whose organs resemble colored threads suspended in rainwater. They scrape bacterial films from the undersides of leaves, keeping open the microscopic pores through which many plants breathe. When threatened, they loosen their grip and become droplets, falling into lower foliage with their limbs folded against them.

Six-legged bark browsers travel in narrow processions along the braided trunks. Their front limbs end in rasping fans; their rear pairs grip the wet surface. Colonies cultivate pale algae in grooves they score into bark, defending these gardens against molds with secretions from chambers in their heads. A single colony may extend through a connected grove several kilometers wide, though no individual travels more than a few hundred meters in its lifetime.

Soft-winged insects rise after warm rain. Their wings are not built for distance. They drift from leaf to leaf, mating, pollinating, and dissolving within days. Many have no mouths. Their bodies contain symbiotic spores acquired during a submerged larval stage, and their brief flight exists mainly to lift those spores from one canopy pond to another. When clouds of them settle, whole branches become pale and feathery. By morning the insects are gone, consumed or liquefied by the leaves on which they rested.

In the crown ponds live frilled amphibious animals, black against tea-colored water. Their throats carry branching membranes through which they both breathe and display. At rest the frills are dull, nearly invisible. During courtship, blood and bioluminescent bacteria fill them with slow violet pulses. Eggs are laid in clear ropes around submerged twigs. The embryos do not all develop at once. Some hatch within weeks; others remain dormant for centuries, waiting for a particular sequence of temperature, tannin, and mineral content that may occur only after the host tree splits or falls.

Predators here cannot depend on pursuit. The land is too dense, the footing too uncertain, and warning travels too readily through connected wood. They become places instead.

One kind stands among broken stems with its limbs locked upright and its skin furred in lichens. It may remain motionless through several bloomings. Its metabolism falls until fungi begin to grow through the outer layers of its hide. Small creatures shelter beneath it. Browsers nibble growth from its flanks. Only when sufficient warmth presses against the sensory pits between its ribs does the arrangement unfold: stems becoming jointed legs, dead bark separating into jaws, a hollow trunk proving to have been a patient abdomen.

Another predator lives beneath floating mats. It has a flat, radial body and spreads threadlike feelers through the root weave above. It does not sense prey directly. It feels the distortion of shared tension: the cadence of feet, the distribution of weight, the periodic settling of a sleeping animal. Over many hours it loosens the mat from beneath, biting through selected fibers until a section gives way. The prey descends almost without a splash. After feeding, the predator anchors the severed roots back together, and within a season the wound closes.

Yet predation is infrequent. Flesh is difficult to acquire and costly to digest in a place where plants and fungi reclaim exposed nutrients immediately. Many apparent hunters eat only once in years. Some die while waiting and are not distinguishable from their disguises until they fruit.

There may also be descendants of those who shaped the squared columns, the concentric marsh basins, and the alcoves along the old escarpments.

If they survive, they are not readily separable from the work they left behind.

They may be the pale figures occasionally seen within root galleries: slow, many-jointed beings with fanlike hands and bodies wrapped in cultivated membranes. The membranes photosynthesize weakly, filter fungal spores from the air, and seal wounds when pressed against them. These beings carry no manufactured objects. Instead, they host useful organisms in folds of tissue—light-producing nodules, antiseptic molds, hard-secreting larvae, seed clusters held dormant beneath the skin.

They do not build by cutting and assembling. They redirect growth.

A corridor begins with the patient pinching of certain buds. A wall is made by feeding one root face while starving the other. A water channel may require generations of pressure, grafting, and selective decay. Their architecture is neither wholly intentional nor wholly grown. It is a negotiation conducted with organisms whose responses may mature after the cultivator is dead.

For this reason, their settlements cannot be abandoned in the ordinary way. Once unattended, they continue developing. Doors narrow into valves. Roofs become marsh. Sleeping chambers fill with clear water and amphibian eggs. Structural columns root into neighboring groves and cease to belong to any local design. The old inhabitants may have vanished, or they may simply have relinquished the distinction between habitation and habitat.

Perhaps the long-limbed canopy walkers are their diminished descendants. Perhaps the beings remain below, in the warm galleries where the hidden rivers pass, tending changes whose purpose will not be visible for another thousand years. Perhaps they never existed as a separate species at all. The regularly spaced alcoves and uprights may be expressions of the plateau’s own distributed order, produced as shells are produced by mollusks or chambers by insects: evidence of behavior without evidence of a single architect.

Whatever lives here becomes involved in the land’s awareness.

A wounded animal bleeds, and fungal bodies rise nearby. A sleeper exhales through the night, and leaves cup inward to collect condensation beneath it. Repeated footsteps make no road; instead, roots thicken at the margins and guide future travelers into the same passage. A corpse does not remain an object long enough to be mourned in bodily form. Moss seals its eyes. Fine roots enter the mouth and lungs. Mineral passes into leaf, leaf into grazer, grazer into water. Years later, fruit may appear in the place where it fell, bitter with compounds once carried in its blood.

The plateau does not cherish its inhabitants. Neither does it ignore them. It includes them with an intimacy that makes those ideas difficult to separate.

Its oldest living structures are the braided columns that hold entire upper landscapes. No one organism formed them. Their cores contain the mineralized remains of thousands of trunks, some grown upright, others descended from branches, others assembled around earlier hollows. Water climbs through them in slow pulses. In dry intervals, the columns contract audibly. In wet years, they swell and open old chambers inside themselves, releasing seeds that have waited since the climate last resembled the present.

These columns may be the closest thing the plateau has to memory.

Fungal threads preserve chemical traces of fires so ancient that no charcoal remains. Root knots retain the patterns of vanished rivers. Dormant bulbs respond to conditions absent for hundreds of millennia. When rare sequences of rain, warmth, and mineral flow recur, extinct landscapes seem briefly to return: red ground-cover appearing beneath black leaves, pale flowers opening in geometric fields, winged creatures hatching from ponds where no adult of their kind has flown within living memory.

Then the conditions pass. The old world folds back into the present.

At the greatest scale, all creatures here are messengers. The marsh grazers transport gardens. The glass-bodied feeders clean the breathing surfaces of leaves. The canopy walkers send news through wood. Predators open gaps and return concentrated minerals to the roots. Even the vanished builders, if vanished they are, persist as altered circulation: water taking a straighter path through a column, roots curving around the remembered shape of a room.

And beneath all of them lives the slowest inhabitant: a being without a face, boundary, or singular life span, warm under cloud and deep with drowned forests. It drinks stone through roots. It lets mountains soften inside it. It feels each creature briefly, not as a name but as pressure, heat, breakage, chemistry, and return.

Nothing lives upon it for long.

Everything lives within it.
