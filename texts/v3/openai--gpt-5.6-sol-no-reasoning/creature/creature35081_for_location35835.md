The plateau is inhabited less by creatures than by continuities.

In the hairline fractures lives the black filament-growth: patient conductive colonies spread between adjoining mineral phases. Most of the time they resemble stains trapped in the stone, their metabolism proceeding at the pace of accumulated voltage. Each filament is both cell and wire. Together they bridge strata that would otherwise discharge abruptly, feeding on the small electrical inequality between one side of a seam and the other.

When a field storm arrives, they become briefly extravagant. The stains raise branching glass forests into the thin air, every limb tuned to the passing charge. For a few minutes or hours, whole fracture systems glitter. Then the branches release microscopic needles—seeds, spores, electrodes—and collapse into brittle powder. The enduring organism remains below, dark and nearly two-dimensional, waiting through another decade of silence.

In protected hollows grow the ivory fans. They are less like plants than self-recording mineral events. Once during each great thermal cycle, the living rim adds a translucent ceramic layer to the outer edge. The layer behind it dies and becomes support. Thus each fan carries its own ancestry as structure: thousands or millions of dead generations holding forward a living margin only molecules deep. Their groves do not sway. They ring faintly when electrical pressure changes, producing tones too weak for the air but perceptible through stone.

The plateau’s animals live under movable plates.

They are broad, translucent, and astonishingly light: a skin drawn across branching internal trusses, with many hooked legs around the body’s margin. Most of their volume is emptiness. Dense flesh would cost too much to move and would fare badly when the ground abruptly changes phase. Beneath a plate they fasten themselves at several points, releasing all but one grip when a stress pulse passes so that the body can turn with the stone rather than tear against it.

They graze on charged reproductive needles and scrape conductive films from sheltered seams. Their mouths are not openings so much as patterned surfaces that sort particles by electrical potential. What they cannot digest is fitted into their trusses. An old individual therefore contains a mineral biography of every district it has crossed.

They venture out after electrostatic storms, in the brief interval when the fields are exhausted and the black colonies have shed their spores. Then the plains acquire a sparse, furtive motion. The creatures skate from shadow to shadow on dozens of legs, transparent enough to show the dark grains moving through them. If danger approaches, they flatten until the ground’s own charge pins them down. From a distance they resemble scraps of film caught on the surface, though there is scarcely enough wind to move them.

Their predators, if that word applies, are subterranean.

They inhabit not chambers but traveling discontinuities in the crust. No whole body has been seen. What appears at the surface is a sequence of coordinated effects: a row of plates tipping upward, a darkening along a fracture, several grazing animals suddenly emptied of charge. The thing below may be one organism extending for kilometers, or a colony that communicates through stress. It follows phase boundaries as other creatures follow rivers. During crustal waves it can migrate astonishing distances without moving through ordinary space, passing from one suitable crystal lattice into the next.

The high drifting knots are stranger still. They hang tens of kilometers above the plateau, compact black tangles with veils too fine to see except when they occult stars. Their lift comes from the planetary field. Their nourishment may come from charged dust or from the field itself. They migrate along magnetic contours, often in loose processions whose spacing remains exact across hundreds of kilometers.

Sometimes one descends.

It lowers filaments toward a tower or an active fracture-garden, stopping far above the ground. The filaments branch, collect a corona, and withdraw. Whether this is feeding, pollination, measurement, or communication is uncertain. The plateau organisms react to it: black growths flower out of season, the plate-dwellers freeze beneath cover, and buried presences divert from their paths. The knot then rises and rejoins its line, carrying something invisible with it.

The artifacts also inhabit the plateau, though they may not be alive in any biological sense.

The towers practice a narrow, durable form of awareness. They monitor light, heat, crustal inclination, and fields moving beneath the surface. They alter their skins, redistribute internal mass, and preserve their relationship to a vertical that is not always the local vertical. A tower can remain inactive for centuries, then bend through a ground swell as if rooted in a deeper geometry. It does not resist the land. It continually renegotiates what “upright” means.

The rings maintain differences.

Each encloses a region only slightly displaced from its surroundings: a delayed hour, an earlier season, an alternate loading of the crust, a version of the horizon in which a distant ridge rose somewhere else. Their work is subtle because large discrepancies may have been corrected long ago. What remains are maintenance errors. Dust emerges late. Shadows disagree. A fracture enters one side and fails to continue from the other until years afterward.

Life gathers near these errors. Filament colonies feed on potential differences that are not entirely electrical. Ivory fans build asymmetric layers, thicker on the side facing a delayed landscape. Some plate-dwellers learn the timing of a ring and wait beside it for spores cast yesterday to arrive tomorrow.

The orbital motes are the most active inhabitants. They descend singly or in coordinated swarms, stop above the ground, and assume whatever shape their task demands. There are no operators within them. Their intelligence, if intelligence persists, is distributed among the orbiting network, the towers, buried lines, and the aligned mineral plain in the north.

A descending machine may spend weeks examining one grain because that grain records a magnetic reversal, a vanished atmosphere, or a previous reconstruction of the escarpment. It rotates the grain, strips away a few atoms, compares them with patterns stored elsewhere, and returns it to nearly the same place. “Nearly” is sufficient. Over ages, these minute corrections accumulate into landscapes.

The seven northern walls belong to the deepest resident of all.

Something occupies the empty center without entering ordinary space. The walls delimit its absence. Their foundations descend not merely through distance but through successive physical conditions, anchoring the disk to strata and states that no longer exist at the surface. The aligned atoms between them form an interface. During magnetic storms, the buried presence expresses itself as racing lattices of light: calculations too large to remain underground, or reflexes passing across a nervous surface the size of a country.

The plateau avoids it instinctively and mechanically. Animals turn aside before sensing the walls. Dust is diverted. Stress fronts divide. Descending machines never land inside the disk, though they sometimes arrange themselves around its circumference and wait for years.

It may be an imprisoned engine, an archive, a regulator, or the residual mind of the system that made the plateau. It may be the reason the crust still rehearses its impossible motions. Each tidal flex, phase transition, rebuilding cliff, and silently lifted continent could be part of a computation whose variables are mass, altitude, and time.

Or the system may be holding it shut.

The life in the seams does not distinguish between these possibilities. It feeds on the corrections. The drifting knots follow their altered fields. The plate-creatures shelter when the deep pulses come. Towers adjust their brightness. Rings preserve their careful disagreements. Above them, the orbital network continues replacing pieces of a world-scale mechanism whose makers have been absent so long that absence itself has become one of the plateau’s stable conditions.

What lives there has learned not to oppose age, turbulence, or emptiness.

It becomes thin enough to occupy a crack. Slow enough to use centuries as seasons. Broad enough to treat a continent as a single body. Distributed enough to survive being broken. Much of it is dead architecture carrying a microscopic frontier of life. Much of it is machinery that has shed the distinction between damage and maintenance. And the oldest inhabitant may be the plateau itself: every ridge, ruin, organism, and moving fault participating in one immense, unfinished act of balance.
