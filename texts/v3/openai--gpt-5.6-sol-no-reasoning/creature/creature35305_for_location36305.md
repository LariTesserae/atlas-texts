The people who live along the upper wall are built, culturally and physically, around retention.

They call themselves by many district names, but outsiders translate the broadest of these as the Fastened. The translation is imperfect. The original word means secured against falling, committed to a burden, entered into civic record, and held in trust until one’s materials can be returned. To live is to be attached—not merely to the cliff, but to a chain of obligations.

Their bodies are compact and low-centered, though the differences between them and their distant ancestors are most evident internally. Water circulates only through narrow, valved vessels deep inside the torso. Broad regions of the body are almost dry. Joints turn in waxy lubricants; outer tissues are layered with flexible plates that limit loss to the air. The mouth and breathing passages close with muscular folds when not in use. Eyes sit behind clear inner shutters, and hearing is supplemented by pressure-sensitive membranes in the palms, soles, and along the ribs.

They can hear ordinary sound, but they trust vibration more. A hand placed on a railing may tell them that a lift is moving three streets away, that a crusher has entered the heavy part of its cycle, or that a slab beneath the district has begun to split. Even casual conversation includes touch. Friends rest knuckles against the same table. Workers hold a shared bar during dangerous operations. Lovers sometimes sleep with a cord drawn between wrist and wrist, reading one another’s minute movements through it.

Their outer garments are not primarily for warmth. They are dust seals, chemical barriers, and attachment systems. A citizen going outside wears a close suit with stiffened seams, a hood or face shutter, and at least two independent tethers. Belts carry hooks of different shapes for civic lines, freight rails, raw stone, and the soft ceramic loops used inside homes. The hooks identify occupation and district as readily as uniforms might elsewhere. Old people wear theirs polished nearly featureless by decades of use.

No one walks carelessly near an edge. This does not make them timid. They climb exposed trusses, cross open grids, and descend shafts kilometers deep with an ease visitors find unnerving. Their courage lies not in ignoring danger but in accounting for it completely. Before crossing a gap, they touch the line, inspect the anchor, exchange load with the person behind them, and proceed. Recklessness is not admired. Precision is.

Children are tethered before they can stand. In early infancy they sleep in shallow wall cradles with mesh lids, not because they are likely to fall from them, but because unsecured sleep is thought to produce an anxious disposition. Their first formal lesson is the Three Attachments: self to line, tool to self, line to tested stone. Their first game is often a sorting exercise in which harmless objects are passed downward through a miniature city without any being dropped.

They do drop things, of course. Childhood has its rebellions. A child may steal a flake of shale, lean over a protected shaft, and release it simply to experience the forbidden disappearance. Such incidents are treated seriously, but not always punished harshly. An elder may make the child calculate the possible path of the fragment through every terrace below. In some districts the child must descend with a retrieval crew until the stone is found or its safe resting place established. The lesson is not that falling is sinful. It is that consequences continue beyond sight.

Their cities have governments, guilds, lineages, unions, sects, and criminal networks, but nearly every institution began as some form of maintenance compact. Authority belongs, at least in theory, to whoever accepts liability. A council does not merely grant permission for a bridge; its members place their names and material shares against the bridge’s future failures. If it falls, repair costs may be taken from their households generations later.

This produces long memories and elaborate records. Agreements are incised into sheltered stone, stamped into ceramic tablets, encoded as patterned joints in public handrails, and repeated orally by clerks trained never to paraphrase. Paper-like materials exist but are regarded as temporary. Digital records are convenient but morally weak: too easy to alter, too dependent on uninterrupted power, too weightless in the imagination. A promise worth making should leave a mark that must itself be maintained.

Names work similarly. A person has a private household name, a public work name, and a line-name linking them to those responsible for their training. After a major mistake, the line-name may be invoked before the individual one, making the failure collective. After an exceptional act of repair, the names of teachers and apprentices are spoken together.

Households are seldom based only on blood. A typical household includes kin, coworkers, former apprentices, pressure migrants, and one or two people bound by water debt. Water debt is the deepest noncriminal obligation in the city. To spend sealed water saving another’s life creates a bond that cannot simply be repaid in currency. Some districts require years of service; others say it joins two households permanently. The recipient is not considered owned, but neither party may pretend the expenditure did not occur.

Most citizens never see liquid water outside medicine or ceremony. They encounter it as numbered cartridges in municipal walls, as a reading on a body monitor, as a ration balanced against industrial need. They know intellectually that water can gather into pools, but many find the idea disturbing. An untethered volume that shifts its shape, infiltrates cracks, and spills under gravity seems almost aggressively unsafe.

In ritual chambers, when drops strike hot stone and become vapor, reactions vary. Some bow. Some reach toward the brief cloud without touching it. Some weep, which produces another strange and costly appearance of water; public crying is therefore intimate in a way that nakedness is not. Mourning rooms recover moisture from the air.

Their dead are not buried. There is no soil, and burial would strand useful matter beyond inspection. Bodies are dried further, separated, assayed, and distributed. Mineral plates may enter insulating ceramics. Metals return to municipal stocks. Organic fractions feed cultivated industrial fauna or are reduced into chemical precursors. A small amount of each person is usually incorporated into a household anchor, so the dead literally help hold the living to the city.

This is not considered gruesome. Waste would be gruesome.

The people who perform these separations are called Returners. Their status is high but uneasy. They understand anatomy, chemistry, genealogy, and property law, since a body may contain municipal implants, inherited minerals, guild-owned seals, and water owed to several accounts. Returners are expected to be incapable of haste. A rushed disassembly is one of the few acts nearly every district agrees is sacrilege.

Religion varies across the inhabited band. Some traditions revere the cliff as the Last Stable Thing. Some teach that the abyss is not evil but unfinished, a region where distinctions dissolve under heat and pressure. Others insist stability itself is temporary and that ethical life consists of controlling the manner of one’s eventual descent.

One old sect worships neither cliff nor abyss but the Anchor: any point where force is knowingly transferred from one thing to another. Its shrines are structural nodes—bridge roots, cable heads, buttresses—kept meticulously clean. Worshippers place a hand on the load-bearing member and remain still until they feel the city moving through it.

Another faith maintains that every falling object remains part of the moral world until it comes to rest. Its priests keep maps of old losses: tools, freight containers, collapsed dwellings, bodies from ancient accidents. Pilgrims sometimes descend to verify that a centuries-old object has finally stopped.

Their industries shape their temperaments. Workers think in cycles longer than shifts. A kiln keeper may inherit a firing process begun before their birth. A quarry face is approached through negotiations spanning generations. Because machines move slowly, workers are trained to notice tiny deviations: a new harmonic in a bearing, a fractional delay in a valve, a tremor traveling against the usual direction. Much of labor consists of waiting attentively.

Silence is therefore not emptiness. It is a working condition.

Conversation tends to be measured around machinery, with speakers leaving gaps to hear what the stone is doing. Quick talk is associated with markets, children, and politics. In private, speech can become dense and playful. Every district has metaphors for load, tension, abrasion, and attachment. A dependable person is “deep-cut.” A transient fashion is “surface paint.” A secret is “an untallied weight.” To forgive someone is to “take slack from the line.” To die suddenly is to “pass the last terrace.”

Music is composed as much for surfaces as for air. Performers strike, bow, heat, cool, charge, or tension sections of architecture, sending patterned vibrations through floors and rails. Audiences sit or stand in contact with the structure. Some compositions travel across several boroughs over hours. One district introduces a pulse into a main cable; others answer through connected spans. Listeners may feel a bass sequence through their feet long before the audible tones arrive.

Visual art favors changes that are slow but real: balanced stone fields, reactive metal films, etched surfaces meant to become legible only after centuries of abrasion. Artists frequently design for descendants they will never meet. A finished work may be criticized as impatient.

Food is manufactured, cultivated in sealed chemical vats, or harvested from managed animal colonies. It is dense, oily, saline, and served in small portions. Meals are not centered on abundance but on traceability. Hosts often name the vat, batch, mineral source, and energy allotment of a dish. The closest equivalent to fresh produce is newly cast nutrient resin, still warm and soft enough to cut.

They keep animals, though “keeping” may mean allowing a colony to inhabit civic infrastructure under negotiated conditions.

Copper mites are used to read electric fields. Skilled tenders carry ceramic combs on which the mites arrange themselves according to local charge. Cliff-kite eggs are protected beneath bridges, and the hatchlings’ first flights are celebrated because they reveal the health of updrafts. Ribbon animals in lift shafts can sense fraying cables before instruments do; handlers train them to gather at marked inspection plates when distressed.

Hinge-backs are common household scavengers. They are flat, pale creatures with overlapping shells and a patient, deliberate gait. They consume spilled resin, shed skin plates, oxidized crumbs, and certain dangerous molds of reactive membrane. Children decorate their shells with removable mineral clips. A household without at least one hinge-back is considered either very poor or excessively sterile.

Glass gnats are less beloved. They swarm around electrical leakage, filling apartments with delicate clicking as their bodies strike walls. Yet their appearance can save lives. A sudden dense congregation often means a cracked insulator or exposed conductor. Killing all of them is therefore considered foolish; even pests may be informants.

The great bellows beasts of the depths inspire a different feeling. Few citizens have seen one at close range. Their migrations are felt through the flexing of windows and the deep pulses in the cliff. Pressure-basin communities follow them, harvest their cast-off filters, and sometimes guide herds away from unstable foundations using subsonic machinery.

These basin people are adapted to heavier air. They are broader through the thorax, stronger in the valves of the ears and sinuses, and accustomed to carrying buoyant freight that would be unmanageable above. Upper-wall citizens stereotype them as slow, secretive, and dangerously relaxed about overhead loads. Basin people, in turn, regard cliff dwellers as brittle creatures obsessed with hooks and records.

Trade binds them. The depths provide compressed gases, sulfur compounds, rare salts, and living tissues that cannot be cultivated at upper pressures. The wall sends ceramics, precision mechanisms, charged metals, and expertise in stabilization. Counterweighted trains carry more than cargo: marriages, fugitives, pilgrims, diseases of resin and shell, new slang, old grudges.

Far above, plateau walkers maintain the buried lines and survey towers. They travel in widely spaced caravans so that a crust failure cannot take everyone at once. Their suits are mirrored and their shelters squat, often buried to the roof. Plateau culture is shaped less by falling than by distance. A missed rendezvous may not be discovered for months. Their greetings involve exchanging position histories; their songs are lists of bearings disguised as poetry.

They live alongside the thin-legged charge feeders, sometimes following the herds to locate active mineral boundaries. A few families claim to communicate with the vast subsurface membranes by placing electrode arrays across their growth fronts and waiting years for a change in pattern. The city’s academies dismiss much of this as frontier mysticism while quietly purchasing the data.

No one agrees whether the membranes are alive. This disagreement matters because some advance toward buried power lines. If they are animals, they may be diverted or cultivated. If machines, perhaps they can be instructed. If geology, they may be quarried. Entire legal schools have formed around growths that move a handspan in a century.

The city also contains those who live outside permitted systems.

Unfastened communities inhabit abandoned mines and closed factories beyond the inspected terraces. The name given to them suggests lawlessness, but they are not literally without anchors. Many are more meticulous than citizens because no municipal crew will rescue them. What they reject is recorded attachment: inherited liability, water debt, guild custody, compulsory bodily return.

They salvage forgotten equipment, cultivate illicit organisms in warm conduits, and tap power from old lines. Some allow their dead to remain whole in sealed chambers. Some commit the shocking act of placing a body on a controlled descent platform and lowering it into the depths without recovery. City authorities call this theft of materials. The Unfastened call it release.

Smugglers move among these settlements using disused rails. Their most valuable cargo is not water, which is too conspicuous, but biological tissue rich in bound water: eggs, membranes, medicinal cultures, pressure-beast glands. They also traffic in untethered objects made specifically to be dropped—small polished weights designed to whistle, spark, unfold vanes, or release colors as they fall. Possessing one is illegal in most districts. Dropping one is a private thrill, a political statement, or a prayer.

Accidents haunt everyone. A major fall may begin with something trivial: a cracked hook, a fatigued shelf, a mite colony bridging the wrong contacts. The city responds through closure shutters and catch-nets unfurled between terraces. Signals pulse through floors. Citizens flatten against inner walls, secure loose objects, and wait.

Afterward come the Path Readers. They reconstruct the descent from impact scars, cable vibrations, dust plumes, and witness timings. Their diagrams may cross dozens of jurisdictions. Responsibility is assigned not only to the final failure but to every neglected condition that allowed it. Trials can last decades. Sometimes an accused person dies before judgment, and their household anchor remains provisionally burdened until the case concludes.

Yet the Fastened are not a joyless people. Their humor is full of impossible falls, incompetent surveyors, pompous councilors whose speeches need extra anchors, and lovers who confuse one another’s tether clips in the dark. They race maintenance crawlers along safe rails. They gamble on which balanced shard will turn first during a gas tide. They gather at clear overlooks to watch cliff kites crossing the sun.

During the warm evening release, households open interior shutters and sit on black thermal slabs. Hinge-backs emerge to clean between their feet. Lamps brighten one by one down the escarpment. Through shared stone they feel freight trains beginning their descent.

Sometimes a bellows herd passes in the depths. Cups tremble. Window membranes bow inward and recover. Conversation stops while the pulse enters the district, travels through pillars and floors, and fades into the upper rock.

Then everyone begins speaking again, a little more softly.

Below them are creatures so large and slow that they resemble moving terrain. Above them are migrations visible only as occasional glints. Within the cliff, membranes creep along seams older than the city. On the walls, names are gradually erased. In the railings, hands polish metal toward smoothness.

They live among all these rates of change and do not consider their world barren.

To them, barrenness would mean a place in which nothing bears weight, nothing leaves a mark, and nothing answers when touched.
