The people of the tiled plain call themselves the Near Ones, because anything beyond the reach of breath may be farther away than it looks.

They are dark-haired, heat-burnished, and slight, though not frail. Their feet are broad from walking warm tile, their palms ridged with lime, fiber, soot, and clay. Most wear short wrapped garments of woven reed or softened bark, with glazed beads sewn into the hems. Against the perpetual condensation they oil their skin and braid their hair tightly. Their oldest workers wear clay masks over the mouth and nose—not to hide their faces, but to keep each breath close enough to hear.

Every Near One is attached to a small precinct: a cistern, orchard, kiln, reed house, workshop, shrine, or open room of the Unroofed House. This attachment is more reliable than kinship. A woman may point to an ancestor seated across the court, yet call the keeper of her basin “mother,” though both appear the same age. Families are understood as patterns of maintenance. Whoever resumes your task when your hands stop is your child.

Whether anyone is born or dies is difficult to establish.

Infants are sometimes found asleep in newly fired jars large enough to hold them, their bodies damp and warm, with clay beneath their nails. The jars are always said to have been empty the previous day. The children grow, but only through discontinuities: a girl remains small for as long as anyone remembers, then is seen one noon carrying water with an adult’s shoulders. No one witnesses the change. Likewise, the dead are not buried. They sit in ancestral rooms, breathing too faintly to stir a thread, accepting bowls of oil and figs that never quite diminish. At times one rises, takes up an old tool, and quietly returns to work. No one remarks upon it.

The Near Ones rarely name themselves aloud. Names spoken at a distance may attach to the wrong person, or to a reflection, or to someone not yet present. Instead they identify one another by touch: two fingers to the wrist, a palm against the shoulder, foreheads briefly meeting. Their private names are sequences of taps made on collarbones. Their public names describe repeated acts—the Woman Who Knots the Third Cord, the Boy at the Green Stair, He Who Turns the Bowls Before Noon.

They distrust novelty but do not fear it. New things are dangerous because the world has no appointed place for them. A newly invented knot may tighten around something miles away. A fresh melody may leave a bird unable to complete its circuit. An unfamiliar doorway may open where a person was standing. Therefore every alteration is introduced ceremonially and in miniature. Masons test a new wall by arranging crumbs. Potters test new vessel shapes in beeswax. Gardeners propose a cut by laying a red thread along the branch and watching it for many days.

Their greatest experts are not rulers or priests but **accommodators**: people trained to recognize which contradiction is harmless and which may spread. They know that the leaning colonnade can be approached only by its reflection, that the third landing of the three-step stair must be crossed backward, and that a fish-shaped absence should be released immediately into shade. They paint marks where shadows are permitted to end. They move households when interiors begin exceeding their walls. They listen through tiles for changes in the water’s pressure.

Their law consists largely of prohibitions against interruption. Do not stop the seven birds from rising. Do not fill a joint deliberately left open. Do not catch the same falling leaf from two sides. Do not answer an ancestor until the ancestor has spoken with living breath. Never stand between a goat and the basin that rises when it drinks.

No one commands the whole plain. Each precinct keeps itself, exchanging cord, charcoal, oil, fish, fruit, pottery, and remembered routes with its neighbors. Disputes are settled face-to-face, close enough that both parties can hear the other’s breath. If agreement fails, a wet cord is stretched between them. They wait until one end dries. It never does, but usually someone relents.

They are builders in a world where completion is indistinguishable from origin. Masons repair cracks that have always been freshly broken. Potters make replacements for bowls that cannot decay. Thatchers renew roofs whose leaves remain green. They do not regard this as futile. Maintenance is how they remain among the living. A thing untouched may cease to be near, even if it is still visible.

The animals are maintained differently.

The seven reservoir birds are treated as a single creature with seven bodies. Their blue ledge is scrubbed every morning before they return to it. Children count the wingbeats, not because the number varies, but because one day it might. White crabs are fed mortar crumbs at the drainage mouths. Lizards are never named, lest they pause at the wrong hole. Beetles polishing the fruit are believed to preserve its moment of falling.

The fish are considered the cisterns’ thoughts. Bronze fish near the surface are simple thoughts—thirst, heat, brightness. Those below are more complex, though no one can reach their appointed depths. When someone closes a hand around a fish and raises only its absence, the cool shape is pressed against a fevered brow or carried briefly over a disputed boundary. Such absences cannot be stored. They slip through baskets, jars, and memory.

The goats of the Unroofed House belong to no precinct. Their spiral markings alter only when no one is looking directly at them. Water-keepers copy the patterns onto tablets, then compare them with the drains. The maps never correspond to the present system. Some depict channels buried below the face-down tiles; others show waterways high above, on the province’s inverted continuation. One old goat bears a spiral that appears to map the inside of the gray stone.

The three yellow dogs are the only animals whose repetition is imperfect.

They accompany the expedition on the white causeway, yet they are also seen near distant kitchens, under tilted trees, and asleep beside doors wrapped in white cord. Two always look ahead. The third looks back. People leave scraps for it at the margins, although the scraps remain untouched. Some say the dogs belong to the travelers. Others say the travelers follow the dogs. Children believe that if the backward-looking one ever barks, distance will loosen and every long-delayed journey will arrive at once.

Among the Near Ones there are also figures less securely human.

The well-keepers live on stairways descending beside water. Their skin is cool even at noon, and turquoise tesserae sometimes show through wounds instead of bone. They claim to remember chambers beneath the paving, but each describes a different architecture. One speaks of halls lined with sleeping faces. Another remembers orchards growing downward from painted rafters. A third insists there is no lower world at all, only the same uppermost surface repeatedly turned over.

The kiln-tenders are powdered white with ash and have hair singed to identical lengths. They sleep beside unfired vessels, listening to them contract before they enter flame. They say every pot already contains its eventual shard. Some can identify a vessel’s break by tapping its unbroken rim.

The gardeners are often green-stained to the elbows. They understand plants not as organisms but as openings. A vine is an aperture stretched lengthwise; a fig trunk is a door thickened into wood. They prune with extreme care, following old cuts exactly. Those who have looked into an accidental wound speak reluctantly of inner arcades, feathered darkness, miniature reservoirs, or another gardener looking outward with the same knife.

In the Unroofed House live the room-people. Their lives are exposed to the central brightness, but each behaves as though enclosed. They duck beneath absent lintels, knock before crossing bands of black tile, and lower their voices where walls would have stood. Smoke from their hearths gathers against invisible ceilings. At night—if the slight dimming of the glare deserves that name—they lie on mats and listen to footsteps moving through rooms no one can see.

They believe the House is not unfinished. Its walls have merely been built elsewhere.

At the round shell platform, attendants care for the gray stone. They wash only its dry western face. They never touch the damp east. They are selected from people who can remain still without becoming fixed in repetition. Each wears one blackened hand and one whitened hand, so that no gesture toward the stone is wholly of making or unmaking.

They deny worshiping it.

They call it the Guest.

No tradition agrees on its arrival. Some say it fell before falling existed. Some say the entire tiled province was constructed around it as a container. Others maintain that the stone is an unfired model of the world: hollow, handled invisibly from outside, and not yet placed in the kiln.

When struck, its note reaches everyone. The Near Ones turn toward it because the sound is the only event they share at the same moment. For the length of that hollow tone, distance becomes honest. The far hear what the near hear. Ancestors raise their heads. Fish leave their depths. The travelers on the white causeway seem almost to take a step.

There are those who wait for the note to continue.

They are called the Listeners Beyond the Note. They sit around the shell platform with bowls of water balanced on their knees. Most are ordinary men and women who once heard, after the stone fell silent, something further: a second resonance too low for the ear, a sound felt in mortar and teeth. They believe the first note is only a knock from the other side.

The attendants consider them harmless, provided they do not answer.

For beneath the human routines, beneath the planted marshes and appointed animals, something else inhabits the plain: not a creature in a chamber, but a vast inward pressure. It is present in the water below the tiles, in the upward-curving margins, in courtyards larger than their boundaries, in the shade cast by reflections. The Near Ones do not give it one body. They speak of it through practical warnings.

Do not sleep with your ear against a wet joint.

Do not count the layers beneath a loosened tile.

If a well opens into sunlight, cover it before that light casts your shadow.

If you hear your own hammer from two paces away, put it down.

They know that the province may be an interior: a vessel, a kiln, a cistern, a skull, an egg, a house whose walls have been turned outward. They know, too, that interiors imply an exterior. But they have no word for whatever might live there. “Outside” in their speech means not a place, but an action—something done to a boundary.

The backward-looking dog may see it.

That is why, when its mouth opens on the far white causeway, everyone who notices becomes very still. The potter’s hand pauses near the wheel. The gardener holds the blade against the old cut. The women twisting fiber keep the cord taut between them. Even the attendants at the Guest avert their eyes from its hair-fine seam.

They wait for the bark that does not come.

Perhaps the dog has been barking all along, and the sound, unlike the stone’s, cannot cross distance.

Perhaps it is waiting for someone near enough to hear.

Or perhaps the whole bright province—its people, ancestors, birds, fish, impossible gardens, falling walls, and water held under immeasurable pressure—exists within the held breath before that single bark.
