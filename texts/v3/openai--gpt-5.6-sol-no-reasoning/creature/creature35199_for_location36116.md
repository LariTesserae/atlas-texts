They are a narrow-bodied people, though not frail. Childhood in ladders, crawlways, and steep-cut stairs gives them strong hands, flexible feet, and a climber’s economy of movement. They pass one another sideways without interrupting conversation. They squat where lowlanders would seek chairs, sleep in wall hollows, and can ascend several tiers carrying a jar against one hip without spilling its sealed contents. Even the elderly retain formidable fingers. A person who can no longer climb may still braid rope, sort seed, watch infants, memorize stores, or sit near an ancient joint in the masonry and listen for change.

Their bodies bear the cleft’s colors. Skin is burnished by reflected red light and powdered with the pale dust used for cleaning. Hair is worn close, braided flat, or wrapped tightly so it will not snag on pegs and ladders. Clothing consists of fitted layers rather than loose robes: fiber tunics, hide aprons, wrapped leggings, narrow shoulder cloths. Anything trailing is dangerous. Workers wear cords around the waist to which tools are tied individually, for a fallen awl may become a weapon hundreds of feet below.

Ornaments are similarly secured. Beads are sewn down. Bronze is too scarce for display except as tiny inherited plaques, thin rings, or bells no larger than seeds. People favor polished horn, dyed lichen felt, carved bone, pigeon feathers, mirrored shell, and bits of dark stone brought from the plains. Their finest garments are not brilliant but intricate, made of fibers knotted so densely that the patterns appear only when the cloth bends.

They speak softly at close quarters. There is no need to raise the voice in a place that magnifies it. Privacy depends less on walls than on custom: one does not answer words heard through ceilings, shafts, or cliff echoes unless directly named. Children learn this alongside climbing. They also learn the whistle language of the cleft—brief, piercing phrases for danger, lifted freight, blocked passage, fire, missing animals, and the arrival of strangers below. Every tier has a recognizable cadence. Adults can often identify a caller by the shape of a single note.

Their society is organized by obligations more than possessions. A household may occupy certain rooms, keep certain jars, and use particular looms, but its standing rests on what it maintains for everyone else. One lineage tends the freight ropes at the lower stair. Another seals the storm jars. Another breeds the black wall bees and knows how to remove their combs without killing the colony. Others inspect bridges, clear gutters, tend communal ovens, preserve seed, manage dung pits, train antelope, or watch the cliff for new cracks.

These responsibilities pass down through families, but not inflexibly. A child afraid of heights will not be made a rim tender merely to satisfy ancestry. Duties are exchanged through long negotiations involving elders, apprentices, and neighboring households. The town cannot afford reverence for tradition when tradition produces incompetence.

Authority belongs chiefly to those who keep count. Water sealers record the age and contents of buried jars. Granary stewards measure seed loss to mice and mold. Lichen keepers memorize which wall was last harvested in whose childhood. Rope wardens know every main line by touch: which strands came from upper-vine fiber, which from antelope hair, which were spliced after a sandstorm, and which must be replaced before the freight season. Their records are tied into patterned cords, incised on potsherds, sung in work chants, and repeated publicly so that no single keeper can alter them unnoticed.

The most respected people are often called listeners. Some listen to stone with little horn or clay cups held against the walls. Some listen to disputes. Some listen to the hauling rhythm and can hear when a team is tiring before the rope begins to jerk. Their virtue is not silence but attention. In the cleft, catastrophe often announces itself quietly: a new grit beneath a door, a change in a pulley’s complaint, bees abandoning a wall tube, a bridge humming at the wrong pitch.

Nothing loose may be thrown over the edge, and this law shapes them far beyond refuse. Every object has a known resting place. Tools are counted after work. Children are taught never to set a cup on a parapet, never to flick a seed husk into open air, never to assume that “away” is empty. Even words are imagined as things that fall. A reckless accusation is said to have been dropped. A rumor that harms someone below one’s social tier is “a falling stone.” Apologies often take the form of repair: the offender mends a stair, replaces a household’s worn lashings, or works beneath the person injured until the debt is judged safely grounded.

Their harshest penalties rarely involve imprisonment; spare rooms are too valuable. A dangerous person may lose the right to handle fire, climb unaccompanied, work above occupied passages, or speak in public counting assemblies. Exile is uncommon and feared. Descending the mesa is not merely leaving home. It is entering a world without walls, where sound vanishes and the horizon offers nothing to hold.

Birth takes place in the middle tiers, away from the fire chambers and the exposed rims. Infants receive drops from the sealed water stores, one of the few occasions when clear water touches the lips unmingled. A newborn’s first gift is a knotted safety cord. For several years, children wear bells sewn close to the body, not to ward off spirits but so adults can hear where they are among ladders and roofways. The bells are removed when a child can cross the town alone and recite the falling law without prompting.

Youth is marked by climbing outward. Small children know the dark inner passages first. Later they are allowed onto roofs, then public bridges, then the lower and upper stairs. Their final passage into adulthood is not a leap or display of daring, for the cleft distrusts useless risk. Instead, each carries something fragile from one gate to the other: an unsealed bowl of flour, a warm egg, a wick kept glowing inside a pierced clay cup. The test is care under distraction. Friends crowd the lanes, animals obstruct the way, and elders ask questions from balconies. Success means arriving with the thing intact.

Marriage, adoption, and household joining are practical as well as affectionate arrangements. Since rooms are nested and expansions difficult, unions are described spatially: a person goes “inward” to join an established set of chambers, or “outward” when a new sleeping alcove is opened near a facade. Households may include several paired adults, widowed siblings, apprentices, unrelated elders, and fostered children. Kinship follows maintenance. The one who teaches a child to tie load knots, tends them through fever, and assigns them a share of stored food is family whether or not blood is shared.

The dead remain in the town only briefly. Bodies contain too much useful substance and too much danger to be sealed casually into inhabited walls. They are dried in warm chambers, wrapped in old household cloth, and carried to wind hollows on the distant summit pavement, where birds and weather reduce them. Later, selected bones return. Some become awls, handles, gaming pieces, or sounding taps used to test masonry. This is not considered desecration. To become a tool held securely by descendants is a respected continuation of life. Names remain with duties, songs, and repaired places rather than with graves.

Their sacred beings are not imagined as lavish givers. No benevolent river spirit could seem plausible here. They honor what withholds, endures, returns slowly, or makes limits visible: sealed water, shaded stone, patient lichen, the wind that brings dew yet punishes carelessness, the mesa that supports the town but never notices it. Shrines are rubbed rather than adorned. A worshipper presses dusty palms to a polished surface and leaves behind almost nothing.

The deepest rituals concern opening what has been sealed. When a storm jar is broken open for illness, witnesses gather. The clay plug is weighed, the wax scraped and saved, the quantity removed announced aloud. A little water is touched to the patient’s brow, but none is poured onto the floor. At rare communal ceremonies, a single shallow basin may be unveiled so children can see water lying open. They stare at its moving reflection with the unease other peoples reserve for flame.

Firekeepers possess a related gravity. Their hair and clothing are cropped especially close, and they carry lidded sand pots at all times. They tend the communal ovens, render succulent oil, smoke medicines, and extinguish every ember after cooking. They are trained to tear down a burning awning without hesitation, even if it belongs to their own household. During dry wind, they patrol the upper tiers by smell.

Daily food is dense, dry, and laboriously transformed: bitter shrub flour leached with processed pulp, seed cakes softened in milk, roasted beetles ground with salt, pigeon eggs, fermented cactus pith, smoked curds, lichen seasonings, and occasional meat from an old antelope or trapped bird. Meals are taken in small portions throughout the working day. To eat something wet is a luxury; to waste moisture by boiling carelessly is shameful. Guests are offered shade before food and food before water. A host who gives a stranger a mouthful from a sealed jar has made a promise stronger than hospitality.

Trade brings what the cleft cannot produce: timber, salt, shells, hides, resin, metal fragments, and news. Lowland traders announce themselves from far below because arriving unheralded can halt the freight stair for hours. By the time they reach the gate, half the town knows their names, cargo, animals, and recent quarrels. Visitors find the people watchful but not unfriendly. Every newcomer represents both opportunity and added demand on stores.

The inhabitants, in turn, trade cordage, dyes, medicines, sealing pitch, carved horn, hardy seed, cliff-bred pigeons, and their expertise with ropes and stone. Their woven load lines are famous. A cord made in the cleft is said to give warning before it fails. Some are braided with deliberate tonal strands so that under tension they hum, changing pitch as the load approaches danger.

Music grows naturally from such knowledge. Work songs coordinate hauling, grinding, plaster tamping, and bridge tightening. Evening music is quieter: bone flutes played into jars for resonance, finger tapping on stretched hide, stones clicked in repeating patterns, and layered voices passed between tiers. Because echoes complicate rhythm, their songs leave broad spaces into which the cliffs reply. Outsiders often mistake the reply for another choir.

Humor thrives there too, much of it based on misplaced sounds, obstructed routes, animals in dignified places, and the endless rearrangement of rooms. People joke that no one truly knows how many inhabitants the town has because some are always behind a wall, below a floor, or being counted twice through an echo. They imitate squealing pulleys, pompous pigeons, and council elders who take three hundred words to authorize a new peg.

They are not uniformly cautious. Adolescents climb forbidden ledges, race across bridges, and paint tiny figures where no elder can safely scrub them away. Smugglers conceal lowland liquor in pitch jars. Lovers meet in disused passages. Families feud over shade, noise, inherited niches, and whose antelope damaged whose drying mats. A person may hoard seed, falsify a rope count, or secretly draw water from a sickroom jar. The town’s systems are strict because its people are fallible, not because they are naturally virtuous.

Yet they possess an ingrained instinct for mutual consequence. When a hauling call begins, idle hands take up the rope without asking whose cargo rises. When a child whistles the alarm note, every nearby adult looks. When wind strains the bridges, conversations stop so people can hear the lashings. In a place where one household’s flame, dropped vessel, broken gutter, or neglected crack can endanger hundreds, independence is understood differently. Privacy is cherished; isolation is suspect.

The nonhuman inhabitants are woven into this same economy.

Pigeons are the town’s multitude: food thieves, message bearers, weather signs, and sacred nuisances. Families recognize favored birds by pattern and temperament. Their droppings fertilize planting pockets; feathers fill bedding and ceremonial cords. A sudden silence among them can empty a market faster than any shouted warning.

The cliff antelope are compact, sure-footed, and opinionated. They move through steep pens and narrow ramps with more confidence than visitors do. Their keepers milk them in the dim hours, comb out their coarse underhair, shape their horns after death, and speak to them with low clicking sounds. Old animals often know the work schedule well enough to complain before a late feeding bell.

Dogs guard roofs and lower passages, barking not only at intruders but at hawks, falling debris, and unfamiliar movement on the plains. Cats belong to granaries more than to people. Bats occupy protected recesses and are welcomed for devouring moths, though their evening emergence covers the town in a fine rain of dry droppings promptly swept for fertilizer. Bees are housed in clay tubes built directly into sun-warmed walls. Lizards are tolerated wherever they hunt insects, and some households paint small lizard signs beside warm cracks to warn children not to plug them.

Even pests are rarely treated as pure waste. Seed mice are trapped and eaten or fed to cats and snakes. Beetle larvae are harvested from dung beds. Moths too damaged for breeding are dried into poultry feed. The small brown snakes beneath granaries are left alone unless they enter sleeping rooms. People say the town has no vermin, only creatures found in the wrong tier.

Above all live the lichens, slower than animals and in some ways more socially important. A child may be born into the guardianship of a wall they will never see harvested. Elders point out faint boundaries between patches assigned to different age cohorts. To scrape another generation’s lichen is theft from people not yet grown. During lean years, this restraint becomes one of the cleft’s severest tests.

The inhabitants therefore live surrounded by futures. Rope fibers drying under the brief sun will become a bridge years later. A sealed jar may be opened for an infant not yet conceived. Clay packed into a wall will raise a floor beneath unknown feet. A lichen field darkening now may belong to grandchildren. Every repair enters the body of the settlement, and every useful fragment waits for another form.

When the wind climbs from the plains, the people feel it before the awnings move. Bees retreat into their tubes. Pigeons turn their heads. Antelope lift their narrow muzzles. Workers secure their tools by reflex, mothers draw children from exposed roofs, and rope wardens lay their palms against the main lines.

Then the pale-backed leaves turn together, and for a moment every tier becomes visible: faces in hatchways, cats on parapets, hanging cages, old hands at looms, firekeepers beside their clay basins, children gripping bridge cords, listeners touching stone.

What lives in the cleft is not merely a population sheltered from the desert. It is a single accumulation of habits, creatures, obligations, and salvaged matter—a life made durable not by abundance, but by the refusal to let anything fall unused.
