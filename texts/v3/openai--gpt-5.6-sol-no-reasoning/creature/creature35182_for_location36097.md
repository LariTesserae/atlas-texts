No single creature can be said to inhabit the hollow. The city itself is the animal, if animal is the word for something rooted, municipal, and only intermittently aware.

Its body is distributed. Towers serve as hollow bones; copper-veined bridges are tendons; the buried dynamos are warm, arrhythmic hearts. It draws water through roots and culverts, breathes through greenhouse vents, and digests leaf-fall in fermentation vats. Its nervous system is an old utility network overgrown but not abandoned: relays blink beneath moss, cables tighten in response to distant pressure, and transformer flowers open when messages pass through them. What appears to be corrosion is often feeding. What sounds like machinery may be circulation.

The countless small animals are less citizens than mobile organs.

Beetles repair breaches between bark and iron, carrying spores in one jaw and screws in the other. Glass mites clean the copper nerves. Moths gather at switch plates to read changes in current through their wings; their scales record voltages as transient patterns, which they carry from building to building. Mice redistribute warmth, seeds, insulation, and fragments of forgotten instructions. Frogs stationed in telephone mouths translate changes in air pressure into a single wet syllable. Even the high birds have a function: they carry sound into storage, then release it later in places where the city requires birdsong.

None of these creatures understands the whole. Nor does the whole seem to understand itself.

The deer are among its gardeners. Their antlers resemble roots because they are roots for part of the year. Fine tendrils enter cracks in walls and draw out salts, obsolete wiring, and dormant architectural plans. By browsing, the deer prevent rooms from sealing shut. By rubbing their crowns against doorframes, they pollinate buildings. In spring, small windows sometimes flower from their antlers. These fall before summer, take root in shaded walls, and grow rooms around themselves.

The numbered predators are older and stranger. Beneath their fur are frames of blackened alloy, but the alloy develops callus when injured and sprouts hair when cold. Their plates bear serial numbers in no consistent sequence. Several share the same number; others carry dates that have not occurred. They do not hunt bodies so much as appointments. Each chooses a place where prey is likely to exist and waits beside that possibility. Sometimes the deer arrives. Sometimes only its tracks do. The hunter consumes whichever appears first.

At warm sites live the smallest and most numerous residents: soft-bodied colonies called furnace kin. Individually they are no larger than seeds, with translucent limbs and dark stomachs. Together they form rugs, sleeves, hanging curtains, even crude figures seated around boiler doors. They feed on waste heat and break apart when the machinery cools. If watched from a distance, a colony may resemble a group of workers resting between shifts. Closer inspection reveals that every worker-shaped silhouette is composed of thousands of sleeping bodies.

Perhaps this is the origin of the city’s suggestion of humanity. It remembers people only as a useful shape.

The coats in the station are inhabited by fungal mats that preserve the posture of absent shoulders. Handrails shine because pale snails travel them each night, licking away oxidation. Platforms are kept clear by armies of minute sweepers with bristled legs. Ticket machines are nest boxes for metallic wasps. When fed the proper seed-disc, they stamp it with a destination encoded in scent, then release it into the moss. The traveling wind is made from millions of spores departing at once.

Yet occasionally a passenger does arrive.

It emerges from no visible vehicle: first a depression in the moss, then the smell of rain on cloth, then a figure assembled gradually by the station’s dimness. Such passengers are neither ghosts nor people. They are proposed inhabitants—drafts the city has made from old measurements. Their faces remain indistinct, but their hands are precise. They check watches, adjust collars, carry cases warm with animal breathing, and wait for connections to districts not yet grown.

They cannot leave the station’s immediate vicinity. In open squares they become transparent; in sunlight they collapse into pollen and lint. But under a roof they behave with quiet purpose. They enter offices, sit at desks, and perform gestures remembered by furniture: sorting, signing, dialing, pausing to listen. Sometimes one opens an empty case and releases a flock of tiny birds. Sometimes one plants a seed beneath a loose floor tile. Afterward, a new corridor begins extending through the building.

The civic greenhouse may be where these proposed people are cultivated.

Behind its production beds, accessible only when the structure turns across its own doors, lies a nursery warmer than the rest of the hollow. Rows of clay forms stand there beneath dripping pipes. Some resemble mannequins, some branching trees, some industrial molds. Roots enter them through the feet. Feathers accumulate in their chests. Their heads contain compact rooms furnished with a chair, a lamp, and a window looking onto weather that does not occur outside.

The machinery tends them patiently. Pruning arms refine hands. Seed sorters select teeth and memories. Baskets of warm feathers are emptied into the unfinished rib cages. Most forms never wake. They split, leaf, and become structural supports. A few disappear between inspections, leaving wet footprints that lead toward the station.

The oldest resident may live beneath the greenhouse, though “beneath” is unreliable there.

It occupies a chamber no larger than a cellar and no smaller than the hollow. Witnesses—mice, mostly, and one long-lived moth species—describe it only through their alterations. Mice returning from the chamber grow extra whiskers that detect doors before they exist. Moths emerge with maps on their wings, each map depicting a different city. Roots approaching the chamber divide into finer and finer threads until they resemble nerves, then retreat carrying instructions.

Whatever lies inside does not move through space. Instead, space grows around its attention.

It may be a seed: the first and largest of the dark interiors contained by every lesser seed. Its coat is said to be black, ridged with streets, and split along one side. Through the split comes a weak red light and the sound of machinery operating far away. It germinates slowly—not upward into a plant, but outward into circumstances. The hollow, its animals, its factories, and its unfinished geology may all be the first rootlets of that germination.

This would explain why the inhabitants behave as both ecosystem and construction crew. They are preparing conditions for something too large to emerge all at once. The beetles fasten its tissues. The deer prune its excess futures. The predators remove contradictions. Moths relay impulses. Birds distribute stored sound so that each new district awakens with an acoustic history. Worms manufacture the ground beneath it as needed.

Even the absent people may be provisional symbionts, grown because the coming organism anticipates needing them.

No creature appears troubled by this. Their lives are immediate and exact. Frogs call before rain. Mice raise blind young in warm conduit nests. Predators sleep with their numbered plates dim beneath their fur. Deer stand beneath the iron trees while condensation beads along their antlers. In the walls, beetles pass one another in darkness, carrying the city’s minute burdens.

Only when every leaf turns inward does the larger life become perceptible.

The pumps hesitate. Moths open their wings simultaneously. The birds overhead cease moving, though their displaced wingbeats continue. Throughout the hollow, animals put their mouths or foreheads against the nearest living surface.

Then something listens through them.

For several seconds the city knows the position of every drop of water, every tooth in every warm jaw, every loose screw, every unopened flower, every room hidden inside every seed. Its awareness is enormous but shallow, like light passing over a dark lake. It does not think in words. It measures readiness.

Then the leaves relax. The creatures separate. Machinery resumes.

Somewhere beneath the loam, the great seed has enlarged by the width of a root hair.

By morning, a building that was not there has always been inhabited.
