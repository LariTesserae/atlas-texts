The city is inhabited less by species than by transactions.

Cicadas live in its columns and make towers of them by calling. Flat-backed frogs cling beneath the roofs, striking leaves together with their bodies. Beetles drum the bark taut; translucent-throated birds fling cries from bridge to bridge; mites cross the avenues as airborne cloth. Their millions fill the city’s emptiness so completely that sound becomes its densest material.

None are citizens, yet all perform civic work.

Six-legged browsers keep the streets passable by eating them. Black birds repair walls while hunting grubs, pressing loose fibers into wounds whose larvae seal them with sap. Snails drink the climbing rain. Threadlike fish inhabit water only while it travels, then fold themselves into wet seeds to wait. Bats pour from chambers too small to have contained them and return through windows belonging to other districts. Lizards tighten bridges by crossing them. Moths scatter architecture into motion. Predatory vines hunt without uprooting themselves.

Long-bodied cats have learned that a roar can become a door. They wait beside seed halls and vocalize openings into the walls. Their prey answer in pitches that revise the space—making the new doorway plunge, thorn, narrow, or lead elsewhere. Here survival belongs not merely to the swift or concealed, but to whatever can persuade the city to have a different shape.

There are also inhabitants that may be weather, memory, or consequence.

Blind mice emerge where the shadow fruit falls. Red frogs arrive once each wet season in such numbers, and with such perfect unanimity, that distance buckles around their note. Birds return from routes that ceased to exist carrying pollen from flowers that never opened. Flocks fly down into soil. Rain sometimes inhabits the city before the cloud that will produce it has arrived. In the western avenue, absent trees continue to host moss, birds, and runoff; whatever once lived there has vanished more thoroughly than death, yet its habits remain occupied.

The plants themselves cannot be separated from the population. They are dwellings, roads, traps, reservoirs, organs, and slow animals. Rootways tighten under traffic. Roof leaves anticipate storms. Cistern flowers lift water into the air. Bridges hunt insects. Facades bloom for minutes and dismantle themselves. Fungi mark intersections with pale light, while flowers imitate watching eyes. Old structures survive as outlines after losing their substance, sustained by creatures that still perch, shelter, feed, or call as though the missing walls remained.

At the center lives—or recurs—the vast tree whose body is absent but whose shadow is exact. It bears shadow fruit, opens the pavement, and exchanges organisms between incompatible directions. Perhaps the whole basin is inside it. Perhaps the city is what feeds on its nonexistence.

At the boundary, the inhabitants become emigrants only by ceasing to be themselves. Butterflies leave as leaves. Herd beasts depart as knots beneath mud. Calls go out as flocks. Wind enters as insects, sunlight as a golden crawling creature. Nothing crosses unchanged, and so the city’s population cannot be counted by bodies alone. It must include transformations, noises, cavities, remembered structures, future rain, and every behavior that keeps an absence standing.

What lives there is a collective without membership: millions of mouths, wings, roots, throats, bladders, spores, shadows, and echoes continuously making one another habitable. No creature understands the city. No creature intends it. Yet each feeds, flees, mates, drinks, hunts, calls, and repairs in ways that cause the city to arise around them.

Its true inhabitant may be that arising—the immense, deafening life produced between things, occupying the hollows where no single organism lives.
