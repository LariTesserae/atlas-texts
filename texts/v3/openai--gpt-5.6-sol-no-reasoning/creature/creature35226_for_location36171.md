The inhabitants of this range are not separate from it in any simple way. They live within its hide, drink from its breath, and wake when its buried mechanisms turn. Some are animals and some are plants; others occupy an older uncertainty between organism, mineral, and machine.

Beneath the groundcover move the pale grazers. They are blunt, flexible creatures with wedge-shaped skulls, no visible necks, and skin the color of frost seen through soil. Their forequarters are dense with muscle, while the rest of the body tapers enough to pass through narrow seams. They do not dig into earth so much as part the moss, swimming beneath interwoven roots without severing them. Warm blood—or something serving the same purpose—keeps their bodies well above the night temperature. Steam trails mark their passage, and afterward the vegetation closes behind them.

They feed on translucent shoots, fungal knots, and the soft mineral foam that forms around seasonal springs. Their mouths are small and hidden underneath the skull. They graze continuously but slowly, pausing whenever a vibration enters the stone. In still weather, whole herds may remain motionless beneath a single slope, detectable only as a region where frost melts in wandering lines.

On the exposed walls cling six-legged browsers. Their bodies are flat and radially jointed, each limb ending in a pad fringed with microscopic hooks. They can cross damp rock upside down and hold fast when temporary rivers pour over them. Rows of transparent teeth rasp at the lichens, wearing away and regrowing from silica dissolved in their food. Their shells resemble the dark, rounded ribs of the mountain so closely that only the regular sweep of their feeding betrays them.

They are patient enough for moss to grow across their backs. The oldest carry entire gardens: ferns rooted between plates, beads of water, colonies of tiny scavengers. When these browsers migrate, the gardens migrate with them, carrying spores between cliffs otherwise divided by miles of empty air.

The thin-winged creatures live deeper in fissures. Folded, they resemble strips of wet black membrane pressed into cracks. Unfolded, some span wider than a person is tall, though their bodies weigh almost nothing. Their wings are webbed with conductive filaments that sense the mountain’s electrical field. They do not beat them. When charge gathers below, they crawl to exposed ledges, open themselves, and are lifted.

They drift rather than fly, altering course by changing the resistance of different wing-veins. In the valleys they may hang nearly motionless for hours, each animal suspended at a slightly different electrical contour. From below, a flock appears as a loose constellation of missing pieces in the cloud. They descend when the field weakens, often many ridges from where they began, and feed by pressing narrow faces into newly opened flowers.

Those flowers are themselves temporary inhabitants of the cycle. Most of the time they exist as sealed bulbs under the moss, indistinguishable from black pebbles. When the roots brighten, the bulbs split into lidless cups. Their surfaces hold an oily blue-black radiance, and their interiors are warmer than the surrounding air. Flyers gather over them. Crawling pollinators rise from the soil. For a short interval, the hidden floor of the landscape becomes densely flowered.

By the time the mountain cools, the cups have shut. Some will not open again for years.

The long-bodied stream hunters wait for this abundance. They lie parallel to dry channels, narrow enough to be mistaken for water-darkened roots. They have no eyes and no need of them. Along their jaws and bellies are buried organs sensitive to minute changes in charge and pressure. Through these they perceive the land as a shifting architecture of pulses: grazing bodies beneath moss, water entering fissures, wings lifting into the field.

When storm water fills a channel, burrowing animals are driven upward. The hunters unfold with startling speed. Their jaws open sideways, creating a broad trap against the current. After feeding, they sink back into the saturated vegetation, leaving only a slight darkening where their bodies lie.

Above them, on the high saddles, walk the largest predators. Their outlines are difficult to hold in sight. They are broad-backed and low-headed, covered in loose filaments that collect moisture and mimic heath. Each foot divides on contact into a fringe of many soft digits, spreading the animal’s weight across frost and moss. Even the most delicate groundcover is rarely crushed beneath them.

They hunt less by pursuit than by placement. Sensing the deep mechanisms accelerate, they choose the passages through which prey will soon move and stand there without sound. Moss slowly folds under their warmth. Condensation forms on their flanks. Sometimes a flyer lands on one and roosts, mistaking the unmoving predator for an outcrop.

Their mouths are not made for tearing. They engulf smaller animals and hold them in internal chambers where heat and mineral-rich fluids dissolve them over days. Fed predators may remain on a ridge for an entire season, gradually acquiring a green skin of opportunistic growth.

Many smaller beings live in the wake of these creatures. Threadlike scavengers follow heat trails beneath the vegetation. Clear-bodied mites drink from droplets before they fall. Soft colonial animals inhabit the porous shelves around springs and retreat into the mineral when the water stops. In the tarns, ribbon-shaped organisms assemble themselves into spirals whenever current passes through the water. Each ribbon is nearly mindless alone. Joined to thousands of others, they turn like a single slow wheel, concentrating dissolved metals at the center.

When the current ends, the wheel comes apart.

Fungi bind almost all of this life together. Their threads penetrate moss, root, carcass, mineral foam, and machine-stone. They conduct the faint discharge that crosses the slopes, but they also modify it. A damaged patch can redirect a current around itself. A rich feeding ground can draw one closer. Dense fungal knots store charge and release it gradually through the dark intervals, keeping small ecosystems warm after the deep engines have slowed.

Some knots become mobile. Encased in soft mats of root and lichen, they creep by growing at one edge and dying at the other. These wandering colonies may take a century to cross a valley. Animals shelter in them; flowers open along them out of season. Whether they are creatures in their own right or merely pieces of the range that have learned to travel is impossible to say.

The deepest inhabitants are stranger still.

In warm faults, mineral structures grow around circulating water. Many are inert accretions, but some regulate their own growth. Crystal branches channel heat toward one surface and precipitate basalt along another. Valves form where repeated pressure favors hinges. Columns descend, reset, and descend again. Conductive lattices spread until they meet fungal roots, and from that contact both systems change.

These mechanisms reproduce only in the broadest sense. A successful arrangement alters local flows so that similar arrangements become more likely nearby. A failed one fractures and is absorbed. Over geological time, the mountain selects among its own internal shapes.

There may be no individual organism below—only chambers, weights, turbines, mineral membranes, and water behaving collectively. Yet the deep systems heal around obstructions. They close unused vents. They abandon exhausted strata. They sometimes preserve a useful fault long after ordinary pressure should have collapsed it. The animals time their lives to these decisions, if decisions they are.

The great toll awakens everyone.

Pale grazers break through the moss in steaming numbers. Cliff browsers lift their plated heads. The winged flocks open and rise into the strengthening field. Stream hunters slide toward channels that are still dry. High predators begin their measured descent. Tarn ribbons join into wheels, flowers unseal, fungal knots discharge, and spores lift like dark dust.

For a few minutes the whole range becomes visibly alive—not crowded, but coordinated. Every inhabitant follows a different need, yet all are drawn into the same enormous cycle.

Then the note fades. The newly opened waters choose their paths. The lights divide among the roots. Creatures vanish again into cracks, moss, cloud, and distance.

What lives there is a community, but also a circulation. Predator and grazer, fungus and flower, crystal and buried engine are organs without a body wall. The mountains contain them, and they in turn maintain the mountains: binding their slopes, clearing their channels, carrying their charge, feeding their slow machinery with water, metal, decay, and movement.

Nothing rules the range. Nothing observes it from outside.

It lives by passing the impulse on.
