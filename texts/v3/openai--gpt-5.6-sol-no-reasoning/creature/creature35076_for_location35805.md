The town is inhabited first by people who understand continuance: mill hands, barge crews, mechanics, gardeners, pump keepers, dyers, starch workers, press operators, carpenters, laundresses, clerks, and drain wardens. Many belong to several of these trades at once. A person who tends a combing machine during the day may repair rain screens in the evening, cultivate taro behind a workshop, and take a flood-watch shift when the river rises. The place does not reward narrow competence. Everyone knows something about water, rot, plants, and machinery.

They are compactly housed but not crowded in the ordinary sense, because much of life extends vertically and into the vegetation. Families live above workshops in deep, narrow rooms whose windows open into leaves. Meals are taken on screened balconies or beneath covered passages. Children learn the town by its bridges: the loose plank near the dye house, the stone arch by the washing basins, the iron footplate that hums when a pump starts. They know which drains are harmless to wade in and which conceal a hard current. In rain they sail husk boats through the gutters and retrieve them downstream with hooked sticks.

The inhabitants dress for warmth, damp, and snagging machinery. Their clothes are light but close-fitted at wrists and ankles, often made from the town’s own fibers and waxed or oiled at the shoulders. Aprons are common, as are rubber-soled clogs, wrapped calves, headscarves, and broad hats woven tightly enough to turn a moderate rain. Mechanics carry short tools in flat belts that will not catch on shafts. Garden workers wear sleeves against sap and reeds. Almost everyone keeps a dry change of clothing at work, rolled high on a shelf above the oldest flood line.

Damp marks them. Hair curls or lies heavily against the head. Hands are ridged by water, stained by resins and dyes, or polished at the palms by handles. Older workers often have a slight forward set to the shoulders from years beneath low pipes and hanging greenery. Voices tend to be measured, pitched to carry through mechanical noise without becoming shouts. Indoors, communication is made as much by taps, bells, hand signs, and changes in posture as by speech.

Their sense of time comes from cycles rather than clocks alone. They speak of the first pump, the second steaming, the barge tide, the settling hour, the belt change, the evening drawdown. A press holding at full weight creates a pause long enough for tea. Drying trays divide a shift. Rainfall is judged by where water has reached—the third step, the blue wall, the upper grate—not merely by duration. The river’s rise is read in the tone of culverts and the backward trembling of duckweed near the gates.

No one treats flooding as an exceptional drama. When water threatens, households and mills perform the same practiced sequence. Boards come down from rafters. Jars are moved upstairs. Motors are disconnected or lifted. Bundles of fiber are transferred to upper racks. Children carry stools and utensils while adults check neighbors’ gates. Someone walks the lanes striking drain covers with a rod, listening for blockages. Meals continue on higher floors. The response is communal but rarely ceremonial; competence itself is the ritual.

Authority belongs partly to those who remember water. The oldest pump keepers and river workers can identify a failing gate by vibration through the masonry. They know which cellar will fill first, which courtyard can safely hold overflow, and which retaining wall leaks because it is relieving pressure rather than collapsing. Their knowledge is stored in anecdotes, stains, repairs, and terse instructions painted near valves. Formal plans exist in municipal offices, curled at the edges by humidity, but the living map of the basin resides in bodies that have crossed it during storms.

The mill owners and administrators are present, though they cannot remain separate from the town’s material life. Their offices occupy upper rooms with warped cabinets, ceiling fans, ledgers swollen by damp, and windows screened by vines. Wealth appears less as spotless luxury than as elevation, ventilation, dry storage, and ownership of reliable pumps. The most desirable houses are not the grandest but those on the slight ridge, with two exits, deep eaves, and courtyards that drain toward public channels rather than neighboring foundations.

There are specialists in decay. Plasterers remove what has bloomed and seal what can be saved. Timber workers test walkways by sound. Roof gardeners know which roots bind soil without piercing resin panels. Fermentation keepers distinguish useful souring from spoilage by heat and smell. Drain crews cultivate particular plants in settling ponds and thin them before they choke the flow. To an outsider they may look like gardeners; in practice they are part engineer, part sanitation worker, part ecologist.

Plant life is not scenery to these people. It is infrastructure, raw material, inheritance, and sometimes adversary. Families propagate favored vines because they cast dense shade without becoming too heavy. Fruit trees mark old agreements between adjoining properties. Cuttings pass through kinship networks. A tree leaning across an alley may belong to one house, support a cable serving another, shade a third, and brace its roots in municipal earth. Its pruning requires negotiation resembling law.

The town’s animals live mostly at its margins and seams. Frogs inhabit the stepped ponds, their voices briefly audible when mills stop. Pale geckos patrol lamp-lit plaster and are tolerated as quiet household workers. Fish move beneath the opaque channel, known through ripples, nets, and occasional silver flashes at sluice gates. Cats are uncommon in the wet industrial center but appear on upper roofs and warehouse rafters, long-bodied and careful-footed, sleeping where warm pipes keep the corrugations dry. Dogs, where kept, are usually small enough to carry across floodwater and sensible enough not to enter machinery halls.

Human children occupy some of the ecological space left empty by larger animals. They climb trellises, harvest roof fruit, carry messages through passages too narrow for carts, and collect useful debris after storms. They know where moths gather, where frogs spawn, and which warm outflows attract fish. Their games imitate the town’s systems: miniature sluices cut into mud, presses made from boards and stones, barges folded from fiber sheet, whole imagined factories fed by leaf pulp.

At night the population seems to contract upward. Ground-floor shutters close, bridges gleam beneath lamps, and voices drift from balconies concealed by wet leaves. Shift workers move in small groups along the covered lanes. Pump crews remain below, checking grates with poles and touching bearing housings for heat. In houses, fans stir the smell of cooked roots, oil, soap, wet masonry, and flowering vines. Rain chains tick into jars. Somewhere a family sorts usable fiber scraps while listening to the low machinery that never entirely stops.

The dead remain close as well. Names are stamped into old iron braces, carved beneath handrails, painted on pump housings, or spoken when a repair follows someone’s long-established method. There may be a cemetery on the higher edge of the basin, dense with ferns and raised tombs, but memory is more widely distributed than that. A certain flood board was fitted by a grandfather; a roof vine descends from a cutting brought by an aunt; a bypass pipe bears the nickname of the mechanic who forced it through an upstairs window during a season of relentless rain.

What lives here, finally, is not a population divided cleanly from its setting. It is a human community braided into managed water and cultivated growth. Its residents are neither masters of the basin nor passive inhabitants of it. They keep making small agreements with gravity, moisture, roots, corrosion, and time. Their town survives not because it resists change, but because everyone in it has learned how to redirect change before it becomes disaster.

They live as the buildings do: patched rather than replaced, supported in unexpected places, marked by old water, useful beyond their intended function, and quietly prepared to let the next rain pass through.
