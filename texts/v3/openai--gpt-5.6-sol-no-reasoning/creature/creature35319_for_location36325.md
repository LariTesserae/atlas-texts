The basin’s inhabitants are difficult to separate from its geology. Nothing here possesses the quick, declarative movement of familiar animals. Life, if that is what it is, has adopted the country’s patience: translucent, mineral-colored, and most active at rates the eye mistakes for stillness.

Beneath the flooded crusts live the many-legged crawlers. Their bodies are clear enough that the dark grit below can be seen through them, interrupted only by faint internal knots and slow pulses of clouded fluid. Their numerous limbs are hair-fine and arranged along the body like the teeth of a delicate comb. They do not swim. Instead they fasten themselves to opposite sides of a seam and draw forward by imperceptible contractions, crossing in an hour the distance a raindrop covers in a moment.

Their shape is not constant. In broad cracks they spread flat, becoming disks or branching films. In narrow pores they lengthen into almost invisible threads. A stranded individual gradually stiffens and turns pale, indistinguishable from a vein of fresh salt until water returns. Then its joints loosen, transparency passes through it from one end to the other, and it resumes its migration.

They appear to feed where pressure is changing. When water enters a previously dry fissure, they gather along the advancing edge—not behind it, where dissolved minerals are plentiful, but immediately before it, in stone that has not yet visibly become wet. Perhaps they sense minute flexures in the rock. Perhaps their bodies draw nourishment from electrical differences between saturated and unsaturated mineral. Whatever the process, they leave no scrap, shell, stain, or waste. Their only trace is an occasional row of tiny, evenly spaced pits in a seam.

Deeper down are the pale threads. Some are certainly crystalline growths, but others recoil from light and return after darkness. They hang in flooded cavities as loose skeins, branching without symmetry. A disturbance at one end travels through an entire cluster as a slow whitening. They may be rooted organisms, colonies, or merely a stage in the many-legged creatures’ lives. At times a crawler reaches one of these masses and is taken into it. At other times a portion of the mass separates, gathers itself around angular grains, and eventually crawls away on newly formed limbs.

The black forms inhabiting the columns are creatures of air pressure rather than open flight. Folded into their holes, they resemble flakes of volcanic glass. Rain and intensified resonance wake them. They ease out onto the wet stone, bodies softening into thin, dark membranes stretched over a sparse framework of ribs. When a pressure pulse passes through the stone forest, they release their grip and flatten themselves against the answering movement of the air.

They do not flap. They are carried from one vibrating surface to another, steering by wrinkling their edges. Most journeys span only a hand’s breadth, yet colonies can spread across the basin by a succession of such crossings. They favor narrow fins and hollow chimneys, attaching themselves at points where the stone’s vibration is strongest. There they remain pressed against the rock, perhaps drinking condensation, perhaps absorbing the tremor itself. An occupied column has a slightly muffled voice.

Their bodies bear no visible eyes or mouths. Under luminous breaks in the cloud, however, they turn edge-on to the reflected light. Hundreds may align at once, converting a cluster of towers into ranks of black, trembling strokes. If startled, they retreat not toward shelter but toward louder stone.

The orderly chambers contain another kind of inhabitant, though it is rarely visible as a body. The loose stones within them do not remain where they are disturbed. Pebbles return to pebbles, fist-sized fragments to fragments of equal weight, larger pieces to the chamber’s rear. The sorting happens during the deepest nocturnal groans, when the floor transmits long waves from unseen cavities. A stone may quiver, rotate, and advance the width of a fingernail. Over many nights the whole arrangement restores itself.

Something occupies the intervals among these stones. Occasionally a silver meniscus rises where no water lies, outlining a low shape that crosses the chamber and vanishes between two slabs. At other times several pebbles lift together by less than a grain’s width. No track follows. The dry threshold remains inviolate.

Whether this hidden resident built the chambers is uncertain. It may instead have arisen because of them: a coherent knot of pressure, trapped and made persistent by the converging note. Each chamber sustains a different tone, and whatever dwells within seems fitted to that tone as precisely as a soft-bodied animal fits its shell. If a supporting slab shifts and the note changes, the sorting changes too. In damaged chambers, stones gather uselessly against one wall, and the silver outlines appear fragmented.

At the northeastern water ring, all these uncertain forms converge.

The many-legged creatures lie layered beneath the glowing intersections, their transparent bodies overlapping until they resemble thick plates of living ice. Pale threads climb from fissures in the bed and pass through them, linking one body to the next. Above, black membrane-creatures cling to the encircling wall in dense bands. They launch whenever a drop strikes the still surface, riding the almost inaudible displacement that accompanies each submerged circle of light.

The gathering is neither feeding nor mating in any recognizable sense. It is closer to an assembly of parts.

As luminous circles overlap, the creatures below alter their arrangement. Limbs extend from one body and are accepted into another. Internal pulses synchronize. The pale threads brighten at their junctions, reproducing on a smaller scale the geometry shining across the floor. From time to time an entire layer rises slightly from the bed without disturbing the surface. It holds there, organized into a broad translucent sheet, then settles again.

During the largest gatherings, the ring itself changes pitch. The effect spreads outward through the stone forest. Hollow columns answer first, then fins, plates, submerged shelves, and finally the small chambers across the flats. Their sustained notes enter alignment one after another. Black forms leave their perches in great numbers and pass between the towers like windblown ash, though there is no wind. Hidden presences stir among sorted stones. Pale threads emerge from fissures far beyond the depression.

For several minutes, the lowland behaves less like a habitat than a single distributed animal.

Its hard parts are the resonant formations. Its circulation is the wandering water. Confined air becomes breath; pressure pulses become signals; the clear crawlers, threads, and membranes serve as mobile tissues, carrying minute adjustments from one district to another. The chambers may be sensory organs or knots of memory, maintaining stable notes against which every alteration can be measured. The luminous pool is not necessarily a heart or brain, but it is where the basin gathers its awareness most densely.

Then the alignment passes. The creatures separate. The dark membranes fold into column holes. Threads withdraw below the limit of light. Transparent crawlers resume their nearly motionless journeys along the seams.

Only the rearranged stones remain to suggest that anything assembled there at all.

Thus what lives in the basin may be counted in several ways: as a sparse fauna of clear crawlers, responsive filaments, and pressure-gliding black membranes; as invisible occupants of resonant shelters; or as one immense, unfinished being whose organs have not yet decided they belong together.

It is newborn by the measure of stone. It is still learning its boundaries.

Every groan is a test of its skeleton. Every seep redraws its interior. Every rare fracture becomes a sensation carried from tower to tower until the whole lowland has registered the loss. The inhabitants do not merely endure these changes. They attend them, collect beneath their intersections, and perhaps preserve them in ordered stones and altered tones.

The basin’s roar, then, may be more than incidental noise.

It may be the sound by which this scattered life continually discovers the shape of the body it inhabits.
