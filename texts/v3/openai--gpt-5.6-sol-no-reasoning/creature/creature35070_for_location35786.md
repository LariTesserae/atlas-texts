The Green Hollow is inhabited by a community shaped less by darkness than by intervals: the long restraint between rains, the brief abundance when the ceiling leaks, and the rapid retreat into mud, root, and stone afterward.

Its largest permanent inhabitants are the hand-climbers of the root canopy. They are warm-bodied creatures no heavier than a clenched fist, covered not in fur but in fine, dark skin with a soft grain like the underside of a leaf. Their ears are broad, translucent, and mobile. Veins show through them whenever one passes near a shaft of amber light. Their forelimbs end in narrow hands with opposing inner digits, allowing them to travel upside down along roots, transfer between hanging cords, and descend the vertical root-columns without touching the floor.

They seldom leap and never fly. Instead they move hand over hand with extraordinary patience, freezing whenever the roots tremble. Their dry clicks are both sight and speech. A solitary click measures the nearest curtains of vegetation; a paired click warns that something large has shifted below; scattered replies maintain the colony across partitions too dense for its members to see one another. Before the rains they descend toward the pools, licking moisture from stone joints and biting open the soft new fungi. They also hunt moths, larvae, mites, and the smallest geckos, though they will sit motionless beside one another rather than compete noisily over food.

Their young are carried against the belly, clasping folds of skin until their hands strengthen. Later they are left in cradles woven from living rootlets. These are not true nests: the adults bend and knot only what is already growing, and the roots continue to thicken around each cradle. Abandoned cradles remain as woody baskets in the ceiling. Moss fills them. Moths sleep inside them. Sometimes the translucent skin of a dead infant remains stretched between the fibers, mistaken at first glance for fungus.

Closer to the floor live the hollow’s long-bodied amphibians. They are smooth, dark, and heavy-headed, with reduced limbs and mouths wider than their bodies suggest. Most lie folded into wet root creases or buried in mud with their nostrils exposed. When seepage begins, they emerge all at once. Their calls are too low to carry cleanly through air; instead they press throat and jaw to the ground, sending brief pulses through mud and stone. A human hand laid on a flood shelf would feel the courtship before hearing it.

They lay soft ropes of eggs in temporary basins. The hatchlings are voracious because their pools may exist for only hours. They eat fungal spores, larvae, shrimp, sibling eggs, and one another. Some mature enough to crawl away before the water fails. Others respond to shrinking pools by secreting a clear sheath and fastening themselves beneath leaves. There they remain as curled, glassy commas until the next rain.

The three permanent pools support slower lives. Blind shrimp comb drowned root hairs with delicate fans. Colorless fish rest beneath the tannin-dark surface, their eyes reduced to silver dimples under the skin. They sense falling droplets through the water and gather below likely feeding places before anything lands. A broader pale hunter lives among them—perhaps a salamander, perhaps something descended from fish—which buries itself in the soft bottom and opens a circular mouth when prey passes overhead. It is seen only in pieces: a finlike fold, a clouded flank, the sudden white interior of the mouth.

Some of the smaller fish travel when rain connects the basins. Their fins are muscular and jointed enough to brace against moss. They cross wet shelves in short, convulsive advances, pausing beneath leaves to draw air through the lining of the mouth. Many fail. Their bodies are quickly opened by beetles or drawn into the digestive cups of the bladder plants. Those that succeed carry eggs and parasites from pool to pool, keeping the three waters from becoming entirely separate worlds.

The mud banks belong to pale crabs. Their shells are thin and cream-colored, stained rust at the joints. Each maintains a burrow above the permanent waterline, lining it with chewed root fiber. During the dry hush it closes the entrance with a carefully fitted plug of mud, leaving only pores fine enough for air. When rain approaches, the crabs emerge before the first seepage. They stand at their doorways with claws raised, reading pressure changes through the sensitive membranes between their leg segments.

They are gardeners without intention. They drag fungi, dead larvae, leaf tissue, and dung into their burrows, and discarded fragments sprout or mold around the entrances. Their tunneling aerates the banks. Collapsed burrows make channels for seepage. In this way whole green shelves begin around generations of crab holes.

The geckos inhabit the ceiling between the hand-climbers’ routes. Mineral mottling breaks up their bodies: pale bands like calcite, red patches like clay, black specks like wet stone. Their toes spread over condensation films without slipping. They drink by drawing their eyes into their mouths with long sweeps of the tongue. Most have learned the rhythms of the clicking colonies. At a warning pair they flatten themselves. At the diffuse volley that follows heavy seepage, they move toward the amber fissures, where insects gather.

Thread-snakes live deeper in those fissures. They are scarcely thicker than root hairs and often lie braided among them. Their jaws open just wide enough to take sleeping moths, froglets, or gecko eggs. A feeding snake may remain anchored in a crack for half a day while it draws prey inward by alternating contractions. Its scales carry faint luminous bacteria, so that after feeding it sometimes appears as a green line retreating into stone.

The insects and mites comprise most of the hollow’s animal life. Mirrored beetles trap a layer of humid air beneath their wing cases. Root moths spend nearly all their adult lives hanging head-down, wings closed, waiting to mate during the rain interval. Their caterpillars tunnel through dead portions of roots but avoid living tissue, perhaps deterred by sap, perhaps tended away from it by the plants’ chemistry. Pinpoint larvae in the mud flash blue-green when disturbed. One pulse draws others; a whole bank may briefly resemble a low night sky before predators arrive and the lights go out.

The plants are equally predatory and responsive. The broad black leaves along the walls turn inward during dry periods, protecting stomata beneath their waxed surfaces. When condensation thickens, they unfold. Their bruised green flesh attracts small grazers, and some leaves appear to exploit this: weak seams tear under an animal’s weight, folding it into a sticky inner surface.

The pale-cupped plants of the pools are more direct. Their cups collect condensation and drowned insects, but their deepest nourishment comes during breeding intervals, when eggs and hatchlings crowd the shallows. Fine inward-pointing hairs prevent escape. A filled cup slowly lowers itself beneath the water, digests its contents, then rises empty days later.

Fungi connect nearly everything. Their luminous threads run beneath root skins, into mud banks, around old stone, and through the abandoned cradles of the canopy dwellers. Some fruit only when the first storm water reaches them. Others produce soft translucent bodies in the final hours of seepage, when animals are hungriest. Their glow is probably not meant to light the chamber; it may attract grazers that disperse spores, or warn them away until the spores mature. Different species make different use of the same dim colors, and the animals have learned distinctions invisible to an outsider.

The old stone ring has its own population. Crabs occupy the undermined joints. Moss grows thickest on its inner face. A nearly transparent snail grazes mineral film from the fitted surfaces, leaving clean spirals that can look like deliberate markings. Tiny white worms live in the water beading along the seams. During rains they emerge simultaneously, hanging in threads from the masonry, and the ring seems fringed with fine moving hair.

Something larger may also use the ring.

Tracks sometimes begin at the pool’s edge and end at the three steps: broad, five-part impressions pressed deeply into fresh mud, accompanied by no tail drag. They do not resemble the hands of the ceiling colonies, though they may belong to unusually large individuals. Moss is periodically rubbed from the lowest step. The parallel grooves on the upper one hold scraps of translucent shed skin and crushed fungal tissue. No den has been found in the wall where the stairs terminate, but roots there are often displaced, and clicks made nearby receive answers that are lower, slower, and separated by intervals too long to map easily.

The other inhabitants avoid the steps during the hot hush. When rain begins, however, beetles gather there. Amphibians lay eggs in the footprints. The hand-climbers descend and sit around the ring, each gripping a root or stone seam, their ears turned toward the sealed wall.

Nothing emerges while they are watching.

After the seepage ends, there is sometimes one fewer animal at the pool, and a new dark track leading upward to the place where the steps stop.
