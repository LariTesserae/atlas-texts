The people of the basin are pale, though not uniformly so. Their skin carries the colors of materials repeatedly handled: chalk at the knuckles, gray fungal stain around the nails, bluish salt along the scalp, black glass dust embedded in old cuts. In districts beneath strong veins, generations acquire a faint translucence about the ears and eyelids. In the dim wards they are broader-faced and dark-eyed, with pupils that close to pinpricks when an unexpected sheet of brilliance crosses the street.

They walk softly and low. This is partly custom, partly caution. Raised heads are vulnerable to descending stone, and a hard heel can conceal the tremor of a shifting floor. Most go barefoot or wear thin sole-wraps made from cured gut, textured differently at toe and heel. Through these they read dampness, powder, warmth, and vibration. A child who cannot yet name the neighboring plazas may nevertheless find home in darkness by the grain of the road.

Their cities have taught them not to confuse possession with permanence. A household owns what it can number, dismantle, and carry: door slabs, roof skins, cistern stoppers, grinding bowls, sleeping mats, sealed cultures, hooks, and jars of ancestral fragments. Walls are less often owned than occupied. A family may inhabit masonry laid by strangers dead for centuries, dismantle it when a fault begins to murmur, then discover its numbered blocks already bear older numbers beneath their own.

Kinship follows carried things. One belongs to the people of a particular hearthstone, jar, fungus strain, or copper tool, even after the group has divided and migrated to opposite sides of the basin. The oldest families are not those with the longest genealogies but those whose objects have the longest repair histories. A hook with six generations of owners is spoken of as if it remembers them all.

Their names are similarly provisional. Children receive a private name at birth, breathed against the crown of the head so softly that only household members know it. Public names accumulate from conditions: Born-During-Whitefall, Bitter-Pool Daughter, Three-Rains, Warm-Sole, Carries-the-Blue-Door. If the condition becomes dangerously inaccurate, the name changes. To retain an obsolete name is considered either holy or foolish.

Few professions are solitary. Light steering requires watchers stationed at intervals, relaying adjustments with flashes, cords, and hand signs. Cistern keeping involves tasters, sealers, crack listeners, and those who memorize the behavior of invisible drains. Builders work in circles around a structure, each person responsible for feeling how the blows of the others travel through the stone. Even butchery is communal: blood cannot be exposed without attracting fliers, scavengers, and disputes over nutrients.

The most respected specialists are listeners.

They lie with cheek or breastbone pressed to the ground and distinguish the city’s ordinary movements from dangerous ones: sledges, herds, water behind stone, the ticking expansion of salt, a hidden cavity beginning to shed its roof. They use shallow ceramic bowls filled with shell beads, oil, or milk. Patterns of ripples reveal vibrations too fine to feel directly. Some listeners are practical surveyors. Others interpret distant impacts and returning voices as messages. The distinction between those occupations is debated but never settled.

A listener may halt a market by raising two fingers.

No one complains until afterward.

The mirror guilds are less revered and more powerful. Their crews determine which chambers receive brightness and when. They are recognizable by scarred cheeks and layered eye veils: sliding mica shutters, smoked gut membranes, little perforated plates of bone. Their forearms are crosshatched with burns from concentrated reflections. They speak in geometric shorthand and have a reputation for arrogance, since a slight adjustment of one plate can warm a nursery, spoil a fungus vault, wake a colony of fliers, or leave a council sitting in darkness.

They insist that light is neither divine nor free. It must be caught, divided, moderated, and repaid.

When the ceiling brightens violently, citizens cover reflective objects and shelter in the blue shadows of walls. Animals flatten themselves against stone. Only the mirror crews remain exposed, hauling plates into harmless angles while their warning flashes leap from district to district. Later they collect the tiny creatures killed by the glare and render them for oil.

Water keepers possess a different authority. They taste cisterns with strips of porous ceramic held beneath the tongue. A good keeper can identify metal seepage, fungal contamination, carcass rot, bitter salts, and the first influence of the black river. Their mouths are often ulcered; their teeth wear strangely colored bands. When a keeper declares water unfit, the cistern is sealed even if a district must move.

Citizens eat what can be extracted without sunlight, soil, or waste. Fungus loaves are steamed in sealed pots, shaved into translucent sheets, or fermented until sharply mineral in flavor. Herd milk is mixed with ground shells to make pale curds. Small grazers are roasted whole on hot slabs. Bladder fliers are bled into bowls, their skins dried for lamp membranes and valves. Nothing is discarded casually. Hair becomes cord filler. Bones become meal for growth rooms. Excrement is sorted by diet and destination. The dead feed sealed chambers unless they have died of an unknown wasting, in which case they are placed in dry hollows and watched.

Meals are eaten close together and in relative darkness. Brightness invites fliers and exposes food to rapid skinning by microbial films. Conversation is conducted by touch as often as by voice: fingers on a wrist for disagreement, a knuckle against the shoulder for warning, two taps at the base of the thumb to request water. Lovers develop private tapping languages. So do thieves.

Their speech is quiet but elaborate. Words for distance are poor; words for surfaces are exact. They distinguish rock that powders under pressure, rock that rings but should not be struck, rock that sweats before rain, rock warm from buried water, and rock warm for no understood reason. Their verbs mark whether an action is expected to remain possible. One does not simply live in a house, cross a road, or drink from a pool. One lives-there-while-it-holds, crosses-while-open, drinks-before-change.

Promises are made accordingly. Marriage does not mean “until death,” because death is too fixed an image for people whose dead become food, tools, warnings, or migrations. The common vow is closer to: *I will move when you move, and when we divide, I will carry your number.*

Children spend much of their early life learning controlled fear. They are taught to freeze at the dry hiss preceding a salt fall, to climb at the first cold touch of the river, to cover their mouths when gray dust descends, and never to pursue a familiar voice into an unlit gallery. Echoes can arrive late enough to imitate the dead. In some quarters, children are forbidden to answer anyone who calls them by an obsolete name.

Their games rehearse evacuation. They build miniature districts from pebbles and race to remove every piece before a cup of water drains through a pierced bowl. They blindfold one another and follow sole-textures through alleys. They trade shell creatures as citizens, livestock, councilors, and floods. The winner is not the one who accumulates the most, but the one who loses the fewest when the board is overturned.

Old age is valued for memory but distrusted for certainty. Elders remember routes that no longer exist and often insist that water will return to channels now lifted above the basin floor. Some are correct after decades of seeming foolishness. Councils therefore maintain two records: current maps on breakable clay and elder maps sung in low, repetitive phrases. The sung maps preserve impossible paths, buried plazas, vanished stairways, and districts beneath collapse zones. When a new fissure opens, the elders’ useless songs sometimes become directions again.

Religion, where it can be separated from engineering, concerns openings.

Birth is an opening. Rain is an opening. A fracture, a drained cistern, a slaughtered animal, an exposed fungal chamber, a door revealed beneath fallen stone—all belong to the same dangerous category. Openings release nourishment, movement, contamination, or history. They must be witnessed and closed correctly.

Before unsealing a growth room, cultivators press chalk to their lips and name everything that should emerge. Before cutting flesh, butchers tap the floor to warn nearby scavengers that a boundary is about to fail. When a ceiling fall reveals unknown architecture, witnesses form a wide ring and sit without speaking. They wait to see whether the opening breathes.

This is not metaphorical. Some doors exhale warmth. Others draw dust inward in slow, steady threads.

The clean walls discovered after collapses trouble the city because cleanliness ordinarily proves recent labor. Nothing exposed to the basin remains unstained for long. Dust settles. shell creatures attach themselves. damp leaves salt rims. Yet these walls emerge without powder in their joints or films in their hollows. Their surfaces are often smoother than water-worn stone and bear shallow marks resembling construction numbers, though no known numbering system can read them.

The citizens do not agree on who lives beyond those doors.

Some say an older population inhabits sealed districts beneath the present city, moving only when noise above informs them that the surface has shifted. They imagine people even paler than themselves, without eyes, cultivating heat instead of fungus and drinking water before it enters the known channels.

Others say the doors belong to the city’s future. The basin settles, opens, and briefly exposes rooms not yet built. Their warmth is the warmth of hands that will someday shape them. To enter would be to steal shelter from descendants.

The river traders claim the occupants are aquatic and rise through the stone when black water comes. Mirror workers insist that the warmth is concentrated light traveling through buried crystal. Listeners rarely offer explanations. They report only what they hear: occasional dragging, three-beat taps, and once, from behind an unopened slab, the unmistakable tightening of rope fibers.

There are people who go in.

They are called returners whether or not they return. Most enter with nothing that might catch or contaminate: no metal, no flame, no fungus, no family object. They tie a cord around one ankle, but cords come back severed without abrasion, or continue feeding inward far beyond their measured length. A few returners emerge within minutes carrying dustless shards, unfamiliar shells, or water warm enough to steam in the cool air. Some come back after years and insist only hours passed. Some emerge through different doors across the basin.

They seldom describe inhabitants. They speak instead of rooms too large for their entrances, of rain falling upward in narrow shafts, of plazas crowded with abandoned household objects made in styles not yet known. One returned with a numbered block bearing the mark of her own hearth lineage, including repairs that had not yet occurred.

She became a councilor until the ground beneath her plaza opened.

There are also less dramatic occupants: the creatures sharing every seam of the city, many too small or slow to be noticed as alive. Warm-wall burrowers pulse faintly inside kiln masonry. Flat scavengers memorize the opening times of doors and gather beforehand. Shell swarms arrange themselves along routes that, days later, water follows. Herd animals refuse certain paving stones and kneel before others, pressing their blind faces down as if listening to something below.

The citizens watch these animals more closely than they admit. Civilization may depend on mirrors, cisterns, numbered walls, and councils, but survival often begins with a pale beast lifting its head for no visible reason.

Nothing in the basin lives alone. Every body is pasture, vessel, building material, warning instrument, ancestor, and eventual food. Every district is an arrangement among water, light, stone, and appetite. The people do not consider their city unfinished. Completion would mean rigidity, and rigidity belongs to things just before they crack.

They say the city is alive because it can be taken apart.

They say the cavern is alive because it cannot.

And beneath both, behind the occasional clean door, something appears to be keeping its rooms ready.
