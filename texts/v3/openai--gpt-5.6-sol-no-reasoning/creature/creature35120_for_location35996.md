The people of the summit call themselves by the names of their waters.

A household may be of the First Condensation, the Crooked Spill, the Well That Trembles, or the Sluice Below Blue Lichen. These are not surnames in the strict sense. They change when channels change. If frost diverts a stream into another court, the household it sustains may wake beneath a new name. Those who move between districts carry several names at once, each valid under a different sky.

They are lean, broad-footed people, shaped by cold, distance, and the absence of anything large enough to carry them. Their calves are strong; their toes spread widely from a lifetime on wet stone. They walk with a measured, testing gait, touching questionable paving with a staff before committing their weight—not merely from fear of collapse, but because a step can occasionally be longer on the ground than it appeared from above.

Their clothing is grown, woven, and felted. Outer cloaks come from the fibrous skins of cushion-plants, beaten with greenstone clubs and layered with fungal felt. They shed mist but become stiff in frost. Beneath them are garments of braided reed membrane, pale as old bone, sometimes waterproofed with wax skimmed from fleshy leaves. Color is used carefully. Gray and green are ordinary; white marks mourning or dangerous work; red is reserved for measurement cords and those responsible for them.

They have no leather, horn, wool, silk, feathers, or fur. None of these substances exists even in legend.

Consequently, their bodies supply many of the metaphors other peoples would take from animals. A branching channel has fingers. A storm has shoulders. A doorway has lips. A district’s roads are its veins, though the word also means roots and cracks in stone. To say that rain is “walking” is not poetic. Rain proceeds, chooses streets, hesitates at walls, and sometimes returns by another route. It is therefore a citizen of uncertain residence.

A person is distinguished from other citizens chiefly by answerability. People can be questioned and may be held to what they say. Water can be questioned but not compelled. Plants answer too slowly for most law. Stone answers only under pressure, heat, or centuries of weather, and so its testimony is considered reliable but rarely convenient.

Children learn these distinctions gradually.

A newborn is not considered fully arrived until condensation has formed on its skin at dawn. The attending household collects these droplets with a strip of dry reed and releases them into the nearest working channel. This joins the child to the local water system and establishes where the birth occurred, which can otherwise be difficult to determine in houses whose rooms deepen under rain.

Infants are carried against the chest in woven slings. There are no cradles that rock, because self-moving furniture is regarded with caution. Instead, babies sleep in shallow stone beds warmed from below by ceramic vessels of hot water. Their first toys are nested cups, knotted cords, smooth pivots, and little channels cut into slabs of clay. They learn pouring before throwing, and measuring before counting.

They also learn silence.

Not absolute silence—the city contains songs, arguments, recitations, laughter—but the skill of hearing whether a space has altered. Before entering an unfamiliar court, even a small child will clap once, then wait. A trustworthy echo returns from where the walls appear to be. If it returns from beneath, or arrives before the clap is finished, the child backs away.

This caution does not make them timid. It makes them exact.

Their houses occupy minute portions of enormous ruins. A family of seven may live in one corner of a roofless assembly hall, enclosing three bays with reed screens and low masonry. They prefer chambers that have endured long enough to become rounded. Sharp corners indicate youth, and youth in a building implies that it has not yet discovered how it will fail.

A dwelling’s most important room is the drying room, not the hearth chamber. There cords, cloaks, seeds, fungus cakes, wooden gate-pins, and records made on clay slats are protected from mist. Fire is used sparingly. There are few woody plants, and the braided white trees grow too slowly to burn casually. Fuel comes from dried reed pith, compressed cushion-plant trimmings, fungal bricks, and black peat carefully cut from designated beds.

Most food is cultivated in wet courts sheltered from the wind. The people eat swollen reed hearts, steamed root lattices, fleshy leaves, seed pastes, lichen washed repeatedly to remove bitterness, and several large fruiting fungi grown in underground rooms. Fermentation is central to their diet. Clay jars hold sour green mashes, clear mineral broths, dark fungal curds, and thick pastes that can be stored through long dry seasons.

Nothing in their cuisine is hunted. Nothing is milked. There are no eggs.

A feast therefore celebrates abundance by age, rarity, and transformation rather than flesh. At a wedding or the reopening of a buried channel, a household may bring out a jar sealed before anyone present was born. The oldest ferments are almost transparent and carry tastes described as “deep frost,” “sun on a closed cistern,” or “the air behind a wall.” Some have matured in districts where time moves unevenly, and their legal age is the subject of formal debate.

Death is understood as the failure or refusal to continue answering.

Because their language does not sharply divide the living from the self-moving, a dead person is not suddenly reclassified as inert. The body continues to cool, stiffen, release water, nourish fungal films, and alter the room around it. These are actions, but not answers. Relatives sit nearby and ask simple questions until dawn. When no reply comes under the new condensation, the death is recognized.

Bodies are not buried deeply. The deeper strata are too uncertain, and no one wishes to place the dead where “before” may be adjacent rather than beneath. Instead, the body is wrapped in fungus felt and laid in a shallow garden of dismantling molds. Over several years, soft tissues are returned to cultivated soil. Bones are washed in running water and fitted into the walls of the household cistern, where they whiten beneath the surface.

Thus the dead remain within the water route of their people.

Their names are spoken when the cistern is cleaned. Some households claim that changes in the water’s taste correspond to memories of the dead, though magistrates do not admit this as evidence.

Political authority belongs neither to warriors nor landowners, for there are no armies and land cannot reliably be owned. It belongs to those who understand continuities: channel keepers, route witnesses, frost readers, cord bearers, and custodians of old decisions.

The smallest civic unit is the Catchment: everyone whose drinking water depends on the same set of surfaces. A Catchment may include households separated by days of walking, while excluding the house next door if its roof drains elsewhere. Each sends a listener to seasonal councils. A listener’s duty is not to persuade but to repeat every claim accurately in another location. Anyone found improving an argument during repetition is removed.

Laws are recorded on paired objects. One copy is kept in a dry archive; the other is placed where the law applies. A rule governing a bridge may be incised directly into its parapet. A rule governing rain may be written on soluble clay and exposed during a storm. If the text dissolves completely, the law must be reconsidered. If part remains, the surviving words retain force.

Justice is mostly hydraulic.

A person who steals water owes labor upstream. Someone who blocks a channel without consultation must reopen it and follow the diverted flow until every affected garden has been identified. Deliberately misleading a traveler about distance is among the gravest offenses, because it can exile a person without moving them far at all.

Violence exists, but it is intimate and rare. Their weapons are the same tools used for stone: chipped blades, mauls, wedges, heavy staffs. No culture of the hunt has taught them to admire pursuit or killing from afar. Murder is described as “closing another’s weather.” Its punishment is usually not death, but exclusion from maintained routes. The condemned must live where no communal channel reaches, a sentence that can be merciful in one province and fatal in another.

Their scholars are field workers rather than philosophers secluded in rooms. They travel with red cords, plumb stones, ceramic clocks, water levels, waxed tablets, and bundles of marked reeds. They measure roads in clear weather, then again under fog. They count how many breaths a doorway contains after rain. They leave bowls at intervals to determine whether the same storm has fallen in two places.

The most respected are the Comparers, who bring incompatible measurements together without forcing them to agree.

A Comparer may state that a hall is forty paces long from the eastern entrance, seven days long from the west, and absent when approached from below. All three findings are entered into the civic record. Contradiction is treated as a property of the hall, not a failure of thought.

This habit shapes their theology, insofar as they have one.

They do not worship creators. Creation implies a completed act, and nothing around them appears completed. They offer no sacrifice, since there are no animals and plants are already participants in every household. Instead, they practice acknowledgment. Before altering an ancient channel, workers describe aloud what the water currently does. Before cutting a white tree, they spend a full season growing replacement stems from its roots. Before dismantling a wall, they wait through rain, frost, clear sun, and fog to learn whether the wall occupies the same place under each.

The unreachable buildings in the Still Country are not called divine. They are called the Unnear.

Every settlement around the pale square maintains watchers, though watching is not considered a profession. Households rotate the duty. A watcher sits at the moss boundary with covered water, dry food, a clay tablet, and no measuring cord. Cords are forbidden there because they may return joined, knotted, or of a different length, and the desire to interpret these changes has cost too many lives.

The watcher records which buildings are visible, how sharp their edges appear, and whether any entrance coincides with the horizon. They also record themselves: number of fingers, direction of shadow, remembered name, last meal, and the location of their own back. This final entry is not a joke. Near the square, one’s front and back can become matters of observation rather than anatomy.

No one approaches the buildings now. But the moss margin contains old paths pressed deep into it, all beginning toward the pale rock and curving, sooner or later, back to their origins. Some return after a few hundred paces. One completes its loop only after crossing seven provinces. Another emerges from a stairwell in the north, though its traveler set out walking south.

There are stories of those who attempted approach and did not return, yet these are not the stories that trouble the inhabitants most.

More troubling are accounts of travelers who returned before setting out.

Such people arrive exhausted at their own houses, carrying worn tools, unfamiliar scars, and exact knowledge of conversations that will occur days later. They are given shelter but not questioned until their earlier selves have departed. When both versions coexist, they avoid looking at one another. Usually the returned one vanishes during sleep, leaving condensation in the shape of a body. Occasionally both remain.

The law recognizes them as one person with divided obligations.

Music is made without stretched hide or gut string. Flutes are cut from hollow reeds. Stone chimes hang in sheltered courts. Water organs breathe through submerged ceramic chambers as cistern levels rise and fall. The largest instruments are architectural: whole stairways tuned so that wind passing across their shafts produces tones too low to hear directly but strong enough to tremble in the ribs.

Songs are often mapped rather than written. A melody may require the singer to walk a particular route, with intervals determined by turns, steps, and changes in elevation. If the route lengthens under fog, the song gains measures. If a stair contracts in frost, notes disappear. Thus no performance is definitive.

Their histories are similarly geographic. Instead of saying that an event happened long ago, they may say it happened “three walls beneath the current rain” or “before the western road returned.” A historian is expected to know which past can be reached from the present and by what route.

The oldest records concern the builders, though no one is certain these were a different people.

Carved scenes are rare and badly softened, but some show upright figures hauling blocks, cutting channels, and planting gardens. They look much like the present inhabitants. There are no depictions of conquest, crowned rulers, supernatural beasts, or animals of any kind. Again and again, however, the figures are shown passing stones hand to hand toward something beyond the edge of the carving.

Many believe the city was never more populous than it is now—that the vast halls, plazas, reservoirs, and roads were built not for crowds but for weather. A square may be sized to catch a particular rain. A roofless hall may align winds from provinces hundreds of kilometers apart. The concentric neighborhoods may be valves in a hydraulic system whose fluid is not wholly water.

Under this interpretation, the people do not inhabit an abandoned city.

They are its smallest moving components.

Others reject this as a dangerous diminishment. They point to kitchens large enough to feed thousands, sleeping platforms extending from horizon to horizon, and latrine channels polished by unmistakable use. They say the city was once full, and that its population did not die but was distributed: into roads, districts, alternate durations, unreachable interiors. When an echo answers in an unborn voice, they hear not prophecy but citizenship continuing elsewhere.

Children favor a simpler account. They say everyone lives in the distant buildings and appears sparse only because the city has placed its people too far apart to meet.

Whatever the truth, loneliness is their ordinary condition, not always their sorrow.

A traveler can go weeks without seeing another face, yet find evidence of care: a cleared drain, dry fuel under an overturned jar, a red cord tied before an unsafe stair, fresh clay sealing a cracked cistern. Hospitality is often anonymous. One leaves provisions not for a known guest but for whoever occupies the road next.

When people do meet, they touch foreheads and exchange measurements.

“How far have you come?”

“Four days in sun, eleven in mist.”

“Under which rain?”

“The narrow rain moving east.”

“Were you the same length when you began?”

“As far as I could determine.”

Only then do they exchange names.

At night, households close reed shutters, cover every vessel, and listen to water moving beneath the floor. Sometimes a gate lowers in the distance with a long wooden groan. Somewhere beyond the inhabited bays, another mechanism answers. The sound may take hours to arrive, or precede its cause.

No one imagines that the city belongs to them. Ownership would be absurd. The plateau exceeds any lifetime, any census, perhaps any single arrangement of space. Their task is more modest: keep the water moving; keep routes witnessed; prevent useful walls from falling; leave warnings where the world has become thin.

And when the unreachable buildings appear in the cups, they place lids over them—not in terror, but in courtesy, as one closes the eyes of someone who does not wish to be watched.
