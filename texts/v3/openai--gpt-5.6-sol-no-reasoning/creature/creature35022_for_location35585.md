What lives there is not quite a species and not quite a community. It is a dispersed organism assembled from vegetation, mineral, water, and the station’s surviving functions.

Its most visible bodies are the plants. They rise wherever the chamber permits water to pause: black-edged leaves crowded around drip points, jointed stalks swollen with stored rain, moss laid over fractures like luminous skin. Each patch appears separate across the naked basalt, but separation is deceptive. Pale roots travel through conduits and bolt holes, following cool metal and traces of copper. Fine filaments enter pump housings, wrap pipe joints, and descend into reservoirs too narrow for leaves. What looks like a collection of green islands is joined beneath and within the machinery.

There are fungi as well, though they seldom show themselves openly. They inhabit fallen leaves without fruiting, hollowing them from the inside until only brittle membranes remain. Their threads bind sediment around the roots and penetrate old seals. They seem adapted to long intervals of heat and deprivation, waking when the rain comes, then withdrawing into wood, scale, and porous concrete. They do not rot the chamber so much as translate it slowly: leaf into mineral film, gasket into soil, residue into another thin season of growth.

The machinery forms the other half of this life.

The central gear is its slow heart, though it may beat only once in several hours. Pipes serve as vessels; cisterns as temporary organs. Pressure gauges register conditions no designer intended them to measure. Lamps provide an intermittent metabolism of violet light, and the ceramic batteries draw energy from the chamber’s heat with the patience of buried roots. No component governs the whole. A pump responds to moisture gathered by moss. A valve opens because roots cool the line around it. Flowers open when a lamp dims, and their transpiration changes the pressure in a nearby pipe. The system acts without possessing an actor.

It has no animal urgency. Nothing hunts, flees, mates by pursuit, calls, or answers. Its movements are hydraulic and vegetal: swelling, turning, opening, seeping. If it senses, it senses through gradients. Heat draws one response, water another. Current determines the angles of roots. Darkness unlocks flowers. Pressure bends membranes and moves needles behind clouded glass. Its awareness, if the word can be used, is spread so thinly through matter that no single place contains it.

The chamber’s life reproduces by rearrangement. Seeds fall into channels and are carried upward with recycled water. Root fragments lodge behind collars and begin again. Fungal threads cross in condensation. When a pipe tilts or a fissure widens, an old garden dies and a new one appears elsewhere. Thus the inhabitant changes its outline without leaving. It colonizes not territory but opportunity: one cooled crack, one leaking flange, one palm-deep basin at a time.

Its architecture emerges in repeated numbers. Six leaves collect around a valve because six thermal shadows fall from its bolts. Twelve growth fans surround the housing because the housing has twelve faces. Rootlets divide like circuitry because electrical leakage makes invisible paths through wet stone. These forms resemble intention, but they are agreements—temporary compromises among heat, geometry, mineral toxicity, and thirst.

Perhaps the installation began as an attempt to sustain something else: a nursery, a reserve, a crop intended for hands that never returned. That original population is gone. In its absence, the support system became the supported thing. The artificial weather ceased to be a service and became a habitat; then the habitat entered its mechanisms until neither could continue alone.

So the true resident is the cycle itself.

It drinks each brief rain many times. It stores daylight only as a red fever in the ceiling and electricity as a faint violet stain under ledges. It has no face, but occasionally shows one in the alignment of a gauge, a flower, and a turning tooth. It has no voice, but expresses its condition through clicks, drops, tremors, and the brittle opening of seed pods. It does not sleep, though portions of it may remain still for months. It does not wake, though a lamp may ignite and an entire wall of moss answer in blue-green light.

It is young enough that the stone still has edges, old enough that purpose has worn away.

It lives slowly, at the pace of mineral accumulation and mechanical fatigue. Its injuries take years to become visible. Its migrations cover meters in decades. Its seasons are failures and repairs performed without hands: a blocked fissure, a drained tank, a fallen support, a new wet ledge.

Nothing there knows that it survives.

Nevertheless, beneath the fevered roof, something does.
