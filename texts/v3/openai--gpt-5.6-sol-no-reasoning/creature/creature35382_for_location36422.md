They are seam-dwellers: farmers, masons, shepherds, potters, surveyors, water drawers, carpenters, plasterers, and children born beneath ceilings low enough to touch. They are not a hidden remnant or a people driven into ancient ruins. Their city is younger than many of them. They remember when present neighborhoods were solid clay.

Clay shapes nearly everything about them. It powders their sleeves, cakes beneath their nails, and records their hands in unfinished walls. Diggers at the perimeter cut it into slabs; carriers bring it inward; brickmakers temper it with chopped straw and grit. Potters turn finer portions into jars, drain tiles, signal chimes, and cistern linings. Even children learn to judge a handful by feel: wall clay, vessel clay, sealing clay, or clay too wet to trust.

The excavators are respected, but they are never romanticized. They work in crews because solitude at the face is forbidden. One person cuts, another clears spoil, a third watches the roof and listens. Their tools are simple and meticulously maintained—horn picks polished by palms, hardwood wedges blackened and hardened in fire, stone blades resharpened until they become too small for anything but scraping. A good digger is not the strongest. A good digger knows when the clay has changed.

They emerge from a shift pale with dust, their hair wrapped and their mouths covered with damp wool. They speak little while working. Taps, gestures, and touches to the arm carry instructions. Many acquire an unconscious habit of looking upward whenever they enter a room.

Beside them work the roof-keepers. These carpenters and surveyors possess the city’s most consequential knowledge. They read pillars as farmers read weather. They know which cracks are merely drying plaster, which beams complain harmlessly in the cold, and which faint noises demand an immediate evacuation. Their measurements are practical rather than abstract: cord lengths, water lines, the sag of a loaded timber, the weight of earth above a span. They keep baked tablets showing not just property and streets but burdens—columns of marks recording what rests upon what.

A householder may own a room, but never the support inside it. Roof-keepers enter homes without invitation when necessary. They may remove a cupboard, open a wall, brace a sleeping chamber, or forbid a family from using its hearth. People resent them in the ordinary way that people resent authority, yet obey quickly. Everyone knows someone who has heard a roof begin to fail.

Water drawers have a different prestige. They descend into the lowest quarter wrapped in felt against the cold. Working in pairs at narrow wells, they lower leather buckets into darkness and listen through the rope. The buried river cannot be seen clearly; it is recognized by a changed echo, a tug against the line, a smell of wet gravel. Water drawers develop powerful shoulders and delicate hands. They can distinguish a bucket striking stone from one dragging in silt, and can tell by the rope’s vibration when a knot has begun to slip.

During drought they become objects of scrutiny. Each vessel is counted. Reservoir lids are sealed, household allotments marked on clay tallies, and arguments conducted in low, furious voices. During rain, however, their solemnity dissolves. They hurry through the streets opening channels and shifting basins while children follow the new water from court to court.

Children here know weather without always seeing the sky. Sunlight advances across particular walls. Clouds dim entire blocks. Rain announces itself first as footsteps overhead, then as tapping in catchments, then as the rush and resonance of water descending through the city. A child may learn the shapes of shadows before learning the shapes of clouds.

They play at construction. They make miniature chambers in spoil heaps, brace them with twigs, and flood them with cups of water to see what survives. Their games include evacuation drills, ration counting, rat trapping, and arguments over imaginary boundary markers. They are taught early to become still when the floor trembles. An adult may ask, “Cart, drum, or settling?” and expect a serious answer.

They also serve the city as listeners. Small enough to enter service gaps, they inspect vents and carry messages through crowded lanes. No one mocks a child for reporting a tick, creak, damp patch, or unfamiliar draft. Many reports prove harmless; the habit is valuable regardless.

Families are broad, porous arrangements. A household may include blood relatives, apprentices, widowed neighbors, displaced families awaiting new rooms, and surface workers who descend only at night. Privacy is made with curtains and custom rather than distance. People avert their eyes when beds are lowered, speak softly around sleepers, and recognize that overhearing is not permission to repeat.

They live close enough to know one another’s routines by sound. The scrape of a particular stool, the cough of an old plasterer, the rhythm of a neighbor grinding grain—these become part of a district’s identity. When one sound disappears, someone notices.

Clothing is layered wool, felt, linen where it can be afforded, and leather at knees and elbows. Colors are muted by dust, but not absent. Dyers favor ochre, madder red, soot-black, and the yellow-green of certain surface plants. Bright cloth is often hung near light shafts, where it can actually be seen. Workers wrap their hair. Water crews wear narrow belts with knives and spare cord. Surveyors carry knotted lines coiled across the chest, making their profession recognizable from a distance.

Most people have two kinds of footwear: soft-soled indoor shoes that make little noise, and tougher boots for ramps, fields, or wet districts. Bare feet are common in houses but discouraged in streets, where brick splinters, dropped tools, and drainage channels make them vulnerable.

They eat grain porridge, flat breads, goat cheese, onions, beans, dried fruit, eggs, and occasionally meat. Food is heavily associated with place because fire and ventilation determine where it can be prepared. Several blocks may share a cooking chamber beneath one flue. Families mark their covered pots with impressed seals and leave them in the care of hearth-tenders, who coordinate fuel, smoke, and cooking times.

Thus meals are private in ownership but communal in making. At dusk the hearth chambers fill with steam, bread smell, and quiet bargaining. News circulates there faster than it does through official runners.

The potters occupy warm districts beside kilns whose waste heat passes into baths, drying rooms, nurseries, and infirmaries. Their work is everywhere: storage jars, lamp cups, water pipes, measuring vessels, signal tiles, drain sections, lids, seals, and the tablets on which the city remembers itself. A fine painted bowl may be treasured, but a perfectly fitted pipe joint wins deeper admiration.

They know fire better than most citizens and are consequently subject to strict oversight. Kilns are watched in shifts. Fuel is weighed. Flues are inspected before lighting. A potter who lets smoke enter a residential lane faces more than a fine; they may lose the right to fire work at all.

Aboveground laborers form a half-separate population. Shepherds, orchard keepers, farmers, catchment cleaners, and watchkeepers spend long days beneath open sky. They descend carrying weather with them: sun-warmed wool, rain, mud, leaf mold, animal smell. Underground-born children crowd around them for news of frost, migrating birds, cloud banks, and distant fires.

Surface workers often speak louder and walk faster than those below. They complain that the city makes them stoop even in high chambers. City dwellers joke that surface people cannot hear anything because wind has filled their ears.

Yet nearly everyone crosses between the two worlds. The people do not consider themselves creatures of darkness. Their wealth lies in fields, flocks, trees, and rainfall; their safety, craft, storage, and sleep lie below. A house is underground, but an estate may consist largely of what grows above its roof.

Birth occurs in warm interior rooms, often near the potters’ drying chambers. Newborns are introduced to the household first and to daylight soon afterward. When weather permits, they are carried to a shaft at noon and held briefly in the descending light. This is neither wholly religious nor merely practical. People say that a child should feel the sun directly once before learning it through plaster and reflection.

The dead cannot be allowed to consume much habitable space. Bodies are taken to surface burial grounds beyond the catchments and fields, where drainage will not endanger the city. Some families keep a small fired-clay token impressed with the dead person’s thumb or palm. These are set into household walls, carried during relocations, or embedded near district pillars. As the city grows, the dead therefore remain not in tombs but as touches in the architecture.

Their beliefs center less on an underworld than an overhead world. The earth above is not imagined as crushing hostility; it is shelter, weight, boundary, and material awaiting negotiation. They distinguish between supported earth, sleeping earth, wet earth, and earth made restless by careless hands. Their rituals resemble inspections: touching pillars, pouring a measured spoonful of water into a new cistern, tying wool around the first brace of a chamber, standing silently after a wall is opened to hear how the new space settles.

Before a room receives its permanent use, it may remain unnamed for weeks. People watch its temperature, dampness, acoustics, and traffic. A chamber intended as a granary may prove too wet and become a washroom; a proposed house may carry voices too clearly and become a workshop. They believe that function should be discovered rather than imposed too quickly.

Their civic leaders are coordinators more than monarchs. Authority belongs to councils of districts, water keepers, roof surveyors, surface stewards, hearth wardens, and work-crew representatives. The most powerful office is often held by the person who can close a street, ration a cistern, or halt an excavation. Decisions are proclaimed by runners and clay-note signals, then copied onto tablets.

Disputes concern airflow, noise, waste clay, access to shafts, heat from workshops, and rights to newly opened rooms. Property is never entirely simple. One family may own the walls, another the room above, a guild the beam passing through both, and the district the pillar beside the bed. The city’s law has grown elaborate because its structures are inseparable.

Punishment rarely involves confinement; enclosed space is too valuable. Fines are paid in labor, material, water rights, fuel, or loss of workshop privileges. Reckless digging is among the gravest offenses. A person who secretly enlarges a room endangers strangers they may never meet, perhaps streets away. Such offenders can be expelled to surface compounds or barred from owning underground property.

There are wealthy inhabitants, but wealth does not express itself mainly through size. Even prosperous households cannot casually remove a pillar or claim a light shaft. Status appears in lime plaster, dry storerooms, fine wool, imported timber, painted ceramics, private water jars, and sleeping rooms far from workshops. The richest can afford quiet.

The poorest live near excavation fronts, animal ramps, or damp lower districts, where rents are cheap and relocation frequent. They patch walls with mismatched brick, sleep behind woven partitions, and earn wages carrying spoil inward. Yet the advancing edge also offers opportunity. A carrier may become a brickmaker; a brickmaker may claim the first lease on a newly finished room. The city’s boundary is harsh, unstable, and socially fluid.

Animals inhabit human margins. Goats occupy outer chambers and know the ramps better than some children. Chickens scratch beneath light wells, scattering dust through bright columns. Dogs are few and usually small, bred as ratters, alarms, and companions rather than guards. They learn not to bark without cause. A persistently noisy dog is sent to the surface flocks.

Rats are trapped with intense communal diligence. Cave crickets are ignored unless they suddenly migrate, which may indicate moisture or changing air. Woodlice are treated almost as messages: where they gather, rot is sought. Bats in abandoned headings are left alone unless they foul a catchment. The people’s attitude toward animals is less mystical than diagnostic. Creatures reveal what air, wood, and water are doing beyond human sight.

They are quiet, but not joyless. Music favors felt drums, reed pipes played softly, humming, and clay chimes with controlled tones. Singing is intimate because chambers carry voices unpredictably. Large celebrations are held in broad courts where hanging wool absorbs echoes. Dancers use stamping patterns that can be felt through the floor, though roof-keepers approve the gathering place beforehand.

Festivals follow both agriculture and construction. First rain is celebrated by opening decorated basins beneath selected gutters. The completion of a major light shaft brings a noon feast in its beam. When two excavation fronts meet as planned, their crews exchange tools through the final thin wall before breaking it fully. A new district begins not when its rooms are finished, but when air can circulate through it safely.

Their humor is full of structure. A stubborn person is “a pillar in a doorway.” A gossip “has more outlets than a smoke flue.” Someone confused has “lost the knot in their measuring cord.” A reckless promise is “a roof laid on wet brick.” Surface people are teased for fearing ceilings; underground people for blinking at the horizon.

Still, fear is part of them. They fear smoke most immediately, bad air invisibly, floodwater suddenly, and collapse in the deep animal part of the mind. Many dislike sleeping beneath newly opened ground. Some refuse to enter wells or excavation headings. After any structural failure, even a small one, the district grows watchful and sleepless. People pause mid-conversation to interpret harmless creaks.

But they do not experience their city primarily as a trap. They experience it as work made habitable.

Everywhere they can point to human decisions: this wall was once the edge; that nursery was clay five years ago; this market’s bricks came from the reservoir chamber; that pillar was left because an apprentice noticed a fault in the ceiling. Their map is also a genealogy of labor. They remember places by who opened, braced, plastered, flooded, repaired, or first slept in them.

They are a people living not inside a finished city, but inside a continuous civic act. Their deepest virtue is attentiveness. They listen before cutting, measure before loading, share heat, count water, preserve airflow, and leave support where support is due.

Around them, untouched clay waits without menace or welcome. Each morning crews go to meet it. Each evening they return carrying another portion of the future inward in baskets.
