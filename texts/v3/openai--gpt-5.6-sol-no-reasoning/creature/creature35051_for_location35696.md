The inhabitants are small, broad-bodied people shaped by cold, altitude, and the perpetual labor of balance. They have wide feet and strong, splayed toes, though these are seldom seen outside the warm lower rooms. Their hands are similarly broad, with blunt fingers, heavy nails, and pale webbing at the first joints. A child can brace one hand across a sluice no adult elsewhere would think to grip. An elder can identify a failing wall by laying two fingertips against it and feeling how the water trembles through the stone.

Their skin ranges from weathered gray-brown to the muted gold of old bone, but immersion leaves it startlingly pale. Most have light eyes: colorless blue, cloudy green, silver flecked with black. This is not merely an adaptation to glare. Their eyes distinguish minute differences in reflected light. They can read the depth and speed of water from the shiver of brightness on its surface, and see a loose roof stone in the distortion of the sky mirrored around it. In direct sun they narrow their eyes almost shut. In mist, they see easily.

Their hair is dense and usually dark, greased against wetness and worn in close braids or coils under felt caps. Loose hair is considered hazardous near wheels and channels. Beards are cropped, forked, or threaded through bone rings. Long braids indicate neither rank nor beauty but responsibility: each strand may represent a channel, spring, gate, or dependent household entrusted to its wearer. When that responsibility ends, the strand is cut and dropped into the corresponding water.

They dress in layers of felt, hide, woven hair, and cured fish skin. Nothing hangs freely. Sleeves tie at the wrists, hems fasten around the thighs, tools sit high against the ribs. Their boots have soft felted soles for silence and grip, renewed so often that a person may wear the same boot uppers for decades over hundreds of different soles. Outer garments are patched in watershed colors—not bright dyes, which are scarce, but subtle bands of soot-black, clay-red, lichen-yellow, mineral blue, and undyed white. In a flood, these markings tell rescuers where a person belongs and which routes they are likely to know.

They carry remarkably little metal. A copper needle may be shared by an entire ascending row of houses. A knife belongs less to its bearer than to the sequence of people trained to sharpen it. Most everyday implements are stone, horn, shell, bone, or wood brought at great expense from below. Their most characteristic tool is a short forked lever tucked through the belt. It serves as pry bar, gate key, ice hook, ladder catch, splint, measuring gauge, and, when struck against stone, a warning instrument whose vibration travels farther than its sound.

They do not organize themselves primarily into families. They belong to flows.

A household may consist of blood relatives, widowed gatekeepers, apprentices, displaced neighbors, and children transferred from lower rooms after a channel changed course. What binds them is shared responsibility for a roof, cistern, retaining wall, or section of drainage. Marriage often means joining two water systems rather than two lineages. Courtship is practical and intensely observed: prospective partners clear a clogged runnel together, repair a winter seal, or spend a thaw watching a gate that must neither jam nor open too early. Affection is expected to show itself as attention. To remember that someone’s sleeping recess lies beneath a dangerous cornice is a form of tenderness. To wake before them and break the ice above it is nearly a declaration.

Children are raised communally in the warmest tiers. They crawl through low passages adults cannot enter, retrieve bone pegs from culverts, and learn the town first as temperature and sound. Before they are taught names for distant places, they memorize which stones sweat before a pressure surge, which pools are safe to drink from, where an eel’s sudden absence means poison or collapse, and how many heartbeats it takes water spilled at one threshold to appear three courts below.

Their games are rehearsals disguised as mischief. They build miniature towns in runoff basins and divert streams through them with pebbles. They race bits of feather along competing gutters. They knot cords, lower them into the principal spring, and argue over whether the returned curves retain the rules of the game. Older children hide polished shells in channels for younger ones to track by changes in flow. No child is punished for dismantling something merely because it was old. They are punished for failing to understand what else depended on it.

Adulthood begins with the keeping of a night gate alone. The initiate spends one full dark beside a chosen sluice, wrapped in felt, listening to the mountain. They must decide without instruction when to break ice, when to divert overflow, when to wake those below, and when to do nothing. Most say the hardest lesson is the last. The town is full of motion, and an inexperienced keeper mistakes intervention for care.

Their elders are not chiefly repositories of history. Memory here is suspect when it hardens. Elders are valued for having survived enough changes to distrust resemblance. They know that a flood behaving like the flood of forty years before may still be following an entirely different weakness. The best of them speak conditionally, offering several explanations at once. “It may be ice beneath the west shelf,” one might say, “or the deep thing turning, or the old spill seeking the room we sealed.” Certainty is treated as a dangerous luxury.

Those who can no longer climb tend laws.

They sit in sheltered galleries with slate across their knees, recutting inscriptions softened by water. This is neither clerical work nor simple copying. Witnesses gather to debate each worn word. If a phrase has vanished, no one pretends to know it perfectly. They infer it from surviving strokes, remembered disputes, scars in nearby architecture, and the present direction of runoff. Thus law passes continuously through weather and judgment. Some elders become famous for preserving a single difficult line unchanged through decades. Others are remembered for altering one word at exactly the right time.

Their leaders are called, in the closest translation, the People Who Stand Where Waters Disagree. They have no permanent hall. During ordinary days they move from confluence to confluence, settling obligations between upper and lower households. Their authority is strongest in crisis and fades rapidly afterward. A leader who continues issuing commands once the water has settled is mocked as someone trying to gate mist.

Disputes concern damage, neglect, access to warmth, and the direction of future flow. Compensation is rarely paid in objects. Instead, the responsible party may owe nights of gate-watching, a season of ice-breaking, labor on a neighbor’s wall, or surrender of roof space to a shared cistern. The gravest offense is knowingly sending danger downward. Even murder is understood partly through drainage: not only who caused death, but who altered the conditions around it, who ignored warning signs, and who benefited from the resulting vacancy.

They have few prisons. A person judged dangerously careless may be forbidden from living above others. Such people must occupy the lowest habitable chambers, where every neglected channel reaches them first. Exile is rarer and often harsher, for beyond the bowl there may be no path, food, or shelter. The worst sentence is severance from maintenance: no one will share a ladder, warn of ice, or include the condemned in rebuilding. Few survive it long.

They eat constantly and in small amounts. Cold and labor permit no elaborate meals except after major drainage. Fish are trapped in baskets, eaten fresh, dried against warm vents, fermented in sealed crocks, or ground whole into dense cakes. The pale eels are fatty and valuable but never taken from certain cisterns, where they are believed to feel deep pressure before any instrument can. Crustaceans are crushed into salty paste. Goat milk becomes hard, sour curd. Rodents are not eaten except during famine; their warning behavior makes them fellow workers, though no one would say so ceremonially.

The warm springs support thin mats of edible growth cultivated on stone frames beneath the water. These yield soft green sheets rich in minerals, tasting of iron and rain. Silver weeds from roof seams are used sparingly for medicine. Food brought over the rim—grain, dried fruit, roots—is rare enough that a handful may be divided among twenty people. Visitors sometimes mistake this sharing for ritual. It is partly that, but mostly no one in the town trusts a richness consumed alone.

Cooking takes place over vents, heated slabs, and channels redirected beneath pots. Open flame is uncommon. Fuel is too scarce, roofs too crowded, and smoke too valuable to waste; it lures the black birds, dries felt, preserves fish, and reveals hidden air currents. A family may tend a fist-sized ember bed for generations, carrying coals in stone cups when rooms are dismantled.

The animals are incorporated into civic life without being domesticated in the usual sense.

Goats choose their own sleeping roofs. A household favored by them accepts both the milk and the burden of reinforcing beams. Their movements are read as commentary on the town’s stability. If goats abandon an upper tier, humans follow. If they gather unexpectedly on a roof believed unsafe, the roof is inspected before they are driven off; often they have found a warm seam or a firm line of support invisible from below.

The communal rodents are fed scraps near listening holes. Children record where they emerge, where they refuse to enter, and whether they carry bedding uphill. To kill one intentionally is a serious offense, though traps are set when their numbers threaten food stores. Even then, the trapper must open the wall afterward and examine what drove them into inhabited rooms.

The black birds belong nowhere and are welcomed everywhere. They steal needles, fish eyes, cord fibers, and bits of reflective stone. Some learn household routes and arrive moments before a familiar sluice is opened. The people insist they are merely observant, not prophetic. Nonetheless, a bird tapping repeatedly at an inner wall will bring everyone running.

The winged creatures that rise inside droplets are called the brief folk. During their emergence, work pauses when possible. Infants are held up to see them. The droplets are said to contain one perfect image of the town, viewed from no fixed direction. Catching one is considered unlucky—not because the creatures are sacred, but because anything able to survive only a few minutes should not lose one of them to curiosity.

Death is handled without pretending the dead have become permanent.

Bodies are washed in three waters: one from the household flow, one from the principal spring’s outer channel, and one gathered fresh from the sky. Useful garments and tools are removed. Teeth may be kept as toggles or counters; hair becomes cord; bone, after long weathering, may be carved into pegs, whistles, needles, or legal markers. This is not thought to imprison the dead. A person’s body is considered another structure that can continue to bear weight.

The remaining flesh is placed in a high cleft for birds and cold. Names are cut on small slate pieces and hung in the water feeding the deceased’s household. As the name erodes, mourners recut it if grief still requires a visible shape. Eventually they stop. The blank slate remains hanging until a flood takes it.

They do not believe the vanished are gone in any simple sense. People persist as alterations: a stair made shallower for an injured parent, a channel curved around a child’s former bed, a law reworded after someone’s mistake, a gate handle polished by one habitual grip. To ask where an ancestor is buried would confuse them. They are likely to point in several directions.

Their speech is soft, breath-efficient, and crowded with words for movement through water. They distinguish water that presses, water that searches, water that returns along an old route, water held unwillingly by ice, water made dangerous by brightness, and water that appears still because something beneath it is moving at the same speed. Many statements include an indication of vertical consequence: who is above, who receives below, what lies between.

Names change throughout life. A child has a cradle-name, usually derived from a minor sound, weather event, animal, or peculiarity noticed soon after birth. Later names come from work, location, or remembered action: Opens-at-Four, Sleeps-Behind-Warm-Stone, Mended-the-Blue-Spill, Counted-Eight-Correctly. A person disgraced by negligence may lose a name. A person who saves many lives may refuse the new one offered, fearing it will make others trust them too easily.

Their songs are nearly inaudible. Many are sung against stone rather than into open air, using the wall as a resonator. The singer rests brow or teeth to the surface and hums; listeners elsewhere touch the same connected masonry and feel the melody in their jaws. Work crews coordinate through pulses tapped into flooded channels. Lovers may exchange whole conversations through adjacent walls, too faint for anyone not already listening in the right place.

Their art is made for change. They carve faces where spray will erase expression in a chosen order. They braid patterns into spill screens that become visible only when clogged. They set pale and dark stones in channels so that a design appears at one water level and disappears at another. They suspend bone pieces beneath drops, producing rhythms that alter as ice accumulates. A masterpiece may last a single thaw.

The central spring is tended by a small, austere body of specialists. They are neither priests nor rulers. They call themselves the Returners, though outsiders might call them keepers of altered things. They record what enters the bright pool, how long it remains, what comes back, and which properties have changed. Their records are kept redundantly: on slate, in knotted cords, in arrangements of stones, and in memorized recitations. No one method is trusted alone.

The Returners are recognizable by their asymmetrical tools. Their rods are already bent, their bowls slightly uneven, their cords tied with deliberate false knots. Perfectly straight or regular objects are never lowered into the pool if their loss would matter. Experiments require the consent of households downstream, since an altered object may carry consequences into shared water.

They have learned a few things and understood none of them.

The pool does not restore life, though it sometimes produces movement in the dead. It does not undo damage, though it may redistribute it. Broken objects may emerge whole but brittle elsewhere. A chipped cup may return smooth while an identical chip appears in the rim of a nearby cistern. Knots are especially troublesome. The spring seems to treat them as events rather than forms.

On rare occasions, a Returner lowers an object during the cavern pulses to ask a practical question. A cord marked with watershed intervals may return curved toward the next failure. A model gate may emerge swollen at the hinge that must be watched. But answers are never clean enough to become prophecy. The spring offers resemblance, displacement, and implication. Anyone claiming it has spoken plainly is removed from its care.

Below all of them is the slow inhabitant of the caverns.

The townspeople have no agreed image of it. Some describe a blind animal with a body soft enough to fill miles of cracks. Some believe it is a colony of pale eels so old and interwoven that it moves as one creature. Others say the mountain itself has organs: chambers, valves, a throat, and something like a sleeping will. The oldest pressure maps resemble anatomical drawings too closely for comfort, though no one knows whose anatomy they depict.

They call it the Underlying, a term that means foundation, cause, burden, and sleeper depending on context. It is not worshiped. Offerings are not thrown into the caverns, because clogging an unknown passage would be criminal folly. Instead, people maintain the spaces through which its exhalations pass. They count its pulses. They compare intervals. They teach every child the climb routes for eight and nine.

Seven pulses are the ordinary adjustment of the deep waters.

Eight means the Underlying has encountered resistance.

Nine means it is attempting to make room.

The prohibition against speaking of ten is not superstition alone. The last time ten pulses came, generations ago, the town’s records disagree about what followed. Several slate accounts terminate in the middle of words. A wall painting shows the bowl empty of buildings but full of fish. One recitation claims everyone climbed and watched a second town rise briefly beneath the first, roof to roof, with pale figures looking upward through the water. Another says nothing happened until the following spring, when children began remembering rooms that had never existed.

There are inhabitants who may have come from that event.

They are few, and they pass easily among the others except in certain light. Their eyes reflect not the visible sky but moving water. They know obsolete routes through houses built after their birth. Sometimes they pause before blank stone as if waiting for a door to open. They are excellent at finding the lost and poor at explaining where they found them.

The town does not persecute them. Permanence is already distrusted; identity receives no special exemption. Such people are given work in shifting districts, where an intuition for absent architecture is useful. They are often the first to enter after a major flood. They move through newly opened passages with the hesitant familiarity of someone returning home after a long absence.

And after every exhalation, all distinctions momentarily collapse: leader and child, Returner and law-cutter, native and stranger, human and animal. Everyone able to carry does so. Everyone able to listen presses a hand to stone. They retrieve fish from sleeping rooms, lift goats from wedged roofs, free birds from ice skins, mark dangerous hollows, count the missing, and redirect warm water toward those trapped in the cold.

They do not rebuild the town as it was.

That would be considered both impossible and unwise. They ask where the water now wishes to pass, which stones have found a stable bearing, what the animals have already understood, and which former arrangements have lost the right to be restored. A surviving wall may become a floor. A bedroom may become a channel. A shrine may be sealed inside a retaining pier, still tended through a hole no wider than a finger. Neighbors wake beneath different roofs and remain neighbors because the same runoff still joins them.

By evening, temporary laws are hanging in the wet lanes. New ladders lean where stairs used to be. Children have already named the fresh pools. Someone has begun carving a face above an unclaimed doorway, giving it features the spray will spend the next century removing.

Through it all, the people remain quiet—not solemn, and not unafraid, but attentive. Their restraint comes from living where every sound, object, and decision travels. Laughter is common, though usually close-mouthed and brief. Grief is common too. Nothing is denied its passing.

They live as if existence were neither possession nor endurance, but upkeep: the continuous, intimate work of allowing one another to remain for a little longer inside the changing shape of the world.
