The inhabitants call themselves nothing.

A name must be carried from one moment into another, and very little here survives that journey unchanged. The forest has no need to distinguish its trees. The immense bodies in the valleys do not answer one another. Even the small things hidden in stone possess only brief, chemical recognitions: kin, warmth, current, danger, stillness. Whatever intelligence exists in the drowned range is distributed too widely—or lives too slowly—to gather itself into a word.

The most numerous inhabitants are the forests.

Each apparent tree is less an individual than a standing confluence. Its root threads enter mineral seams, follow them for miles, and emerge in other groves, sometimes on opposite faces of the same mountain, sometimes in valleys that do not geometrically adjoin. What looks like a trunk is a place where this buried network has chosen to become visible.

Its overlapping skins inhale more than water. They take in heat, dissolved stone, magnetic inclination, pressure, and minute differences in altitude. Every translucent vane is an instrument of orientation. Together, a crown tastes the shape of the surrounding country.

When the current changes and a whole forest slowly turns, it is not merely feeding. It is looking.

The forests see in gradients. A moving animal appears to them as a knot of displaced warmth. A storm is known days before its arrival as a distortion of pressure traveling through the roots. The silent branching void of lightning is perceived as a sudden blindness, a white wound in their awareness. They remember such wounds by thickening their vanes in the same pattern generations later.

They do not think quickly. A decision can take decades to pass from one end of a grove to the other. Their impulses travel as changes in sap, calcification, and the angle of leaves. A question may begin on a plateau and reach the pendant woods centuries afterward, altered by every trunk through which it has passed.

Most questions concern direction.

The forests are obsessed with what is above.

Not the brighter water overhead, which they understand as weather, but the quality of highness that gathers around peaks and stone rings. Roots crowd toward it even when it contradicts gravity. Sap rises toward it. Seeds germinate facing it. When a ring changes the local down, nearby trees twist until their crowns again point toward altitude, producing spiraled trunks and forests shaped like whirlpools.

Pendant woods are not confused forests. They are faithful ones. The world changed its direction around them, and they continued to grow toward the same invisible height.

Beneath their skins live smaller inhabitants.

Some are no larger than grains of silt: many-legged cleaners that graze dead tissue from the vanes, translucent sacs that drink excess metals, and flat colonies that spread over wounds like living bandages. Every mature trunk houses thousands of species. A few never enter open water. They are born inside the warm channels of the tree, reproduce there, and are dissolved by it when they die.

Others act as nerves.

These thread-bodied creatures braid themselves through root masses and carry contractions from grove to grove. Individually they are almost insensible. In great numbers they give the forest something like pain. When rock breaks, millions tighten at once. The message spreads outward, slow and pale, until distant branches fold though no current touches them.

The bead-colonies moving along contour lines are another phase of the same life. They are the forests loosened from the ground.

At certain intervals, a grove releases portions of its awareness into the water. Each bead contains a speck of mineral memory: the taste of a slope, the direction of a lost summit, the timing of a storm. The colony travels along equal altitude because it is seeking the place where that memory fits. When it finds a receptive mountainside, it darkens the stone like a stain. Centuries later, a new wood rises there already inclined toward currents it has never experienced.

The thin, six-finned creatures belong to these migrations.

Their transparent bodies are nearly empty. They have no stomach and little blood. The black organ turning inside each one is made of layered magnetic tissue surrounding a grain of ancient rock. It does not point north. It points toward the creature’s place of origin—not its birthplace, but the particular degree of highness at which its lineage first entered the world.

They drift through passes with their fins wrapped close, making tiny corrections as the inner needle turns. Some carry forest beads in chambers along their spines. Some carry spores from hanging groves to upright ones. In return, the forests supply them with sugars and repair their fins when they rest among the vanes.

They are not travelers by choice. They are instruments used by the range to compare distant places.

Near the stone rings, their compass organs spin wildly. Most flee. A few enter and are altered by the passage. They emerge oriented toward an altitude that does not exist nearby and spend the remainder of their lives following it. Such creatures may be seen crossing open water with absolute purpose, descending into abyssal valleys or swimming straight into mountain rock. Occasionally one vanishes against a solid slope without collision, as though the correct direction were also an entrance.

The eyeless crack-dwellers are older.

They have soft, jointed bodies, fans of white cilia, and mouths built to rasp bacterial films from stone. Yet feeding occupies little of their lives. They wait through years of current for moments of complete stillness, then emerge to taste the water.

Motion overwhelms them because they sense not movement but sequence. Each cilium registers which event comes before another. In ordinary currents, too many local futures arrive at once, and the creatures retreat in distress. When the water stops, the possibilities narrow. They crawl out and sample the order of the world.

Around unstable plateaus, their cilia sometimes whiten entire rock faces. There they test intersecting valleys, frozen moments, and distances that have folded together. If the sequence is safe, the colony advances. If it is not, they withdraw before the danger has happened.

Travelers once mistook this for prophecy. It is closer to touch.

The stone rings have inhabitants of their own, though they are difficult to recognize as living.

The cold patch inside an empty circle is one. The central pillar in another may be the same organism in a denser state. Rings that appear abandoned often retain a slight preference: sediment curves around them, roots avoid their openings, and nearby creatures dream in common rhythms.

These beings live in orientation rather than matter. Stone accumulates around their boundaries as shells accumulate around soft bodies. They feed on differences between one down and another, on the strain produced when gravity disagrees with altitude. Where that strain is rich, a ring grows.

No block was cut or fitted. Each was persuaded into place over thousands of years.

A ring begins as a minute chill in the water. Mineral flakes turn toward it. Sand settles in a curve. The curve closes, and within it the local meaning of descent changes. As the organism matures, it becomes capable of holding several directions at once. A central pillar forms if those directions can be reconciled. If they cannot, the center remains water—colder, clearer, and deeper than its dimensions permit.

The pulses that pass through the mountains may be their reproductive signals.

When one sounds, every ring in the range answers by briefly tightening its boundary. Loose particles align. Hanging forests contract. Creatures with compass organs turn toward altitudes they have never visited. Sometimes a new coldness appears on a remote plateau. Millennia later, stones will have gathered around it.

Yet the rings themselves may only be organs of something larger.

This suspicion begins with their common altitude. It continues beneath the plateaus, where roots entering the rings do not die but disappear from ordinary space. On rare occasions they return elsewhere, thickened and pale, bearing mineral signatures from mountains impossibly distant. All the circles may be openings in a single body that intersects the range thousands of times.

If so, that body does not live in the water.

It lives in highness.

The great valley-crossers are more plainly animals, though no one has seen an entire one.

A raised ridge may be a back. A slowly folding elevation may be a fin. The pressure preceding them suggests bodies hundreds of miles long, but their wakes imply still greater dimensions, as if only a narrow portion enters the valley while the remainder passes through neighboring versions of it.

They are not fish. Their flesh is composed of alternating layers of dense fluid and porous stone, with forests rooted along the upper surfaces. Some mountains thought to be fixed may be sleeping valley-crossers sedimented into the terrain. Their breathing would explain the periodic pulses felt through bone and bedrock. Their migrations might account for peaks that change position over centuries.

They feed rarely. When they do, entire plankton curtains vanish from a basin without disturbance. The creature does not open a mouth. It alters the pressure within its body, and the occupied water simply becomes part of it.

The forests bow before their arrival not because of the water they displace, but because they recognize the mineral chemistry of their own roots. Groves grow upon the valley-crossers. Spores germinate in their porous hides. When one passes, every stationary forest nearby senses a moving piece of the ground and inclines toward it as toward kin.

During the darker season, the great animals rise toward the summits. They circle the stone rings but do not cross them. Sometimes they remain motionless beside a high plateau for years, close enough that their hidden warmth changes the local climate. New gardens bloom along the slope. Small creatures leave the rocks and gather on the unseen body.

Then a pulse passes through the range, and the visitor descends.

There may be one greater inhabitant.

Its existence cannot be separated from the landscape.

The evidence lies in recurrence: mountain chains curved like the folds of an organ; thermal rivers that carry compounds resembling nourishment; forests acting as sensory tissue; valley-crossers moving like cells through immense vessels; stone rings maintaining a shared altitude like pores along an invisible skin.

Perhaps the drowned range is not a place occupied by life. Perhaps it is life at a scale that makes geography appear inert.

Its thought, if thought is the word, occurs through erosion. It compares one shape of mountain with another over ages. It moves forests to test new arrangements of sensation. It lets valleys overlap, holds slopes in unfinished moments, and relocates peaks as slowly as a sleeping creature shifts its limbs. The impossible rings are points where its inner orientation touches the world.

The rare animals are transient concentrations of its attention.

The forests are its patient senses.

The vast bodies below are impulses passing through it.

And the ancient pulse, arriving from whichever direction one faces, may be neither call nor heartbeat. It may be the momentary act by which the whole range notices itself.

Between those moments, everything living there waits—not idly, but with the profound occupation of beings for whom stillness is a form of travel, growth a form of memory, and extinction only another change in direction.
