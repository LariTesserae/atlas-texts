The inhabitants are not a people, though a visitor might first mistake their works for evidence of one. They are the works themselves: an interdependent commonwealth of alpine plants, fungi, lichens, and microbial life whose bodies have become streets, chambers, roofs, cisterns, and walls.

No individual among them is large enough to contain the settlement’s apparent purpose. The pillar-thick shrubs know only pressure, moisture, damage, warmth, and light. Their roots brace loose stone and fuse beneath repeated footfall. Their stems hollow as they age, becoming dry corridors and insulated rooms. Their leaves overlap wherever heat escapes. When cold air enters, bark-lips swell and draw together; when breath or meltwater returns, they relax. A house is therefore not owned by a plant. It is a temporary behavior shared by several.

The oldest inhabitants are the crawling dwarf trunks. Bent flat by centuries of wind, they form the city’s deep frame. Their living tips may be far from the weathered centers of their bodies, so that what appears to be a grove is sometimes one ancient being repeatedly rooted along its length. Mosses cover its scars. Liverworts occupy the wet folds of its bark. Lichens mine its exposed surfaces grain by grain, and wiry vines stitch separate limbs together. These elders do not stand above their dependents. They carry them.

Between them live dense, low communities of cushion plants. Each cushion consists of innumerable tiny shoots packed closely enough to retain warmth. The outer shoots freeze, dry, and blunt the wind; the inner ones remain moist and faintly active. Over decades, dead leaves accumulate beneath the living surface and become soil. Other species take root there, making every cushion a hill, nursery, burial ground, and neighborhood at once.

The settlement’s more conspicuous structures belong to colonial plants with thick bulbs and braided stems. Their chambers are enlarged internodes, emptied pith, or spaces deliberately maintained between interlocking bodies. Fine hairs line the interiors, trapping still air. Sap-rich tissues generate a little heat through respiration, seldom enough to feel warm to a hand but enough to keep certain walls flexible and water briefly liquid. During severe cold, sugars arrive through fungal threads and grafted roots from older, better-stored districts. The chambers survive because the whole settlement feeds them.

Fungi inhabit nearly every boundary. Their pale filaments run through bark, soil, dead stems, and fissured stone, linking plants that could not otherwise exchange nutrients. Fruiting bodies appear as stairs, ledges, shutters, and shelves, but these are only seasonal surfaces of a much larger hidden population. Beneath the lanes, fungal networks regulate the passage of minerals and decay. They digest fallen structures without dismantling the city, converting each collapse into food and foundation.

Lichens hold the outermost territory. They spread over raw rock where rooted plants cannot yet live, dissolving mineral grains with acids and catching airborne dust in their crusts. They are slow even by the city’s measure, but they are its pioneers. Behind them come mosses; behind mosses, rootlets; behind rootlets, the first load-bearing stems. Thus each new district begins as a stain on stone.

The black flowers in the deep courtyards are among the settlement’s briefest inhabitants. Their dark petals absorb what little heat penetrates the canopy, while their silvered margins shed frost toward the leaf-cups below. Small white insects shelter in their centers. Some feed on pollen; some are fed by sugary secretions and, in return, carry grains between chambers. Neither plant nor insect can travel far, so flowering passes through the settlement in waves, each courtyard opening only when neighboring tissues signal suitable warmth.

The carnivorous cups make use of what animal life exists. Midges drown in their rain-dark wells and are dissolved slowly by acidic fluid. Yet the cups also shelter larvae during freezes, and some close before digestion begins, preserving rather than consuming their occupants. Hunger and refuge are not stable categories here. The same chamber may be a cradle in one season and a stomach in another.

Seed pods are the nearest things the city has to wandering citizens. Jointed and sensitive to moisture, they contract unevenly as they wet and dry. Over hours or days they drag themselves along root channels, turning toward trickling water or away from exposed cold. Some reach the cistern garden and open there, releasing seeds into its descending chain of leaf-bowls. Others lodge in cracks, where their swelling coats prise apart the rock just enough for a root to enter.

The rare animals live within opportunities the vegetation permits. Silver-furred mice nest in dead root arteries, lining them with shed fibers and fungal felt. They eat seeds, grubs, and the sweet callus that forms around injured stems. Their tunnels aerate old tissues, and their droppings carry spores into dry chambers. Yet the city can close around them. A route used one winter may tighten beyond passage by the next, leaving the mice to discover newly hollowed veins.

The dark birds are visitors rather than residents. They descend singly, compact and round against the cold, probing moss for insects or tearing open swollen pods. Their disturbance triggers pollen release and shakes retained snow from the canopy. They depart before the nocturnal constriction, perhaps because instinct remembers what happens to creatures caught when the vents fold shut.

There are smaller lives everywhere: mites among leaf hairs, translucent worms in meltwater films, bacteria in resin wounds, algae beneath clear ice, rotifers dormant in frozen moss. Most spend much of the year in suspension. A few hours of thaw may be enough for them to feed, reproduce, or migrate the width of a leaf. To them, the city’s internal weather—the opening of a pore, the fall of a droplet, the cooling shadow of a folding frond—is climate on a continental scale.

No inhabitant perceives the settlement whole. The roots answer gradients. Leaves answer light. Fungi answer chemistry. Vines answer tension; flowers, temperature; bark, injury; moss, water. Yet their responses pass from body to body. Roots graft. Fungal threads redistribute sugars. Volatile signals drift through enclosed lanes. Pressure on one stem changes the growth of another. A wound alters the chemistry of the runoff and is detected downstream.

Together, these exchanges produce something broader than any single organism’s awareness. The city notices weight as thickening beneath it. It notices wind as constriction. It notices drought as the closing of pores and redirection of water. It notices a breathing animal as warmth and carbon dioxide, opening certain chambers while sealing others. Its attention is slow, distributed, and inseparable from growth.

Its memory is equally physical. Repeated passage hardens a lane. Recurring frost makes a hinge. Old damage leaves amber seams that redirect future strain. Water remembers its route by deepening gutters; roots remember water by following them. There are no records except form, and no form that is not also a record.

What lives there, then, is not merely a multitude of plants. It is a society without citizens in the ordinary sense, and perhaps an organism without a single body. Every inhabitant remains itself—moss, shrub, fungus, flower, lichen, insect, mouse—yet each is also tissue in a larger, changing anatomy.

The settlement lives there.

Its streets are its vessels, its chambers its lungs, its cistern garden a many-mouthed kidney. The ancient trunks are bones that continue to grow. Fungi are both gut and nervous web. Snow is stored water; stone is food not yet softened; decay is neither waste nor death, but internal weather.

It has no ruler, no builders, and no fixed design. It survives by turning every pressure into architecture and every ending into another inhabited surface.
