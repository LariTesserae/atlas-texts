The dominant inhabitants are not the creatures most easily seen, but those suspended in the shallow water and sealed into the mud: algae, bacteria, minute crustaceans, worms, insect larvae, eggs, spores, and translucent fry. They occupy every temporary pool as though it were a whole sea. When water withdraws, many retreat into damp seams or endure as buried cysts, waiting for the basin to fill again.

Small fish thrive in the old channels. They are narrow-bodied and mud-colored, with pale bellies and fins suited less to sustained swimming than to quick turns through sedge roots and beneath stone shelves. They feed in flashes, dimpling the surface after insects, then vanish at the passage of a shadow. In isolated pools they crowd together, surviving warm, oxygen-poor water by gulping at the air or lying nearly motionless in cooler hollows.

The frogs are compact, broad-toed animals patterned in gray, olive, and rust. By day they wedge themselves into wet clay or press beneath overhanging rock, where condensation keeps their skin slick. Their stillness makes them look like lumps of mineral until the heat softens. Then one begins calling, and others answer across distances too great for their small bodies to suggest. After rain they emerge in numbers, pairing and laying ropes or films of eggs in water shallow enough to warm quickly.

Insects possess the air. Fine-bodied midges rise in columns over dark pools. Water striders skate on the reflective skins. Beetles dive with silver bubbles held against their bodies. Dragonflies patrol the channels, pausing on the highest available points—which may be only the tip of a rush or the exposed horn of a stone. Their aquatic young wait below, buried to the eyes, taking tadpoles and fry with sudden extending jaws.

The birds are the basin’s most visible citizens. Long-legged waders pass through the water in deliberate steps, stirring the mud with narrow feet or probing it with sensitive bills. Some are pale enough to dissolve into glare; others carry dark backs, barred wings, or rust-colored throats that resemble the stained rock. They do not form dense crowds except where a shrinking pool concentrates food. More often they keep broad intervals, each tending a private circumference of ripples.

Smaller shorebirds run along wet margins with startling speed, stopping all at once to peck at something too small to see. Broad-billed birds sieve the soft bottom. Taller hunters stand without movement until a fish comes close, then strike and recover their stillness. Farther out, floating birds sleep with their heads turned into their feathers, drifting almost imperceptibly across warm pools. The basin’s scattered flocks are always assembling and breaking apart, guided by water levels invisible from the ground.

The broad-footed grazers are heavy, low-slung animals made for yielding country. Their toes spread in mud rather than sinking deeply; their legs are thick below the joint and darkened by wet silt. Sparse coats protect them from sun without holding too much heat. They feed on sedges, low mineral plants, and tender growth exposed as water recedes. Family groups move single-file over remembered firm patches, each foot falling near the impressions of animals long dead. At the seeps they scrape salt and other minerals from the rock with rough tongues.

Smaller hoofed animals visit at cooler hours. They are lightly built, wide-eared, and almost the color of dry clay. They graze quickly, lifting their heads often, uneasy in a country with so little cover. Their young flatten against low shelves or among sedge clumps, relying on stillness and mottled coats rather than concealment.

Predators live by the boundaries between wet and dry. Lean, long-legged hunters follow the waterline, taking frogs, stranded fish, nesting birds, and whatever the floods leave exposed. Their paws are broad and their tread careful. They seldom pursue prey far across open ground; instead they listen beside reed clumps, nose along inherited tracks, and wait where a narrow channel must be crossed. Smaller predators slip under stone lips and through sparse vegetation, hunting eggs, lizards, insects, and burrowing animals.

Reptiles belong to the warm backs of rock. Flat-bodied lizards spread themselves against the stone in the morning, changing from the dull colors of night to patterns of ochre and violet-gray. At danger they do not run far, but disappear into cracks scarcely wider than their skulls. Snakes follow frogs and small fish into the shallows. Some lie in algal water with only nostrils and eyes above the surface; others coil beneath damp shelves, patterned like mineral stains and nearly impossible to distinguish until they move.

Burrowers occupy the firmer, salt-paled ground. Small rodents excavate shallow chambers above the flood line, plugging entrances against midday heat and wet-season water. Crabs or crablike scavengers honeycomb softer banks where old channels retain enough moisture. Their pellets of worked mud gather around their holes, only to be flattened by the next sheet of rain. Everything edible is carried below: seeds, shed insect skins, fragments of eggshell, drowned larvae.

Most of the basin’s inhabitants are adapted not to abundance or scarcity alone, but to alternation. They breed quickly when rain spreads across the flats. They migrate when pools contract. They sleep underground, seal themselves in mud, store eggs resistant to drying, or follow the retreating edge of water. Few depend on any single pool, channel, or season. Their home is the shifting pattern itself.

There is no master of the place. Grazers alter the sedges; frogs consume the insects; birds consume the frogs and fish; predators take birds and young grazers; scavengers dismantle what remains. Algae feed snails and larvae, whose bodies become wings, calls, feathers, scales, and bone. Droppings enrich shallow water. Tracks become runnels. Burrows admit rain. A hoofprint holds enough water for eggs to hatch.

Thus the basin is inhabited everywhere, though rarely crowded in any one place. Its life is dispersed across films, edges, hollows, and intervals: creatures colored like stone, creatures transparent as water, creatures known chiefly by calls traveling through haze. They do not impose themselves upon the ancient floor. They enter its slight depressions, follow its nearly vanished channels, and time their lives to the patient recurrence of water.
