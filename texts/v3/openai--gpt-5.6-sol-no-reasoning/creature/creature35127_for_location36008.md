The people of the basin are few, though the town continually implies a greater population. They pass through galleries built for crowds, work rows of machines longer than their shifts require, and cross loading yards where their footsteps are the only traffic. A stranger might assume most residents are indoors. After several days, it becomes clear there are simply not many of them.

They are lean without appearing starved, dressed in close-fitted layers that resist drizzle and do not snag on thorns, valves, or unfinished grafts. Their outer garments are made from leaf laminate and oiled fiber: gray, brown, faded green, occasionally marked at the shoulders with the muted colors of a workshop or water district. Aprons carry dark stains from resin and seed oil. Gloves are often fingerless, since so much of the work depends on touch—testing the warmth of bark, the tremor in a pipe, the pressure beneath a bud that ought not yet to open.

Nearly everyone carries tools. A curved pruning blade is most common, followed by a narrow wrench, a folding gauge, and a vial or two in padded loops. The vials contain hormones, fungal cultures, sealants, anticoagulant saps, or plain colored water for tracing a plant’s circulation. None is labeled with words. Their corks, knots, and beadwork identify them by touch, useful in rain or darkness.

The basin does not divide its workers neatly into gardeners, mechanics, builders, and physicians. Such distinctions survive mostly in old records. A person who services a pump-tree must understand fluid pressure, root disease, metal fatigue, and the temperament of the colony into which the pump has been grafted. Those who maintain dynamos listen to the copper windings and examine the pale cambium around them for heat stress. Rail keepers measure gauge, prune lateral growth, and negotiate with adjoining property colonies for the water needed to keep the hardwood lines from shrinking.

They describe their trades with verbs rather than titles. One keeps the west conduits. Another wakes presses. Another settles walls. Someone else attends fruiting signals or walks drought through the nursery beds. A respected worker is said to have a good interval: knowing when to intervene, when to wait, and when a failure should be redirected rather than corrected.

Children learn the town first through its prohibitions. Do not step on silver roots after rain. Do not lean against a wall while it is drinking. Never cut a blue-tied shoot, even if it blocks a public way. Do not follow the sound of water below the third culvert. If a doorway closes behind you, wait before forcing it; the building may know about a strain you do not.

Later, each prohibition unfolds into practical knowledge. Silver roots are swollen and easily scarred. Drinking walls can pull warmth from a body with alarming speed. Blue ties mark load-bearing growth. Deep culverts contain old channels whose maps are unreliable. A closing doorway often means that dry tissue elsewhere has shifted the frame. Caution becomes understanding by accumulation.

Their houses occupy the smaller woven structures or chambers hollowed without killing the broad trunks. Interiors are plain, warm, and irregular. Floors rise subtly toward the walls. Shelves emerge where needed and are encouraged to remain, while unwanted knobs are bound flat during growth. Sleeping recesses are lined with dry pith felt. A household stove vents through a flower chimney whose throat opens when heated and seals against cold air after the fire dies.

Possessions are limited less by doctrine than by the inconveniences of living architecture. A heavy cabinet may distort a floor over several seasons. A sealed box invites mold. Anything kept long enough acquires roots, stains, or obligations. Thus most belongings hang from pegs or travel in baskets: clothes, tools, ceramic cups, coils of cord, packets of seed, copper tokens, folded maps. Objects inherited across generations are often mineral—glass lenses, porcelain handles, steel instruments, fragments of brick engraved with names—because the living portions of an inheritance have usually changed beyond recognition.

Meals come largely from what the town produces while doing something else. Pressed seed mash is fermented into dark cakes. Conduit vines yield edible tips when trimmed. Cooling ponds grow thin sheets of green curd. Fruiting walls produce small, astringent bulbs that are stewed with oil until sweet. Fungal vats beneath the mills provide most substantial food, their output varying with factory heat and the quality of runoff.

Nothing is served in abundance, but little is wasted. Even celebration food retains the basin’s restrained character: hot loaves of steamed pith, clear broth scented with resin, preserved fruit opened from ceramic jars, and brittle sheets of sugar drawn from sap. Strong drink is made from rain-swollen bladder plants. It tastes faintly mineral and leaves the tongue numb.

The people speak quietly, not from solemnity but because the settlement rewards listening. Machinery announces trouble by subtle changes in rhythm. Roots click beneath pavement before lifting it. A healthy cistern makes one sound while filling and another when a membrane has begun to tear. Voices are kept beneath these signals.

Their speech is full of growth used as grammar. Plans are trained rather than made. A dispute is allowed to branch. A debt takes root. A marriage grafts households, though the term does not imply permanence: grafts can fail cleanly, grow into each other, or remain joined while exchanging almost nothing. Death is called release when the body is given to industrial compost, and burial when someone is sealed in mineral ground beyond the embankment. Release is more common.

Names change with responsibility. A child has a household name; an apprentice acquires a work name; anyone entrusted with a major system may be called by that system’s location. The keeper of the western seep becomes West-Seep in public speech, even if their family still uses the name given in infancy. When they retire, the name passes on. Old records can therefore appear to describe impossibly long-lived individuals until one understands that duties, not bodies, are being chronicled.

Government is similarly attached to functions. There is no mayor and no permanent council chamber. Water keepers meet when water requires it. Rail tenders meet over gauge and passage. Heat workers decide winter nursery allocations. Those whose colonies share buried roots must approve deep excavation, because damage in one district may surface years later in another.

Agreements are recorded through scar patterns grown into witness plants. A cut made in a prescribed shape is treated with pigments and hormones, producing a ridge whose age and sequence can be read later. Important covenants may occupy an entire trunk. The oldest witness plants have become difficult texts: clauses swallowed by new bark, exceptions distorted by lightning, adjacent agreements fused where trunks leaned together. Interpreters spend years learning to distinguish deliberate scar from disease or accident.

They do not imagine these records to be infallible. Law, like everything else, lives and alters. The authority of a scar lies not in being permanent but in showing how it healed.

Conflict tends to center on access: water, warmth, light, root room, and the right to cut. Theft is usually diversion. Sabotage is usually infection. The gravest offense is to introduce an unregistered strain into shared soil, since its consequences may not appear for decades. Accused strains are quarantined in ceramic beds. Accused people are rarely confined; instead their tools may be taken, their graft permissions suspended, and their paths through living structures revoked. A town that can close its own doors has little need for a prison.

The oldest residents carry the basin in their bodies. Their fingers have thickened from sap and cold metal. Their hearing is tuned to pumps and distant water. Many bear pale chemical burns or fine black lines where fungal resin entered cuts and healed beneath the skin. Joint pain is common in wet weather. So are respiratory ailments among those who work fermentation rooms or pollen mechanisms.

Some replace damaged parts with cultivated aids. A stiffened hand may wear a living brace that tightens when warmed. Missing fingers are answered by hooked wooden prostheses whose grip slowly adjusts to the wearer. There are breathing collars grown from porous tissue, ear trumpets shaped like pale flowers, splints that feed on skin salt and must be pruned monthly. These devices are not considered part of the town’s architecture, though outsiders often struggle to see the distinction.

The basin’s amphibians occupy an uneasy place in local thought. Workers lift them from active rail lines after prolonged rain and set them beside drains, using wet bark rather than bare hands. Children are told not to count them aloud. Engineers note their appearances on maintenance charts because they may indicate pressure changes in the old pipe network. Some residents insist the creatures are merely animals. Others call them overflow bodies: temporary forms taken by the buried systems when the town holds more water than it can quietly contain.

No doctrine has settled the question, and no urgency attends it. In the basin, uncertainty is often managed rather than resolved.

The black bird is regarded differently. Its visits halt work because it belongs unmistakably to elsewhere. It brings no message anyone can interpret, yet its presence reveals the sky as a route rather than a ceiling. People watch it drink from the reservoir lip, cock its head at the signal fruit, and depart over the embankment toward the dry roads. For the rest of the day, conversation tends to turn toward distant places.

Few residents leave permanently, but nearly everyone has some relation beyond the basin. Copper comes from elsewhere, as do ceramics, coal for the small steam engine, certain medicines, and the salt required by people if not by most structures. Freight carriages depart carrying oil, resin fittings, woven laminate, seed stock, and dormant machine cuttings packed in damp felt. Outsiders occasionally arrive to commission a workshop organism or purchase a pump adapted to poor water.

Visitors find the inhabitants courteous and difficult to hurry. Basin workers distrust deadlines stated without weather. They ask where a device will live, what will shade it, who will feed it, and what should happen when it outgrows its purpose. A client seeking an object is made to describe an entire future habitat. This can feel evasive until the completed machine survives forty years with only seasonal pruning.

The town’s small population is not accidental. Its systems could feed more people, but not without changing the balance of heat, water, and root competition that has stabilized over centuries. Residency is therefore inherited, exchanged, or granted alongside a vacancy in the town’s work. Newcomers serve a long provisional period. They learn not only a trade but the basin’s pace: to recognize action that looks like waiting, and waiting that is actually neglect.

Those who cannot adjust often leave during the cold dry season, when the woven walls stand bare and the settlement seems most skeletal. Those who remain tend to do so decisively. The town enters their habits. They begin collecting condensation without noticing, touching railings to judge temperature, listening before opening a door.

At dusk they return along root roads that soften beneath their steps and slowly rise again. Lamps are few. Some carry low bioluminescent bulbs in mesh cages; others navigate by the pale signal fruits, by remembered knots in the pavement, and by the different warmths of buildings. Meals are eaten near vents or shared hearths. Tools are cleaned of sap. Wet garments hang where walls will draw them dry.

Conversation travels through the houses in low intervals. Someone reports a flowering valve. Someone has seen an amphibian in a pipe thought sealed for sixty years. A rail has narrowed near the seed depot. A child has been accepted into the cooling works. A witness tree has exposed an older scar beneath a split in its bark, and tomorrow several households will gather to decide whether the recovered agreement still binds them.

Then the chimneys close. Collection tendrils move over the metal outside. The inhabitants sleep inside structures that continue working around them—circulating water, sealing wounds, shifting their weight by fractions.

The town is alive, but not in place of its people. Nor are the people its masters. They are its brief, mobile organs: carrying tools and memories where roots cannot reach, interpreting strains too slow for machines to notice, cutting what growth cannot restrain in itself. The basin shelters them, employs them, and will outlast each of them, yet it depends on their small corrections.

Without the town, they would be mechanics, gardeners, millers, surveyors, and scavengers.

Within it, they are keepers of the interval between cultivation and repair.
