The beings of the shale are not easily separated from the shale itself. They do not merely camouflage themselves against the plateau; they continue its surfaces, repeat its layers, and inherit its ancient, flattened geometry. Shell becomes pavement, herd becomes drainage, colony becomes seam. Even where an animal can be lifted free, its underside bears the pattern of the bedding plane beneath it, as though stone had pressed its memory into the body.

Most numerous are the laminar drinkers. Each is a thin, many-jointed creature no larger than a hand, though age may broaden some until they resemble shields. Their overlapping plates are dark and finely grained, stained blue, red, or charcoal by the minerals they process. Along the front edge lies a fringe of transparent combs. At dusk these rise almost imperceptibly and catch condensation. A droplet is divided among the comb-teeth, filtered, and passed backward through capillary grooves to the next body in the colony.

No individual drinks alone. Those nearest an exposed rim harvest the air but lose more water to evaporation; those deep within the formation are protected but cannot reach the wind. Joined edge to edge, they create a living aqueduct. Colonies may spread across entire escarpments, subtly changing shape over generations as the prevailing currents alter. Their young are almost fluid and move through microscopic gaps between adults. Only after finding an unoccupied edge do they harden into the local color and grain.

When drought persists, the drinkers close. Their plates draw together until every soft part is sealed, and the colony becomes a continuous mineral-looking crust. It can remain so for years. During these intervals, slow electrical exchanges continue through contact points in the shells. Whether this is communication, metabolism, or merely the equalization of charge is impossible to distinguish. Here, those functions may never have become separate.

The tall basin animals are water-bearers. Their many legs are threadlike but immensely strong, arranged in paired rows beneath long, vaulted bodies. Each foot spreads into a broad pad that rests on the shale without scraping it. Their backs contain stacks of hollow chambers lined with pale membranes. At night the chambers open to the cold air and gather vapor; before day they close, holding a succession of clear internal lenses.

They migrate between condensation grounds, salt fields, and buried cisterns. A single crossing may take most of a season. Direction passes through the herd as slight changes in posture. The first rank inclines its chambers; hours later the animals behind repeat the adjustment, until the entire procession has bent toward some distant seepage path. They neither lead nor follow in any simple sense. Each responds to pressure, charge, mineral scent, and the minute tremors conducted through the rock by thousands of feet.

The pellets they leave are not refuse alone. Each is a layered capsule of salts the animal cannot presently use, bound in a matrix that resists the dry wind. Smaller creatures open these deposits grain by grain. Some carry the contents underground. Others arrange the husks into low ridges that trap ice. Over centuries, the abandoned rows become dark parallel strata, and later herds use them as routes.

Beneath the basins live the sorters: blind burrowers with soft wedge-shaped heads and rings of muscular filters. They swallow mineral water from porous seams, retaining rare dissolved elements and expelling the common salts through six or eight vents. The crystals emerge as damp paste and harden in geometric heaps around the burrow mouth. What appears from above to be an immense hexagonal field is the joined work of generations, every chamber positioned according to stress lines in the shale.

Sorter colonies regulate the hidden reservoirs. When pressure rises, they open their bodies and allow water through. When it falls, they swell against the fissures and close them. Entire valleys may therefore be supplied by something resembling a system of valves, though no creature knows the whole system and none controls it. Larvae drift through the groundwater until fluctuations lodge them in suitable cracks. There they grow into the shape required by the aperture: round, branching, ribbon-thin, or spread across several meters like a buried diaphragm.

Around warm faults, life becomes denser and stranger. The interlocking fault-dwellers have shallow metallic carapaces and soft conductive tissues branching underneath. Individually they can move, but in congregation their limbs mesh with those of their neighbors. They assemble into sheets kilometers long. Heat from below drives chemical differences between one portion of the sheet and another; charge accumulates, then passes from body to body.

The resulting blue illumination begins as a dim line at one end of a fault. Each animal brightens only as it receives the pulse and darkens as it sends it onward. The current can take hours to cross a basin and days to traverse the longest fractures. During breeding seasons, the pulse determines when sealed brood chambers open. Young emerge behind it in a pale advancing zone, consume the mineral films exposed by the adults’ movement, and settle into the spaces left by deaths in the older layer.

The sheet retains patterns. A fracture, a predatory intrusion, or an unusual influx of warm water changes the pathways through which later currents travel. Repeated events establish preferred routes. Thus the congregation possesses a kind of memory, not held in any one animal but engraved into the conductivity of the whole. Some patterns endure longer than individual lineages. A pulse may turn aside from a fissure because a disturbance occurred there centuries before.

Predators are correspondingly slow. The greatest are broad, nearly featureless masses that rest beneath loose plates or along the sheltered sides of escarpments. Their flesh contains hydraulic chambers. By filling those on one side and emptying those on the other, they advance in contractions so gradual that mineral dust settles undisturbed on their backs. A large hunter may take days to cross the length of its own body.

They do not pursue individuals. Instead they reshape routes. One spreads across a seepage line, another warms a patch of ground enough to divert condensation, a third releases salts that cause the laminar colonies to loosen at their edges. Over weeks, prey are guided toward folds in the hunter’s body. Enclosure comes so slowly that escape remains possible almost until the last moment, yet the animals caught inside seldom panic. They alter direction, divide, or surrender their stored water to neighboring bodies before being dissolved.

Some predators feed only once in many years. Between feedings they host entire ecologies: rust filaments grow on their backs, drinkers tile their surfaces, and dormant young shelter in their pores. A hunter rising from quiescence may carry away a piece of landscape complete with grazers, scavengers, eggs, and miniature reservoirs.

The sail-bearers inhabit the higher pavements. They are low-bodied, with one or several transparent membranes folded tightly along the spine. When pressure becomes favorable, the sails rise into the wind. The animals do not fly. They allow themselves to be lightened just enough that their feet scarcely touch the ground, then glide for kilometers without a sound. Some transport spores and larvae in grooves beneath the membrane. Others trail charged filaments through the air, gathering the faint electrical difference between cloud and stone.

Where the great blunt ribs rise, shelter creates abundance. Their protected faces are covered in clinging forms stacked from base to summit: cup-bodied condensers, mineral grazers, pale thread colonies, and heavy shelled animals that spend their lives ascending a few meters. From afar this accumulation resembles fur. Close by, it is a vertical city without permanent architecture, every ledge occupied by layered bodies and every body occupied in turn by smaller ones.

Communication throughout the plateau is chiefly a matter of pressure and delay. A foot placed on stone, a valve closing belowground, or a shell changing charge may be detected far away, but perhaps only after hours. Responses propagate at different speeds. One signal travels mechanically through shale, another chemically through seepage, another electrically along a colony, and another biologically as animals turn and their neighbors turn after them. What the plateau “knows” at any moment is distributed across these overlapping times.

There are sleepers deeper than the burrowers. Their dormant young fill old tunnels as dry translucent grains, each sealed around a minute reserve of water. Some do not open for centuries. Heat, pressure, and a particular dissolved salt must arrive together before they soften. Consequently, organisms may emerge into a surface ecology that no longer resembles the one that produced them. Many fail. Others unfold ancient structures that become useful again: forgotten filters, obsolete mouthparts, chambers suited to rainfall patterns absent for ages. The past repeatedly enters the present in living form.

Death is seldom visible. Bodies are emptied, partitioned, and inherited before they decay. A shell becomes a condensation plate. A chambered back becomes a nursery of filterers. Conductive tissue is threaded into the fault congregations. Even the hardest carapaces eventually separate into flakes and lie among the shale, where new laminar drinkers settle upon them. Fossil and organism differ mainly in the speed at which they participate.

No creature possesses the plateau, yet nearly all of it is held. Water belongs for a night to the combs, for a season to the herds, for a century to a sealed cyst, and for an age to the blind cisterns. Salt passes from reservoir to sorter, sorter to field, field to grazer, grazer to predator, and predator back into stone. Charge runs briefly through a million joined bodies and disappears into the cloud.

The closest thing here to a single dominant life is therefore not any species. It is the exchange itself: the immense, reticulated continuity formed by all bodies, tunnels, routes, and conductive seams. Its organs are temporary. Its circulation may take centuries. Its memory lies partly in tissue, partly in trails, partly in altered strata. It has no center from which it can perceive, yet disturbance in one basin may eventually change the behavior of creatures beyond several horizons.

When condensation begins, this greater being becomes briefly apparent. The flat drinkers open. Underground valves relax. Water-bearers incline toward the darkening ground. Sail membranes lift into the pressure of the wind. Along a remote fault, the first blue segment shines, then gives its light to the next.

Nothing announces awakening. There is no cry and scarcely any motion.

The stone simply acquires function.
