The inhabitants are people, though at first glance the town seems populated only by vegetation and machinery.

They are slight, deliberate figures in waxed coats and close-fitting hoods, dressed less against cold than against perpetual wet. Their clothes are laminated from bark fiber, woven stems, and oil-treated cloth. Aprons are stiff with resin. Boots have broad, softly ridged soles that grip mossy stone without cutting the living root paths. Nothing hangs loose: belts, hair, sleeves, and tools are secured against flywheels, vines, and narrow passages. Metal buckles are rare. Fastenings are ceramic, carved wood, braided cord, or tough strips of layered bark.

Moisture marks them. Their hair is cropped, bound, or covered; their hands are pale from damp but heavily callused at the fingertips and palms. Sap stains, charcoal, green copper, and machine grease work permanently into the creases of their skin. Many smell faintly of whichever district employs them—starch and fermentation, hot cellulose, seed oil, wet ash. Their voices tend to be low and close, shaped by a place where shouting is both unnecessary and difficult beneath the machinery.

They have become extraordinarily attentive to vibration. A pump’s failing bearing, a blocked intake, or a slipping belt may be recognized through a railing before it is heard. Workers pause with two fingers against pipes or lay a palm on root-braced walls. Children learn the ordinary tremors of their homes as surface children might learn birdsong. A change in the floor’s hum wakes sleepers. Silence is alarming.

Their town has no separate class of gardeners in the ordinary sense, because nearly everyone tends plants. Still, there are specialists: rootwrights who train living supports; waterkeepers who manage the Basin and its reed partitions; fitters who maintain pumps and slow motors; fiber workers, press crews, kiln tenders, seed crushers, channel clearers, and ceiling inspectors. The most trusted among them are often those who understand two systems at once—a machinist who can judge the health of a root, or a cultivator who knows how altering a bed will change the load on a pump.

They do not treat the vegetation as decoration, nor quite as wilderness. Individual plants are known by function, age, temperament, and location. A supporting trunk may have a name and a maintenance history extending through generations. Families remember when a certain root entered a wall, when it first took weight, and which rooms were surrendered to it as it thickened. Cutting such a structure without agreement would be regarded as both sabotage and butchery. Yet sentiment does not prevent pruning. Growth must be negotiated with, and negotiation includes disciplined harm.

The absence of animals shapes everything. There are no pets, pests, livestock, pollinating insects, or vermin lurking at the edge of lamplight. No one sets traps. No one listens for wings or scratching in the walls. Flowers that require pollination are worked by hand with brushes, puffs of air, or vibrating ceramic forks. Seeds are precious and carefully catalogued. Children may know animals only from diagrams, old carvings, and words whose practical meanings have faded. Leather, wool, bone, horn, milk, and meat belong to inherited stories rather than daily objects.

Their food comes from tubers, stems, leaves, grains grown beneath the brighter shafts, pressed seeds, algae cultures, and carefully managed fermentation. Long warm galleries hold trays of pale shoots. Reservoir margins produce edible reeds and floating greens. Salt and mineral supplements are measured with unusual seriousness. Meals are soft, dense, and strongly flavored with smoke, sour brine, bitter leaves, and aromatic resins. Eating is communal partly from custom and partly because cooking heat, clean water, and dry storage are expensive to duplicate.

Homes occupy whatever spaces the working town can spare: former offices behind mills, lofts under fern-heavy roofs, chambers partitioned by woven willow and curtains of broad leaves. Furnishings are raised from the floor or built directly into walls. Beds hang on cordage above runoff channels. Cupboards seal with waxed gaskets. Personal possessions are few, wrapped carefully, and repaired obsessively. A dry box containing letters, drawings, seed records, or a dead relative’s tools may be the most valuable object in a household.

Kinship extends through maintenance. People belong not only to families but to sections of the town: a pump line, a reservoir gate, a ventilation shaft, a root bank, a mill. Rights to occupy rooms are inseparable from duties toward the systems that keep those rooms habitable. A household that receives warm water from a steam return also cleans its strainers. Those living beneath a condensation roof clear its gutters. Neglect is not private here; it travels downhill.

Children are given small inspection tasks early. They learn to clear leaf fragments from grates, distinguish useful algae from fouling growth, and report any new trickle rather than stopping it themselves. They are taught never to pull an unfamiliar stem, never to step across an untested root bridge, and never to mistake a dry channel for an abandoned one. Their games imitate the town’s work: routing water through miniature clay channels, building tiny trellises, recognizing machines by sound alone.

Age is respected as stored practical memory. Old inhabitants know vanished routes beneath current gardens, the buried shapes of buildings, and the sequence by which water reaches the Basin after different kinds of surface rain. They remember which walls can accept another pipe bracket and which mineral crusts conceal dangerous cavities. Because maps become obsolete as roots swell and passages shift, memory remains part of the infrastructure.

Their records are made for dampness: glazed tablets, incised ceramic tags, waxed cloth rolls, stamped metal strips, and cords knotted in standardized patterns. Pipes are marked not only by color but by texture so they can be identified in darkness. Important instructions are carved into handrails where they can be read by touch. Written history is practical and local—flood heights, crop failures, pump replacements, root illnesses, relocations—yet through such records the town’s deeper history survives.

They reckon time by cycles of labor and water. A day may mean a complete sequence of shifts rather than the passage of sunlight. Larger periods are remembered by floods, major root divisions, machinery overhauls, and the opening or closure of corridors. Birthdays matter less than apprenticeship dates. A person may say they were born during the rebuilding of the east screw pump or before the old canal became reeds.

Their ceremonies are restrained, because gathering too many people in one place obstructs work and strains ventilation. Marriages or household unions are marked by joining maintenance obligations: two names added to the same section of pipe, gate, or cultivated wall. Apprenticeship ends when a worker is trusted to stop a machine alone. Death is treated without animal imagery or ideas of returning to soil untouched. Bodies are reduced through controlled composting in sealed vaults, then introduced gradually into nonfood root beds. Memorial plaques are set where the resulting growth enters the town’s structure. The dead become neither metaphorically nor invisibly present; they become measurable fertility and load-bearing wood.

Their ethics are built around consequences that travel. Water released here arrives elsewhere. A root cut in one room may loosen a ceiling two streets away. A machine run too fast may damage a crop beyond the wall. They distrust suddenness, private alterations, and unmarked repairs. Permission is less about authority than about ensuring that everyone downstream has been warned.

Even so, they are not solemn at all times. Their humor is patient and technical. They tease one another about noisy repairs, stubborn valves, overfed vines, and channels that refuse every proposed route. Musicians use tuned pipes, struck ceramic, taut plant-fiber strings, and the town’s own engines as drones. Songs are designed to fit beneath the mechanical roar, with close harmonies and repetitive rhythms that workers can maintain while their hands are occupied.

Arguments are conducted amid the same practical intimacy. Space is scarce, and every new use displaces another. A family wants a room kept dry; cultivators want its seepage. A factory needs a wider belt run; rootwrights refuse to cut the supporting growth. Waterkeepers demand that production slow before a storm’s delayed runoff arrives. Politics consists largely of deciding what may be moved, what must be endured, and which living structure has acquired the right to remain.

They rarely hurry. Running on wet stone is dangerous, rapid machinery is destructive, and an impulsive repair can be worse than a leak. Urgency appears instead as concentration: shutters closing over switchboards, crews taking assigned places, hands opening spill gates in exact order. During floods, the whole population seems to become one distributed mechanism. Some clear channels, some brace partitions, some watch gauges, some carry seedlings and dry records upward. No one commands the water to stop. They make room for it.

Seen from above, crossing root bridges through amber pools of lamplight, they can look like small components inside a vast green engine. Yet the engine is not separate from them. Their habits have trained the roots; their ancestors laid the pipes; their bodies consume the crops and return to them. They do not live beneath nature or inside a ruined town. They live in a structure whose masonry, machinery, vegetation, water, and human attention have become mutually dependent.

What lives there is therefore not simply a hidden community of people.

It is a single, crowded ecology with human hands: cautious, industrious, damp, and always listening.
