The people who live in the range are few, and none of them belong there in the effortless way of the fish.

They inhabit the maintenance station on the upper saddle, inside pressure hulls bolted to basalt. Their quarters are narrow, curved, and continually resonant. Pumps pulse through the walls. Turbines transmit a low vibration through the station’s feet. During strong tides, loose objects tremble against their restraints and the sleeping bunks hum against bone.

The crews arrive in rotations and measure their lives by maintenance cycles rather than sunrise. They wake under electric light, drink condensed water, check pressure seals, clear intake warnings, and study current forecasts that can become obsolete within minutes. Before going outside, they dress in layers of insulation and hard shells, test valves twice, then descend through a lock into water cold enough to punish any failure immediately.

Outside, they move slowly.

They work with both hands whenever possible, tethering tools and themselves to fixed lines. A dropped wrench may vanish down a valley. A loose cable can whip hard enough to break a limb. Kelp fouls grilles, wraps around sensor masts, and winds itself into turbine cages. Sand scours joints. Salt reaches every weakness. Much of the crew’s labor is repetitive: scraping growth, replacing seals, checking welds, listening for changes in bearings, and fastening back what the sea has begun to unfasten.

No repair remains finished for long.

The station workers are technicians, divers, engineers, biologists, and pilots, though these distinctions blur in a place where everyone must understand pressure, weather, machinery, and the behavior of currents. They know the saddles by temperament. One pass gives warning before it reverses; another does not. One terrace sheds stones after storms. One ravine collects torn kelp until the whole mass breaks loose and travels downslope like a slow avalanche.

They learn the local animals as neighbors of unequal familiarity. Seals wait beside the lock when divers emerge, curious about tools and bubbles. Rockfish gather in the lamps. Crabs occupy warm recesses around housings and must be brushed aside before panels can be opened. Conger eels claim abandoned structures and defend them with more conviction than the crews defend anything not vital.

The workers do not romanticize the larger animals, but they stop for whales.

A call entering the range passes through steel as well as water. It can be felt through a gloved hand resting on an exterior rail, or through the deck beneath a bunk. Sometimes several whales travel the chain together, unseen beyond the kelp and green distance. Their voices descend through the valleys, bend around summits, and return as softened echoes. For a few minutes, the entire station seems to be inside the throat of something vast.

Most inhabitants, however, are not human.

The kelp itself is the range’s principal architecture. Each plant holds to the mountain with a crowded fist of rootlike tissue, though it draws nourishment from the water rather than the stone. Its stalks rise toward light and bend beneath every tide. Whole groves lean one way, recover, then bow in the other. Their blades are torn, grazed, scarred, and continually renewed. Older stalks carry small worlds: hydroids, bryozoans, snails, juvenile crabs, eggs, worms, and films of organisms too small to distinguish with an unaided eye.

Among the upper fronds live fish that survive by becoming collective. Individually they are slight, bright-bodied animals with little defense. Together they form shifting volumes of silver. The school contracts when a seal enters the forest, divides around kelp trunks, and closes behind it. Each fish responds to its nearest neighbors, yet the result resembles a single creature turning its skin toward the light.

Rockfish keep a different order. They hover over ledges and among the vertical stems, holding position with small movements of their fins. Some are old enough to have watched entire stands of kelp vanish and return. They shelter in the same cracks season after season, leaving to feed when the currents deliver plankton and smaller prey across the slopes.

Eels occupy the interior of the mountain’s broken surface. They live in lava tubes, collapsed pockets, turbine footings, and the hollow beams of wreckage. Often only a head is visible: thick-jawed, watchful, breathing with an expression that appears threatening even when it is merely still. At night they leave their shelters and follow scent through the dark.

Crabs live almost everywhere there is bottom to cross. They climb through holdfasts, travel over sand, gather beneath carcasses, and probe newly disturbed sediment. Storms are disasters to many animals, but opportunities to them. When the currents uncover buried shell beds or break open a decaying mass of kelp, crabs arrive from every direction until the ground seems to articulate itself and move.

Seals belong to the upper forests. In open water they are swift; among kelp they become precise. They fold their bodies between stalks without touching them, roll upside down beneath the canopy, and use rock shelves as places to rest, scratch, and watch. Young seals investigate lamps, bubbles, cables, and divers. Older ones keep farther off, appearing briefly at the edge of visibility before the forest closes behind them.

Beyond the summits, whales follow the ancient volcanic chain as if it were a submerged coastline. The mountains concentrate food and give shape to otherwise open water. Some whales feed in the plankton-rich currents. Others hunt fish driven together above the ridges. They may never touch the range, yet its valleys guide their sound and its upwellings guide their journeys.

Below the kelp line, life relinquishes green.

Sponges spread over the rock in pale masses, filtering the slow traffic of the water. Cold-water corals build branching thickets without sunlight, their living polyps catching what drifts past. Basket stars fasten themselves to exposed points and open their divided arms into the current. Worms inhabit tubes made of mineral grains and secretion. Small crustaceans swarm over surfaces that appear empty until a lamp approaches.

Many deep inhabitants do not chase food. They wait where the mountain delivers it.

Detritus descends from the forests: fragments of blade, fecal pellets, molts, eggs, bodies, and organic dust. Some is consumed while falling. Some gathers in gullies. A landslide may bury a community that took decades to establish, while exposing fresh stone nearby. Colonization begins again at the margin of ruin.

In the deepest water, animals make their own brief light. A disturbance produces green-blue sparks. A swimming body leaves a fading trail. The lights are not enough to illuminate the valley; they reveal only motion—an alarm, a lure, a collision, a living thing announcing itself for less than a second and then becoming dark again.

Even the station has acquired a living skin. Algae coat its upper surfaces. Tube worms ring sheltered seams. Anemones flower from old bolt heads. Fish wait beside outflows where the water is marginally warmer. The crews scrape, poison, polish, and replace, but abandonment is always visible in advance. If they left, the intakes would clog, the turbines would seize, and the hulls would eventually fail. Kelp would pull at the towers. Corrosion would open the steel. Eels would enter first, then crabs, fish, anemones, worms, and sponges.

In time the machinery would cease to be machinery and become another reef of rounded material on the mountain.

Nothing in the range truly lives alone. The kelp requires the upwelling. The fish require the kelp. The seals require the fish. The deep corals depend on what falls from the lit water. The turbines depend on the same tides that strain their blades, and the people depend on sealed walls, maintained cables, and one another.

What lives there is not a single creature but a layered population arranged by light, pressure, current, and stone: bright schools beneath the waves, patient mouths in crevices, forests bending across ridges, filter-feeders opening in darkness, whales sounding through drowned valleys, and a handful of humans enclosed in steel, listening to the whole mountain range move around them.
