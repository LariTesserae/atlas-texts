The chamber is inhabited by a whole miniature ecology arrested at the threshold of becoming.

Its plants are pallid reeds, colorless mosses, blue-black liverworts, waxen fungi, and rare one-petaled flowers that cradle permanent drops of water. Its animals are equally strange and reduced: white crabs compressed into cracks, long-legged mites, glass-winged beetles, tiled colonies of flat snails, threadworms, densely packed minnowlike creatures, tiny heron-shaped beings, seed-sized grazers, and blind amphibians fitted perfectly into hollows in the stone.

Yet none of them live by ordinary succession. They do not grow, feed, flee, breed, sleep, or die. Their gestures persist without progressing: wings are forever humming, throats forever calling, antennae forever trembling. They are less individual creatures moving through time than living features of the chamber, as fixed to their places as mineral veins or droplets.

Something resembling intelligence may also dwell there—or may once have done so. Pebble arches, shell borders, a six-step stair, a mica enclosure, and a delicate bridge suggest builders, though no inhabitant seems capable of making or using them. Perhaps the builders are absent. Perhaps every creature collectively constitutes them. Perhaps the structures are intentions the stone formed on its own.

Ultimately, what lives beneath the slab is not merely a collection of organisms but a complete, self-filling world: an unborn cavern whose ecology, weather, architecture, noise, and motion all exist simultaneously. It admits no newcomer because it contains no emptiness. Every body occupies its exact socket; every sound inhabits its allotted air; every drop fills its ordained hollow.

The chamber itself is the true creature—bright, damp, clamorous, unfinished, and eternal—while all the beings within it are its organs.
