The inhabitants are not creatures so much as agreements between form and substance.

Twelve pale growths occupy the chamber, though counting them is difficult. What appears to be one may vanish into a seam and resume across the room as another; what appears to be several may share a single hidden continuity through the masonry. They have no central trunks, no crowns, no discernible beginnings. Each consists of flat, chalk-white ribbons laid along the architecture’s lines. At every intersection they divide cleanly, always at right angles, as if branching were an act of measurement.

Their translucent fans are neither leaves nor scales, but something between glazing and foliage. Layered in shallow niches, they catch the sourceless brightness and distribute it through themselves. No vein carries sap. Instead, hair-fine bands of opacity pass from plate to plate, resembling diagrams of circulation after circulation has become unnecessary. Their roots are endings fitted into joints. They penetrate nothing, displace nothing, and draw no nourishment in any familiar sense. Brick, mortar, crystal, and plant merely touch, and the touching is sufficient.

They do not grow because they are already the complete expression of their available space. They do not die because nothing in them is waiting to be replaced. The smallest fan beneath the central platform is no younger than the broad plates in the upper cornices. All are equally ancient, equally finished.

Yet they are not inert.

They live by correspondence. Each plant is aligned with some component of the chamber’s everlasting chord. One belongs to the high metallic filament in the inscriptions; another to the low pressure under the paving; another to the clear overtone above the crystalline basin. Their pale tissues do not visibly vibrate, but the sound passes through their pattern—or perhaps their pattern is the sound made spatial. A branch forks where a harmonic divides. A fan overlaps another where two tones coincide. Their roots cross sealed courses because resonance recognizes no obstruction.

Together they constitute the chamber’s sole faculty of perception. They do not hear change, for nothing changes. They apprehend perfect continuance. Their awareness, if awareness can exist without succession, is not a stream of impressions but one immense, unbroken recognition:

*this is fitted to this.*

Among them there may be one inhabitant less easy to see.

It occupies the basin on the central platform, though it has no body distinct from the upright crystal needles. The needles are its organs, each transparent point facing another so closely that their tips nearly meet. What lies between those points is narrower than emptiness and brighter than the chamber around it. There the clear overtone seems to gather—not louder, but more singular.

This basin-dweller has no name, face, appetite, or boundary. It may be the collective interior of the twelve growths, concentrated in the only space left comparatively open. It may be what planted them. It may be merely the chamber’s workmanship becoming so exact that exactness acquires presence.

If it thinks, its thought contains no words and requires no time. It is the thought of completion: every recess occupied without crowding, every surface worn without damage, every note sustained without effort. The blind doors and useless wells around it are not monuments made by vanished hands. They are representations of functions the inhabitant does not need—entry, depth, passage, storage. The tiny staircases portray movement for something that exists everywhere it belongs already.

Perhaps that is why no moving animal can enter. A body would bring sequence with it: arrival, disturbance, hunger, heat, departure. It would cast a shadow, however slight, and introduce the possibility that one thing might become another. The chamber permits resemblance to bodies—shells, eyes, wings, spines—but not embodiment itself. Its inhabitants have retained only those aspects of life compatible with permanence: branching, responsiveness, mutual fit, and presence.

They do not wait in the wall.

Waiting would imply a future.

They inhabit instead the completed relation among mineral, light, pattern, and tone. Break the wall, and none of them would be carried out on the fragments. Each fragment would acquire the whole arrangement inwardly, because these beings are not located where their pale fans appear. They live wherever matter has been worked past ornament into inevitability.

The chamber is their body.

The chord is their consciousness.

The twelve motionless plants are the grammar by which that consciousness knows its own perfection.
