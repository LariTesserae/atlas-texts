They live nearest the pumps, though they do not belong to them.

At first they are easily mistaken for reeds. They stand in the ankle-deep water in loose companies, each body no wider than a wrist, lifted on four or six dark legs whose backward joints rise above the surface. Their hides are gray where dry and almost perfectly black when wet. From a distance there seems to be no head—only a narrow continuation of the torso, tapering upward until the darkness takes it.

Then one turns.

The upper part unfolds into a shallow, translucent hood. Beneath it are rings of small eyes, pearl-pale and without pupils, arranged not to look outward but down: at ripples, reflections, submerged mineral seams, and the tiny pressures made by moving feet. They have poor sight in the ordinary sense. A lantern confuses them. What they perceive most clearly is displacement. To them, every animal is a moving hollow in water and air, every machine a pressure-song, every fracture a long bright wound.

Surveyors call them stillwalkers. The workers use no spoken name for them. When one must be indicated, a wrench is laid beside two nails, with the wrench pointing toward the nearest warm fissure.

Stillwalkers graze, although little vegetation passes through them. They feed chiefly on thin films that form where unlike conditions touch: warm water meeting cold stone, new basalt meeting old mud, a reflection falling half a second out of step with its source. They lower their hoods to such boundaries and remain there for hours. Fine cilia descend from the hood’s rim and comb the surface without breaking it.

Afterward the boundary is diminished. A treacherous patch becomes briefly coherent. Reflections catch up. Weight drains from a few square meters of ground. The animals do not repair the basin; they consume its smallest contradictions before those contradictions can accumulate.

Their mouths are scars along the underside of the body. They open only when pressed against something.

Because of this, they gather at leaks.

Around the ceramic fences of the eastern complex they stand shoulder to shoulder, hundreds deep, all facing inward. The oldest occupy the front ranks. Their hides there become cloudy with borrowed age: peeling in one patch, newborn and tender in another, mineralized elsewhere into sharp little plates. They feed on stray duration seeping between the fence posts. Some grow ancient from one side and embryonic from the other. When the imbalance becomes too great, they walk away in opposite directions.

Not halves of one body. Two complete animals.

One remembers what approached the leak. The other remembers what departed.

The workers collect neither, though they mark both with loops of soft wire. Over generations these divided lineages have become common enough that certain herds consist almost entirely of counterparts. They travel on parallel routes several kilometers apart, matching pace without ever meeting. When one kneels, the other lifts its head. When one is taken by a fissure eel, the distant counterpart becomes still and remains still until rain covers its back.

Their reproduction is less dramatic and more difficult to observe. During the locomotive’s nine-hour exhalation, adults enter the vapor and arrange themselves around the short-lived creatures forming there. They do not eat them. Instead, each stillwalker permits several of the vapor animals to crawl beneath its hood and vanish into the translucent tissue.

Months later, if months remain locally consecutive, the adult presses its body into warm mud. It leaves behind a row of jointed impressions. Water gathers in these prints even on a slope. Each pool develops a skin, then a pulse, then a set of folded legs. The young emerge one limb at a time over several nights. For their first year they are flat enough to live beneath the water’s surface tension, moving upside down under rain pools and feeding on the minute delay between a falling drop and its reflection.

They are preyed upon by nearly everything.

Moths spear them with hollow tongues. Silver amphibians swallow them in rows. Beetle colonies surround isolated nursery pools and drink until only dry footprints remain. The stillwalkers do not defend their young, but they alter the ground around them. Adults stand in rings and patiently consume every instability except one: the nursery itself. Predators crossing the ring may become too heavy to advance, or find that the last step repeats, or arrive beside the pool before they began approaching it. Most learn to hunt elsewhere.

The largest adults bear whole ecologies.

Their backs are shallow and warm. Beetles shelter there. Red fans take root in seams of the hide. Threadlike worms string themselves between the legs and sift the water as the animal moves. Some ancient stillwalkers carry fist-sized concretions mistaken for stones until the host bends beneath them. Inside each concretion is a curled organ that continues to beat after the animal dies.

They seldom die cleanly.

A stillwalker overcome by age or contradiction walks toward one of the fresh basalt ridges and places its hood against the advancing edge. It remains until the stone reaches it. The body is not crushed. Instead it thins, darkens, and becomes a branching flaw inside the glassy rock. For years afterward, when the ridge advances, the trapped shape advances with it.

During warm rain, these shapes can sometimes be seen walking within the stone.

There is another inhabitant, rarer and less certain.

The workers call attention to it by placing every tool upright.

It lives below the open water but above the buried factory, in whatever interval permits both descriptions to be true. No complete body has been observed. A long crease appears in the flooded plain. Beetle colonies divide around it. Eels withdraw into cracks. Pylons answer one another before it arrives, sounding their low iron notes in reverse order.

Then the crease passes beneath.

The water does not rise. It becomes deep.

For several minutes an ankle-deep sheet may possess the pressure and darkness of an abyss. Pale things ascend through it from impossible distances. The legs of stillwalkers vanish downward without shortening. Those caught directly over the passage remain standing, but their hoods flare wide and every eye closes.

What moves beneath them is inferred from contact: a warm extent; a slow articulation; surfaces like overlapping industrial doors. On two occasions, maintenance crews drove probes into its wake. The first brought up black tissue threaded with copper. The second returned as a sapling, rooted at both ends and bearing metal fruit.

After such a passage, the workers emerge from the pumping houses. They inspect bolts, listen at hatches, and arrange tools in long branching lines. Stillwalkers gather among them, touching wet coats with their hoods. For a time there is no distinction between herd and crew. Animals turn the smaller wheels. Workers stand knee-deep in pools, consuming nothing visible. Birds orbit lower.

Then the buried factory completes some fraction of its turning.

The thing below settles, or is drawn onward, or becomes machinery again.

The basin’s most numerous inhabitants are smaller than all of these: transparent motes suspended in the wet dark. They occupy air, water, mud, flesh, and the interiors of pipes. Under magnification each resembles a knotted tube surrounding an empty center. They have no organs. They live by making neighboring processes agree.

Where they swarm, rain falls downward. Heat passes from warm things into cold ones. Causes precede effects by a usable margin. A wound closes in the direction of healing. A traveler retains one location at a time.

Every creature carries them. The workers cultivate them in sweating wall tanks. Stillwalkers harbor dense colonies beneath their hoods. The pale amphibians packed into gauges are fed on them, and their body color indicates whether a machine remains inside ordinary sequence.

Without these motes, the basin would not become stranger.

It would become simultaneous.

Their population is declining.

This may be why the pumps labor harder each season. It may be why more cables disappear into the clouds, why ceramic fences multiply around the dark stains, why the workers sometimes stand together for days with tools laid in patterns too large to read from the ground.

The animals know in their various ways. Eels coil tighter in the conduits. Moths seal their switch boxes with pulp. Beetles move their colonies onto the workers’ backs. Counterpart herds draw closer along their parallel routes.

And the stillwalkers leave the warm fissures.

In immense, silent ranks, they cross the flooded terraces toward the east. Their backward-jointed legs spread no ripples. Their pale eyes watch the failing agreements beneath the water. Young cling under their bodies; gardens and colonies ride their backs. Wherever they pass, the marsh holds together for a little while.

Seen from a pumping-house roof, their procession cannot be viewed whole. It is revealed in sections by vibration, by the sudden absence of beetles from stones, by thousands of narrow bodies passing through the lantern’s short tunnel and entering darkness again.

They seem to be migrating toward the factory.

They may be gathering to repair it.

They may be gathering to feed.

Or perhaps, after an age spent grazing on the basin’s minor contradictions, they have recognized the great contradiction beneath them—the buried engine that processes duration, the machine older than its new foundations, the mechanism whose single turn keeps the lowland forever beginning.

Patiently, without cry or haste, everything that lives there is moving closer.
