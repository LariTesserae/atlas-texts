They are the Custodians, though the word in their own language once meant something nearer to *those who remain correctly placed*.

No reliable census exists. Each inhabited district reports the number assigned to it in the oldest registers, and the sums have not changed within recorded history. Births are entered as restorations. Deaths are entered as vacancies. If the two fail to correspond, clerks delay both entries until they do.

The Custodians are roughly human in outline, but resemblance is difficult to judge. Outdoors they wear layered suits of black ceramic cloth, pressure membranes, and rigid collars that conceal the head. Their masks have no transparent faceplates. Vision comes through recessed apertures and is translated into dim internal patterns, sparing their eyes from lamps and the horizon’s perpetual pallor. Their hands usually have six apparent fingers, though two are delicate tools folded alongside the living ones. Their boots distribute weight so precisely that an adult crossing dust may leave no readable track.

Uncovered, they are narrow-bodied and almost hairless. Their skin is gray-brown, dry, and faintly lustrous with mineral compounds secreted to prevent moisture loss. The rib cage is deep, the muscles economical. They breathe slowly. A resting Custodian may take only three or four breaths in an hour, storing and exchanging gases in folded organs along the spine. Much of the body’s water is bound in gels that do not readily evaporate or freeze. Blood, where it still deserves that name, moves intermittently: a dark saline medium pushed through the limbs only when work requires it.

They are warm because the plateau is warm. Away from heated stone, they become sluggish, then rigid. This is not immediately fatal. Workers trapped during conduit failures have been recovered decades later and revived beside machine rooms, sometimes resuming their final spoken sentence.

They eat little. Food is manufactured in sealed vessels from carbon, salts, cultured tissue, and carefully reclaimed fluids. Meals are dense wafers or warm black paste taken alone, because opening the mouth in company is considered intimate and faintly hazardous. Free water horrifies them. They understand it as an unstable industrial reagent: corrosive, migratory, capable of weather and life. Children learn its phase diagrams before they learn old poetry.

Their settlements are households only in an administrative sense. A dwelling may contain thirty individuals who share heat exchangers, fabrication allowances, and responsibility for a particular section of the city, yet seldom gather in one room. Kinship follows maintenance duty rather than descent. Someone who inherits your valves, legal obligations, and place on the shift ledger is your child, whether or not either of you had any part in making the other.

New Custodians are assembled slowly.

A communal chamber cultivates a body from archived cell lines, incorporating small corrections for accumulated damage. Equal mass must first be surrendered: usually the remains of a deceased resident, supplemented with stored organics and mineral stock. Nothing is spoken of as creation. The official term is *continuance*. For several years the new resident is soft, damp, and kept behind multiple seals. Childhood is regarded as a dangerous liquidity from which the person gradually hardens.

Their names are not fixed. Indoors, a Custodian may carry a private name, a work name, a legal designation, and the inherited name of an older person whose duties they assumed. Outdoors, where sound travels, they communicate through taps transmitted along walls, narrow-beam light, or pressure pulses passed from suit to suit. To call a name into the open would detach it from its proper place and allow the whole plateau to hear.

They do not know whether the plateau listens. They behave as though listening and enforcement are the same process.

Most spend their existence tending systems that need little intervention. They inspect seals, compare pressures, replace one bearing with its exact counterpart, and certify that heat has gone where heat was recorded as going. Their patience is not passivity. It is a highly developed attention to discrepancies. A different vibration in a buried turbine, a dust border displaced by a finger’s width, or a coupling note that fades a second too soon may occupy an entire guild for decades.

Innovation is not forbidden, but it incurs a debt. Any new shape, procedure, or thought must demonstrate what old thing it replaces. The two altered staircases in the eastern factory are visited by apprentices because they prove that novelty can survive if its mass and consequences are balanced exactly. Philosophers have argued for nine centuries over what was lost to compensate for them.

The city’s principal occupations are therefore not mining or manufacture, but accounting in its broadest sense. There are keepers of matter, keepers of heat, keepers of silence, keepers of profile, and keepers of names. Horizon surveyors travel to the designated stones beyond the districts and spend years measuring the silhouettes of distant structures. Acoustic clerks catalog every metallic note that crosses the plateau. The most respected specialists compare present maps with maps too old to read, searching for changes smaller than the thickness of their engraving tools.

They possess art, although much of it would look like calibration.

Music is performed through structures rather than air. Players place resonant clamps on walls and induce vibrations felt through the teeth and sternum. A composition may last generations, one tone sounded at each replacement of a particular component. Sculpture consists of internal voids whose exteriors preserve the required shape. Poems are written as inventories in which slight deviations from standard phrasing carry emotional meaning. Their greatest epics appear to be warehouse lists until one notices that the sequence of bearings describes grief.

They grieve without monuments, since a new monument would alter the skyline or redistribute mass. Instead, the dead person’s duties pass to another, along with some harmless irregularity: a preferred route, an unnecessary pause beside a furnace-glass window, a habit of touching every seventh doorway. Over centuries, these gestures become indistinguishable from ritual. The city may be full of mourning that no one remembers beginning.

The soot-colored creatures are called balance moths, though they neither fly nor resemble moths. Substation workers tolerate them because their branching dust patterns reveal charge gradients more delicately than instruments do. Some Custodians believe each creature is a loose fragment of the city’s regulatory intelligence. Others claim they are ordinary life adapted beyond recognition. Neither position accounts for their failure to breed or vanish.

A balance moth that enters a dwelling is not removed. Residents draw a line around it and reroute their movements until it leaves, even if this takes years.

The six-limbed beings at the compression yards are called standers. Little is known of them. They sometimes occupy vents assigned for maintenance and cannot be displaced without violating heat quotas, so work is postponed. Old illustrations show the same individuals in the same locations, identifiable by tears in their translucent membranes. It is possible that they are not animals but instruments whose function has been forgotten. It is equally possible that some Custodians are instruments under the same misunderstanding.

Beneath the inhabited levels live the Unentered.

This is not an official population. Maintenance crews encounter sealed doors with warmth behind them, elevators that descend after their controls end, and figures visible through furnace glass in chambers absent from every plan. The figures wear no protective suits. They are taller than present inhabitants and stand motionless around obsolete machines. Sometimes a pressure message rises through the rock in an archaic code:

**CONDITION MAINTAINED.**

No reply has ever been acknowledged.

The central square belongs to no district. Custodians enter it singly, never by procession. They stand at the prescribed distance from the pale disk and listen to the impossible surf. Since none has seen an ocean, each imagines it differently. Some think the sound is made by an immeasurable quantity of free water moving under gravity. Some believe it is the city before equilibrium, when matter still traveled without being counted. Children often imagine a vast black surface beneath a bright sky, rising and falling because it has not yet learned restraint.

The disk is attended by a small order whose members surrender their names upon appointment. They measure its temperature, record the unchanging sound, and prevent anyone from opening the shaft. Their oldest law is not engraved with the others. It survives by direct transmission from one attendant to the next:

**What is beneath must not be allowed to finish.**

Whether this refers to a machine, an organism, a geological process, or the city itself is unknown.

The Custodians do not expect salvation, invasion, discovery, or dawn. Their language has futures, but no ordinary future tense; events to come are described either as scheduled recurrences or as faults. Hope survives chiefly as precision: that the next part will fit, that the pressure will remain within tolerance, that the inherited name will be passed on without distortion.

Yet they are not without curiosity. A worker lingers at an unlabeled elevator. A surveyor wonders whether one distant tower has shifted against the dark. A child hoards a blue spark inside a sealed glass bead, though possession of unregistered light is an offense. In private chambers, people whisper the city’s outdoor name and wait to feel whether the stone responds.

And sometimes, when a balance moth reaches the pale disk and opens its conductive spines, the Custodians watching from the facades see blue light travel through the branching pattern on its back.

For that moment, the pattern resembles rivers.

No living Custodian knows what a river is. Still, something in them recognizes the shape.
