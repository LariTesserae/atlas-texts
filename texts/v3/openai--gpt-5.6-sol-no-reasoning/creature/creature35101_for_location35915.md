The people who live here are sturdy without being broad, deft rather than graceful, and seldom entirely dry. Damp has shaped them as surely as labor has. Their hands are roughened by wet rope, bark, lime, and cold stone; their nails carry clay and green stains; their boots are patched repeatedly and packed with sedge against the chill. Even children learn to place their feet on slick roots and rounded steps without looking down.

They dress in layers that can be shed, wrung out, exchanged, and dried above a hearth: coarse wool close to the body, bark-cloth or linen where it can be afforded, felted vests, short cloaks rubbed with fat or wax, and caps that shed rain toward the shoulders. Long trailing garments are impractical. Aprons have deep pockets for pegs, cord, pruning knives, chalk, bread, and whatever escaped creature must be carried home. Most adults keep a hooked stick nearby—part walking staff, part shepherd’s crook, part branch-puller, drain-clearer, and rescue tool.

No one here is simply a householder. Each person is also, in some measure, a gardener, water keeper, animal catcher, roofer, pruner, and watchman. A weaver may interrupt the loom to lift a grate before a storm. A miller knows how to graft apples. A child sent for onions may return with a duck under one arm and news that a terrace wall has begun to seep. Competence lies less in mastering one craft than in noticing what has shifted.

They read the basin continuously. They know the difference between harmless dripping and the first sound of a channel backing up behind a wall. They can tell by smell when a reed bed is failing, by the tilt of a willow screen when its roots have loosened, by the behavior of rooks when hard weather is coming over the ridge. Water rights are learned alongside kinship: who may lift which sluice, at what bell, for how long, and whose gardens must receive the overflow afterward. Children memorize the sequence by carrying carved markers from gate to gate.

Their households are seldom composed only of parents and offspring. Houses fit the available roots and walls rather than any preferred idea of family, so people arrange themselves accordingly. Grandparents sleep near warm flues; unmarried siblings share lofts; apprentices occupy platforms over workshops; widows keep rooms adjoining cousins; herders come and go with the seasons. Fosterage is common, partly from custom and partly because accidents, fevers, and difficult births leave gaps that the town closes quickly. A child may eat breakfast in one house, work under an aunt’s eye in another, and fall asleep beside a neighbor’s oven while waiting out a storm.

Privacy exists, but mostly as courtesy. Walls muffle little. Everyone hears arguments, coughing, lovemaking, spinning wheels, crying infants, and livestock shifting below the floor. People learn not to acknowledge all that they know. Curtains, lowered voices, a broom laid across a stair, or a particular knot tied at a doorway serve where doors and distance cannot.

Status comes from usefulness made dependable. The most respected inhabitants are not necessarily those with the largest herds or driest storerooms, but those whose judgment can be trusted when water rises: sluice keepers, terrace masons, midwives, root-workers, millwrights, healers, and elders who remember how the basin behaved before a channel was moved. A person who can settle a quarrel over flow without leaving either garden dry has more influence than someone rich in copper.

There are, nevertheless, poorer and wealthier households. Prosperity appears in quiet forms: a sound chimney, spare winter fodder, glazed storage jars, several good wool blankets, a roof that does not require bowls during rain. The fortunate lend pack animals, tools, and dry loft space; the unfortunate repay in labor, grazing rights, mushrooms, charcoal, or future portions of fruit. Wealth cannot isolate anyone completely. A neglected drain above a rich house is still a flood below it.

Their names cling to features of the place. Someone may be known by a spring, bridge, pollard, millrace, or roof meadow rather than by occupation. Formal names repeat across generations, so practical bynames proliferate: Reed-Hand, Three-Geese, Under-Beech, Cold-Step, Red Thread, Bent Gate. A person who moves across the basin may acquire a new name gradually, as moss acquires a wall.

Disputes are loud, public, and usually practical. Geese, branches, wandering pigs, blocked channels, shade cast on beans, and the timing of millwater cause more bitterness than abstract doctrine. Arguments are heard beneath the central willow or in the beech hall, where speakers must compete with birds overhead and water beneath the floor. Evidence may consist of old notches on a sluice post, the testimony of three neighbors, or the remembered course of a stream before a landslip. Punishments favor repair: dredging a pond, rebuilding a wall, tending another household’s reed bed, surrendering water hours, or replacing a damaged tree with several years of care. Deliberate poisoning of water, reckless fire, and secret damage to retaining roots are treated with exceptional severity.

They are affectionate in similarly practical ways. They sharpen one another’s tools, turn damp bedding, save hot stones for late arrivals, and send children with broth to anyone whose chimney has gone cold. Courtship often begins in shared work: carrying hurdles, beating flax, gathering snails after rain, or keeping night watch during floods. Lovers tie small, recognizable knots in vine passages or leave polished pebbles where a particular person will find them. Marriage joins labor and water claims as much as households. Its promises name terraces, animals, elders, unborn children, and the obligation to answer bells in darkness.

Their dead do not leave the settlement entirely. Burial ground is scarce, wet, and threaded with roots. Some bodies are laid in narrow stone chambers on the better-drained slopes; others are wrapped in wool and bark beneath orchard terraces. Bones from older graves may be gathered into clay-lined repositories to make room. Trees planted for the dead are not inviolate. In time they are pruned, pollarded, climbed, and harvested like other trees, though the family tends them and remembers whose nourishment entered their roots. Memorials are usually useful things: a step, a bridge rail, a cistern lip, a bench under cover.

Their beliefs, insofar as outsiders can separate belief from habit, are concerned with reciprocity rather than purity. Water should leave a household no fouler than necessity makes it. Fruit trees must be thanked by pruning them well. The first loaf from new grain is divided among hearth, livestock, channel, and people. Before cutting a major limb, workers touch the trunk and announce what they intend—not because they expect an answer, but because unannounced cutting is associated with haste, and haste kills trees.

They do not imagine nature as gentle. Water drowns, roots split walls, boar destroy a season’s work, damp takes the lungs, and a falling beech can erase a family. Nor do they imagine themselves separate from it. To them, the basin is not wilderness tamed into a town. It is a crowded body in which human beings have secured a precarious organ.

Children are everywhere, employed early but rarely in stillness. The smallest gather twigs, eggs, snails, and windfall fruit. Older ones carry messages along routes adults cannot easily fit through, crawl beneath platforms to clear screens, frighten birds from seedbeds, and learn which animals can be bullied and which must be coaxed. Their games imitate civic emergencies. They build miniature sluices, dam gutters, float bark boats through drains, stage rescues from imaginary floods, and hold trials over stolen water. Adults complain about these games while judging, not without pride, which child understands flow best.

The elderly remain central because the town’s knowledge is cumulative and poorly suited to writing. A channel map is useful, but it cannot show how water behaves after three days of eastern wind, or which wall hums before it slips. Old people remember buried culverts, vanished paths, trees planted as braces, and agreements made when two households were one. They sit where traffic is thickest, mending baskets or sorting herbs, apparently inattentive, and correct everyone.

Their speech is rich in direction, texture, and degrees of wetness. They distinguish water that seeps, beads, sheets, backs, scours, wanders, or rises invisibly through a floor. Directions follow flow rather than compass points: above the dye yard, below the split spring, across the old runnel, two sluices toward the pond. Time is kept by bells, chores, shadow where visible, and recurring sounds—the mill starting, geese released, evening gates closed. Appointments are approximate until water is involved. Water hours are exact.

Meals are hot, sour, smoky, and sustaining. Porridges of oats or barley appear daily, enriched with goat milk, onions, mushrooms, nettles, or rendered fat. They eat hard cheeses, eggs, beans, cabbage, apples stored wrinkled through winter, eels and pond fish, occasional pork, and small birds when permitted. Fermented foods are everywhere: pickled greens, soured milk, fruit pastes, weak ale, and sharp cider. Bread is dense and often baked with seeds or chopped herbs. Food is rarely served without some grit, leaf fragment, or inquisitive animal nearby.

Celebrations are less an escape from work than a deliberate excess of it. At midsummer, channels are cleared together, then decorated with flowers and strips of dyed wool. In autumn, livestock are counted, apples pressed, roofs inspected, and the year’s best animals garlanded before winter slaughter begins. Weddings may involve grafting two branches onto the same stock. The opening of a repaired millrace draws nearly as much festivity as a birth. Music favors instruments that survive damp: drums, reed pipes, wooden clappers, horns, and voices loud enough to carry through water noise. Dancing takes place on platforms tested beforehand by masons.

They laugh readily and without delicacy. Much of their humor concerns people falling into channels, goats appearing in forbidden places, solemn officials interrupted by poultry, and the bodily consequences of sour food. Nicknames can be merciless, but sustained cruelty is poorly tolerated; crowded life requires that mockery eventually make room for reconciliation.

Outsiders often mistake them for poor forest folk. They see mud walls, patched clothes, smoke-blackened rooms, animals underfoot, and tools made from bone and wood. They fail to see the town’s sophistication because it is distributed among thousands of small acts rather than concentrated in monuments. The inhabitants can divide a watershed among hundreds of users without emptying it, grow food on roofs that also shelter families, maintain living structures across generations, and turn erosion into power without pretending to abolish it.

They are wary of visitors who admire the greenery but ignore the rules. A guest who plucks from a trained vine, moves a board, blocks a gutter with baggage, or washes dye upstream of a drinking step quickly discovers that hospitality has boundaries. Yet travelers who listen are fed, dried, and questioned eagerly. News from beyond the ridges is valued; so are new seeds, metal scraps, songs, grafts, remedies, and stories of waterworks elsewhere.

At dusk they emerge from workshops and gardens carrying the day’s damp with them. Faces appear beneath hoods; children are counted; animals are driven toward their proper gates. Someone checks the pond level. Someone else climbs the central willow to retrieve a basket or a child. Smoke thickens under the leaves, lamps glow behind horn and oiled cloth, and wet clothes begin steaming over hearths.

Then the shower crosses the basin. No one stops for it. They lift collars, cover baskets, curse the goats, and keep moving—each person attending to some small portion of the place, while beneath their feet and houses the gathered water runs on.
