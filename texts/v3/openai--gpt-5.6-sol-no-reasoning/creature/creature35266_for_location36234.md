The people of the bowl call themselves the **Foreholders**: those who make a place ready before it is needed.

There are scarcely two hundred of them, though no census remains accurate for long. A child may move into an empty quarter to tend a spring; an old mason may leave their household and take up residence beside a wall whose growth they have watched for decades. They are counted less by dwelling than by obligation. One person belongs to the reservoir steps, another to the northern gutters, another to three unfinished houses and the moss chamber beneath them. Asked where they live, they usually name the thing they maintain.

They are small communities stretched over a settlement of impossible size.

Distance has entered their manners. They lift a hand when passing even close friends, signaling first and speaking only if both have time. Across plazas they use square flags with bold patterns: food ready, tool needed, child missing, water rising, stranger seen. Mirrors are used in clear weather, though their flashes are softened with pierced screens so they do not resemble emergency signals. At night, lantern shutters converse between isolated roofs.

Their clothing is layered wool, bark cloth, and oiled fiber, cut short enough not to drag through runnels. Most wear broad shoulder capes that shed water into gutters stitched along the hems. Hands and forearms are protected by woven guards against stone edges and wet cord. Moss-workers go barefoot in gardens to avoid crushing new shoots; builders wear sandals with replaceable wooden soles. Pale mineral dust settles into everything, so even freshly dyed garments soon take on the muted colors of the mountain.

No family possesses a house in the usual sense. It occupies several rooms, maintains several more, and adds work to others intended for unknown residents. Empty houses are swept, aired, supplied with kindling, and periodically slept in. This last duty is important. A building left entirely without human breath is thought to forget the scale of a person. Each winter, families rotate through vacant neighborhoods, lighting one hearth at a time and listening for faults in the roofs.

Their children know an enormous city in intimate fragments. They may be able to identify every dripping eave in a deserted court yet never have visited the opposite ridge. They play at diverting water with chips of bark, build miniature walls around seepages, and imitate the council’s drop-measured speeches. They are taught early not to step on pale stone that appears wet when the surrounding paving is dry. That sheen may indicate newborn rock, still yielding enough to hold a footprint for years.

The city has no single ruler. Its nearest equivalents are the **Listeners**, chosen for their ability to distinguish meaningful changes in the city’s continuous noise. A Listener can hear when a basin has filled, when a water wheel has lost a tooth, when a stone plate’s pitch has risen because its supporting wall is being taken into the mountain. They walk at night and during heavy rain, when most inhabitants remain under cover, memorizing the city by sound.

The most respected craftspeople are not architects but **accommodators**. Their work begins when a plan becomes impossible. They widen a stair around an emerging knob of stone, lift a threshold above a new seep, or remove half a roof so that condensation can continue feeding a garden. Their skill lies in preserving intention without insisting on shape. An old accommodator may point to a curved wall and explain that it has served, in order, as a granary, bridge, nursery, spillway, moth shelter, and public bench.

Surveyors form another quiet order. They carry charcoal, waxed cords, plumb bobs, and precious copper pins. Every new swelling of rock receives a screen, a date, and a set of measurements. Surveyors do not claim that the stone is alive. They object equally to anyone saying it is not. Their records extend back farther than any genealogy and show that the bowl is rising unevenly, as though something beneath it were taking slow breaths.

The oldest surveyor is said to know places where the mountain has erased doors. She can lead a visitor to what looks like an unbroken wall and indicate, by subtle changes in texture, where a family once entered its home. Such sealed rooms are never broken open. Bowls of water are left outside them. Whether this is mourning, courtesy, or fear depends on whom one asks.

Water keepers have authority to alter almost any building. If a roof starves a seep, they may dismantle it. If paving sends rain too quickly into a fissure, they may pry up the stones and plant ferns. They taste cisterns, clear leaves from open mouths, and release tiny gray frogs into newly established pools. They refuse sealed storage even during drought. Water kept from weather, they say, becomes only possession, and possession is the beginning of thirst.

The frogs are regarded as minor citizens. No one owns them, but births are recorded when tadpoles appear in a basin that held none the year before. A cistern abandoned by frogs is examined before people drink from it. Children sometimes carry them between pools in wet hands, though adults discourage this; the frogs are believed to understand the mountain’s waters better than people do and should choose their own routes.

The annual arrival of swifts briefly changes the city’s population more than any human migration. Work slows. Roof beams are inspected and, where possible, left undisturbed. Children sleep in upper galleries to hear the wings. The birds’ droppings are collected for gardens, but no nest is touched until winter. When the swifts depart, the bowl seems so empty that people spend several evenings making additional noise—striking plates, singing into wind holes, and setting every harmless water hammer in motion.

The white grazer on the northern ridge has many names and no agreed nature. Some insist it is an ordinary animal whose tracks appear strange only because so few large creatures reach the bowl. Others claim the same individual has watched the city since the first foundation trench. It is never pursued beyond the ridge. Pebble rings placed around its prints are less shrines than temporary boundaries around the unknown. While a print remains, arguments are not permitted near it.

The inhabitants have little interest in ancestry as a chain of ownership. Names on walls matter more than bloodlines. A builder’s name cut into an unfinished course may be followed decades later by those of children, adoptees, neighbors, travelers, or anyone who continues the work. Thus a building possesses a lineage even when a family does not. To add one’s name is not to claim completion, only participation.

Death is treated similarly. The dead are not buried in the shallow soil or sealed into tombs. Their bodies are carried through one of the lower fissure passages to ledges where cold descending water passes over them. Nothing recoverable is expected to remain forever. Personal tools return to workshops; garments are unraveled; names are placed on whatever work the dead left unfinished. A person is considered present while some task still bears the shape of their hands.

Birth, by contrast, is marked by opening something. A blocked runnel is cleared, a shutter is removed from an unused room, or a doorway is cut into a wall that previously had none. The child is carried through the opening before receiving a name. Names often refer to pauses, surfaces, sounds, or changes: Between-Drips, Fern-Shadow, Third-Echo, Pale-Step, Roof-After-Rain. A second name may be earned when the person assumes a civic duty.

Strangers are expected, though rarely encountered.

Every empty dwelling contains dry fuel, a coarse blanket, a vessel, and signs indicating the nearest open water. Food is not left because it would spoil, and because the city’s welcome is meant for arrivals, not vermin. Doors have latches against wind but no locks. Someone entering a prepared house is permitted to remain through a full cycle of seasons before being asked their intentions.

The Foreholders are not naïve about guests. Watchers on the ridges signal movement from below. Volcanic-glass blades are tools first but can cut flesh, and the heavy stone mauls require no adaptation at all. Yet defense is not organized around walls or gates. Instead the city can redirect its water. Lanes become slick, stairs flood, bridges are lifted, fog screens are hung, and sound devices are altered to conceal footsteps or suggest movement where none exists. The empty quarters make pursuit bewildering. Residents know which blind alleys contain low passages, which courts amplify whispers, and which apparently solid walls can be unpegged and folded inward.

Violence is rare partly because no one can imagine conquering so much vacancy. Possession loses force in a city where a thousand unclaimed rooms wait in the rain.

Their greatest disputes concern purpose. Some believe the city must continue expanding, because its incompleteness is a promise to the future. Others argue that the mountain is already revising the plan and that human work should contract into a few inhabited districts. A third group maintains that building and yielding are one practice: walls should be raised specifically so the mountain can answer them.

These disagreements unfold in the chamber of drops.

A speaker kneels beside the central bowl and offers a statement between one fall of water and the next. Long explanations must be broken into pieces. Interruption is impossible without visible discourtesy. Listeners learn to distinguish a completed thought from one merely suspended. Decisions may take days, not because the inhabitants are indecisive, but because the ceiling grants speech irregularly.

The pauses shape their thought. They distrust arguments that cannot survive silence.

There are also entities in the city that are not quite inhabitants, though the Foreholders often speak of them as if they were.

One is the waterwork itself: thousands of channels, vessels, levers, hammers, pipes, skins, and plates that answer weather with patterned sound. No one person understands the whole arrangement. Repairs alter rhythms elsewhere. A new gutter may silence a chime three streets away; clearing a pool may awaken a hammer that has not struck in a generation. Certain recurring sequences have acquired names and interpretations. The Seven Knocks announces sustained rain on the eastern ridge. The Hollow Answer means a subterranean jar is nearly full. A deep irregular booming called the Buried Walker has no known source.

Another inhabitant is the blue-black creeper on the seep walls. Each leaf folds under a sufficiently heavy drop, exposing white beneath. During a downpour, great facades flicker from dark to pale as though passing messages too quickly for human eyes. Natural philosophers track these waves and claim they reveal minute differences in the wall’s slope. Children insist the plant is applauding.

But the oldest and most pervasive resident is the mountain’s new stone.

It has no recognizable will. Its growth is too slow for appetite and too selective for simple accumulation. It rises under unused stairs but also beneath busy rooms. It seals some thresholds and shapes others. It embraces foundations, lifts paving, rounds sharp corners, and produces smooth projections where no mineral should collect. Sometimes it seems to improve a structure: buttressing a weak wall, forming a seat beside a reservoir, closing a draft. Elsewhere it is wholly inconvenient.

The Foreholders refuse to call these acts gifts or attacks. They say only that the mountain is **taking part**.

Fresh growth is screened with woven hurdles to keep feet and tools away. People visit it as they might visit a sleeping infant or an undecided council. At first it can be scratched with horn. Within a year it dulls stone blades. Over decades it acquires the pale, faintly veined hardness of the surrounding ridges. No one has seen the instant of its appearance. Even those who keep overnight vigils eventually look aside, blink, or succumb to sleep. By morning, the charcoal line no longer fits.

Some believe the almost empty city was not made for future human multitudes at all. They suggest its courts, pillars, reservoirs, and unfinished walls are scaffolding offered to the mountain so that it may learn architecture. According to this view, the people are not founders waiting for citizens. They are tutors to something beneath them—something young, immense, and exceedingly slow.

Others reverse the idea. The mountain is constructing the city, and the people are the temporary scaffolding.

Most inhabitants live without choosing between these beliefs. They clear gutters, split stone, plant damp courts, and add their names to courses begun by the dead. They prepare rooms for strangers whose faces they cannot imagine. At dusk their lamps glow far apart, each flame held in an expanse intended for thousands.

Around them, water speaks through the works. Frogs occupy open cisterns. Moss softens floors. Swifts return to beams raised by people long gone. Beneath screens in vacant plazas, pale stone swells imperceptibly upward.

Thus the place is not truly uninhabited. It is inhabited by keepers and expectations, by weather given mechanisms, by gardens admitted into public life, by absent generations with rooms already waiting, and by a mountain that has only begun to discover its own shape.
