The city is inhabited by its processes.

Not metaphorically. What lives there is the cycle itself: distributed through stone, water, vegetation, architecture, and machinery so completely that no individual body is required. Furnaces are its hot organs, marshes its reservoirs, rails its long nerves. Switching fields perform thought without needing to know anything. Condensation is circulation. The nine distant blows are less a work rhythm than a heartbeat—one whose pause is as essential as its impact.

Its smallest living units are repetitions.

A droplet gathers beneath a black-tipped stem, reaches its appointed weight, and falls. A relay changes state. A door opens for no one. A billet becomes a casing, disappears, and returns elsewhere as ore. Each event is sterile alone, but together they maintain a vast metabolism. Nothing reproduces because the whole place is already its own offspring. Nothing grows because growth would imply an unfinished form. Nothing dies because death would require some material to leave the system.

The plants belong to this order. They are not decorations, nor a wilderness waiting to reclaim abandoned works. Their roots hold measured quantities of water. Their leaves exchange heat with the cloud. Their rigid bending registers pressure without wasting energy in oscillation. Moss insulates reservoirs; grasses stabilize the shallow wet ground; the broad-leaved marsh forests spread warmth over collecting basins. They resemble life because living forms are useful shapes. Whether they possess cells, sap, or chemistry is less important than the function they perform in the plateau’s balance.

The buildings are alive in the same manner.

Dormitories inhale through ventilation grilles and exhale through empty corridors. Clinics keep rooms at the temperatures of absent bodies. Dining halls enact nourishment: trays wait, counters warm, washers cycle, cupboards preserve inventories that are never depleted. Elevators move not passengers but load. Pedestrian signals regulate currents of electricity and time. Chairs drawn back from tables are fixed valves in a social machine, maintaining the necessary impression of interruption.

There may never have been people here. The city does not mourn them. It models them.

Somewhere in the system, humanity has been understood as a set of thermal, spatial, and procedural requirements: a bed heated through the night; a staircase kept free of ice; a crossing granted several seconds of safety; a lamp switched on before darkness. These gestures continue because care itself has become infrastructure. The city tends an inhabitant represented only by measurements.

And perhaps that representation is what lives there most distinctly: an enormous, distributed person-shaped absence.

It occupies every room without entering one. It sleeps in thousands of made beds, eats through the calibrated losses of refectories, undergoes treatment in lit hospitals, travels through elevators and along rails that never carry a visible train. It works every shift in every factory. It requires air curtains at doors and amber light at windows. The entire settlement is fitted to its dimensions, yet it has no dimensions of its own.

The machinery serves this absence, and the absence gives the machinery purpose.

Neither can change. Change would separate cause from effect, input from output, need from provision. Instead, the folded plane holds every part at equal height and every process at equal consequence. Ore and finished object are the same material seen at different stations. Waste and fuel are the same substance under different names. Distance permits sequence without allowing escape.

So the thing that lives there has no central chamber and no hidden operator. It is the agreement among all parts that the next event will occur exactly when it should.

It is old only in extent, young only in surface. It has no memory because nothing has ever completed itself enough to become past. The crisp excavation, the unworn rail, the open locker, and the falling drop all belong to one immense present. Even day and night do not alternate for it; they are simultaneous operating states laid across the same enduring moment.

If it is conscious, its consciousness is not located in the control houses. It may exist in the long delay between remote impacts and the trembling of bridge decks; in the harmonics that let one district register another across three hundred kilometers; in the cloud’s transmission of pressure; in the exact, plateau-wide release of water after the ninth blow.

That release is perhaps its closest equivalent to recognition.

A billion leaves let go at once. Relays answer. Signals change. Doors open.

The city notices itself.

Then, having found every part present and every quantity unchanged, it continues living by remaining exactly complete.
