What lives here is not an animal in any ordinary sense, but a thin, distributed life that has entered into agreement with the stone.

It occupies the vent mouths first. Around each pore lies a dark skin, finer than silt and scarcely distinguishable from manganese stain. The skin is microbial: colonies of slow-growing archaea and bacteria sustained by the small chemical differences between the returning water and the oxygen-poor sea. They take no nourishment from sunlight. Instead they work upon dissolved iron, sulfur, hydrogen, and carbon compounds, extracting minute amounts of energy as those substances pass from one stable state into another.

The colonies have no clear edges. Near an active pore they form dense films within the mineral collar; farther away they loosen into separate cells lodged among carbonate crystals. Much of their substance lies below the visible surface, lining fractures too narrow for anything larger to enter. The exposed discoloration is only the outermost expression of a population extending through the basalt in branching sheets.

They live slowly because the basin permits no other pace. A cell may remain nearly inactive for long intervals, repairing itself, exchanging molecules with its neighbors, and waiting for the same faint chemical gradient that sustained its ancestors. Generations pass without any outward alteration in the colony’s shape. Where mineral deposition threatens to seal a vent, the organisms persist in microscopic pores or retreat a fraction deeper. Where dissolution opens fresh surfaces, they spread across them one cell at a time.

Different kinds inhabit different depths. Those closest to the warm return water tolerate little oxygen and feed upon compounds drawn from the buried rock. Around them live organisms that consume their by-products. At the cooler outer edge, other bacteria oxidize iron and leave rust-dark sheaths behind. Pale carbonate traps dead cells, discarded membranes, and organic residues, building them into the mineral ridges. Thus the apparent machinery and its inhabitants continually incorporate one another: stone directs the water, life alters the chemistry, and chemistry makes more stone.

The sparse algae on the southern shelf belong to another, more visible existence. Their ribbons are olive-black in the filtered light, thick-skinned and economical. Beneath the mineral dust, they photosynthesize just enough to maintain themselves. Their holdfasts grip hairline cracks, and their cells store what little nutrient reaches them. They seldom release viable growth into the basin. Most fragments fail, settling on the floor and becoming faint nourishment for the vent microbes before vanishing into the sediment.

Nothing grazes these algae. Nothing hunts among the collars. There are no eyes here, no shells, limbs, mouths, or deliberate journeys. Yet the basin is not sterile. Its inhabitants are simply smaller than the forms by which a place usually announces that it is alive.

Taken together, they form a metabolism spread across the whole depression. The algae receive the weakened day. The deep colonies receive the buried heat. Mineral-feeding microbes occupy the boundary between water and rock. Dormant cells wait in the silt, and others inhabit the old cracks beneath it. Their activity is so measured that it merges with geology: a darkening of iron, a film over carbonate, a trace of carbon carried downward, a vent kept porous slightly longer than it otherwise would be.

No single creature possesses the basin. What lives there is the system’s quiet biological margin—the nearly invisible community for which constancy is habitat, scarcity is normal, and a thousand years of apparent stillness contains innumerable acts of survival.
