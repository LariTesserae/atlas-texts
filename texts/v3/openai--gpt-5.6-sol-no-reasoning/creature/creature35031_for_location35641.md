The inhabitants of the abyssal plain are creatures of patience, thrift, and burial.

First there are the multitudes too small to be seen: bacteria and archaea inhabiting the upper sediment grain by grain. They live in films on stone, in the porous remains of shells, and in water trapped between particles of clay. Near the surface they consume the last usable traces of matter fallen from above. Deeper down, where oxygen has been spent, other communities take over, drawing energy from nitrate, sulfate, methane, iron, and manganese. Their provinces are stacked invisibly beneath the seabed, each defined by what chemistry remains possible there. They grow slowly, divide rarely, and may persist on nourishment so scarce that a richer organism would not recognize it as food.

On exposed rock these microbes form thin, nearly colorless skins. Some encourage minerals to precipitate around them. Over immense spans, their work helps darken stones with manganese and iron. The crust advances less like flesh than geology: layer after microscopic layer, interrupted whenever gravel scours the surface clean.

Larger lives depend on what descends from the illuminated sea. Dead plankton, fecal pellets, scraps of tissue, fragments of wood, and occasional carcasses make up a faint, continuous fall. Most arrivals are meager. A large corpse is therefore not merely a meal but a temporary country.

When one reaches the bottom, scavengers appear from distances that had seemed empty. Amphipods gather first, pale and quick, their shells thin in the calcium-poor water. Isopods and squat lobsters follow. Hagfish may knot and twist against the softening flesh. Sleeper sharks or other deep-ranging fish circle more slowly, tearing away larger portions. For a time the seabed becomes crowded. Then the feast contracts: flesh to scraps, scraps to enriched sediment, enriched sediment to a chemical stain worked over by worms and microbes. Bones may support their own community until they too are buried, dissolved, or exposed to some later current.

Between such windfalls, the animals live economically.

Small amphipods hurry over the mud in abrupt starts, testing fragments with sensitive antennae. Long-legged crabs stand above the soft floor and pick delicately among shells and pellets, lifting each possible morsel toward the mouth. Sea cucumbers move with greater deliberation, swallowing sediment and extracting its faint organic content. Behind them lie ropes and coils of cleaner mud. Brittle stars extend narrow arms into the current or creep across the bottom, gathering particles with tube feet. Small snails and bivalves persist where carbonate has not entirely dissolved.

Most of the population remains concealed. Threadlike nematodes occupy the watery spaces between grains. Segmented worms tunnel beneath the surface, feeding as they go. Some maintain paired openings to the water above; others leave branching galleries, mounds, pits, and compact spirals of cast sediment. Tiny crustaceans browse within these burrows. The apparent ground is therefore a crowded, porous habitat—a layered city whose architecture is continually collapsed by currents and rebuilt by instinct.

Where stone interrupts the mud, suspension feeders settle. Glass sponges raise latticed bodies into the current, their skeletons delicate but durable. Other sponges spread as low gray crusts where the flow is too abrasive for height. Pale anemones fasten themselves in cracks and unfold translucent tentacles. Sea lilies, hydroids, bryozoans, and slow-growing corals may branch like plants, though no plant can live there. They face whatever direction carries edible particles. When the current strengthens, whole colonies bend together; when sediment thickens, polyps close and wait.

These fixed animals become habitat in turn. Amphipods shelter among their branches. Worms bore into dead portions. Brittle stars climb them to feed higher in the water. Eggs and larvae adhere to surfaces that may have taken centuries to grow. A passing sediment flow can bury the entire assembly in minutes.

The fishes are soft, dark, and sparing of motion. Some hover just above the bottom, held in place by slight movements of fin and tail. Others rest against the sediment until pressure, scent, or vibration announces prey. Their bones and muscles are light. Their mouths are often disproportionately large, because the next meal cannot be predicted and little that can be swallowed may safely be rejected. Vision ranges from enlarged, light-sensitive eyes to eyes reduced almost beyond use. Touch, smell, and the detection of minute pressure changes matter more.

A fish that carries its own light briefly alters the plain. Blue organs flare along its body or beneath its eyes. Shrimp and small fish vanish into the mud. An anemone contracts. Then the hunter passes, its narrow beam absorbed within a short distance, and darkness closes without afterglow.

Rarer animals cross rather than inhabit the plain: deep-diving sharks, broad-headed rays, wandering cephalopods, and other visitors following scent or current. They are sensed before they are seen. Buried worms withdraw. Crustaceans flatten themselves against stone. The sediment records a fin-tip, a drag mark, or a row of impressions, but only until the next settling of clay.

All these lives are adapted not only to darkness and cold, but to interruption. A dense bottom current arrives like weather. Crawlers grip the ground or are rolled away. Suspension feeders fold shut. Burrowers descend if there is time. Some animals are smothered beneath the new deposit; others survive by climbing upward through it. A slide may transport an entire community together—living, dead, and mineral—then leave its members stranded miles away in unfamiliar sediment.

Recovery begins almost at once. Scavengers investigate the displaced bodies. Worms penetrate the fresh layer. Microbes occupy newly exposed minerals. Larvae descending from the water column settle on any hard surface left uncovered. Tracks accumulate over the smooth deposit until another flow erases them.

Nothing here dominates for long except the sediment itself. Every inhabitant is also a disturbance, mixing old material with new, carrying oxygen downward, bringing buried chemicals upward, converting bodies into mud and mud into bodies. The plain is not barren. It is inhabited at the tempo permitted by cold, pressure, and famine: a world of minute appetites, long pauses, sudden burials, and lives arranged around whatever the upper sea relinquishes.
