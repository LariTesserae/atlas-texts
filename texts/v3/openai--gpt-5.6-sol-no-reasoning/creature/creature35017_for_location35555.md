The most characteristic inhabitants are not the fish, though fish are everywhere. They are the crews who keep the sea from becoming a different sea.

They live inside the factories, in dry pressure chambers nested among tanks and turbines. Their quarters are compact and heavily partitioned: bunks, kitchens, clinics, workshops, control rooms, all joined by round-doored corridors whose walls sweat at the seams. Nothing loose is left unsecured. Cups fit into recesses. Chairs lock to the floor. Every room carries gauges for pressure, oxygen, temperature, and hull strain. The inhabitants learn to read these at a glance, as casually as someone elsewhere might look through a window.

Most rarely see open air. Their outdoors is water.

They leave through flooded locks in armored suits or compact maintenance vehicles, descending onto lit roads that cross the plating. At a distance they resemble slow, brightly colored crustaceans. They walk with magnetic soles where currents are strong, hauling themselves along rails when the pumps change state. Tools hang from their harnesses on retracting lines. Each helmet bears lamps, cameras, and identification marks broad enough to be read through silt.

Their work is continual and largely corrective. They clear mussels from sensors, replace failed lamps, seal nutrient lines, inspect welds, release animals trapped against screens, and cut kelp away from moving assemblies. They enter abandoned conduits where eels have settled and excavate drainage slots claimed by crabs. Before closing a valve, they check not only what machinery depends on it but what has begun living downstream.

They are not custodians of nature in any simple sense. They kill freely when operation requires it. Intake tunnels are flushed. Accidental reefs are scraped down to metal. Whole colonies vanish beneath solvent, heat, or dredging blades. Yet the crews know these colonies intimately. A broken exchanger may be identified by the ray that sleeps above it. A pipe junction may be called the red-anemone crossing long after the anemones have been removed. Official maps show pressure sectors and service routes; working maps accumulate shark grounds, spawning clouds, eel ducts, and places where shrimp turn the water white at dimming.

Some inhabitants never leave the dry interior. Dispatchers manage currents from rooms filled with basin diagrams. Lamp operators conduct dawn across hundreds of kilometers, bringing each bank up in sequence. Ecological technicians monitor oxygen, bacterial load, and migrations because a bloom can choke a factory as effectively as mechanical debris. Dredge controllers redraw sediment fields. Traffic coordinators guide cargo submersibles through waters shared with tuna and rays.

They understand the ocean as a set of conditions rather than scenery. A warm current is a reactor’s surplus. A season is a maintenance cycle. Darkness is a grid fault moving west. Storms can be predicted from pump schedules, but never perfectly, because water amplifies small errors and living things gather where models expect emptiness.

Children, if they are present, grow up with peculiar certainties. They know that daylight has circuitry. They know the floor can be opened. They distinguish distant machinery by sound: the low pulse of circulation pumps, the finer vibration of rail traffic, the sudden concussion of a pressure gate. Open expanses do not frighten them as much as silence. Silence means that something vast has stopped.

Other inhabitants are less recognizably human in their habits, though none need be strange in body.

Maintenance machines range over the basin in patient populations. Dredgers crawl on broad tracks, nosing through sediment. Inspection drones move in shoals along weld lines, their lights blinking as they exchange position and data. Long-bodied pipe crawlers vanish into apertures too narrow for crews. Welding vehicles cling to seams and trail momentary suns. They are not animals, but from a distance their repeated routes resemble grazing, nesting, or migration.

The sea’s actual animals have organized themselves around these routines.

Small silver fish live by the lamp towers, feeding on films and drifting scraps. Their schools tighten at alarms and spread again once a noise becomes steady. Shrimp hide in shadowed recesses during the bright work cycle, then rise in clouds as each bank dims. Mussels establish dense, layered settlements in every protected bolt well. Crabs patrol gutters and drainage slots, losing claws to closing grilles and returning whenever the mechanisms reopen.

Eels occupy the machine’s unused possibilities. They live in dead conduits, spare cable sleeves, cracked housings, and narrow gaps beneath access covers. Some remain so long that crews mark the openings rather than evict them. When flow begins unexpectedly, an entire local population may emerge at once, streaming over the plated plain in search of another hollow.

Larger fish belong to the moving infrastructure. Tuna ride circulation loops that may carry them around the basin. Rays follow buried heat lines with the fidelity of animals following coasts or magnetic fields. Sharks wait where exclusion screens bewilder smaller fish, learning the timing of intakes and shift changes. Their territories are made of vibration, temperature, and schedule rather than rock.

No creature wholly understands the place that sustains it. Fish treat lamps as day, pipes as reef, and pump-driven water as current. Workers treat vast migrations as loading on filters and fluctuations in sensor readings. Machines register both workers and animals as obstructions, thermal signatures, or authorized targets. Each inhabits a different version of the same constructed depth.

What lives there, then, is a society of maintenance and opportunism.

The crews preserve the machine because it preserves their world. The animals exploit the machine because there is no other world beneath it. Bacteria arrive first on each clean plate, followed by algae, shells, grazers, hunters, inspectors, and finally the scraping tools that return the surface to bare manufacture. Then the succession begins again.

Nothing is native in the old sense. Nothing is invasive either. There is nowhere outside the construction from which belonging could be measured.

The inhabitants belong by endurance: by learning the lamp cycle, tolerating the turbines, finding warmth in conduits, shelter in fastenings, food in leaks, and warning in the changing note of pumps. They live not despite the artificial ocean but through its operation, adapting to every repair until maintenance itself becomes an ecological force.

And throughout the basin, wherever white light reaches the steel floor, life waits for the machine to make another mistake.
