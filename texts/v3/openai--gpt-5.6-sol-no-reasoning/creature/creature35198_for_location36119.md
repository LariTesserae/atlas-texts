The highlands are inhabited less by fixed species than by lineages capable of surviving revision. Everything that lives there can endure displacement, inversion, burial, sudden abundance, and the quiet betrayal of distance. A creature adapted only to a place would soon lose it. The successful inhabitants are adapted instead to processes: condensation, mineral flow, pressure, rotation, and the brief correspondences between one landscape and another.

Among the bare lava fields live **stone-drinkers**, compact six-legged animals covered in dark velvet fibers. At rest they resemble patches of soot caught in shallow depressions. Their feet spread into porous pads that seal against rock, drawing moisture and dissolved salts from microscopic fractures. Near dusk they climb onto exposed slabs and arrange themselves along condensation lines. Droplets collect on their backs, creep through the fur, and disappear beneath overlapping plates along the spine.

Stone-drinkers have no permanent mouth. When food is plentiful, a seam opens anywhere along the underside and extrudes a ring of rasping tissue. When threatened, this closes without a scar. They graze on mineral films, fungal dust, and the soft surfaces of young basalt. Over generations, colonies inscribe broad dark paths across the rock—not roads, though they can look like roads from a height. If the ground begins to turn, they lock their legs together and become a living mat. Whole colonies may be carried intact into a new valley, where they wake beneath an unfamiliar gravity and begin drinking again.

The moss forests support small herds of **foldbacks**. These low, heavy grazers have flexible skeletons composed of hundreds of interlocking mineral wafers. When a plateau shift approaches, the wafers release from one another. The animal collapses gently, ribs and limbs spreading outward until its profile becomes almost flat. Moss grows across its dormant hide. Loose stones collect in the hollows of its body. Predators walk over it without recognizing meat.

Hours or years later, when the pressure in the ground has passed, the foldback draws itself together. Often it rises with part of the landscape still attached: ferns rooted between its shoulder plates, fungal towers on its haunches, a pool of rainwater cupped along its spine. Older animals carry mobile gardens. Shoals of tiny swimmers breed in them, and glass-winged insects follow their migrations to feed on the flowers growing from their backs.

Foldbacks do not navigate by sight. The crowns of their heads bear fans of metallic cartilage sensitive to buried current and moving ore. Herds follow these flows through darkness, fog, and altered terrain. Their routes may appear erratic on the surface, but below ground they trace the branching circulatory system of the highlands. At a rich seam, the herd kneels and presses its headplates to the stone. A low thermal blush passes from animal to animal—one of their few social signals.

They are hunted by **seam-runners**, long-bodied predators whose asymmetrical limbs are suited to places where gravity leans. A seam-runner may have twelve legs along one side and only three along the other, though the difference changes after molting. Its body hangs across competing downward directions like a bridge. Rather than pursuing prey, it waits where gravity collects loose matter. Pebbles, leaves, water, and eventually animals drift toward it.

Its jaws are delicate. It feeds by laying translucent membranes over its prey and allowing local weight to press the flesh through them. The process is nearly silent and may take several days. What remains is not a carcass but a flattened mineral likeness embedded in the ground. Many apparent fossils in the highlands are seam-runner meals only hours old.

Above the ravines drift the **spore shoals**. Each animal is little more than a warm bladder around a digestive thread, with long feathered vanes for steering. They resemble seedpods because plants and animals here have repeatedly borrowed the same successful shapes from one another. In moist air, thousands float together, turning as one body through the brown cloud. Their vanes sift spores, pollen, fungal metal, and minute airborne eggs. Different substances make them differently buoyant, so a shoal separates into colored layers after feeding.

When the air dries, they descend onto cliff forests. Some hang harmlessly from vines. Others bury their vanes in bark and become, for a season, reproductive organs of the trees. They carry sap, exchange genetic material, and inflate again when the forest releases them. No clear boundary exists between the animal and the grove during this interval. A spore-shoal individual may rise bearing leaves, roots, or several newly grown eyes patterned after knots in the wood.

Larger pockets of dense atmosphere contain **cloud swimmers**: pale, ribbon-bodied animals that never descend to the ground. Their fins beat slowly against fog thickened by heat and volcanic gas. The largest are longer than a ravine is wide. Mineral grains accumulate in their tissues until their bones conduct electricity, and during storms their skeletons become visible through the skin as branching blue frameworks.

Cloud swimmers feed around vent mouths, filtering heat-loving microorganisms from each exhalation. They know a mountain by its breath. When vents close, they circle in tightening spirals until rising pressure opens another passage. Sometimes they follow an exhalation through the mountain and emerge hundreds of kilometers away, carrying parasites and seeds between valleys that have no surface connection.

They reproduce during the synchronized upheavals. Adults enter descending cloud spirals and shed portions of their conductive skeletons. Lightning joins the fragments into loose frameworks, around which new flesh condenses. For several minutes the young exist only as luminous diagrams inside the fog. Then they become heavy enough to swim.

The cliff forests are not entirely stationary. Their black-barked trees interlace roots into broad, flexible floors, trapping moss, soil, and colonies of smaller organisms. When condensation shifts to another rock seam, the entire canopy can detach. Gas bladders swell beneath the root mat while vines contract along the cliff face. Over weeks, the forest crawls toward moisture. On steep slopes it may migrate vertically; across thin regions it sometimes crosses open air.

These traveling groves are inhabited by **hinge apes**, though they are neither true apes nor shaped consistently enough to deserve the name. They have four grasping limbs when the forest lies flat, six when it climbs, and no discernible limbs while passing through a geographical fold. Their bodies reorganize around whatever branch network supports them. A hinge ape separated from trees becomes a confused bundle of joints and eyes, capable of living but unable to decide on an anatomy.

They tend the traveling forest without building anything. They strip diseased bark, distribute luminous fungi, and chew channels that guide condensation toward the roots. They make no tools because their teeth, fingertips, and temporary bones assume the required forms. Their social lives are expressed in warmth. Family groups sleep pressed together, exchanging slow patterns of heat through the canopy. From a distance these conversations appear as faint blooms of infrared color moving from tree to tree.

Some hinge apes remember forests that have vanished below rotating plateaus. Their memory is not held entirely in the brain. They scar branches in complex chemical patterns, cultivate particular fungal threads, and teach young trees to grow in the shapes of lost terrain. A very old grove can contain a living map of valleys that no longer exist—or have not yet opened.

Near the temporary pools live **glass broods**, translucent swimmers with bodies assembled in concentric rings. They hatch whenever mineral-rich water enters a hollow. Their eggs may have waited in dry stone for millennia, or may have arrived as fossils from the opposite side of the basin. A brood develops quickly: algae eaters within minutes, hunters within hours, reproductive adults before the pool begins to drain.

As water diminishes, they crowd into deeper rings and dissolve into one another. By the end, thousands of animals have become a single clear mass at the bottom of the hollow. This mass mineralizes into what looks like a cloudy lens. When water returns, it separates again—sometimes into the same species, sometimes into forms appropriate to the conditions of a different age.

Around the pools grow scarlet mosses inhabited by **pulse mites**. These tiny animals store electrical charge gathered from fungal roots. At intervals they discharge together, sending a dim red wave across the moss. The wave coordinates flowering, warns of predators, and briefly stuns the glass broods at the water’s edge. Valleys can communicate through linked moss beds. During electrical weather, messages travel for hundreds of kilometers, though it is unclear whether the content belongs to the mites, the moss, the fungi, or the highlands themselves.

Beneath everything spread the **metallic fungal nations**. Their visible fruiting bodies are small—silver fans beneath stones, iron-black threads in wet cracks—but their buried root mats extend across entire watersheds. They consume oxidized ore and extrude purified filaments, building conductive lattices through the rock. These networks carry lightning, chemical signals, heat, and slow mechanical stresses.

They are alive at a pace difficult for animals to perceive. A fungal decision may take centuries. A fruiting response may happen fifty generations after the stimulus. Yet when buried systems synchronize, the networks act with startling speed, routing current into dormant strata and illuminating the mountain’s interior.

Many animals depend on them. Foldbacks lick exposed filaments for iron. Cloud swimmers follow their electrical fields. Stone-drinkers graze their youngest growth. Seam-runners lay eggs in warm nodes. The fungi, in turn, use animal migrations to bridge breaks in the network. Conductive spores cling to feet, fur, intestines, and bones. Every herd is also a moving circuit.

In the thin regions live organisms for which a single body is insufficient.

The most common are **alternates**, deer-sized browsers glimpsed in several adjacent positions at once. Each contour is incomplete: one may contain the front legs, another the digestive organs, a third the animal’s shadow. They remain healthy only while all versions stay near one another. When threatened, the contours scatter through different possible routes. Later they reunite, sometimes incorporating a version that escaped a danger the others suffered. Thus an alternate may bear a healed scar from an attack that, locally, never happened.

They feed on ridges that have not settled on a material. One contour grazes mineral lichens, another bites soft basalt while it is briefly flesh, and another consumes the potential leaves of trees that might grow there. Their droppings are notoriously various: dung, gravel, seeds, small bones, or warm cavities in the air.

Their predators are the **unmade**, which cannot be viewed directly because observation forces them into harmless anatomies. They hunt through expectation. An animal that becomes certain it is being followed may begin finding bites in its tracks, blood on the path ahead, or the skeleton of itself lying around the next turn. The safest prey animals possess weak continuity of mind. Stone-drinkers frequently forget where their bodies end. Foldbacks interrupt consciousness when flattening. Hinge apes distribute fear into trees.

One larger inhabitant traverses the whole range: the **horizon beast**. It is rarely identified as an animal. From the lowlands it appears to be a dark section of the highland wall, perhaps a plateau slightly higher than those around it. Within the mountains, it manifests as a sequence of unrelated features—a warm escarpment, a line of vents, several valleys whose rivers pulse at the same interval.

Its tissues are rock, forest, fungal lattice, and compressed gas. It may be one organism or a migratory stage through which portions of the landscape periodically pass. Over decades, its apparent ridges contract. Basalt columns descend beneath it like muscles. Gas chambers fill and empty in coordinated breaths. Eventually it advances, not by moving across the ground but by persuading adjacent country to become part of its body while its far side returns to ordinary geology.

Animals respond long before it arrives. Foldback herds kneel in converging lines. Cloud swimmers gather above its vents. Metallic fungi brighten beneath its path. Traveling forests turn their growing edges toward it. When it passes, species are redistributed, valleys exchange ages, and the distinction between migration and mountain-building becomes meaningless.

There may also be intelligences in the highlands, but none announce themselves with artifacts.

Certain vent systems breathe in mathematical sequences. Some traveling forests repeatedly arrange themselves into branching forms visible only during electrical illumination. Herds occasionally gather around blank cliff faces and wait for decades, departing moments before the stone rotates open. Echoes without sound sometimes repeat not the movements that just occurred, but corrective versions: a foot placed more safely, a head turned toward an unseen predator, a flock choosing the valley that remains open.

Whether these are messages, instincts, or mere consequences of unstable reality is impossible to determine. In the highlands, intelligence need not be contained in a skull. It can occur between a fungus and a thunderstorm, between a herd and a mineral current, between two forests that meet for one afternoon after ten thousand years apart.

Nothing living there calls the country permanent.

At dawn, after a continental synchronization, the inhabitants emerge without surprise. Stone-drinkers taste the fresh cliffs. Foldbacks lift beneath gardens damp with unfamiliar mist. Shoals descend to trees that surfaced during the night. Alternates collect their scattered bodies from several possible mornings. Deep below them, the fungal lattices cool and darken, carrying the last charges toward a mountain not yet risen.

Then the highlands become quiet again, and all their creatures resume the patient work of inhabiting a world still deciding what it is.
