The valley is inhabited chiefly by animals made for thin air, cold ground, and enormous distances.

Dark, shaggy ungulates move across the gravel fans in extended herds, traveling between mineral licks, geothermal marshes, and the scattered sedge beds. They are heavy enough to dislodge loose stone yet, seen from the surrounding heights, become no more than a narrow black current. Smaller grazers live closer to the ground among talus and frost-heaved blocks. They feed on cushion plants, moss, and short herbs, retreating into burrows whose entrances steam on cold mornings.

The lake supports the most conspicuous population. Geese, cranes, and high-altitude ducks crowd its open margins, especially where warm springs prevent the water from sealing over. They nest on moss rafts and muddy islands, forage through the brief thaw, and rise in clamorous flocks when disturbed. Above them, raptors use the mountain updrafts, hanging almost motionless beneath the clouds before folding into sudden descents.

Elusive ground predators follow the herds and smaller grazers. They leave broad pawprints and tail marks beside the springs, shelter in geothermal cavities, and move mainly through fog, dusk, and broken terrain. Scavengers watch the cliffs and crossings, finding animals killed by falls, exposure, or shifting rock. During summer, dense swarms of insects gather above the wet ground and provide another concentrated source of food.

The valley’s smallest inhabitants are also its first settlers. Microbes live in mineral water, wet gravel, and heated rock; some endure sulfurous springs and extreme changes in temperature. Lichens spread slowly across stable stone. Mosses occupy warm seeps, while cushion plants and pale sedges take hold where thin sediment has accumulated. These organisms make the first traces of soil on newly exposed ground.

Nothing lives everywhere. Life is compressed into cracks, ledges, burrows, marsh edges, open water, and warm faults. A single spring may sustain plants, insects, nesting birds, grazers, and the predators that attend them, while kilometers of nearby gravel remain almost bare. The valley’s inhabitants form temporary communities along seams of heat and water, repeatedly displaced as springs migrate, fissures open, marshes drain, and rockfalls cover living ground. They persist not by mastering the basin, but by continually finding its newest habitable margins.
