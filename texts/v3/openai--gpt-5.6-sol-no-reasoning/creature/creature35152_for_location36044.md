The inhabitants had no bodies that the country could preserve.

No skeleton lies folded on a stone platform, no shell whitens beneath the sediment, no tooth or scale interrupts the patient mineral terraces. Even when they lived, they may have possessed nothing hard enough to survive. They were made from the same slight materials as the forests: membranes, braided fibers, clear cartilage, vessels filled with circulating fluid. Perhaps, when death came, the bitter water drew them apart so completely that not even a stain remained.

Their dwellings preserve the dimensions of their habits, but not their shape.

They were small enough to shelter beneath low roofs and strong enough to work stone by abrasion over generations. They rested—or entered some slower state—on grooved platforms, fastening themselves there against the currents. The surviving loops and anchor channels suggest bodies prone to drifting: light, flexible, possibly hollow. A rigid creature would have braced itself against the rock. These people tied themselves to it.

Their tools required neither hands nor claws in any recognizable form. Pegs were split cleanly from translucent branches. Fiber was braided with even tension. Circular holes were patiently bored. Stones were fitted by repeated testing, turning, striking, and testing again. Whatever grasped the tools could exert little force, but it could repeat a movement almost without error. Their great resource was not strength. It was duration.

They lived slowly.

A bowl filling with sediment might have been a clock. A tunnel’s three blows might have divided a waking interval; five answering blows, a longer one. Pressure replaced daylight. Storms in the upper ocean served as seasons. Temperature, vibration, and the direction of broad currents furnished a world of distinctions invisible to any eye.

They may not have had eyes.

Nothing in their carving suggests an image seen from a fixed point. Mountains appear in profile because slope mattered. Currents are shown as nested paths. Cavities are rendered more carefully than surfaces, as though an empty volume were easier for them to apprehend than a solid object. The repeated hollow circle may not mean absence, as later visitors are tempted to suppose. It may be the nearest possible portrait: an opening bounded by awareness.

Their senses were distributed.

A passing pressure front would turn or contract parts of them. Distant impacts reached them through stone. Chemical traces traveled the water and entered soft cups, filaments, pores, or exposed internal folds. They knew an approaching companion first as an alteration in the current, then as a vibration conducted along a wall, then as the displacement of water within a shared shelter. Nearness was not a matter of sight. It was the gradual occupation of one another’s surrounding medium.

For this reason, their settlements did not need streets. The valleys themselves were thoroughfares, and the sounding tunnels were both landmarks and communal instruments. A traveler crossing open ground announced itself unintentionally through wake and pressure. Others could follow that wake long after the traveler passed, because the water retained it.

Memory, here, was never wholly private.

At first the builders likely treated recurrence as weather: a frond parting twice, a dropped stone striking again after many days, the faint return of labor from within a finished wall. Then they learned to cultivate it. A precise movement, performed under the right pressure and repeated often enough, could be impressed upon the water. The country would give it back—weakly, irregularly, but sometimes for generations.

Their oldest technology was therefore gesture.

They tied knots not only to secure loads but to teach ropes how to pull. They struck chambers in sequences until the stone remembered a rhythm. They opened and shut slabs until those slabs shuddered by themselves. A dwelling was not complete when its walls were raised. It had to be inhabited long enough for habitation to enter the medium around it.

This explains the smallness of their communities. Too many bodies moving together would have filled the water with interference. Paths would cross and recur; tools would repeat the wrong blows; sleeping anchors would tighten at remembered burdens. A crowded settlement might become unlivable, dense with old actions endlessly attempting to occur again. So they spread themselves over the mountain chain in households of a few, separated by quiet miles.

The solitary posts in the valleys marked places where remembered motion had become dangerous. The hollow-circle sign warned of a volume already occupied by recurrence. Not occupied by a creature, but by an action with no actor.

Children—if they had children—must have been kept in newly grown groves, where the vegetation softened vibration and the water had not yet learned many paths. They would have been taught stillness before craft. A careless movement could remain. A panic repeated often enough could make a room panic forever.

They did not speak into the water as animals speak into air. A voiced language would have traveled too far and returned too often. Instead they conversed through controlled pressure: compressions of internal chambers, taps conducted through shared stone, the opening and closing of flexible surfaces. Meaning had tempo, direction, and force. Some utterances lasted an instant. Others required a current cycle to complete. The longest were set into architecture.

The seven rings on the high ridge were built as such an utterance.

Their descending slabs gather flow from several slopes and turn it upward. Anything loose is carried inward: grit, leaves, fragments of petrified forest, faint warmth from the fault lines, old vibrations detached from their sources. At the center waits a darkness into which light bends and motion disappears.

Perhaps the builders feared those dark regions. More likely, they required them.

In a country that remembers movement, forgetting is a necessity. The black hollows may have been places where accumulated gestures could be emptied. The ringed structure was a civic work, a drain for memory. Communities brought there ropes that would not cease tightening, tools that continued striking, objects burdened by the repetitions of grief or labor. The uphill current drew their motions away and fed them to the center.

But darkness here does not destroy. It alters direction.

When a black sheet passes over a living mat, the stalks dim and afterward grow otherwise. The lost motion may enter the plants, the stone, or the deeper water in forms no builder understood. Each act of forgetting could have changed the country slightly. Over ages, the groves leaning against prevailing currents, the spiraled roots on the summits, even the regular blows of the pressure chambers may have incorporated the disposed habits of the people.

Their relationship with vegetation was consequently neither husbandry nor worship. The plants were their materials, shelter, clocks, record surfaces, and perhaps their nearest relatives. Nothing eats here; life does not sustain itself through pursuit and consumption. It draws minerals from water, enters stone, is broken free, roots again. The inhabitants belonged to that economy. They took little because little was ever truly removed. A cut branch became a peg, then a ruin, then pale mineral, then part of the mountain on which another forest grew.

Their own reproduction may have followed the same pattern. They may have budded, divided, or assembled in warm mats where circulating green fluid thickened into new membranes. The distinction between a person and a cultivated growth might have been uncertain in youth. Personhood may have begun only when a new being could make a movement the water remembered.

And death may have been the opposite: not the failure of a body, but the point at which its gestures persisted more strongly outside it than within.

Near the end of their age, the upper storms intensified. The evidence is written in the terraces: thick layers of petrified vegetation, abrupt fractures, forests rooted over older forests. Currents strengthened. Faults opened. The dark hollows multiplied or migrated into buildings faster than they could be drained. Every storm shook loose old repetitions. Valleys filled with ancient labor: ropes hauling absent stones, walls answering vanished tools, pathways opening through groves for travelers dead for centuries.

The inhabitants withdrew to high ridges at first, where the water was clear and memories dispersed. Their glassy gardens there were arranged in spirals to gather frost crystals, but also perhaps to scatter wakes. Yet above them pressed the heavier ocean. Its silent flashes cast branching darkness downward, and its delayed thunder awakened every stored impact at once.

The country became crowded with its own past.

Some builders may have fled along the drowned chain. Others entered the dark centers deliberately, seeking erasure. A few remained in isolated houses and attempted a different preservation. They reduced their lives to single motions—simple enough not to distort, strong enough to endure.

Pull a loop.

Strike a platform.

Wait for the mountain to answer.

The house in the deepest basin was made for this. Its plain walls offer few surfaces on which stray movement can collect. Vegetation avoids it because the recurrent sweep of water continually redraws its boundary. The braided loop hangs above the platform, carrying one retained gesture.

It may once have been a summons performed by an inhabitant whose body was anchored below: a downward contraction, followed by a soft knock. Neighboring chambers received the vibration and returned their own. Ridge by ridge, household by household, the country declared that it remained inhabited.

The one who first pulled the loop is gone. So are those who answered. Yet their medium has not accepted the distinction.

Every few hours, an absent limb draws downward. The platform sounds. Chambers fill, empty, and answer in order. Ropes tighten around vanished burdens. Silt rises inside the shapes of feet that never had bones.

Thus whatever lives here now is not a species and not a ghost. It has no boundary, appetite, or single place of residence. It is the accumulated behavior of a people dissolved into water, stone, architecture, and plants: a distributed inhabitant made from recurrence.

The drowned country itself has learned their motions.

And, with immense patience, it continues to perform them.
