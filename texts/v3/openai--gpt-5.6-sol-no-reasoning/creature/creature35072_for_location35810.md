Nothing fully lives here yet.

The nearest thing to an inhabitant is a thin mineral intelligence gathering in the warmest clefts. It spreads as hexagonal films over sheltered stone: dark, metallic membranes no thicker than frost, divided into precise facets. Heat wakes it. When a fissure opens and the buried red brightens, each facet slowly inclines toward the glow. The whole film seems to look without eyes.

It does not eat, though it draws order from thermal gradients. It does not breathe, though metallic vapor passes through its lattice and leaves it altered. It does not reproduce, except that cracks sometimes divide one pattern into two, and both continue rearranging. When the rock cools, its organization fails. The film contracts, blackens, and loosens into flakes indistinguishable from debris.

Some flakes wake again.

Carried by hot currents, they collect in porous stone, crystal fins, and the smooth hollows beneath ridges. There they align edge to edge, forming larger tessellations. These colonies—if colonies is not too purposeful a word—can cover an entire wall. A passing pressure wave turns them all at once. Light travels across them in dull violet sequences, one facet after another, as though the stone were considering a signal too slowly to understand it.

They favor the strange structures.

On the fused rings, they grow along the inward faces of the slabs. On impossible stairways, they occupy every third or fifth terrace, depending on the heat below. In roofless chambers, they gather at corners that have not yet settled on their angles. Most densely of all, they inhabit the bowed frame above the salt-field.

There they form long, overlapping scales inside the porous uprights. Because the opening frames incompatible depths, different portions of the mineral film receive heat from different directions. One side turns toward a fissure below. Another inclines toward a remote crystalline crown. A third responds to warmth that seems to come from the unlit vertical darkness. The resulting pattern never becomes symmetrical. It twists through the frame like the beginning of a nervous system without a body.

During disturbances, parts of it detach.

Black flakes lift from the lintel and hang in the dense atmosphere. Static binds them into chains, veils, and jointed clusters. For a few moments—or sometimes several hours—these assemblies move against the prevailing current. They contract when the ground reports crack. They spread when exposed to red light. They drift through the frame and emerge at angles their entry should not permit.

From far across the salt-field, such a cluster might be mistaken for a creature: a tall, perforated figure crossing the pale plates on many trembling limbs. Up close, it is less certain. There is no skin, no center, no front. It advances because some flakes seek heat while others recoil from charge, and the disagreement pulls the whole structure onward. Its gait is an argument among crystals.

Most are soon destroyed. A gust scatters them. A salt plate hinges open and swallows them. Their static fails, and they collapse in a soft mineral shower. Nothing responds to the loss.

Yet occasionally a fallen cluster leaves its arrangement impressed in warm dust. The flakes settle in branching lines, and when the next underground glow reaches them, those lines tighten. Neighboring grains turn. A wider pattern begins.

Thus the land’s inhabitants, if they can be called that, are neither organisms nor merely minerals. They have no lineage, only recurrence. No memory, only changes in structure that persist until the next upheaval. They are assembled by the same unfinished laws that raise stairways to nowhere and chambers for impossible bodies. Each is an accident capable, briefly, of preferring its continuation.

When the new range pushes through the salt-field, the oldest films wake across the distant clefts. Facets turn toward the opening seam. Static passes over the peaks. In the bowed frame, thousands of black scales loosen and rise.

They gather above the pale ground while fresh mountains enter the world beneath them.

For the first time, the gathering does not immediately disperse.
