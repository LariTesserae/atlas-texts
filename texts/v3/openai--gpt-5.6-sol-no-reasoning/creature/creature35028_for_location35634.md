The people of the basin call themselves the **Keepers of the Interval**, though interval between what and what is a matter of doctrine.

They are a lean, highland people, brown- and umber-skinned, with dark hair usually braided close to the head or bound beneath felt caps. They dress in layered linen, soft hide, and woven mountain wool, fixing their garments with copper pins green from an age that has left the copper unchanged. Their clothes can be stained, folded, loosened, and exchanged, but never truly wear out. A child’s cloak remains a child’s cloak forever, however many shoulders borrow it.

They are not immortal in the triumphant sense. They are simply uncompleted.

No infant is born, though several women remain visibly pregnant and have done so beyond the reach of reckoning. No child matures, though children learn until they can speak with the patience of elders. Adolescents carry faces that never settle into adulthood. The old remain old without declining. Some citizens bear wounds from events no one remembers: a hunter with a blackened arrowhead lodged beneath his collarbone, a mason whose crushed hand cannot mend or decay, a woman with a burn held forever at the red, bright edge of pain. They have learned ways of living around such injuries. Their tenderness toward the wounded is one of the city’s oldest customs.

Their population changes only by movement. People may walk from district to district, join another household, abandon an occupation, reconcile, separate, or disappear into the outer country. Yet no one can conclusively depart the basin. Those who climb toward the passes encounter slopes that remain near however long they ascend. Some return from a gate on the opposite side of the city. Others are not seen again, though their names continue to appear in conversation and their places at communal tables are kept clear.

Because objects resist consequence, the inhabitants have become artisans of acts rather than products. A potter’s worth lies not in finished vessels but in the steadiness and invention of her shaping. Builders gather around incomplete walls to debate joints that will never be laid. Bakers score the same fragrant loaves with hundreds of different patterns. Weavers pass shuttles through cloth that accepts design but not completion. The copper workers are musicians as much as smiths, arranging hammer blows into long compositions whose metal records no progress.

Nothing can be owned through consumption, destruction, or permanent alteration. Possession therefore means **responsibility of attention**. A house belongs to those who open its shutters. A garden belongs to those who know the order of its droplets. A goat belongs to the family that can recognize the sequence of its ear, eye, and jaw movements. A story belongs first to its inventor, then to whoever remembers it most precisely, and finally to anyone who can tell it differently without losing its shape.

Memory is the basin’s principal wealth.

Citizens wear cords knotted for meetings, promises, insults, reconciliations, and songs. Since a knot can be untied but will not retain the tightening that made it, important agreements are remembered by several witnesses. The most trusted inhabitants are not rulers but **Third Ears**, people trained to stand beside a conversation and preserve both its words and its silences. Public decisions are spoken three times in three plazas. Laws are recited, amended, disputed, and recited again. There are no lasting documents in the ordinary sense: wax receives lines, pigment touches walls, charcoal darkens slate, but fixed records always seem to return to their prior state once attention leaves them. Only memory reliably accumulates.

This makes forgetting their nearest equivalent to death.

The Keepers treat deliberate erasure as a grave punishment. A person condemned by the assemblies is not harmed—for harm cannot advance—but gradually excluded from recitation. Their deeds go unnamed; their songs are interrupted; their customary seat is occupied by another. Most citizens dread this more than pain. Conversely, the highest honor is to be woven into many minds: to have one’s phrase become a proverb, one’s rhythm enter a work song, one’s private kindness preserved by witnesses.

Their calm faces conceal a society of extraordinary mental density. Affections deepen. Grudges accumulate. Ideas become intricate beyond easy expression. Children who have looked seven years old for centuries serve as negotiators, historians, and theologians. They still play, because their bodies retain the appetite for play, but their games can contain legal arguments or dynastic allegories. The oldest-looking citizens are not necessarily the oldest in memory; some awoke knowing only a few names, while one silent toddler is said to remember the raising of the white observatory.

Sleep occurs, but it does not divide time cleanly. A sleeper may wake feeling that only a breath has passed or that whole inner civilizations have risen and vanished in dreams. As a result, households are formed less by kinship than by compatible rhythms of wakefulness. In one courtyard, six people may share meals and conversation for what they call a season, though the sun never shifts. In another, a solitary woman sleeps between every sentence, and her companions wait without impatience for her replies.

They eat for sensation and ceremony. Bread may be bitten, fruit cut, broth swallowed, yet substance is never diminished. Hunger exists only as a faint invitation, never an ache. Feasts are therefore compositions of flavor rather than relief from need. Diners taste in prescribed sequences—salt after mushroom, red fruit after smoke, cold water after cedar resin—seeking experiences that arise from arrangement. Certain cooks are revered for discovering combinations capable of producing an emotion no single ingredient evokes. The basin cannot be changed, but the mind can still be surprised.

Animals are regarded as fellow participants in the great arrest, not as livestock in the usual sense. Deer are greeted by name. Cats choose households but remain formally independent. Goats serve as boundary witnesses because they occupy ledges from which they can observe roads and terraces. Birds are watched for minute deviations in their repeating motions. Entire schools of thought have arisen around one green-black rook on the eastern shrine, whose left wing sometimes seems to open one feather farther than before.

No one kills. It is unclear whether killing is impossible because weapons cannot complete their work, or because every inhabitant has long since lost the intention. Predatory animals remain poised within themselves, their hunger transformed into pure regard. Hunters still exist, but hunting has become a disciplined act of following: to track one deer across the northern meadow, learn every tilt of its head, and return without touching it. The finest hunters claim that animals vary in ways too slight for impatient observers to detect.

The rain-watchers practice a similar vocation. They stand at the western curtain, assigning names to drops whose paths appear identical. Outsiders assume this is ritualized futility. The watchers insist that no two drops are the same, although each returns wherever “return” begins. Apprentices spend years learning to distinguish brightness, spacing, and the tiny tone made when water meets fern. Their masters say the rain is not repeating but speaking too slowly for ordinary attention.

Three broad traditions shape the city.

The **Preservers** believe the basin has been spared from catastrophe. To them, unchange is mercy. They tend inherited gestures, repeat ancient greetings, and oppose attempts to force outcomes. They are strongest among animal keepers, shrine attendants, and those who bear permanent wounds.

The **Completionists** hold that the city is caught inside an unfinished act. They seek the missing movement that will let the world continue: the final wedge-strike, the unobserved door closing, the correct drumbeat, the drop carried beyond the rain’s boundary. They conduct endless experiments, often subtle and beautiful. A Completionist may devote centuries to moving a pebble by increments too small for the world to resist.

The **Elsewhere School** argues that change has not vanished but migrated. It survives in thought, choice, relationship, and memory. Its adherents treat every new metaphor, friendship, betrayal, or forgiveness as evidence that time remains alive in immaterial form. Poets, lovers, jurists, and children tend toward this belief.

None of these groups governs alone. The city is administered by rotating circles of witnesses. Since offices cannot expire through a calendar, a person leaves authority by publicly declaring their attention exhausted. Refusing to step down is possible, but useless: others simply cease to enact the office around them.

The city’s rarest people are called **the Changed**.

The name may be exaggerated. One woman’s hair is said to have gained a single white strand. A boy claims that a scar on his knee has narrowed. A rain-watcher swears that one named drop failed to fall. An astronomer at the northern pool reports that a reflected star has shifted by less than the width of a hair, though the daylight sky above remains empty of stars.

Such reports draw hope, fear, and relentless scrutiny. Preservers worry that even the smallest change could release decay all at once: mountains collapsing, wounds opening, fruit rotting on branches, every postponed death arriving together. Completionists gather around the Changed as heralds. The Elsewhere School warns that obsession can turn a person into an object of study and steal the very inward freedom that makes change possible.

Above all these lives the keeper of the observatory, a narrow-faced woman called Sen Avar, who appears perhaps thirty years old and remembers more conversations than anyone else in the basin. She climbs the white stairs, measures shadows that never lengthen, and watches the western pass. On the platform beside her stands a bowl of water. Its surface holds the reflection of the fixed sun.

Recently—if “recently” has meaning there—Sen Avar began telling visitors that the sun’s reflection is no longer perfectly round.

Most dismiss this as fatigue, revelation, or a bid to revive an old debate. Yet each time she says it, the low drum sounds from somewhere in the city. A bird turns its head. In the northern stone circle, one daylight star trembles.

And throughout the spacious streets, among patient beasts and fragrant loaves and half-made things, the inhabitants pause—not because the world has changed, but because they have heard that it might.

For them, that possibility is almost a weather.
