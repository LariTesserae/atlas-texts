The inhabitants of the summit are, at first glance, the countless small things fitted into its fabric.

They live not merely in the enclosure but in the marks of its making. Pale crustaceans fold their legs into hammer dents and old rope grooves. Blind swimmers occupy the slack water beneath arches, their ribbon bodies tied into slow, living knots. Transparent bells hang from lintels. Minute grazers travel the warm masonry with scraping mouths, harvesting the colorless bacterial veils hidden in seams. Predators, flat and dark as soot laid by a thumb, cover the remnants of carved faces and wait there so long that generations of smaller creatures mistake them for part of the stone.

All of them are parsimonious. They spend motion as though it cannot be replenished. Light, too, is used with restraint: a few cold points under a belly, an amber ring briefly opened, a signal kindled beneath a shell and smothered at once. Even hunger proceeds slowly. The animals eat the chemistry of the walls by degrees—first through the veils, then through one another—and the entire congregation is sustained by warmth, minerals, and the nearly imperceptible circulation of death.

They have learned the summit’s inverted law. Eggs are fixed beneath ledges. Scavengers gather overhead. Burrowers work upward through the talus pressed against the vault. Their bodies assume that whatever becomes loose will eventually rise.

The long-shelled ones seem less like animals adapted to the place than a moving part of the place itself. They are ancient-looking, narrow and immensely long, with shells worn smooth where corridor and body meet. Each travels the same circuit: through a grille into the abyss, around the blunt crown, back through another opening, then down the corkscrew stair. They cannot turn in the passages. They do not pass one another. Their procession is therefore also a mechanism, ordered without any visible overseer. Perhaps they carry something too subtle to see: heat, spores, dissolved metal, memory. Perhaps their abrasion keeps the route open. Perhaps the summit would slowly close without them.

Yet these are not the only things that live there.

Something abides in the masonry as a whole.

It has no single visible body. Its warmth is distributed through blocks laid by vanished hands. Its metabolism occurs in mortar joints, bacterial films, copper contractions, drifting grains, and the patient rounds of shelled creatures. The six walls are less its dwelling than its ribs. The galleries resemble channels within an organ; the grilles, guarded pores. What the small fauna consume may be its exhalation. What they return in death may be how it renews itself.

Its center is the basin suspended in the crown chamber.

The basin appears empty because its contents are not gathered inside it. They gather on its underside, where one clear drop takes years to become distinct from the surrounding sea. During that long formation, the summit seems to be concentrating something dispersed throughout itself: geothermal warmth, mineral salts, the waste and deaths of animals, traces worn from idols and tools, perhaps even whatever remains in the stone of those who built it. The braided mineral cords hold the basin level against both ordinary buoyancy and the summit’s contrary pull, as if maintaining the precise attitude required for this slow distillation.

When the drop releases, every animal recognizes an event older than instinct.

They withdraw not in panic but in observance. The blind fish turn toward it. Sediment rises aside. Predators remain still beneath their skins of soot. Even the long-shelled procession seems, for that interval, secondary. The drop sinks through a world where loose stone rises, choosing the abyssward direction that nothing dead or broken can follow. It passes the inward-facing figures like a celebrant descending among witnesses and enters the inaccessible fitting at the lowest basin.

Then the summit wakes.

Warmth travels through the walls. Hidden veils thicken. Grazers feed. Eggs split open on the ceilings. The long-shelled circuit recommences. Thus the being that lives there may be neither god nor machine nor animal, though it shares something with all three. It is a composite life assembled by builders who possessed only stone hammers, wet wedges, rope, clay, shell-lime, chipped glass, and a little copper. They did not imprison it inside the enclosure. They gave it an anatomy.

The upright figures on the highest terrace may therefore represent neither ancestors nor prisoners. They may be the remnants of its makers standing permanently around the organ they built, facing inward because the important immensity was never the ocean outside. Or they may depict the summit’s first inhabitants, slowly surrendered to anonymity so their substance could join the walls. Shoulders become posts, faces become bowls, inscriptions become burrows. Identity is abraded into function.

Perhaps the builders themselves never left. Not as ghosts, and not as recognizable minds, but as distributed residues continually lifted, sorted, consumed, and distilled. Their tool heads and idols rise into the crown chamber. Their copper gives its occasional note. Their worn images shelter eggs. Every few years, some minute fraction of the whole is gathered into the impossible droplet and sent downward through the fitted hole.

Where it goes cannot be seen.

It may descend through a hidden conduit miles into the ocean floor that supposedly is not there. It may feed an organism below, vast enough that the summit is only one organ raised remotely above it. Or the droplet may be the summit’s seed, released again and again into the black beneath, each one carrying instructions for another small made world.

Whatever lives in the enclosure does not seem to distinguish architecture from ecology, artifact from animal, ritual from metabolism. Its body is everything within the six walls: the veils and grazers, the inverted rubble, the waiting predators, the long shells in their grooves, the carved figures losing their names, and the empty basin slowly becoming full.

It lives at the highest point by sending its most precious substance down.
