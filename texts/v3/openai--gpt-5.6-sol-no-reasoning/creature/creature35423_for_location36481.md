The district is inhabited less by a population than by an arrangement: creatures, people, and machines fitted into one another’s habits until it is difficult to say where maintenance ends and instinct begins.

The most visible lives are the smallest. Silver moths sleep in trembling layers on transformer housings, opening and closing their wings with fluctuations in the current. Water beetles skate the drowned lanes with captive bubbles shining beneath their shells. Translucent fungi occupy cracks between bricks, folding themselves tight whenever the floor begins to vibrate. Blind minnows gather in the enamel tanks kept by the human residents, while eels inhabit cable conduits, surfacing when the electrical load diminishes. Old current has left pale grids and branching scars along their bodies.

Pale crabs live in telephone boxes. They sort bits of insulation by color, packing red, yellow, blue, and black fragments into separate terminals as though restoring a code no one else remembers. Rats keep to dry routes invisible from the flooded streets. Their existence is betrayed by opened cupboards, gnawed mineral crusts, and orderly deposits of empty snail shells.

Black birds nest in the ventilation system. No one knows how their first ancestors entered, or whether they were ever birds of the upper city. They have lightless eyes and near-silent wings. They do not sing. Instead, they attend to the machinery. Before a pump engages, every bird along a duct turns toward it; before a pressure change, they climb to higher perches. The people below sometimes watch the birds rather than the gauges.

The rainfish belong more completely to the district than any other animal. They swim through wetness itself: up sweating walls, across ceilings, around columns, and along the bright seams beneath pipes. When condensation thins, they halt at the edge and wait, trembling in place for hours or days. Heavy weather above summons them in thousands. They crowd around warm panels and glide over tiled chambers in translucent schools, vanishing back into mortar as the city dries.

Human beings are fewer, and they live apart. Each household occupies a catwalk, service platform, gantry, or room lifted above the waterline. Their dwellings are chosen not for address but for conditions: a reliable draft, a warm pipe, a wall that leaks clean water, a machine whose rhythm deters rats. They collect condensation in glass, cultivate fungal fiber, weave moth silk, and raise minnows. Their tools are tied down against vibration. Their furniture has narrow feet so as not to obstruct runoff.

They speak quietly and seldom use names at a distance. The pipework can take a spoken word elsewhere and release it long afterward, so careless speech may return from drains or empty rooms. Messages are more often left in chalk beside gauges: circles for safe passage, fractions for water levels, gill-like diagrams for breathable chambers. Clocks are useless to them. They reckon time by flywheel revolutions, pump sequences, moth emergences, floods, and the growth of calcite across scored bricks.

They do not rule the district. They negotiate with it.

Some of them serve as maintainers, though no one appears to hold that occupation continuously. A person may spend years without touching a valve, then wake before a pressure change and walk into the tunnels carrying oil, chalk, wire, and a polished wrench. Work is done slowly. A grille may take a week to clear because removing debris too quickly changes the airflow. A valve may be opened by one degree each cycle. Brass is polished not for beauty but so fresh corrosion can be read.

Yet not all maintenance can be attributed to them. Grilles clear when everyone has slept. Bearings are found newly greased in sealed rooms. Footprints emerge from flooded tunnels and end at switchboards, though no returning prints appear. Sometimes the chalk symbols beside a gauge are shaped for hands with too many joints. The residents do not discuss these signs as mysteries. They copy them into ledgers, obey the useful ones, and wait to see whether the machines agree.

The machines themselves may be the district’s oldest inhabitants. Pumps breathe, pistons nod, relays click, and belts crawl through the darkness long after their loads have disappeared. None performs a comprehensible task alone. Together they maintain an equilibrium so intricate that intention and consequence have separated. One pump lowers a room by flooding another. One turbine warms a nesting duct. One idle rail occasionally bears some unseen carriage beneath the silt.

The people treat these mechanisms as large, slow animals: dangerous less from aggression than from the scale and patience of their movements. They listen through railings, place teeth against pipes, and read pressure through the soles of their feet. A machine that has repeated a cycle for a century is trusted more than a newly repaired one.

Beneath all these inhabitants lies the suspected reservoir. Whatever occupies it is known only by displacement. At long intervals, water throughout the district rises against one side of every basin, then settles. Eels leave the conduits. Rainfish flatten themselves into mortar. Crabs abandon their sorted wires. The black birds turn downward.

The humans remain still until the movement passes.

They do not call what lies below a beast, a machine, or a body of water. Such distinctions have little use here. It may be the final component of the system, or the reason the system was built. It may sleep, circulate, or merely answer distant pressure from the buried river and the weight of the city above.

Whatever it is, the whole district appears adapted to its breathing.
