The plateau is inhabited first and most completely by plants.

They do not grow as separate specimens so much as a continuous, many-layered organism laid over the stone. Mosses, liverworts, sedges, creeping ferns, and fine-rooted herbs braid themselves into a buoyant ground several meters deep. Their upper surface is bright and close-textured; beneath it lies a drowned archive of stems, trapped rain, black humus, and pale roots feeling toward the rock. Some portions bear enormous weight. Others are only floating lids over cold water.

Low shrubs inhabit the wind by yielding to it. Their trunks divide early and travel sideways, making long woven bodies that may be mistaken for several plants. Hard cushion colonies occupy places where the stone approaches the surface. They grow outward by minute increments, presenting domes of tiny leaves and, in the warmer season, constellations of white flowers. In sheltered folds stand the plateau’s giants: broad-leaved plants with thick, watery stalks and leaves large enough to collect whole shallow pools. Their margins remain perfect. Nothing browses them.

Along cliffs live the hanging communities. Ferns and mosses root at the brows, then lower veils into the mist. Their roots, stems, and dead fibers conduct water downward, so that every escarpment supports its own suspended wetland. Small plants germinate in these curtains without ever touching conventional soil. Where warm vapor escapes from machinery, unfamiliar broadleaf species gather in dense, almost tropical patches, living through the cold by borrowing heat from vents and cooling housings.

The stations have inhabitants as surely as the basins do. Moss occupies their seams and gutters. Ferns settle beneath dripping valves. Pale moths sleep under ledges warmed by electrical cabinets, their wings the color of condensation-clouded metal. Midges rise from undisturbed pools whenever the air becomes still enough for them to hold formation. They are sparse, black pinpricks over the reflected sky, but much of the plateau’s outward flowering depends upon them. Other plants rely on rain striking their blossoms, on wind, or on closed flowers that complete their work without visitors.

Birds are rare and appear more like events than residents. A narrow-winged bird may enter from beyond one rim, cross kilometers of open green, and vanish over another without descending. It has no flock behind it and leaves no perceptible mark. Its call is consumed by the engines.

People live here only intermittently and by accommodation. They are pump tenders, pressure engineers, cable operators, botanists, line crews, and inspectors of foundations. No dense settlement can be supported on the saturated ground, so they occupy scattered huts and the habitable compartments of the larger stations. Their lives are measured by maintenance cycles and pressure readings rather than by traffic or crowds.

They dress for water more than cold: sealed boots, bright shells, gloves textured for wet alloy. Near operating turbines they communicate by hand signals, helmet lights, or messages passed through headsets. They learn the vegetation as another person might learn roads. Silver sedge means a load may pass. Red cushions promise shallow stone. A flat reach of glossy moss may be a roof over several meters of water. Experienced workers do not test uncertain ground with a single hard step; they lower their weight gradually, keeping it broad.

Their vehicles are similarly adapted. Broad mesh wheels press the plants down without cutting them, and long freight platforms distribute machinery over multiple axles. Walkways flex. Buildings rest on rafts or forests of shallow feet. Heavy components are rarely placed directly on the plateau, even for a moment. A dropped crate can break through the green surface and disappear into the water beneath, leaving only a dark-edged wound that roots begin closing almost immediately.

Inside the stations, human spaces are compact and vibration-haunted. Cups tremble on tables. Tools are hung in fitted racks so they cannot migrate across the floor. Windows wear perpetual beads of water, and the view beyond them alternates between brilliant green, white rain, and reflected glare. Workers sleep within the turbine’s bass note until true quiet becomes disorienting. In distant huts, where the noise is reduced to a pressure in the walls, they can tell which pump has engaged by the way water shivers in a basin.

No one here truly masters the water. The crews draw it from fissures, lift it through pressure stages, cool engines with it, and send it down the escarpments to populations far below. Yet it escapes every arrangement without violence. It condenses on pipes, bleeds through collars, returns along roots, and rises in basins thought drained. The workers maintain direction, not possession. Their maps must be revised because streams diffuse, channels heal, and the weight of a station slowly alters the living ground beneath it.

Some installations are no longer inhabited by people. In them, the plateau’s slower occupants take over. Moss advances across control rooms where roofs have opened. Sedge grows through floor grilles. Rootlets enter cable conduits and follow them into darkness. One abandoned station has sunk until its lower story belongs to black water. The remaining roof carries a bog bright with flowers, and moths shelter under its rustless lip. It is neither wholly ruin nor wholly habitat, but a gradual exchange of one kind of occupation for another.

Deeper than the visible plants lives a largely unseen community: fungi threaded through the root mats, films of algae in clear runoff, bacteria in the oxygen-poor layers where old vegetation becomes dark substrate. These are the plateau’s quiet builders. They bind, soften, ferment, and preserve. They turn fallen leaves into depth and open water into eventual ground. Their labor is slow enough to resemble stillness, but much of the high country rests on it.

Thus what lives here is not abundant in the familiar sense. There are few moving bodies, few voices, few eyes. Life expresses itself instead as coverage, persistence, and transformation. It hangs from cliffs, floats over ponds, enters concrete, catches mist, cushions machinery, and manufactures earth upon bare young stone.

Even the engines seem, from a distance, to have joined this ecology. They breathe vapor, radiate heat, pulse through the root beds, and circulate the plateau’s oldest element. People tend them like immense metal organs. Plants grow over them without stopping them. Water passes through them and returns.

The high country’s inhabitants are therefore difficult to separate into native and intruding, organism and structure, ground and growth. They are mats becoming soil, stations becoming trellises, workers learning to move like water striders, and water continually escaping every boundary. All live provisionally upon a landscape that is still deciding what kind of land it will be.
