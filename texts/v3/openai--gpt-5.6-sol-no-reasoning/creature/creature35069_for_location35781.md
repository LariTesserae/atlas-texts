The people of the high country are adapted less in body than in habit.

They tend to be slight, deliberate, and difficult to startle. They walk with one hand near a rail even on level ground, because levelness is provisional, and they pause before thresholds to let the room beyond establish its dimensions. Children learn early not to race elevators, trust reflections, or retrieve a dropped object until it has chosen which machine it means to approach. Nearly everyone carries chalk, cord, a folding gauge, and a small cup for catching clean condensation.

Their clothing is layered against damp rather than cold: pale coats with ventilated seams, soft-soled boots fitted with retractable magnetic teeth, gloves thin enough to feel structural vibration. Colored tabs indicate district, trade, and the version of the municipal map by which the wearer navigates. Jewelry is commonly made from obsolete keys. No one remembers all the locks they once opened.

Most inhabitants are keepers of continuity. They tend filters, survey corridors, reconcile interior and exterior floor counts, guide the divided river, net suspended snow, and adjust homes when the mountains rise. Entire professions exist to accommodate slow impossibilities. Corner auditors certify new angles in old rooms. Distance clerks time walkways at different industrial loads. Reflection witnesses record events seen in glass before or after their occurrence. Steepness crews install ropes in neighborhoods that have gradually become inclines. Index engineers compare proposed buildings against examples that may already exist.

No one expects a structure to remain itself without attention.

Homes occupy whatever volumes machinery has spared: former control rooms, hollow buttresses, decommissioned reservoirs, cavities between two generations of wall. Their furnishings are hinged, nested, or suspended. Beds are fixed to two surfaces in case gravity revises its preference overnight. Tables have shallow lips. Domestic shrines hold bowls of river water drawn from several manifestations—canal, pipe, atrium fall, rooftop pool—so that a household may participate in the river’s continuity even when it cannot see it.

Families are often organized by proximity rather than descent. A household may include blood relations, apprentices, displaced neighbors, and one or two people whose former apartment slowly ceased to connect with any known corridor. Adoption is common after architectural events. So is inheritance by adjacency: when a room migrates across a wall, its new neighbors assume certain obligations toward whoever lives inside.

They speak quietly. Loud voices vanish into cladding or return from the wrong direction, while a palm on metal carries information more faithfully. Thus much communication is tactile. Warnings are tapped along railings. Shift changes pass through floors as coded tremors. Lovers sometimes sit without speech, wrists against the same pipe, feeling distant presses cycle beneath them.

Their language distinguishes many kinds of here. There is the place shown on maps, the place reachable today, the place sharing one’s gravity, the place on the same water line, and the place that occupies the same coordinates but cannot presently be entered. Similarly, “old” may mean buried deeply, constructed long ago, or simply no longer represented in the Index. These are not equivalent.

The dead are rarely put into the ground, since ground is only another occupied layer. Some are cremated in orchard furnaces, their ash added to root cartridges. Some are sealed into unused service cavities with a nameplate facing the nearest inhabited passage. River workers may be committed to the water in soluble cases, entering the city’s capillaries and emerging, in theory, everywhere. A few families place their dead in rooms scheduled to disappear through slow municipal drift. Such rooms are visited until the door can no longer be found.

Alongside the people live older, quieter populations.

The magnetic grazers are not machines, though they may have descended from them, and not goats, though they chew, breed, quarrel, and recognize the hands that feed them mineral blocks. Beneath their short black coats are plates that flex like muscle and warm themselves near active conduits. Their young are born with soft hooves and must be carried across iron slopes until the magnets harden. They clean lichen from sensitive façades, but they also eat warning labels, insulation foam, and occasionally the painted outlines around doors. Maintenance crews tolerate this because the animals can detect a failing weld days before instruments do. A herd standing motionless around one bolt is treated as an emergency report.

The white birds belong to the lamps. Their eggs are matte and faintly luminous; their chicks orient toward brightness before they open their eyes. Though their wings are long enough for migration, most cannot cross certain invisible boundaries between lighting fields. When a bank of lamps is replaced, whole colonies must be coaxed across the few meters to the new radiance with mirrored panels. Their solitary metallic call is believed to mark a change in pressure, distance, or time. Officially it means none of these things. Workers listen for it anyway.

The blind eels inhabit every accessible portion of the river and perhaps inaccessible ones as well. Specimens tagged in a rooftop pool have later been found in sealed pipes with no openings large enough to admit them. Their translucent skulls contain fine deposits of ceramic and iron corresponding to districts they have passed through. Hydrologists read these deposits like itineraries. Very old eels carry traces of alloys absent from the upper country and are released without dissection.

The red pond colonies are harder to classify. By day they are scattered grains adhering to submerged surfaces. At lamp-dimming they gather into flat, leaflike shapes and drift just below the water. The leaves differ from pond to pond. Some resemble orchard species; others reproduce plants known only from fragments printed on buried signs. Gardeners sometimes transfer them to root cartridges, where they dissolve and improve fruiting. Index staff prohibit this practice after several trees produced doors among their branches.

Translucent moths live around switchgear and transformers. Their wings respond not to light but to electrical intention: cabinets attract them most strongly just before being activated. Electricians watch their swarms to predict faults and unauthorized use. During deep industrial cycles, millions rise from the lower vents and arrange themselves in pale sheets across the basin. Seen from above, these formations resemble technical drawings. Nobody has determined what they depict, though certain arrangements have later appeared as new districts.

There are also beings known chiefly by the accommodations made for them.

Warm hollows in bearings contain nests of soft-bodied creatures never observed outside machinery. Vent grilles are built with one bar removable so something inside can pass during rain. Food disappears from locked loading bays, but only when the river runs overhead. In the oldest apartments, residents leave a chair facing the wall for the person who occupies the room at its other interior height.

Far below, where industries move in near silence, something enormous changes gear. The inhabitants do not claim it is alive. They merely note that its cycles lengthen in winter, that water gravitates toward the floors above it, and that the Index adds rooms whenever it turns in sleep.

The Index itself has inhabitants, though its census is contested. Its towers house engineers, archivists, bridge tenders, specimen keepers, and people born in prototype apartments whose corresponding neighborhoods have not yet been constructed. Such residents are called precedents. Some leave to find the later buildings that resemble their homes; others refuse, fearing they will become redundant when the full-scale version is completed.

Archivists move continually through the Index, tagging each chimney, room, culvert, and stair with its known counterparts. Their work is never finished. Occasionally they encounter a door whose label bears the archivist’s own handwriting, dated years ahead. Protocol requires them not to open it alone.

At the margins of settlement live the unregistered: households that follow migrating corridors, orchard workers who sleep among the root columns, salvage clans inhabiting gantries as they climb the young ranges. They know routes absent from public diagrams and recognize weather by the taste of runoff. Some claim to have crossed the last ridge.

Their accounts disagree. Beyond it, they say, lies a neighboring basin identical but older; or an open plain of actual stone; or a wall of white illumination on which the high country is still being drafted. One traveler returned carrying soil with nothing manufactured beneath it. No one believed her until seeds planted in it grew downward without encountering a door.

She now lives near the highest platform, where she keeps the soil in a sealed basin. Visitors come to look at it. Most find it unsettling: dark, loose, purposeless material, performing no secondary function.

At evening the inhabitants secure cups beneath favored drips, draw curtains against delayed reflections, and check whether their rooms have retained the same number of corners. The birds fold themselves beneath the awnings. The grazers lie against warm façades. Moths tremble around dormant switches, anticipating commands not yet given. In the walls, the river passes from one form to another without breaking.

Then the lights turn blue, and every living thing becomes briefly visible: silhouettes on bridges, nests in vents, fish over streets, workers on distant gantries, pale vines reaching toward absent floors.

For that hour, the basin seems less like a city occupied by life than a single immeasurable habitat—one layered creature whose inhabitants are also its organs, tending the structures that contain them while those structures, slowly and with bureaucratic care, rearrange the conditions of their existence.
