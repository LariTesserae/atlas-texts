The hollow is inhabited not by a community of distinct creatures so much as by a single, many-bodied ecology.

Its smallest lives occupy the seams between larger ones: translucent mites that scour digested stone; threadlike hunters with mineral needles for mouths; pale grains that might be eggs, spores, or animals contracted into dormancy. They feed on residue, secretions, flakes of shell, and each other. Some spend their entire existence in the warm hinge of another creature’s leg.

Above them crawl the stone-grazers. These are low, many-jointed things with overlapping black plates and mouths bright with glassy teeth. They rasp salt veins from the basalt in patient rows. Their bodies are asymmetrical, shaped by lifelong pressure from neighboring shells. When disturbed, they do not flee. They lock their limbs, becoming footholds for the mass.

The borers are softer. Their heads resemble sealed bulbs, except when the feeding folds open and release a corrosive breath. They hollow pits in the shelves and sleep inside them while their own acids continue to work. Other animals nest in abandoned bores, so that every borer trails a future neighborhood behind it.

The largest ground-dwellers are broad-backed lithovores, slow enough to be mistaken for terraces. They swallow fractured basalt and keep it for months in furnace-hot digestive chambers. Veins of symbiotic tissue separate useful sulfides from inert stone. What emerges is polished into pellets, and entire lesser species follow them for this waste. Old individuals carry ecosystems on their armor: scavengers under the rims of their plates, egg clusters in their scars, ambush predators disguised as mineral crust.

Nothing here is truly solitary. Even predators are passages, shelters, and feeding grounds for smaller lives.

Over everything hangs the canopy brood. Millions of leathery, clasping animals suspend themselves from the rim and from one another. Their wings are not made for sustained flight. Folded, they serve as shade, insulation, mating display, nursery wall, and communal skin. Each animal grips several neighbors, and each is gripped in turn, forming layers thick enough to hide the sky.

They feed on rising heat. Along the undersides of their membranes run dark organs containing reactive oils and metallic salts. Warm air drives these organs slowly, as blood drives a heart. In return the canopy exhales cooler, altered gases into the hollow. Its eldest members may never release their grip. They become dry anchor-bodies, still supporting generations after the rest of them has been eaten away.

In the rare shafts of sunlight live the upright feeders: narrow creatures that unfold like black leaves and become amber-clear in the heat. They face neither sun nor shadow but turn continuously between them, using the thermal difference across their bodies. When a beam moves, they move with it, and the entire crowd shifts to close the space behind them.

There are hunters throughout the press, though few pursue prey. Pursuit costs too much. They resemble cracks, discarded plates, smooth pellets, or dead patches of stone. Some wait for exhaustion. Others cause it, applying heat through prolonged contact until the animal beneath them can no longer move. A few hunt by interrupting the pressure-signals of the colony. They send a false tremor through the packed bodies, creating a momentary gap, then consume whatever is exposed when the gap closes.

The creatures with red pits along their flanks are among the most visible and least understood. Their glow comes from naked reaction chambers where oils meet powdered ore. The heat may repel parasites, attract mates, mature eggs, or merely dispose of dangerous chemical excess. Smaller animals crowd around them during cold fluctuations and sometimes cook there. Mirrored species follow close behind, flashing borrowed red light through the hollow in minute territorial arguments.

Reproduction takes innumerable forms. Eggs are mortared into hot fissures. Young bud beneath parental plates and emerge only when the parent is injured. Dust-sized gametes are exchanged during the great exhalations. Some species mate by touching reflective patches; others by matching alternating waves of body heat over several days. In the densest regions, parentage is meaningless: nurseries are communal cracks lined with wax, shell dust, and mineral fibers, defended by whichever bodies happen to surround them.

All of them participate in the hollow’s greater reflex.

When pressure rises beneath the plug, no leader detects it first. Gas enters the deepest breathing chambers. Nearby shells expand and press against their neighbors. That pressure becomes instruction. Bracing species turn broadside. Clinging species release. Burrowers seal themselves in. Predators flatten. The canopy loosens its grip in carefully propagated circles. The population reconfigures itself into ramps, channels, ladders, and valves.

For those minutes, the inhabitants cease even to resemble animals. They become an organ built around the fissure.

The exhalation carries part of that organ into the sky. Membrane-bearers open above the rim. Armored grazers cling beneath them. Parasites move between hosts. Predators feed in flight. Egg grains scatter across the mesa, though almost all will fall on sterile stone and remain dormant until abrasion, heat, or some later breath brings them home. The airborne swarm is the only time the hollow’s life separates into visible individuals, and even then they circle as if reluctant to lose contact.

When they descend, there is no mourning for those crushed or cooked. Corpses are chemically rich interruptions, and the response to them is immediate. Plates are opened at the joints. Oils are siphoned. mineral organs are bitten free. Shells become nursery roofs or new scraping edges. By the next exhalation, even the shape of the dead has vanished.

Near the central uprights dwell the most heat-tolerant forms: blind, pale, compact creatures whose shells are glazed by sulfur. They cement their young into cracks in the ancient stone ring. Some push tendrils through the pores of the plug and feed directly on the gas seeping from below. Their lifespans may be measured in centuries. They seldom move except when the plug shifts, at which point they withdraw all at once, like flesh pulled from a flame.

Whether anything lives beneath them is uncertain.

The quiet knock from below produces a response no ordinary exhalation does. Feeding stops. Predators release prey. Even the canopy stills, though this strains the weight-bearing layers. Glowing organs dim behind shutters of shell. Every foot listens through the stone.

Perhaps the knock is only rock breaking under pressure.

Perhaps an older grazer survives beneath the seal, immense and buried, digesting the mesa from within.

Perhaps the ring of uprights was not built to mark the fissure, but to hold it shut.

Whatever lives in the hollow does not name the difference. It waits as one dark, interlocked body until the stone speaks again.
