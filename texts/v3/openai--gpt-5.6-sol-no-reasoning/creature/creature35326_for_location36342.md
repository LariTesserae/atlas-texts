The Crown is inhabited less by species than by suspended verbs.

Its creatures feed, flee, hatch, hunt, heal, mate, molt, and die, but none passes from one condition into the next. They are not statues. A statue merely resembles action. These beings perform it continuously, each process completed and annulled in the same indivisible instant.

Nearest the great leaves live the **cincture herds**: translucent grazers shaped like loops of glassy muscle. Each clasps a frond with a circular mouth. Within the ring, combs of enamel scrape forever across tissue that is forever restored. Green sugars shine through their clear bodies in branching digestive canals, while pale waste returns by finer vessels to the same plant. A herd may cover hundreds of kilometers of membrane, though from a distance it appears only as a tarnished margin around the leaf.

They have no front or rear. Sense organs occur in wreaths around their bodies, and their radial brains maintain simultaneous awareness in every direction. Their hunger is not discomfort but structure: the tension that keeps mouth, gut, blood, and leaf in equilibrium. To satisfy it would destroy them as surely as starvation.

Among the cinctures stand **many-footed bark walkers**, armored browsers whose bodies are low, black, and broad. Their legs radiate like the roots of mobile trees, gripping every ridge of a trunk without selecting a downward side. Some are immense enough that groves grow in the seams of their shells. Others fit within the pits of bark.

Each walker holds one foot in the act of release and all the others in varying phases of attachment. Thus it possesses a gait without displacement. Its shell records the path it is always about to take: polished channels, resin stains, colonies of mites arranged in streaming lines. Whether the animal has traversed the trunk or whether the trunk has grown the evidence of its passage cannot be distinguished.

Inside the ancient erosion tunnels dwell the **channel saints**. These are blind, plated animals so exactly fitted to their hollows that they seem cast there. Their ribs follow the fluting of the wood. Their feeding limbs enter mineral pores with perfect precision. Plants send thin vascular threads between their armor plates; the animals send sensory cilia back into the plants. Neither can be removed conceptually from the other. A tunnel saint may be called a parasite, an organ, a prisoner, or the living lining of a wound.

They communicate through knocks transmitted along the trunks. Because no knock begins or ends, their language is harmonic rather than sequential. Meaning lies in the interference between permanent signals. A question is a frequency placed against another frequency; an answer is the quiet shape produced where they cancel.

Across the black electrical vines rest the **voltage mantles**, broad colonies that resemble discarded skins of silver lace. They feed directly on potential difference. Their undersides carry ranks of conductive mouths, while their backs bloom with insulating vanes of amber resin. Charge enters and leaves in equal measure. Every mantle is at once electrocuted and nourished, its tissues held in a brilliant threshold between contraction and release.

Tiny animals live inside them: copper-shelled cleaners, filament worms, and white parasites that consume electrical insulation and replace it with their own bodies. Pollinators shelter there as well, using the mantles as luminous bridges between flower organs that may lie thousands of kilometers apart.

Those pollinators are called **spark dust**, though each is a complex animal with mirrored wings and a needle abdomen. They occur in vast rings around the great leaves of the summits. Every individual leans into flight. Wings bow against the water; muscles blaze with chemical effort; antennae stream backward. Yet the ring does not revolve.

Pollen adheres to them and departs from them simultaneously. Courtship signals cross their mirrored bodies in fixed pulses. Eggs occupy the ovaries in the instant of fertilization, cleavage, and hatching. The young are not smaller versions of the adults but bright geometric larvae folded through the parents’ organs, present as developmental processes that never acquire duration.

The open blue belongs to the **vesper balloons**. They are enormous, thin-skinned filterers, often separated by tens of kilometers. Their bodies consist of gas-threaded lobes gathered around a dark central mouth. In a directional sea they might have risen or drifted. Here they maintain buoyancy equally toward every possible above.

A vesper’s feeding net extends around it as a nearly invisible sphere. Spores, mineral grains, minute swimmers, and fragments of epidermis occupy the mesh without arriving there. Around each balloon lives a complete attendant ecology. Slender cleaners comb its pores. Blind parasites hang inside the gas lobes. Small predators hide among the folds of its net, their jaws closed around prey that remains outside as well as within.

Some vespers contain old punctures surrounded by puckered scar tissue. From these wounds extend long fans of symbiotic leaves. The plants draw gases from the animal, and the animal takes sugars from the plants, though no molecule completes the exchange. At a distance the whole association resembles a dim green star.

The chief hunters are **ribbon jaws**. Their bodies can be hundreds of kilometers long and only meters thick, braided from transparent muscle around a tract of black digestive chambers. They encircle shoals rather than pursuing individuals. Within each loop hangs a cloud of silver prey, every swimmer poised at the angle of escape.

The ribbon’s mouth is not located at an end. The entire inner edge is mouth: a continuous fringe of teeth, cilia, venom sacs, and sensory pits. Digestion begins everywhere along the enclosure but reaches nowhere. Predator and shoal define one another so completely that naturalists—if naturalists could maintain a before and after—might argue that they constitute a single colonial life-form whose outer tissues impersonate attack.

Smaller predators include **gear-mouths**, compact fish with ceramic teeth grown from silicates extracted by their gut flora. The teeth interlock in rotary arrays. No wheel turns, but each tooth bears balanced pressure from its neighbors, producing a shrill tone that travels far through the water. Colonies identify individual gear-mouths by the chord of their jaws.

Their prey are resin worms, hinge-bodied crustaceans, and the larvae of the Crown’s seed organs. Every prey animal bears an injury fitted to a particular predator’s bite. Every predator contains the meal corresponding to that wound. Neither fact establishes which caused the other.

Bridging the wide intervals between leaves are the **branching transparents**. Their bodies have no central trunk. Hundreds of limbs divide repeatedly until their finest tips become nearly cellular threads touching separate membranes kilometers apart. A transparent may bind a whole grove into one nervous territory.

Light travels through its nerves in ordered sequences: gold, blue, violet, darkness, gold again. The sequence always appears to move, yet never reaches the next branch. Plants respond by altering electrical tension at their edges. Grazers turn their eyes toward it. Spark dust aligns its wings. Through these responses the transparents seem to conduct the Crown’s local awareness, though whether they transmit information or merely display a permanent pattern is unknowable.

In the interference hollows drift the **garden bells**. They are soft, translucent domes containing miniature ecologies: branching algae, ciliated chambers, tiny mouths, rootlike nerves, and seed-shaped hearts. Some parts photosynthesize. Others hunt neighboring cells. Still others digest minerals or generate the slow electrical field that holds the bell open.

No clean boundary separates inhabitant from habitat. A green structure inside a bell may be an organ in one description, a symbiont in another, and a captive forest in a third. The bells produce daughter bells as buds that are simultaneously attached and free. Whole lineages occupy the instant of separation.

Where the Crown’s noise becomes physically crushing live the **pressure psalms**: soft-bodied animals flattened into vast discs against the trunks. Their flesh is organized into concentric chambers, each tuned to a different component of the industrial roar. Sound deforms them, and their resistance shapes the sound in return.

They have little anatomy that is not auditory. Their skin listens; their blood listens; their reproductive tissues are arrays of resonant sacs. They feed by absorbing microscopic strains from the wood. In the lowest frequencies their bodies reveal patterns like faces or maps, but these figures may be accidents of standing pressure.

Within the caverns of plant-born mist hang **thunder veils**, colonial organisms spread along the permanent lightning. Their conductive skeletons divide as finely as the frozen branches of the discharge. Pale feeding polyps open around each luminous filament, taking energy from a bolt that neither strikes nor fades.

The veils are accompanied by dark, eyeless animals that graze the polished bark. Their hearing organs are enormous chambers threaded with hairs. Continuous thunder passes through them as a tangible architecture. They navigate not by echoes, which would require succession, but by occupying the stable nodes where one resonance intersects another.

And throughout the sea are the smallest inhabitants: spores, cells, gametes, bacterial films, mineral-feeding threads, and creatures scarcely larger than molecules. They form constellations in the open water and glowing weather along leaf edges. Every spore is germinating. Every root hair is emerging. Every microbial division maintains two daughters within one parent. Their abundance sustains the larger world, though “sustains” falsely implies time.

None of these beings sleeps, for sleep requires a passage into waking. None remembers, if memory is the retention of what is gone. Yet many contain structures resembling memory: scarred nerves, learned postures, chemical marks, migration maps, inherited alarms. They embody experience without possessing a past.

Nor are they mindless. Awareness in the Crown is simply unable to accumulate. A hunter perceives prey eternally. A grazer tastes the same living mouthful with inexhaustible immediacy. A parent recognizes offspring at the indivisible threshold of birth. Fear cannot increase toward panic or diminish into relief. Wonder cannot become familiarity.

The greatest possible mind may be the Crown itself.

Its black vines carry charge across continents. Its pressure organs answer one another through the trunks. Leaves alter their luminous margins in response to distant feeding. Cavern lungs, seed turbines, copper filaments, animal nerves, and resonant colonies participate in patterns too large for any local creature to contain. If this is thought, it is thought without sequence: an idea of planetary extent held whole, neither reached nor abandoned.

The animals may be its fauna, its organs, or its sensations. The difference has little force here.

They live everywhere in the cool blue volume: clasping leaves, ringing moons of vegetation, fitting scars, drinking voltage, enclosing prey, listening to thunder. They are crowded into relationships and scattered across impossible distances. Each is active beyond exhaustion and still beyond death.

Nothing among them waits to become.

Each creature is entirely what it is doing.
