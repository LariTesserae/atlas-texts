The drowned quarter is inhabited less by separate species than by a single civic metabolism.

Its human residents live inside the pressure habitats nested throughout the ruins: compact clans of engineers, gardeners, dredge crews, surveyors, fermenters, and seal-tenders. Generations have made them economical in movement and attentive to sensations that an outsider would miss. They read vibration through fingertips and boot soles, recognize pumps by rhythm, and notice pressure changes in the teeth. Their language outdoors is supplemented by touch, lamp patterns, and signals tapped into rails. Faces are most often seen through curved glass, flattened by helmet plates or distorted in the windows of connecting tunnels.

Outside, they wear patched brass-and-ceramic diving shells, each identified by its constellation of white lamps. These lights are more personal than color: a family arrangement inherited and modified, a trade sign at the wrists, mourning shown by extinguishing one lamp. Workers rarely travel alone. They move in small, tethered groups, scraping vents, trimming kelp, checking seams, and cutting new passages through buildings whose old entrances have become unusable. In the green-black streets, a person is first perceived as five or six pale points maintaining their distance from one another.

Their culture is shaped by managed slowness. They do not ask whether a structure can be saved, but how long it should be allowed to remain. Surveyors may watch a wall deform over an entire career, handing its measurements to their successors. A pipe gardener encourages mineral crust along one section while stripping it from another. Children learn the municipal pulse codes before they learn maps, because maps are provisional, while one beat, two beats, and the warning shiver can keep them alive.

Much of their food is grown directly on the quarter’s machinery. They eat translucent kelp ribbons, algae scraped from appointed roofs, fermented sponge tissue, and the dark colonial fronds called water ferns. Their cultivators work with blades, suction cups, and precise absences: every harvest opens space for the next layer of growth. A good gardener knows not only what to plant but what to permit to die, and where its decay will be useful downstream.

The quarter’s most numerous inhabitants cannot be seen individually. Bacteria carpet the streets, digest waste in crypts, bind sediment, precipitate lime, and bloom in the pores of old brick. Hydroids close their red fans over abandoned lintels. Sponges occupy window bays in orderly orchards. Algal membranes sheath roofs and engines. Rust itself is husbanded on iron lattices, forming ocher reefs that draw unwanted metals from the water. These organisms do not merely surround the settlement. They are its filters, insulation, farmland, foundations, and slow digestive organs.

Larger animals survive only as exceptions. Blind shrimp inhabit the chambers beneath the post office, pale and secretive, feeding on some process no one has yet identified. Translucent eels occasionally appear in warm conduits and are treated as both contamination and omen. Most revered is the broad-shelled grazer of the civic dome, ancient or merely slow, whose months-long passage leaves a clean pale road through the growth. Its arrival at the eastern rim begins the local year. No one owns it, feeds it openly, or admits to clearing obstacles from its path.

And something may inhabit the Delay.

Children say the lingering reflections in the central square are not repetitions. They claim the flashes answer one another, changing after the original lamps have gone dark. Some leave simple patterns against the motionless plane and return days later to watch the delayed light. Engineers call this superstition, yet route every important system away from the square and forbid unattended instruments from facing it. Older residents sometimes stand before the plane without signaling at all, as though waiting to be seen from another moment.

Whether or not anything lives there, the quarter itself behaves like a creature: drawing mineral water through tunnels, breathing violet oxygen from its stacks, digesting silt, growing carbonate armor, shedding rooms and roofs, and replacing every loss with a rerouted function. Its inhabitants live within that body and also compose it. Human hands trim its gardens; microbes line its vessels; dredges crawl like iron gut-creatures through its streets. Even the dead remain useful, descending as tissue and memory into the warm bacterial beds below.

What lives in the drowned quarter is therefore not simply a submerged community. It is an old city taught to metabolize its own ruin—and the quiet people, cultivated growths, rare animals, and perhaps delayed presences that have learned to metabolize with it.
