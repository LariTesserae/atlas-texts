Its citizens are builders without blueprints, tenants without property, and materials for structures they will never see completed.

Nearest the living walls are the fixed inhabitants. Coral polyps occupy the brightened rims of ancient towers, each animal nearly negligible alone, together laying down another thin season of stone. They extend their translucent crowns when the current brings food and withdraw at the first tremor of danger. Their dead remain beneath them, so each colony lives upon a compacted ancestry: generations converted into foundation.

Beside them grow other bodies that resemble plants only because they stay in place. Hydroids make stinging thickets. Feather worms lift ribbed fans from mineral tubes, sorting the water grain by grain before vanishing into their shafts. Soft colonies swell in sheltered chambers and slacken when the current changes, giving whole walls the slow appearance of lungs. Oysters fuse themselves into ridges. Tube-builders crowd upward in brittle neighborhoods, sealing abandoned chambers and creating new ones between their shells.

Sponges inhabit what looks solid. Some spread over exposed surfaces in low, colored crusts; others enter limestone and dissolve it from within. Water passes continuously through them, drawn into innumerable pores and expelled in clear streams. Their bodies are districts of canals rather than single rooms. Small shrimp, worms, and threadlike crustaceans live inside these channels, stealing food, shelter, or both. A sponge may be animal, aqueduct, apartment house, and agent of demolition at once.

The smallest inhabitants are almost everywhere. Bacteria veil warm fissures in rust, cream, and black-green. They inhabit the mud, the films on shells, the mouths and intestines of larger creatures, and the buried surfaces where no free oxygen reaches. Some live on what descends from the upper sea. Others feed on minerals carried from below. Around the seeps they form the first edible layer, converting stone-borne chemistry into flesh. Blind shrimp comb these mats with tireless limbs, and translucent crabs defend patches scarcely larger than their spread claws.

The mud has its own population, largely invisible. Bristle worms tunnel through it, swallowing sediment and leaving altered sediment behind. Clams lie buried with only paired siphons reaching upward. Small crustaceans occupy branching burrows whose entrances open and close with each passing body. Pale, soft animals move so slowly through the lower layers that their paths persist after they have gone. Some never encounter open water. To them the city is not arches and towers but a packed darkness crossed by gradients of warmth, salt, decay, and pressure.

Burrow-makers are followed by burrow-takers. A shaft excavated by a worm may later hold a shrimp, then a juvenile eel, then eggs guarded by a fish too large to turn around inside. Narrow hunters learn the old borings better than their makers did. They enter one opening and emerge from another face of the reef, tasting the water ahead. Some wait with only their mouths visible. Others patrol a private network of ducts, their skin made pale by generations spent among stone and shadow.

Crabs are the city’s restless custodians. They climb roofs, probe crevices, shear growth from walls, steal eggs, carry scraps, and are themselves carried away. Some are broad and armored, suited to exposed crossings. Some are nearly transparent and live among tentacles that would sting anything else. Decorator species fasten living fragments to their shells until they resemble pieces of the district they occupy. Hermits move through borrowed shells, making homes of the dead and surrendering those homes as they grow. Around them, tiny cleaners pick parasites from joints and gills, tolerated because their labor is useful.

The schooling fish inhabit open water as though it were another kind of building. They crowd the avenues in shifting thousands, silver flanks catching what little light descends. No single fish commands a turn. A pressure change travels through the school, and the whole mass folds around a tower, opens for a predator, or compresses until it seems one muscular animal with many eyes. At strong tide they face into the current and feed. At slack water they loosen into alleys and courts, searching the walls for smaller prey.

Grazers work along every reachable surface. Beaked fish scrape algae and living tissue from old carbonate, leaving pale scars and clouds of powdered wall. Snails travel more slowly, rasping films in narrow paths. Urchins occupy ledges after dark, their spines feeling ahead of them, their teeth reducing crust and stone alike. These animals are citizens and weather. Much of the sand below the towers has passed through their bodies.

In the courts live the concealed flat-bodied ones. Rays settle upon the black mud and pulse their fins until silt covers them. Their outlines disappear first, then their backs, until only raised eyes and breathing openings remain. They wait for buried shellfish or careless crustaceans. When they rise, the ground appears to lift with them. A single stroke leaves a hollow, a spreading brown cloud, and a temporary map of wing-shaped pressure in the surrounding water.

Predators occupy the city according to scale. Small ambushers hide in feeding fans, shell mouths, and sponge vents. Narrow-jawed fish hover beneath overhangs, motionless except for their gills. Eels hold chambers whose entrances are polished by repeated use. Larger hunters move the principal streets. Pale sharks and other long-bodied fish pass between towers with economical strokes, never hurrying until the instant of attack. Their arrival is known before they are clearly seen: schools draw tight, shrimp stop clicking, soft crowns vanish, and bioluminescent walls ignite in their wake.

Some hunters make light. They hang lures outside dark recesses or carry luminous organs beneath the eye or along the belly. The light is not illumination in any generous sense. It is bait, disguise, signal, warning, or momentary betrayal. Comb-jellies drift like clear vessels through the upper lanes and fracture blue when touched. Minute plankton answer turbulence with sparks. Small crustaceans release shining clouds to confuse pursuit. In the quarter whose walls flash green, every large passage is briefly recorded, the water remembering a body for several seconds after it has gone.

The city’s migrants are as important as its residents. Shell-bearing herds cross the outer plain along grooves worn by repeated journeys. Fish arrive to spawn in certain chambers and depart before their young hatch. Eels may spend years in one duct, then vanish into routes leading beyond the basin. Larvae of fixed animals drift everywhere: soft, minute claimants searching chemically and by touch for a surface on which to become immobile. Most are eaten. A few settle on roofs, rubble, bones, or the backs of slower creatures and begin new structures far from their parents.

There are opportunists that appear only after storms. They sense the changed water before the debris arrives. Burrow mouths open. Scavenging fish rise into the water column. Crabs abandon defended patches. When leaves and insects descend from the distant land, creatures that otherwise keep to separate levels meet in a climbing multitude. The feast is brief and dangerous. Hunters rise beneath it. Afterward, uneaten plant matter catches in fans and arches, where it softens into food for smaller mouths.

Death draws another succession. Scavengers attend a falling body in layers. Fish take eyes, fins, wounds, and exposed softness. Amphipods and isopods enter the openings. Crabs pull apart what reaches the floor. Worms and bacteria inherit what cannot be carried away. Shell and bone remain longest, but even these are bored, rasped, dissolved, encrusted, buried, and uncovered again. A rib becomes a brace for tubes. A skull becomes a nursery chamber. A shell shelters an animal, then anchors a colony, then vanishes into pavement.

Few inhabitants experience the whole city. A polyp knows the current across a few centimeters of wall. A shrimp knows the pores of one sponge and the timing of the fish that comes to be cleaned. A ray knows feeding courts by the taste of disturbed mud. A shark knows long routes between dense schools and warm fissures. A larva knows only descent, chemical invitation, and the final touch of a suitable surface.

Together these partial lives make the settlement. The grazers carve it; the fixed colonies raise it; the borers open it; the filter-feeders alter its currents; the burrowers ventilate its floor; the scavengers prevent anything from remaining merely dead. Predators regulate its crowds, and migrants connect distant quarters. Even parasites participate, passing through bodies that pass through streets, inhabiting the city by inhabiting its citizens.

No creature intends the towers, avenues, courts, or buried chambers. Each seeks food, safety, purchase, warmth, mates, or a place for eggs. Yet every choice leaves form behind. Repeated refuge becomes a polished opening. Repeated passage becomes a road. Feeding becomes excavation. Growth becomes architecture. Death becomes masonry.

What lives there is not simply an assembly of marine animals. It is a society without recognition, made coherent by current and appetite: innumerable private worlds overlapping until they produce a city-sized life.
