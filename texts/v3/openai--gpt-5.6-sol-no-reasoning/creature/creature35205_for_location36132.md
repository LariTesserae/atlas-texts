The valley is inhabited by remnants that have never completed becoming ghosts.

The pale birds on the shelves are the most visible of them. They resemble mountain scavengers enlarged by cold: broad-breasted, ash-white, with narrow, almost eyeless heads and wings that trail along the stone when folded. Yet they neither feed nor foul their ledges. No nests lie beneath them, no feathers drift down. Their bodies are not wholly present in any single place. What appears to be one bird may stand on several cliffs at different moments, its cry passing among those positions like a needle stitching them together. It does not fly. Flight is simply the part of its existence the valley omits.

The hoofed creatures are less complete. They live principally as tracks.

Something small and sure-footed circles the pools, drinking without disturbing their surfaces. It has a divided hoof, a low white body, and perhaps no head—or a head always lowered beyond sight. Its paths occur in fragments because only certain steps have settled into the world. Between the last print of one sequence and the first of another, the animal persists invisibly, crossing distances that never become traversed. The white shape sometimes seen on the flats is not necessarily the creature itself. It may be all those missing steps gathered into one motionless body.

The moss lives differently. Each black ring is an organism with no need to spread. Beneath its crisp circumference is a dense mesh of fibers penetrating frost, rock, and the dark water below. It draws nourishment not from light but from arrested motion: the unfallen impact, the suspended drop, the cold current denied expression at the pool’s surface. Its circles mark places where an event has been held long enough to become soil. If cut or burned, it is found intact again—not healed, but returned to the only condition in which it exists.

The translucent blades in the seams may be its fruiting bodies, though nothing here fruits. Their bubbles contain minute weather: a gust, a warm exhalation, a pressure change, each sealed before it could pass. Held against the ear, a blade gives off a faint sound like grass bending in a summer wind. Breaking one releases nothing. The valley retains both blade and fracture briefly, then keeps the blade.

The stone rings and the shelter are inhabited as well, though not by anything that can stand outside them.

Something sleeps in the shelter’s impossible interior. Its breathing is slow enough to mingle with the underground thunder, and the dripping heard beside it may be saliva, cave water, or a clock made from sound. It has no visible dimensions. The doorway is its mouth only in the sense that both are passages which return whatever enters. Those who lean into the darkness sometimes hear their own breathing from within, but deeper, heavier, and already asleep. The inhabitant may be the sleeper they would become if they remained in the valley long enough. It may be everyone who has ever rested there, compressed into a single unending sleep.

The cairn beside it is tended by an unseen gatherer. This being ranges farther than the valley’s laws appear to allow. It collects stones from coasts, volcanoes, riverbeds, vanished glaciers, and mountains that cannot be reached from the basin. But it does not carry them across distance. Rather, it persuades distant stones that they have always belonged together. The wet stone remembers the sea continuously; somewhere, perhaps, a corresponding hollow in a beach fills and empties around its absence. The gatherer is never seen because its work occurs only after it has been completed.

More pervasive than any of these is the life under the valley.

The subterranean thunder is not a heartbeat, although it can synchronize with one. Nor is it machinery, avalanche, river, or tectonic strain. It is the sound of an immense body attempting one movement. The cliffs are portions of that body exposed: shelves like joints, scree like scales, old ice banding it like cataracted tissue. The dry channels are grooves worn by impulses that never reached their extremities. What seems to be a basin may be the shallow impression around something buried face-up beneath the world.

It has been rising, perhaps. Or turning over.

Because motion cannot conclude here, the attempt has become permanent. Each pulse beneath the gravel is the same exertion, neither repeated nor finished. The waterfall’s absent impact, the answering cliffs, and the reversed footsteps are secondary effects of that struggle. Even the birds’ calls may be air driven through hollows in its buried frame.

At the center of the concentric gravel arcs is its nearest approach to the surface. The arcs are not constructed boundaries but pressure marks, like rings around a submerged eye. The bare circle remains empty because the thing below cannot occupy it without completing its movement. Rain fills the space instead, every drop preserving a different instant of the descent that once began there.

Within those drops lives the valley’s closest equivalent to a person.

The dark figure seen overhead is not a reflection, despite its obedience. It is the observer as registered by the place: not the body standing below, but the full shape of its actions, including those not yet performed and those already erased from memory. The figure lags when the valley has not finished receiving a movement. It leads when the movement has been accepted before the observer decides upon it. At times it may cease copying altogether and stand with its head inclined, listening to the thing beneath the gravel.

Anyone who remains eventually acquires such a counterpart. The valley does not keep arrivals as histories or corpses. It distributes them among its existing facts. A voice may become one of the cries from an empty ledge. A footfall may join the sequences around a pool. Breath may enter the shelter. Blood may supply the iron scent in the air without diminishing in the wound. A final glimpse of white clothing may persist on the flats, fixed at a distance no approach can close.

Thus the inhabitants are not a population but an ecology of arrested acts.

They eat without consumption, migrate without crossing, sleep without duration, and die without decay. Predator and prey cannot complete the exchange that would distinguish them. Birth, too, can only exist in fragments: a shell with nothing hatched from it, a print smaller than the others, a second cry nested inside the first. Nothing multiplies, yet the valley slowly becomes more crowded—not with bodies, but with unresolved evidence.

And beneath all of them lies the oldest inhabitant, still making the single movement that shaped the basin.

Perhaps it fell from above when there was still an above. Perhaps the mountains rose around it to contain the impact. Perhaps the world ended here and failed to finish ending.

Whatever it is, the valley is not its prison.

The valley is the portion of its life that has so far occurred.
