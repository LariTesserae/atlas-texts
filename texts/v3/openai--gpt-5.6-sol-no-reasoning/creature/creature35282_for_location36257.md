What lives here is small enough to be mistaken for an accident of the stone.

Most of it consists of solitary, nearly spherical cells suspended in the transient films along the fracture’s cooler lip. They have no stable colony, no continuous skin over the basalt. Each is a clear, soft bead clinging to a microscopic hollow or drifting in water only molecules deep. Their membranes are unusually dense and heat-resistant; within them, folded mineral-binding proteins protect the machinery of life from dissolved metals and abrupt swings in acidity.

They live on imbalance. Where hot basalt meets cooler infiltrating water, iron, sulfur, and hydrogen compounds pass between chemical states. The cells intercept those exchanges. They do not consume light, though light continually erupts around them. Instead, they harvest electrons released at the wet crystal faults, using the stone’s charge gradients to assemble the few compounds they need. A flare may energize them, sterilize them, or tear them loose. To survive is to remain near the luminous sites without occupying them.

There is no dependable boundary between dormancy and death. When the film evaporates, the cells contract into glassy spheres wrapped in silica and sulfur-bearing molecules. Some revive with the next condensation. Others remain sealed against the rock until heat breaks them down or a growing crystal encloses them permanently. Generations are brief and widely separated. Reproduction happens only during rare intervals when temperature, salinity, and charge align; then one translucent bead pinches into two, and the pair drift apart before the water disappears.

The fracture also receives a visitor.

It is a blind groundwater crustacean, thread-thin and almost colorless, narrow enough to pass through pores that would not admit a grain of coarse sand. Its shell is flexible rather than heavily armored, divided into faint glasslike segments. Long antennae extend ahead of it, tapping the basalt and recoiling from the sharpest edges. Short limbs comb wet surfaces for cells and mineral-coated organic traces. There is little to eat, so it wastes no movement.

It does not belong to the hottest part of the pocket. Usually it advances only until its heat-sensitive antennae encounter intolerable water, then folds backward into the pore from which it came. On rare visits, cooler groundwater briefly dilutes the pool margin. The animal enters farther, outlined by blue-white flashes passing through its transparent body. Its gut becomes the only dark line inside it. It grazes for moments, taking in a handful of cells and silica grains, then retreats as the water heats again.

Sometimes one fails to retreat.

The body cooks, clouds, and comes apart. Scavengers do not arrive. Dissolved minerals sheath the remains; heat and acidity dismantle them; the next influx scatters what is left. For a short time, its death feeds an unusual bloom of cells. Then even that abundance vanishes, leaving no visible stain.

Beyond these organisms, the stone itself can seem inhabited. Light runs from fault to fault in branching responses. A droplet touches one charged site; neighboring defects answer; a pale geometry wakes several centimeters deep and extinguishes in sequence. The pattern changes with every alteration of pressure and salinity. It possesses memory only in the physical sense: each discharge affects what can fire next. It senses nothing, wants nothing, and nevertheless behaves with enough intricacy to resemble a buried nervous system.

The actual inhabitants do not understand this radiance. The cells answer it chemically. The crustacean flinches from the heat that accompanies it. Neither sees the chamber as a chamber.

For the cells, existence is a succession of wet instants separated by desiccated suspension. For the animal, it is a dangerous widening in the groundwater maze, briefly rich in food. There is no creature here for whom the whole place exists at once.

When the seam finally seals, some cells may remain trapped in droplets within the basalt, dormant until pressure and chemistry erase them. If the fracture opens instead, the falling pressure will boil away their habitat. The last discharge will race through the fault network, and the remaining wet cells will be dried or destroyed in its wake. The crustacean, if one is present, may escape through the cooling pores—or become the largest inclusion sealed into the rock.

Until then, life persists sparsely: clear beads on black stone, and, once in many cycles, a pale articulated thread feeling its way through the light.
