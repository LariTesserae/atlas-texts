The town is inhabited by maintainers.

They are pump tenders, kiln crews, dredge hands, engine mechanics, barge clerks, signal operators, canal wardens, electricians, and painters forever covering rust before it becomes damage. They live in the raised houses behind the flood walls, on tiled floors without cellars, with boots drying on the upper steps and oilskins hung beneath shallow awnings. Their clothes smell of brine, damp concrete, furnace dust, and machine grease. Rust stains their palms; mineral water whitens the seams of their boots.

They are not hurried people. Urgency belongs to accidents, and the town has organized itself to prevent accidents from becoming events. Work proceeds by gauges, tide tables, sirens, and inherited sequences. At certain tones, gates are opened. At certain water marks, crews walk out with hooks and rakes. A stopped engine draws more attention than a storm. Children learn the three-part knock of a healthy pump before they learn the names of distant places.

Many families have served the same works for generations, though “generation” here feels less like succession than another shift reporting for duty. A daughter takes over the settling-pond records her father kept; a machinist uses gauges engraved with an uncle’s initials; apprentices learn to cast replacement parts from patterns older than anyone alive. The municipal storehouse is almost a civic shrine: racks of wooden forms, valve wheels, obsolete seals, numbered keys, and drawings specifying exactly how everything must be restored.

Their houses are spare but not barren. Caged fans turn in the windows. Enamel basins wait beneath persistent drips. Furniture stands on metal feet, and cupboards begin above the flood line. Kitchens hold jars of salt fish, pickled crab, chilies, and sour marsh greens. Radios murmur under the industrial bass. At night, families sit on their steps because the rooms retain the day’s heat, speaking across drainage channels while geckos hunt around the lamps.

The town’s children inhabit the unused edges of engineered space. They balance along flood walls, fish beneath warm-water outlets, collect blue-black shards of basalt from road cuts, and race bottle caps through gutters after rain. They know which settling ponds burn the skin, which pipes are merely warm, which yards are watched, and where bats sleep in the viaduct joints. They draw the town as six streets inside a circle, crossed by pipes and rails, with almost nothing beyond it but water.

Older residents read minute changes invisible to outsiders. They can hear a worn bearing through the general vibration, smell a clay batch fired too hot, or tell from the mosquitoes that a sump has been neglected. They distinguish rain on the foundry roof from rain on the salt sheds. Their memories attach not to transformations but to interruptions: the week Generator Three went silent, the night a canal gate jammed, the season the east kiln burned with an unfamiliar color.

Alongside them lives another population, equally adapted to the timetable. Gulls occupy roofs as if they were cliffs. Terns feed where the rakes expose small creatures in the ponds. Black waders step between rails, withdrawing moments before wagons move. Silver fish mass in artificial warmth; mud crabs climb ladders after dark; rats travel the elevated pipeways and warehouse shadows. Geckos station themselves beside green signal lamps. Bats emerge at the dusk whistle. The animals do not merely endure the works—they use them as habitat, recognizing waste chutes, valve cycles, and quiet intervals as natural rhythms.

Even the microorganisms belong to the settlement’s labor. Rust-red films tremble over saline ponds, thickening and thinning according to mineral flow. Lichen grips neglected roofs. Purslane stores water in soot-dusted leaves. Reeds hold the canal margins without being allowed to advance. Life survives here by occupying seams, outlets, ledges, sumps, and scheduled releases.

No single species rules the basin. The people restrain the marsh, but the marsh rises through their floors. Machines drive the pumps, but salt corrodes the machines. Birds consume the factories’ abundance; workers repair what the birds foul. Crabs block grates, crews clear them, and the next tide brings more crabs. Everything participates in the equilibrium, troublesome and necessary at once.

What lives here is therefore less a community than a continuous act of upkeep: human, mechanical, microbial, tidal. Its inhabitants are patient, damp, soot-marked, and intensely attentive. They preserve a town that never advances and never quite decays, listening through rain and engine noise for the smallest deviation from the pattern that keeps their shallow world in place.
