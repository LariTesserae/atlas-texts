The people of the Underwood are slight, long-limbed, and difficult to distinguish from the growth around them when they remain still. Their clothing is layered moss-felt, woven root fiber, and translucent sailback membrane, all pale greens and frost-whites. Loose fabric is dangerous, so every garment wraps tightly or trails in narrow streamers that indicate the direction and temper of the air.

They go barefoot whenever the cold permits. The sole is an ear here. Through skin pressed to bark, they feel strain, distant impacts, the movement of hidden water, and the first quick shiver of a coming rupture. Children learn the language of tension before they learn hammer speech. An adult may stop in mid-conversation, place one foot against a wall, and listen to something happening hundreds of kilometers away.

Their bodies bear the habits of suspension. Toes spread widely. Hands are strong, scarred, and seldom empty. Most wear harnesses even at home, fastening themselves to handrails with short braided cords. Falling is not considered clumsiness or misfortune but a constant appetite of the world. Every child is taught three holds, four knots, and how to sleep without unclenching from a vine.

No one knows how many communities inhabit the Underwood. Villages vanish behind new growth, drift apart, or wake grafted to strangers. A settlement may spend generations believing itself alone before a storm joins its branch to another forest and places unfamiliar doors within a day’s walk.

For that reason, the inhabitants do not define kinship entirely by blood. Kin are those held by the same wood.

A household may include relatives, travelers stranded by a fracture, children inherited with a fused raft, and sleepers discovered inside a reopened chamber. Names change when landscapes change. A person has a childhood name, a branch name, and often a storm name given after surviving some great rearrangement. The old names are not discarded; they accumulate like growth rings. Elders may need several breaths to introduce themselves fully.

They cultivate rather than farm. Gardens are folded into the host plant: cups of edible gel, strings of beadlike tubers, sweet bark blisters, and thin leaves that can be dried into insulating sheets. Gardeners guide sap by binding, scoring, shading, and singing against the wood. Their skill lies in asking without taking enough to weaken the structure.

The finest gardeners are called persuaders. They can make a branch widen into a floor, turn a scar outward into a rain cup, or encourage a root to follow a suspended cord across open air. Their work may require decades. It is never wholly obedient. A house requested from a trunk may become a stair, a bridge, or a chamber with no entrance. Good persuaders accept correction from the plant.

Fungi are tended by people who live nearer the warm interior chambers. Their skin is often stained amber or violet by spores, and they carry a sweet, damp smell rare in the open cold. They manage the slow rot that heats villages, knowing precisely how much dead tissue can be consumed before a supporting rib becomes unsafe. Outsiders sometimes distrust them because they work with decay, but no settlement survives without their hidden gardens.

Water-tasters memorize the pulse of buried rivers. They press lips to cracks, listening through their teeth, and identify a current by mineral sharpness, sap bitterness, fungal warmth, and rhythm. Their maps are sung rather than drawn because channels migrate with growth. A water song describes not fixed places but sequences: three slow pulses, a bitter vein, a downward fork, a chamber that breathes.

Messengers travel the great root networks with hammers of resonant wood. Most settlements know a common vocabulary of simple beats, but every branch alters sound, stretching one rhythm and swallowing another. Expert listeners recognize not only the message but the route it has taken. A warning may arrive carrying the acoustic signatures of several unknown forests through which it passed.

Sometimes the wood inserts pulses of its own.

These are called the unstruck answers. Some communities treat them as sacred speech. Others insist they are pressure shifts, feeding burrowers, or old signals trapped in loops of living tissue. Listeners who devote themselves to the answers become strange with age. They pause for hours against walls. They answer questions days late. They claim the Underwood does not speak in words but in inclinations: join, loosen, drink, bear, open.

They disagree most sharply over the dead.

In many villages, a corpse is bound to a young branch with the face turned toward the canopy. The branch slowly encloses the body, drawing minerals from it and growing around the bones. Years later it bears a cluster of white, bell-shaped fruits. These are not eaten. Their dried shells become cups, rattles, or masks, so the dead remain useful without being consumed.

Elsewhere, bodies are lowered into the void on germinating cords. As the corpse descends, seeds root in clothing and flesh, making a narrow hanging garden. Families return to tend it until distance and growth make descent impossible. Such gardens become green lines vanishing into the dark, their lowest leaves belonging to no known season.

The worst fate is not death but separation without witness. When someone falls, those above strike their name into the wood until the pulses fade. If no impact, answer, or snag is felt, the missing person is called still descending. They are not counted among the dead. Bowls may remain set aside for them across generations.

Travelers of the open air are unlike those who stay within branches. They are lean even by Underwood standards and often shave their heads so frost cannot gather in their hair. Their seed-sail baskets carry almost nothing: dried pulp, condensation cloth, a stone cutting edge, spare line, and a tiny hammer. Weight is measured with ruthless care. Sentimental objects are carved hollow.

They navigate by canopy contractions, spore drift, and the posture of distant forests. Since maps become false, they keep books of tendencies: where winds often turn, which ceiling wounds open together, what kinds of root masses follow certain storms. A skilled air traveler can look at a hanging forest on the horizon and tell whether it is approaching, receding, rotating, or being born.

They fear calm more than wind. In a dead current, a seed sail may hang for weeks over nothing, moisture exhausted and every visible landform remaining the same size. Travelers caught there sometimes cut pieces from their own craft to make smaller drifting seeds, releasing messages in the hope that one will touch a root.

A few people live almost entirely in flight. These drift clans lash several seed sails together and cultivate light plants along the basket rims. They catch ice grains in fine nets, hunt winged condensation insects, and steer by altering the growth of their living rigging. They land only to repair, trade, give birth, or bury someone in wood. Settled people admire them, distrust them, and accuse them of forgetting how to listen through their feet.

The sailbacks are the only animals they regard as older inhabitants rather than prey or pests.

A sailback may carry an entire ecology across its wings: fern mats, translucent mites, little flowering bladders, and pools no wider than a hand. When one lands, specialists called quieters approach without tools. They remove broken vines, comb ice from breathing slits, and gather only membrane already peeling from the animal’s trailing edges.

Some sailbacks return to the same forest after intervals longer than a human life. Their wing gardens may then contain plants from continents that no longer exist. Seeds collected from them are planted ceremonially. Occasionally one germinates into a species known only from songs.

Children are warned never to wake a resting sailback, but they are also told a gentler story: that every creature or person who falls far enough lands eventually on the back of one passing below. This is why sailbacks never come down empty.

Other animals are less welcome. Bark-burrowers can hollow a handrail from within. Frost grazers devour condensation films before villagers can collect them. Warmth-seeking fliers cluster near fungal chambers and suffocate sleepers if not driven out. Yet even these are rarely exterminated. Their waste feeds gardens, their tunnels become capillaries, and their sudden migrations may warn of structural failure.

The inhabitants possess no single religion, but nearly all their beliefs turn around support.

They do not ask who made the world. They ask what holds it.

To bear weight is virtuous. To distribute strain is wisdom. To take more warmth, water, or strength than one returns is the nearest thing to sin. Promises are made by fastening two cords to the same living branch. Marriage, adoption, and political alliance use the same rite: each party leans outward until the cords grow taut, trusting the others to counterbalance them.

Their oldest stories concern the first people waking inside a sealed trunk and cutting toward the light. Another tradition says people descended from the canopy as fruit, but forgot what they were before ripening. Some claim humanity came from below and has been climbing ever since. No account explains why human bones are so readily accepted into new wood.

They count time by canopy dimmings, growth flushes, condensation cycles, and great storms. Age is less important than continuity. An old person is not merely someone who has lived many years, but someone whose remembered world no longer touches the present one. Such elders recall vanished rafts, extinct water tastes, and routes through forests now scattered across the void.

During calm periods, villages exchange songs, fungal cultures, tools, spouses, and cuttings. Feasts are held in the warm chambers, where people unwrap from their tight outer garments and sit close enough to share heat. Food is served in soft leaf bowls: bitter root paste, frozen sap shaved into curls, warm fungal loaves, insect eggs, and rare sweet nodules from the ceiling-fed trunks.

Music is percussive because percussion travels. Small instruments imitate the speech of branches: clicking seed shells, bone pegs, taut root strings, hollow wood struck underwater. Songs performed for those in the room are quiet and intricate. Songs intended for distant settlements are simplified into rhythms the forest can carry.

Their art often disappears by design. Patterns are cut shallowly into bark so growth will swallow them. Faces are braided from young shoots and allowed to distort as the stems thicken. A masterpiece is admired not for remaining unchanged, but for producing an interesting scar.

Only the most stubborn artists work in stone.

When the black rising storms begin, every distinction between profession, family, and belief collapses into preparation. Gardens are cut loose before they can become sails. Warm chambers are banked. Doors are opened rather than sealed, allowing pressure to pass through. Children and water cultures are moved to the strongest interior ribs. Hammer messages begin and continue until the trunk’s shaking overwhelms them.

Afterward, survivors crawl into a world that may contain unfamiliar horizons.

They strike the wood beneath them.

The first answering beats determine everything: who remains, what has joined, what has broken, where water still moves. Sometimes replies come from known villages, their rhythms distorted by new paths. Sometimes there are no replies at all.

And sometimes the answer is close, strong, and perfectly clear, coming from inside a fresh root mass that did not exist before the storm.

Those who cut into such growth have found chambers already shaped like dwellings. They have found ladders with no builders, warm pits without fungi, objects enclosed in pale wood, and marks resembling hands with too many fingers. Once, according to widely repeated testimony, a new branch opened by itself and released three people dressed in unknown fiber. They were alive, frost-covered, and unable to understand any spoken or hammered language.

They pointed downward whenever asked where they had come from.

Whether they were strangers, ancestors, or something grown in the likeness of people remains unresolved. The Underwood closes evidence quickly. The chamber that held them thickened, sealed, and became load-bearing before anyone could study it.

That is the deepest unease of those who live there: not that the green world is hostile, but that it is attentive.

It catches their water, carries their voices, closes around their dead, and reshapes itself beneath their weight. It makes rooms where rooms are needed. It builds bridges toward burdens. It answers blows no hand has struck.

And after every catastrophe, when the survivors beat their names into the roots, something vast beneath or above them seems to listen—then grows.
