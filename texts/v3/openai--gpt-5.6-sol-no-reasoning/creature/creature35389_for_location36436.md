The inhabitants are not a population so much as an accumulation: animals gathered wherever the town permits stillness, fitted into its architecture like a second, softer masonry.

Pale moths line the warehouses. They begin at the concrete floors and continue up brick piers, across beams, around skylights, and into the rafters, each resting so closely against the next that their folded wings form a continuous nacreous surface. Seen from a doorway, a warehouse appears packed with some fibrous, breathing mineral. Each moth is warm. Each has weight. Dust clings to the fur of its thorax. Yet none settles, wakes, or dies. A few remain caught in flight, wings raised or lowered, occupying the air as firmly as fixtures bolted to the walls. Their voices persist as a low collective combustion.

In the rail yards live long-legged beetles, black at the joints and pale along their narrow backs. They stand between sleepers in disciplined ranks, all oriented along the tracks as though migration had become confused with timetabling. Their feet grip rusted rails and gray ballast. Antennae lean into the vibration of the mills but never quiver. They leave clear the points, crossings, and wheel paths, obeying an industrial order older or more absolute than instinct.

The canal and drains belong to transparent crabs. Their shells are clear enough to show dark organs suspended within, though nothing pulses there. They crowd the smoked water in tiers, those beneath supporting those above, until the canal seems paved with glass bodies. Raised pincers hold silt, droplets, scraps of black foam, and occasionally one another. Their clicking continues without any visible movement of claw or mouth. In the inspection pits they stare upward through the water, packed so tightly that their eyes resemble beads laid in oil.

On the roofs lie the heavy gray creatures. They are roughly seal-shaped, but no shore could have made them. Their hides are smooth, cool, and faintly bristled along the spine. Six small feet clasp corrugations, gutters, ridge tiles, and chimney braces with delicate fingers. Their lidless eyes face different directions, making every roof appear watchful. Colonies sag the old structures beneath their combined mass. Deep calls issue from their open throats in perfect union with the compressors, as though the machines breathe through them—or they through the machines.

The cable birds are the most numerous and the least convincingly alive. Their bodies have the narrow sheen of drawn metal; their translucent vanes suggest feathers remembered by glass. They crowd every wire without causing it to dip farther. Some have spread wings, but these do not balance or beat. Some hold their beaks open around notes so sharp that the electrical hum seems merely their lower register. The cables tremble. The birds do not.

Other forms occupy the margins between recognizable kinds. White articulated predators crouch under awnings with too many joints folded inside their limbs. Soft, eyeless things fill warm recesses behind boilers. Bristled animals shaped like wedges press themselves beneath loading platforms. Small faces appear among pipe clusters, attached to bodies hidden by valves and flanges. There are creatures that resemble unfinished mammals, creatures built around a single polished shell, and creatures whose limbs divide repeatedly into finer gripping parts. Each is complete. None shows youth, injury, hunger, age, or repair.

They neither compete with the machinery nor avoid it by chance. Belts pass through untouched corridors in the densest colonies. Gauges remain visible. Service hatches can open. Chains travel within clear loops bordered by motionless wings and claws. The inhabitants fill all places not required by the town’s operation, as though poured into the remaining volume and allowed to set.

Predators keep their teeth against prey. Moths remain just above hot housings without scorching. Canal creatures hold droplets that do not fall. Chests are arrested halfway through expansion. Muscles preserve the tension of springing, feeding, fleeing, mating, calling. Their warmth is not generated and their odor does not disperse. They possess life as a permanent material property, the way iron possesses hardness.

Their only activity is sound.

No throat contracts, no wing vibrates, no shell strikes another, yet every interrupted call extends without exhaustion. Together the voices form an ecology without behavior: barking in the roofs, clicking underground, furnace-roaring in the warehouses, needlelike cries along the wires. The calls lock into the machinery so precisely that it is impossible to know which sound belongs to which body. Perhaps the fauna gathered around compatible frequencies. Perhaps the frequencies produced bodies to contain them. Perhaps each animal is the visible shape of one component in the town’s single sustained noise.

Around the great valve in the square, thousands of the smallest inhabitants face inward. They are different species, if species has any meaning here: miniature crabs, blunt-headed birds, six-footed gray pups, white segmented hunters, moths with wings open like pages. All direct their eyes and mouths toward the bent spoke. Their unending cry has neither alarm nor worship in it, because both would require change. It is simply the cry appropriate to the incomplete turn.

Nothing governs these creatures. Nothing tends them. Nothing is born among them, and nothing can be removed by decay. They are not waiting to resume. Resumption would mean that their arrested gestures belonged to a past and pointed toward a future, but the raised claw, the descending wing, and the closing teeth are whole conditions in themselves.

What lives in the basin is life released from sequence: abundant, warm, vocal, and forever incapable of becoming anything else.
