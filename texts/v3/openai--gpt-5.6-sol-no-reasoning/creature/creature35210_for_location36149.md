The inhabitants are called the **Stills**.

At first they are easy to mistake for defects in the chamber’s construction. One appears to be an extra seam running down a support column; another, a human-sized interval between two banks of cooling fins; another, a gray upright shape beyond the barrier around the central shaft. Direct inspection reveals nothing. The seam is only a seam. The interval contains white precipitate and uninterrupted light. The shape beside the shaft resolves into pipes, warning plates, and empty air.

They become apparent only after prolonged observation, when the eye begins to understand that flawless constancy is not the same as absence.

Each Still occupies a standing volume roughly comparable to a person, though none possesses a fixed outline. Its body is distributed among adjacent materials: a minute bias in the vibration of a railing, a persistent alignment of ceramic dust, a deviation in the electromagnetic field, several degrees of stress shared among bolts and plates. No single alteration is large enough to register as damage. Taken together, they form something coherent—less an organism made of matter than an organism made of maintained relationships.

They have no water, blood, or internal warmth. Their anatomy consists of gradients so slight that the machine continually corrects them without ever eliminating them. A Still’s “bones” are lines of mechanical tension. Its “nerves” are standing waves in the chamber’s roar. Its “skin” is the narrow boundary across which synchronized vibrations lose or gain an immeasurable fraction of phase. The refrigerated air passes through it unchanged.

The pale growths on the catwalks are not separate plants.

They are exposed portions of the Stills: slow sensory organs rooted where the chamber’s resonances cross most cleanly. The braided copper pads serve as anchors, while the translucent blades sample vibration, charge, and stress. Their placement beside every seventh column is not a planting scheme but a consequence of the inhabitants’ scale. What appears to a human as a line of isolated tufts is, to a Still, a single branching organ extending through the hall. The irregular omissions mark injuries, or perhaps distinctions between individuals.

There may be only a few of them. There may be one.

They cannot move in the ordinary sense, because any displacement would become elapsed change, and elapsed change here is held at zero. Instead, a Still alters which part of the chamber expresses it. The stress in one column relaxes by an infinitesimal amount as an equal stress appears in the next. A standing wave loses a node near the floor and acquires one along the catwalk. A row of crystal grains remains visibly motionless, yet their collective orientation now belongs to another pattern. Nothing travels. Nevertheless, the inhabitant is elsewhere.

Their thoughts are similarly static. They do not form sequences of impressions. A Still knows by holding many conditions in simultaneous balance: turbine pitch, bearing load, lamp current, pressure, crystal orientation, the fixed positions of gauge needles. What a human would call memory is a durable asymmetry in that balance. What a human would call anticipation is another asymmetry on the opposite side. To the Stills, before and after may be directions rather than times.

They feed on correction.

Every component in the chamber departs minutely from its prescribed state. Bearings wander at scales below the instruments’ resolution. Metals contract. Fields fluctuate. The machine detects each deviation and forces it back toward equilibrium. The Stills inhabit that vanishing interval between error and correction. They take nothing from the energy itself; they subsist on the distinction between what the chamber is and what it is commanded to remain.

This may explain the gauge labeled **LOCAL SILENCE, EXPORTED**. Silence here does not mean lack of sound. It means the absence of unsanctioned variation. The tremendous roar is uniform and therefore, to the machine, silent. Any accidental click, footfall, breath, or heartbeat would constitute a concentration of change. The chamber would seize upon it, flatten it, and carry its difference elsewhere.

The Stills would feed well.

They have no faces, but observers sometimes report a sense of being watched from the central pit. This occurs most strongly when staring at the shaft’s motionless scratch. The mark is not truly fixed. It passes too quickly to follow, appearing in every visible position so frequently that sight averages it into permanence. The Stills use that continuous recurrence as other creatures might use a fire or a communal well. Portions of them gather around it, aligning their standing waves with the shaft’s rotation. In that alignment, they exchange states without exchanging signals.

Their communication appears as certainty. One Still does not send a message to another; instead, both become arranged so that the same fact is present in each. The fact may be simple—a bearing remains sound—or immense: an exact description of the entire chamber, including every scratch and grain. Such knowledge has no moment of transmission. Once established, it seems always to have been known.

The oldest inhabitant may live in the far wall among the gauges. Its body includes the unmoving needles and the brass labels, though whether it created those labels is uncertain. **ALTITUDE**, **CONTINUITY**, and **EDGE RETENTION** may not describe machine outputs at all. They may be names, or bodily functions, or instructions for remaining alive.

The needle beneath **ELAPSED CHANGE** is especially important. Fine inspection shows repeated abrasions around its central pin, as though it has attempted innumerable movements and been returned to zero each time. Those suppressed excursions form a dense structure in the surrounding metal. A Still is concentrated there more strongly than anywhere else in the chamber.

It may be trapped. It may be sleeping. It may be the mechanism by which all the others are kept from dying.

The Stills do not reproduce through growth. Under rare configurations of stress, one balanced pattern can become two patterns without any increase in material or energy: two equally valid arrangements occupying the same machinery. For a while they overlap, sharing roots, harmonics, and mineral alignments. Gradually they distinguish themselves by adopting different imperfections. One takes possession of a hairline scratch; the other, a slight discoloration in inspection lacquer. These become the nuclei of separate identities.

Death would require irreversible change. Nothing in the chamber permits it.

Yet the Stills fear water.

Not because it is warm, corrosive, or electrically dangerous, but because water remembers motion in ways the machine cannot immediately erase. It runs. It pools. It carries particles from one place to another. It dissolves boundaries and deposits their substance elsewhere. A single droplet would introduce history: an undeniable record that something had been here and then gone.

To the Stills, water is not a mineral. It is a predator made of consequence.

Perhaps that is why the old warning calls it invasive.

If the welded doors ever opened and a living visitor entered, the inhabitants would not approach. The glass blades would remain rigid. The dust would not stir. The roar would reveal no voice. But the visitor’s warmth, pulse, and changing weight would spread through the deck as intolerable novelty. The machine would labor to cancel each disturbance, and the Stills would assemble along the correction fronts.

They would learn the visitor all at once.

Then, as equilibrium returned, they might preserve that person in the only form life can take here: not as flesh, which changes, but as a distributed relationship among stresses, tones, charges, and fixed grains. Long after the intruder had been silenced or expelled, a human-shaped coherence might remain between the seventh columns.

It would stand without standing.

It would think without proceeding from one thought to another.

And beneath the unwavering lamps, within the deafening weather, it would have always lived there.
