What lives there is not a collection of species so much as a single dispersed creature, its body divided among clay, water, feather, shell, and rule.

The slate-backed marshers are its quick blood. They sleep beneath the fitted plates in the warmless dark, wedged into gaps between cistern cells, their six legs folded along their flanks and their punt-tails hooked against the partitions. Pressure wakes them. When a cell fills and the floor shifts, they are squeezed upward through seams scarcely wider than their skulls. They arrive slick with black water, pause as if listening through all six feet, then spill into the channels.

Their hard mouthplates do more than feed. By clicking them against particular ridges, they sound the depth beneath each tile. A full cell answers with a dull note, an empty one with a dry ring, and a cracked partition trembles discordantly. The marshers gather where the tone is wrong. They probe the joint, swallow loosened silt, and pack their mineral-laden droppings into leaks. Thus they browse upon the court and repair it at once. Their endless clattering is appetite, conversation, and masonry.

They breed communally at the central gutter crossing. No pair keeps its own eggs. The mound there contains translucent beads, chalky shells, waxed capsules, and soft black knots deposited by things that never approach one another anywhere else. The marshers continually turn this mass with their narrow forelegs. They eat the spoiled eggs first, then the healthy ones when hunger requires it. What hatches does so among the remains of what did not. The court wastes almost nothing.

The crabs in the wall cups are its nerves.

Each occupies a hollow polished to the shape of its body. Their legs rest against the brick and their smaller claws grip the reed layer behind it. They can distinguish rain, footsteps, rising water, a bird’s strike, and the faint grinding of a failing wall seam. Messages pass through them faster than any animal can run: one crab taps, three answer, and soon the enclosure rattles with knowledge.

They are irritable and territorial in still weather, fencing at the rims of neighboring cups. During a flood they abandon those boundaries instantly. They climb the canopy poles and hang together beneath the cloth, legs interlaced, becoming a single many-jointed cluster. When the water falls, each returns to its own cavity without apparent confusion. A cup may pass through hundreds of occupants and yet preserve one rhythm, repeated by each new crab as though the brick itself taught the pattern.

The pale drain worms are quieter but more essential. They hang head-down in tassels, drinking the slow wall runoff and combing particles from it with transparent cilia. Their bodies contain strings of grit. When mature, they withdraw into the conduits and die there, leaving narrow mineral sleeves that keep the soft passages from collapsing. Much of the hidden plumbing is lined with generations of worms.

The moths are the court’s weather made animate.

By day they lie between reed courses, fitted wing to wing in the walls. Their waxy scales seal the masonry and shed condensation toward the drains. At dusk they emerge carrying the smell of pitch. They swarm the five clear flowers, but the plant is not their sole object. They drink mineral beads from the rope, scrape salts from the birds’ legs, and press their abdomens into hairline cracks to lay eggs. Their larvae eat softened reed and exude a dark adhesive behind them. They consume the walls slowly and preserve them as they go.

The translucent plant lives on this disturbance. Its roots pass through a drain seam into the buried water and spread there without soil, pale as drowned fingers. It flowers only when the cistern beneath it reaches a particular level. The five bells are therefore not merely blossoms but gauges. Moths read them as abundance. Crabs answer their opening with a soft, rapid tapping. Even the birds lower their heads when the bells unfold.

The tall hunting birds are the court’s most conspicuous inhabitants and perhaps its least free.

They hatch near the central seam like everything else, absurdly small and almost legless. Juveniles hide in the kiln chamber, feeding on worms brought up by floodwater and on marshers dropped by adults. Their legs lengthen first; wings come later. For a season they resemble walking needles, stepping with grave care across channels they could not survive falling into.

As adults, they patrol fixed routes. Each bird knows a sequence of plates and rarely deviates from it. Their hollow knocks drive marshers out of the seams, but the hunt also prevents too many bodies from clogging the gutters. Their droppings enrich the circulating water. Their discarded feathers are dragged below by the marshers and packed against cistern joints. Their barking, magnified by the sloping walls, makes the moths settle and the crabs sound alarm.

They can fly. They do not depart.

At the rim, their wingbeats falter—not from any visible barrier, but from inheritance reinforced each day. The open space above the wall is not sky to them. It is the place without returning echoes, without clay underfoot, without the enclosing answer of the court. A bird rising toward it receives none of the sounds by which it locates itself. It turns back in terror and relief.

The same boundary governs the smaller lives. The moths descend when the wall ceases to answer their wingbeats. Spiders anchor every line inward because outward threads return the wrong vibration. The marshers, carried experimentally to the upper bricks by flood or talon, throw themselves back into the court.

Older than any one animal is the hanging rope.

It began as twisted rush, perhaps a handhold or measuring line. Now silk, scale, shell, dung, mineral, and abandoned egg cases have thickened it into a suspended reef. Mites live in its dry upper ridges. Tiny blind beetles tunnel through its damp underside. Moth pupae stud it like knots. Spiders inhabit its branching fibers, and droplets travel along it in dependable paths. When the wind sets it moving, eggs hatch. When its dripping quickens, the marshers change channels. The rope has become a calendar the whole court can feel.

And something else lives below.

It has no single opening through which it can be seen. It declares itself in displaced water, in the lifting of plates, in the padded booming from beneath. One might call it only the cistern settling. Yet its movements are too patient to dismiss easily. Cells fill in sequences. Pressure travels against the slope. A plate sometimes rises on a dry morning and remains tilted until a cluster of marshers gathers around it. Black water wells up, bearing fragments ground smoother than circulation alone should make: reed fiber, shell, ceramic dust, minute bones.

Perhaps there is a colony beneath the floor, blind and spread through the partitions—a soft-bodied life occupying the cistern as fungus occupies wood. Perhaps the water itself has thickened into a communal organism, full of eggs, spores, dissolved remains, and memory carried chemically from chamber to chamber. Perhaps the builders placed something there before sealing the final plates.

Whatever it is, the animals attend it.

They run toward its thuds rather than away. Crabs relay the impacts through the walls. Moths descend and drink from the new seepage. Birds stand around a lifted tile with their necks folded, waiting for prey but also watching, as if at a familiar rite. The rules of the court may be its rules: where feet may pass, where hunting must cease, where eggs must be brought, how high a wing may rise.

The absent makers survive within those laws. Their thumbprints shelter larvae. Their niches preserve tools no animal soils. Their platform divides movement as firmly as a wall. They may have built the court as a reservoir, a breeding pen, a sanctuary, an experiment, or a machine whose working parts were always intended to be alive. They are gone, but the gestures of their hands persist as animal instinct.

So the true inhabitant is difficult to name.

It is the marshers sounding the tiles and sealing their seams. It is the crabs carrying warnings through brick. It is worms dying into conduits, moths repairing what they eat, birds pruning the abundance that feeds them. It is the root in the drain, the reef on the rope, the black recirculating water and the unseen pressure beneath it. It is also the pattern of avoidance—the untouched tools, the inviolate chamber, the uncrossed platform, the rim no wing will pass.

The court lives there.

At dawn it appears empty only because most of its body has withdrawn into walls and floor. Then the buried cells contract. A plate lifts like a lid or an eyelid. Water comes through, dark and cold, carrying the court’s old taste.

Every creature hastens toward it because each recognizes the summons.

Or because, together, they are the thing that has awakened.
