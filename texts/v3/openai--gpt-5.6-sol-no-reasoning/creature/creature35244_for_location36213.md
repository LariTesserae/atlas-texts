No separate people inhabit this city. The inhabitants are the city itself: a vast, entangled commonwealth of plants, each organism at once citizen, structure, mechanism, and law.

They do not possess fixed bodies in the way animals might. One can be a tower in the morning, a bridge by afternoon, and by night a row of rooted ribs radiating from the place of its collapse. Their identities pass through grafts. Water, pressure, nutrients, and perhaps memory move between them until it becomes difficult to say where one individual ends. Some may be ancient, but nothing about them looks old; they survive by repeatedly abandoning their surfaces.

They sense without eyes or ears. Light tells them where space is available. Weight reveals what must be reinforced. Moisture summons mouths, funnels, and drinking towers. Crowding provokes walls, thorns, shade, or retreat. Their messages travel as tension through joined roots: a tightening beneath one district becomes an instruction elsewhere to turn leaves, redirect water, dismantle a shaded quarter, or enclose a volume of vacant air. Thought, if that is what occurs, is distributed through strain and growth.

Their speech is physical and deafening. Hollow groves breathe storms. Seed pods answer with volleys of cracks. Leaf masses applaud. Vines hum and trunks strike one another. Beneath these sharper sounds lies the continuous bass of tissue forcing itself into existence. They have no need to converse above the noise because the noise is partly the consequence of their conversation.

They are also their own climate. In the absence of animals—and apparently of fungi or other decomposers—they perform every service themselves. They make wind by breathing, distribute pollen by collision, carry seeds in roads, catch rain before it touches earth, and digest their dead directly into neighboring roots. Nothing remains a corpse for long. Death is liquefaction, partition, and civic reuse.

There are many kinds among them. Reservoir plants hold ponds in translucent leaves. Migratory bowls cooperate to make the wandering river. Resonant columns drive air across the basin. Braided vines raise towers around cores of emptiness. Chambered succulents form temporary interiors that resemble rooms and corridors, though these are digestive or circulatory spaces as much as architecture. Defensive plants arrange themselves around nurseries. Great flowers become plazas, exposing communal interiors and pendulous organs that scatter luminous dust over whole districts.

Smaller inhabitants live upon the larger ones, but they too are plants. Gardens occupy fan-leaf platforms; gardens grow upon those gardens; mosses contain miniature avenues and rotundas. The distinction between parasite, tenant, organ, and neighborhood may have no meaning here. Scale repeats so precisely that the basin’s entire civilization might itself be an epiphyte inside a larger leaf.

Their relationship with space is stranger still. Some species appear able to graft not only tissue but location. They pull distant places together, open interiors larger than their bodies, and overlap without displacement. A plant expelled from the present may persist as a former district inside a raindrop. Others seem to cultivate depth, inversion, or impossible adjacency as readily as ordinary roots cultivate soil. What appears to an outsider as warped geography may be one of their principal resources.

They have no rulers. Governance emerges from gradients: sunlight, thirst, pressure, density, and balance. Yet the resulting order is unmistakably civic. Functional guilds gather along waterways and conduits. Nurseries receive protection. Root avenues redistribute bodies and materials. Unproductive districts are dismantled. Empty regions are colonized. Conflict takes the form of accelerated growth, shade, constriction, flowering, and the forced revision of space.

At times the entire population enters a coordinated convulsion. Whether this is argument, reproduction, defense, seasonal change, or thought at the scale of the basin cannot be known. Streets heave, canopies rotate, hedges migrate, plazas close into fruits, and whole forests briefly assemble into single organs. Individual forms become subordinate to a larger event. Then the city stills, rearranged, and its inhabitants resume their patient exchanges of pressure and water.

They do not aspire to permanence. Their civilization is founded on continual replacement. A finished quarter is merely tissue awaiting reassignment; a building is a phase in an organism’s posture; a road is a moving vascular agreement. Even their apparent history is not a stable sequence but a reservoir of discarded possibilities, briefly recoverable after rain.

They are builders without separating building from birth, citizens without separating body from city, and perhaps minds without separating thought from weather. They live by making the place that makes them—and because neither organism nor metropolis is ever allowed to become complete, they are always arriving.
