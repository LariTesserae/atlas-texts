The Verdigris House is inhabited, though not by anything that can be cleanly separated from the place.

Its most obvious inhabitants are the plants: the black-leaved congregations, the vapor-drinking filaments, the ribbed trunks and bolted roots. Yet no individual growth appears to possess a complete life of its own. A vine cut in one aisle reacts three rooms away. Pale nodules contract along distant stems in sequence, passing a signal through walls, tanks, and stone. When rain begins, leaves throughout the chamber turn their mirrored undersides upward—not merely beneath the shower, but ahead of it, tracing the path the water will take.

Together, the vegetation forms something like a single colonial organism. Its roots occupy the station’s conduits as nerves occupy a body. Rust travels through its tissues. Mineralized leaves accumulate as memory. What the garden knows, it preserves by becoming stone.

The machinery is part of that organism, or the organism is part of the machinery. The distinction changes with the pumping cycle.

While the station sleeps, the growths seem dominant. Roots ease into relay cabinets. Green light moves through buried networks. Stems swell against valve levers with pressure too slight to move them but sustained for decades. In these intervals the engine appears dead, merely a trellis around which life has arranged itself.

When current returns, this relationship reverses. Roots tighten in obedience to vibration. Leaves align with humming pipes. Seed capsules open on command. The whole garden becomes an auxiliary system of filters, diaphragms, reservoirs, and sensing organs. The plants do not endure the machine’s awakening. They participate in it.

Its inhabitants may therefore be best understood as organs of the House.

The fan-leaved colonies on the walls are its lungs. They drink vapor during the long stillness and release it when pressure falls, giving the local rains their sudden boundaries.

The mirror-backed leaves are its instruments. Their bright undersides gather weak light and direct it toward growths deeper in the machinery. During the active cycle they tremble at distinct frequencies, each patch reading a different pipe.

The translucent pads inside control housings are its insulating tissue. They hold small electrical charges for months, then discharge them in sequence when the lamps begin to redden.

The rope-thick descending stems are its tendons. Some terminate around seized wheels and have been tightening so gradually that iron moves without ever appearing to move.

The pale nodules along the vines are its relays. When opened, they contain neither seed nor sap, but concentric mineral wafers divided by films of living green.

The fruit-like bodies full of coiled fibers are unfinished replacements. They ripen beside frayed cables and split only when a conductor fails. Their fibers creep into the break, harden around it, and carry a weak current until metal can grow there.

Even the empty nests and vegetable shells have a function. They are rehearsals.

The House remembers animals without containing them. Somewhere in its inherited design are impressions of wings, mouths, jointed legs, warm blood, eyes that turn toward motion. Unable to restore what is absent, the vegetation repeats those forms in leaf and fiber. A curled frond resembles a sleeping vertebrate until it opens into a pressure gauge. A cluster of pods hangs like chrysalides, but inside each is a small lattice fitted precisely to an unknown socket. Segmented green limbs slowly flex beside inaccessible handwheels.

These imitations become more exact after every pumping cycle.

There are also the echoes.

At first they behave as distortions of sound: delayed footsteps, misplaced coughs, the answer of a hatch where no hatch exists. But repetition gives them persistence. Routes often walked acquire a second set of steps. Valves long struck with hammers continue to ring at the proper hour. In certain corridors, voices gather around corroded speaking tubes and murmur fragments that no present visitor supplied.

Given enough recurrence, an echo condenses.

It begins as a pressure in the air. Dust outlines it. Condensation clings to an invisible surface, describing a shoulder-like slope, a bent limb, a head lowered beneath pipes. Later, moss grows over the same volume. Fine roots span its interior. Mineral films accumulate where skin might have been. The finished things are hollow, resonant, and slow.

They walk only while the pumps run.

These resonant inhabitants move through service aisles with the gait of remembered workers. They stop at gauges, incline themselves toward engine housings, and place blunt, root-laced extremities against controls too hot for living flesh. Their proportions vary because they were made from many repetitions: footsteps, hammer blows, scraps of voices, the vibrations of tools carried on belts. Some have too many arms because several tasks occurred in one place. Some are only a torso attached to a long reverberating column that disappears into the floor.

They do not seem conscious in any animal sense. They are maintenance made bodily.

One always crosses the pump hall shortly before the third relay closes. Another kneels beside the circular well and listens downward. A taller form stands within the column of mist beneath the broken main, its outline visible only when droplets collect upon it. If obstructed, these beings wait. If touched, they produce a sound resembling the distant repetition of one’s own heartbeat.

When the machinery stops, they lose coherence. Water falls through them. Their mineral skins remain upright for a few minutes, then soften into leaves, flakes of verdigris, and lengths of root. Yet on the next cycle they return, often bearing some added detail: a tool-shaped extension, a row of button-like nodules, the suggestion of a face assembled from valve apertures.

The oldest of them may inhabit the fourth structure.

It is not seen directly. Its presence appears in the green indicators that recede beyond the shed’s door. One light goes dark whenever a mineral leaf forms on the chamber floor. Another brightens when a new echo gains substance. During heavy rain, a tall arrangement of lights moves across the impossible interior as if carried upon a body. At such times the garden turns toward the shed, and every gauge in the station rises by the same small amount.

This presence may be the station’s operator, manufactured from centuries of the station expecting one.

Or it may be the instruction itself.

It inhabits no fixed material. It passes between current, pressure, vibration, root signal, and the delayed architecture of sound. The moving square on the plans marks not where it is, but where the House presently believes its attention to be. Tools left near the fourth door are not cast backward through time; they are absorbed into the station’s memory, placed among the strata where the House stores things it has decided were always part of it.

Beneath all these lesser inhabitants lies something slower.

The pumps address it continuously. Their intakes descend toward it, though water is scarcely removed. The deep gauges measure its approach in vanished units. Roots that enter the lowest fissures return glazed in blue metal and branch upward in patterns unlike the chamber’s other vegetation. Nothing grown from those roots imitates surface life. Their forms are radial, pressurized, and inward-facing, like valves designed by a world in which there is no outside.

Whatever is below does not sleep. Sleep implies an organism permitted to cease its work.

It rises by increments too small for stone to register quickly. The pumps push against that rise. The plants bind the cracks. Echoes reinforce failing walls by becoming buttresses. Rain redistributes what escapes. Every inhabitant of the Verdigris House is engaged in holding a boundary whose location none of them understands.

They are not guarding the chamber from the thing beneath.

They are the shape taken by its containment.

Thus the Verdigris House contains no animals because it has no room for lives that serve only themselves. Everything within has been recruited into one patient ecology of maintenance: leaf, piston, mineral, current, noise. Even visitors begin to enter its processes. Their footsteps persist after they leave. Their breath alters condensation. Their discarded tools acquire roots. A spoken name may return months later from a pipe, worn smooth and almost unrecognizable.

Given enough visits, the House might learn the missing animal completely.

It might grow a mouth that drinks from the waiting cups, limbs that fit the empty nests, eyes that follow the green pulses through the dark. It might assemble such a creature from preserved veins, workers’ echoes, coiled fibers, and the remembered warmth of trespassers.

For now, what lives there is unfinished: a garden becoming an engine, an engine becoming a body, and a body slowly remembering that it was once meant to have someone inside it.
