Those who live inside the hedge are not one people so much as a compact among small lives.

No citizen can be identified by shape alone. Beneath their wrappings they may be furred, chitinous, long-faced, or joint-limbed. Some have bright, many-faceted eyes; others navigate by whisker, scent, or the trembling of wood. A few can cling upside down beneath a wet leaf. A few shed their skins. Children sometimes change so greatly while growing that an outsider would call the adult and infant different creatures.

The city does not mind. It recognizes its inhabitants by conduct.

A citizen carries warning ahead of danger. A citizen does not cut a green vein without tracing its water. A citizen leaves seed at the granary, helps raise flood gates, observes the ant crossings, and lowers their lamp when the shrew comes. Anyone who keeps these agreements long enough belongs. Anyone who abandons them becomes merely another animal in the hedge.

Most citizens are tiny, even by the standards of the place: upright beings in coats of felted fiber, seed-down, moth scale, and stitched membrane. Their clothes broaden their silhouettes and conceal the distinctions of body beneath. Hoods are common, waxed against condensation. Feet are wrapped in spider silk for purchase on slick bark. Those who work near open water wear buoyant belts of pith. Bridge crews knot themselves to safety lines made from grass fiber and gut.

Their hands—or the forelimbs used as hands—are darkened by sap and cold. They carry fish-bone wedges, thorn hooks, shell scrapers, flint teeth, and little clay pots kept warm against the body. Metal marks the most important professions. The keeper of the ancient needle-awl is not a ruler, but doors open for them. The two iron knives are brought out only for surgery, treaty-cutting, and the severing of diseased wood. The sacred splinters are carried in processions because rust, unlike the city, seems to remember a world beyond growth.

They live in households assembled as much by necessity as kinship. A cistern tender may share a stem-house with a midge herder, two orphaned bridge runners, an old fungus gardener, and someone who wakes only during thaws. Eggs, pupae, infants, and winter sleepers occupy the warmest inner chambers. Parenthood is often communal. In a place where a swollen bud can split a nursery overnight, no one claims a child so exclusively that others cannot carry it to safety.

Their days are measured less by light than by moisture. The first drip from a known leaf edge begins one watch; the cooling of the central stone begins another. They know the schedules of aphids, the sleeping hours of midges, the intervals between a snail’s journeys. A resident may spend an entire working period transporting one bead of water, rolling it along a waxed groove with paddles of bark.

Food is small, rich, and carefully divided: pollen paste, sap, tender fungal skins, lichen meal, mites’ eggs, marsh seeds ground in shell mortars, and aphids preserved in wax. Hunger is ordinary enough to have etiquette. One does not watch another eat unless invited. The granary serves children, sleepers, and flood crews first. During lean cold, meals are taken in darkness so no household must display what it has.

Many citizens tend animals that could kill them.

Aphids are brushed, counted, guarded, and moved between shoots. Mites are driven through moss lanes with bitter-smelling twigs. Pale springtails are kept near compost hollows, though no fence can hold them when they leap. Some beetles tolerate harness. Snails do not. Snails are negotiated with through scent, obstruction, and patient redirection, as one might negotiate with weather that possesses appetite.

Ants are nearer to a neighboring power than livestock. The city studies their roads, pays offerings, retrieves the injured, and never assumes yesterday’s agreement survives today. Spiders are landlords, enemies, hunters, and public contractors by turns. Their silk holds bridges together; their hunger empties them. Certain old spiders are addressed by title. None are trusted.

The hedge also contains lives outside citizenship: jaws under bark, larvae in stems, soft colonies drinking from green tissue, sleepers folded in leaves. They eat and are eaten without trial or malice. To the citizens, wilderness is not beyond the walls. It passes through the market, grows under the bed, hatches in stored food, and sometimes becomes a neighbor.

Accordingly, courage among them is rarely dramatic. It is the cistern worker climbing into the rain. It is the watchkeeper striking the snail alarm and remaining on the road long enough to mark its direction. It is a lamp-bearer standing still while a spider crosses the line above. Heroes are those who preserve routes for others.

Their leaders are similarly practical. Authority belongs temporarily to whoever understands the present danger: root readers during growth, water clerks during rain, scent wardens when ants are moving, quiet captains when a shrew enters. Councils meet on smooth bark and argue by laying seeds in carved grooves. Decisions of great consequence may take hours; a single drop can interrupt proceedings by striking the chamber roof hard enough to scatter everyone.

They bury little. Bodies are too useful and the city has too little ground. The dead are named at the lichen gardens, wrapped in fiber, and placed where roots, mold, and small mouths can take them apart. A pinch of colored lichen records each name until the patch grows over. Tools return to the household. Clothing is unstitched. Nothing of practical value follows the dead.

What remains is obligation.

When a citizen dies on a bridge, those who used that bridge owe one day’s labor to its repair. When a household is taken by a shrew, neighboring doors receive its survivors. When an unnamed body is found after floodwater recedes, it is counted as belonging to the city if it carries so much as a tied knot.

Around the central stone, even these busy beings become still.

They approach the seep bareheaded, allowing cold condensation to settle on fur, feelers, shell, or skin. No one agrees what the stone is. The root readers call it an interruption. The well keepers call it a source. Children say something beneath the marsh is slowly pushing out a tooth. Those who maintain the iron pieces suspect an affinity between stone and metal, for tools migrate in its presence and are found arranged with unnerving care.

Some nights, when the lamps are shuttered and the hedge has ceased creaking, small figures are seen standing on the plaza’s edge. They do not step onto the stone. They leave objects beside it: a broken thorn hook, a dead larva, a seed with an unusual mark, a child’s shed skin, one perfect scale from a moth.

By morning, the offerings have often been sorted.

No one admits to doing it.

Beyond the plaza, the rest of the population continues its immense, minute labor. Jaws pare wood. Fibers tighten as they dry. Herds shift along stems. A bridge runner crosses an open span with a blue lamp at either end of a carrying pole. Far below, rafts knock softly against moss. Somewhere a door is being grown rather than built, its edges guided together by hands whose owners may not live to see it finished.

That, more than form, is what lives in the hedge: creatures maintaining a future inside something alive, knowing every street may close, every wall may swell, every district may be eaten, and building nonetheless.
