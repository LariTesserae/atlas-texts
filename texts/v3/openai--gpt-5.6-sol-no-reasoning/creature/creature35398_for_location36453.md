The people of the Low Garden are small, six-legged makers clad in their own history.

Over each narrow body lie overlapping scales of fired clay, stamped one by one with marks that may signify lineage, craft, obligation, or merely the kiln in which the scale hardened. They replace these plates throughout their lives. Shed scales are not discarded: they become labels, roofing, shims, ancestral records, or armor for the very young. Thus settlements are partly built from former selves.

They live in mat-shuttered niches cut into the basin wall. There they sleep on shelves, cultivate dry fungi on copper, store edible waxes, and keep heat in thick jars. They have no use for fire and no concept corresponding exactly to water. Thirst is, to them, a loosening of the body’s salts. They cure it with resin, warm mineral foam, or contact with certain pulsing plants.

Their hands are the first pair of limbs: delicate, forceful, and rarely still. With stone hammers, fibers, wedges, abrasives, and patient measurement, they enlarge the enclosure. Some works require several lifetimes. A maker may begin a doorway knowing only descendants will pass through it. Authorship therefore belongs less to individuals than to sequences of hands, each inheriting the pressure and angle of the last.

They do not distinguish sharply between building, gardening, husbandry, and repair. A wall can be fed. A vine can be trained into a hinge. An animal’s migration can serve as a clock or cutting tool. Architecture may reproduce; vegetation may perform masonry. Their greatest skill is recognizing what a thing is becoming before it has entirely ceased to be what it was.

Around them flourishes a society of organisms adapted not merely to heat and dryness, but to the garden’s habits of causality.

Wooden-winged creatures feed on warm tiles and metallic vapor. By removing heat from what they consume, they excrete cold as soft beads, which the builders collect for tending delicate sprouts and tempering kilns. Their larvae may be incubated in cup-shaped vessels placed where two shadows overlap.

Knee-high tripods follow a thirteen-day circuit on cane legs, passing through obstructions as though walls were intervals rather than matter. Their route is older than many structures and more authoritative than property. Builders consult the herd before laying foundations, though the groove worn by its feet sometimes wanders independently and must itself be followed, fed, or fenced.

Ivory mites polish walls and graze upon residue, including residues of events. Some eat abandoned consequences: a dent whose blow never arrived, a scar detached from its wound, wear left by an object that ceased to have existed. Where such mites gather, surfaces become unnaturally innocent.

Ribbon-bodied grazers browse folded leaves and mosaic seams. Burrowing masks move through the lower vaults, presenting ceramic faces without visible bearers. Predators haunt recurring errors. Rather than stalking prey, they wait beside a loose knot, a miscounted stair, a tool habitually placed too near an edge. When the mistake happens again, they strike.

The plants are equally industrious. Copper fans harvest temperature differences. Rod-thickets feed on tarnish. Ocher pillars convert footsteps into rising sap. Self-mortaring species build foundations around their bodies; mosaic vines dismantle floors and reassemble them as supports. Fallen leaves remain alive long enough to fold and bind the next season’s growth, so each generation physically instructs its successor.

A purple vine lives on near-events. It flowers where a tool almost fell, where a predator nearly missed, where someone considered speaking and did not. The builders cultivate it cautiously, because dense blooming can make unrealized possibilities more substantial than completed acts.

No single creature governs the garden. Authority belongs to custom: every edge must acquire a fastening, every fastening a use, every useless thing a future. Damage is managed like weather. Silence is harvested only on appointed days. Gates are approached according to age, risk, and direction of consequence. The dead are laid under blank panels, not as burial but as preparation. When doors eventually appear in those panels, descendants open them with tools inherited from the deceased. What comes through is not always a body. Sometimes it is a room, a craft, a new species, or a task left unfinished.

At the garden’s center, all these lives converge upon the roofless pavilion and its infinitely recessed mosaic. The builders appear to regard it neither as shrine nor map. It is closer to an organ: the place through which the enclosure notices itself.

At the fixed noon, animals, plants, and makers attend it together. Hammers are lowered. Tripods interrupt their circuit. Winged feeders seal themselves to the bronze columns. Leaves turn inward. Even predators leave repeated mistakes unguarded.

They wait for the tap rising through the nested gardens.

No one knows whether it is a signal from below, the sound of construction in a smaller world, or the blow by which the Low Garden itself is continually made. Yet after it comes, the inhabitants disperse without surprise. Surveyors seek the newborn angle. Mites test its surface. Vines extend tentative hooks. Builders bring wedges, fibers, and stamped clay markers.

Whatever the new corner was before, it will not remain empty long.
