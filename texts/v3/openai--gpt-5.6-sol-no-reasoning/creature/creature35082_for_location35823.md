The city belongs to feeders.

Not one species, nor even one kind of body, but a vast congregation of mouths adapted to what the pumps, drains, factories, tides, and one another provide. They live without pasture, seed, leaf, root, or shade. Their food descends from conveyors, washes from slaughter floors, blooms invisibly in warm effluent, collects beneath gratings, or arrives already alive in the belly of something smaller.

The most visible inhabitants are the gulls. They form a second surface over the city: white and gray on tar roofs, parapets, pipe racks, tanks, and floodwalls. Older birds know the industrial schedule precisely. They face the rendering works before a waste gate opens, gather at settling ponds after washdown, and drift toward the harbor when warm-water pumps begin. Their chicks hatch among gravel, cable trays, and matted feathers. Roof drains become territorial boundaries; ventilation housings become cliffs. When disturbed, thousands lift together, and for several seconds the sky appears to fragment.

Below them live the pigeons, in the dry warmth of abandoned buildings and idle machinery halls. Generations have been born without seeing trees. They nest in switch cabinets, collapsed ducts, crane housings, and the hollow letters of disused company signs. Their plumage has darkened with soot and damp, and many carry rust-colored stains on their breasts from bathing in iron-rich puddles. They feed indoors, on spilled grain, insect husks, insulation fibers, and whatever the gulls leave behind.

Along the water, narrower lives predominate. Herons wait beside sluices, motionless except for the vibration passing through their legs. Terns hunt above discharge canals. Rails vanish into tangles of discarded cable rather than reeds. Cormorants line the outfall rails, spreading wings that never quite dry in the saturated air. Compact flocks of waders move across flooded yards as if the black gravel were a tidal flat, probing between rail ties, bolts, glass, and fragments of slag. Their migrations have bent around the city. Some no longer continue farther.

The drains belong to eels, crabs, rats, and things less often seen. Eels enter with the tide and remain in the warm underground water, pale-bellied and scarred by grilles. Crabs climb concrete channels, feeding on grease films and drowned insects. Rats travel the elevated pipes and buried galleries as humans once used streets. Their colonies are organized around valve chambers, refuse compactors, cable tunnels, and the warm foundations of turbines. They can distinguish a pump starting from a train passing overhead. They know which vibrations mean floodwater, which mean food, and which mean danger.

In stagnant sumps, mosquitoes rise in dark veils. Flies gather where animal matter crosses from sealed machinery into open air. Beetles inhabit warehouses whose stored goods vanished decades ago, surviving on fungus, droppings, carcasses, paper, leather seals, and each other. The city’s smallest residents live in accumulations: damp feathers under vents, sludge behind floodgates, salt crusts mixed with organic dust. Bacterial skins return to every shaded waterline, quickly rebuilding the city’s nearest equivalent to a meadow.

Fish crowd the harbor because the city feeds and heats it. Mullet nose through oily basins. Shoals pulse under wharves, silver only when they turn. Larger fish wait at pump outlets, where stunned prey emerges into oxygenated water. Seals rest on drowned breakwaters and rusted crane booms, heavy and watchful, slipping into the brown water when boats approach. Some bear old loops of cable or plastic around their necks. Others have learned to follow maintenance craft, taking fish driven from culverts by propellers and noise.

The feral dogs live between these populations. Lean, salt-stiffened, and often hairless in patches, they den beneath loading platforms, inside dead vehicles, and in the cavities between successive seawalls. During the day they sleep under tanker trailers where the metal radiates a steady warmth. At dusk they assemble near waste gates. They hunt rats, injured birds, stranded fish, and sometimes one another. Their territories follow industrial boundaries: a pack may control one refinery yard yet refuse to cross the open asphalt between it and the next. They recognize uniforms, sirens, and shift buses. They rarely bark.

People live here too, though in smaller numbers than the animals and with less exposure to the city itself. They inhabit sealed rooms, filtered cabins, elevated dormitories, and control centers reached through covered corridors. Many know the outside chiefly through cameras and instrument panels. Their work is to keep water on the correct side of walls, pressure within tolerated limits, corrosion slow enough to manage, and animal bodies out of moving machinery.

They have adapted less by changing themselves than by adding layers around themselves: respirators, rubberized clothing, cooled vehicles, decontamination vestibules, sealed windows, raised walkways. They speak of districts by their hazards and functions rather than by streets. Children, where there are children, learn the warning tones of floodgates before they learn birdsong. Maintenance crews read droppings, tracks, feathers, fish scales, and gnawed insulation as part of the city’s instrumentation. A sudden absence of gulls may indicate a gas leak. Rats climbing from a tunnel may mean a failed pump. Fish massing at the surface may precede an oxygen collapse.

No inhabitant is wholly separate from the machinery. Birds nest in its heat. Dogs time their feeding to it. Fish gather in its discharge. Rats use it as shelter and road. Humans preserve it because without the pumps the city would become shallow sea, and without the factories there would be little reason for humans to preserve it. Even the seals, farthest from the enclosed control rooms, rest on structures built for ships that no longer come.

Together they form a life without greenery: flesh, feather, scale, chitin, hair, bacterial film. Their shelter is steel and concrete. Their seasons are maintenance cycles, tides, rains, outages, and the changing temperature of effluent. Their abundance depends on damage that never quite becomes ruin and systems that never quite become secure.

When the city is still, they disappear into its geometry. A gull becomes another pale fitting on a roof. A heron resembles a length of pipe. Seals look like wet sections of collapsed breakwater. Rats vanish into rust shadows; dogs into the darkness beneath axles; fish into brown water. Then a gate opens, a siren sounds, or a vehicle enters an empty yard, and the whole city reveals its true population at once—lifting, scattering, surfacing, running, and wheeling through the damp mineral air.
