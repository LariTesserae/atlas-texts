The animal arrives folded inside the droplet.

At first it is only a pale flaw in the water: a branching opacity that might be a salt crystal, except that its branches withdraw from the sunlight. The drop rests between the blades of glass, convex on either side, and the creature occupies its entire interior without touching the surface. Seen from above, it is smaller than a grain of ash. Seen through the curved water, it extends across a white and vaporous country.

It has no fixed number of limbs. Those nearest the eye resemble six long legs jointed backward and armored in translucent silica. Farther away, the same structures divide into ridges crowded with migrating animals. What appears to be its hide is a terrain of black plates, warm pools, yellow mineral fans, and narrow crevasses full of life. Weather travels across it. Tiny predators hunt in the seams between its scales. Living chains hang from its undersides and tighten whenever it moves.

The animal is not host to the country. It is one of the country’s oldest inhabitants, grown so large inwardly that the distinction has failed.

Its body feeds on differences of scale. Wherever a droplet contains a lake, wherever a short fall deepens into an abyss, wherever near and far exchange places, it extends a mouth. These mouths are not openings but transitions: a smooth patch becoming suddenly vast, a reflection acquiring depth, a narrow channel admitting more bodies than its width should allow. Through them it consumes distance. The warmth it gives off is what sustains the drifting sacs, and the minute loss of proportion around each feeding is what permits the cleft to remain larger within than without.

For most of its existence it lies beneath the inverted black summit, wrapped around the sealed heat below. The lightning there is the movement of impulses along its nerves. The rain rising from the reservoirs is moisture drawn through its skin. When it turns over, gravity must find new routes through the fissure, and every suspended drop points toward the rearrangement.

It is neither ruler nor god. Much of the cleft feeds on it.

Raspers graze the colorless films along its plates. Glass-shelled animals anchor themselves in its pores. Flat ambushers mimic its reflections. When fragments of its armor loosen, hunters carry them as tools, sometimes for generations. The rounded shards older than the island are its shed scales, passed from vanished clefts in mountains not yet risen. Scars upon other creatures record their ancestors’ encounters with those scales in futures that the animal has already crossed.

It surfaces rarely. To emerge is dangerous, because daylight insists on one size at a time.

Within the bead it begins to simplify. Its remote ridges draw together. Storm systems become pulses beneath a membrane. Herds on its back vanish into pores. Hundreds of apparent limbs resolve into six, then four, then none. For an instant it resembles an embryo with a crown of hooked glass. Then it resembles a transparent mite. Then a seed. Each form is accurate, but each excludes too much.

Around it, the inhabitants of the lip flatten themselves against the wet stone. Their stillness is not fear. They are holding the interior open. Long-legged grazers brace across microscopic channels. The living nets draw taut in the depths. Swimmers align their bodies with currents too faint to feel. Predators refrain from feeding. Across the whole seam, countless small acts preserve the path by which the immense animal can remain connected to its own dimensions.

The warm droplet climbs over the fissure’s rim.

Outside, the summit air is thin and cold. Wind touches the water and begins to strip heat from it. The animal responds by unfolding—not into space, but into consequence.

Its shadow reaches the neighboring ridge before its body has left the drop. Mineral crusts split along lines corresponding to its joints. Clouds above the island develop a small circular opening. Deep below, magma cools in the shape of a footprint, though nothing has stepped there. For one heartbeat the barren cone is merely one dark plate on the back of something vaster, and the surrounding ocean is a film gathered between plates.

Then the droplet breaks.

Most of it runs back into the crack. Part becomes mist. A fraction clings to the outer glass, carrying what now appears to be an ordinary pale animal: soft-bodied, blind, nearly featureless, small enough to stand beneath the hooked edge of a sulfur crystal.

It takes one step into the open air.

The island settles several inches into the sea.

The creature pauses. Behind it, at scales no exterior observer could distinguish, the crowded country adjusts to the absence of its weight. Corridors harden. Migratory pools reverse direction. The inverted summit dims. Somewhere on an unbroken ledge, hunters begin waiting beside a fracture that will occur centuries later.

The animal takes another step. Its tiny feet leave no visible mark, but each contact places a new depth inside the stone. Hairline cracks branch ahead of it, shallow from the outside and bottomless within. They are not nests or burrows. They are the continuation of its body’s oldest function: making room where there is none.

It moves toward the summit’s edge, carrying remnants of its country in the films between its limbs—grazers, raspers, ambushers, transparent chains, and colorless feeders clinging to a warmth already fading. To an ordinary eye it would be nothing more than a wet speck crossing black volcanic glass.

But the speck’s shadow contains weather.

And wherever that shadow touches, the newborn island becomes inhabited all the way down.
