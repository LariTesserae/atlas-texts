The Rookery belongs chiefly to the white birds, if belonging can exist where nothing arrives, departs, or changes.

They inhabit not only the chamber but its impossible abundance. There are more of them than its dimensions permit. Behind each visible rank of birds seems to be another: wings flickering between condenser fins, black eyes shining through grates, translucent claws closed around pipes no thicker than wire. Their feathers are chalk-white near the warm relays and glass-clear at the frozen tips. Rime locks their breasts into little armored plates. They fly perpetually without traveling, held in the upward violence of the fan, each body repeating one indivisible exertion. They do not tire. They do not strengthen. No feather loosens and falls.

Their nests are exact circles assembled from washers, ceramic chips, clipped wire, and scales of insulation. Within them lie eggs that are warm on one side and glazed with ice on the other. Nothing hatches. Some birds bend over these eggs in permanent attendance, beaks almost touching the shells. Others hang upside down from cable bundles or brace themselves in open relay boxes while blue contacts chatter beside their heads. Whether they built the nests or were installed with them is impossible to determine.

The sumps belong to the blind eels. They are silver only when they bend toward the inspection lamps; otherwise they disappear into black coolant. Each is arrested within a turn it endlessly attempts to complete. Muscles pass their waves from head to tail, yet the curve of the body never advances. Their mouths open and close on water already inside them.

Pale shrimp live in the transparent flow meters, suspended among bubbles that neither rise nor collapse. They pulse their many limbs as if swimming against a current, though the meter’s marked volume never alters. Worms cloud every bead and film of water. Each droplet is a minute crowded world, full of pale bodies writhing without displacement. Red mites occupy the ice in fixed constellations, their legs industriously traversing distances smaller than themselves and never crossing them.

Six-legged beetles inhabit the motors. They prefer the narrow warm chambers around bearings and insulated coils, where their dark shells shine with condensed oil. Their jaws work against copper, lacquer, and steel but leave no scar. Some hold minute mites between their mandibles. The prey struggles; the predator bites; neither act reaches its conclusion.

Above them hang the wire chrysalises. Their braided casings tremble in the airflow, silver strands furred with frost. Something jointed can occasionally be discerned within, pressing outward in a gesture of emergence that has neither begun nor failed. No adult form is known.

There are three plants, though “plant” may be only the nearest available category. The colorless moss strands in the graphite tray have the translucence of nerves. Copper-green crystals run through them like a rigid sap. They possess no visible roots and cast almost no shadow. The turbines dust them continuously with graphite, yet they never become covered. They are always the same length, always bearing the same minute droplets at their tips.

And perhaps something larger lives there—not behind the machinery, but as the agreement between all its parts.

It has no separate body. Its lungs are the birds and the fan. Its circulation is the captive water. Its nerves are copper capillaries, wet cables, and hammering relays. The fixed needles of the gauges may be its eyes, or merely the expression of its unchanging health. It does not consume the creatures; it includes their unspent effort. Every wingbeat, muscular ripple, attempted bite, falling droplet, and contracting coil contributes to a life too distributed to move.

If so, the Rookery itself is the inhabitant, and all the smaller beings are organs of its continuance. It lives at the threshold of its first instant. It is always taking its inaugural breath and never receiving it. The warm red wheel beside the entrance may be its heart, though it neither turns nor pulses. No one touches it because the room feels less like an abandoned machine than an animal maintaining perfect tension against the next moment.

What lives there is everything caught in the act of becoming what it already is.
