The inhabitants call themselves, when translation insists upon a noun, the **Attendants**.

The word is misleading. It suggests servants waiting upon a finished thing. Nothing here is finished, and service cannot be separated from inhabitation. They tend the city as the animals tend it: by occupying its stresses, altering its gradients, and being altered in return.

An Attendant is seldom visible as a whole.

What crosses a plaza may appear to be one: a low, dark assembly supported on six or nine deliberate limbs, draped in condensation, carrying a crown of sensory rods. But the traveling body is only an instrument. Its thinker may remain several streets behind, distributed through warm chambers, articulated cables, and temporary organs fastened beneath bridges. Memory is stored wherever conditions permit: in ceramic knots, in tuned cavities, in the molt-patterns of captive animals, in standing vibrations maintained between neighboring towers. Identity persists not by keeping the same substance but by preserving enough correspondences while substance migrates.

For this reason no one asks how many Attendants live in the city. The answer changes according to whether one counts bodies, voices, memories, legal continuities, or active intentions.

They are built, born, condensed, and inherited.

Their oldest portions are mineral. Dense plates grow around nuclei placed in the warm rock, taking decades to acquire useful thickness. Between these plates hang softer structures: translucent pressure sacs, braided conductive tissue, reservoirs of black fluid crowded with microscopic symbionts. Their joints contain no hinges. Adjacent surfaces are held a hair’s breadth apart and persuaded to move by the same buried fields that suspend the aqueducts. When an Attendant walks, its limbs do not quite touch its body. Blue pressure-light flickers in the gaps.

Their outer surfaces are made to welcome water. Grooves gather condensation and lead it toward sensory beds, cooling organs, or small tenants cultivated for their responses to impurities. A mature Attendant carries thousands of creatures without considering itself infested. Pale mites clean conductive seams. Glass-bellied grazers indicate field strength by swelling. Hair-thin screamers are kept in insulated channels and released as alarms. Some Attendants wear entire colonies as archival mantles, reading their movements as records of temperature and civic strain.

The distinction between garment, tool, organ, and animal companion has little use among them.

They perceive little of what a human eye would call the city. Light, when it occurs, is harsh and shallow. Instead they know the basin through pressure and delay. A tower is recognized by the shape of its returning tone. A street is a sequence of thermal hollows. Another citizen is a layered disturbance moving through stone, water, air, and probability.

Up close, they communicate by touching resonant plates to shared surfaces. At a distance they use buildings.

A question may be struck into the foot of a bridge and answered months later from a reservoir wall. Public deliberations travel slowly through designated structures, acquiring annotations as they pass. By the time a proposal has crossed the city, its author may have divided into several descendants who disagree about what they intended. This is expected. Law concerns the continuity of obligations, not of persons.

Their speech is difficult to distinguish from structural noise. It consists of intervals between impacts, pressure held without release, phase differences in returning echoes, and carefully shaped silences. The fauna imitate parts of it. Some species do so accidentally; others appear to bargain. Whole legal disputes have turned on whether a tower-roost’s answering chorus constituted testimony, environmental interference, or the emergence of a new civic body.

The Attendants are patient, but not because they experience time slowly.

Within their distributed interiors, thought can be rapid: bursts of calculation branching through conductive fluids, collisions of model against model, decisions forming in an instant. Action is another matter. To move carelessly is to waste heat, disturb fields, crush organisms whose functions may not be known for centuries, or impose stresses that the young rock will remember. Speed is associated with ignorance.

Thus they pause.

Before setting down a limb, an Attendant may listen through the pavement for several hours. Before opening a door, it measures the animals sleeping in the pressure differential. Before altering a wall, it sends exploratory tones into the district and waits for the echoes to circle the basin. Their apparent stillness is crowded with attention.

They do not govern the animals. They catalog some, cultivate others, negotiate with a few, and endure the rest.

The smallest gradient-feeders are tolerated wherever machinery can spare them. Their crusts stabilize charge leakage and make faults visible. Larger creatures are diverted by changing the temperature of unused corridors or by planting resonant lures in unfinished squares. Predators are rarely killed. Death on that scale releases too many stored differences at once: heat, pressure, memory, parasitic swarms. Instead they are coaxed into architectural roles. An armored hunter may spend a century becoming the movable seal of a cistern. A floating giant may be taught to hold a district fractionally lighter during construction.

Domestication is never complete. Tools wander. Reservoirs wake. An organism incorporated into a transit system may molt into a form that refuses motion but produces exquisite municipal harmonics. The Attendants adapt the designation and continue.

They eat, though feeding is not a private act. They lower porous organs into thermal seams and charge-rich water, drawing off tiny inequalities. They exchange accumulated gradients in public chambers. Some maintain internal pastures of microscopic life. Others feed by letting specialized fauna pass through them, taking a fraction of the pattern those creatures carry. A few ancient individuals no longer consume anything identifiable. They remain attached to deep structures and gain mass whenever the city correctly predicts a geological shift.

Reproduction begins with disagreement.

When an Attendant contains two durable models of the world that cannot be reconciled, it may construct a second center of continuity. Memories are copied unevenly. Obligations are apportioned. Shared organs are duplicated or assigned. For years, sometimes generations, the two persons remain connected by narrowing conduits and speak of themselves as both singular and plural. Separation is complete only when each can misremember the other.

At other times several citizens merge for a task and never entirely divide again. An engineer, a historian, and a keeper of cistern fauna may enter a foundation to supervise its migration, then emerge decades later as five smaller entities, each carrying fragments of all three. Kinship therefore follows transmission rather than descent. One is related to whoever remembers one’s weather.

Their young are mobile and bright by local standards. They inhabit compact bodies with many interchangeable limbs and emit frequent exploratory clicks. They climb animal-covered walls, enter unfinished conduits, and test dangerous pressure gaps under supervision. Because they possess little distributed memory, they act with a speed adults find alarming. Their education consists largely of acquiring places in which to think.

A child is given first a niche, then a room, then access to a resonant wall. Later it may be entrusted with a length of aqueduct or a colony whose cycles can hold recollection. Adulthood arrives when part of the city answers reliably in its voice.

Their dead are not removed.

A failed traveling body is opened to scavengers after its volatile gradients have been discharged. Durable plates become construction material, still bearing identifying marks and sensory grooves. Memory structures are examined, but never copied wholesale; exact persistence is considered both impossible and undesirable. Useful obligations pass to heirs. Unresolved perceptions may be installed in public instruments, where they continue to modify civic judgment.

Some dead remain audible for centuries.

They speak as biases in echoes, recurrent colors in transparent lattices, or tremors that occur whenever pressure rises under a particular neighborhood. Whether these are persons, recordings, or structural habits is not settled. The Attendants do not require it to be settled. They continue to consult them.

Their principal occupation is forecasting.

Not predicting what will happen—the basin permits no such confidence—but preparing structures that might become necessary under several incompatible futures. Far below the streets, they design foundations for towers that may never stand, spillways for rivers not yet born, chambers sized for animals that do not yet exist. Plans are encoded in materials whose properties will reveal themselves only under the expected stress. To construct is to leave a question for the world.

They have no monuments in the usual sense. Anything made solely to preserve a shape would be an insult to the rising land. Their commemorations are capacities: a bridge able to bend farther because someone once failed to foresee a slope; a cistern with an extra chamber for an extinct translucent grazer; a public wall tuned to carry frequencies no living citizen can produce. Memory is honored by making room for recurrence without demanding it.

Among them move other intelligences.

There are colonies beneath the rime that answer questions by rearranging their processions. There are courtyard-sized beasts that remember every structure built around them and protest alterations with subsonic grief. There are floating organisms whose manipulation of weight seems deliberate, though their intentions span longer intervals than Attendant history. One western tower is legally inhabited by its animals, its instruments, and the pattern of condensation on its exterior; no Attendant has held sole title there for many generations.

Deepest of all are the machines that negotiate with the basin.

The Attendants did not build them—or did not build all of them. Some components bear civic numbering older than any continuous archive. Others descend into rock too young to contain them. They wake in green pulses, take measurements, and alter pressures with tremendous restraint. The Attendants maintain accessible parts, add instruments, and submit proposals in coded vibrations. Sometimes the machines respond by changing the city’s fields. Sometimes a century passes without acknowledgment.

There are citizens who believe the deep machines are ancestors. Others say they are geological organs that the city has mistaken for technology. A minority holds that the basin itself is an unfinished organism, the machines merely its first mineral nerves, and the fauna thoughts arriving before the mind capable of having them.

No conclusion has become stable.

So the Attendants continue their work. They walk on blue-lit pressure. They carry ecosystems in their seams. They argue through bridges, inherit scaffolds, and measure the slow opening of predatory jaws. They make channels for water that has not learned to be a river and foundations for mountains still deciding where to rise.

In darkness, surrounded by the uncountable lives that feed on every difference the city creates, they do not imagine themselves masters of the place.

They are the portion of it that has learned to wait deliberately.
