The basin is inhabited less by creatures than by custodianship distributed across water, root, stone, and weather.

Its most numerous inhabitants are the low plants. Bronze sedges weave themselves into load-bearing mats, while translucent rushes carry dim currents through their stems. Feather-leaved thickets tighten over submerged channels, gripping the rock whenever gravity loosens or changes direction. Broad, gray-furred fronds comb moisture from the air and feed it into the peat beneath them. These plants do more than survive the marsh: they hold it together. Their roots spread laterally across the basin, stitching shut small failures in distance and anchoring stones during intervals when weight becomes uncertain. Every hummock is a living repair, accumulated over centuries—an island whose true function is to keep “here” connected to itself.

The few trees are more itinerant. Their crowns hover independently above the marsh, tangled black branches filled with small silver leaves. Water descends from them in vertical cords that may serve as roots, tethers, or both. When conditions dry, they lower themselves onto the moss; when channels prepare to migrate, they drift toward the banks that do not yet exist. They seem less planted in places than attached to suitable futures.

Colorless lichens inhabit the basin’s dry center. They never receive rain, yet alter their branching forms after every storm. Their pale diagrams correspond to the waterways several days in advance. The marsh follows them: channels shift, roots loosen, trees relocate, and stone begins weathering where water will later pass. Whether the lichen foresees these changes or issues them is unknowable. It may be the basin’s slow nervous system, or merely the visible rash of calculations occurring elsewhere.

Animal life is sparse and unstable.

Pale swimmers appear in pools without remaining faithful to any one body of water. Their fins hang like torn membranes. When threatened, they reverse themselves through their own surfaces—inside becoming outside—and disappear into a ring of ripples. They may flee into the brighter landscape reflected beneath the water rather than through the pool itself.

Farther off, long-legged waders move among the reeds. Their narrow bodies cannot settle on a fixed number of limbs: four, six, and eight-legged arrangements flicker through one another as they walk. They feed by inserting their beaks into the underwater sky. What they draw out cannot be seen, though it resists. The birds, if birds they are, make no calls. Perhaps their voices arrive elsewhere, or at another time.

Larger things are known chiefly by consequence. Three-toed tracks end in open water. Paired furrows emerge from unbroken stone. Vast spiral trails flatten kilometers of sedge without snapping a stem, then contract over several days into a single wet hollow. Whatever makes these passages may be immense, or may simply occupy distance differently—its whole migration touching the marsh at once before withdrawing point by point.

There are also transient inhabitants: unrealized bodies that separate briefly from a traveler on the black flats. A missed step may continue walking. A hesitation can remain behind. A fall that never happened may rise, cross the stone alone, and disappear into the reeds. They possess no lasting substance, but for several seconds the basin accommodates them as readily as any animal.

Beneath and around these lives is a greater presence without a visible body. It announces itself as low vibration, displaced sound, rearranged gravity, anticipatory erosion, and the equilibrium by which matter lost in one place appears elsewhere. It may be the basin’s original engineer, though it behaves less like a machine than a law that has grown local habits. Its structures are invisible until wet: curved planes, membranes, and enormous hollow cylinders intersecting the air. Rain travels along them, sometimes upward. They flex when the deep vibration passes, and the entire wetland shifts by a distance too small to measure.

At the center of the dry region, this presence has an apparent shape only in reflection: a tall, narrow darkness rising through another sky. Direct approach is impossible. Straight paths curve back without seeming to turn, mist bows around an absent volume, and sound duplicates itself near the center. It may be an inhabitant, an instrument, a missing object, or the opening through which the basin’s hidden order enters the world.

No single organism rules the place. If there is intelligence here, it is shared among predictive lichen, migrating water, corrective erosion, root-bound distortions, hovering trees, and geometries revealed by rain. The marsh itself may be the inhabitant: ancient, distributed, and only intermittently aware in a way an observer could recognize.

Its life consists in maintenance.

Water tests the world. Stone redistributes what is lost. Lichen lays out tomorrow’s channels. Trees move to meet them. Roots close the small wounds in continuity. Strange animals harvest from reflected distances. The unseen planes bear rivers overhead. Then the vibration comes, the basin slips fractionally against the universe, and every living mat tightens its hold.

What lives there is whatever keeps the place from coming apart.
