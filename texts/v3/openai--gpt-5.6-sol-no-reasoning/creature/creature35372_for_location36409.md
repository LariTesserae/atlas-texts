The one who lives there is not often seen whole.

Those who climb to the hollow may find signs of it and mistake them for the work of several creatures: a narrow trail through the sedge; fresh moss pressed flat beneath a shelf; fish bones laid in a clean row beside a spring. Once, perhaps, they glimpse a pale hand withdrawing into a crack where no person could fit. More often they see only water shaking on a windless stone.

It lives beneath the broken ring of shelves, in the wet seam between peat and mountain.

Its body is small enough to curl inside the stone shelter, though the shelter was not built for it. Crouched, it resembles a thin child wrapped in grey wool. Standing, it is taller than expected, all long shins, narrow shoulders, and hanging arms. Its proportions alter subtly with posture, as if no single arrangement of its bones has become final. At times it goes upright over the marsh, stepping from tussock to tussock with grave precision. At others it travels close to the ground, fingers and toes finding holds among roots and grooves, nearly as quick as a marmot.

Its skin has the dim, mottled colors of water over rock. There are traces of rust-red at the joints, sulphur yellow along the throat, and bluish darkness beneath the ribs. These are not markings so much as small living colonies: lichen, algae, and minute flowers established upon it. When it remains still, droplets gather along its arms. Moss thickens in the shallow hollow above each collarbone.

Its hair grows in coarse black-green strands, usually wet. It hangs over the face and is threaded with shed down from the cloud-swifts. Beetle wing cases gleam in it like beads. In spring, frogs sometimes fasten their ropes of eggs to the trailing ends while it lies half-submerged in a pool.

The face is quiet and difficult to remember. The eyes are dark, without obvious whites, and reflect whatever weather stands above the basin. Its nostrils are narrow slits that close in rain. Its mouth is broad but seldom opens far. When it does, the teeth are small, crowded, and polished smooth. It has no language shaped for conversation. Its voice is a set of borrowed sounds: a goat kid’s cry, three notes of wind in a groove, the clink of hoof on stone, the wet rattle of frogs.

Each sound is exact. Their sequence is not.

It may answer a marmot’s alarm with rainfall, or call an eagle in the voice of a newborn goat. Sometimes, from beneath a shelf, it repeats a human cough heard many years before. The repetition has grown faint with use but remains unmistakably human.

Water passes through it as freely as breath.

It drinks by laying its palms against a seep. The moisture disappears into the fine cracks of its skin and emerges elsewhere: at an elbow, along the spine, beneath the nails. When it sleeps, rivulets gather around it and run toward its body even where the stone tilts away. This is the source of the hollow’s small disobediences. The creature does not command water. Rather, the water recognizes it as another depth, a place into which it might descend.

Its oldest paths lie under the surface. Through minute fissures it feels every spring in the basin, every weight placed on soaked turf, every root opening in darkness. A goat drinking at the western wall touches its awareness. So does a beetle burrowing into moss, or a climber setting one boot on a hidden stepping-stone. Thunder enters it all at once. During storms it trembles violently, and the uphill-running veins quicken as though the whole mountain were drawing breath through its body.

It feeds without urgency. It grazes on tender sedge tips, licks minerals from the same ledges as the goats, and catches the tiny spring fish in cupped hands. It eats frogs only after spawning, never before. It cracks beetles between its back teeth. From bird nests it takes addled eggs and dead chicks, leaving the living untouched unless winter has lasted too long.

What it cannot eat, it arranges.

Bones are sorted by shape beneath the quiet shelves. Feathers are tucked into vertical cracks. Bright stones are carried from one side of the basin to the other and placed where afternoon light can strike them. After a kill, it follows the fox and gathers what remains. Thus the small white skeletons among the reeds are often cleaner and more complete than chance would make them.

The animals know it, though none treat it alike.

Marmots scold it but do not flee far. Cloud-swifts mob it whenever it approaches the outer rim, tangling claws in its hair. Frogs climb onto its hands in cold weather. The tiny fish shelter beneath its feet. Eagles watch it with the severe attention given to another predator, though it has never been seen to hunt them.

The cliff goats are closest to it. They permit it among their newborns and sometimes shelter around it during hail, their bodies making a rough white wall. It cleans blood from birthing stones. When a kid falls into a fissure, the creature lowers an arm farther than its frame should allow and draws the animal out. Old goats visit the marked veins before they die. It stays beside them, licking salt from their faces until they become still.

The small fox hates it.

Their quarrel has continued through generations of foxes. The creature raids caches; the fox steals its rows of bones. The fox urinates on the triangular marks, and the creature patiently washes them clean. They stalk one another around the marsh but rarely fight. In severe weather they occupy opposite ends of the same stone shelter, each pretending not to know the other is there.

People once knew it more clearly.

The low shelter belonged to those who climbed during the thaw months to gather herbs, attend the goats, and listen to the springs. They did not worship the creature, exactly. They regarded it as one regards dangerous weather or a deep crossing: with practical courtesy. The forked posts were raised to show where gifts might be left without being washed away. The copper cup was for collecting water from the upward-running seams. The bundles of bone and red thread were requests for safe births—human or animal.

The carved cuts beside the shelter do not count days. Each marks a time the creature was seen.

There are fewer cuts than visitors might expect.

It did not come when summoned. It disliked flame, iron, and loud human grief. Yet sometimes it crouched beyond the hearth’s glow and traded. A needle for a polished pebble. A handful of salt for the whereabouts of a missing goat. A song, repeated once in its broken voice, for a cup of warm blood after a difficult birth.

The last people ceased coming regularly long ago. Their path failed in a rockfall below the notch; valleys changed allegiance; perhaps the old knowledge thinned into a story no one wished to risk a climb for. Still, someone renews the grass cords. The creature never does. Its fingers can plait sedge but cannot tie a knot that holds.

When a visitor enters the hollow now, it withdraws below the shelves and listens through the water.

It knows weight before shape, hesitation before speech. A careless traveler may never see it, though gear will be found damp and small edible things may disappear. A respectful one might notice that stepping-stones emerge more clearly through the pools, or that cloud closes the dangerous rim. An injured climber may wake in the turf-roofed shelter with moss packed into a wound and the copper cup standing full beside them.

But anyone who chips at the marked veins, fouls a spring, or kills among the seven trees meets another inhabitant entirely.

Then it rises from every seep at once.

The basin darkens, though the sky may remain clear. Water swells through peat and under boot soles. Paths vanish. The harmless sideways droplets join into lines that move with purpose, entering cuffs, packs, mouths, and the gaps between fingers gripping stone. The creature’s borrowed cries sound from all sides: eagle, infant, fox, thunder, human cough. It never pursues beyond the rim. It only makes the hollow refuse to release what has offended it.

Afterward, its anger passes quickly. It restores overturned stones. It presses exposed roots back into peat. It carries the dead beneath a shelf where the noise of the mountain is muted, and there it waits until water has taken every scent away.

Most days contain no such violence.

Most days it tends the modest particulars of its world. It frees waterweed from a clogged spring. It watches seven ash trees bend and straighten. It lies belly-down beside a pool while pale frogs rattle around its face. It sleeps through noon under a ledge, one hand sunk wrist-deep in black peat. At dusk it walks the ring of shelves and looks outward whenever the cloud opens.

It does not seem lonely. Loneliness requires an idea of elsewhere.

Yet on rare clear evenings, when valleys appear far below and distant smoke lifts from inhabited ground, it stands in the eastern notch. The wind draws water from its hair. Swifts wheel around its shoulders. For a long time it listens to sounds too remote for any ordinary ear.

Then it answers with the old human cough.

The mountains return it from peak to peak, diminished but recognizable, until the little hollow seems briefly occupied by a multitude.
