The people who live here are compact, heavy-limbed, and immensely practiced. They walk with the cautious, flat-footed gait of those raised on slick clay and shallow water, toes turned slightly outward, knees always ready to yield. Shoulders broaden early from carrying yokes through passages too narrow for carts. Hands are thickened by brine, lime, hide, and wet grit; the fingertips of potters and channel keepers are worn almost smooth. Their feet are often bare, but seldom unprotected: soles are painted with grease and ash, or wrapped in rawhide when crossing fresh volcanic stone.

They smell of smoke, wet wool, rendered fat, and mineral water.

No one in the city lives far from animals. A household is not counted only by its people but by the bodies it shelters and the work those bodies can perform: two adults, three children, an old aunt, four goats, six message birds, a cart dog, a box of water-sensing burrowers, and perhaps a fractional claim on a district ox. Wealth is measured partly in mouths, yet not all mouths are considered burdens. A well-trained dog may be valued above a strong young laborer; a lineage of calm mill cattle may carry more prestige than a lineage of human ancestors.

The inhabitants do not draw a hard boundary between settlement and herd. Children sleep against warm animal flanks during the luminous hours called night. Old people sit in pens because beasts respond to their familiar voices and smells. Birth, sickness, mating, slaughter, and butchery occur within the same walls as cooking and trade. The city understands life through bodies under management: feeding, bleeding, breeding, resting, harnessing, healing. Its deepest knowledge is anatomical and behavioral.

Its healers are therefore also drovers, midwives, bone-setters, and slaughter inspectors. They know how to turn an obstructed calf, drain an infected hoof, splint a child’s forearm with shell and hide, and recognize poisoned water from the spasms of caged animals. Their remedies contain little that would elsewhere be called herbal. They use salt, ash, clay, fat, milk, blood, marrow, fermented grain, powdered shell, hot stone, steam, pressure, and careful cutting. Wounds are packed with clean mineral paste. Feverish bodies are lowered into particular cisterns whose waters are judged by taste and warmth. Illness is classified by rhythm: a quick tremor, a slow swelling, a skipped breath, a pain that returns with the rain.

Because the city is so loud, its people speak with their whole bodies. The local sign language is not a substitute for speech but its equal. A hand held at the shoulder may indicate a district, an animal, a quantity, or an obligation depending on the angle of the wrist. Facial grammar is precise. Eyebrows mark questions; the direction of the chin distinguishes past from intended action. Deals can be conducted across a court without a spoken word.

Names have spoken and visible forms. A person may be called, aloud, something brief enough to penetrate noise—Kett, Om, Ra, Tash—while their signed name may recall a bent finger, a scar, a habitual movement, or the horns of the household’s founding cow. Intimate names are sometimes made by touch against the palm and cannot be used at a distance.

Hearing is not regarded as the chief avenue to truth. A shouted claim can vanish into the general clamor; a witnessed gesture remains accountable. Contracts are enacted in deliberate sequences before several observers and then impressed into clay. Instead of writing long sentences, the inhabitants record patterns of obligation: seal marks, thumb hollows, cord impressions, hoof symbols, measured rows of punctures. The clay tablets are thick, blunt, and durable, made to be handled with wet or dirty hands. Archives resemble stacks of small bricks.

Witnesses hold an important social rank. Some are chosen for exceptional visual memory and trained from childhood to recognize hands, gaits, household marks, animal brands, and standard measures. During disputes they reproduce agreements gesture by gesture. A good witness may remember thousands of transactions and the exact placement of those who observed them. Blindness does not exclude a person from the role, however; vibration-readers sit with palms or bare feet against prepared floors and testify to mill stoppages, unauthorized night traffic, or the number of animals driven through a gate.

The city’s rulers, if they can be called rulers, are keepers of repetition. They do not claim a divine right to invent law. Their authority comes from remembering dimensions, schedules, shares, and sequences: how wide a lane must remain, how many courses belong in a wall, whose herd drinks first, how much clay each district owes after rain, when a gate should close, and which household must provide beasts for the western mill.

Novelty is treated with suspicion not because the people lack imagination, but because improvisation can disturb a system whose dependencies are too numerous for anyone to comprehend. A widened house narrows a passage; a narrowed passage delays dung carts; delayed dung starves a kiln; a cold kiln ruins pipe sections; broken pipes flood sleeping halls. Custom is therefore understood as accumulated intelligence. “It has this shape because we are still alive” is both proverb and argument.

Yet change does occur. It enters disguised as repair.

An improved drainage lip is said to restore the old profile. A more efficient harness is attributed to an ancestor. A district that needs another sleeping hall may “discover” that the customary boundary was misremembered. Innovators succeed by proving that their innovation is actually a return to proper form. The cleverest citizens are not open rebels but interpreters of precedent.

Their religion, likewise, is less concerned with creation than with continuation. The world is imagined as still soft and unfinished, liable to lose its form unless pressed, stamped, sounded, and renewed. Daily labor has a ceremonial aspect because every repeated act helps persuade reality to remain consistent. Bricks are laid in counted courses. Cistern rims receive handprints from every household that draws from them. At the fixed hour of rain, tools are briefly lowered—not in silence, which is impossible, but in stillness—while water begins to strike roofs and courtyards.

The cloud is not viewed as a sky. It is the Low Cover, a permanent substance with weight and temperament. Some say it is the underside of an immense hide stretched over the plain. Others teach that it is condensed breath from all living creatures, held above the city until the appointed hour when part of it returns as rain. The rare suggestion that something lies beyond it is treated not as heresy but as a peculiarity, like claiming to remember one’s own birth.

There are specialists who study the cloud by watching brightness, measuring echoes, and releasing marked birds upward. The birds always return wet, frightened, or not at all. Their findings are preserved in sober clay records, though no useful change has ever been detected. This failure does not diminish the observers’ prestige. In an unchanging world, proving that a thing remains unchanged is serious work.

Fire is more sacred and more dangerous. It alone seems capable of forcing the young earth to become final. Clay enters a kiln soft and returns fixed; flesh enters flame and becomes smoke, grease, and bone; mineral colors emerge brighter or blacker than before. Kiln masters are consequently regarded with a mixture of dependence and unease. Their arms are latticed with burns. They marry mostly among other kiln households, and they maintain private signs that can be seen through smoke.

Death does not lead to burial in soil, for there is scarcely any soil, and the water table rejects deep graves. Nor are bodies commonly burned whole, since fuel is precious. The dead are dismantled with solemn efficiency. Flesh may be rendered or given to designated animals; clean bones are sorted according to use and relationship. Some become roof ribs in the family dwelling, some handles for familiar tools, some counters in memorial games, some flutes or hinges, some sealed deposits beneath thresholds.

This is not considered a reduction of the person. To remain useful is the desired afterlife. A grandmother may continue as the polished latch touched by each descendant entering the sleeping room. A drover’s finger bones may form toggles on the harness of his favorite cattle line. The skull is usually kept intact longer than the rest, coated in clay, marked with the household pattern, and placed in a wall niche until no living person remembers the face. Then it too may be worked.

Memory is material because the landscape forgets nothing. Children grow up among visible consequences: the rut made by a famous caravan, the wall blackened by an old kiln fire, the sharp fragments of jars dropped before their grandparents’ births. They learn history by walking it. Teachers lead them along refuse banks and expose layers with shell scrapers: flood clay, ash, slaughter bone, imported grain husks, broken roof daub. The city’s past is not a vanished country. It is a stack beneath the hand.

This gives the inhabitants an unusual sense of time. They do not imagine it as flowing away. Time accumulates. It makes thickness, weight, and obstruction. Too much past can block a channel or crowd a house. Forgetting is therefore an active civic duty. Old records are sometimes soaked back into pulp; unusable bone is ground; obsolete walls are dismantled course by course. To destroy carelessly is taboo, but to unmake by proper procedure is honorable. There are guilds of removers whose task is to prevent memory from physically choking the city.

Children belong first to sleeping halls, then to households. They are raised in groups amid livestock, noise, and many adults. Before they can speak, they are taught signs for water, hoof, fire, stop, danger, mine, yours, and witness. Games imitate civic labor: laying pebble streets to standard widths, routing trickles of water through clay, sorting bones by shape, guiding beetles or shell-creatures through tiny corrals. They learn balance by running on wet wall tops and patience by sitting beside tethered birds.

Their hearing is tested not by faint sounds but by meaningful ones buried in noise: the changed pitch of a wheel about to seize, the panic note of a goat, the splash that means a channel has backed up. Many adults suffer ringing ears or partial deafness. This is not regarded as a profound disability. The city is already built around gesture, touch, light, rhythm, and sight. Those born wholly deaf often become excellent overseers, negotiators, and teachers of children.

Beauty lies in ordered density. A handsome building has tight courses, an even curve, clean runoff, and repairs fitted so well that the sequence of generations remains visible without weakness. A handsome animal is calm in harness and economical in movement. A handsome person is steady-handed, broad-backed, clear in gesture, and attentive to the space around others.

Adornment uses what the city possesses: polished horn, incised shell, glazed clay, feathers, teeth, colored salts, copper foil thin as skin, beads of volcanic glass. District colors appear on horn tips, ear tags, collars, doorposts, cheek stripes, and the rims of water jars. Hair is kept short or bound under hide caps because constant damp encourages lice and fungal sores. People rub their skin with fat and fine ash against the rain.

Festivals do not interrupt labor so much as synchronize it. At appointed intervals, every district stamps bricks together, or turns its mills at the same rhythm, or releases its birds in a single dark spiral. The greatest ceremony is the Accounting of Breath, when herds and people are counted at gates. Each living being passes beneath a clay arch and is marked: hand, hoof, paw, or wing pressed briefly against wet plaster. For several days afterward the walls bear a crowded census of impressions. Rain slowly softens them, but never entirely erases them before the next accounting.

Music favors instruments that can survive and penetrate the city’s roar: enormous clay horns, skin drums stretched over ceramic frames, shell rattles, bone clappers, and tuned paving stones struck with padded mallets. Subtle music exists too, but it is felt rather than heard. In sleeping halls, performers tap low patterns into walls and floors while listeners lie with cheeks or sternums against the surface. Love songs may be drummed directly along a shared partition.

Affection is practical and bodily. Lovers grease one another’s cracked hands, clean mineral crust from each other’s hair, trade sleeping places nearer the warm animal wall, or memorize one another’s private palm-names. Marriage is less a union of isolated individuals than an agreement between labor groups, animal claims, cistern shares, and sleeping rights. Separation is possible, but the disentangling of tools, beasts, debts, witness chains, and ancestral bone objects may take years.

Strangers from greener countries find the inhabitants severe. They are disconcerted by the absence of flowers, shade, quiet, and private space; by the frankness of slaughter; by old bones incorporated into everyday things. The city people, in turn, consider outsiders wasteful and dangerously inattentive. Visitors burn wood casually, fail to read hand signs, let clean water run unused, or assume that an empty-looking patch of clay belongs to no one. Worst of all, they often speak of the plain as barren.

To its inhabitants, nothing could be less true.

The plain produces water, salt, clay, stone, warmth, roads, walls, vessels, and the exact conditions under which their animals and institutions endure. It merely does not produce green life. Green things belong to elsewhere: fragrant, unstable regions beyond the cloud belt, where roots crack masonry, insects hide in leaves, rivers alter their banks, and seasons make promises they do not keep. Imported timber is valued, but a living forest sounds to city ears like a place where the ground is concealed and countless uncontrolled things rise overhead.

They prefer what can be seen.

They prefer the honest wall, the numbered herd, the channel whose course was cut by known hands. They prefer the low cloud that gives the same light each day and the rain that arrives at its hour. They trust the city’s enormous noise because it is made of identifiable needs: hunger, labor, friction, water, warning, trade.

Silence is what frightens them.

When a mill stops unexpectedly, people emerge into the lanes. When birds cease circling, watchers climb the gates. When a whole pen falls quiet, even the deaf feel the altered air. Silence means a broken sequence, a missing pressure, something no longer answering what has always called to it.

And so they continue: compact bodies among compact walls, shaping the soft world by repetition. They dredge yesterday’s channels, rebuild yesterday’s courses, count the breathing creatures, and press fresh marks beside old ones. They do not believe they inhabit the beginning of anything. As far as they know, beginnings are what happen constantly—the moment clay meets a hand, the moment rain strikes dust, the moment a newborn animal first draws breath beneath the cloud.

Everything begins.

Nothing is permitted to begin alone.
