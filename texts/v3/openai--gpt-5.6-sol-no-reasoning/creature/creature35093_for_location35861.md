The people who live here have learned not to confuse stability with stillness.

They are human in the ordinary ways that matter most. They sleep, quarrel, raise children, forget appointments, cultivate preferences, and complain about public maintenance. They bruise. They grieve. They grow old, though unevenly: a person may keep the same face for twenty years and then seem to age through an entire decade in one wet season. Whether this is caused by the city or merely made more visible by it is disputed.

Their bodies tend toward the long and lightly built, adapted less by design than by habit to warmth, dimness, and great distances that do not always behave consistently. Skin is commonly deep-toned, though the spectrum is broad; many complexions carry a subtle gray, bronze, or blue cast from mineral exposure. Beneath condensation light, old scars sometimes show as pale branching lines. Eyes are large-pupiled and quick to adjust, but direct sunlight—when the blurred disc clears enough to produce it—is uncomfortable. People shade their faces and wait for the brightness to pass.

Hair is worn cropped close, bound flat, or gathered under loose hoods so that rain forming beneath balconies and lintels cannot collect in it. In underground districts, longer hair is common. Some inhabitants braid translucent rings cut from dead cane into it. The rings hold droplets that tremble in response to nearby fissures, serving as ornament, warning, and crude navigational aid.

Clothing hangs in quiet layers: porous wraps, sleeveless coats, divided skirts, narrow trousers, and soft-soled shoes that allow the pavement to recognize a gait. Fabrics are grown, extruded, or persuaded from sheets of fungal fiber. When dry they appear black, brown, or smoke-colored; when wet, woven patterns emerge in copper, violet, and green. Formal garments are designed to be rained on. At civic gatherings, people sometimes stand beneath controlled interior showers until their clothing blooms with family colors, district marks, professional signs, and old promises.

A garment that remains plain when wet is associated with mourning, anonymity, or municipal authority.

There are fewer inhabitants than the city seems capable of holding. No census agrees with another. The discrepancy is too large to be explained by error, but not large enough to become an emergency. Registers count homes that have sealed themselves around absent occupants, families who live in more than one district without traveling between them, and individuals repeatedly encountered in public places who do not appear in any dwelling record. The municipal offices list these last as “unplaced residents,” provided they obey water customs and do not open isolated doors during rain.

Most people live in small households. Kinship is recognized, but proximity and repeated care matter more. A household may include siblings, former partners, children with several acknowledged parents, elderly neighbors, and one person who arrived through a transit pavilion years ago and cannot identify the district from which they came. Such households are called courts, after the shared open spaces around which many homes arrange themselves.

A court does not necessarily remain in one building. Rooms migrate. Walls soften and close. Doorways follow use. If two households eat together often enough, their kitchens may gradually become adjacent even if their homes began on opposite sides of a lane. If a relationship deteriorates, corridors lengthen, entrances turn away, and interior acoustics cease carrying voices between rooms. Separation is therefore partly emotional, partly architectural, and partly a matter for mediators trained to negotiate with the building.

Children understand the city early, though not by theory. Before they can read, they are taught to test whether a shadow belongs to what casts it. They learn to watch the direction of water before crossing a temporary river and to greet doors discovered in open vegetation without touching them. A common childhood game involves following the migrating edge of a garden while trying never to step twice on pavement of the same hardness. Another is to place cups beneath roof lips and guess whether the first drops will rise or fall.

They are discouraged from imitating figures in the eastern field.

Despite this, children do so constantly.

They arrange themselves along invisible stairs, lift their feet to absent steps, and wave into empty high windows outlined by condensation. Sometimes a child pauses in the middle of the game and listens as if someone has addressed them. Adults do not panic unless the child answers with a name unknown to the court.

Education takes place in low communal houses that alter their interiors according to the lesson. Mathematics is taught through water routes, borrowed distances, and the geometry of rooms larger within than without. History is kept in several parallel forms because chronological records are unreliable near certain districts. Students learn the history of what happened, the history of what the buildings remember, and the history implied by the older maps. These versions are not forced to agree.

The oldest surviving civic records claim the first inhabitants arrived after the ground rose, but the language is ambiguous. It may also mean they rose with it.

Some residents believe their ancestors built the city. Others say the city assembled a population appropriate to itself from travelers, refugees, survey teams, and people misplaced by distant transit systems. A quieter tradition maintains that human habitation is one stage of the city’s growth: people are temporary organs through which it learns preference, regret, hospitality, and restraint.

This idea is considered poetic in public and operationally plausible in municipal planning.

The closest thing the city has to a government is the Office of Agreements. It occupies a shallow crescent-shaped complex beside three cisterns and is never entered through the same door twice. Its workers do not command the infrastructure. They represent human interests in negotiations with it.

They petition pavements not to reroute during funerals. They ask occupied houses to preserve enough fixed geometry for infants and the elderly. They mark land where roots, water, and habitation have reached incompatible claims. They interpret the low tones of empty transit pavilions. They oversee rainfall allocation, though “oversee” often means noticing where rain has already chosen to occur and devising a fair explanation afterward.

Its officials are identifiable by dry-gray clothing and bracelets of porous stone. They are patient, exact, and widely teased. A good official can spend an afternoon with one hand against a wall, determining whether a missing doorway represents structural adaptation, domestic conflict, or a refusal by the house itself.

There are other professions peculiar to the city.

**Water readers** walk the channels before dusk, tasting mineral traces and predicting which temporary rivers will form. Their maps are useful for only a few hours. They carry shallow ceramic spoons and speak in directional verbs: water is said to remember, avoid, rehearse, or reconsider.

**Threshold keepers** attend solitary doors during wet cycles. They do not open them. Their task is to prevent others from doing so and to record whatever sounds come through. Most hear transit tones, wind, or distant rooms. A few record voices speaking several minutes before the speakers arrive nearby.

**Volume stewards** manage public interiors during gatherings. They know how many people a room can borrow space for without returning it violently. They also locate residual pockets after crowds disperse: cupboards with the depth of halls, stairwells containing an extra landing, corners where voices take too long to come back.

**Root advocates** represent old trees and reservoir-growing vegetation in civic disputes. They are botanists, legal specialists, and patient listeners. Their work is taken seriously. A tree that has occupied a site longer than the surrounding structures has strong standing.

**Continuity physicians** treat ailments of sequence rather than flesh. Their patients have often traveled at an incorrect transit hour, slept beside an opening fissure, or crossed water against the flow. Symptoms include memories that arrive before the events that cause them, wounds that close but continue hurting, and the conviction that one’s home is waiting several days in the future. Treatment emphasizes routine, shared meals, familiar scents, and walking the same route until the pavement recognizes the patient consistently.

Ordinary medicine is sophisticated and unobtrusive. Responsive membranes seal injuries. Mineral baths encourage bones to knit. Certain fruits are cut open and laid humming-side down over fevered skin. Much care still depends on touch, observation, and somebody remaining nearby through the night.

The inhabitants are not worshipful in a simple sense. They hold ceremonies, but few agree on what receives them. At the beginning of a wet cycle, some households place empty bowls beneath tables to acknowledge water emerging from the undersides of things. At funerals, the dead are laid on bare stone until their shadows settle in the correct direction. The body is then given to a sealed garden, a deep cistern crypt, or, rarely, a house that has consented to absorb it.

Names of the dead are spoken during rain, when surfaces are most receptive. Buildings sometimes preserve them. Years later, a room may make the acoustic shape of a person’s voice without repeating any recognizable words.

No one builds monuments higher than trees. Remembrance spreads laterally: a path, a fruiting hedge, a bench that becomes warmer when occupied, a line of copper color awakened across leaves. Important lives are commemorated by changing how people move through a place.

The inhabitants value spaciousness. Privacy is not enclosure but distance—the right to have an interval of meadow, garden, silence, or unused architecture around oneself. A person under strain may be granted an empty lot rather than a room. They go there to sit among waist-high leaves while the nearest occupied building remains visible but cannot be heard.

This makes the city appear lonely to visitors. It is not. Attention travels well across open ground. People know which roof lights belong to which court, who has begun taking the eastern road at unusual hours, whose rain has failed to arrive, and which underground elevator has remained suspended too long. Help is often offered indirectly: a basket left where a migrating doorway will reach it, a channel cleared upstream, a transit pavilion occupied at an inconvenient hour so its tone will stop troubling a nearby house.

Markets form after rain, when paths are lit and fruit is easiest to judge. They are loose, quiet affairs spread across plazas and roof terraces. People exchange food, cultured fabrics, shaped stone, access hours, preserved acoustics, and small containers of district water. Currency exists, but favors and maintenance rights are equally important. A person might pay for a tool by agreeing to walk a neglected street for a month, giving the pavement enough repeated use to remain coherent.

Art is difficult to separate from civic function. Singers perform beneath bridges so that fragments of their songs enter the water and emerge in impossible channels. Gardeners breed moss that reveals color in remembered patterns. Architects compose houses by establishing habits around an initially featureless mound. Dancers work with responsive floors, teaching them rhythms that may persist for generations.

The most respected artists are those who make something the city chooses to continue.

They are wary of permanence, but not of commitment. Permanence here means refusing revision, which is both unnatural and dangerous. Marriage vows, civic oaths, and apprenticeships are therefore phrased as recurrent choices: not *I will never leave*, but *I will return by every route that still knows me*. Not *this house is ours forever*, but *while it makes room for us, we will make room for it*.

Disputes often concern interpretation. Did a bridge shorten for everyone or only for one traveler? Did a house erase an entrance as a judgment, an accident, or an invitation to approach elsewhere? Is localized rain public water if it falls inside a private foundation? Such matters are argued slowly. Evidence includes witness testimony, surface response, root direction, acoustic residue, and the behavior of animals.

The six-legged grazers are tolerated and sometimes followed. Because their hides show nearby places as they looked moments earlier, lost objects can occasionally be recovered by watching the reflection on a feeding herd. Children try to stand behind them and glimpse themselves in the recent past. The animals usually angle their bodies away.

The black birds are never counted aloud. When they appear along a dry channel, people avoid passing between two of them. Some say the birds mark a route that water intends to use. Others believe the intervals between them are the actual creatures and the bird-shaped forms merely boundaries.

The enormous thing beneath the plain is treated like a seasonal hazard, an elder, or a geological fact according to temperament. When it passes, surface residents stop pouring water. Underground residents extinguish unnecessary vibration. Children lie with their ears to floors. No one has seen it, though there are competing diagrams in the schools: a buried animal, a mobile fold in the rock, a transit organism carrying districts too deep to reach, or the root of something whose crown exists elsewhere.

Afterward, certain people dream of pressure, dark water, and a body long enough to pass beneath the city for days.

The unsettling surface sky has produced two broad cultures. Surface dwellers are attentive to weather, distance, and the eastern field. Those below favor stable communities, controlled moisture, and the immense artificial depth above their ceilings. Neither regards the other as separate, but misunderstandings persist. Surface people say underground residents trust architecture too much. Underground residents say surface people have mistaken exposure for honesty.

Many alternate between the two. Pregnancy, illness, mourning, or prolonged shadow events may draw someone below. Adolescence, courtship, artistic work, and certain forms of recovery often bring them back to the surface.

A small number live almost entirely in transit pavilions. They know which hours connect which districts and which benches sometimes open onto places absent from municipal maps. They are called hourfarers. Some are couriers; some are fugitives; some simply prefer a life without a fixed doorway. They smell of wet mineral dust, carry little, and are careful never to promise arrival at a specific time.

Visitors also appear through the pavilions. Most come intentionally from settlements beyond the plain. A few enter believing they are stepping into stations in other cities. The inhabitants receive them kindly but ask three questions before offering directions:

What direction was the water moving when you arrived?

Did your shadow enter with you?

Have you been here before, later than now?

An unsuitable answer does not lead to punishment. It leads to observation, a warm meal, and a night in a room with four manually fixed walls.

The city’s greatest collective anxiety concerns the empty eastern field. Not fear exactly—fear would be simpler—but recognition without memory. During strong condensation events, the absent architecture becomes nearly complete. Water outlines balconies far taller than local trees, avenues suspended above the grass, and windows crowded with movements.

On such evenings, people gather at a respectful distance. Some bring instruments. Some bring chairs and food. Some refuse to look. The Office of Agreements places dry-gray officials around the rings, though no agreement has ever been established with whatever appears there.

The figures in the suspended city seem human at first. Then their proportions shift with the angle of observation. They ascend stairs without reaching higher levels. They gather at windows facing downward rather than outward. Occasionally one raises a hand at precisely the moment someone in the field does the same.

There are residents who believe these figures are ancestors from the drowned basin, inhabitants of the city before it was persuaded into its current geometry. Others think they are future citizens waiting for the terrain to finish. The underground schools teach that they may be neither people nor images, but unrealized arrangements: possibilities made visible by water.

A few inhabitants feel called to them.

Such people begin by dreaming of rooms with high windows. Their shadows lengthen eastward before anyone else’s. Responsive walls become reluctant to recognize them. Transit pavilions open for them at incorrect hours, revealing not another station but wet stairs suspended in dim air.

Most seek treatment. Some accept municipal supervision and live ordinary lives for decades.

Others walk into the field during condensation.

They do not vanish dramatically. They continue across the grass until distance and darkness should obscure them. Then droplets gather around their outlines. For several minutes they appear to be climbing. By the time the water falls, they are gone.

Their households do not hold funerals. They maintain a place at the table during rain.

Yet daily life remains stronger than mystery. People wake to condensation tapping beneath eaves. They eat cool fruit and bitter leaves, repair ceramics, negotiate rooms, tend luminous borders, argue over routes, flirt beneath bridges, and return home by paths that have shifted only slightly. Children race the illumination along wet grass. Elders sit in courtyards and identify districts by the taste of their water.

At night—if the deeper dimness can be called night—the city’s inhabitants are visible from a roof as scattered, deliberate signs. A wet sleeve flashing violet. A lantern shaded toward an unmatched shadow. Figures crossing temporary rivers in the proper direction, even when it requires a long return. Families gathered in rooms whose walls have expanded to hold them. Solitary walkers pausing to feel whether the ground is about to revise itself.

They do not think of themselves as living in an impossible place. Impossibility is a word for systems observed from too far away.

To them, the city is unfinished, temperamental, generous, and alive in ways that do not excuse neglect. It must be attended to. So must a child, a cistern, a grieving house, a road losing confidence in its destination. Citizenship consists largely of such attention.

And beneath all their customs is a patience that resembles courage. They live on young ground. They know the plain may still be deciding what it is, that their homes may be provisional, that another city sometimes appears in water and watches them through windows not yet present.

Still, they plant—carefully, never beneath the wrong shadow.

They build—never higher than a tree.

And when the rain begins under the roofs, they set out bowls.
