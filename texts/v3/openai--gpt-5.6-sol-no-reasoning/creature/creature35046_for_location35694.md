Those who live here are difficult to count, partly because they keep apart, partly because not all of them are continuously present.

A resident seen at a distance appears tall and lightly made, with the narrow economy of something evolved—or redesigned—for heat. Their limbs are long, their heads carried very still. Loose garments hang from shoulder frames and hip belts without touching much of the skin. The cloth is usually dull black, rust, or mineral white, but its surfaces brighten briefly in response to pressure waves, so that a passing transit detonation moves across a crowd as a pale shiver.

There are rarely crowds.

The people arrange their lives in constellations, like the buildings: one person tending a market vault, another visible half a kilometer away on a shaded balcony, three more separated across a garden large enough to be mistaken for countryside. They wave across these distances with mirrored paddles or changes in posture. Familiarity does not require proximity. Intimacy, especially, is practiced far apart.

Their bodies are sensitive to vibration. The soles of their feet are broad and densely ridged; even through footwear they can distinguish an approaching vehicle, a migration of grazers, a shift in the buried pulse. Children learn the subterranean rhythms before they learn public speech. They lie belly-down on warm paving and name distant events by feel: rain opening a channel, a tower correcting itself, a house sealing after abandonment. Adults retain the habit of pausing mid-conversation when a new frequency enters the ground.

Their ears are small and often covered. Hearing without protection is considered both unhealthy and indiscreet. Most wear collars, jawpieces, or delicate webs stretched behind the skull, devices that separate the city’s layers of noise. Through them, the transit reports can be reduced to soft clicks, animal calls sorted from civic announcements, and a single person’s voice carried intact across a boulevard. Removing such a device in company is an act of trust. Removing it outdoors is an act of surrender.

Speech is used sparingly. At close range they communicate with minute movements of fingers and throat, supplemented by bands of color that pass over the clothing. Spoken names are not exchanged casually, because the city repeats sounds. A name uttered near a station may return days later in an empty district, spoken by an announcement system that has placed it in the wrong tense. People therefore keep several names: one for civic machinery, one for strangers, one for those living beyond the permitted sleeping radius, and one that is written but never sounded.

They are not entirely uniform. Some have ordinary skin, though it tends to carry metallic dust in its creases. Others possess translucent patches along the ribs or throat, beneath which dark branching organs expand during periods of silence. A few older residents wear bodies that appear partly ceramic: porous cheeks, articulated fingers the color of fired clay, seams colonized by harmless moss. Whether these are prostheses, adaptations, or the city gradually incorporating its citizens is not discussed as a meaningful distinction.

Nearly every resident carries water. Not much—a spoonful, perhaps, held in a living vessel attached to the chest. The vessel curls open beneath sweating walls and leaf tips, collecting particular droplets while rejecting others. This water is used for medicine, greeting, gardening, and proof. When two people meet after a long separation, each places one drop on the other’s wrist. If both drops travel toward the pulse rather than toward the ground, they accept that they have arrived in the same version of the city.

They are careful about versions.

The thin towers do not always keep measurements in agreement, and the inhabitants have developed habits for surviving small contradictions. They mark their height each morning on doorframes. They count fingers after transit. They leave weighted strings hanging in rooms to determine which direction is presently down. Appointments specify not only a place and time, but a temperature, a background frequency, and the expected number of shadows. Anyone arriving with the wrong number waits outside until conditions reconcile.

In the abandoned district of conflicting elevations, a few people still live among the animals. They call themselves the Inclined. Their bodies have adapted unevenly: one leg may be older than the other; hair falls in several directions; tears climb toward the temples. They serve as surveyors, retrieving objects and occasionally people lost between incompatible floors. They speak in paired statements, one describing what is true from here and another what is true from elsewhere. It is considered rude to ask which they believe.

Most residents are custodians of some kind, though few own what they tend. One listens to a kilometer of wall for blocked pores. One trims the edge of a sheet-tree so it will turn cleanly with the wind. One records the frequencies preserved in station moss. One wakes dormant organisms in a dry channel to determine whether rain has occurred upstream—or will. The café keeper maintains a single table because, under certain conditions, a second table would constitute a gathering. The station attendant welcomes two passengers into a hall built for thousands and checks whether either has dreamed of architecture.

Their work is patient because the city is more alive than obedient. Houses must be coaxed into useful shapes. A room preparing to bud is fed scraps of mineral foam and spoken to through the wall. Migrating doors are tagged with braided vines so they can be found again. Furniture is kept light and bitter-tasting to discourage neglected dwellings from digesting it. When a house closes into its seed form, neighbors do not attempt to reopen it. They carry it to an empty foundation, orient its narrow end toward the unseen sea, and wait. Sometimes years pass before it germinates into a different building.

The residents eat chiefly at dawn, when walls exhale. They scrape fragrant mineral films from ceramic surfaces, mix them with marsh starch and the silver bodies of channel swimmers, and steam the paste inside folded black leaves. They cultivate fungi in acoustic chambers, each strain nourished by a particular civic noise. Transit-fed varieties are dense and sharp. Those grown on antler calls are sweet and hollow, filled with warm vapor. Silence-fed food is rare. It is served uncooked and disappears on the tongue before its flavor can be named.

Their children are few and are not raised inside a single household. A child moves through a circuit of dwellings, sleeping alone in small nursery structures positioned safely between adults. At night, caregivers speak through the ground by tapping foundation plates. Thus a child falls asleep within a field of distant pulses: reassurance without dangerous nearness.

Dreaming is treated as a public emission. Before sleep, residents report the general category of dream they expect—water, pursuit, ancestors, impossible rooms—and the municipal system assigns them a safe location. Those prone to architectural dreams sleep farthest apart. Lovers may share waking lives for decades without ever sleeping within sight of one another. They build paired bedrooms on opposite sides of gardens and leave lights burning in both. In the morning they meet halfway, compare what followed them out of sleep, and inspect the ground for new weight.

The smooth forms in the old dense neighborhoods are not called ruins. They are called agreements.

People say they formed when too many sleepers dreamed the same shelter at once. Walls thickened around the thought before anyone woke. Doors became uncertain, then ceased to have existed. The inhabitants were not crushed or buried; they were included. Instruments detect faint internal weather. On some mornings condensation beads across the surfaces from within.

No funerals take place near these forms.

Ordinary death is handled by the houses. The dead are seated in an unused room with water vessels, personal frequencies, and a cutting from a favorite plant. Over several weeks the room contracts. When it opens again, the body is gone and a warm ceramic object remains, perforated like a seedpod. These are placed high in rope-vine colonies, where sleeping animals gather around them. During storms, some pods speak in the voices of the dead, but only about moisture.

The residents do not consider this survival. They are precise about survival.

There are also citizens who arrive only through transit. They step from displaced air wearing fashions no district recognizes, carrying permits issued by offices not yet built. Some claim to have traveled from the far side of the plain. Others insist the journey was between dates, not places. They are housed temporarily in market vaults, where their measurements are taken each morning. If they remain consistent for seven days, they may choose a vacant dwelling. If not, they are asked to wait for the next appropriate detonation.

A handful of people have no visible bodies at all. Their presence is inferred from doors that move against the house’s intention, cups lifted in silence, paths appearing where no lamp post stands. Civic records classify them as residents because they consume water and request repairs. They tend to occupy the upper levels of towers and are said to lean them away from approaching pedestrians, whether from shyness or to prevent contact.

Beyond the people, the city’s most numerous inhabitants are the little lives coating every usable surface. Wall beetles graze in tessellated herds. Engineered mites carry waste in glistening chains, passing each fragment mouth to mouth until it is reduced to heat. Condensation pipes are inhabited by translucent ribbons whose internal organs flash directional signals to the water. Children watch them to predict where the gutters will flow that evening.

The six-legged browsers in the plazas are tolerated as municipal gardeners. Each has a tall, fibrous body twisted around a hollow central space. Small animals shelter inside them while they walk. Their antlers, when present, are not bone but resonant growths pruned by specialists. An untrimmed browser can amplify its call strongly enough to open windows several districts away. They freeze at changes in the subterranean pulse because something beneath the plain occasionally answers them.

The black-winged flocks are less tame but intimately familiar. They sleep in rope-vines in layered masses, wings folded into transparent shingles. At dawn they release themselves in sections, first the outer animals, then those beneath, until the colony seems to turn inside out. They navigate not by sight but by the city’s predicted explosions. A flock knows where a vehicle will arrive before the station does. Transit planners monitor their movements and quietly revise schedules to match.

Flowers are regarded almost as fellow citizens. Their mass openings during silence are recorded as assemblies. Pollinators—needle-bodied, frantic, carrying blue light in sacs beneath their wings—wait at the edges of quiet zones and plunge in the moment sound fails. Residents caught inside an opening field remain motionless. A closing flower can fling a seed hard enough to pierce clothing, and the seeds prefer warm bodies as temporary soil.

The predators of the ornamental forests seldom attack people. They are broad, soft organisms that drape themselves between trunks, nearly invisible except when they snap stretched membranes to generate misleading cracks elsewhere. Their prey flees from the false sound and into the predator’s folds. During a silence pocket, however, this hunting method fails. The predators collapse into anxious bundles, and some residents approach to remove parasites from their sensory fringes. They are dangerous, but in silence they are embarrassed.

The deeper ponds contain inhabitants no census accepts. Long-legged amphibians stand on the lilies and lower threadlike tongues into depths that may not be beneath them. Larvae enter the water and emerge from cloud. Objects returned by the ponds often bear tooth marks, handwriting, or warm fingerprints. Once, a sounding line came back attached to a bell. The bell rang downward whenever anyone nearby lied.

At night, when bright mouths move through the sky without visible bodies, inhabitants extinguish unnecessary color. They cover water vessels, close the breathing apertures of their clothing, and stand beneath broad leaves until the mouths pass. The aerial predators do not seem to hunt flesh. They descend toward inconsistencies: mismatched shadows, misplaced memories, a person whose name has been spoken in two districts at once. What they take is subtle. Their victims remain alive but may lose all recollection of a street, a sibling, or the fact that water falls.

The silence pockets alter everyone.

Animals follow their moving boundaries because relief from the city’s constant violence is irresistible. People are more wary. To enter a pocket is to lose the background pulse by which they orient themselves. Some become nauseated. Some hear internal sounds they mistake for speech. Ceramic-bodied residents develop fine cracks. Yet silence is also when blue flowers open, when certain medicines can be prepared, when the hidden organs beneath translucent skin unfold.

There are specialists called hush walkers who travel within these zones. They wear trailing cords that remain outside the boundary, maintaining contact with the noisy world. They collect pollen, repair membrane predators, examine silent machinery, and retrieve those who have followed the quiet too far. Hush walkers claim the pockets are not empty of sound. Rather, something inside them is listening so completely that no vibration escapes.

When the distant lights appear beneath the ground, the city’s inhabitants do not gather to watch. Gathering would be unsafe, and they have seen what shared attention can make.

Instead, one light comes on in each occupied dwelling.

Residents stand alone on terraces, in stations, under leaf groves, along the sharp nacreous ridges. Even the Inclined emerge from their contradictory district. The unseen citizens lift cups. Browsers lock their branching antlers toward the horizon. Black-winged flocks settle all at once. Houses suspend their breathing.

Beneath the plain, the second city illuminates itself district by district: avenues first, then towers, then immense shapes for which the surface city has no corresponding architecture. Its lights are too large, too deep, and arranged with the same extravagant intervals as those above.

Some believe it is the city’s buried predecessor, waiting for the young crust to become transparent.

Some believe it is the city’s future, seen through one of the land’s geometric fissures in time.

Some say it is the population for whom all this space was designed.

The oldest residents offer a quieter explanation. They say the plain is not empty between inhabited districts. It is sleeping. The towers, roads, gardens, houses, animals, and widely separated people occupy only those narrow places where its dreams do not overlap.

And when the silence passes overhead, what switches on below is not another city.

It is the rest of the citizenry opening its eyes.
