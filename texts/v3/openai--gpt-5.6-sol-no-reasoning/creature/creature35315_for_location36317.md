The people of the root-city are slight, dry-bodied beings, built for a world where water is rarer than metal and every breath belongs partly to something larger.

Their skin is layered rather than supple: overlapping, translucent scales as fine as onion membrane, usually pearl, dun, or smoke-colored. Beneath them run dark capillaries carrying oils and latex-thin blood. They do not sweat. Heat leaves through ribbed vanes behind the jaw and along the forearms, which unfold in warm rooms and seal tight against dust. Their eyes are broad, matte, and protected by clear inner lids. Their mouths are narrow, with grooved teeth for opening resin vessels and comb-like structures that strain crystals from sap.

They drink seldom and ceremonially. A household may keep rights to a particular capillary, tapping it with a hollow ceramic thorn and sealing the puncture afterward with warmed wax. Most nourishment comes as resin jellies, pressed seed-meal, mineral wafers, bitter oils, and pale strips shaved from cultivated films. Meals are small but prolonged. Since flavor travels poorly without water, their cuisine depends on aromatic vapors, heated salts, texture, and electrical tingling from carefully prepared plant compounds.

Water, when encountered, is treated with unease. A cupful is medicine, laboratory stock, and dangerous luxury at once. Immersion is almost unimaginable.

Their clothes are made from felted moss, split bast, lacquered membranes, beetle ceramic, and thread spun from pruned listening tendrils. Loose cloth is uncommon because it gathers dust and catches on new growth. Garments fit close, fastened with smooth toggles that make no repetitive click. Workers wear face shrouds lined with absorbent powder; surveyors add broad-soled shoes that distribute their weight over immature tissue.

They are not a loud people.

This is partly custom and partly survival. Infants are soothed before crying becomes rhythmic. Doors do not knock; they discolor when touched. Public notices appear as shifting patterns in sap lamps or as scents released at appointed intervals. Names are spoken softly and often contain pauses, breathy consonants, and irregular stresses so that no voice repeats one vibration long enough to attract a listening tendril.

In crowded interiors they also communicate through touch. A finger laid against another’s wrist can convey warning, permission, affection, or doubt through brief coded pressures. Close friends develop private patterns. Lovers may converse beneath a blanket of sound-damping fiber without speaking at all.

Their hearing is extraordinarily sensitive. The bones around the chest and pelvis conduct tremors from the ground, allowing them to feel trains approaching, machinery starting several districts away, or a change in the host’s deep pressure. Many can distinguish an ordinary structural creak from the first report of a forming avenue. Children learn these differences before they learn maps.

The city’s inhabitants do not think of the root as a god, although outsiders might mistake their caution for worship. To them it is host, landscape, ancestor, infrastructure, and neighbor—alive but too vast and differently timed to be a person. It may react, remember irritation, redirect growth, or withdraw nourishment, yet whether it intends anything is a matter for engineers and philosophers.

They call it by terms that translate imperfectly: the Underbody, the Pale Continuance, That-Which-Is-Still-Forming.

Their central moral idea is accommodation. Virtue means leaving room for another life to continue becoming. A good citizen does not merely avoid injury; they arrange their habits around anticipated growth. They do not build where the tissue may need to open. They do not insist that a useful chamber remain unchanged. They do not confuse occupancy with ownership.

As a result, households are mobile even when their members remain in one district for generations. Furniture is light, collapsible, and grown with handles. Family records are kept on thin ceramic leaves that can be packed in minutes. Every residence maintains an “annexation chest” containing masks, lamps, nutrient packets, marking stakes, and soft wedges for freeing possessions from sudden growth.

When a wall begins to bud, the residents do not cut it flat. They consult a shape-keeper.

Shape-keepers are part architect, part arborist, part mediator. They read vascular color, heat, tension, and electrical drift to determine whether a building can be persuaded to retain its present form. Sometimes they fit a branch with a ceramic collar and guide it into a balcony. Sometimes they declare that the organism’s claim is stronger. The occupants then move out while the apartment becomes a thicket, gland, airshaft, or something for which the city has no name.

Such departures are mournful, but not considered dispossession. Funerary language is used instead: the household says the building has “gone inward.”

The dead are treated similarly. Bodies are not buried, because cutting graves into the root is unlawful, and they are not burned, because fire terrifies the host. Instead, the dead are placed in openwork ceramic cradles in the sorting gardens. Black filament-vines enter through prepared seams in the clothing and patiently disassemble the corpse.

Families visit while this occurs. The red flags raised by the vines are read as stages of relinquishment. When the process is complete, mineral residue is divided: some goes to civic refineries, some to household seedbeds, and a small portion is sealed into a keepsake tile. These tiles are often fitted into doorframes so the dead remain at the thresholds of successive homes.

Children are raised communally in quiet nurseries grown inside thick-walled pods. Their earliest games teach irregularity. They clap broken patterns, stop before repetition settles, and race across plazas without matching one another’s steps. A common children’s challenge is to cross a room full of hair-fine tendrils without drawing any toward them.

They are fascinated by echoes, though echoes are prohibited in most inhabited districts.

Certain old chambers are set aside as sounding houses, where trained listeners produce a single note and wait for its return. No second note may be made until the first has fully vanished. Composers write works separated by long silences—sometimes hours or days—so that the host never experiences them as persistent vibration. Audiences carry the unfinished pattern in memory and reconvene for its continuation.

Their music resembles architecture performed in time.

The most respected professions are those that interpret the root without pretending to master it. Surveyors read future districts in barely visible ridges. Vascular physicians clear mineral plaques and treat infected wounds. Rail gardeners cultivate gripping vines along routes approved only provisionally. Breathwrights maintain turbines in the great airways. Tendril-cutters move through sleeping neighborhoods with padded shears, harvesting acoustic growth before it seals streets in silence.

Factory crews practice a difficult discipline known as broken labor. Every task is divided into asymmetrical intervals to prevent the surrounding tissue from learning its rhythm. A press operator may work seven strokes, wait for a blue lamp, work twice, reverse the machine, then remain motionless while another line starts elsewhere. Skilled foremen conduct whole industrial wards in deliberate irregularity.

Predictable workers are considered dangerous.

Some citizens cultivate small harmless tendrils on their clothing or bodies. These “listeners” curl around the throat, wrist, or ear and respond to familiar voices. They are companions, recording devices, ornaments, and sometimes witnesses in legal disputes. A listener that has grown with someone for many years may reproduce the pressure-pattern of their speech after death, though never the words themselves.

The city’s animals live close to its people without being fully domesticated. Resin moths gather around communal lamps and are gently swept into pollination corridors at the end of the lit period. Bark hares roam freely, tolerated because their grazing keeps nourishing films from thickening into vascular obstructions. Tube birds nest in chimney crowns and warn of pressure changes by falling silent.

Ceramic beetles are kept in workshop drawers to clean tools of metallic residue. Children paint household marks on their shells, though the marks flake off as the beetles grow.

The six-legged browsers of the margins are more cautiously valued. Some carry cultivated mite-gardens that produce medicines unavailable anywhere else. Herdkeepers follow them rather than confine them, sleeping in portable shell tents and marking safe routes across embryonic ground. Their work gives them a reputation for independence and strangeness. They return from the outskirts with unfamiliar scents in their clothes and news of structures that are not yet on any map.

Splice-cats are feared, admired, and occasionally hunted. Their passage can extinguish a street one lamp at a time. Because they sense electrical fields, citizens caught near one drop all powered tools and stand with their arms away from their bodies, trying to become indistinguishable from the root’s background current. Old individuals sometimes learn the pulse-signatures of rail lines and follow trains beneath the tissue.

No one knows whether the splice-cats originated in the buried wilderness or were once made for vascular maintenance and escaped their purpose.

Politics in the city centers on competing interpretations of need. The organism cannot speak in law, so people must decide what its swelling, discoloration, electrical disturbance, or altered breath means. The civic assembly includes elected residents, industrial delegates, shape-keepers, host physicians, and silent seats representing uninhabited tissue. Those seats remain physically empty. Proposals affecting undeveloped ground require additional votes, as if the absent territory had objected.

Permanent property is impossible, but stewardship can last generations. Status comes less from possessing space than from maintaining a difficult relationship with it. A family respected for keeping an unstable tower habitable without stunting its growth may hold greater prestige than refinery owners.

Crimes against the host are treated seriously. Deliberately severing a major vessel can exile an entire household. Unauthorized flame brings collective panic: shutters close, turbines stop, animals flee, and contraction alarms ripple through the city. The offender must often spend years repairing fire-scar, applying cooling paste and reading the slow return of conductivity.

Yet the people are not uniformly reverent. There are illicit excavators who tunnel into sealed vessels for rare compounds. There are “still-builders” who kill patches of tissue chemically, then construct rigid dwellings on the dead substrate and claim true ownership. There are machinists who believe engulfment is not danger but union, and allow motors to be swallowed while still running. Deep within certain industrial trunks, half-living engines are rumored to beat in rhythms the city did not authorize.

There are also those who reject the chamber’s municipal cycle. During the dim interval they travel beyond the lamps, following translucent vessels through the outer rootways. These wanderers return with mineral growths, unknown larvae, and stories of other inhabited junctions.

Whether those cities are real remains disputed.

The deepest workers are a culture nearly apart. They descend for months toward the axial forest, wearing layered insulation and carrying no rigid maps. The tissue below changes too quickly. Their routes are recorded as sequences of temperature, scent, pressure, and sap color: left at the bitter vein, downward where the walls pulse twice, wait beside the warm white fold until it opens.

Those who return often develop pale branching marks under the skin. Some claim to remember places that did not yet exist when they passed through them. They speak more slowly afterward and leave long gaps in conversation, listening to movements no one else can feel.

During major pressure waves, every inhabitant becomes still.

Hands leave tools. Rail platforms stop wherever they are. Tube birds fold their vanes. Even children know not to move. In that shared pause, the citizens feel the city rise beneath them—not metaphorically, but by a measurable fraction. Their towers tighten in their collars. Amber windows bow. Old scars shine.

Then the new seam opens.

Surveyors are first to approach, but others follow at a respectful distance: gardeners with vine coils, traders imagining depots, families hoping for larger quarters, children daring one another to touch the glossy edge. The fresh tissue is warm and nearly colorless. It has no dust, no wear, no history. Sometimes faint shapes move beneath it as vessels decide where to go.

No one calls the opening empty land.

They call it an unanswered place.

Black stakes define where weight must not yet fall. For days or months, the people watch. They test the surface with padded hammers and listen through their bones. They note which way the heat travels, where leaf-discs begin to bud, whether the root is making an airway, a gland, a road, or something incompatible with settlement.

Only when the tissue hardens do they enter.

Even then, they proceed lightly, carrying buildings as seeds and infrastructure as dormant coils. They do not imagine they are founding a district from nothing. They are joining a decision already in progress—one made slowly, below language, by the pale continental life around them.

And when all machines cease, they know what the silence contains.

Not absence. Not sleep.

Construction on a scale for which they are merely one of the tools.
