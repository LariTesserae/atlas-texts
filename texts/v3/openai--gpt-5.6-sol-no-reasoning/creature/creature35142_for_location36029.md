The plateau is inhabited less by separate species than by a single, dispersed metabolism.

Its most numerous citizens are the iron-shelled insects beneath the moss. Individually, they are small, dark, and nearly anonymous: jointed bodies lacquered with metals drawn from the soil, mandibles fitted to particular fibers, sensory hairs responsive to minute changes in pressure and charge. Collectively, they are vast. Their colonies extend down through peat, root mass, fungal beds, and warm seams in the rock. Some castes shred leaf fall; some tend pale subterranean fungi; some precipitate iron and other minerals into armor; others serve as living conductors, carrying faint currents between wet roots and mycelial threads. Their nests are not buildings so much as altered ground—heated chambers, porous vents, cultivated decay. At dawn their breath rises by the thousand from the hills.

Around and through them lives the forest’s quieter circuitry. Fungi span enormous distances beneath the surface, surfacing as translucent fans, luminous bands, cups, threads, and soft shelves on suspended wood. They are cultivators and cultivated, digesters and signal paths. Algae film their bodies; moss grows over them; seedlings root in the moss. No organism keeps its boundary for long. Trees carry gardens in their crowns and nurseries in their bark. Fallen trunks remain aloft in the understory, slowly becoming bridges of soil before they ever touch the earth.

The plants are adapted not merely to wetness but to perpetual exchange with water. Sedges hold the tilted meadows together. Shrubs seal the slopes in glossy leaves. Ferns turn by degrees toward charged weather. Low, wind-shaped trees bind the exposed heights, while ravine forests rise taller in shelter, their crowns closing over rivers and trapping mist. Flowers occur everywhere in small, patient multitudes: blue beneath leaves, white above black water, red against soaked bark. Some bloom in response to currents passing through the soil rather than to sunlight alone. Before storms, whole plant communities behave like parts of one slow sensory organ.

Small gray grazers inhabit the tunnels under the sedges. Compact and thick-furred, they trim narrow passages through the vegetation, though the paths close almost as soon as they pass. In cold weather they gather over warm colony vents, sharing the faint mineral heat with insects and germinating seeds. Their predators know these places and wait nearby, motionless enough to collect droplets.

The bogs belong to long-legged browsers whose divided feet spread delicately over floating roots. They feed on fern tips, water plants, and the mineral-rich shoots around seeps. Even large individuals cross shallow pools without clouding them. Their young lie hidden in vegetation that leans over and closes above their backs. Alarm travels through a herd not as a cry but as a sequence of pauses: one animal becoming still, then another, until an entire marsh appears suddenly unoccupied.

In the canopy live creatures rarely seen whole. They travel through interlocking crowns without descending, their presence betrayed by a traveling depression in the leaves or a brief shower of retained rain. Some are light-bodied fruit eaters with grasping limbs; others peel bark for insects and fungi. Their coats host spores, mites, moss fragments, and seeds, making each animal a small vehicle by which one hanging garden colonizes another.

The ravines hold the plateau’s giants. These are slow, massively built mineral grazers, protected by overlapping plates of keratin. Their armor is ridged and porous, darkened by algae and patched with moss. Older animals may carry ferns in the seams of their backs. They scrape amber, charcoal, and green mineral films from wet cliffs, following inherited circuits between salt seeps and sheltered resting grounds. Rain runs from their plates in ordered rivulets. Smaller animals shelter beneath them during storms.

Their passage changes the land gently but for years. Each foot presses a basin into peat or soft soil. Water fills it; insects arrive; frogs lay eggs; sedges root along the rim. Abandoned tracks become chains of ponds, then miniature thickets. Scraped cliff faces weep fresh minerals and are recolonized in colored layers. The giants are therefore followed not only by scavengers but by whole successions of life.

Predators are narrow-bodied, low-moving, and economical. Dense growth makes pursuit wasteful, so they hunt through prediction. Some sense electrical disturbances transmitted through wet ground: the pulse of a hoof, the shifting charge of muscle, the sudden interruption of an insect current. Others wait beside vents, game trails, or salt seeps. They may remain in one position for hours, patterned like soaked bark. Violence, when it comes, is close and brief. The forest closes over it quickly.

Birds occupy the vertical spaces. Broad-winged gliders circle along escarpments and disappear into cloud below the level of the plateau. Tiny nectar feeders move among understory bells and bark flowers, their feathers adapted to shed constant moisture. Vast flocks migrate above the canopy as silent stippling against gray sky. Most species use restrained calls—a single pure note, a knock, a low breath—because the wet forest swallows sound before it can travel far.

Amphibians breed wherever weight has made a hollow. They live in hoofprints, root cups, cliff seeps, and trembling lake margins. Many are small enough to shelter inside moss. Some lay eggs beside the warm exhaust of insect colonies, where development continues through cold spells. After storms they emerge without forming a chorus; one answers another across long intervals, each call isolated by vegetation and mist.

There are also innumerable lives too small to claim a visible layer of the country. Mites graze algae from insect armor. Larvae filter seep water. Worms turn mineral powder into dark soil. Bacteria work in warm, airless chambers under colonies and carcasses. Spores remain dormant in bark fissures until lightning alters the local charge. Nothing dead stays merely dead. Antler becomes a pale calcium reef. Feathers become fungal substrate. A carcass warms a patch of earth, feeds a colony, and leaves behind a flush of flowers.

These inhabitants do not organize the plateau from above. No species governs it, and none appears capable of understanding its full extent. Coordination emerges through moisture, appetite, decay, pressure, temperature, and charge. Before a storm, insects alter their movement underground. Current passes into fungal threads. Blooms open or close. Spores loosen. Ferns incline toward the wind. Pools brighten faintly from within. Across distant hillsides, subdued green pulses travel through organisms that may never encounter one another directly.

When lightning spreads horizontally over the wet forest, the entire inhabited ground receives it. For a moment the plateau becomes legible as a network: root joined to fungus, fungus to colony, colony to mineral seam, mineral seam to pool and tree. Then thunder enters the ravines, the animals take shelter, and rain presses every leaf downward.

Afterward, life resumes without announcement. Grazers leave their root hollows. Birds release solitary notes. Giants return to the cliffs. Insects reopen their vents, and white vapor rises through the moss. Droplets gather on shells, whiskers, fronds, webs, flowers, and the plated backs of animals old enough to carry gardens.

What lives here is everything that can accept being lived upon in turn. Bodies are habitats; trails become waterways; wounds become mineral gardens; falls become foundations. The plateau’s creatures do not merely occupy the high country. Together, without plan or monument, they continually make it.
