They are a people of patience, though they do not consider patience a virtue. To them it is simply the correct rate at which consequences can be observed.

Their bodies are human, but altitude has written itself into them over generations. They tend toward deep chests, long torsos, dense webs of fine vessels beneath the skin. Their resting breaths are quiet and frequent. Visitors notice the darkness of their lips and nail beds, the economical way they climb, the small pauses they take before speaking after an ascent. Children learn early not to run uphill for pride. Exertion is treated as a calculation, not a test of character.

They are compact in their movements. In narrow passages they turn sideways without interrupting conversation, hands already finding rails polished by centuries of use. They do not gesture broadly near gauges, hot fittings, or exposed drops. A local can pass through a crowded mechanical corridor with the unconscious precision of fluid moving through a manifold.

Their clothing is layered, close-fitting, and severe in outline. Inner garments conserve heat and moisture; outer coats shed ash and resist sparks. Most have reinforced shoulders and narrow sleeves so they will not catch on wall hardware. Colors come from oxides and fired minerals: carbon black, iron red, salt white, copper blue, sulfurous yellow used sparingly as warning. Cloth is costly where nothing grows, so garments are patched with ceramic scales, metal thread, reclaimed membrane, and strips of felt made from industrial fiber. A coat may pass through five owners while retaining the names of all of them inside its collar.

They carry water close to the body in flat sealed vessels, partly to keep it from freezing and partly because water without an identified custodian is suspect. Cups have lids. Baths are timed, communal, and highly regulated, but not austere. Bathhouses are among the city’s most elaborate interiors: warm chambers of black tile, condensation channels worked in geometric patterns, mineral pools graded by temperature and prior use. To wash beside another person is an intimacy; to offer untouched rainwater is almost extravagant.

They have many words for water’s history. Not merely clean and unclean, but sky-first, roof-touched, skin-returned, stone-drawn, engine-sweet, pipe-old, ash-bitter, and water that has waited too long in darkness. Children memorize these distinctions alongside numbers and fault alarms. A person accused of misreporting water is judged more harshly than an ordinary thief, because the offense falsifies the substance’s future.

Their households are not arranged principally by blood. The basic domestic unit is the wall-sharing group: those whose rooms exchange heat, pressure, sound, and structural risk. They may be relatives, partners, apprentices, pensioners, or strangers assigned together after a terrace shift. They maintain one another’s seals and watch one another for altitude sickness. A family can quarrel privately, but a wall group must remain mechanically honest.

Marriage exists, though it is less consequential than joining foundations. Couples may live apart for years if their work belongs to different thermal zones. The more binding ceremony occurs when several households accept responsibility for a shared anchor, cistern, or pressure door. They stand before the exposed mechanism, recite its maintenance history, and add their names to the inspection plate. The promises are practical: who will wake when it ticks incorrectly, who will surrender heat during a failure, who will take the children upward if the lower rooms must be sealed.

Children grow among mechanisms that are never described as harmless. Their toys include jointed blocks, balance frames, miniature gutter systems, and slabs that fracture along hidden lines. They learn to read gauges before they read prose. A common game is to predict where a trickle will travel across a tilted plate while an adult slowly changes its orientation. Another is played with weighted pieces on a board whose level shifts imperceptibly through the day.

They are not forbidden from noise, but are taught that sound belongs to everyone who can hear it. Shouting is reserved for immediate danger. Music therefore favors low pressure, resonance, and touch. Instruments are built into walls or floors: strings stretched through ceramic cavities, rods sounded by thermal contraction, drums too deep to hear clearly but felt through the ribs. Public performances can last an entire evening while producing only a few notes. Audiences lean against stone to receive them.

The condenser towers are regarded as instruments, though no one claims to have built them for music. Their pre-rain tones form the basis of the oldest melodies. Singers imitate these notes in close, breath-efficient harmonies just below normal speech. Outsiders often fail to realize singing has begun.

Their language reflects the moving ground. Directions are rarely absolute. One goes heatward or skyward, basinward or along-strata. “Here” may mean the current place; another form means the place as surveyed; another, the place expected after settlement. Buildings have addresses that include both location and drift. Personal names often do the same. A formal introduction gives one’s name, wall group, birth terrace, and present alignment.

Certainty is grammatically expensive. Statements distinguish what was seen, what was measured, what was inferred from movement, and what was reported by a machine. This does not make them indecisive. Once evidence is categorized, they act quickly. But someone who says “I know” when they mean “I suppose” is considered dangerously vain.

Their engineers have unusual social standing, though “engineer” is too broad a translation. There are listeners who diagnose hidden stresses from sound; keepers of flexible joints; readers of old survey systems; cultivators of crystal structures; water accountants; fault historians; pressure physicians; and specialists who spend their lives tending a single process begun before their birth. The highest prestige belongs not to inventors but to continuers: those who inherit work without accelerating, interrupting, or subtly corrupting it.

A master may be celebrated for allowing a beam to grow for sixty years without introducing a flaw. No one expects the master to see it completed.

Because most important work outlasts individuals, authorship is distributed across generations. Objects bear lines of tiny marks recording every custodian. Failures are named for conditions, not people. Successes are seldom named at all. The city distrusts the fantasy of the solitary maker. Nothing solitary survives there—not a wall, not a process, not a person.

Their politics follows maintenance. Each terrace sends delegates from its water, heat, structure, and habitation councils. Debate is slow because every proposal must be translated into several timescales. What will it do tomorrow? During the next storm? After forty cooling cycles? When the fault moves? When the district above has drifted half a meter? Laws may include dates hundreds of years away, at which point they reverse, expire, or become binding.

Public office is marked less by privilege than by access to unpleasant knowledge. Senior officials know which retaining walls are temporary, which reservoirs are contaminated, which streets will eventually fail to meet, and which ancient machines have changed their behavior. They are expected to speak calmly without pretending safety is permanent.

The people do not worship the mountain exactly. Worship would imply an attention they do not believe the mountain possesses. They regard it as active but not alive, formative but not intentional: a vast unfinished condition in which they have taken up residence.

Their sacred feeling attaches instead to continuance—the improbable passage of heat, water, knowledge, and obligation through structures that must change without coming apart. Shrines are often maintenance niches containing an old valve, a broken gauge, a first survey pin now far from its engraved position. Offerings are not left to accumulate. A worshipper cleans the niche, replaces a seal, records a reading, and departs.

The dead are not buried; there is no soil, and the mountain is too useful to surrender cavities casually. Bodies are dried in low-pressure chambers. Water recovered from them enters a legally distinct reserve, never used for drinking but sometimes introduced into mineral fountains maintained by descendants. The remaining material is fired. Bone becomes pale ceramic aggregate incorporated into lintels, household tiles, or small acoustic pieces set inside walls.

Thus the dead remain not as images but as altered structural properties. A room may sound different because a grandmother is in it.

They have no inherited symbolism of green abundance. Their ideal landscapes are not gardens but stable fractures, well-spaced columns, slow accretions, surfaces that reveal how they cooled. Lovers give one another stones whose interiors cannot be known until cut. Mourning colors are glossy rather than dark. Celebration is marked by exposing fresh metal to weather so that its transformation begins in public.

Their art often consists of controlled incompletion. A sculptor prepares the conditions under which brine, heat, voltage, and time will produce a form that cannot be fully predicted. The maker may adjust flow for decades, then hand the piece to a successor. Finished work is viewed with mild suspicion. Finality belongs to breakage.

They are attentive to the dark films in the pipes. Officially, the films are a maintenance class: conductive fouling, category seven, responsive to potential gradients. Unofficially, many workers greet them before opening a conduit. Some leave tiny branching patterns scratched beside inspection ports, marks that resemble both circuit diagrams and family trees.

There are competing beliefs about them. One school says the films are merely chemistry made complicated by scale. Another says they are life prior to appetite, learning before desire. A more unsettling view holds that they are not native to the caldera at all but are expressions of the buried machines, distributed through plumbing because metal and brine serve as their nervous tissue.

Pipe workers report that established films respond differently to familiar hands. This is not accepted as evidence, but neither is it ignored.

The glass-winged visitors are treated with more open tenderness. When they appear, ordinary work slows despite regulations against distraction. People gather beneath condenser towers with covered cups, hoping to catch water after the creatures have drunk from it. Their warm shells are collected only after cooling and registered by district. To own one privately is illegal; to have witnessed one fall is remembered for life.

Children call them rain’s hinges.

As for the things crossing the salt basin, the city maintains watch stations and refuses consensus. Surveyors log the holes. Materials specialists analyze the silica. Night crews argue over whether distant rhythms can be heard through the rock. Expeditions descend with strict return schedules, but some return carrying equipment whose plumb biases have changed. Others find their clocks ahead by several minutes.

The inhabitants are not eager to resolve the mystery. They know classification can become a kind of blindness. Animal, machine, and geological process are distinctions the caldera repeatedly embarrasses.

In the deepest levels live the ring keepers. They are few, pale from years without direct sun, and recognizable by the double levels they carry: one for ordinary geometry, one for the local pull. They maintain the sealed chambers around the oldest machines, though much of their maintenance consists of observing surfaces that have not visibly altered in centuries.

They speak more slowly than other citizens and avoid metaphors of cause. Asked what the rings do, a keeper may answer with the list of things that happen near them: water inclines, clocks separate, tools acquire preference, dreams become spatially consistent among sleepers. They will not say whether the machines prevent something worse.

When a ring moves, the nearest residents close their doors and place shallow bowls of water on the floor. Families sit around the bowls without touching. The water tilts first. Sometimes it forms a surface whose angle corresponds to no direction recognized by the room.

Children are told not to be afraid, but to remember.

That instruction contains much of the city’s character. Not bravery. Not obedience. Attention.

They attend to hairline cracks and minute pressure changes, to the first tower tone before a storm, to the drift of thresholds and the histories of water. They notice when familiar echoes return late. They know which wall warms before an intrusion of magma and which stair has moved enough to alter an old person’s balance. Their restraint is not emotional coldness. It is a form of care adapted to consequences.

They laugh, though softly. They gossip over inspection work. They complain about council arithmetic, badly aligned doors, inherited tools, and young technicians who trust new sensors more than old noises. Adolescents climb forbidden radiator fins to watch the basin reflect the sky. Workers cheat at balance games by warming one side of the board. Lovers meet in open colonnades where their breath is taken by altitude and mistaken for feeling.

During rain, all of them become busy.

Children fasten collection cloths. Elders identify contaminated runoff by smell. Mechanics stand knee-deep in sudden steam, striking gutter hinges loose with padded hammers. Water clerks seal samples in numbered glass. People call readings from terrace to terrace, their voices briefly joined by the tapping of drops on hot stone.

Only when every cistern is assigned, every overflow diverted, and every dangerous channel closed do some of them stop working. They step into the outward-facing rooms or onto the covered stairs. They watch the temporary streams draw their first uncertain paths through the city.

Some hold out bare hands.

The gesture wastes almost nothing. A few drops strike the skin and vanish into heat. But for that moment they possess water with no history at all—water that has touched neither mountain, machine, nor law.

Then they close their hands and return to the gauges.
