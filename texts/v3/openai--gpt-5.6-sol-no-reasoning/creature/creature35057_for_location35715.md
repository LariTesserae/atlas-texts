The people of the bell are slight, broad-footed, and habitually bent—not deformed, though outsiders might think so, but shaped by low lintels, violent air, and the lifelong instinct to keep their weight close to a trustworthy surface. Even in open courts they crouch a little. Their toes spread widely, gripping dust and seams in the bronze, and the soles of their feet are callused into intricate maps. A barefoot citizen can identify a familiar street by its tremor long before recognizing its walls.

Their hands are more expressive than their faces. Fingers are kept bare despite the abrasive grit, and wrists are decorated rather than concealed. Bracelets would interfere with speech, so people wear bands above the elbow: ceramic rings naming household, trade, debts, bereavements, and public duties. The inner wrist is treated as intimate. To touch it without permission can be an insult, a proposition, or a challenge depending on the fingers used.

They are not deaf. They hear too much.

Children learn early to separate useful vibration from the bell’s immense, continuous voice. Adults can feel a neighbor descending a staircase three rooms away, distinguish a cracked brace from a loose cooking vessel, and recognize district announcements through several adjoining walls. Age brings “bronze sleep,” when the great tone overwhelms subtler sensations. Elders then carry polished listening rods, resting one end against a railing or floor and the other against the bones of the jaw.

Spoken language survives in fragments. People shout warnings, laugh aloud, and keen at funerals, but ordinary speech is considered vague and wasteful. Their true language is tapped into skin: quick patterns at the wrist, palm, forearm, shoulder, or back. Meaning changes according to pressure and location. A message tapped on bone is factual; the same rhythm pressed into flesh is emotional or uncertain. Two-handed conversation permits tense, irony, and several kinds of silence.

Names are rhythms. A child receives a household rhythm at birth, but earns a personal variation after surviving the first great reversal of air unaided. Some names are impossible for an injured or elderly hand to make, so descendants preserve the names of the dead by drumming them on doors during eclipses.

The inhabitants dress in close-wrapped cloth made from bellvine fiber, resin-stiffened at knees and shoulders. Loose hems are shameful as well as dangerous. Cords run from belts to tools, visors, baskets, and one another. A prosperous person is not heavily adorned but securely fastened. Poverty can be seen in mismatched knots and fraying tethers.

Every citizen carries an anchor line. Indoors it is looped casually through wall staples. Outdoors, when the bell inhales, it is clipped to communal rails. Lovers sometimes knot their anchor lines together in public, a gesture more binding than any spoken vow. The phrase for trust translates roughly as *to hold the end that cannot be seen*.

### Households

A household is defined less by blood than by a shared root court. Each garden supports only so many mouths, and each requires constant tending: scraping away invasive lichens, sweeping mineral dust into measured heaps, draining poisonous secretions, and ensuring that no root fan reaches a neighbor’s bronze. Thus families include gardeners, etchers, structural keepers, rope-makers, adopted children, old companions, and the descendants of anyone whose name appears on the repair plaques.

Inheritance follows responsibility. A person inherits the wall they have maintained, the stair they have braced, or the plant they have kept alive—not necessarily the chamber where they sleep. Ownership without upkeep is considered a contradiction.

Meals are small, frequent, and oily. Bellvine meal is pressed into green-gray cakes and softened over ember moss. Ash powder is mixed with wax and bitter root secretions. Hook-grazers are eaten only after festivals or infestations; their foil-like shells are saved for inlay. Nothing is drunk. Moisture exists only within plant flesh and warm pastes, and the old songs about water are treated with the baffled reverence accorded to miracles. Children often imagine rivers as moving ropes, or as streets that cannot be tied down.

### Trades

Gardeners hold the city’s most immediate power, but structural keepers command its deepest respect. They crawl into old foundations, press their faces to arches, and diagnose changes in the bell’s resonance. Their bodies are marked with pale mineral dust and black crescents from confined passages. They speak slowly by hand, often pausing with a palm against the nearest wall as if consulting it.

Etchers cultivate metal-eating colonies in clay matrices. Each workshop has hereditary strains distinguished by appetite and digestive pattern. A skilled etcher can grow a toothed edge, a spring, or a mesh fine enough to sift seed dust. Their failures are dangerous: a neglected colony may pass beyond its mold and begin consuming a railing, wall plate, or section of the bell itself. Unlicensed lichen is burned with caustic ash.

Rope-makers work with bellvine fiber, sail-beast membrane, and hair. They test every cord by harmonic strain, tightening it between posts and listening through their fingertips. No craft attracts more superstition. Rope-makers do not cut a taut line after dark, do not splice during a low note, and refuse to make thirteen-strand cords for anyone who cannot explain their intended use.

The rare hunters who butcher stranded sail-beasts are both admired and avoided. They smell of resin for months afterward. Their knives are ceramic-backed bronze, grown with deep serrations, and their armor is layered from old visors and cured membrane. Sail-beast flesh is mostly inedible, but its ribs become beams, its skin becomes wind cloth, its resin fuels kilns, and the hard hooks around its mouths make excellent anchors. A single carcass can enrich a district and kill dozens before it stops moving.

Then there are the listeners below.

Officially, no such trade exists.

### Faith and memory

The people do not agree that the bell was made for ringing. Some believe it is a vessel, some a prison, some an instrument whose true audience lives outside the opening. A minority teaches that the bronze sky is a cast-off shell and that the living creature which formed it has long since departed.

They rarely worship the unknown founder. Creation is too remote to be useful. Their devotions concern maintenance: the keeping of heat, roots, mortar, rhythm, and fastening. Shrines are built around ancient repairs. Offerings are practical—fresh cord, a wedge, a bowl of lichen suppressant, a day’s labor pledged to an unstable stair.

Their closest idea to sin is negligence.

Funerals are held during the downward current. The dead are wrapped tightly, weighted with etched plaques, and lowered into the brilliance rather than released. Families pay out the cord until it reaches its full length, hold the body suspended through one cycle of the bell, and then cut the line together. A body allowed to fall loose is believed to remain socially unfinished, still capable of being summoned by its name.

The names of the dead are added to household rhythms. Over generations, some households acquire names too long for one person to drum, requiring several relatives to perform them together.

### Children

Children are tethered to long ceiling lines until they learn the air cycles. They swing through domestic chambers, kicking off walls and rafters, often more at ease in three dimensions than adults. Their first toys are knot frames, clay clappers, and dead whisper-stalk joints that twitch toward vibration. They play at sail-beasts, sealed houses, and “loose thing,” a chasing game in which one child is carried shrieking toward an imaginary drop.

They are warned not to tap idle patterns against walls. Not because noise is irritating—the bell makes irritation meaningless—but because rhythm is an address.

Most obey. Some discover that certain old foundations answer with a faint return pulse. Children call these responses *the other hand*. Adults call them settling stone.

### Government

The city has no single ruler. Each inhabited district sends a hand to the Brace Council, whose meetings are conducted around a circular bronze plate so that every member can speak into it. Voting is done by sustained pressure rather than raised fingers; hesitation can be felt by all.

Political power rests on three questions:

Who maintains the railings?

Who controls viable root courts?

Who decides when a neighborhood must be sealed?

Sealing is the harshest civic act. Clay doors are raised across every entrance, possessions are removed if time allows, and the district’s rhythms are erased from the announcement rails. Sometimes sealing follows collapse, poisoning, or an etching outbreak. Sometimes it follows an answer.

The empty neighborhoods remain a wound in public thought. Their buildings are sound. Their ovens can still be seen glowing during eclipses. Their gardens are too healthy. Every few years someone argues that the city, diminished and crowded around failing root courts, should reclaim them.

Survey parties enter with padded boots and severed anchor lines so no accidental rhythm can travel back through the rails. They return with contradictory reports: fresh sweep marks, warm sleeping hollows, tools arranged beside unfinished work, bellvine pods harvested from the inside. Once, a party found a clay wall covered in wrist-language impressed by hundreds of hands.

The message appeared to be a list of names.

Several belonged to the surveyors.

### Those who answer

Beneath legitimate society lives a scattered fellowship who believe the second law is cowardice.

They listen in isolated chambers cut free from the main foundations, using long rods lowered toward the unseen clapper. They map the deliberate rhythms and claim they are neither random nor singular. Something below is teaching itself the city’s hand-language, slowly, from old announcements conducted through the bronze.

The listeners say the sealed districts did not die. They answered and were changed.

They say the emptiness there is deceptive because the inhabitants have learned to move only during the loudest cycles, when ordinary senses are blinded by vibration. They say those vanished citizens no longer speak wrist to wrist but wall to wall, distributed through foundations, roots, doors, and railings. A neighborhood, in this account, has become one person.

The Brace Council dismisses this as a cult born from isolation and bronze sleep. Yet foundations are increasingly cut away from the bell, and public rhythms are shortened each generation.

No one now drums a complete census.

### The deeper inhabitants

Far below, life gathers around the clapper.

The citizens know it only through shed skins caught on rising air, through resinous strands wrapped around old ropes, and through shapes crossing the brilliance where no sail-beast should be able to hover. The clapper may be the size of a mountain. Its repeated near-touch with the surrounding bronze creates fields of heat, metallic vapor, and violent force. Whatever lives there has evolved around impact.

Perhaps the slow patterns come from colonies striking the clapper’s suspension with mineral limbs. Perhaps they are feeding calls. Perhaps the bell itself has, over centuries of vibration and etching roots, developed structures analogous to nerves.

The whisper-stalks seem to know. During certain rhythms they do not merely turn toward the wall. They bend downward, all across the city, joint by joint, until every leaf points into the radiant gulf.

Then the hook-grazers hide. Ember moss cools. Bellvine pods close so tightly their seams disappear.

The people extinguish their lamps and crouch in rooms shaped by generations of hands. Each person grips another’s wrist, not speaking, feeling pulse beneath pulse beneath the colossal pulse of the bell.

From below comes the measured pattern.

It is never exactly the same.

But it always begins with the oldest household name in the city.
