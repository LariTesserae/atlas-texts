They are a people of strong hands, broad chests, and unhurried attention.

Their bodies have been shaped by water from birth. They are long through the shoulders and back, supple at the waist, with powerful fingers and slightly splayed feet suited to gripping rope and stone. Their hair is usually cropped close or braided tightly against the scalp. Loose hair is considered a private extravagance, worn only inside screened chambers where no current can seize it. Clothing consists of fitted wraps, weighted hems, harnesses, and belts crowded with hooks. Nothing is allowed to flutter unless it is meant to signal.

Children learn knots before spoken names. They are carried in netted slings against an adult’s chest until they can control their buoyancy; afterward, they move through houses by clutching walls, vines, older siblings, and whatever patient hand appears. Their first independent journey is rarely a swim across open water. It is a blind passage along a familiar guide rope, eyes covered, identifying each turning by its knots.

They have no word exactly equivalent to walking. Their verbs distinguish pulling oneself along, drifting under supervision, climbing against buoyancy, descending with weights, passing through vegetation, and crossing open water with no object in reach. The last is associated with bravery, foolishness, and grief.

They are quiet even when gathered in numbers. Underwater they speak with hands, faces, shoulders, and touches transmitted along shared ropes. Some signs are made against the body so they can be understood in darkness: two fingers at the wrist for danger in the current, knuckles to the sternum for low breath, a circling thumb in the palm for unstable stone. Families develop private variations. Married partners may conduct entire quarrels through pressure applied hand to hand.

Voiced language belongs chiefly to the air bells.

There, with only their heads above the trembling boundary, they become startlingly loud. Their speech is breathy and resonant, shaped by the rarity of air and by the strange acoustics of clay chambers. They favor long vowels because these carry well through bells; names often sound like calls made across a cavern. A conversation is governed by breath rather than rank. One person speaks while the others float below, watching, then dips back into the water to surrender the bell. Interrupting someone means physically entering their air, an intimacy permitted among lovers, small children, and enemies.

Silence is not absence to them. It is the ordinary substance of society.

Their most respected citizens are listeners: vent-tenders, stone surveyors, current readers, gardeners who can feel distress in a vine by holding its tether, and elders able to distinguish a settling wall from a waking fracture. A skilled listener lies with one palm on basalt and the other open to the water. The mountain reaches each hand differently. From the interval between those sensations, they make judgments upon which whole districts depend.

No one truly owns ground. Fresh stone is too changeable, and every foundation remains subject to the mountain’s revision. A household possesses attachments: its ropes, frames, air bell, garden ties, wall screens, shelves, sleeping nets, and rights to certain vents or thermal wells. If the stone beneath a dwelling becomes unsafe, the household removes itself piece by piece. To insist that a house remain where it was built is regarded as a kind of madness.

For this reason, ancestry follows structures more readily than places. An old air bell may have hung in nine dwellings on four ridges and still be spoken of as the same family home. Its clay bears repaired cracks, children’s finger marks, soot from ancestral meals, and lines incised to record every relocation. When descendants gather beneath it, they say they have returned, regardless of where the bell now hangs.

Their houses are inhabited by more than people.

Blind crabs patrol the warm channels cut through floors. Small filter-feeders cling to wall screens and close when strangers approach. Domestic luminous molds are cultivated in shallow ceramic portraits—not images of faces, but branching patterns associated with particular persons. The mold of someone healthy glows steadily. If it pales, relatives may infer illness, neglect, or a disturbance in the minerals feeding the household wall.

Some families keep schools of tiny fish rather than pets. The fish recognize feeding gestures and sleep in woven globes near children’s nets. Others tend a single cup-plant for generations, dividing its roots only when a household divides. To give away such a cutting is both adoption and promise.

Food is soft, mineral, smoky, and often eaten from lidded vessels. They cultivate orange-fleshed tubers, translucent fruits, kelp hearts, moss pastes, and dense cakes made from sifted microbial blooms. Sealed pots simmer for days in thermal wells. Feast food is cooked in gas chambers and carries the coveted taste of flame: charred vine rind, blistered fruit skin, or a sliver of vent-metal heated and plunged into broth.

They eat sparingly beneath air bells because chewing consumes speaking time.

Most meals occur in water. Diners secure themselves around a low frame and take morsels from covered bowls, opening each lid only long enough to remove a piece. Cups are unnecessary, though ritual vessels exist for freshwater gathered in the Weather. This water is tasted in droplets placed directly on the tongue. It is described as thin, cold, and almost flavorless—therefore mysterious. At weddings, the pair share one freshwater bead between their mouths without allowing it to escape into the sea.

Marriage is less a joining of bloodlines than a joining of breathing rights. To marry is to be admitted beneath another household’s bell during scarcity. The formal vow is made underwater, without speech, while both people exchange the knots that identify their home routes. Only afterward do they rise into air and say one another’s names aloud.

Children may belong to several interdependent households. Gardeners, vent-tenders, and kiln workers often foster one another’s young so that no child’s survival depends on a single district. Kinship terms distinguish those who fed you, those who taught you routes, those who shared air with you, and those whose bodies resemble yours. The person who first leads a child through open water holds a role comparable to parent, sponsor, and witness.

Their bodies bear the city’s hazards. Scars from obsidian are narrow and pale. Vent burns leave glossy patches. Many elders have thickened knuckles, damaged hearing, or permanently reddened eyes from mineral-rich water. Lung ailments are feared more than wounds. Someone who cannot safely use a communal bell may sleep beneath a private bladder, attended through the dim periods by relatives who massage the chest and sing into the membrane so the vibration can be felt.

Their songs are composed as much for touch as for hearing. Singers press a throat, jaw, or sternum against a wall, rope, bell, or another person’s body. A chorus may extend across several streets through a continuous lattice of taut vines. Those farthest away hear little but feel the pattern in their hands. Funeral songs are played into the basalt beside the dead until the first mineral skin begins to form.

They do not imagine death as ascent. Bubbles ascend and are lost; bodies descend only if abandoned. A proper death is an attachment.

The deceased is weighted not to imprison them but to introduce them to the mountain. As coral and mineral growth obscure the human outline, relatives trace the changes and memorize new contours. Ancestors are visited for advice, though no one expects words. A hand rests on the branching mound; the question is held in silence; whatever current, tremor, or animal passes next may become the answer.

This has produced a cautious theology. The mountain is not usually called a god. Gods, they say, would already know their own shape. The mountain is becoming, as the city is becoming, and requires assistance. Builders extend its intentions. Gardeners teach it softness. The dead teach it memory. Cracks are not commandments, but suggestions that must be tested.

Their priests, if they can be called priests, are the Keepers of Unfinished Things. They maintain abandoned frames, half-grown bridges, sealed doorways, failed bells, and foundations awaiting geological changes that may never come. They forbid the premature dismantling of certain projects. An unfinished structure can remain under their care for centuries, accruing interpretations. Some believe the city’s final form already exists in potential within the young basalt. Others hold that completion would mean death.

The most solemn taboo is to force an answer from stone.

No repeated hammering. No reckless drilling. No opening of a warm seam before its gases have been tasted by moss. No building over the oldest vent-crabs’ routes. Surveyors who misread a fracture may relinquish their bone rods and spend years tending burial coral. Yet failure is not always disgrace. The mountain is young, and uncertainty is recognized as a property of the world rather than a defect in the observer.

Their politics resembles net-making. Districts send knot-speakers to shared air halls, where proposals are embodied in cords. Every affected ward ties its conditions into the same length: breathing allotments, labor promises, garden access, evacuation routes, risks to burial ledges. A simple decision produces a simple cord. A difficult one may become a dense object requiring several people to carry and months to interpret.

Laws are kept in touch-readable archives. Knotted ropes hang in calm chambers behind woven screens, protected from grazing animals and mineral crust. Clay tablets are used only for matters intended to outlast the fibers, but clay is distrusted because it cannot be untied. A rope law may be amended visibly. A tablet pretends permanence.

Status is shown not through size but through slack. The poor must keep every object tied close. The prosperous have rooms calm enough for tools to rest unsecured, gardens broad enough to drift through, and air sufficient for idle speech. The greatest luxury is an empty chamber containing nothing but still water.

The Weather folk are considered elegant, cold, and slightly uncanny. Their skin is often patterned with pale mineral marks from the sinking rain, and they decorate themselves with freshwater shells or clear membranes stretched on narrow frames. They speak more than those below, having better access to trapped air, and their songs imitate the ticking of droplets on roofs. Lower districts accuse them of mistaking observation for work. Weather folk reply that someone must watch what comes from above.

At the warm lower margins live vent clans, broad-handed people whose clothing is darkened by black grit. They understand gas by smell, shimmer, and the behavior of crabs. Their apprentices carry small caged moss beds; if the moss folds, they retreat. They are admired, needed, and avoided at feasts, since an odor of sulfur follows them even after washing.

Gardeners often stain their arms with plant pigments so companions can recognize them in dense growth. Their work is intimate and combative. They untangle orchards from streets, coax roots into baskets, harvest gas without tearing leaves, and repel slow grazers without injuring them. Many claim that plants move differently around particular people. A gardener may therefore be chosen by a district’s vines rather than by family occupation.

Children adore the ray-shaped visitors and give them names that adults pretend not to remember. When one appears, daily labor stops along its path. People hang motionless among the gardens and watch it graze. Its route is later tied into a commemorative cord and stored among civic records, though no one agrees whether the animal has delivered a warning, a blessing, or nothing at all.

The unseen larger creatures occupy a different region of thought. They are called by circumlocutions: the Far Pull, the Orchard-Taker, the Scale-Bearer, What Passes Below. Children are warned not to mimic the tightening of ropes caused by distant bodies. Some adults leave offerings at the lowest boundaries—fruit, polished shell, a braid of hair—while publicly denying the practice.

The great clear scales are treasured but never cut. They are installed as walls in communal halls, where they bend lantern light and make everyone beyond them appear enormous and remote. During rites of uncertainty, a person sits behind one while neighbors contemplate the distorted shape. It is a reminder that a life may be correctly seen and still not be understood.

When the Lung Below fills, the entire city prepares.

Loose tools are tied down. Children are fitted with extra harnesses. The ill are moved near broad bells. Gardeners braid the longest fronds together so they will bend rather than tear. For days, citizens keep one hand on the mountain whenever possible. They feel pressure gathering in the stone.

Then comes the exhalation.

Bubbles erupt from walls, floors, burial ledges, streets, and old foundations. The inhabitants disappear in silver turbulence. Some laugh visibly without being heard. Some weep. Air that is usually measured breath by breath surrounds them in useless abundance, racing upward through the city.

When the violence eases, they enter temporary rooms beneath newly trapped pockets. Families speak together without taking turns. Choirs form in accidental chambers. Children shout until they cough. Lovers say things too wasteful for ordinary years. Old disputes are settled, or made worse, in the extravagance of unrestricted breath.

Afterward comes repair. Ropes are retied, bells inspected, gardens combed free of grit, and new cracks mapped. Some fissures will be warm. Some will be dead. Some may lead into chambers no person has entered before.

The first explorer does not go alone. One remains at the opening, one holds the route rope, one listens to the rock, and one advances with a lamp of living mold cupped between both hands. If there is space, they test it for air. If there is stone, they touch it for warmth. If something moves beyond the light, they do not pursue it.

They return and add a knot to the city.

Thus the inhabitants endure: not by mastering the mountain, nor by imagining the sea can be escaped, but by fastening themselves carefully to change. They live among things that are cooling, growing, filling, thinning, or waiting. Their wisdom is provisional. Their monuments breathe. Their ancestors branch. Their homes can be untied.

And whenever the dark geology offers them some new ledge, bubble, fracture, or living frame, they gather around it—not to declare what it is, but to listen for what it might permit them to become.
