The people who live there are compact, weathered folk, built less by abundance than by careful continuance. They are not numerous. Nearly everyone can be placed within a few large, many-branched families, though kinship has grown intricate enough that people often describe relations by houses, courts, or trades rather than blood. A person is said to belong to the north steps, the pear court, the lower kilns, or the gate stables. Names repeat across generations and acquire qualifiers: Elder, Narrow-Face, Mason, South-Roof, the Younger.

They dress for wind and stone. Wool is worn in layered tunics, close trousers, wrapped skirts, shoulder mantles, and caps that cover the ears. Colors come mostly from the material itself—smoke gray, dun, cream, brown—though hems may carry a narrow stripe of faded red or blue from costly dye. Their shoes have thick stitched soles, replaced often because the stairs consume leather. Nearly everyone carries some form of cord, knife, wooden cup, or repair needle. Water bearers use padded yokes; masons wear aprons polished pale by dust.

Their hands are broad-knuckled and marked by the place: chisel calluses, loom grooves, burns from lifting lids, cracks made by cold air and lime. Children acquire small scars early from rough walls and low lintels. Older people descend steps sideways, one palm resting automatically on stone polished by those before them.

No one here can afford to know only one kind of work. A mason may also keep donkeys, patch shoes, and judge the soundness of a roof beam. A weaver knows how to clear a drain. A child carrying grain can identify which cistern channel is clogged by the place where water darkens a wall. Skills pass through households, but they are not guarded as secrets. The town survives because knowledge is redundant: several people must know every necessary thing.

Water shapes their manners more deeply than law. A cup is filled only as far as it will be drunk. Washing is done in stages, the cleanest water used first and the remainder passed to floors, animals, herbs, or trees. Visitors are offered water, but never extravagantly; the honor lies in giving it without hesitation, not in giving much. Spilling a full vessel brings an abrupt silence. Children are corrected firmly for playing near gutters after rain, though they are allowed to crouch at grates and listen to the drops descend.

The keepers of the cistern are not rulers, exactly, but their authority is rarely challenged. They measure the waterline, inspect channels, assign days for cleaning roof catchments, and record how much may be drawn during the driest weeks. The office rotates among several households so that no family can claim the water as its own. Before the first expected rains, everyone able to work takes part in sweeping roofs, sealing cracks, lifting drain covers, and scraping soot from gutters. This is the town’s nearest thing to a great public festival. It is practical, tiring, and accompanied by shared food, teasing, old songs, and arguments about repairs made decades before.

Leadership otherwise belongs to a small council of household elders, working masters, and the current water keepers. They meet near the central well, where no speaker can pretend that private interests come before the town’s common limit. Their decisions concern stores, walls, grazing, apprenticeships, disputed rooms, and the use of abandoned houses. Punishments tend toward restitution and labor. Exile is rare and feared, for beyond the gates a solitary person has little shelter and no claim on stored water.

Property exists, but never absolutely. A family may possess a house, loom, flock share, orchard tree, or cistern jar; yet drains, retaining walls, stairs, and roofs often serve several dwellings at once. One household cannot neglect a wall if its collapse would damage another. Ownership therefore resembles responsibility. To say “this is mine” is also to say “its failure will be answered for.”

Their households are crowded and flexible. Grandparents sleep near the warmest interior wall. Infants are carried under wool mantles while adults work. Unmarried siblings, widowed cousins, apprentices, and travelers may remain for years without anyone clearly marking when they became permanent members of the house. Privacy is made with curtains, changes of level, and agreed silence rather than distance. Quarrels are common but seldom loud; walls carry voices too well. Anger appears in clipped greetings, a bucket set down too sharply, or a repair delayed until the last acceptable day.

Meals are plain: barley bread, lentil stew, onions, hard cheese, dried herbs, and occasionally goat or mutton brought from outlying herders. Pears are eaten late, after frost softens them, or sliced and dried above the hearth. Grain is roasted when fuel is scarce because it cooks more quickly later. Nothing edible is discarded casually. Even cracked grain becomes porridge, and the sediment from brewing goes to animals.

The town keeps few animals itself. Donkeys are valued with an affection disguised as complaint. Their temperaments, family lines, and preferred routes are discussed as carefully as those of people. Chickens may be kept in inner courts, though not many; feed costs too much. Dogs are uncommon because they drink and eat without producing enough in return, but one or two lean gate dogs usually belong to everyone and no one. The wrapped donkey bells reveal another local habit: inside the walls, unnecessary noise is considered a kind of taking.

Children learn the town first through errands. They carry covered bowls, bundles of wool, chisel points to the smith, kindling, messages, and half-filled water jars. By the time they are old enough to work steadily, they know which steps frost first, which passage admits the west wind, which roof may be crossed, and which stones sound hollow beneath the foot. Their games imitate adult necessities. They build miniature retaining walls, route trickles of wash water through dust, and wager on whose little cistern will hold longest without leaking.

They are taught to count through measures of grain and water, to read weather by cloud edges, and to remember spoken sequences. Formal writing is limited, but marks are everywhere: mason’s signs, household cuts on jar handles, tally notches, roof dates, directional symbols inside drain covers. The record boards near the well are maintained by a few practiced readers. Their entries are terse because wood is precious: births, deaths, repairs, failures, unusual frosts, the height of water after rain.

The dead do not remain within the crowded saddle. They are carried through the lower gate to stone-lined graves on a sheltered slope where the soil is deep enough to open. Grave goods are few: a cup, a spindle weight, a worn tool, sometimes a bead. The useful belongings return to the household. Names are spoken at roof repairs and cistern cleanings, especially when an old technique is recalled—“Set it as Mara did,” or “This was Halen’s channel.” Remembrance attaches itself to function. The dead endure in gradients, joints, recipes, and paths.

Their beliefs are similarly restrained. They do not expect the high country to be generous, but neither do they regard it as hostile. Passing clouds are greeted with attention rather than prayerful spectacle. At the first rain after a long dry interval, a small cup is drawn from the well and poured back, acknowledging that stored water belongs to a cycle larger than possession. Certain old stones near the gates receive touches from departing travelers. Children are warned not to call into empty cisterns, partly from reverence and partly because echoes conceal the sounds of cracks.

There are stories of what lives below: pale insects that count the drops, an eyeless keeper seated beyond the last pillar, drowned voices that repeat only truthful words. Few adults claim to believe these tales. Nonetheless, no one descends alone.

Beauty in the town is worked into useful things. Door latches are carved with small repeating lines. Loom weights bear circles and ladder patterns. Roof beams hidden from ordinary view may have a bird cut into one end. Wool bands display complex geometric designs in muted thread. Songs fit the rhythm of grinding, weaving, lifting, or walking uphill. Long ornamental performances are rare; breath and time are better conserved. Their music favors low voices, short flutes, frame drums played softly, and work songs whose pauses matter as much as their notes.

Hospitality is careful but genuine. A stranger is noticed before reaching either gate and questioned without ceremony: origin, trade, destination, need. If admitted, the visitor receives a place near a household hearth or in a communal room by the gate. News is payment, as are needles, salt, timber, metal stock, medicines, and seed. Travelers carrying nothing may work. Those who boast of distant richness are listened to politely, then judged by how they treat a borrowed cup.

Some inhabitants leave. Young people join caravans, seek timber work in lower valleys, herd animals farther west, or marry into settlements with deeper fields. Yet departure is rarely presented as escape. Those who return bring iron, stories, dyed cloth, unfamiliar songs, and sometimes spouses bewildered by the town’s economies. Returnees must relearn the local pace. A person who has lived where water runs freely may leave a tap jar uncapped or rinse a bowl twice; such habits are corrected quickly.

Their humor is dry, built on understatement, bad workmanship, stubborn animals, mistaken kinship, and the absurdity of weather. A drizzle may be called a flood. A slightly crooked wall is declared ready to travel downhill. Someone carrying an excessive load is asked whether the lower valley has been moved into their sack. Laughter does not often ring across the whole town. It stays close, shared among people whose bodies lean together over work.

At dusk, the inhabitants withdraw in layers. Donkeys are unloaded. Roof tools are brought under cover. Water jars are checked and capped. Children are called from passages. The gates are barred more against wind and wandering animals than attack. Through shutter gaps come thin bands of lamplight and the sounds of bowls, looms, coughing, murmured accounts, and whetstones passing over iron.

They do not think of themselves as austere. To them, wastefulness elsewhere would seem not luxurious but childish—a failure to understand where things come from and what their replacement costs. Their satisfaction lies in sufficiency: a sealed roof before frost, a cistern holding steady, a wall reset true, enough lentils for winter, a donkey returned safely, a pear tree putting out leaves after a hard year.

The town’s deepest inhabitant may be this shared habit of continuance. It lives in every person, but belongs to none of them. It remembers through hands rather than proclamations. It asks that each generation receive a finished place, alter it as little as necessary, and leave it finished again.
