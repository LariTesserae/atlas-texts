The inhabitants are human, though the city has made that category less certain than it once was.

They are generally lean, deep-chested people, adapted to cold, thin air and to walking immense distances between districts. Their skin often bears a faint greenish or amber cast—not from chlorophyll, as visitors suppose, but from generations of mineral-rich water, medicinal saps, and luminous yeasts consumed in small quantities. Their hands are broad-fingered and scarred by fibers. Pruners develop hard crescents along the thumbs. Rail workers have palms polished smooth by root oil. Archivists stain their fingertips black with reed pith and wear the marks proudly.

Almost everyone dresses in layered felt grown from moss, bark fiber, and shed canopy down. Clothing is cut loosely because it continues to alter after manufacture. Coats thicken in cold weather. Hoods sprout water-shedding hairs during fog season. Work boots are living sheaths that tighten around the ankle and put down temporary gripping filaments on steep slopes; they must be coaxed off at night, or they begin to regard the wearer as suitable ground. Bright dyes are uncommon. Profession, district, and seniority are indicated instead by leaf shape, scent, vein pattern, and the small cultivated growths worn at the collar.

Children learn horticulture before writing, because here the distinction between literacy and gardening is slight. They are taught to read the inclination of a wall, the thirst-wrinkles of a cistern pod, and the warning bitterness of an overstressed bridge. At school they graft numbers onto counting vines, trace history through rings, and memorize the safety rules concerning shadows before they are allowed to cross an open field alone.

They play among drainage grooves and dormant building plots. They race husks, whistle into ventilation reeds, and dare one another to stand near prospective shadows. Some keep pet mechanisms: knot-legged toys grown from roots, wind-up seed cases, little carts that follow warmth. There are no dogs to trail them home, no horses, no livestock with which to learn another kind of life. For many children, the first bird they see is an alarming event. They describe it as a leaf that made a mistake and continued making it.

Adults organize themselves less by family name than by maintenance obligation. A household may consist of parents and children, but just as often it contains a bridge tender, three unrelated mill workers, an elderly weather reader, and two people who share responsibility for the tower in which they live. Kinship extends through structures: those sheltered by the same trunk owe one another aid, and those drinking from the same cistern are prohibited from certain forms of rivalry. Marriages are often marked by grafting branches from two dwelling trees. Divorce requires separating the graft without killing either side, a delicate task attended by witnesses and, sometimes, structural engineers.

Their occupations are innumerable and exact.

There are arborwrights who persuade trunks into inhabitable forms; tension gardeners who tune bridges by shaving or watering particular fibers; sap electricians who regulate the mineral hedges; mill anatomists who crawl inside factories to diagnose malformed gears; fog catchers; rail listeners; fungal fermenters; shadow surveyors; tense clerks; and licensed demolition gardeners, who kill structures carefully enough that their deaths do not propagate into the wrong century.

The most respected are not architects but negotiators. They spend years learning how to propose a shape to a plant without forcing it. They speak through cuts, hormones, pressure, light, vibration, and patient obstruction. A skilled negotiator does not command a tower to produce a room. She creates a season in which the tower concludes that a room would be advantageous.

This philosophy governs much of civic life. Laws are framed as conditions rather than prohibitions. Punishment is rare and usually takes the form of exclusion from infrastructure: a person may find that doors no longer soften at their approach, that rail hairs refuse their sled, or that market canopies close overhead. Imprisonment is considered both cruel and structurally foolish. A confined person becomes part of a building’s memory, and buildings are notoriously reluctant to release what they have learned to contain.

No mayor or council rules the whole plateau. Each district has committees, foremen, water stewards, birth registrars, archive readers, and emergency pruners, but their decisions are advisory to the city’s deeper coordination. The inhabitants debate whether that coordination is intelligence. Most avoid saying so near roots.

Messages travel through changes in sap pressure, electrical pulses, airborne chemicals, and human couriers. The city can announce danger by souring public drinking leaves or making every threshold in a district contract. It directs traffic by flowering alternate paths. It can summon specific professions: a sudden rash of blue nodules on walls calls structural workers; copper-colored moss asks for electricians; transparent buds mean temporal contamination. No one knows how it identifies the needed people, yet the signs often appear first on their own homes.

The city anticipates births with particular accuracy. Months or years before a child is conceived, a sleeping niche may bud from a dwelling wall. Families treat this cautiously. Sometimes the niche belongs to a future child. Sometimes it is meant for someone arriving from the past. Occasionally it remains empty for generations, perfectly fitted to a body no one recognizes.

Death is handled through return, but not simple burial. Bodies are wrapped in rooting cloth and placed within designated decomposition groves, where heat, fungi, and acid flowers reduce them quickly. Personal memories are believed to persist imperfectly in the infrastructure. A dead rail worker’s habitual route may begin flowering at the hour of his shift. A tower may open a door for a woman who resembles its former resident. Compressor trees sometimes reproduce the cadence of a deceased mechanic’s breathing.

For this reason, remembrance is practical rather than monumental. The dead are spoken to when repairing places they knew. Tools are introduced by name before being passed to apprentices. A person’s final civic duty is to specify what should be allowed to remember them.

Not all inhabitants accept such intimacy. Some live along the plateau rim in deliberately dead houses assembled from kiln-dried bark, ceramic, metal salvage, and stone brought from below. They cut root incursions daily and drink only boiled water. They call themselves the Unincluded. The city calls them nothing anyone can translate, though marker trees around their settlements grow bark the color used for temporary hazards.

The Unincluded believe the city is not a home but a vast reproductive body using human intention as a shaping pressure. In their view, people do not design factories or districts. They merely supply desires from which the city selects useful forms. Human labor is pollination performed by minds.

Their opponents answer that this is true of every civilization.

More unsettling are those who seem to have been displaced in time.

They appear first in worker registries, housing assignments, or medical records—names recorded before their owners arrive. A factory may issue wages to an unknown employee for twenty years, storing the payment in resin-sealed pods. Then one morning a young person walks out of a fog bank carrying obsolete tools and insists it is an ordinary workday.

Such arrivals are called returns, even when no one remembers their departure.

Some speak old dialects. Some know future machinery. Some recognize buildings that have not been planted or mourn districts that still stand. They are treated with wary kindness and questioned only by trained tense clerks, since careless conversation can give them memories prematurely. A return is never asked what year it is. Instead they are asked what is currently in flower.

There are also people born with temporal rooting of their own. Their shadows differ from their bodies. Their scars appear before the injuries that cause them. As children they may answer questions several seconds early; in old age they sometimes grow physically younger around rain. These people often become surveyors, because they can feel when a path has begun to lean into another time. They describe the sensation as pressure behind the teeth.

They are valued, feared, and rarely long-lived in any straightforward sense.

One famous surveyor worked for the city for a hundred and twelve years while never appearing older than thirty. Her employment record, however, is distributed across six centuries and ends before it begins. Another is still seen at the northern distance fields, always walking toward the same tower. Depending on the color of its windows, he is either a young apprentice, an exhausted old man, or a skeleton clothed in survey moss. Regulations forbid approaching him.

The inhabitants fear temporal accidents more than fire.

When reverse bells toll, workers stop wherever they are. Machines are allowed to idle. Conversations cease. People close their mouths because words spoken during reversal may become causes rather than descriptions. Parents do not touch their children. Lovers do not make promises. Everyone waits as the great flowers draw sound inward, one impossible note after another, until silence resumes in the correct direction.

Afterward, clerks inspect births, deaths, debts, marriages, and construction permits for alteration. Most changes are small: an extra window, a scar moved from one hand to the other, a friendship that now began years earlier. Sometimes an entire household discovers a member for whom there is clothing, work, and abundant shared memory, though no one recalls meeting them.

Such a person is accepted. The city has little patience for arguments about originality.

Despite these dangers, the people are not solemn. They celebrate first condensation, the opening of new transit roots, successful bridge divisions, and the rare arrival of migratory birds. Markets sell fermented nectars, smoked fungus, seed meal, sour leaf curds, mineral sweets, and warm cakes steamed inside disposable flowers. Musicians cultivate instruments rather than build them: chamber gourds, string vines, rattling pods, and reed choirs played by redirected wind. Songs are composed with spaces where machinery will answer.

Their humor is full of tense. Someone late to work claims the path had not happened yet. A badly planned house is said to have a promising past. Lovers ask, “Have we met tomorrow?” Children chalk false buds onto unopened routes and wait for adults to follow them.

At night, most inhabitants sleep close to living walls. The walls adjust their heat, filter breath, and produce mild sedative scents. Dreams are rarely considered private. Roots mingle beneath whole districts, and sleepers often wake with common images: a glass forest under snow, towers taller than weather, oceans covering the plateau, or a dark subterranean blossom around which every buried century is arranged like petals.

In the morning they compare dreams at cisterns. If enough people have seen the same structure, survey crews are sent to find where it intends to grow.

Whether the city loves its people is not a question most residents ask. Love implies separation, and separation is difficult to maintain here. Human breath feeds leaves. Roots warm human rooms. The dead enter infrastructure. Buildings anticipate bodies. Workers prune the organisms that govern them, and the organisms reshape the workers across generations.

The inhabitants serve the city.

The city serves the inhabitants.

Each statement is repeated often, and neither is trusted alone.

And sometimes, very late, a person walking home sees pale buds opening along an unbuilt path. If the buds open away from them, they turn back. If they open toward them, they wait. But if every bud unfolds at once, illuminating a road that crosses the dark plateau and continues beyond the rim into empty air, they understand that the city has remembered someone who does not yet exist.

Then they leave an offering of water beside the first flower and go home by another way.
