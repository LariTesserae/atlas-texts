The people who live there are difficult to see.

They have learned not to linger in open courts, where heat rises from black paving and the noise arrives from every direction at once. They move instead through wall passages, roofed galleries, maintenance trenches, and the inhabited cores of buildings whose outer rooms have long since been surrendered to water. A warehouse showing one lit doorway may contain several hundred people farther inside, nested behind dissolving facades. From the street there is only a figure crossing a gantry, a hand withdrawing a signal shutter, a lamp changing color behind wired glass.

They are generally compact in build, strong through the back and forearms, deliberate on wet stone. Most wear close-fitting caps or wrapped headcloths, heavy aprons, and coats darkened at the shoulders by condensation. Their boots have broad, replaceable soles cut with channels that shed mineral paste. Metal fasteners are avoided where possible; buckles corrode, zippers seize, and decorative chains become green scars against the skin. Clothing closes with ceramic toggles, horn pins, waxed cord, or narrow straps of treated hide.

Nearly everyone carries hearing protection, though no one calls it that. There are molded plugs of wax and fiber, padded cups clamped under the jaw, and elaborate family-made devices fitted into the ear and carved with ownership marks. Long exposure has shaped the population nonetheless. Older citizens often hear only selected regions of sound: the low concussion of a failing pump, perhaps, but not speech; the scrape of a rail bearing, but not music. They read faces closely. Their hands are rarely still.

The common language has two bodies. One is spoken at intimate distance, mouth beside ear, stripped of unneeded syllables. The other is made of fingers, wrist angles, shoulder movements, and touches against the arm. It began as industrial signaling but has grown capable of gossip, legal argument, insult, tenderness, and prayer. From across a court, two people can conduct a quiet quarrel beneath machinery loud enough to shake dust from the roofs.

Names are often signed before they are spoken. A person may be known by a hooked finger for the district of their birth, a brushing motion for the sound of their trade, or a downward spiral recalling some childhood accident involving a drain. Spoken names change as hearing changes. Signed names tend to endure.

Children learn vibration before language. They lie on warmed floors and are taught to distinguish the regular labor of turbines from the uneven tremor of a cavity opening in the rock. Games are played by placing a palm against a pipe and guessing what passes through it: hot water, slurry, compressed air, return steam. They float scraps of fired clay down gutters and race them between grates. They dare one another into abandoned outer corridors, where old doorways stand in ranks and water taps softly through rooms no longer wide enough to inhabit.

A child who notices an unfamiliar tremor is not dismissed. The adults kneel and listen through the soles of their hands.

The city’s most respected inhabitants are not its owners but its listeners. They work in pairs or small crews, entering the damp interstices between successive walls. One presses cheek or instrument to masonry while another records the rhythm of seepage, grain-fall, hidden boiling, or stressed iron. They know which knocks are trapped stones settling and which are warnings from a load-bearing chamber. Their maps are layered and constantly revised. They chart not only streets and pipes but pressure, temperature, mineral taste, wall thickness, and the direction in which a room is becoming smaller.

Alongside them work the sheath masons, who build new walls inside old ones. Their craft is less like construction than surgery. They leave channels for the water, pockets for new deposits, and soft joints where the city may shift without tearing itself apart. A good mason does not defeat erosion. A good mason persuades it to proceed elsewhere.

Other trades descend through families: valve keepers, grate divers, rail polishers, cistern readers, sediment clerks, baffle cleaners, and the licensed harvesters of conduit scale. Scraped mineral is sorted by color and hardness, then ground for mortar, glaze, medicine, pigments, or industrial flux. Nothing produced by the city is considered wholly waste. Even rust is gathered from catch basins and pressed into dense red tiles.

The pumping authority is the nearest thing the city has to a sovereign. It is part engineering office, part archive, part priesthood, though its members reject the last description. Its officials wear pale coats marked with dark horizontal bands recording their level of access underground. They govern by pressure tables, closure notices, and flow allocations. They decide which district may draw heat, which foundry must slow, which ancient conduit can be sacrificed, and which families must move inward when an outer wall reaches the end of its useful thickness.

Their headquarters occupies no grand plaza. It lies beneath one, in chambers whose floors hum continuously. Petitioners communicate through speaking tubes or place written cases into sealed ceramic cylinders. Hearings may last months because the relevant evidence is not an eyewitness but a stain advancing across a foundation.

Property is understood differently here. A family rarely owns a building. It owns a sequence of inward retreats: rights to certain rooms now, to the rooms behind them when the outer course fails, and perhaps to a share in whatever wall must eventually be raised between. Old households possess genealogies of doors. Their histories are recited as movements through architecture:

the room with three windows;  
the room after the eastern window was sealed;  
the narrow room inside the lime wall;  
the upper chamber reached through the former pantry;  
the present rooms, for as long as they remain dry enough.

Marriage contracts specify access to cisterns, maintenance obligations, evacuation priority, and the inheritance of wall thickness. The wealthy are not those with the largest houses, but those with the deepest habitable cores, the most reliable pressure allotments, and several possible routes to high ground during a storm.

There is little casual ornament, yet the inhabitants are not austere. They decorate what water cannot easily erase. Glazed tiles are set on interior faces. Hand signs are embroidered into cuffs. Families keep polished fragments of vanished thresholds, old valve wheels, ceramic labels from buried streets, and sections of pipe whose deposits show unusual banding. A wedding gift may be a thin slice of conduit stone, cut and polished to reveal fifty years of alternating dark and pale growth.

Music exists mostly as vibration. Floors, rails, pipes, stretched wires, and hollow ceramic vessels are struck or bowed. Some performances are felt through benches rather than heard. During festivals, a district briefly adjusts its machinery so several pumps fall into related rhythms. Residents gather barefoot or place their hands on designated walls. To an outsider, the result may seem indistinguishable from ordinary industrial thunder. The citizens feel melody passing through stone.

Silence is sacred and alarming.

When machinery must be stopped, people emerge from their inner rooms and stand in the streets, unsettled by the size of unmasked space. They hear dripping, breathing, fabric shifting, insects on warm metal. Birds pour from the exhaust baffles and wheel above the roofs as if released from the city itself. Some citizens weep during a full shutdown. Others become nauseated. Children are warned not to shout, because in silence every unsupported wall and moving body seems dangerously exposed.

Funerals are often held during partial stoppages. The dead are wrapped in porous cloth and placed in mineral chambers built into sacrificial walls. Warm seepage passes over them. In time, what remains is enclosed by deposits and becomes part of the wall’s growing interior. The chamber is marked outside with a glazed handprint. When erosion eventually reaches it, descendants collect whatever the water returns: a tooth, a pin, a pale cast of folded cloth. Nothing is expected to remain forever, but neither is disappearance considered immediate.

Those who maintain the figlike trees form a small, peculiar office. They examine leaves for changes in salt, measure the spread of roots, and listen to trunks with ceramic horns. A tree’s sickness may indicate contamination; a sudden flush of growth may mean a cistern wall has cracked. The keepers are accused of loving their charges, which they deny with the formal irritation of professionals. Nevertheless, scraps of ribbon sometimes appear on the western branches, tied there during night shifts.

In the lowest districts live the tunnel crews. They are pale from artificial light and marked by burns from mineral-hot water. Their helmets bear glass faceplates, and their gloves reach to the shoulder. They descend to clear grates, scrape bacterial films from heat exchangers, and inspect black cisterns whose far walls cannot be seen. They do not discuss the eel-shaped things with outsiders. Among themselves they distinguish many varieties by the wakes they make, the softness of their impact against tools, and whether nearby instruments lose pressure.

The larger blockages have their own vocabulary, none of it official. A “sleeping bend” is a pipe that changes shape between surveys. A “breathing floor” rises and falls without seismic cause. A “white appetite” is a conduit whose mineral growth closes from several directions at once. When crews vanish, reports cite steam, collapse, bad fastening, or unauthorized entry. Their families may receive a sealed jar of water from the affected level. The jar is kept warm for a year and then poured into a household wall.

Food comes largely from elsewhere: grain, oil, dried fruit, preserved meat, and compressed leaf cakes arriving on the slow trains. Within the city, fungi are cultivated in warm ventilation galleries. Pale crusts are harvested from clean cisterns. The stiff reeds of the settling ponds provide fiber after repeated washing. Certain bacterial films are fermented into sour pastes valued by workers who have lost much of their sense of smell. Meals are dense, salty, and eaten in small interior kitchens where utensils are ceramic and steam coats the ceiling.

People drink carefully. Every neighborhood knows the taste of its water: iron, lime, clay, bitter salts, a trace of sulfur, the sweetness that sometimes precedes a pipe failure. Guests are offered water from the host’s registered line. To refuse it is a serious accusation.

Most citizens seldom leave the plateau. Those who do find other places disturbingly soft and quiet. Soil seems untrustworthy. Forests appear impossibly crowded with uncontrolled life. Freely running rivers strike them as obscene waste. They sleep poorly without the bass pulse beneath the floor and may place small engines against the bedframe to imitate home.

From afar, they are nearly absent from their own city. This is partly habit and partly design. Life is safest behind walls, below glare, close to instruments, within reach of a handrail or speaking tube. The vast courts, empty avenues, and monumental warehouses belong to transport, pressure, drainage, and machines. Human beings occupy the residual spaces—yet they do not think of themselves as diminished.

They regard the city as a collective body whose visible structures are only its shell. The true settlement is made of attention: the hand testing a pipe, the clerk comparing bands in a wall, the child pausing at an unfamiliar vibration, the mason deciding where water should be allowed to go.

They do not believe they conquered the plateau. Nor do they imagine they merely survive upon it. Their oldest civic phrase, usually signed rather than spoken, has several possible translations:

**We hold it up.**

**It holds us down.**

**Together, we continue.**
