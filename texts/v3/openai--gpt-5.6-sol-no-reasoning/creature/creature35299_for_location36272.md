Those who live beneath the roof call themselves, simply, the **Supported**.

The name is less a claim of protection than an acknowledgment of burden. Everyone is held up by something and expected, in turn, to hold something else. A household supports a lintel. A guild maintains a cistern. A widow tends the pier built through her kitchen. Children brace shelves against walls that breathe outward in the rain. Even the dead remain useful: their sealed jars are mortared into counterweights, stair cores, and arches whose loads are carefully recorded by family.

To ask a person where they live is to ask what rests upon them.

“I am Teren of the Third Catchment, under the Red Causeway, bearing the west kiln stair.”

“I am Sava, whose rooms uphold the House of Measures.”

“I sleep beneath the market, but only since the wall opened.”

Their settlements have no clear boundaries. A ward is defined by shared drainage, by the sequence of bells heard at night, or by agreement over which cracks are dangerous. Families may occupy rooms separated by several levels yet consider themselves one household because their cooking smoke enters the same flue. Neighbors who can hear each other through a wall may never discover a passage between their chambers.

They are short-lived by their own reckoning, though no one agrees on the length of a year. Time is measured in roof-rains, kiln firings, counterweight descents, and the whitening of mortar. A person’s age may be given as “forty-three clean falls” or “old enough to remember when the Ninth Wheel skipped.” For contracts, they use drip clocks and marked lengths of slowly hardening stone.

Their bodies carry the place. Their shoulders are broad from poles and levers; their feet spread around slick tile. Many have pale, scarred hands from lime burns, and a mason’s hearing often fails early. They wrap their hair close against dust and machinery. Clothing is layered wool, felt, beaten fiber, or stitched scraps of water-resistant membrane, usually hung with small ceramic tags identifying household, guild, safe passages, and debts of support.

They distrust ornament that does not work. Jewelry is made as plumb bobs, clasps, sounding rings, chalk holders, tiny levels, or emergency wedges. A beautiful thing is admired most when it also keeps a door from closing.

## Households of the burden

A Supported household rarely consists only of kin. It includes whoever maintains the same structure: rope-makers, rain-catchers, kiln tenders, abandoned children, debtors, elders who remember inaccessible conduits, and strangers accepted after a collapse. The household’s central possession is not its dwelling but its **burden charter**, a clay tablet listing what the dwelling holds up and who must repair it.

Inheritance follows load rather than blood. If a daughter can hear faults in ceramic columns and her brother cannot, she inherits the pier. If an outsider saves a stair during a roof contraction, the stair’s household may adopt them immediately, whether they consent or not. Abandoning a load without transferring it is among the gravest offenses.

Rooms are never considered permanent. Furniture has handles and narrow legs. Beds fold into wall recesses. Cupboards are built as braces. Families keep “moving lines”—ropes already threaded through eyelets—so their possessions can be drawn into another chamber when a floor rises, a door disappears, or wet grit begins extruding through the tiles.

Newborns are presented first to the house, not to the community. A spotter carries the infant from pier to pier while relatives strike the masonry and listen. At whichever support gives the clearest answer, a small clay palm is fixed to the wall. This becomes the child’s **first bearing**. Some return to maintain it all their lives.

No one leaves a baby beneath a decorative ceiling.

## The listeners

The most respected people are often the **listeners**, sometimes called wall-ears. They travel with bone mallets, tuning forks, wax tablets, coils of string, and cups of varying thickness. By tapping pillars and pressing their teeth to stone, they claim to hear hollows, fractures, water, hidden stairs, growing grit, and the slow approach of a new aperture.

Some are frauds. Some are merely skilled. A few know things that skill does not explain.

A good listener can identify a sealed room by its silence. They can tell whether a counterweight is descending freely or dragging against bodies. They can predict which side of an arch will fail and order everyone to pile rubble upon the opposite side until the load becomes honest again.

Listeners do not say a structure is safe. They say it is “speaking evenly.”

Their guild keeps the oldest maps, though these resemble musical notation more than plans. Corridors are recorded as rhythms of footsteps; walls as sequences of tones; shafts as changes in breath and pressure. A route might be written:

> Seven low strikes, water beneath.  
> Turn where the fifth echo doubles.  
> Descend until the jaw-note ceases.  
> Do not answer the bell bird.

Apprentices are selected for patience and strong teeth. Many eventually become quiet, unable to stop attending to every settlement crack and distant impact. Old listeners sleep in suspended hammocks so the floors cannot talk through their bones.

## Rain families

When the roof begins to drum, the **catchers** emerge.

They are among the few who move freely across household boundaries. Falling water belongs to no one, and obstruction of a catcher during rain is both crime and sacrilege. They climb terraces with folded cloths, hooks, ceramic gutters, and shoulder poles. Their movements are swift, rehearsed, and nearly acrobatic. Children run beneath them replacing full jars with empty ones.

Ownership begins only when a drop stops falling. This creates constant argument.

Water caught in a cloth belongs to the catcher. Water striking a private roof and flowing into a public trough belongs to the ward. Water that pauses on a person’s sleeve may be drunk but not stored. Condensation is governed by different laws and can be inherited. Cistern water belongs partly to those who maintain the walls and partly to those living downhill, since a collapse would drown them first.

Rain families cultivate reputations for fairness because every household depends upon them. Their jars are sealed with distinctive knots, and breaking such a seal without permission can lead to one’s name being removed from shared catchments.

Their oldest members taste water for metal, lime, soot, clay, old blood, and “lower attention.” Water thought to have been noticed from beneath is poured into load-bearing cracks, never drunk.

## Masons and persuaders

The Supported do not say they construct buildings. The world constructs. People **persuade** it.

Masons are trained to work with tendencies already present: the lean of a column, the pressure behind a wall, the direction a stair wishes to extend. They mistrust straight lines longer than a body. They leave witness gaps around new work so later generations can see how it shifts. Every repair must preserve some remnant of what stood before, even if only a sill, a dust line, or one brick set sideways in the replacement wall.

To erase a structure entirely is believed to make its load homeless.

Homeless loads are blamed for sudden collapses, bent tools, unexplained heaviness in rooms, and people waking unable to rise. When demolition is unavoidable, masons build a **burden seat** nearby: a small arch, usually knee-high, packed with fragments of the removed wall. Travelers leave pebbles on these seats to give them something further to bear.

Fresh stone is harvested by extruders’ guilds. When a sealed aperture begins pushing out pale squared material, the guild builds a frame around it and marks the growth each shift. The stone is cut while soft with wire, paddles, and wet knives, then carried away to cure. No one has discovered what pushes it outward.

The largest apertures are treated like dangerous livestock. They are given room, watched constantly, and never approached from directly in front. Some stop producing if insulted. Others extrude faster when sung to. A few have produced shapes resembling fingers, teeth, keys, or miniature staircases. These are not used as building material. They are sealed in burden shrines and argued over.

## The hinge-poor and the door keepers

Because soft iron is rare, doors turn on polished stone pins, swing from rope, slide in grooves, or balance so delicately that a child can shift them. Door keepers maintain these arrangements and memorize which openings change.

They distinguish between doors made by people and **arrivals**—entrances manufactured by sealed chambers. An arrival may begin as a dark line, widen into a seam, develop a lintel over several days, and finally open onto a place that should be elsewhere.

No arrival is entered immediately.

First, a bowl of water is placed at the threshold to reveal vibration. Then chalk figures are drawn on both walls and compared for distortion. A weighted cord is lowered across the sill. Finally, someone speaks a formal greeting:

> “You have opened. We have noticed. Bear us without claiming us.”

If the echo repeats the words correctly, a surveyor may enter. If it adds a word, the door is bricked halfway closed and watched.

Door keepers are considered peculiar even by lowland standards. They often develop attachments to particular thresholds and may sit beside them for decades. They insist doors have temperaments: shy, hungry, officious, deceitful, homesick. Some doors widen for burdens but pinch empty-handed travelers. Some permit a person through only once. Some open more easily for those carrying an object from the room beyond.

## Surveyors

Surveyors know that the lowlands cannot be measured, yet spend their lives measuring them.

They work in teams of three: one stretches cord, one records knots, and one watches for revision. If a corridor lengthens during measurement, the watcher rings a hand bell and the result is entered as a negotiation rather than a distance.

Their records never say that a hall is thirty paces long. They say:

> “Thirty paces when approached dry from the kiln side, carrying no flame, after the second wheel knock.”

Maps are layered ceramic leaves hinged with cord. Contradictory routes are preserved rather than corrected. A good map shows not where a place is but the conditions under which it can be reached.

Surveyors settle boundary disputes, though their decisions are provisional. They are also called when a staircase gains or loses a tread. Added treads are marked with red slip and never stepped on first by a child. Removed treads are mourned with a small cup of dust, since the law against complete demolition may apply even when the world itself is the demolisher.

## Kiln people

Kilns provide warmth, tools, jars, tiles, light screens, and the innumerable ceramic pieces on which life depends. Their keepers occupy the warmest districts and are recognizable by singed brows, cracked fingertips, and lungs darkened by smoke.

They are regarded with mingled gratitude and suspicion because flame behaves strangely with depth. Kiln people know how low a fire may be carried before it turns blue. They build their furnaces in stepped chambers, raising or lowering fuel baskets to tune the color.

Blue flame is never left unattended.

When it appears, the kiln is screened, talking stops, and everyone counts backward from the last wheel knock. If the flame leans toward the floor, the fire is smothered with powdered tile. If it stands upright and produces no heat, the chamber is abandoned and its doorway narrowed.

There are tales of kiln tenders who stared too long into blue fire and began building downward in their sleep. They wake with clay under their nails and plans for staircases no sane mason would approve.

## Grit shepherds

Where fine material rises in lines through paving joints, **grit shepherds** tend it.

They brush the grains into ridges, divert foot traffic, record the speed of emergence, and predict how the ground will buckle. Their work is slow enough to pass from parent to child. A shepherd may spend an entire life guiding a ridge a hand’s width away from a cistern wall.

They speak of grit as though it has intention but little intelligence. It can be coaxed with vibration, blocked temporarily by water, or encouraged to compact around ceramic ribs. Young shepherds sleep beside active seams to learn their faint hissing speech.

Some ridges eventually harden into walls. When that happens, the shepherd who tended the first grains is named the wall’s ancestor, whether living or dead.

## Jar harvesters

In the vessel halls, harvesters remove only the outermost jars.

They work without flame, following guide ropes and the low hum that passes through the clay when drainage wheels turn. Every vessel is tapped before being moved. Empty jars answer sharply. Cracked jars whisper. Jars containing liquid are marked and left for specialists. Any jar that gives no sound at all is reburied behind a wall of smaller vessels.

The deeper jars are never touched.

Harvesters say the darkness inside them is compressed by the weight of all the architecture above. If released, it would not merely extinguish lamps; it would unfold into corridors, shafts, rooms, and perhaps a night large enough to contain a sky.

Their guild denies this belief in public and enforces it absolutely in practice.

## Lid wardens

The villages built across the Plain of Lids are inhabited by the **wardens below**, though outsiders sometimes call them knock-keepers.

Their houses straddle the circular slabs without placing full weight on any single lid. Floors are carried on branching piers. Hearths are forbidden. Water jars are set between handles, never atop them. Children learn to walk softly and are punished for games involving repeated rhythms.

During the wet season the lids knock from underneath. Wardens record the patterns on clay cylinders, one row of impressions for each night. Most knocking is irregular. Sometimes neighboring lids answer one another. On rare occasions a pattern moves across the plain faster than a person can run.

The wardens do not lift the lids because they consider refusal an active duty. Each village maintains elaborate lifting frames—beams, ropes, stone counterweights—kept in perfect repair but never used. To neglect the possibility of opening would be irresponsible. To open would be worse.

Their dead are not buried. A body is placed on a narrow bier above the household lid until the next rain. If the lid knocks, the dead person is cremated elsewhere. If it remains silent, the body is built into the house.

Some wardens claim the handles grow warm beneath particular stars, though none of them has ever seen a star.

## Children

Children belong everywhere and are forbidden almost everywhere.

They crawl through narrow maintenance spaces, retrieve dropped tools from drainage channels, carry messages through markets, and discover new rooms because doors often widen more readily for them. They play at survey by changing the knots on each other’s cords. They race blind runners. They dare one another to place ears against warm benches before anyone sits down.

Their games imitate structural danger:

- **Collapse**, in which one child forms an arch and others pile objects on their back.
- **Rain Law**, a ferocious argument over drops flicked from wet fingers.
- **Extra Step**, played on stairways, where each child secretly chooses a tread that “was not there yesterday.”
- **Bearing**, in which players must cross a courtyard while carrying something that carries something else.

Children are warned never to answer a sound from beneath the floor if it knows their name. Naturally, many spend hours whispering names into grates.

Those born during roof-rain are believed to sense changes in level. Those born during a contraction are said to be lucky only if they cry before the columns stop ringing.

## The blind runners

The small ticking animals are called **rules**, **foldlings**, or **little measures**.

Their bodies consist of narrow jointed segments under tight colorless skin. When still, they fold into angles resembling discarded tools. They have no visible eyes and appear to navigate by tapping the floor with many fine feet.

They emerge before minor structural shifts. A household that sees one crossing its room will lift breakables from shelves and sleep elsewhere. Three moving in the same direction indicate water. A runner that stops and unfolds completely is considered an omen of immediate collapse.

No one intentionally kills them. Dead ones are set into wet plaster as measuring marks, although their bodies often vanish before the plaster cures, leaving ruler-straight cavities.

Some surveyors keep them in clay boxes, but captive runners develop extra joints and become useless for prediction.

## Cistern sails

The pale creatures in deep reservoirs are rarely seen clearly. They have broad translucent backs raised like folded cloth, with thin trailing limbs that do not seem to stir the water. They surface beneath falling drops and sink when covered by shadow.

Cistern keepers believe they filter poisons. Others insist they produce them.

When one is seen, drawing water stops until it disappears. A cup is then lowered and left untouched overnight. If the cup rises empty, the cistern is sealed. If it returns full, the water is safe but reserved for the sick. If the rope returns cut, masons inspect the walls for a new door.

There are stories of very old sails large enough to bear houses. Such creatures are said to drift through drowned levels below the inhabited wards, carrying ruins on their backs.

## Bell birds

Bell birds are the only creatures treated almost as fellow citizens.

Their gray membranes fold around them when they roost, making them resemble bundles of damp cloth. They have small hooked feet, no obvious beaks, and faces that are either featureless or too deeply wrinkled to read. They nest alone at great height.

A bell bird chooses a hanging object and strikes it repeatedly: pipe, cup, chain, tile, old helmet, glazed shard. Another answers from far away, selecting a related tone. Their conversations may cross entire districts.

People use them for orientation and weather warning, though not reliably. Sudden silence often precedes roof-rain. Rapid descending tones may indicate a released reservoir. A single repeated note sometimes continues for days before a major collapse.

Bell keepers hang arrays of tuned objects in high vaults to attract them. The birds ignore carefully made instruments and favor cracked bowls, bent pipes, and objects associated with accidents.

It is forbidden to trap one. When a bell bird dies, its chosen instrument must remain hanging until it falls of its own accord.

Some listeners believe the birds do not speak to one another. They believe each is addressing something within the roof.

## The pale colonies

The waxy drain-fans, bead strings, and frostlike crusts are collectively called **the almost-living**.

Scrapers harvest them with shell blades. Different forms have different uses: warm-vent frost numbs pain; drain fans strengthen glue; translucent beads burn with a nearly smokeless flame. Apothecaries cultivate some varieties on wet ceramic frames while insisting this is not gardening.

The distinction matters. Plants are unknown, and many Supported regard rooting as a disturbing idea: life entering a crack, dividing stone, and taking nourishment without accepting a burden charter.

Scholars debate whether the colonies are animals, mineral exudations, unfinished materials, or residues of the world’s machinery. Under magnification by water-filled glass, some appear to contain slowly turning wheels. Others recoil from spoken measurements.

A rare black form grows only on objects that support nothing. Its appearance is taken as a warning that the object must be given a load immediately.

## Those who attend from beneath

No one agrees on what blue flame attracts.

The common name is **the Underattention**, as though it were not a creature but an act of noticing. It is blamed for warm floor seams, footsteps that stop when imitated, shadows around drain holes, and the sensation that a shaft has become much deeper while one looks into it.

Lower workers carry inverted cups on cords. If a cup begins forming a mineral pillar toward the floor, they leave. Kiln tenders scatter ash and check whether it settles downward. If it clings to the undersides of beams, they extinguish every light and retreat without speaking.

There are people who claim to have seen inhabitants below: large pale hands withdrawing around corners, figures assembled from stacked bowls, wet faces impressed into soft stone. Such claims are neither believed nor mocked. The witness is given food, watched for several days, and never again allowed to work alone.

Occasionally someone vanishes from a locked chamber. Later, thumbprints of human size appear on fresh bricks elsewhere.

## The additional footstep

Nearly every ward tells of the **Companion**, though descriptions vary.

It is not seen. In empty courts, it adds one footstep to a traveler’s count. If two people cross, there are three sets. If a crowd crosses, the extra tread is still distinct, always slightly late.

Custom requires travelers to leave space for it. Benches have an unclaimed place at one end. Work crews carry one more cup than they need. At communal meals, a scrap of bread is set beneath the table—not as an offering, people insist, but as courtesy.

The Companion has saved lives. Its footstep has halted at unsafe thresholds when human steps continued. It has also led travelers into sealed districts by walking once where no path was visible.

Listeners disagree whether it is one presence throughout the lowlands, a property of courtyards, an echo displaced from the future, or simply the architecture ensuring that no load passes unsupported.

## The lowest builders

The old people say the vibration in the jaw is construction continuing below.

Most mean this metaphorically. Some do not.

A scattered fraternity called the **Downward Witnesses** studies new extrusions, oversized thumbprints, altered staircases, and the rhythm of deep impacts. They argue that the makers of the lowlands have never departed. They are simply buried beneath their own work, continuing to build upward without knowing—or caring—who occupies the levels above.

The Witnesses lower soft clay tablets down wells and retrieve them days later. Often the tablets are blank, abraded, or coated in mineral skin. Sometimes they return with marks: parallel grooves, broad fingerprints, or diagrams that resemble load paths.

Once in a generation, a tablet comes back bearing a recognizable symbol.

The Witnesses never reveal these publicly. They carry them to the Plain of Lids and add them to a sealed archive built over one of the smallest slabs.

Their doctrine is simple:

The world is not a ruin.

It is scaffolding.

The roof is not the top.

And everyone living in the lowlands—people, rules, bell birds, cistern sails, the almost-living, and whatever waits beneath the lids—is being borne upward as part of a structure whose intended load has not yet arrived.
