The plateau is inhabited less by separate species than by a single dispersed metabolism, its organs divided among stone, water, weather, plant, and animal.

At its deepest level live the slow colonies: vast films of fungi, bacteria, and stranger membrane-forming organisms spread through the vesicles of the basalt. Some feed where metal-rich groundwater crosses warm faults; others occupy cool cavities nearer the surface. Together they form conductive tissues many kilometers long. They pass ions and electrical charge between regions, open and seal mineral channels, pump water against gravity, and extrude glossy nodules containing salts, waxes, enzymes, pigments, and ceramic-like armor. They do not think as an animal thinks. Their awareness, if it deserves the word, is hydraulic and chemical—a recognition of pressure, temperature, scarcity, and flow.

The plants are their surface partners.

Sparse thorn trees root in the old drainage lines, each standing almost alone beneath a flat, austere crown. Their bark resembles charred cord, and their leaves remain folded through the dim day, opening only in the deepest night to absorb moisture directly from the air. Fine branches gather condensation and conduct it inward. Their roots tap the buried network, exchanging sugars for water and minerals.

Jointed grasses occupy shallow seams where flood silt collects. Waxy succulents wedge themselves into fissures. Around temporary pools rise hollow-stemmed plants that serve simultaneously as reservoirs, resonators, insect nurseries, and breathing shafts for the soil below. Most vegetation spends much of its existence withdrawn: as sealed seed, sleeping root crown, or dormant filament. After rain, whole basins may flower at once, though the blooms are dark—violet-black, rust-red, or glossy enough to reflect the hidden lightning rather than the absent sun.

Across the open stone move the great grazers. They are tall, narrow animals built for distance, with long legs and folded sails along their backs that can be raised to shed heat or collect damp air. Their hides are almost perfectly black. Pale mineral deposits gather around their nostrils and eyes, marking the paths of evaporated moisture.

They do not migrate toward greenery so much as toward atmospheric events. They know which escarpments will sweat, which hollows will hold floodwater, and where underground warmth will bring fog close to the ground. They travel in widely spaced files, following infrasonic footfalls transmitted through basalt. Their ancestral routes have become broad channels in the land, worn shallow by generations and briefly converted into rivers whenever storms cross them.

In daylight—or what passes for daylight under the cloud—they retreat into lava tubes, stand in the shadows of pillars, or fold their limbs beneath them in shallow depressions. At night they gather under condensation ledges and scrape moisture, salt, and microbial film from the rock. As they move, they carry spores and microbial cultures in their mouths, guts, hides, and hooves, inoculating one watershed with the life of another.

Their predators are low, broad, and many-jointed. Plates run along their ribs, flexing against the ground to send and receive vibrations. They hunt heat rather than silhouettes. A grazer’s footprint remains visible to them as a fading thermal shape, and a recently disturbed stone can betray the passage of prey. When the basalt grows uniformly warm and thermal trails disappear into the background, the predators wait near water pockets or narrow crossings. Their stillness can last for days. They need no chase if thirst will eventually bring the herd to them.

Around these larger animals thrives a multitude adapted to brief opportunities.

Translucent beetles descend slick rock faces headfirst, gathering condensation in channels along their shells. Pale moths remain sealed in fissures until sudden humidity softens their cocoons. Long-eared burrowers use vascular vanes not chiefly for hearing but to cool themselves and pump air through their tunnel colonies. Small scale-covered scavengers follow the migratory files, feeding on dung, shed skin, broken nodules, and the bodies of animals that fail between water sources.

Temporary rivers contain their own compressed world. Armored fish persist through drought as living knots buried in hardened clay. When floodwater returns, they unfold, feed, mate, lay sealed eggs, and die or re-enter dormancy before the channels vanish. Rafts of microscopic organisms travel downstream in hours, colonizing pools that may not have existed the previous night. Predatory larvae grow with extraordinary speed, while filter feeders turn opaque floodwater clear enough to expose the mineral bottom.

High above, winged scavengers ride heat rising through the dark air. They may number in the thousands, but their bodies absorb so much light that only distant lightning reveals them: a vast turning sheet, present for an instant and then erased.

Nearly all these creatures are quiet. Sound would advertise too much across the bare plateau. Communication travels instead through stone and water. Grazers stamp coded patterns. Predators answer with flexing rib plates. Insects drum against hollow stems. Burrowing colonies alter the resonance of their shafts. Sometimes many populations synchronize, and an entire valley begins to hum below the threshold of ordinary hearing. Salt grains tremble at pool edges; water skins wrinkle; underground membranes open their chemical gates in sequence.

No species commands this system. None could survive it alone.

The deep colonies raise water toward roots. Plants feed the colonies with sugars and seed the clouds with aerosols. Clouds preserve the darkness on which the surface membranes depend. Grazers distribute organisms and minerals across otherwise isolated basins. Predators keep them moving, preventing their weight from sealing the porous ground. Burrowers ventilate the basalt’s upper layers. Flood creatures capture nutrients that would otherwise wash into inaccessible ravines. Scavengers return every carcass, shell, and nodule to circulation.

Even the weather is partly alive. Warm vapor rises from biological vents. Nocturnal leaves and microbial films release particles around which cloud gathers. When a breach opens in the ceiling, plants close, animals flee, and exposed membranes withdraw into stone. Increased evaporation and aerosol release then thicken vapor beneath the opening until shadow grows back across the land.

What lives here is therefore not merely a collection of organisms on a plateau.

The plateau itself behaves like an organism: black stone for skeleton, cloud for skin, rivers for brief arteries, fungal and bacterial membranes for nerves, migrations for circulation, and the deep geothermal gradient for a heart that never beats yet never stops. Its thoughts, if they can be called thoughts, take centuries to cross it. A spring shifts. A herd changes its route. A forest of thorn trees appears where there was bare basalt for ten thousand years.

To a visitor, almost nothing happens.

To what lives there, the whole dark country is continuously at work.
