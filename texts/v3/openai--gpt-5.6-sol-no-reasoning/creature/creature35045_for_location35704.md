The lowlands are inhabited less by species than by an entire metabolism.

Its most numerous creatures are scarcely larger than grit. They occupy wet films on the basalt, feed on salts and mineral-softening microbes, and withdraw into glassy cysts whenever the water vanishes. In dryness they resemble dust, shell splinters, or patches of discoloration. Rain gives them limbs. Within minutes they graze every wetted surface in translucent swarms, laying eggs already prepared to endure the next drought. Some bear chambers of captive microorganisms in their bodies; others carry dormant swimmers from pool to pool without seeming to know it.

Above these are the shelled multitudes: low, many-legged animals whose interlocking plates make the flats appear paved. They scrape stone with mineralized teeth, trade heat by piling together, and communicate through impacts passed from shell to shell. Their armor is habitat. Mites graze its seams, threadlike parasites inhabit its joints, and pale cleaners wait beneath the plates to eat whatever feeds there. When threatened, whole acres lock into a single tessellated surface. When feeding, that surface loosens and flows.

Soft grazers emerge from beneath it—blind, folded things that swallow residue, feces, eggs, shed membranes, and one another. They have no permanent shape beyond the arrangement of their muscles. A narrow-bodied grazer can spread until it is almost a skin; a broad one can draw itself through a crack scarcely wider than a vein in the rock. Many survive dry intervals by braiding together into communal cords, their outer bodies dying to seal moisture around those within.

The temporary pools belong to animals that spend most of their existence waiting to become aquatic. Armored discs unfold from flakes. Transparent threads hatch inside rain droplets. Eyeless swimmers emerge from cysts lodged in predators’ gills or cemented beneath the mouths of grazers. They breed with desperate speed, filling every hollow with egg-ribbons and larvae. As the pools shrink, some burrow into mudstone; some climb into respiratory chambers offered by larger animals; and some let themselves be eaten, using the eater as transport to the next rain.

The pools’ hunters are black, flexible ribbons, long enough to cross several hollows without leaving the water in any of them. Their bodies flatten to the depth available. Along their undersides run rows of pressure organs that read the drumming of distant skulls through the submerged rock. They follow courtship calls, bite the callers in half, and are themselves attacked by mouths fastening from below. In drought they knot with their own kind and push through damp seams as a single muscular cable.

On the drier shelves live winged forms, though flight is exceptional. They hang in packed curtains beneath overhangs, feeding on condensation, airborne dust, and the bodies of neighbors that fail to maintain their grip. Their wings are primarily resonators. By vibrating them against cavities in the stone, they generate the immense subterranean thrum that passes through the basin. Only during mating surges do they launch. Then they flash blue-white in coordinated sheets, mate in collisions, and fall back into the crowd before anything can claim their silhouettes.

Blind quadrupeds browse through these colonies. They are low and broad, with flexible feet and mouths crowded by dozens of fine teeth. Their flesh is hidden under living mantles of cleaners. The cleaners remove parasites, dead tissue, and food trapped around the jaws; occasionally the host closes its mouth and consumes them, after which survivors immediately return. No lasting distinction exists between service, grazing, and predation.

There are hunters that shine. Most display light only inside the mouth: cold green chambers lined with inward-pointing hooks. The glow attracts rain-swimmers, winged animals, and the curious young of larger species. These predators often remain buried beneath shell debris with only the luminous cavity exposed. A false pool of green light opens in the dark; those who enter it feed the animal beneath.

Other predators work by silence. They emit pulses that interfere with the basin’s ordinary vibration, confusing anything that navigates through the ground. Around them, calls fail to carry and coordinated swarms break apart. Their prey become directionless long before the hunter arrives. For this reason, every inhabitant treats an unexplained quiet as immediate danger.

The giants beneath the basin are animals only in the slowest sense. Sediment fills the gaps around their limbs. Mineral deposits thicken their armor. Runoff has carved channels across their backs, and generations of smaller creatures have worn feeding paths between their plates. Each supports pools, nesting cavities, parasites, grazers, and predators found nowhere else. Some small species complete their entire lineage without descending to the true ground.

A buried giant may remain still for centuries, breathing through vents mistaken for warm fissures. It rises when water fails too long, when unusual rain alters the pressure of the bedrock, or when another giant’s pulses reach it through the basin. Its first movement is easily confused with erosion. Then a ridge tilts, a pond drains uphill, and whole settlements of animals flee across its body. Its joints groan like shifting strata. Smaller life answers in panic, turning the giant’s otherwise quiet migration into a moving storm.

Nothing inhabiting the lowlands is wholly an individual. Every animal carries passengers, symbionts, eggs, diseases, scavengers, or prey held in reserve. A shell is a village; a gut is a sea; a sleeping body becomes warm ground. Even the giants may be organs of some slower arrangement, their rare migrations tracing a pattern too large in distance and duration for lesser creatures to perceive.

And above all of them moves the thing that makes the basin empty itself.

Its shape is unknown because it extinguishes response before it arrives. Silence spreads ahead of it—not merely the absence of calling, but the suppression of vibration itself. Animals lock their armor, stop their hearts, flatten their bodies, and surrender every shared outline. Even the giants do not move.

The presence never touches the stone. It displaces condensation and presses shallow dimples into water. Calcite needles ring as it passes, although no wind follows it. There may be a vast body overhead, or a migrating colony so diffuse that the air itself has become animal. It may feed by drawing airborne life upward, by shedding corrosive matter, or by harvesting sound. No creature below appears able to sense more than its advancing boundary.

What falls in its wake is edible.

That is enough.

The moment it has passed, the basin resumes itself with compensating violence. Shells unlock. Wings hammer the cavities. Burrowers erupt. The hunters enter among the grazers, and the grazers feed on the hunters’ leavings. Every fallen particle is claimed before it reaches bare stone.

The lowlands live as a crowd afraid of emptiness. Their inhabitants fill water before it gathers, fill bodies before they cool, and fill silence before something else can enter it.
