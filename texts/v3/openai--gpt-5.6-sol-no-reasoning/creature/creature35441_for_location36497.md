The city itself lives there.

Not metaphorically, and not in the simple manner of a mechanism continuing after its makers have gone. There were no makers—or none distinct from what they made. No population once filled the avenues. No bodies stood on the balconies, traveled in the black vehicles, or gathered beside the sea. The apparent provisions for habitation are organs misread as architecture.

What lives in the summit crack is a single distributed being whose anatomy has been folded into urban form.

Its streets conduct pressure, heat, water, and information. Its plazas are sensory fields. Reservoirs regulate its chemistry. The transparent towers are slow-growing structures of memory, recording each strain the mountain has suffered in minute changes of crystalline alignment. Doors are valves. Bridges are tendons. The shifting districts are not neighborhoods being rearranged but thoughts changing shape.

The black vehicles are impulses moving through its nervous system. At junctions they pause because the city is comparing tensions across widely separated parts of itself. When they divide, they carry revised instructions into newly opened fractures: soften here, brace there, admit water, shed heat, wait. Their apparent aimlessness comes from the scale of the decisions they serve. A journey that resembles an idle circuit may be one syllable in a calculation lasting centuries.

Its sea is both blood and mouth.

Because life has never entered the water, the city keeps it chemically bare. It draws the pool upward, partitions it, and sends it through capillaries finer than dust. There the water tests fresh stone, transports dissolved metals, cools stressed joints, and enables reactions that cannot occur in the dry heat. When droplets appear beneath roads or across plazas, the being is not leaking. It is tasting itself.

Rain is its season of abundance. The grain-sized shutters are pores opening; the sudden gleam of avenues is circulation made visible. During those few wet minutes, the city accelerates. Signals cross it in shining fronts. Dormant chambers become active. New folds are tested and abandoned. The whole organism briefly approaches a state resembling wakefulness.

For most of the long day, however, it dreams slowly.

Its consciousness—if consciousness is the right word—is geological. It does not distinguish between an hour and a tremor, between weather and touch. The mountain’s flexure is conversation. Pressure speaks to it through crystal; heat gives rhythm; condensation brings news from the atmosphere below. It knows the outer world as a changing set of loads upon its skin.

It may not know that the crack has an outside.

The creature has no stable dimensions. In ordinary space it occupies less than a room, but only a small portion of it lies in ordinary space at any moment. Its body extends through folded directions as roots extend through soil. What appears from the entrance as impossible depth is genuine depth, though not depth aligned with down. Some districts lie beside one another without sharing a direction of approach. Some organs are separated by kilometers internally while touching across the thickness of a wall.

This is why its proportions vary so wildly. The finger-wide gates and dust-small apertures do not admit creatures of different sizes. They mark transitions between scales at which the city performs different kinds of work. Beyond one opening, stress becomes geometry. Beyond another, geometry becomes stored instruction. Certain vast plazas may be spaces in which the being holds mutually incompatible versions of itself until the mountain’s next movement selects one.

It grows only when injured.

Each fresh fracture enlarges its contact with the world. Blue points survey the bright broken planes like sensation traveling over an exposed nerve. Then the city enters the wound—not to heal it shut, but to preserve its openness, lining it with conduits and braces until damage becomes anatomy. The young mountain continually tears; the creature continually incorporates the tears.

For this reason, it has no final form. Geology is not merely its environment but its heredity. Every fault determines what it can become next. The summit’s uplift supplies the variations; pressure and survival select among them. Its evolution occurs within a single life, district by district.

It has no cells. Nothing in it reproduces independently, feeds, or dies in the familiar way. Its smallest active components are arrangements rather than objects: temporary relationships among mineral lattices, water molecules, heat gradients, and folded space. Remove one and there is nothing alive to examine. Restore the pattern and activity resumes. Its substance is the mountain, but its body is the set of instructions by which the mountain is made to behave otherwise.

The absence of ordinary life is therefore deliberate, though not hostile. Cells would cloud the water, alter the mineral films, consume useful gradients, and introduce uncontrolled memory. The city keeps its sea sterile as an animal keeps an electrical organ insulated. Any spore arriving with the rain is separated, disassembled, and returned as simple chemistry before it can germinate. The process is so complete that it leaves no residue.

It is possible that the mountain is merely a juvenile stage.

The city climbs as the summit rises, inhabiting each new seam, extending braces deeper into high-pressure stone. Far below, those braces may converge upon structures too large and too deeply folded to detect. The visible settlement could be only a bud at the surface of something distributed throughout the uplift—a sensory organ briefly exposed to rain and sky.

Or it may be a seed.

It has built no homes because it awaits no citizens. It maintains no seats because it expects no bodies. The empty chambers may instead be templates for conditions not yet present: pressures, atmospheres, geometries, or minds. The city may be rehearsing forms for an eventual transformation, constructing and discarding whole districts as an embryo folds tissues that do not yet have names.

During rain, when condensation reveals the lacework in full, certain remote towers seem to repeat the outline of the mountain itself. Deeper structures repeat the same outline again, altered slightly, and within those are still smaller repetitions. Perhaps it is learning the peak. Perhaps it is making an interior model precise enough that, one day, the distinction between model and mountain will fail.

Until then, it listens.

Its choral contraction at midday is a metabolic release. The pulse felt in the teeth is the nearest thing it has to a heartbeat. The structured darkness in its deepest openings is not emptiness but unexpressed architecture: possible organs held in reserve, waiting for a fracture with the correct shape.

Nothing walks its streets because the streets are what move.

Nothing drinks from its sea because the sea is already inside the drinker.

And nothing inhabits the city because, in the crack near the summit, city and inhabitant have never been separate.
