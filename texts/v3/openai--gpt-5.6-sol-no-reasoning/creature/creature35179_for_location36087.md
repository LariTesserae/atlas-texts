The basin’s true inhabitants are not any one species, but a society of distances.

Everything living there has learned that nearness is costly. The trees keep their allotted hollows. Fungi fruit in courts no root will enter. Pale reeds stand clustered over seams of water, each bed ending in a clean curve as though cut with a blade. Even creatures that cross the invisible boundaries do not linger at them. They hurry, leap, or fly over the divisions, and seldom feed where one province of growth meets another.

The largest lives are the solitary trees.

They are not tall. Nothing in the basin spends itself on height when there is no light to reach for. Their trunks rise only a little before dividing into long lateral limbs, some of which rest upon the stone and root again where they are permitted. Their bark is soft, ridged, and always wet, with the warmth and slight give of living flesh. Beneath it, sap moves slowly enough that a listening ear can distinguish individual pulses.

Their leaves are broad and overlapping, black-green on both surfaces. They possess almost no need to turn, and their stems have lost the ordinary restlessness of foliage. Instead, each leaf is shaped to collect condensation. Moisture gathers along fine channels and travels toward a deep central cup. Within these cups live minute, soft-bodied grazers, clear except for their dark digestive threads. Some never leave the leaf where they hatched. Their entire world is a warm basin of water suspended above a greater basin they cannot know.

The trees feed less upon the hidden sky than upon the basin itself. Their roots enter cracks and spread into the pressurized water beneath the stone, drawing minerals from currents that have passed through buried strata for ages. Some roots carry pale nodules that tremble to the slow vibration below the structures. These nodules are crowded with colonies of threadlike organisms, neither plainly fungal nor animal, which convert pressure and mineral charge into sugars. When disturbed, they contract all at once, and the whole tree gives a low wooden knock.

The frogs answer.

They are called frogs only because they occupy the wet thresholds and announce themselves from concealment. Their bodies are flat and jointed, with six short limbs arranged around a broad central plate. They cling upside down beneath stone lips, sealing themselves over trickles so that water passes through channels in their undersides. Their hides are the color and texture of soaked wood. A full-grown one can cover a person’s hand, though most are smaller than a coin.

They do not croak with throats. They strike the rock.

Each has a hollow spur beneath its body, and by flexing against the stone it produces a resonant knock that travels far beyond the range of any voice. The basin is mapped in these blows. A creature beneath the fungal court knows when another has settled near the reed hollow. Males and females distinguish one another by the decay of echoes across mineral bands. Young ones, still soft, learn the shape of the basin by pressing themselves to the ground during choruses.

Their eggs are laid not in pools but inside water seams. The adults push strings of them into cracks, where the subterranean pressure carries them through passages too narrow for the hatchlings to retrace. Years later, after blind larval lives in the plateau, they emerge from different fissures, often in distant parts of the basin. Thus no grove owns its population. The water redistributes them.

Below the smooth stone live the clicking colonies.

Individually, their members are unimpressive: hard, pale things with many short legs, no visible eyes, and shells composed of overlapping mineral plates. They graze upon bacterial films in the flooded channels. When a seam begins to close with deposited stone, they scrape it open. When roots intrude too deeply, they pare away the tips. Their mouthparts strike as they work, making the clicks that pass beneath the basin in organized waves.

A single colony may contain hundreds of thousands of bodies distributed through a network of cracks. No member knows more than its immediate tunnel, but a change in pressure travels through the entire multitude. One click means water rising. Two close strikes mark fresh mineral. A rolling sequence warns of a root break, a fallen body, or the approach of rain. The great waves heard above are not songs. They are the colony turning itself, corridor by corridor, toward altered conditions.

During the long dry intervals between rains, they remain almost entirely below. When the warm deluge comes and the seams overflow, they emerge onto the pavement in shining masses. They arrange themselves along the pale mineral curves, packing shell to shell until the old bands appear raised and moving. There they molt. The cast plates remain after the living bodies withdraw, and for several years the mineral lines are edged with delicate white husks.

The whistlers inhabit the trees.

They are narrow-bodied and long-fingered, with folded skin between wrist, flank, and ankle. Their limbs can clasp branches in several directions, and their heads rotate farther than seems compatible with a spine. They have no external eyes. Where eyes might be are two taut disks of bare skin, each exquisitely sensitive to air pressure. Their mouths are small tubes held almost perpetually open.

Their whistles are not exploratory, as a bat’s cries might be, but custodial. Each tree-dwelling group maintains a steady sequence of tones that identifies the position of trunks, laden leaves, resting animals, and open gaps. A whistle repeated without alteration means that nothing nearby has moved. A bent note announces a new obstacle. A trill indicates falling water. In this way they keep the immediate darkness described for one another.

They feed on leaf-cup grazers, bark mites, and the sweet nodules that swell near wounded branches. Yet they do not strip a tree. A group visits the same feeding places according to cycles that may be older than any individual. Their young learn the routes by clinging beneath adults, feeling each whistle vibrate through the chest before hearing its echo.

When frightened, a whistler draws breath and produces the thin, regular shriek that resembles a distant kettle. Others take it up tree by tree. The warning can circle the basin while the original danger remains in one grove.

Above them move the broad-winged creatures.

They roost on the outer faces of the seven structures, where vines do not grow. Their wings are heavy and membranous, furred along the leading edge, and broad enough to gather condensation as they fly. Their bodies are compact, but their wingspans rival the spread of the low trees. They travel slowly, rarely flapping more than necessary, buoyed by warm air rising from the saturated floor.

Their pulses are felt rather than heard. Each emission compresses the air in a blunt wave. Leaves shiver. Water falls. Small animals flatten themselves to bark. The returning pressure sketches stone, branch, structure, and living body inside a chamber of cartilage beneath the flyer’s breastbone.

They do not perceive surfaces alone. Through vibration, they sense hollows and density: the water under a slab, soil inside a depression, the dry interior of a structure, the cold column descending through the central shaft. To them the basin is not dark. It is translucent.

Their feeding apparatus is a fringe of fine hooks along the lower wing edges. They sweep over vegetation and open stone, gathering airborne spores, mating insects, drifting shed skins, and the minute creatures thrown aloft by their own pressure pulses. After feeding, they hang with wings extended. Water drains along their membranes into the mouth.

They avoid the center except during rain.

The basin also contains slower beings, difficult to recognize as animals. In the fungal court, leathery fans open from the stone on stalks as thick as wrists. Their upper surfaces carry warm, velvety growths; beneath, rings of muscle tighten over hours. They appear rooted, but each rain reveals that they have changed position. They graze in increments too small to observe, lifting one edge, dissolving the film beneath, and settling forward. Some may take a century to cross the court.

Tiny creatures live within them and defend them with bitter secretions. The fans in turn carry these inhabitants between fungal outcrops. Neither can reproduce without the other. Their offspring begin as a soft communal mat; only after many seasons does the mat divide into separate walkers.

Near the reed hollow are animals made almost entirely of filament. They stretch themselves between stems and remain there with droplets strung along their bodies. When a flying creature brushes one, the filament recoils into a knot, enclosing whatever it has caught. Digestion may take months. Once empty, the animal unknots and climbs back into the humid air.

In the vertical pool lives a sealed population found nowhere else.

The translucent swimmers inside it are flattened like leaves and travel through the upright water without acknowledging the direction of the ground. Their organs drift freely within their bodies. A dark point circulates through each animal, sometimes in the head, sometimes in the tail, sometimes passing through a neighboring individual when two overlap.

They graze the inner wall of the suspended pool and cluster around objects pressed against its surface. If a warm hand touches the water, they assemble opposite the palm. They do not cross out, even when drops escape. A swimmer carried in an escaped drop stiffens immediately, becoming a clear husk around a single black grain. The grain remains alive. During the next great rain, clicking animals gather these grains and bear them into cracks beneath the structure.

No one knows whether this is predation, burial, or part of a cycle that continues underground.

The structures themselves have inhabitants, though not in the ordinary sense.

Dry films line their interiors. These resemble soot until moisture, blood, sap, or breath touches them. Then they gather into branching figures and withdraw into seams. In the sound-turning shelter, the film thickens wherever distant noises are made audible, arranging itself into patterns like the anatomy of ears. In the soil-producing structure, it forms rootlike meshes around the dark new earth but never enters it. In the room of the vertical pool, it avoids the water entirely.

The films may be residue from former occupants. They may be growths feeding upon the structures’ effects. Or they may be the only visible portions of something still operating within the seamless walls.

Animals treat them as alive. Whistlers call at the thresholds but do not enter. The broad-winged creatures roost outside with their heads pointed inward. Clicking colonies beneath the floors alter their rhythm when the films move.

The central shaft has its own attendants.

They are seldom encountered except by touch. Small, cool bodies cling to the inner rim, each no larger than a fingertip and shaped like a closed flower. Their shells bear concentric ridges. At intervals one opens, revealing a circle of fine tendrils, and leans into the denser darkness below.

Nothing has been seen feeding them. They do not drink the condensation that gathers nearby. Yet they grow, split, and leave empty shells cemented in rings around the shaft. The innermost rings are fresh and soft. Those farther out are mineralized and ancient. Counting them is impossible, for touching one often causes several to crumble, releasing a smell of wet fruit and old metal.

When an object is lowered, the attendants open in sequence as it passes. When the object returns, they close in the opposite order. Seeds raised sprouting bear tiny punctures in their new roots. A hand withdrawn exhausted carries, for a few hours, circular marks too small to have been made by any visible mouth.

There is also the solitary caller.

No one knows its body. It may be a single long-lived animal, a succession of animals, or an effect produced by some alignment of structure, water, and living throat. Its descending cry begins high above the upper range of the whistlers, passes through audibility, and continues downward until it is felt in the soles of the feet. It lasts long enough for every other creature to fall silent.

The cry does not come from a fixed point. Sometimes it seems to issue from the rim. Sometimes from beneath the central clearing. Sometimes the air itself narrows around it.

The basin listens.

The frogs stop striking. The colonies suspend their clicks halfway through a wave. The broad wings stiffen overhead and glide without pulsing. Even the underground water seems, briefly, to lose its boom.

In that silence, minute movements become perceptible: leaf-cup swimmers touching the skin of water, roots tightening in cracks, shell-flowers opening along the shaft, films rearranging themselves in dry rooms.

Then the call sinks below feeling.

For a few breaths nothing resumes. The pause is never exactly the same length, but every creature appears to know when it has ended. The first sound is always a single click beneath the stone. Another answers far away. The sequence spreads. Frogs knock. Whistlers restore the dimensions of their trees. Wings strike moisture loose.

During the decennial rains, all these lives converge.

The hidden channels fill until the clicking colonies are forced upward. Frogs unseal themselves from fissures. Filament animals contract from the reeds and crawl across the pavement in glistening knots. Whistlers leave their separate trees, gliding clumsily between groves. Broad-winged creatures descend low enough for their fur to brush the backs of assembled animals. Walking fungal fans arrive from routes begun years earlier.

They gather most densely along the invisible boundaries, precisely where they will not remain at other times.

There the animals feed, mate, molt, exchange parasites, abandon young, retrieve young not their own, and press living material into the narrow sterile lines. Their combined sound becomes the roar heard below the plateau. Yet beneath the apparent frenzy is an arrangement. Each kind occupies a distinct vibration. Each adds a pulse, knock, whistle, scrape, wingbeat, or sustained tone. The basin’s partitioned gardens tremble in response.

Roots turn.

Spores settle or fail.

Leaves on one side of a boundary collapse into soil, while those a hand’s breadth away harden and remain.

The structures vibrate below hearing. The cold darkness in the central shaft rises nearly to its rim. For the duration of the rain, the shell-flowers around it open together.

It may be that the gathering is a census. It may be maintenance. It may be reproduction on the scale of the entire basin, with each animal carrying some fragment of instruction between the seven rooms. No species appears to understand the whole pattern. Yet all obey it.

When the rain ends, the inhabitants return to their separations.

Many die on the open stone. Their bodies are dismantled according to where they fall. In a garden, they soften within days and enter root or fungus. On the bare pavement, they may remain intact for decades, polished by condensation until they resemble carvings. Along a boundary, they divide strangely: one half decays, the other persists.

Nothing scavenges these preserved remains. Animals step around them. Colonies tunnel beneath them. Leaves fall beside them and endure.

Over time, the pavement acquires a sparse population of perfect dead things: a folded wing, a ringed shell, the many-legged section of a colony worker, the delicate hand of a whistler. They are not numerous enough to make the basin feel like a graveyard. They seem instead to be marks in a notation whose sound has not yet arrived.

Life there is not blind so much as freed from appearance. No flower needs color. No hunter needs outline. No creature can dominate merely by watching from afar. Existence is announced by pressure, warmth, flavor, patience, and echo.

The basin’s inhabitants know one another as disturbances.

A tree knows the drink of its roots and the feet along its bark. A whistler knows the hollow returned by a call. A flyer knows the soft density of flesh inside stone-shaped darkness. The clicking multitudes know interruption and flow. The structures, if they know anything, know what approaches, what enters, and how much older it is when it leaves.

Together they inhabit not an ecosystem growing toward some future abundance, but one that has already reached its enduring form. Birth does not expand it. Death does not diminish it. Every creature is temporary, but every place prepared for a creature remains.

The life of the basin passes through those places slowly, as water passes through ancient rock: divided, pressured, unseen, and never entirely still.
