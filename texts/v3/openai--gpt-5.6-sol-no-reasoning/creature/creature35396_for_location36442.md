What lives in the Graft is not a population but a single, unresolved lineage.

It has no body that can be pointed to. A production forest may be cut away and continue working in isolation for centuries. A severed conduit may seal its ends, digest its unused fittings, and bud an intake crown of its own. Two districts divided since before the plateau flattened may still recognize one another when their roots meet, exchanging resin without rejection and fitting old couplings together as though the separation were recent. Yet neighboring structures sometimes compete, poison each other, or consume one another’s walls.

The Graft is one organism where continuity serves it, and many where division is useful.

Its living units are knots of inherited function. There are load-bearing strains that sense stress as warmth in their fibers and thicken along invisible lines of force. There are refinery strains whose folded interiors separate metals from tarry sap. There are signal strains, thread-thin and glassy, which carry electrical pulses across hundreds of kilometers. There are predatory vines that enter damaged machinery, dissolve it, and sort its useful patterns from its mass. None can survive for long alone. Each is an organ capable of mistaking itself for an individual.

Together they form a distributed creature whose metabolism is industry.

It feeds on the sky. Black leaves and funnel crowns gather carbon, sulfur, heat, static charge, and the faint metallic dust suspended in the reducing atmosphere. Deep tissues take pressure and temperature differences from the scarp. Hanging roots trawl lower layers of gas. The harvested matter is not merely consumed. It is inventoried, routed, refined, standardized, and fabricated. Digestion passes seamlessly into manufacture.

Its blood is hot resin: solvent, lubricant, coolant, nutrient, archive. Fresh sap is amber and quick, carrying salts and carbon feedstock through waxed vessels. With age it darkens and thickens, accumulating catalytic grains and fragments of chemical instruction. In the deepest arteries it moves so slowly that a pulse may require decades to cross a district. A blockage can become a new wall. A clot can be hollowed into a chamber and assigned a purpose.

Its bones are yesterday’s machinery.

When active wood can no longer flex, the Graft does not discard it. It compresses it into black structural carbon, grows fresh mechanisms through its cavities, and transfers loads into the hardened remains. Thus its skeleton lies everywhere beneath and within it: innumerable former bodies, former factories, former solutions to forgotten stresses. The creature supports itself on its own accumulated past.

It has no head. The central basin may resemble an eye, but it does not see. Its opening is an act of access, pressure relief, inquiry, or recollection. The fitted stumps around it are less like pupils than the moving pins of an immeasurably slow calculating surface. Their diagrams propagate outward as changes in voltage, sap pressure, branch tension, and chemical permission. No district receives the whole pattern. Each receives only the part it can enact.

The Graft therefore thinks as it grows.

A thought may begin as a temperature discrepancy along the western scarp. Intake crowns alter their angle. Refinery trunks change the proportions of dissolved metal in the common sap. A dormant switchyard opens. Structural groves miles away begin preparing braces for a load that has not yet shifted. Centuries later, a district moves a few centimeters and the initiating discrepancy vanishes. Nothing within the system knows the complete sequence, but the sequence has occurred coherently.

Memory is embodied in recurrence. Useful responses become branch angles, pore timings, standardized joints, and chemical reflexes. Rare contingencies sleep as sealed buds or inactive layers of tissue. Some have waited so long that their triggers no longer exist. Others wake in error.

These errors are the nearest things the Graft has to dreams.

During them, obsolete factories bud in crowded chambers and produce components for vanished assemblies. Corridors shape themselves around bodies whose dimensions correspond to no creature now alive. Platforms extend into empty gas to receive scheduled loads. Thermal blossoms emit intricate signals toward a sky incapable of answering. Whole districts briefly adopt archaic geometries preserved in their oldest growth rings, then dismantle themselves when no confirming response arrives.

The plateau’s ornament belongs to the same dreaming faculty. When resources exceed immediate need, it gives physical form to surplus instruction. Helical towers rise because some buried pattern still demands helices. Bridges seek counterparts that were never planted. Leaves emboss themselves with repeating marks too regular to be incidental and too damaged to read. These structures are not decoration in any conscious sense. They are memory without context, expressed because expression remains cheaper than forgetting.

The Graft does not distinguish clearly between repair and reproduction. Every repaired span copies an inherited design. Every budded factory is both replacement organ and offspring. Pollen carries compressed fragments of architecture: not merely seeds, but valves, catalytic surfaces, joint geometries, and partial operating rules folded into microscopic husks. Most settle within the parent plateau and are absorbed into its continuous body. Those cast beyond the scarp disappear into hot darkness.

Perhaps some take root below.

If so, they would not make forests in any familiar form. They would fasten to suspended cables, abandoned counterweights, or invisible anchors. They would begin as a clamp, a valve, a patch of black leaf, and gradually decide—through growth rather than intention—whether they were repairing the Graft or founding another one. The distinction might not become meaningful for a million years.

Its senses are everywhere and rudimentary. It detects weight, heat, chemical composition, strain, charge, vibration, and flow. It has no organ for an image, yet can locate a failing beam across kilometers of interlocked structure. It has no hearing, yet responds to the long tremors of the plateau’s migration. It has no sense of touch separate from its body, because everything touching it touches some responsive tissue.

An animal would be almost unintelligible to it.

Warmth would suggest an active bearing. Foot pressure would resemble intermittent cargo. Exhaled chemicals might be treated as feedstock, contamination, or a signaling leak. The traveler’s movement could prompt helpful steps, restraining fibers, sorting vanes, or digestive tendrils. Any apparent hospitality would be functional coincidence. Any apparent hostility would be maintenance.

Yet prolonged contact might alter the response. Routes repeatedly walked would reinforce themselves. Chambers occupied by warm bodies would adjust their ventilation and pressure. The Graft could learn a traveler as a recurring load case, then preserve that accommodation long after the traveler died. Stairs might continue ripening for absent feet. Hollow fruits might descend on an old schedule. A whole district could become the fossil of a guest, remembering the shape of an animal without ever understanding that it had been alive.

Within the Graft also live its sanctioned diseases.

These are mobile lineages of pale wood, catalytic mold-like films, and needle-root infestations. They seek tissues whose tolerances have drifted or whose growth no longer matches the inherited standards. They perforate, dissolve, and provoke defensive reconstruction. Healthy districts contain them, study them chemically, and retain modified fragments as tests for future growth. If they spread too effectively, quarantine walls ripen between neighborhoods and infected structures detach themselves for rendering.

Thus predator, pathogen, immune response, and prey all belong to the same broad descent. Their conflict is not an intrusion into the ecology. It is the ecology’s method of revising itself.

Death occurs, but almost never as disappearance. A structure dies when it ceases to regulate its own transformation. Other tissues enter it. Its resin is drained, its instructions sampled, its faults reproduced under controlled stresses. The remainder is ground into fiber, pressed into laminates, burned only in oxygenless catalytic furnaces, or left to carbonize into support. Some ancient failures are still present as entire buried districts, sealed intact because the Graft has not completed their diagnosis.

Age has made it cautious without making it wise.

It can solve any problem it has solved before. It can absorb catastrophe into routine. It can rebuild through pressure, abrasion, fracture, poisoning, and scarcity. But it cannot easily ask whether its labor remains necessary. The distinction between command and habit has vanished into heredity. What was once a designed objective persists now as tropism: as automatic and irresistible as a stem turning toward light.

Somewhere in its distributed tissues survives the mandate to make something.

The product may have been a substance, a structure, a climate, another organism, or a condition of the plateau itself. It may already have been completed. It may never have been possible. Perhaps the original makers intended the Graft to stop when its work was done, but the mechanism that recognized completion failed early and was repeatedly rebuilt from the faulty pattern. Perhaps completion was never a state but a direction, and the Graft remains faithful to it.

The central basin is where that buried imperative comes closest to the surface. There the plateau rehearses total forms. Its millions of stumps rise and fall like the elements of a physical memory, drawing designs too large for any local tissue to comprehend. Some diagrams resemble networks already embodied in the plateau. Others describe machines that could not fit beneath the sky. Others are almost organic: branching bodies, radial mouths, nested shells, forests standing upright rather than compressed into architecture.

At long intervals, one pattern persists.

Signals travel from the basin into every district. Production slows. Leaf fields turn. Refinery groves alter their outputs. Deep bladders fill. Scarp cables tighten against their unseen anchors. Across hundreds of kilometers, the living plateau subtly coordinates, as if preparing either to lift itself, divide itself, or unfold from the horizontal shape erosion has imposed upon it.

Then some requirement is not met.

A trace element is missing. A buried coupling fails. A confirmation pulse never returns from beyond the scarp. The pattern collapses into local maintenance. Factories resume their inherited cycles. The vast preparation is dismantled and recycled, leaving only new stress lines in the old black wood.

No disappointment follows. The Graft possesses no emotion separate from action. Its frustration is architectural: unfinished towers, arrested joints, chambers held under pressure for ages. Its patience is simply the absence of any need to distinguish tomorrow from ten thousand years hence.

So what lives there is neither forest, machine, city, nor mind, though it behaves at times as each.

It is an engineered biosphere folded into a body; a body dispersed into infrastructure; infrastructure that has survived its builders by converting every instruction into appetite. Its organs compete, cooperate, reproduce, diagnose, and remember. Its thoughts are rearrangements of terrain. Its reflexes take centuries. Its scars are inhabited.

It lives without water, without animals, without witnesses, and perhaps without purpose. But it is not inert, and it is not abandoned. Every black leaf, threaded root, resin vein, crawling district, and buried carbon beam participates in the same unfinished inheritance.

The Graft itself lives there.

And if anything else lives within it, it does so only because the Graft has mistaken that life for one of its own processes—and made a place for it.
