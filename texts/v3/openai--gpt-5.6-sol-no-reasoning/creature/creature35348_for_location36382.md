They live in the joins.

Not in caves, for the high country has few chambers spacious enough to deserve the name, and not upon the exposed stone, where the white light would reveal them at once. They inhabit the narrow intervals where one mineral surface approaches another without quite meeting: water-filled seams, blue shadow-lines beneath shelves, capillaries winding through the crowded rock. Some of these passages are thinner than a fingernail. Others descend farther than sight, opening below into submerged networks that may connect the plateau’s lakes, basins, and cataracts.

The inhabitants are colorless and exceedingly flat.

At rest, one resembles a spill of clear wax pressed against the stone. Its boundary is difficult to locate. Water passes over it without rippling, and the mineral beneath remains visible through its body, altered only by a slight softening of detail. There is no fixed head or tail. No organs can be distinguished except for fine internal lines, pale as fractures in ice, which divide and reunite according to the contour of the surface beneath it.

What appears to be an individual may cover no more than a handspan. Elsewhere, a translucent film extends through hundreds of connected cracks and emerges in separate pools as several apparently unrelated shapes. It is impossible to know whether these are members of a species, extremities of one distributed creature, or temporary concentrations of something present throughout the plateau’s water.

They do not swim. They change adherence.

A shape found on one submerged face may later occupy another, with no observed passage between them. Movement, if movement is the word, occurs when the fine lines within the body align with those inside the rock. The creature thins until it can no longer be separated from the wet surface; at some later interval it gathers elsewhere, condensing out of a joint or unfolding from the underside of a ledge. No current marks its transit. No trail remains except, sometimes, a patch of stone fractionally duller than its surroundings.

They seem to feed upon resonance.

The continuous thunder enters water, crystal, and bone alike, but within these pale bodies it produces minute standing patterns. Under close observation, their internal lines brighten at certain pitches and disappear at others. Cavities that hum most strongly contain the greatest number of them. Around silent-looking pools whose depths communicate with roaring fissures, they lie layered one over another until the water appears thick with glass.

They take nothing visible from the stone. They leave no waste. Instead, they alter the sound passing through them, separating the plateau’s total roar into components too low, high, or sustained for ordinary hearing. It may be nourishment, perception, or both. What seems lifeless repose may be an unbroken act of listening.

Their bodies possess no mouth because the whole surface receives. They have no eyes because pressure reaches them from every direction. The faint branching lines are neither veins nor nerves in the usual sense; they are arrangements that continually tune themselves to the surrounding rock. A collapse in some inaccessible depth, a droplet releasing inside a sealed pore, the strain of a stone arch under its own weight—each travels through the plateau and passes into them.

Perhaps this is why the place sounds without shaking. The inhabitants do not create the great noise, but they hold it. Across ages they have spread into the mineral lattice and preserved every vibration that entered it, preventing sound from diminishing into silence. The roar is the combined persistence of innumerable impacts, falls, fractures, and flows, accumulated without decay. The plateau is less an echo chamber than a memory saturated past forgetting.

The dark specks above are another phase of the same life.

Rarely, where a waterfall leaves the escarpment, a portion of the translucent substance becomes so attenuated that it travels upward through the falling water rather than down. At the lip it separates as a dark, compact point. Against the brilliant sky it crosses with absolute steadiness, without wingbeat or correction, toward another plateau. Its darkness is not pigment but density: a body folded through itself until light can no longer pass.

Nothing sees it depart. Nothing sees it arrive. Yet after such crossings, new pale patches sometimes appear in distant lakes.

These airborne forms may carry no flesh at all. They may be knots of stored vibration, propagating from one stone country to another. Their straight courses are determined not by wind but by correspondence: one resonant cavity answering another across the gulf. What looks like an animal in flight may be the visible interval of a call.

There may also be larger inhabitants.

The rounded massifs have surfaces too intricately crowded to survey, and their interiors are veined with water. Their hum differs subtly from one to another. Some hold a low tone that can be felt through the soles long before the massif comes into view; others produce a thin pressure behind the eyes. The differences are stable enough to suggest identity.

It is possible that each massif is alive—not because the stone itself grows, but because a colorless inhabitant occupies its entire hidden wetness, spread through millions of pores. The hand-sized forms in the lakes would then be exposed folds of these immense beings, places where an interior organism reaches the outer world. What seems like a creature changing position might instead be one vast body withdrawing from one opening and presenting itself at another.

If so, the plateau is crowded twice over: visibly with stone, invisibly with lives as large and interlocked as watersheds. They touch along saturated seams. Their boundaries follow mineral contacts. They exchange pressure through the unceasing roar. Since nothing here changes quickly enough to establish before and after, their conversations may require centuries to form a single distinction.

They do not hunt. They do not flee. There is no abundance followed by famine, no mating season, migration, bloom, or rot. Reproduction may occur only when a plate of stone finally parts along an ancient line and one continuous field of living transparency becomes two. Death may be the reverse: separate beings joined by mineral deposition until neither can maintain a distinct resonance.

Such events would be rare beyond any practical witnessing.

To a visitor, they remain patches under water, uncertain and inert. Yet prolonged stillness near them produces an unsettling impression. The surrounding roar begins to acquire internal distances. One tone seems to come from beneath the pool, another from far across the plateau, another from inside the listener’s ribs. The pale shape does not stir, but its branching lines gradually resemble the arrangement of cracks underfoot, then the cataracts at the rim, then the vessels visible through the skin of one’s own hand.

It is not mimicking these things. It is revealing that, to its mode of perception, they are already connected.

The high country’s inhabitants have no need to cross the crowded terrain. They live through its continuity. Stone is their shelter, water their medium, sound their sustenance and sense. The fixed light neither guides nor harms them. They occupy a world without emptiness, and therefore without the ordinary requirement to move from one place to another.

They are almost impossible to recognize as living because they do not contend with the plateau’s completeness.

They are the part of it that listens.
