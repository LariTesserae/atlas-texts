The people who live here are difficult to separate from the place that houses them.

They are warm-bodied, generally; the doors know them by heat. They have feet suited to yielding roots and wet bark, hands that rest readily against trunks, and lungs accustomed to air heavy with spores, mist, and the green exhalations of civic organs. Beyond that, their bodies vary. Some are tall enough to wade the canals without bridges. Some are small and broad-fingered, able to climb the sweating walls of inhabited crowns. Others wear living mantles that make any underlying shape uncertain. No single form defines citizenship. A frog may participate in a council by altering its song. A glider flock may possess rights over an upper current. A crab colony repairing a wound in a transit tree may be treated, for the duration of the work, as a municipal office.

The city has taught its inhabitants not to value hard boundaries.

Their clothing is cultivated: soft fungal felts, leaf skins, woven root hair, membranes that open their pores in heat. Garments are rarely owned for long. A coat may thicken during the rainy season, produce pockets in response to use, then root into a bedroom wall when discarded. Sandals learn the pressure of a walker and stiffen just before each step. Ceremonial dress often includes transparent bladders of delayed light, so that a person arrives at an evening gathering surrounded by the afternoon in which they dressed.

Most residents carry little. Possessions fare poorly in a city eager to digest unattended matter, and transit trees have a habit of misplacing objects into years or alcoves. Tools are requested from tool-vines and returned as cuttings. Letters are grown as folded petals whose veins darken into script; urgent ones release anticipatory pollen and may be read shortly before they are written. What people keep, they keep close to the body: a flavor remembered by a house, a scar-pattern recognized by bridges, a sequence of taps that opens a family chamber, seeds descended from a departed companion’s garden.

Their homes are not property in the rigid sense. A household maintains an ongoing persuasion with its tree. It feeds certain roots, trims certain buds, gathers in the same places until rooms swell around the habit. Families are therefore partly anatomical. Their history survives as bends in branches, recurring alcoves, a preference for eastern light, or a corridor that still opens for someone dead.

Children learn the city through response. They test which vines will hold them, which pools object to being stirred, how to place a palm on speaking roots without confusing an announcement for thunder. They are warned never to sleep in a transit passage, never to eat a marker meant for the tusked canal-keepers, and never to promise anything beside a listening bud unless they intend the promise to acquire leaves.

No one is wholly certain how children are recognized as citizens. Some say it happens when a dwelling first grows a chamber for them without being coaxed. Others say it is when the rootways begin transmitting their footsteps rather than merely bearing their weight. In practice, the city seems to adopt individuals gradually. It learns their warmth, waste, routines, fears, and favored routes. Eventually the environment begins anticipating them. A bridge extends before they reach the bank. A lantern fruit ripens outside their door. Bitter growth blocks a dangerous path on the night they would have taken it.

The people reciprocate by attending closely.

Their principal skill is interpretation. They read tension in stems, sweetness in the air, the angle of condensation on a railing-vine. They know whether a room is closing from neglect or from offense. They distinguish a civic copper flush from mineral sickness. They can hear, in a building’s hum, whether its insects are carrying news or merely courting. Their engineers are gardeners, physicians, animal trainers, meteorologists, midwives, and negotiators at once. They do not draw plans so much as establish appetites and constraints, then guide whatever emerges.

This does not make them passive. Cultivation can be forceful. Uncooperative tissue is pruned, starved, grafted, infected with corrective fungi, or exposed to carefully selected memories from the deep roots. During disputes, residents may sabotage one another by confusing a house’s sense of occupancy, sweetening the wrong avenue, or teaching a doorway to recognize an enemy’s fever. There are crimes of misleading growth: false paths, counterfeit pollen, predatory rooms concealed as shelters. The most feared thieves do not steal objects. They steal recognition, persuading homes and bridges that another person has never lived there.

Names are therefore more than sounds. A complete name may include body temperature, scent, microbial signature, gait, a personal rhythm tapped through the soles, and the response of certain cultivated cells to one’s touch. Spoken names are only conveniences. The city knows its residents by richer patterns, though it occasionally remembers them incorrectly.

Death is rarely marked by a durable grave. Bodies are given to chosen systems: cistern groves, glider nurseries, fruiting walls, the black peat beneath the plazas. Some residents arrange to become rooms. Others enter the deep roots so that fragments of their chemistry recur during rain. For several seasons after a death, familiar doorways may open at the deceased person’s remembered warmth. Houses sometimes bud an extra sleeping chamber no one can use. These growths are treated gently until they close.

The inhabitants mourn through interruption. A market remains unfolded overnight. A bridge is allowed to span empty water. A household stops guiding its tree and lets the rooms choose their own arrangement. At funerals, stored daylight is released before dawn, making a brief, misplaced afternoon in which the dead person’s routes are walked one last time.

Memory does not guarantee identity. The ancient roots preserve states, not stories, and when old patterns rise into the sap, people may experience impulses belonging to vanished populations. During obsolete seasons, residents dream of pollinators they have never seen. They wake craving minerals from buried rivers. Their hands perform pruning gestures adapted to branches that no longer grow. Some believe these episodes are ancestral communion. Others regard them as civic indigestion.

A few devote their lives to entering such recurrences. They sit against old trunks in the rain and allow extinct weather to move through their bodies. They return altered: speaking in unfamiliar rhythms, frightened by modern flowers, or carrying practical knowledge whose purpose has been lost. Such people are respected, but houses are sometimes slow to open for them, uncertain whether the returning warmth belongs to one resident or many.

Government belongs to no stable class. Anyone may propose a future to a cultivated bud, but not everyone can persuade the city to nourish the resulting model. Skilled advocates feed simulations with carefully balanced traces: canal mud, household scents, fragments of song, projected appetites, remembered floods. The miniature futures develop quickly. Their tiny inhabitants may flourish, revolt, starve, migrate, or invent outcomes no participant expected.

Watching them is not considered entertainment, though crowds gather.

The rejected futures trouble many residents. Officially, planting them in the outer marsh is an act of mercy and epistemic caution: no living possibility should be destroyed merely because it was not selected. Unofficially, everyone knows some models grow beyond their premises. They send pollen back toward the basin. They alter distant lights. Travelers sometimes return insisting they visited a city where an old dispute ended differently and met people who resemble themselves.

The main city’s residents call these stories marsh-fever. They also quietly mark their doors against unfamiliar versions of familiar warmth.

Daily life remains practical. People harvest cistern water, tend chamber buds, trade flavors, ferry seedlings, groom the symbiotic wings of gliders, and bargain with canal animals over blocked channels. They eat fruit, fungal flesh, tender root nodules, fish gathered from flooded courts, and translucent wafers secreted by communal kitchen leaves. Meals are intensely seasonal, sometimes changing within a single afternoon as a neighborhood shifts weather.

Markets sell services more often than objects. One vendor may teach a wall to become acoustically private. Another offers moths that remove a painful scent from a room. A third grows temporary fingers for delicate work. There are specialists who recover items from transit alcoves, though payment is high and success may return the wrong object, the correct object at the wrong age, or a possession one has not yet lost.

There are festivals whenever the city enters a dramatic revision. Residents paint themselves with luminous microbes and climb into newly opened crowns. Musicians coax buildings to resonate through their hollow stems. Dance is performed on bridges that grow and withdraw according to the dancers’ weight, turning choreography into negotiation. At the height of celebration, public pools rise into walking bubbles, carrying singers through the streets with their voices warped by water.

Humor tends toward the ecological and cruelly affectionate. People imitate the pompous vowels of official root announcements. They leave harmless flavored markers that cause the tusked maintenance animals to trim elaborate shapes into a neighbor’s threshold. Young residents dare one another to stand motionless in transit trees until an alcove begins to form. Houses themselves are said to possess humor, usually when they grow a doorway in an embarrassing place.

Privacy exists, but must be cultivated. A private room is one taught not to transmit vibration, scent, heat, or chemical traces into the larger organism. Such rooms are expensive in care and faintly suspect. Total isolation resembles disease. Most inhabitants accept that their homes know when they quarrel, the rootways know where they walk, and weather organs register the combined breath of a crowd. Secrecy means not becoming unreadable, but creating several plausible readings.

The people are not unanimous about the city’s present turbulence.

Some believe it is approaching a new developmental stage—that the basin as a whole will flower, divide, migrate, or wake into explicit consciousness. They prepare by loosening attachments, keeping possessions light, and teaching their dwellings to travel. Others think the increasing eruptions of ancient seasons indicate that the deep network is losing its ability to distinguish memory from instruction. They prune obsolete blossoms and oppose experiments with time-bending organs.

A smaller group believes the main city has already made its decision and that the apparent debate is merely the slow experience of a choice distributed across neighboring moments. To them, the rooms exchanged between buildings, the upward water, the pollen arriving early, and the restless outer settlements are all parts of a single reproductive act.

They do not agree on what is being born.

Whatever comes, the residents continue their intimate maintenance. At night they check the walls for fever. They draw condensation from the windows with their fingertips and taste it for changes in the roots. They bring small animals indoors before hard rain. Families settle into chambers that tighten gently around their sleeping heat.

Around them live innumerable partners, dependents, opportunists, and unclassifiable neighbors: crabs with medicinal mud under their claws; metallic-voiced frogs nesting in civic organs; leaf-winged gliders drinking charge from mist; tiny tusked dredgers following delicious instructions; fungi that remember the shape of garments; fish that graze submerged council benches; insects whose courtship songs vibrate whole houses.

None of these creatures merely occupies the city. Each helps produce it.

And perhaps the same is true of the people. They may call themselves inhabitants, gardeners, citizens, or descendants, but the distinction weakens with every generation. Their breath enters the weather. Their habits become rooms. Their disputes branch into possible settlements. Their dead recur as chemistry after rain.

They live in the city as thoughts might live in a mind: locally, briefly, vividly—believing themselves separate while continuously altering the body that allows them to exist.
