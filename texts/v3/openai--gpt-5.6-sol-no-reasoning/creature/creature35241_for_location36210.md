The saddle is inhabited less by separate species than by a single ecology distributed among many forms: animals, plants, water, stone, and unseen machinery all serving as organs of one arrested creature.

Its most numerous inhabitants are birds. Slate-backed thrushes balance on wet stones with their wings half spread; white, long-tailed birds remain suspended above the moss at the crest of flight; red finches occupy the silver conifers in dense multitudes separated by impossible intervals. A pale hawk descends forever upon a hare that is forever turning beneath it. These creatures do not merely repeat their actions. Each contains the whole action at once—alarm, pursuit, death, feeding, escape—and their unending calls are complete sounds without beginnings or endings.

The wet crevices harbor deep-green amphibians marked as if with rivets. Their swelling throats voice burdened motors rather than croaks. Iron-shelled snails graze the young black rock, laying down clear trails in which tiny sparks climb against gravity. Thin silver fish school through the air, navigating structures no eye can see and ticking like cooling metal when they turn. In the central black water live other fish that cross the boundary between organism and manufactured object: brass and flesh, egg and fossil, skeleton and machine part, all simultaneous phases of one unchanging body.

Larger beings survive only as partial disclosures. Antlers travel through the chest-high mist without revealing the heads that bear them. A six-legged goat stands between the firs as a goat-shaped vacancy, visible only by the rain outlining its absence. A broad-pawed animal is represented by prints perpetually filling with water, though no body arrives to make them. These may not be hidden creatures so much as unfinished ways of seeing creatures whose complete existence cannot fit into a single moment.

The plants are equally animate. Black-green moss cushions the hollows and heals each impression as it is made. Solitary pale ferns unfold with the hiss of valves. Low conifers embody the permanent shape of wind despite the stillness around them. Translucent stalks bear blue bladders that pulse without changing volume, like lungs belonging to something buried under the mountain.

Most revealing are the white-edged flowers. They are not mouths exactly, nor eyes, but apertures through which the saddle’s inner life becomes visible. Within each blossom stretches a different reach of an immeasurable factory: presses, conveyors, hooks, pistons, furnaces, vats, and toothed wheels. The flowers may be sensory organs of the buried works, or small wounds through which its anatomy shows.

There may also be workers. Their shadows bend over stations, cross the mist, and fall across stones that cast other shadows of their own. No workers themselves appear. Perhaps they exist only as the labor implied by machinery; perhaps they are former travelers whose departures never completed. Anyone attempting to cross the ridge joins the place at the lifted foot, becoming another sustained operation: always leaving, never gone.

What truly lives there, then, is the process itself. It inhabits every throat, root, droplet, wing, and gear-shaped shadow. It pumps water without draining a source, feeds bodies without consuming prey, repairs moss without growth, and drives engines without fuel. The animals are alive, though some are also dead. The machinery functions, though it makes nothing. The mountain is young, though no youth passes through it.

Together they form a flawless, unaging organism whose metabolism produces only its own present. Its breath is furnace-roar in cold mist. Its blood is water moving through rock and root. Its nerves are sparks traveling uphill in snail trails. Its thoughts are birds permanently startled into flight. And its sole manufacture—unceasing, immense, and exact—is the instant in which everything within it remains.
