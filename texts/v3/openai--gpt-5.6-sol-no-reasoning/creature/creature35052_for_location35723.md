The people of the plateau are not one species, though from a distance they can seem like variations of a single austere design.

They are tall because height is useful in thin air and under weak gravity, but narrow because mass is costly. An adult at rest resembles a folded instrument: three or four load-bearing limbs gathered beneath an upright thorax, the joints lacquer-dark, the longer members enclosed in overlapping sleeves of pale ceramic. Between those hard pieces lie braided muscles of carbon fiber, contracting by charge rather than moisture. Nothing about them swells or breathes. Their movements are exact, slightly discontinuous, and accompanied by soft ticks as joints lock and release.

Their bodies are dry all the way through.

Instead of blood, conductive dust circulates through branching channels, carried by peristaltic electrical fields. Instead of a heart, they have several pulse-knots distributed through the torso. Damage to one slows them but rarely kills them. Gas bladders buried among their organs store reactive compounds for metabolism and buoyancy; when frightened, exhausted, or deep in thought, they give off faint chemical clicks as valves adjust. Older citizens often smell of hot stone, ozone, fluorides, and the particular mineral feedstock from which their newest parts were grown.

Their heads are variable. Some have a single smooth sensory hood angled toward vibration. Others bear fans of whiskers fine enough to read the static wake of a passing foil-wing. Many possess no eyes in the human sense. They perceive by combining resonance, electric field, polarized light, trace chemistry, and the pressure patterns traveling through the pall. A citizen standing motionless in a plaza may be attending to events kilometers away through the soles of their limbs.

They speak in several media at once.

Close conversation includes limb-taps, shell resonance, electrostatic pulses, and little packets of scented carbon chain. Public speech is conducted through architecture. A speaker touches a civic root, and nearby benches hum with their cadence; road markings alter emphasis; balconies flex in acknowledgment. Some citizens carry resonant backplates shaped by profession or lineage, so that every movement adds a recognizable undertone to the city’s sound.

Silence, among them, is not politeness. It is concealment, injury, predation, or grief.

They have no mouths built for drinking and no ancestral memory of it. Food is gathered as mineral wafers, charged fibers, reactive gases, and cultivated films peeled from designated feeding walls. Eating is often communal: several bodies connect to a low growth and negotiate its stored charge among themselves. What resembles a meal may last seconds or an entire night. The guests barely move, while amber currents pass through their limbs and the table slowly turns gray.

They do not sleep in beds. They anchor themselves to receptive alcoves, lower their internal voltages, and allow parts of their cognition to spread into the building. During these rests, the distinction between person and dwelling becomes temporarily uncertain. A sleeper may lend memory to an archive, sensory attention to a neighborhood, or motor control to repair swarms. On waking, they sometimes bring back impressions that belonged to the structure: the strain in a distant buttress, the approach of a storm of charged dust, the unresolved dream of an unused room.

Their young are not helpless miniature adults. They begin as motile knots of tissue and ceramic seed, assembled from contributions by several citizens, a chosen building, and often a wild plant lineage. For years they move beneath the pall, learning the city through contact with its roots. They emerge only after developing a stable body plan, and even then continue to replace limbs, senses, and organs as their interests become clear.

Parenthood is therefore distributed. A person may have a mineral parent who supplied structural genes, a resonance parent who taught speech, a civic trunk that housed their early mind, and a migratory wheel-plant whose chemistry persists in their charge organs. Kinship is described less by descent than by contribution: who gave one balance, memory, patience, conductivity, a sense of direction, or the ability to recognize danger in quiet.

No adult form is final. Artisans periodically open themselves in growth chambers and cultivate alterations. Transit workers broaden their gravity organs. Archivists grow cool internal folds where delicate memories can be maintained. Those who tend the needle groves acquire conductive crowns and insulating feet. Diplomats sometimes wear temporary sensory arrays modeled on foreign organisms, accepting months of disorientation for the possibility of understanding them.

Consequently, identity does not reside in appearance. It is carried in rhythm: a characteristic sequence of internal pulses, pauses, and harmonic errors. Buildings recognize citizens by this cadence. Friends feel it through the ground before they see one another. Forgery is possible, but considered an intimate violation.

Their civilization is slow without being stagnant. They think naturally in intervals longer than an organic body. A proposal may be planted, allowed to branch through a civic tissue for fifty years, and adopted only when its growth pattern proves stable. Yet immediate decisions can pass through the plateau almost instantly as electrical alarm. They are patient with construction and ruthless in emergencies.

They do not elect rulers. Matters enter the living civic network as competing hormonal states. Citizens strengthen, inhibit, or redirect them by contact, movement, work, and deliberate resonance. Consensus appears physically: pathways thicken, public chambers reconfigure, transport gradients migrate. Unpopular policies become harder to reach. Urgent ones glow through the plazas.

This system is neither serene nor perfectly fair. Old districts possess deeper roots and therefore greater influence. Large bodies impress stronger signals on the pall. Archives may bias decisions toward outcomes that preserve archives. Some citizens refuse connection and live on isolated plates of exposed stone, wearing insulated feet so the city cannot read them. Others cultivate parasitic arguments that spread through public tissues and reproduce before anyone has understood what they propose.

Crime often takes the form of ecological misdirection: inducing a doorway to admit the wrong rhythm, teaching road markings to lead travelers in circles, persuading repair swarms that an intact organ is damage. The most feared offense is damping—creating a pocket in which a person or neighborhood cannot be heard. The hush predators make the cultural terror literal.

Death, too, is difficult to locate.

A body may stop while its remembered rhythms continue in a house, an avenue, or the behavior of descendants. Some dying citizens distribute themselves deliberately, donating judgment to transit roots, sensory habits to observatories, and fragments of temperament to public shelters. Others insist on remaining singular and are carried to the plateau’s edge, where their components are dismantled beneath the leaning towers.

A few refuse both dissolution and memorial continuity. They seal their charge knots, extinguish their signatures, and walk into the immense descending country. No one follows. To follow would deny the act.

Most inhabitants rarely approach the edge towers. Those who tend them are among the strangest citizens: attenuated beings with broad anchoring feet, no public resonance plates, and bodies polished almost featureless by charged dust. They communicate infrequently and claim no understanding of their charges. They measure each atomic turn, maintain the barren ground around the towers, and record the note that crosses the plateau.

During that note, every citizen feels briefly simplified. Private rhythms vanish inside the immense common vibration. Buildings suspend their dreams. Civic disputes collapse into one pressure. Even the disconnected feel it through the mineral beneath them.

Some believe the towers are listening to the planet’s interior. Some say they track an object that set below the horizon before the city’s oldest buried anatomy was alive. Others argue that the towers are not turning toward anything, but away—maintaining, atom by atom, an ancient refusal.

When the resonance ends, the inhabitants test themselves with small familiar sounds. A tap of limb against stone. A pulse sent through a household wall. The answer of a friend across the pall.

Then they return to their dispersed city: tending the mineral forests, negotiating with buildings, cultivating luck, raising their subterranean young, and adding their own brief rhythms to a mountain that remembers almost everything except why it began to listen.
