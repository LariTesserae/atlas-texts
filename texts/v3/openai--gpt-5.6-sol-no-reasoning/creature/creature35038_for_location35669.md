The Green Throat is inhabited by creatures that have ceased to distinguish between organism and mechanism.

Most obvious are the **throat-lantern frogs**, small pipe-dwellers with flattened toes and soot-dark backs. Their name comes from the yellow membrane beneath the jaw, which they inflate not to amplify a call but to display it. In the engine’s prevailing thunder, sound has become useless; color serves instead. A frog grips a warm conduit, turns toward another across the void, and pulses its throat from amber to sulfur-bright in deliberate sequences. During pressure surges, whole rows of them kindle along the pipework like signal lamps. Their eggs are laid in condensation cups, and their tadpoles graze on the oily films of inspection basins. When the machinery falls briefly quiet, the adults collapse their shining throats and press themselves flat, as if embarrassed by the sudden possibility of being heard.

Above them live **semaphore birds**, compact, broad-winged animals built for crosswinds and abrupt reversals. Their feathers are iron gray except for white underwings and tails divided into vivid bars of red, black, and green. They communicate in flashes: tail spread, wing tilted, pale underside exposed for a fraction of a second. A flock passing behind curtains of foliage appears not as individual birds but as a scattered code moving through the chamber. They nest in abandoned junction housings and line them with moss, cable fiber, and flakes peeled from old warning labels. At the opening of the great hatch, they fly downward rather than upward, knowing the difference between an ordinary draft and the nine-minute suction that carries careless things into the high exterior air.

The catwalks belong to **rail-stilts**, long-legged lizards with pale, delicate bodies and feet broad enough to bridge the holes in corroded grating. They rarely see one another directly. Instead, they lower their narrow chins to the metal and listen through bone. A heel-tap made on one bridge may travel through brackets and braces to another bridge several levels away. Each animal has a characteristic rhythm, altered by age, sex, alarm, and the condition of the structure beneath it. Their territories are not measured by distance but by continuity of vibration. Two rail-stilts separated by ten meters of air may be neighbors; two standing within arm’s reach on mechanically isolated plates may inhabit different worlds.

Cables carry the **hammer moths**. These are broad-bodied, velvet-winged insects whose larvae eat the soft inner insulation of obsolete electrical lines without touching the conductive core. Adults settle on taut spans and strike their abdomens against them. Courtship passes along the cable as a rapid tremor, often farther than scent could travel in the hot, disturbed air. Different line materials have produced different populations: copper-wire moths beat slowly; glass-fiber moths make fine, rainlike bursts; those living on braided lifting cable strike in complex triplets. A moth placed on the wrong kind of line seems mute until it learns the new tension.

In the wet basins live **threadfish**, nearly transparent animals whose red organs show faintly through their sides. They are no longer than a finger, though their bodies are narrow enough to enter bolt holes and threaded pipe mouths. Each basin population disappears when the water level falls, retreating through plumbing that looks impossibly small. They return with the next condensation cycle, sometimes carrying unfamiliar markings, evidence that the hidden reservoirs form a larger submerged territory behind the walls. No inhabitant of the visible chamber knows its extent. To the threadfish, the garden may be merely a chain of bright seasonal openings in an otherwise dark aquatic world.

They share those waters with glass shrimp, black striders, oil beetles, and colonies of red bacteria that stain the steel like old blood. Several kinds of eel live deeper in the pipe system. Only their heads are seen, emerging from valves during heavy condensation to take frogs, beetles, or careless birds. Some are colorless. Others have skins patterned like oxidized copper, making it uncertain whether one is looking at an animal or a wetted pipe until the pipe blinks.

The slow wheel supports the **pilgrim snails**. They live entirely within its rust-lined rim and may never encounter a surface that is still. Their shells are bright cobalt, lacquer red, or chalk white, colors that make them conspicuous to birds but legible to one another across the dim curvature of the wheel. They feed on moss and mineral films while the wheel carries their whole country around its imperceptible orbit. A snail may spend its life traveling against the rotation and die near the place where it hatched, having crawled many times that distance over moving ground. Their eggs are sealed beneath rust scales. When a scale breaks free, a clutch may fall through hundreds of meters of open chamber like a small inhabited continent.

The largest animal regularly seen is the **gantry grazer**, though “animal” may be too simple a word for it. It resembles a six-limbed sloth assembled from vine, bark, and tarnished metal. Mature individuals span several paces but weigh little, their bodies being mostly hollow chambers filled with warm air. They move along the undersides of bridges, eating moss, orchid roots, cable sheathing, and the soft corrosion that gathers around bolts. Their claws contain magnetite and lock onto steel during pressure purges. Green filaments grow from their backs and photosynthesize; beetles pollinate the small flowers that open along their shoulders. Whether those filaments are fur transformed into vegetation, plants cultivated by the animal, or a symbiotic organism older than either is not clear. A resting grazer resembles an overgrown brace. Some remain motionless long enough for rail-stilts to establish territories across them.

There are hunters suited to such prey. **Gap mantises** hang from vines with their limbs spread, nearly invisible against the crossings of cable and stem. Their bodies are extremely narrow, but their grasping arms may reach the length of a person’s forearm. They do not pursue. They wait where insects travel between islands of vegetation, sensing distortions in the constant pressure waves. When the engine’s pitch changes, they reposition before the other animals understand that a new air current has begun.

Still rarer are **white kites**, blind, membranous predators that emerge from ventilation ducts during the brief quiet intervals. They navigate by reading warmth and turbulence across the skin of their wings. The engine’s full roar overwhelms this sense, confining them to inaccessible hollows most of the time. In the minute of relative calm, they unfold and glide through the chamber, taking moths and young birds in near silence. When the relay below closes, they flee the returning concussion with such precision that their retreat seems to anticipate the machinery.

The plants are inhabitants too, and many are active participants in the engine’s cycles.

**Pulse orchids** root in rust shelves and open only when sustained vibration loosens a latch within their stems. Their flowers are blue, waxy, and downward-facing. Beetles crossing them are dusted along the back, where the pollen is protected from condensation. When the engine quiets, the flowers close around any beetle still inside and hold it safely until the vibration resumes.

**Chain figs** grow without soil around the immense descending links. Their roots braid themselves into flexible collars, riding downward for years until they reach a warm level, then dying back and releasing seed into the updraft. A single chain may therefore carry successive bands of forest, each one corresponding to an altitude, temperature, and period in the machine’s cycle.

The huge bromeliad in the turbine bowl is less a plant than a district. Its outer leaves are wide enough to walk upon. Their cups contain separate pools with distinct water chemistry: acidic nurseries for frogs, alkaline films where shell-building mites feed, black anaerobic pockets inhabited by worms that never encounter oxygen. The plant flowers perhaps once in several decades, raising a red stalk into the central void. Semaphore birds tear away pieces of it for their nests, spreading sticky seeds to higher collars.

The rootless hanging vines are called **draftweavers**. Some descend from unseen gardens above, but others appear to originate nowhere. They absorb water directly through their bark and minerals from the dust carried in ventilation currents. Their tendrils knot around obsolete cables, copying the cables’ twists so exactly that living and manufactured strands become inseparable. When the overhead hatch opens, the vines rise together toward the blue aperture. Their pale undersides turn outward, and for nine minutes the chamber seems to grow upward.

Fungi occupy the boundary between rust and life. **Rivet caps** digest oils trapped beneath bolt heads. **Bearing lace** forms white geometries over hot housings and withdraws into cracks whenever the temperature rises. Deep orange fungal shelves reinforce corroded seams with mineralized fibers; in several places they may be the only reason a garden platform remains attached to the wall. The engine supports them, they support the engine, and neither arrangement appears designed.

Within the opaque control booth lives a colony unlike any other in the Throat. Thin black insects—perhaps termites, perhaps something descended from booklice—have hollowed the damp paper calendar into a layered nest. They preserve the printed grid while removing nearly everything between the inked lines. Queens occupy the months. Workers travel the days. Dead switches serve as warm brood chambers when current leaks through the panel. The colony changes its activity according to the forgotten maintenance schedule, becoming restless before mirror turns and pressure releases. It may be reading vibrations through the console, or the schedule may have entered its biology over generations. Either way, the dead booth still keeps time.

Larger presences are suggested but seldom seen. Something heavy moves in the hot spaces behind the eastern wall, leaving five-pointed prints in condensation when it crosses a thin plate. Long nests made from bundled wire appear on inaccessible ledges, each surrounding a heap of polished ceramic fragments. During certain months, the lowest pipes carry a sequence of knocks that does not match any known pump. The rail-stilts answer. After a pause, the knocks answer back.

And there may still be people.

Not the engineers who built the mountain; they are absent except for handrails, chairs, numbers, and schedules. But high in the collars, figures are occasionally seen crossing between mirrors. They wear green-gray coverings, move only during the loudest purges, and secure themselves to the architecture with long lines. They never descend to the booth. They harvest something from the reflector wells—bird eggs, seeds, perhaps accumulated water—and leave behind small arrangements of metal tabs that flash in the sun.

The animals treat these figures as weather. Birds scatter before them. Frogs dim. Moths fall still on their cables. Nothing approaches, and nothing gives an alarm.

Whether the figures live inside the mountain, visit from another manufactured peak, or are merely maintenance devices retaining the shape of their makers cannot be determined from below.

The truest inhabitant, however, may be the Green Throat itself—not the engine as it was designed, nor the garden as something that invaded it, but the long accommodation between them. The machinery provides heat, rhythm, light, water, destruction, and renewal. The organisms soften corrosion, redirect condensation, brace failing structures, clog some passages and clear others. Slow motion gives them time to adapt; life gives slow failure somewhere to go.

Nothing there lives independently. Every frog’s flashing throat depends on an obsolete reflector schedule. Every fish migration follows pressure in forgotten pipes. Every flower opens to a mechanical note. Every rust shelf records both water and root. Even the birds’ silence is an adaptation to the mountain’s voice.

When the engine pauses, all these lives briefly emerge as separate things: ticking water, breathing frogs, claws on railings, wings in humid air.

Then the mountain sounds again, and they become one inhabitant.
