The basin is inhabited chiefly by things that do not require the same hour.

In the spring pockets live broad-leaved plants with dark, polished surfaces and stems the color of wet bone. Their leaves tilt not toward the sun above, but toward whichever stone hollow presently contains morning. A single plant may spend years bent over one cup, then begin, by increments too small to witness, to turn toward another as the borrowed dawn migrates. The oldest have grown into low spirals. Scars from dripping water cross their leaves like pale writing.

Among the ferns are smaller growths easily mistaken for unfurling fronds. These are neither entirely plant nor animal. They remain green and ribbed while still, feeding on moisture and light; but after long rain they loosen from the root mats and move. Their progress is made by folding and opening, no faster than a shadow crossing stone. They travel only between pools showing adjacent hours. If startled, they root themselves where they stand, sometimes for a season, sometimes for decades. Thus the basin’s islands of vegetation shift without visibly spreading.

The moss-covered boulders shelter minute pale creatures with many soft feet. They graze mineral skins from the rock and retreat at the first touch of direct daylight. Because noon does not occupy every hollow at once, they can remain active somewhere throughout the day. Their slow migrations keep certain stones richly clothed while stripping their neighbors bare. After rain, the red cups among the hanging lichens open to receive them. Each cup can hold only one. By the following day both cup and occupant have closed, leaving a small dark seedlike knot that the wind occasionally carries to another wall.

The bronze lizard is less an inhabitant than a regular visitor. It comes up from cracks near the basin’s warm outer face and lies on stones holding the true present. Its scales brighten or dull according to the reflected hour nearby. It will cross dawn, noon, or evening, but never a star-lit patch under daylight. At such rims it pauses, tastes the air, and makes a wide, deliberate circuit. Old individuals are missing toes, as if darkness in the pools had bitten at them.

The solitary bird overhead belongs to the lower forests, yet it returns often enough to be part of the place. From below it appears black; when it banks over the basin, its wings flash deep green and rust. It never lands on the open rock. Sometimes it drops a twig, shell, or small bone into one of the rain-hollows. The object sinks through water far too shallow to conceal it and does not return. Whether these are offerings, tests, or merely a habit of the species cannot be known. The bird waits for the pool to still before circling away.

The three-toed tracks are made by something more difficult to see.

It lives where water passes under the floor: in root tunnels, narrow seams, and the hollow beneath the volcanic shelf. Its body is probably slight, though the spread of its toes makes the prints seem large. It comes out only when an hour in one pool agrees with the hour beneath the stone. Then, for a little while, an unseen route opens between them. The creature crosses soft margins but leaves no marks on dry rock. Occasionally sedges divide around a moving absence, or droplets strike a back that cannot be seen and hang there, outlining a narrow head, high shoulders, and a long descending tail. When the wind hums through the largest fissure, a second tone sometimes answers from below—higher, uncertain, almost imitative.

The chipped flakes near the basin’s edge may belong to this creature, though not necessarily as tools. Some are arranged after storms in small, loose fans, their cutting edges turned toward the downhill opening. Rain scatters them; later they appear ordered again. They could be shaped by water, heat, or a patient pressure exerted from beneath. A few carry traces of translucent skin along their chipped sides. None bears blood.

Deeper still, if the continuous pouring has a listener, it is something rooted into the mountain rather than lodged within it. The springs alter course around a central absence. Tree roots approach that region and turn aside. Resonant drops answer one another across it, making the basin sound larger underground than it appears above. Whatever occupies that hollow announces no hunger and performs no visible act. It may be no creature at all, but the basin’s accumulated retention: all the rain it has delayed, all the hours its cups have borrowed, all the sounds the stone has caught and not yet released.

At dusk, when the warmed fissure ceases humming, the hidden pouring remains. The folded ferns stir. Pale grazers move beneath the moss. Somewhere near a spring, three narrow toes press into mud. For a moment the pools exchange faint reflections—not merely stars, dawn, and evening, but fragments of low green country, unfamiliar clouds, and small dark forms bending over water.

Then the surfaces settle, each returning to its appointed sky.
