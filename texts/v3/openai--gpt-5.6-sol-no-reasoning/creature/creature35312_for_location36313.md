The people who live there are shaped less by altitude than by circulation.

They are climbers, listeners, keepers of gradients. Even those who seldom leave one ward know the city vertically: which stair remains dry in the wet season, which lift admits passengers beside freight, which bridge sways under a west wind, which warm shaft can carry a message six levels upward if shouted at the right hour. Directions are given by flow and machinery rather than compass points. Down from the third settling drum. Above the red flywheel. Across where the fig roots enter the old tram house. Beneath the aqueduct, but before its shadow.

A resident may live within a few hundred paces of a relative and still need half a day to visit. Families therefore arrange themselves along routes. Grandparents keep rooms near reliable lifts. Young couples occupy high galleries reached by ladders and narrow stairs. Children are passed between households according to work shifts, water releases, and the seasonal closing of bridges. Kinship spreads through adjacent elevations like a vine trained along a wall.

Their bodies acquire the habits of the place. Most walk with a slight forward balance and test unfamiliar flooring before trusting it. They have strong calves, scarred palms, and an instinctive reluctance to stand beneath chains, spouts, suspended loads, or patches of ceiling too clean to be old. In narrow passages they turn sideways without interrupting conversation. Near machinery they speak with their faces and hands. Gesture varies by district but certain signs are universal: moving lift, bad air, live current, loose stone, water coming, stop the wheel.

They carry little that cannot be secured. Tools have loops. Cups have lids. Babies are wrapped close against the chest or back. Buttons are favored over dangling ties, and hair is bound tightly in working districts. The soles of shoes are soft enough to feel vibration but thick enough for hot grating and chipped ceramic. Many people own two pairs: one for wet masonry, one for ironwork. Upper residents wear bleached cloth, veils, and mirrored lenses against the glare; below, clothing tends toward dark woven fiber, waxed aprons, fungus-resistant wraps, and bright stitching visible in green shadow.

Nearly everyone can recognize a few failures by sound. A dry bearing chatters. A loaded cable sings. Water trapped behind plaster gives a dull, full note under the knuckle. A healthy pump strikes evenly through the floor; an unhealthy one seems to hesitate before every fourth stroke. Children learn these distinctions with nursery rhymes. Older residents may wake before an alarm because a familiar vibration has stopped.

The city’s most respected inhabitants are not necessarily those who command its largest machines, but those who interpret uncertain conditions.

There are wall-watchers who visit chalk marks twice a day, measuring dampness with paper strips, fingertips, small mirrors, and sometimes caged insects. They do not open anything themselves. Their authority lies in waiting. A good watcher can distinguish condensation from seepage, seepage from pressure, and pressure from the breathing warmth of a sealed room.

Root masons specialize in structures that have become partly alive. They build relieving arches around trunks, loosen parapets before roots can burst them, and decide when a tree is holding a wall together more effectively than the wall holds itself. Their surveys may take weeks. They consult gardeners, drainage clerks, and elderly occupants who remember when the crack first appeared. Cutting is the least admired solution. Bracing, redirecting, grafting, and building around are considered finer work.

Water clerks keep records no outsider could read. Their ledgers combine levels, tastes, algae colors, pipe temperatures, fish behavior, rainfall at distant heights, and the testimony of those who heard water moving behind walls. They seal inspection plates with numbered wire and carry blue enamel tags for municipal animals. Some can identify a cistern by the mineral trace it leaves on a cup.

Lift crews are another people almost unto themselves. They work in teams stationed far apart: one at the platform, one at the brake hall, one with the counterweight, one listening in the shaft. Their calls pass by bell, lamp, taut cord, and coded hammer blows. They are patient by necessity. A person who hurries a lift is regarded as not merely foolish but morally unreliable. Lift crews eat while machinery is moving and become alert in stillness.

Ventilation workers enter districts most people experience only as drafts through a grille. They crawl along warm conduits, descend beside fan housings, and carry ribbons, flame lamps, chalk, and small cages of striped beetles. Their profession has produced a private geography of lunglike chambers and roaring vertical throats. They return powdered with lint, pollen, rust, or pale spores and are made to wash before entering public kitchens.

Then there are the foundry households, whose furnaces supply replacement teeth, bearing shells, chain links, brackets, valves, rails, and plates. Their craft is less invention than translation: an ancient component arrives cracked or worn, perhaps altered by five prior repairs, and they must understand what shape it was trying to be. Patterns are built from wax, wood, packed fiber, clay, or pieces of the original. No two replacement parts are precisely identical. The city works because skilled hands know how much discrepancy a machine can forgive.

Many occupations overlap. The woman who tends citrus on a radiant wall may also inspect gutter locks. The barge pilot may breed larva-eating fish beneath his floor. A schoolteacher might keep the vibration log for an entire residential stack. Children who herd goats on maintenance ledges carry messages to crews, collect dropped fasteners, and report new cracks. Municipal duty is not separate from domestic life; it passes through it.

Food comes from every available climate. Lower wards produce cane shoots, peppers, mushrooms, frogs, and fish. Sunward terraces yield fruit, herbs, eggs, and tough little vegetables grown in ash-rich beds. High conservatories cultivate cooler crops and medicinal plants. Pigeons, goats, chickens, beetle grubs, and tank fish all appear in local diets, though ownership tags are checked carefully before slaughter. Recipes are organized around heat sources: bread baked against furnace walls, fruit dried above dynamo rooms, stews left all day beside warm pipes, custards set in cooling-water channels.

Meals have an architecture. Bowls are placed in shallow wall niches so vibration cannot walk them off tables. Hooks hang overhead for bread, fruit, and utensils. In loud districts families eat close together, knees touching, and conversation proceeds through glances and quick signs. In quieter upper rooms, meals can become extravagantly verbal because speech there feels luxurious.

Water is served with ceremony even when no ceremony is intended. A host names its source—cloud screen, roof catch, deep cistern, aqueduct allotment—and sometimes its treatment. To waste it openly is an insult. To offer cool, clear water from a private reserve is more intimate than offering wine. At weddings, the two households pour cups from their respective districts into one glazed basin; afterward the mixed water is divided among them and carried home.

Children are taught not to fear height so much as to classify it. There is safe height, railed and known. Working height, where one clips a belt. Crumbling height, where nothing should be trusted. Moving height, belonging to lifts and counterweights. Empty height, crossed only by birds, cables, and the eyes.

They play accordingly. They race leaf boats through stair channels after rain and wager where the boats will vanish. They press their ears to walls and guess which machine is running. They learn districts by the taste of runoff and the warmth of handrails. In old halls they build miniature cities from broken tile, complete with aqueducts and drainage laws. A child who lets water fall three toy levels without catching it will be corrected by the others.

Their legends are not about wilderness beyond the city so much as interiors not yet opened.

They tell of warm gardens sealed behind brick, where descendants of forgotten workers still live without knowing the sky. They tell of reservoirs so old that their fish carry stamped brass plates in their scales; of a counterweight that has descended for three hundred years but never reached the bottom; of a sleeping engine whose single revolution would pull half the valley sideways. There are stories of roots reading inscriptions beneath plaster, of monkeys rebuilding cable rigs at night, of swifts carrying the memories of dead engineers into drainage towers.

These stories coexist easily with surveys and maintenance orders. A newly discovered chamber receives a number, a hazard classification, and a name. The number belongs to the authorities. The name belongs to everyone else.

Religion, where practiced, tends toward continuance rather than creation. Shrines occupy landings, pump alcoves, bridgeheads, and dead machine housings. Offerings include clean oil, cups of water, polished bearings, fruit, feathers, and small clay models of valves. Some households honor the engineers named on plaques; others address the countless unnamed repairers who kept their work alive. Bells are treated cautiously because bells already have civic meanings. Devotional sounds are more often humming pipes, rubbed bowl rims, tapped railings, or choirs timed to the slow beat of an engine.

Funerals depend on district and trade. Ash may be added to orchard soil, mortar, casting sand, or filtration beds not used for drinking water. A lift worker’s tools are lowered once through the shaft without him. A gardener may be buried in a deep roof bed beneath a sapling, provided the root survey permits it. The dead are thus not said to leave the city. They enter its materials.

Old age is visible in knowledge rather than retirement. Elders remember obsolete routes, buried floor plans, vanished noises. They know that a nursery was once a loading hall, that a damp wall conceals not a cistern but the tiled roof of a house engulfed by later building, that a certain tremor began after a flood sixty years ago and has never worsened. Crews consult them before opening long-sealed structures. Memory is considered a form of instrumentation, imprecise but irreplaceable.

No central authority wholly governs the place. Administration follows systems. Water has its jurisdictions; lifts have theirs; fire, roots, electrical lines, animals, bridges, and air each belong to overlapping bodies with old rights and grudges. A single wall may answer to six offices. Disputes are settled by ledgers, inspections, precedent, and whoever can demonstrate that failure would fall first upon their district.

Status often follows dependency. Upper wards possess light, cooler air, orchards, and cloud water, but they depend on lower furnaces, deep pumps, and drainage access. Lower wards depend on upper releases and aqueduct allotments. No district is allowed the fantasy of self-sufficiency for long. The city’s politics are arguments over sequence: who receives water first, who bears the counterweight’s passage, whose workshops must stop while a root is surveyed, whose lights dim when a pump is restarted.

Strangers are noticed less by appearance than by timing. They walk during lift bells. They pause under counterweights. They step over a trickle without looking up to find its source. They mistake a maintenance ledge for a street or a roof garden for common ground. Residents correct them abruptly, often by grabbing a sleeve, and explain later.

Hospitality begins with orientation. A guest is shown the nearest drinking tap, alarm route, dry stair, communal latrine, fire bell, and place where the floor is not to be trusted. Only then are they offered food.

The animals, too, are residents in more than a decorative sense.

Blue-tagged goats crop vegetation from spillways and inaccessible ledges. Pigeons wait at message coops and learn the routes of electrical failure. Carp turn in black water beneath grilles, watched by clerks for changes in breath and color. Cable monkeys live beyond formal employment now, but crews still leave them scraps near difficult shafts, hoping they will steal loose objects before those objects can fall. Swifts consume insects over the cistern courts; bats take the night shift; snakes keep to the warm roost walls. Beetles travel between conservatories in ventilated boxes, accompanied by permits.

People speak of these creatures as workers when pleased and pests when angry, much as they speak of one another.

There are also residents no census reliably includes: families in abandoned galleries who draw water from unofficial condensation lines; hermits in carbonate towers; scavengers who map disused conduits; children born into moving barge households on the aqueduct; caretakers of sealed gardens who may spend months below without seeing direct sun. Some live by repairing what official crews have not yet reached. Some recover ceramic, copper, glass, and ancient hardwood from collapsing interiors. Some cultivate unauthorized beds in warm, hidden rooms and sell pale vegetables at dawn markets.

The city tolerates them unevenly. What matters, in the end, is whether their presence obstructs flow.

At night, the inhabitants sleep inside a vast instrument. Bed legs tremble. Water ticks through cooling pipes. Chains pass somewhere behind plaster. The upper towers take wind; the lower halls hold heat; frogs call from pans and cisterns. People wake to changes too subtle for visitors to notice—a fan slowing, a pump missing a stroke, rain beginning many levels above.

For a moment they lie still and listen.

Most sounds mean that countless unseen neighbors, human and otherwise, are awake and performing their part. A gate is being eased open. A bearing is receiving oil. A child is carrying a tag. A barge is entering the aqueduct’s night current. A root is thickening in darkness. Fish are rising in a tank. Water is finding the route prepared for it.

What lives there is not one population inhabiting one construction. It is an assembly of people, animals, plants, machines, fungi, water, mineral, habit, and memory, each maintaining and altering the others.

The inhabitants do not merely dwell in the city.

They are among the processes by which it remains a landscape.
