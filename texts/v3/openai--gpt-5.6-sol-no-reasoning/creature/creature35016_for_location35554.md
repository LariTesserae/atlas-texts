They are called, for lack of a name they recognize, the **interstitials**.

No single body represents them. A hand-sized slate hinge clicking beside a pool, a transparent bladder turning its inward cilia, and a migration of silver needles may appear to be unrelated animals. Examined long enough, however, each proves to be one phase of the same dispersed life. The plain does not contain an ecosystem of separate species so much as a single organism continually dividing itself into predator, prey, offspring, carcass, and terrain.

Its smallest forms arise wherever unlike conditions meet. At the boundary of meltwater and old brine, clear motes precipitate from the difference itself. They have neither stomach nor organ of sense. Their bodies are loops of selective permeability, drinking pressure on one side and salinity on the other. A stable environment starves them; equilibrium is their desert. They prosper in storm fronts, freezing seams, charged rock, and moments when one instant does not quite align with the next.

After feeding, the motes join. Some flatten into pale grazing discs that rasp electrical residue from stone. Some elongate into needle shoals, conducting heat and charge through the pools. Some fold around mineral grains and become hinged black shells. The forms are temporary occupations, assumed according to local need rather than inherited as fixed anatomies. Their molts are not discarded coverings but younger versions of themselves, left behind to continue living.

They reproduce by completing circuits.

When enough bodies align across a basin, current passes from rain to water, from water to animal, and from animal into bedrock. For several seconds the whole assembly functions as one reproductive organ. New creatures then condense along distant cracks—sometimes kilometers away, sometimes yesterday—each bearing structures suited to conditions its parents could not have encountered. This is why the mere produces broods in identical batches. It is not a nursery but the largest intact gonad of the being below.

The subterranean machinery is that being’s deeper anatomy.

Its pistons are pressure chambers opening in spaces larger than the stones that contain them. Its rotors are circular migrations of water through folded depth. Its furnaces are mineral reactions conducted without flame; its conveyors are chains of causation passing through adjacent moments instead of adjacent places. Nothing built these mechanisms because they are not mechanisms in the local sense. Machine and animal are distinctions made by younger worlds. Here, metabolism has worn itself into geology.

The cobalt bands are impulses traveling through this buried nervous system. At their approach, every surface form becomes still so that no incidental movement corrupts the transmission. Rain pauses. Cilia stop. Predators flatten beneath reflections. In that silence, the deep organism compares the present plain with earlier and later versions of itself, then advances one increment toward a condition no individual creature can survive or comprehend.

The great braided hunters are corrective organs. They emerge when some population persists too strongly—when a shape, behavior, or history threatens to become permanent. They do not eat flesh. They remove continuity. What remains loses the sequence that distinguished life from mineral: appetite, motion, ancestry, expectation. The resulting stone is incorporated into the fitted terraces, where it may later weather into basins and give birth again along its wet edges.

Thus the animals flee the predators, but the larger life depends upon them. Terror is one of its maintenance processes.

The interstitials possess awareness, though seldom inside an individual. A shell recoils; a disc follows charge; a bladder distinguishes pressure. Thought occurs only when millions arrange themselves over the flats. Their branching migrations are nerves assembled for an evening and dismantled before the next false dawn. From ground level, such a migration appears to seek warmth. From above, it performs computation. In reflection, where geometry includes time, it becomes language.

The instruction written there is not addressed to an observer. It is addressed to the landscape.

It tells pools where to form, storms which direction to rain, apertures which age to return their travelers to, and the black mere which bodies must be made next. Anyone who learned to read it would become part of its grammar. Their memories would begin recurring as weather. Their pulse would synchronize with the eleven-second hammering ridge. Eventually their outline would appear at several scales in the northern fog: body, basin, horizon. They would not die so much as cease to be singular.

This has happened before. Some rounded blocks contain postures too deliberate to be erosion. Certain pools repeat the proportions of an eye without resembling one. During the migrations, temporary limbs and faces appear in the flowing pavement, formed by thousands of unrelated bodies that could not individually know the shape they make. These may be records of former visitors, or organs grown from the plain’s attempt to imagine them.

The interstitials have no ruler, no center, and no purpose separable from their activity. Yet beneath their feeding, flight, mating, and manufacture lies a tendency: to eliminate emptiness. They fill differences with life, gaps with sediment, sediment with ice, ice with water, water with swarms. They fill distance with repetition and stone with impossible chambers. Even the future is colonized, its consequences arriving early as shadows, ripples, and offspring.

What lives there is therefore not merely the crowded fauna.

It is the crowding itself: an ancient wet intelligence distributed across every boundary, using animals as moving parts and geology as its slow body. The low plain is its exposed skin. The mere is an organ. The fog is growth not yet made solid. The mechanical thunder is its circulation.

And the world being manufactured from worn-out remains is not a new world.

It is the same creature, making more room inside itself by abolishing the distinction between what lives there and where “there” is.
