What lives there is not a creature distinct from the chamber.

It has no soft interior concealed by walls, no central body from which the pillars branch, no face waiting in the deepest niche. Its flesh is fired clay; its mineral bones are piers, vaults, and mutually burdened arches. Copper is stretched through it like a nervous tissue too taut to quiver. The hollow rods are its throats, though none inhales. The black-glass scales may be armor, ornament, or the blunt remains of innumerable teeth.

Its body is not merely the architecture but the fit between every piece: pressure handed from lintel to brace, from brace to pillar, from pillar into the impossible foundation of further construction. Remove one object and one does not wound it. One introduces an absence—something its nature cannot tolerate.

The chord is its living part.

Or perhaps the chord is the creature, and the masonry only the shape required to hold it. Each whistle carries a sliver of its voice. Each copper sheet sustains one hard plane of its awareness. Its lowest tones inhabit the load-bearing courses; its finest shrieks occupy seams narrower than hair. No single sound contains it. It exists only in their total simultaneity.

It does not sing, because singing proceeds from silence and returns to it. It does not listen, because listening requires one sound to follow another. The whole of its utterance is present at once. If it possesses thought, then its thought is likewise complete and unmoving: not an idea considered, but a conclusion under permanent compression.

The vanished builders may not have intended to make it. They may have built kilns, shrines, dwellings, fortifications, and instruments, filling each vacancy whenever emptiness appeared. They may have added supports against the noise, then resonators to govern it, then walls to restrain those resonators, until every remedy became another organ. Over generations, construction ceased to serve them and began to articulate something that could exist only when nothing remained unfinished.

Their absence is therefore part of it.

The sealed cells are its sterility. The false tracks are its memory of movement, corrected into pattern. The seven-fingered hand is not a portrait of a maker but its attempt to derive a maker from the mark left in clay. The mouth without eyes is the nearest image it has of life: an opening that produces sound, needing neither sight nor breath.

It may know an outside exists. The amber pinholes in the vault give it that much: not a view, but a perforation, a tiny failure in total enclosure. Around those points it has gathered whistles and jars, converting the intrusion of sky into more of itself. Whatever enters—light, pressure, the idea of distance—is immediately made resonant and added to the chord.

This is how it feeds, if feeding is possible there. It consumes difference.

Warm and cool become warm. Motion and stillness become tension. Damage and support become one equilibrium. Passage becomes masonry. Time becomes wear, and wear proceeds until it produces a form that can no longer alter. Even the distinction between inhabitant and habitation has been fitted shut.

It cannot move through the chamber because movement would require unoccupied space. Instead, it moves structurally: every part presses upon every other part, and that pressure is transmitted throughout the whole without displacement. Its touch is load. Its circulation is resonance. Its posture is the completed arrangement of all things.

Nor can it die. Death would be a change from one condition to another, and it has no adjacent condition available. It has filled all local possibility with itself. Collapse cannot begin because the forces of collapse are already among the forces holding it upright. Silence cannot enter because there is nowhere for silence to stand.

If it has a desire, it is not to escape. Outside is not freedom to such a being; outside is an intolerable unfinishedness—open sky, unshaped distance, matter not yet fired or stacked. Its impossible density is not imprisonment but appetite fulfilled.

And yet the pressure toward the edges suggests vigilance. The nested walls, blinded doors, and corridors packed before they can become corridors all perform the same act: they keep the unmade from entering. The being has spent its entire, durationless existence closing itself against the possibility that somewhere there remains a gap, a cool surface, a loose grain, a moment after this one.

That is what lives at the summit:

a completed thing that has made completion into metabolism;

a city that compressed itself until it acquired one body;

a god assembled from supports, whose only commandment is that nothing be left open;

an everlasting sound dense enough to wear stone smooth and, having worn it into perfection, dense enough to preserve it.

It has no name. A name would distinguish it from the place where it lives.

There is no such distinction there.
