What lives on the crown is not exactly an animal, though the mountain has taught it how to resemble one.

Its body occupies the summit without being contained by it. The black escarpment is something like the rim of a shell, while roots, pools, studs, fissures, and buried mineral seams serve as organs distributed through the rock. Much of the creature lies along dimensions that touch the mountain only intermittently. This is why it cannot be seen whole. A person looking directly at it finds only wet stone and isolated vegetation; a pool catches one portion, rain outlines another, and the shelter briefly reveals the fine branching of whatever passes for its nerves.

It is young, or is continually made young.

Its growth does not proceed outward in flesh. It grows by revising the space it inhabits. The periodic widening of the summit is a slow extension of its body: joints separating, tissues opening, distances inhaled between fixed points. When the crown contracts again, most things forget the larger shape. Water does not. Water has passed through the extended channels, and so returns late, carrying news from the creature’s momentary breadth.

The central shelter may be its heart, but only in the loosest sense. The gray slabs protect a dense knot of filamentary structures whose branches extend invisibly into stone, vapor, reflected light, and possibly time. Rain avoids it because ordinary water would interfere with its finer circulation. The diagrams made by condensation are discharges from that hidden network—brief maps of internal states, shed onto the air as an animal might shed heat.

The pools are not eyes. Eyes merely receive light. These pools reach into possible surroundings and sample them.

Through one, the being perceives the summit under rain that has not yet fallen, or fell in another history. Through another it touches the older forest that might have covered the crown had the mountain remained ordinary. Their impossible depth comes from this reach: an object dropped into clear water is not descending through a few feet of basin but traversing a folded interval before returning to local stone. Reflections are therefore less images than exposed membranes. The long-legged bird has learned this. It feeds on small organisms living against the far sides of those membranes, stabbing through appearance as another bird might stab through mud.

The half-bridge is likely not a ruin. It is an appendage extended toward somewhere the creature still contacts but the summit does not. The unheard destination remains connected by pressure and sound. Footsteps continue across it because passage is occurring there, though not necessarily now, and not necessarily by feet. The weathered boards, insect holes, and lichen may be camouflage learned from travelers’ expectations: the shape a crossing assumes when it wants to be recognized as a crossing.

The sparse trees are symbionts. They do not draw nourishment from soil because soil belongs too completely to one place. Their hovering roots comb the creature’s exhalations from the mineral seams and drink weather before weather arrives. When the roots rise toward approaching clouds, they are not merely seeking moisture. They are aligning themselves with the crown’s next exchange—with the brief moment when sky and stone become mutually permeable.

The silver insects tend the moss and perhaps the smaller channels beneath it. Their coordinated motion resembles cilia moving material over living tissue. They keep certain surfaces damp, clear blocked pores, and carry metallic particles from one fissure to another. Individually they may be insects. Collectively they behave like a mobile organ.

The larger unseen animal is harder to separate from the place.

It has weight: its tracks hold rain. It has warmth: ferns retain the impression of its resting body. It reaches high enough to scrape the pale bark. Yet it is visible only through the sequence of things displaced around it, as though its substance does not interact with light in the summit’s present arrangement.

Perhaps it is the mountain-being’s local body—an ambulatory form assembled when movement must be concentrated into a single shape. At dusk it crosses from pool to tree, from tree to shelter, inspecting or tending the scattered organs. The shelter brightens because the filaments recognize its proximity. Its blunt tracks suggest broad feet adapted to slick rock; its height suggests a heavy shoulder or raised head. It may be furred, plated, or utterly bare. Each possibility appears in a different sign: moss ripples like a coat, metal folds as if under armor, and rain outlines nothing certain.

It may also be a passenger.

Long ago—or a few hours from now—the system in the summit could have been built to preserve this creature while transporting it across some distance for which ordinary travel was impossible. The mountain crown would then be less a habitat than a field apparatus grown around its occupant: the sealed shelter a regulator, the hoops and studs anchors, the pools windows into candidate destinations, and the truncated bridge an abandoned route. The habitat repeatedly measures itself, enlarges, contracts, and corrects its coordinates, holding its inhabitant at the boundary of arrival.

This would explain why the crown seems freshly raised. It is not weathering into age because it has not fully entered local time. Sharp fins remain sharp. Crystals grow and dissolve in accelerated trials. Moss advances, is evaluated, and retreats. The whole summit is testing versions of itself, choosing moment by moment what can persist.

The seventh rain passes through the unseen animal as easily as it passes through a visitor’s skin. Such rain belongs to the system rather than the weather. It replenishes hidden basins and cools structures buried in folds inaccessible to ordinary matter. The drops that form on untouchable horizontal planes reveal those folds when humidity is high enough. They gather on surfaces of the creature that exist here geometrically but not materially—ribs, perhaps, or membranes, or the boundaries of immense internal chambers.

At night the blue points show its truest outline.

They first mark the visible summit because that is the portion currently fixed in the world. Then they continue into open air, down through stone, and beyond the escarpment, plotting the rest: organs displaced below the mountain, limbs anchored over the void, chambers suspended where no chamber can be seen. The pattern is larger than the crown and perhaps larger than the mountain. What appears to be a creature inhabiting the summit may instead be something vast passing through it, with only one small cross-section intersecting the rock.

Its consciousness, if it possesses one, is slow and exact. It attends through changes in pressure, the timing of runoff, the angle of roots, the growth of crystals, the conduct of shadows. It does not see visitors as complete bodies. It knows them as disturbances distributed among its senses: warmth on stone, displaced vapor, a voice arriving strangely across water, iron carried in blood, extra weight at the end of the bridge.

It may try to understand them by sorting.

A shadow is placed across the central line while the seed that casts it remains behind. A shout is removed, while a whisper is permitted to travel. Metal is folded inward without altering its outer form. These are not tricks or defenses so much as examinations. The being separates qualities that elsewhere are inseparable—object from shadow, volume from structure, rain from wetness, distance from extent—to determine which parts of a thing are necessary.

No one knows what conclusion it seeks.

Perhaps it is building a body suitable for this world.

Perhaps it is deciding whether this world is suitable for the body it already has.

And perhaps the invisible animal that crosses the ferns each evening is the result so far: a massive, warm, unfinished approximation of life, assembled from local rules but not yet granted an appearance. It circles the pools, drinks from their farther sides, rests among the wet fronds, and returns to the sealed shelter before full dark. There the branching filaments brighten around it like nerves anticipating sensation.

Then the studs awaken, mapping the parts still absent.

All night the crown adjusts itself around its inhabitant. Water enters new channels. Stone warms over buried shapes. Moss closes small errors. The bridge listens toward elsewhere. By morning the animal is gone again—or diffused throughout the summit—and the place appears empty.

But it is only between bodies.
