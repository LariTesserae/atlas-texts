The inhabitants of the Still Below do not so much occupy their country as continue it.

A claspet at rest is a handful of angular stones arranged around a seam. Its mineral plates inherit the color and fracture pattern of the ground beneath it, while its limbs narrow into hooked iron combs that penetrate microscopic faults. Young claspets have six limbs. With age, the thorax splits without fully dividing, and four more unfold from beneath the original armor. The discarded inner plates are not shed; they remain hinged to the body, making the elder resemble several smaller creatures crushed together.

Their lives are measured in adjustments. A seam weakens. A foot loosens and searches a finger’s breadth to one side. The body contracts. Current passes through tissues so slowly that a pulse may take hours to cross from one limb to another. Colonies are therefore also circuits: millions of claspets braced across branching fractures, borrowing and returning charges among themselves. When alarm passes through such a colony, it does not produce flight. It produces posture. Across miles, every joint tightens in sequence, and the plain makes a sound like cooling metal.

Ceiling grazers inhabit the boundaries between gases. Their broad bodies are supported partly by hooked limbs and partly by buoyant chambers distributed through their flesh. They are softest at the center and increasingly brittle toward the edges, where layers of translucent hooks sieve charged dust from the upper air. Their mouths are not openings but surfaces—dark, folded fields into which flakes settle and dissolve.

A grazer herd maintains its spacing by heat. Each animal senses the warmth of its neighbors and makes infinitesimal corrections by venting or manufacturing buoyant gas. Too close, it sinks. Too far, it rises. Thus entire ceilings are tiled with them, a living mosaic suspended in equilibrium. When frightened, they sometimes vent together. The resulting descent can take days. Their bodies come down without falling, turning slowly through gas layers that distort their shapes, until they settle over a plain like warm, overlapping shields. For centuries afterward, the roof above them may remain bare.

The column beasts are less readily understood.

A visible column may be only one segment of an animal extending through many strata. The ceramic rings of its body overlap downward, each one inscribed by growth with shallow concentric ridges. Between the rings lies a dark, contractile tissue that appears dry but yields slightly under pressure. Its feet—or roots, or lower jaws—enter fractures too narrow for examination. Its upper parts vanish into vaults where the gas cannot be breathed.

Small creatures nest between its rings. Tool-users drive pegs into old scars and suspend dwellings there. Yet no one can agree whether a column beast notices these inhabitants. Sometimes a ring closes over a peg during the course of several generations. Sometimes a settlement wakes to find its supporting column subtly curved, though every point where it touches the floor remains unchanged. The oldest traditions say the circular marks on the glass are not footprints but places where column beasts once stood in shapes the world has forgotten.

The crack predators are called pallids, though whiteness has little meaning where there is almost no light. Their bodies consist of mouths folded around mouths. When compressed, their soft jaws lie in nested sheets, and the whole animal can inhabit the width of a mineral flaw. They perceive damage: the abrupt electrical disorder of a broken limb, a fractured shell, a tool-user frightened into rapid nerve activity. Then the nearest pallid begins to unfold.

It may take hours to emerge. This is still considered swift.

A pallid does not bite so much as cover. Its mouths settle over the victim in layers and draw away the organized charge by which the body moves and senses itself. The drained animal remains warm, breathing if it breathed before, often standing in the posture in which it was found. Some wake years later. Some awaken only in separate parts: one hand able to signal, one cluster of eyes aware, the rest of the body still beyond reach. Settlements preserve such victims and address them patiently. A reply may arrive generations after the question.

Other animals are recognized only when they cease resembling terrain.

The rounded stones of the clicking fields are shell-broods. Each contains a ribbonlike creature coiled around a pocket of pressurized gas. A footstep compresses the shells, and the inhabitants answer by touching internal struts together. These clicks announce mass, direction, and rhythm to the entire field. If the pattern suggests a wounded creature, some broods open along invisible seams and crawl toward it, dragging their hollow shells behind them. If it suggests a pallid, they contract so violently that acres of stone seem to vanish into silence.

The needle fields are inhabited too. Their translucent blades share a deep, leathery foundation and may be the sensory organs of a single buried animal. They consume echoes. Sound entering them travels downward and does not return, though years later a nearby needle may tremble with the same vibration. The tool-users harvest only broken blades. Cutting a standing needle risks waking a response elsewhere in the field—sometimes hundreds of miles away.

Even the compacted ash shelves contain long, segmented things that move by passing grains through their bodies and returning them to almost the same positions. Their passage cannot be seen from one lifetime to the next. They are known from interruptions: tools left on a shelf are occasionally found inside it, while objects once buried emerge cleanly on top. These creatures may account for certain stairs that descend through solid ash without ever filling.

The people of the Still Below call themselves by a touch-pattern pressed against another’s inner wrist. Rendered crudely into sound, it would mean something like **those who leave room**.

They are low-bodied and dense, most standing no higher than the length of a tool haft. Six primary limbs radiate from a plated trunk. Four serve interchangeably as hands or feet; the remaining pair are finer and are kept folded near the mouth, protected from accidental noise. Around these delicate limbs cluster organs for touch, vibration, charge, heat, and the chemical reading of gas. Their eyes are small and useful chiefly for detecting red luminescence or the violet wake of pressure on certain mineral floors.

Their bones are ceramic lattices rather than solid rods. Their muscles draw slowly but can hold tension for years. A resting person hooks several limbs into cracks and becomes difficult to distinguish from an arrangement of old armor. Their breath is shallow and infrequent. In dense gas they seal their respiratory folds and carry trapped pockets of a lighter mixture beneath the plates of the chest.

They molt only after death.

As the living tissues dry, the outer plates separate along predetermined lines. Relatives remove, clean, and fit them over themselves. This is neither disguise nor simple mourning. A plate carries posture: minute stresses laid into it by centuries of movement. Worn against a descendant’s body, it subtly resists some gestures and encourages others. Thus the dead continue as habits. A patient ancestor’s hand-plate steadies a hurried child. The chest armor of an explorer pulls slightly toward the direction in which its first wearer traveled most often.

Because material change is scarce, identity lies less in possession than in sequence. A tool may belong to the person currently performing the gesture for which it is remembered. A dwelling belongs to those who can produce its proper returning echo. Names consist of tap patterns, arrangements of stones, scars deliberately cut into plates, and inherited postures. It is possible for several people to share one name across thousands of years without considering themselves the same being. It is also possible for one person to acquire many names merely by moving a familiar object into an unprecedented use.

Children are exceedingly rare. They begin as flattened buds beneath an adult’s inner armor and may remain there for decades, listening to the parent’s body and the settlement’s echoes. When they detach, they possess four limbs and a soft, unpatterned shell. Additional hands develop only through use. A child encouraged to climb grows grasping limbs; one trained to tend resonant walls grows broad tapping pads; one raised among guide cords develops hooks fine enough to distinguish knots by vibration alone.

Their earliest lesson is stillness, but not obedience. A child is taught to separate the quiet’s inhabitants: the near click of a claspet, the delayed answer of a distant wall, the false intimacy caused by crossing gas, the soft advance of a pallid. Only after this are they taught to signal. Their first deliberate statement is usually a stone placed in a groove. It may be left there until adulthood.

A settlement is a society of acoustical obligations. One household maintains a wall that carries warnings west. Another keeps a floor plate tuned to the deep paths. Another tends captive claspets arranged in ceramic frames, using their minute discharge for heat-work, medical stimulation, and the awakening of long-dormant tools. Leadership belongs not to whoever commands but to whoever can predict consequences furthest through remembered space.

For this reason, builders hold unusual authority and inspire unease. They are called before any permanent alteration: the moving of a stone, the cutting of a stair, the burial of a body. A builder spends years mapping the proposed change through taps and weighted cords. Models are assembled from ancestral shell. Witnesses stand at distant listening stations to report how the chamber’s replies have shifted. If the change is accepted, a final arrangement of voting stones is set into a groove. Those stones may remain long after the structure itself has been abandoned, because the decision is considered part of what was built.

Some specialize in the remnants of vanished construction. These listeners enter ruined chambers tethered to guide lines and tap with padded hammers until the remembered walls reveal themselves. They mark intangible corners with upright bones. They identify doors that no longer exist and close them ceremonially so animals will cease attempting to pass through. The work is dangerous. A listener can become enclosed by an earlier version of a room, visible to companions but unable to cross a boundary that has no present substance.

Those rescued from such places are often altered. They may hear settlements as they sounded before birth. They may respond to conversations laid out centuries ago. Some insist that all arrangements still coexist and that ordinary life is merely the narrow set through which bodies are permitted to move.

Travelers form another distinct kind of inhabitant. They wear shell plates loosely so each one can be detached and left as a marker. They carry cords braided from pallid tissue, which retains a faint sensation of contact at both ends. Their maps are not representations of distance but inventories of return: strike here and receive three answers; cross one gas boundary; pass the warm shape left by a fallen grazer; turn when the guide cord trembles without being touched.

At the margin of the Great Absence, these methods fail.

The upright creatures within it are called the Attentive, though no one knows whether they are animals, people, or one organism expressed millions of times. Each is narrow, sealed, and faceless. Their shells swallow vibration so completely that touching one feels like touching a hole in the world. All face the same direction—not toward any known landmark, but across the plain at an angle that does not vary.

Occasionally an Attentive is found empty. Its shell remains upright while something has departed without making tracks. Such shells are never taken. Settlements build low warning circles around them, careful not to touch either shell or shadow.

A few travelers have gone beyond the final knot of the guide cord. Most did not return. One emerged generations later from a different edge of the Great Absence, though no edge was believed to exist there. Its armor was polished, its tapping limbs worn smooth, and between its plates were flakes identical to those composing the black towers. It would answer only by arranging stones in parallel lines, all pointing the same way as the Attentive.

When it died, its descendants found no body beneath the plates—only another, much smaller set of armor facing inward.

The black towers attract claspets but repel pallids. Their stacked flakes cannot be separated. A person can slip a blade between them and feel the blade continue downward farther than its own length, yet withdraw it unchanged. Thermal sense reveals the impossible descending shadow, cold-edged against the universal warmth. Some tower keepers believe the visible structure is not an object but the topmost recurrence of an endless object passing through layer after layer of the world.

They hold that the towers and the column beasts are related: one made by hands, one by life, both connecting ceilings that appear separate only to creatures forced to inhabit a single depth.

Others believe the towers are taps made solid.

According to them, something below the lowest reachable stratum once struck upward. The impact traveled through the world and, because sound does not always finish here, remains standing in these precisely stacked forms. The inverted towers uncovered beneath are not buried copies but earlier portions of the same message.

The deepest resonances support neither interpretation. They arrive too slowly to study. A settlement may strike a sounding wall, appoint descendants to listen, and receive an answer nine hundred years later. Such answers are lower than hearing. They are felt through planted feet and inherited armor. They cause ceiling grazers to loosen their hooks. Claspets turn by fractions toward the source. Column beasts tighten inside their rings.

And throughout the Great Absence, the Attentive seem—perhaps—to incline.

What lives beneath the final ceilings is known only by these reactions. It may be a creature broad enough that chambers, plains, and strata rest within the articulations of its shell. It may be the ancestral body from which all mineral-armored life descended. It may be dead, with the Still Below occupying the unrotting spaces inside it.

Or it may simply be slow.

The tool-users have a gesture for this possibility: all hands laid flat against the ground, then lifted together by the smallest distance they can manage.

They perform it rarely.

For if the foundation is alive, the inhabitants of the Still Below are not waiting for it to wake. Waking is too brief and simple a notion.

They are living through the earliest, almost imperceptible part of its decision to move.
