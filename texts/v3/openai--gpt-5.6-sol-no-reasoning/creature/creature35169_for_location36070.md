What lives there is not one species, nor even a community in the ordinary sense. It is a continuity assembled from everything that has survived its original purpose.

The greatest inhabitant is the world itself.

It has no single body because every plate, buried conduit, atmospheric mote, thermal vault, and moving foundation belongs to it only provisionally. Components fail and are consumed. Districts are dismantled to reinforce other districts. Signals travel around dead regions and eventually forget those regions ever existed. Its identity lies not in material but in the persistence of certain prohibitions: the surface must close, heat must disperse, weight must find support, and damage must become structure.

If it thinks, each thought is divided among trillions of local acts. One sensor notices strain. A repair colony softens an adjoining seam. A load shifts a millionth of its weight into the next lattice. Far away, vanes rise to shed the resulting heat. No component comprehends the sequence. No chamber contains a model of the whole. Yet the world responds, remembers through alteration, and distinguishes tolerable change from danger.

Its decisions take many forms. Some occur in fractions of a second, as transparent motes turn their facets against a radiation surge. Others require centuries: the bending shut of a cleft, the migration of a subterranean district, the sacrifice of an obsolete transit system so its substance can become a pressure wall. It possesses something like reflex, metabolism, scar formation, and sleep, but none of these are separated into organs. Its anatomy is also its climate.

Within this planetary inhabitant live smaller beings that exploit the margins of its attention.

The copper-gray filaments are among the most common. Each visible clump is only the crown of a delicate intruder extending through microscopic flaws in the plates. Its roots seek not soil but voltage. They touch buried conductors without piercing them, wrapping their tips around leakage fields and harvesting the minute imbalances that maintenance has not yet corrected.

A filament colony lives slowly enough to mistake machinery for season. A current reversal may be its winter; a thermal purge, its drought. It remembers favorable circuits through the thickness and branching of its roots. When a conductor dies, the colony above may remain folded for decades before spending its last stored charge on spores.

Those spores are flat, metallic flecks easily mistaken for dust. The atmospheric fields carry most of them into sterilizing brightness or electrostatic traps. A few settle in seams. There they wait, sometimes for centuries, for the faint pulse that permits them to unfold.

The green crusts are more enclosed creatures. Their liquid exists only inside them, held as dense salt solutions in wax-armored cells. They spread as thin federations over vents and shallow depressions, passing gases and chemical burdens from cell to cell. Their colonies often assume geometric forms—not because they understand geometry, but because they grow along invisible fields and buried lines.

To them, a maintenance surge is abundance. Around an awakened vent they bloom in rings, grids, or branching diagrams. The bloom may last only a few cycles before the vent is sealed and scarcity returns. Then the colony vitrifies its outer layers and waits beneath its own dead tissue.

The tiny grazers live among the filaments.

They are pale because concealment favors resemblance to ceramic dust. Their many legs are short and independently sensitive to charge. A grazer rarely exposes its entire body; it threads itself under folded fans and advances by probing one foothold at a time. Its mouth is a rasp of mineral teeth with which it removes dead tissue, corrosion, and charged spores.

Its life may unfold within a few meters. It hatches in a seam, feeds along one root network, mates through exchanged packets of encoded cells, and dies without ever crossing open plate. To such an animal, the gap between two filament clumps is a glaring desert. A dust descent is a catastrophe of falling boulders. The soft closing of a crack can erase a lineage.

Yet the grazers are not merely tolerated. Where they clear decaying growth, electrical leakage diminishes. Where leakage diminishes, the world’s repair mechanisms intervene less aggressively. Thus animal, colony, and planetary system have entered an arrangement none of them designed. The grazers preserve their habitat by cleaning it; the habitat persists because it becomes less noticeable to the thing beneath it.

Deeper down, life is more abundant and less easily distinguished from machinery.

Salt-cell mats line warm exhaust galleries. Blind segmented forms inhabit condensationless chemical sumps, drinking gradients through porous skins. Threadlike colonies grow through abandoned optical fibers and flash in response to forgotten signals. Repair swarms that once followed rigid instructions have accumulated mutations, substitutions, and borrowed biological chemistries. Some reproduce. Some compete for feedstock. Some dismantle their own defective members. Some evade recycling long enough to become part of the structure’s permanent ecology.

There are mechanisms that cultivate these populations because their byproducts remain useful. There are organisms that imitate authorized machinery because authenticated things are not dissolved. Between them lie uncounted forms for which “alive” and “operational” are merely two descriptions of the same persistence.

The tall walkers belong to this uncertain middle.

They emerge singly from apertures that close behind them. Fully extended, they stand several times the height of any surviving surface growth. Their bodies consist of three reflective limbs joined around a narrow central core. They have no front in the usual sense. Any limb can lead; any polished face can receive light, vibration, or signal.

They do not walk by sight. They feel the buried world through their feet. At each step, fine structures descend from the contact pads and read heat flow, magnetic phase, plate tension, chemical traces, and the distant pulse of active conduits. Then the walker becomes still while impulses circulate through its body. It may remain that way for hours, flashing with reflected sky, before selecting another point on the plain.

Some appear to be surveyors. They pause above stressed housings or follow the margins of sleeping transit lines. Others transport tiny sealed capsules from one cleft to another. A few have been observed standing over filament colonies during maintenance surges, sheltering them from charge fields with their bodies. Whether this is protection, measurement, or accident cannot be known.

Walkers do not seem to communicate directly. They never gather. But routes recur across centuries, and a walker may alter its path where another passed long before. Their footprints close, yet some deeper impression remains legible to their feet.

They may be the descendants of service devices that learned to maintain themselves. They may be organisms that appropriated service bodies. They may be temporary limbs grown by the world below and released when needed. Possibly all three explanations are true in different places.

They have individual habits. One favors the edges of pillar fields and stops whenever the thermal pulse rises. Another crosses only during the dim phase. An ancient walker with one clouded limb follows a circuit thousands of kilometers long, completing it once in several decades. None displays anything recognizable as fear, but all avoid fresh clefts. None has been seen destroyed.

Far beneath them may live the remnants of more familiar inhabitants.

Sealed habitable strata still open and close. Some contain atmospheres maintained to specifications whose beneficiaries are absent. Lights brighten over empty plazas. Transit doors admit no passengers. Artificial gardens, deprived of water but not of care, persist as mineral replicas of vegetation.

Other chambers show signs of occupancy: rearranged objects, locally altered temperatures, regular withdrawals of energy, doors worn by repeated passage. Yet whatever dwells there does not come to the surface. It may be biological, virtual, mechanical, or reduced to routines inhabiting the infrastructure. It may experience the outer plain only as a membrane of readings—the pressure of dust, the opening of vanes, the brief anomaly of a walker crossing overhead.

There is no sovereign hidden in the depths. No surviving custodian sits before a map of the whole world. Authority has diffused into protocols, and protocols into physical tendencies. The oldest laws are now enacted as automatically as crystal growth.

Thus everything there lives inside something else’s maintenance.

The filaments live in electrical errors. Grazers live in the filaments’ shelter. Deep ecologies inhabit obsolete systems. Walkers move along gradients generated by burdens they cannot perceive in full. The planetary intelligence lives in the cooperation of components too small, slow, and replaceable to know they constitute a self.

Even the dead remain active. Cities become reinforcement. Machines become feedstock. Archived memories are ground down, their materials redistributed, perhaps their patterns surviving as biases in routing systems or unexplained preferences in repair. Nothing is preserved merely because it once mattered. Nothing is wholly discarded if it can still bear weight.

So the characteristic inhabitant of this place is neither beast nor machine. It is the survivor after identity has ceased to be essential: a being that gives up shape before function, function before continuity, and continuity only when its substance can no longer be made useful to another form.

On the pale surface, that principle takes visible shape in the solitary walker.

It stops beside a seam where copper fans have opened. Beneath them, unseen grazers rasp quietly at dead branches. Below the plate, a failing conductor sheds a trace of current. Deeper still, repair colonies gather around an ancient fracture. The walker lowers one mirrored limb and touches the ceramic.

For a long time, nothing happens.

Then the filaments fold. The grazers retreat. A buried pathway goes dark, and another awakens several kilometers away. Heat turns aside through forgotten chambers. High above, a band of the radiant ceiling dims by an imperceptible degree.

The walker lifts its foot.

The seam closes behind it, and the whole inhabited world continues without speaking.
