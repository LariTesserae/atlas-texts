The people who live here have acquired the plateau’s habit of dispersion.

They are not numerous enough to crowd the city built for them, and perhaps they never intended to be. A district may contain tens of thousands of residents and still feel like an outpost: a few inhabited towers, broad communal halls, workshops, schools, winter gardens, and then kilometers of wet stone. They recognize one another less by face than by gait, outerwear, and destination. Across such open ground, a person becomes identifiable at a great distance.

Outside, they dress in quiet layers of gray, rust, lichen red, and marsh green. Their coats shed rain, regulate heat, and stiffen subtly against wind. Hoods are deep but acoustically transparent, preserving the distant sounds on which people rely: approaching transit, cracking ice, the alarm calls of birds. Soles adapt to slick basalt without biting into the fragile moss. Bright colors are generally reserved for survey crews, children, and those crossing active freight terraces.

Their movements are measured. They do not hurry casually over wet ground, and they rarely shout. Children learn early how far sound carries, how thin ice announces itself, how runoff alters footing, and how to stand still when a herd passes between buildings. Silence is not solemnity here. It is courtesy, useful information, and shared space.

Indoors, they are warmer than the city first appears. Residential towers contain sheltered courts with damp soil, dwarf trees, herbs, and shallow circulating water. Kitchens and laundries open onto these common interiors, so domestic life gathers around humidity, scent, and soft conversation. Apartments tend to be compact; the true living rooms are shared conservatories, bath halls, repair tables, reading galleries, and kitchens large enough for a floor of residents. Privacy is easy in a thin city. Company must be designed for.

Many inhabitants work at tasks that elsewhere would be called maintenance, though here maintenance includes negotiation with things that will not remain fixed. Ground readers monitor the plateau’s lift and strain. Hydrologists follow streams that may abandon one basin for another. Structural migrators guide buildings through adjustments too slow to see. Weather stewards manage heat and vapor so that one district does not unintentionally send ice or flood toward another. Ecologists coordinate nesting recesses, quiet periods, and migration corridors with transit schedules.

They do not speak of controlling these systems. Their preferred verbs are *guide*, *receive*, *ease*, *follow*, and *make room*.

A foundation specialist may spend a career ensuring that a tower can move six meters east over the next century. A drainage keeper may tend a channel expected to exist for only twelve years. Engineers often design the disappearance of things: roads that separate cleanly into reusable material, reservoir edges that dissolve as the ground tilts, empty hangars that become wetlands without leaving toxins or traps. Completion is regarded with some suspicion. Adaptability is the more serious achievement.

Because much of the city operates without visible machinery, skilled residents cultivate an attention that can look almost superstitious to visitors. They notice when water beads differently on a wall, when birds avoid a frame, when the hum beneath a freight terrace has shifted half a tone. Before instruments confirm a fault, someone has often reported that a railing felt too still or that the moss along a utility tower had darkened overnight. Technical literacy and field intuition are not separate traditions. The best workers are trained in both.

The transit frames shape their sense of geography. Distance is not measured solely in kilometers but in crossings, weather differences, and the reliability of a route. A friend may live four hundred kilometers away and still arrive for breakfast, while a marsh settlement visible across twenty kilometers of sleet may feel remote for a week. People check destination weather before stepping beneath a frame. Public vestibules hold spare coats, dry socks, sun veils, and cold-weather masks for travelers who emerge dressed for the wrong district.

They retain a bodily caution around transit despite its ordinariness. Conversations pause when a frame opens. Nearby workers lift their heads. Birds fall silent, puddles ring, and someone disappears from one horizon to enter another. There is no spectacle, yet few residents pass through without a brief tightening of attention. Children dare one another to stand barefoot in shallow water during activation and feel the concentric tremor through their soles.

The city’s scattered form has produced scattered households. Families often occupy several districts, choosing residence according to work, season, schooling, or migration cycles. Some move south with the grazing herds, not from necessity but because their professions and affections have become entangled with them. Others spend winters in the sunlit upper stories of inversion-bound towers and summers in low marsh stations. A household is defined less by a single dwelling than by a set of rooms, access rights, shared obligations, and recurring arrivals.

Births, deaths, and partnerships are recorded not only by date but by weather and ground state. Someone was born during the eastward rain, when the north basin first overflowed. Someone died in the year the old reservoir tilted. Two people joined households beneath the first return of the black waterfowl. This is partly poetry, partly practical chronology. The plateau supplies events that everyone remembers.

Their ceremonies are rarely monumental. At funerals, bodies may be reduced to mineral and incorporated into removable ceramic markers, garden substrates, or small dark stones kept by several households. Nothing is buried deeply without reason; the ground is young, wet, and in motion. Memorials are often routes rather than objects—a sequence of paths, wetlands, rooms, and views associated with a person. To remember someone may mean walking their circuit in the weather they preferred.

Food comes from enclosed gardens, fungal vats, cold-water cultivation, and imports carried through the frames. But the residents remain attentive to local seasons. Eggs and wild meat are taken sparingly under ecological quotas. Sedge-fed herd animals are sometimes harvested, though many are tracked across generations and treated less as stock than as neighboring populations. Reservoir fish appear at communal tables during brief managed runs. Strong flavors are common: smoke, fermented roots, bitter greens, mineral salts, dark broths, sour berries from sheltered hollows.

The plateau’s birds occupy a large part of the civic imagination. Arrival counts appear in public forecasts alongside rainfall and transit delays. Schools suspend lessons when exceptional migrations pass overhead, sending students to roofs and marsh galleries with lenses and acoustic recorders. Districts argue over dimming schedules during nesting season. Maintenance teams keep records of particular breeding pairs. A predator nesting on a pylon can delay structural work for months, and this is accepted with irritation but little surprise.

Not everyone is patient with such accommodations. There are residents who want denser districts, straighter roads, firmer weather control, and barriers against animals in inhabited spaces. Some consider the city’s restraint an inherited aesthetic masquerading as ethics. Others believe the plateau should remain even emptier and that every new structure is a failure of imagination. Political disputes concern thresholds: how much warmth may be released, how much marsh may be redirected, when a migrating district becomes cheaper to abandon than move, whether human convenience justifies fixing one stream in place.

These arguments tend to be long because consequences unfold slowly. Public decisions are modeled across decades or centuries, and every proposal carries maps of anticipated movement. Children are shown where their district may stand when they are old. Adults vote on projects they will not live to see completed. This has not made them selfless, but it has made short-term certainty difficult to defend.

The oldest residents remember when certain moss skirts did not exist, when a station now surrounded by marsh stood on dry stone, when the western frames opened only twice a day. Their memories are treated as data, though not infallible data. Oral histories are indexed against sensor records. A grandmother’s recollection that rain once sounded sharper on a roof may lead to an examination of changing droplet size, atmospheric particulates, or the roof’s slow alteration under mineral deposition.

Newcomers often find the city lonely. Its great spaces can make human presence seem incidental, and its social customs presume a tolerance for quiet. There are programs devoted to teaching arrival: how to read the path lights, request company without embarrassment, enter communal rooms, recognize dangerous weather, and understand that an empty station may still be fully operational. Most districts maintain hosts whose principal work is to notice isolation.

Those born here are not immune to it. They simply distinguish between kinds of solitude. There is chosen distance, weather-bound separation, working silence, grief, and the more dangerous condition of becoming socially invisible. Buildings monitor occupancy but are prohibited from simulating companionship without consent. Instead, they make gentle opportunities: a garden warmed at the hour several residents usually pass, a repair bench illuminated, a shared meal announced without obligation.

The people themselves are physically varied, shaped more by medicine and personal choice than by the climate. Cold adaptation is often technological rather than biological. Some have altered circulation, respiratory filtering, or low-light vision for outdoor work; others reject modifications and depend on clothing. Long residence does leave subtler marks. Their balance is excellent. They walk without looking down yet seldom slip. They pause automatically before crossing runoff. Many can orient in fog by wind, sound, and the slight gradient of the plateau.

Their faces outdoors are composed, eyes narrowed against rain, but indoors they are expressive and close. Humor tends toward understatement. A district moving several centimeters unexpectedly may be described as “restless.” A three-day storm is “passing through.” When an immense herd blocks a transit approach, the official notice may read: **LOCAL PRIORITY IN EFFECT**.

Children are less restrained. They chase path lights, float miniature survey instruments in new streams, imitate wader calls, and build temporary cities from warm basalt tiles. Their structures are praised not for standing longest but for coming apart cleanly. In school, they learn geology beside civics, hydrology beside history. They are taught that a map describes an intention as much as a place.

At night, the inhabitants become visible as scattered signs: a figure crossing an amber-lit path, silhouettes in elevated gardens, a maintenance crew kneeling beside a puddle that trembles before a distant frame opens. Through pale windows, people cook, argue, mend clothing, study tomorrow’s cloud routes, and watch herds gather below.

They have built an immense city without requiring it to look occupied. Their presence lies less in what rises above the plateau than in what quietly adjusts: a warmed nesting recess, a road curving around new water, a tower shifting its weight as the bedrock lifts, a door opening before a rain-soaked traveler reaches it.

They live there provisionally, but not timidly. They belong by remaining movable.
