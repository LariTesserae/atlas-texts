The things that live there are the ones capable of mistaking repetition for survival.

They inhabit the margin.

Not the basalt, which refuses every footprint and burrow, and not the open water, where life is abundant but held inside its appointed motions. They occupy the underside of the sea: that immaculate plane where pressure, hunger, gravity, and time meet without resolving.

From the mountains they resemble stains moving across blue-black glass.

They are called many things in the surviving industrial labels—*interface fouling*, *mobile condensate*, *boundary workers*—but the stamped diagrams show them only as clusters of short lines beneath the horizontal emblem. No name appears to have been intended for speech.

Their bodies are squat and dark, armored against cold on one side and crushing water on the other. Six or eight limbs radiate from a pressure-swollen torso, though the number is difficult to settle because a limb hidden behind another may remain hidden indefinitely. Each ends in a hook broad enough to catch the ocean’s underside without puncturing it. Between the hooks are combs of translucent hairs. With these they rake the boundary, harvesting condensation, grease, electrical dust, scales, sloughed tissue, and the infinitesimal mineral residue brought up around the submerged peaks.

Their mouths face upward.

They feed through the water without entering it. A circular jaw presses flush against the plane and unfolds on the other side, into the sea, like a flower opening through a mirror. Fish veer close but never quite within reach. The scavengers do not pursue them. Instead they graze on what the fixed migrations continually provide: a torn fin that is always being torn, a cloud of eggs that is always dispersing, the blood of a hunted animal that never weakens.

Nothing is consumed in the ordinary sense. Yet the scavengers remain fed.

They move slowly, hand over hand, hanging above the mountains with their backs exposed to the freezing air. The ocean dimples minutely around each hooked foot. Those dimples are the only imperfections ever seen on its underside, and even they do not travel. A creature may seem to crawl for days while its arrangement of limbs, droplets, and shadow remains exact. Watching one produces the conviction of motion; looking away and back reveals no displacement.

Still, colonies are found where none were seen before.

Their dead do not fall. When one ceases moving, its companions gather around it and take it apart in a meticulous rite that may be feeding, repair, or birth. Plates are unhooked. Limbs are passed from body to body. The pale organs beneath are drawn into threads and pressed upward against the membrane of the sea. For a time there are fewer creatures. Later there are more, though no observer has witnessed the interval in which the number changes.

Their offspring—if they have offspring—are soft white knots carried beneath the thorax. Each knot contains a single black hook. They never appear to grow. Juveniles and adults may instead be separate castes, or the same individual viewed at different points in a cycle the world will not allow to complete.

They are not the makers of the machines.

That distinction is evident in their indifference. They cross pipes where those pipes enter the ocean, but never follow them downward. They cluster around electrical crowns and comb blue discharge from ceramic vanes, yet they do not operate controls or respond to alarms. A scavenger placed near an open service hatch will turn its upward mouth toward the dark water and ignore the machinery entirely.

The machines were made for another inhabitant.

Signs of it are everywhere, although the inhabitant itself is rarely seen.

Ladders fit a figure taller and narrower than a human being, with gloved hands capable of spanning two rungs. Control booths provide one chair, never two. The seats possess a deep vertical groove and a restraint crossing high over the chest. Windows are positioned above ordinary eye level. Maintenance alcoves contain articulated sleeves ending in five-fingered gloves; when cut open, the gloves are found empty, though sometimes their fingers flex in sequence with nearby relays.

The builders—or attendants—persist within the factories as patterns of use.

A rung bends under weight that is no longer present. A door opens before an absent worker reaches it. Condensation parts in the silhouette of a passing body. In long corridors, warmth appears at shoulder height and proceeds at a measured walking pace, accompanied by the scrape of heavy clothing. One can follow such a presence through inspection galleries and pumping halls. It pauses at gauges. It leans toward observation windows. It raises an arm where a lever is fixed.

It never arrives at the next room.

These presences are not ghosts, because nothing here has cleanly died. They are labor retained after the laborer has been removed—or perhaps before the laborer was installed. The machinery contains their gestures as it contains its revolutions: not recordings, but actions deprived of consequence.

Where the mist outlines them most clearly, they appear long-limbed and narrow-headed, suited from throat to foot in layered pressure cloth. A rigid pack rises behind the shoulders. The helmet has no faceplate. Its front is a dark metal disk marked by a horizontal line between two arcs.

Sometimes the disk turns toward an observer.

Then the total sound of the world alters without changing. Every compressor beat, whale-call, electrical snap, and pressure fracture remains at precisely the same amplitude, yet together they resolve into something almost grammatical. Not words, exactly. More like instructions expressed through synchronized noise.

Stand here.

Observe the gauge.

Confirm the boundary.

Do not permit transfer.

The last directive recurs most often.

It may explain why nothing falls from the ocean, why no tower fully reaches the hidden depths, why animals cannot cross into air and stone cannot finish breaking. The factories are not supporting the sea. They are preventing exchange.

The thing they contain is not water.

It lives farther above.

The ordinary fish nearest the interface are only a skin of ecology spread over something much larger. Above them move blind hunters and radiant colonies; above those, forms mistaken for reefs, platforms, or shadows. These immense bodies never approach the boundary, but their organs descend toward it through the marine food web. Every pursuit, migration, shedding, and death transfers some minute quantity downward. The boundary scavengers collect it. The pipes receive what they leave behind. The machines draw it into the basalt.

Deep in the shafts, that gathered substance shines.

It is neither fluid nor electricity, though it behaves as both. It travels through rods without moving and accumulates without increasing. Instruments identify it under several incompatible units: pressure, duration, mass, obligation. Gauges that measure it are the only ones fitted with red sectors. Their needles stand exactly at the line before failure.

Something in the upper ocean feeds upon change itself.

Its visible body may be the entire measured depth of water. The city-long silhouettes are perhaps cilia crossing an internal membrane. The stationary geometries at the limit of sight may be teeth, sensory pits, or machines built inside it by organisms that do not know they inhabit another creature. The fish swim in it as microbes swim in blood.

It does not sleep. It does not move. It metabolizes event.

A cliff collapses, and the collapse is consumed before the stone can land. A turbine completes a revolution, and completion is drawn out of it, leaving only turning. A predator closes upon prey, but the diminishing distance is taken as nourishment. Evening approaches; approach is eaten, while evening remains remote. Wounds offer it loss. Journeys offer arrival. Memory is thin enough to escape digestion for a while, though not untouched.

This is why travelers become uncertain.

At first they remember a road behind them, despite the absence of tracks. Then they remember several versions: a route through the western escarpment, a descent past the hanging rockfall, a night spent inside a turbine hall where night cannot occur. Later the memories cease to arrange themselves in sequence. The traveler recalls entering a building after leaving it and hearing a warning before discovering the headset that delivered it.

Eventually a second silhouette begins to accompany the first in the mist.

It matches the traveler’s height.

Doors open for it.

Empty handrails flex beneath its grip. A chair’s restraints tighten around a body that remains standing elsewhere. Gauges wait for its inspection. In the glass of control booths, the traveler glimpses a figure in layered pressure cloth, its face gradually obscured by a dark circular plate.

The factories do not recruit inhabitants. Recruitment would require change.

They reveal that the inhabitant has always been employed.

There are likely thousands of attendants distributed across the plateau, each walking an uncompleted route between stations. They cannot meet. In places where two patrols should intersect, the corridors lengthen by one doorway or a stair repeats a landing. Their tasks are simple: inspect, confirm, listen, withhold. None knows who issued them. None can disobey, because disobedience requires an act to become different from intention.

Only memory offers resistance, and memory is the most dangerous form of life below the sea.

Some attendants scratch records into the ceramic backs of panels, where the world is slower to refuse alteration. Most scratches vanish. A few remain as pale pressure marks, readable only when blue arcs fire nearby. Across widely separated stations, they repeat the same fragments:

**THE BOUNDARY IS NOT A WALL.**

**THE NOISE IS THE LOAD.**

**WE ARE WHAT PASSES DOWNWARD.**

And, less often:

**DO NOT REMEMBER THE SHORE.**

No shore exists on the plateau. Yet attendants sometimes dream of one.

They dream of water beneath an open sky, waves advancing and withdrawing, stones made round by countless small collisions. They dream of rain that reaches the ground, rivers that arrive at the sea, animals that die and remain dead. In these dreams, the great upper organism is not yet vast. The mountains are molten. The factories have foundations but no towers. Workers stand together around a lever that can still be moved.

They are preparing to stop a catastrophe.

Or to begin one.

Upon waking, if waking occurs there, an attendant may find a wet pebble in one gloved hand: smooth, gray, impossibly old. It is the only rounded stone in the world. It persists for several breaths before becoming a sharp black fragment of basalt, seamless with the ground.

Then the route resumes.

Above, the hooked scavengers comb the underside of the ocean. Fish expose their pale bellies to the air. Vast shadows cross the fixed noon. The greater life in the water feeds on every outcome the world attempts to produce.

Below, the machines labor to keep that appetite suspended.

Between them walks the only creature capable of becoming different, carrying its dangerous, accumulating burden from one identical hour to the next.

It is the witness.

And slowly, unreliably, it remembers.
