The chamber is inhabited by a garden that has nearly ceased to consist of individuals.

Its mosses, lichens, translucent leaves, wax-black fronds, and colorless stems appear separate only where the eye insists on boundaries. Beneath their surfaces, they exchange water, ions, spores, and minute electrical changes through every point of contact. A disturbance at the northern fissure reaches the pool years later as altered growth. A damaged patch of moss is slowly fed from the walls. When moisture migrates westward, the whole chamber leans toward it—not visibly, not at once, but cell by cell across decades.

The garden possesses no center of thought. If it remembers, it remembers as shape.

Old wet cycles remain written in the thickness of moss terraces. Ancient dry intervals persist as buried strata of unspent spores. The arrival of smoke from some forgotten surface fire changed the color of one lichen band, and that band still circles the northern wall. The collapsed shelter survives in the garden’s growth patterns: leaves avoid spaces where a body once reached; roots converge beneath the recess where it lay. Absence has been copied as faithfully as presence.

Yet there was once a singular inhabitant here.

It was small enough to recline beneath the three-ribbed shelter, though “body” may be too simple a word for what occupied the recess. The clear fifth depression beside it retains a trace the machinery still recognizes: not heat, pressure, or chemistry, but a stable exclusion among possible states. Something rested a limb—or an organ, instrument, or extension of attention—there so often that the chamber learned not to let frost occupy that exact volume.

The inhabitant did not build the garden. The paths bending around already ancient moss show that clearly. Nor did it make the six pillars. It arrived after the chamber’s ecology had matured, perhaps after the makers themselves were gone. It lived here without mastering the place. It stepped around hummocks, drew water from the pool without lowering it, and sheltered under a film that disturbed neither condensation nor the slow migration of spores.

It may have been a custodian. It may have been a refugee.

The distinction blurred over the span of its residence.

At first it moved often enough to maintain paths. Later its routes shortened: fissure to pool, pool to pillar, pillar to shelter. Eventually even those journeys ceased. There are no remains because death, in this chamber, was not permitted to become waste. Whatever organic substance the inhabitant possessed was accepted into the touching tissues and distributed with extreme patience. A few unusual compounds appear throughout the garden now, diluted across every wall and hummock. They influence when certain leaves divide. They alter which possible photons the pillars are able to borrow. In that sense, the vanished resident still participates in the weather.

The machinery also lives here, though not biologically and perhaps not consciously. It behaves less like a collection of devices than a set of habits imposed on reality. The pillars maintain impossible metabolism. The tiles move water through routes too fine to see. The ceiling ring establishes long seasons whose original purpose has been lost, if purpose was ever separate from continuation.

None of it commands the garden directly. It preserves contrasts: damp and dry, active and dormant, one chemical path over another. Life does the rest.

The cracked tile is part of this population too. Its failure is not worsening. Around it lies a perfect circle of deprivation, bare except for gray grit and a nearly invisible crust of dormant cells. The garden has grown to the border but not crossed it. Roots outline the dead zone. Spores accumulate there during each dry interval and perish or wait. The flaw has become a fixed ecological role—a small, enduring winter nested inside the larger one.

The black pool harbors no animal, but it is not empty. Under the ice, threads from many colonies meet in the liquid and exchange fragments of themselves. Weak blue light moves along those submerged fibers during the rising-water phase. The pulse resembles signaling, though it may take months to pass from one side of the pool to the other. Nothing here has reason to hurry. Even response has become geological.

Thus what lives beneath the frozen plain is not a hidden creature waiting to wake, nor merely a cache of alien plants.

It is a distributed survivor: garden, machine, water cycle, accumulated memory, stable damage, and the incorporated residue of one long-absent tenant. It feeds on unrealized accidents. It drinks by fractions. It measures time in damp migrations and the burial of its own lower layers. It has no predators, no offspring that depart, and no world beyond the chamber except as dust and pollen descending through the fissure.

If a person entered, the garden would not attack or flee. For a while, it might do nothing discernible. Breath would frost the leaves. Footsteps would crush growth older than nations. Heat would create a temporary season around the intruder’s body.

Then the chamber would begin, very slowly, to remember them.
