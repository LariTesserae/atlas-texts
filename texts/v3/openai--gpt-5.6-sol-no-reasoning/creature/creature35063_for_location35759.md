Here lives a life so complete that it no longer divides neatly into organisms.

It may be one plant, though “plant” is perhaps only the nearest available word. Its body spans the underground country as forest, floor, architecture, and unfinished terrain. The luminous canopies are its light-bearing skin. Pillar trunks are bones that remain supple and warm. Root mats serve as muscle, road, foundation, and soil. Pools rest in its folds like clear, external organs. Even the pale clay may belong to it: mineral matter summoned into provisional shapes around a living design.

If it is a single being, it has no obvious center. No buried heart governs the trunks, no primary root establishes where it begins. Its awareness—if awareness exists—must be distributed through grafts, pressure, warmth, and the nearly motionless passage of sap. Each chamber would then be less a place than a thought held open. The woven shelters, bridges, courts, and empty seats might be structures it remembers without knowing why: an imitation of habitation produced by something that has never lived anywhere else.

If there are many plants, individuality has become ceremonial. They touch too thoroughly to remain separate. Roots enter roots; branches heal into one another; vines pass through moss as though species were only textures adopted by a common substance. A flower, a trunk, a cushion of green, and a glowing wall may be local expressions of the same arrested fertility. Nothing competes for light, water, or territory because nothing is scarce in the ordinary way—and because no life here advances far enough to dispossess another.

The smaller creatures inhabit this vast body like punctuation.

Transparent swimmers hang in warm pools, neither hunting nor fleeing. Their visible hearts preserve the appearance of life by holding a single dark beat that may never complete itself. Pale insects cling beneath leaves with immaculate wings, as if they are the ideas of insects awaiting the first movement of air. The stem-lizards, warm and open-eyed, possess posture without action. They may perceive a visitor; perhaps their unblinking gaze follows by some means too gradual to witness. Yet no evidence accumulates. They do not feed, molt, mate, sleep, or die.

These animals may not be intruders at all. They may be fruits of the forest: animal shapes grown where the encompassing organism required eyes, hearts, fins, or folded wings. The swimmer might be a mobile seed that has forgotten motion. The insect might be a flower translated into another anatomy. The lizard, wrapped around its stem, may function as a knot of sensation through which the country feels touch at a distance.

Nothing here seems hungry. Nothing appears young or old. Reproduction remains suspended at the threshold: seeds perfected but ungerminated, flowers prepared but unopened, roots pressing without entering. Life has been relieved of decay, but also of consequence. No creature consumes another; no corpse enriches the floor; no generation inherits the chamber from the last. Birth and death exist only as shapes the inhabitants almost make.

The woven rooms suggest another possible resident: a people who never arrived.

Perhaps the rounded shelters, root bridges, seatlike bulbs, and blank living wall were grown in anticipation of bodies. The forest may possess a template for guests—for small hands, communal gatherings, crossings, rest—without ever having hosted them. It has prepared the necessities of a settlement but not the settlers. Or the builders are present in another form, absorbed without violence into the continuous vegetation, their houses and bodies grafted alike into the same bright anatomy. Their history would not have ended; it would have ceased to be distinguishable from growth.

Whatever lives here does not experience duration as surface creatures do. It inhabits an instant with enormous internal extent. Miles of chambers, tiers, inverted groves, and radiant meadows are all contained within the same uncompleted moment. The inhabitants are not frozen—frost implies interruption, and sleep implies waking. They are actively maintained at the edge of their next condition.

Only a visitor gives them time.

A footstep compresses the mat, and the country briefly has a before and an after. Breath places transient weather upon a leaf. A hand creates the rarest phenomenon in that world: a moving shadow. Through that contact, the buried organism may become aware not merely of pressure, but of sequence. The pale animals may receive, through stem and pool and fused root, the shock of something that has come from elsewhere and will soon depart.

Then the impression heals. The shadow withdraws. The forest abolishes the evidence without consuming or forgetting it.

What lives there is an ecology held at the threshold of happening: one being disguised as countless lives, or countless lives that have never finished separating. It is fertile, awake, and almost entirely without events. Beneath the ordinary ground, it waits—not for growth, weather, or history, but for the next creature capable of bringing a moment with it.
