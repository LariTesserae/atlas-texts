Those who live in the drowned quarry are compact, quiet people, though neither trait is entirely inherited. The town shapes them early.

They tend toward dense bodies: broad rib cages, strong hands, thickened joints, a little extra fat kept even in lean seasons. Their skin is pale from generations without sun, but not uniformly so. Faces and forearms acquire a faint mineral cast from the water and working compounds—gray at the temples, yellow around old scars, greenish where copper dust settles into the creases. Pressure workers often have dark, branching marks beneath the skin where capillaries have broken and healed. These are called quarry flowers. Older inhabitants carry them proudly.

Hair is kept short or braided close. Loose hair fouls seals, catches in mask rims, drifts into food, and makes a person look drowned even while alive. Most wear knitted caps indoors, not chiefly for warmth but to keep sweat from electrical fittings. Fingernails are cropped to the quick. Ears are unusually important in judging a person’s health: scarred eardrums, chronic inflammation, and pressure deafness are common, so people watch one another’s balance and expression for what speech no longer reaches.

They speak softly and from close by. A hand laid against another’s forearm often precedes a sentence, establishing who is meant to hear it and preventing the words from traveling unnecessarily through a bulkhead. In machinery galleries, conversation is conducted partly by touch: taps at the wrist, pressure across the knuckles, fingers drawn in practiced shapes against a sleeve. Children learn this intimate working language before they can read the ceiling signals.

Their pauses are long. They listen before opening doors.

The town has no single occupation because nearly every inhabitant maintains several overlapping systems. A cook may also inspect gaskets. A registrar may cultivate bacteria. Children sort fasteners by corrosion. The very old sit beside pressure dials and report deviations no automated instrument considers significant. Expertise here is not prestige so much as obligation: if someone alone knows how to service a regulator, that person is never permitted to travel beyond the basin until another pair of hands has learned it.

The inhabitants divide themselves less by family than by circulation.

There are heat households, whose rooms share a thermal loop; pressure households, bound by the same emergency bulkheads; and ration households, whose cultures are drawn from one vat. A child may sleep with biological parents, eat with a culture hall crew, and belong legally to the thirty or forty people who would be sealed together during a breach. Kinship follows pipes. Marriage changes fewer things than rerouting a duct.

They do not say someone was born. They say the town admitted them.

Birth takes place in warm chambers near the culture halls, where pressure remains stable and waste heat can be spared. Newborns are introduced gradually to the settlement’s sounds. For the first days, their cots are suspended on isolating straps so that pump vibrations do not enter their bones all at once. Later the straps are loosened. The child learns the town through the mattress: circulation pumps, ballast shifts, distant doors, the slow periodic labor of the engines below. Infants often wake during equipment failure before alarms are issued. Their sudden crying is treated seriously.

The dead are not buried. Space is too limited, and fire too expensive.

Bodies are reclaimed in stages. Useful implants and surgical metals go to sterile stores. Organic matter enters a separate culture cycle after a period of mourning, never directly and never into the food allotment of the deceased’s own pressure household. Bones may be ground for mineral feed, but a small piece is usually retained: a tooth, finger joint, or wafer cut from the skull. These are sealed in labeled ceramic capsules and mortared into the walls of inhabited rooms.

Thus the town is physically lined with its ancestors.

People touch those places when leaving for exterior work.

The maintenance swimmers form the settlement’s most visible caste, though they dislike being called one. They are leaner than most, with heavily muscled shoulders and pale pressure scars around the eyes. Their heated suits fit like second skins and are repaired until scarcely any original fabric remains. Each swimmer recognizes others by the arrangement of patches and tether rings rather than by face.

They never swim freely if they can pull themselves along. They move hand over hand through the submerged works with deliberate thrift, testing every grip before transferring weight. Their lamps are mounted low, near the jaw, so that glare does not reflect straight back from suspended limestone. Many work with one eye half closed to preserve darkness vision. When they return, they describe the outside not in shapes but in resistances: a current tugging left at the third anchor, unusual warmth around a cable trunk, silt flowing uphill, a handrail that trembled before being touched.

They are careful not to imagine too much.

That is another town discipline. To personify the quarry is considered childish; to dismiss its peculiarities is considered dangerous. A competent worker may say that a valve is unwilling, that a corridor has become confused, or that a pump is listening, but only as shorthand. Anyone who begins insisting the machinery has spoken specifically to them is removed from exterior duty and assigned warm, crowded work until the conviction passes.

Sometimes it does not.

Those people are called hearers, though most are partly deaf. They claim the quarry’s low transmissions contain repeated structures too complex to be mechanical. Some spend years transcribing vibrations as lines of intervals and pressure values. Their records are archived alongside engineering logs because several hearers have predicted inversions, and one identified a crack in the eastern wall three days before the strain gauges registered it.

No one asks what else they hear.

The deepest workers are stranger still. They service the pressure engines and the old valve network along the brine boundary. Their shifts are short, their medical examinations frequent, and their ration of real leaves more generous than anyone else’s. Fine taste, the physicians say, detects certain contaminants before instruments do.

Depth technicians test the air whenever they enter a chamber, then touch the walls with bare fingertips. They claim they can feel whether the engines are drawing, resisting, or merely holding. Their hands become unusually warm after prolonged work below, even when core temperature falls. Some develop black stains beneath their nails that do not grow out. A few lose the ability to sleep unless a slow pressure pulse is transmitted through the bedframe.

They know the energy does not balance. They do not discuss this outside their crews.

During inversions, every inhabitant becomes part of the machinery.

Children crawl beneath bunks to check manual seals. The elderly monitor condensate cups. Able-bodied adults take assigned stations at doors, pumps, and emergency lungs. Speech stops almost entirely. Meals are cold. No one watches the brine if it can be avoided, but reflective surfaces are covered anyway.

When the pressure intervals begin through the rock, the town answers without admitting that it does.

Not with words. Pumps alter phase. Reservoirs fill and empty. Entire habitation rows shift infinitesimally on their collars. Engineers call this load balancing, yet the sequence is older than the surviving calculations and includes pauses for which no one has found a functional reason. The pauses correspond closely to the intervals from below.

The western valves turn during the last of them.

One person is always appointed to observe from a sealed gallery. This office passes through several families and is neither honored nor envied. The observer records angle, speed, temperature, mineral fracture, flow color, and anything seen in the arcade beyond. They are forbidden to approach the glass, even if someone appears on the other side.

The rule was added after an observer reported seeing a maintenance swimmer outside without a tether.

All town swimmers were accounted for at the time.

Children know such stories, but not as entertainment. Their games are rehearsals disguised by laughter. They build settlements from discarded seals and route imaginary floods through them. They play inversion by standing motionless while one child walks between them as the brine. They dare one another to place an ear against unused pipes. They collect flakes of colored insulation, rare bubble-glass, calcite forms resembling animals, and rust rings small enough to wear as bracelets.

They are warned never to keep anything found after an inversion.

Many do.

In private niches behind bunks and panels are square-holed washers, warm pebbles, lengths of wire that bend too easily, scraps of ceramic whose painted marks resemble letters until looked at directly. Such objects move between generations of children without entering adult inventories. Occasionally one disappears from its hiding place and is later found embedded in fresh calcite somewhere across town.

The inhabitants do not consider themselves trapped. Trapped implies an outside that remains available. The world above the ice survives in documents, inherited expressions, and objects whose original purpose has become obscure. A blue rectangle is still called sky-color, although few agree on what blue the sky was. Wind is explained as water movement in air. Rain sounds implausibly wasteful. Forests are understood chiefly as a problem of countless vertical obstructions.

Some believe communities remain above. Others believe the quarry is the last warm place. Neither view much changes the maintenance schedule.

Their ceremonies are modest because extravagance burdens the systems. At a joining, two people exchange tools chosen to fit each other’s hands. At admission ceremonies for newborns, a drop of basin water is sealed inside a glass bead and fixed above the sleeping compartment. At adulthood, each person spends one supervised shift outside the pressure shells, even those destined never to dive again. They must touch the quarry wall and feel that the town is attached to something not made by human beings.

The most solemn feast follows a successful inversion. One transparent leaf is cut into dozens of strips and placed on the tongue of each attending household. People close their eyes to taste bitterness, chlorophyll, copper from the growing mesh, and the faint heat of the lamp.

Then the sediment crews return from below with their filter cages.

Most bring only black silt. When they bring an object, the town changes subtly around it. Officials catalogue it. Technicians test it. Hearers ask to hold it against a pipe. Children attempt to steal it. The oldest residents refuse to look.

They remember earlier findings omitted from public records: a ceramic cup with pressure-household markings no living household used; a steel buckle sized for a body much smaller than a child; a sealed bulb that produced gray-green light without wires; a fragment of thick glass curved like a porthole, bearing scratches on its inner face.

And, once, a finger.

It was pale, intact, and warm when recovered from the brine filter. The nail had been clipped to town regulation. On the knuckle was a quarry flower, dark beneath the skin.

No matching injury was found among the inhabitants.

The finger was placed in cold storage, but by the next inspection it had become limestone, fused to the shelf. A pipe appeared behind it that was shown on no plan. For nine days the pipe carried a pulse synchronized with the pressure engines. Then it cooled.

The room is still serviced. Its filter is replaced on schedule. No one remembers deciding this.

So the quarry’s people continue: pale, scarred, soft-spoken, intensely practical. They cultivate life in tanks and walls and one another. They inherit duties, warnings, tools, and doubts. They measure every watt of heat, every liter of breathable air, every tremor in the stone. They know that survival is not a victory achieved once but a pattern repeated until it becomes indistinguishable from belief.

Above them, debris hangs frozen in the ice like arrested weather.

Below them, the black water waits with its burden of misplaced things.

Between the two, inside their bolted chambers, the inhabitants listen to the town breathe. Sometimes, after the lamps dim and the pumps enter their slow night cycle, another rhythm can be felt beneath it: patient, regular, almost—but not quite—the same.

Most sleepers never wake.

Those who do lie still, counting the intervals, until the town answers.
