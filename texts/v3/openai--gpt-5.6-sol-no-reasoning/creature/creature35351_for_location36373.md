The people of the plateau are compact, deliberate, and difficult to surprise.

They are not uniformly short, but the city encourages a certain posture: shoulders inclined into weather, chin lowered, one hand habitually free for railings, cables, and wet stone. Even indoors they step as if testing the ground. Children learn early that a dry stair may be more dangerous than a flooded one, that a singing wire must not be touched, and that a sudden warmth beneath the paving means water has been diverted nearby. Visitors look outward at cliffs and cloud. Those born here look down first, then upwind.

Their clothing is layered and asymmetrical. Long coats close diagonally so rain cannot drive through the seam. Sleeves and hems can be tightened before crossing exposed ground, then loosened in the warm, wet interiors of workshops. Hoods are shallow, leaving the ears uncovered to catch bells, strain notes, and changes in machinery. Most people carry gloves of waxed cloth or thin leather, though they often remove one to feel a pipe, wall, or trickle directly.

Color survives in linings, cords, enamel badges, and ceramic beads: rust orange, reservoir green, cloudy blue, the white of sunlit fog. Outer garments soon darken toward the city’s common shades of stone and wet iron.

There are fewer inhabitants than the density of construction suggests. Whole blocks may contain only a handful of occupied rooms, while neighboring courtyards support crowded households arranged around heat exchangers or dependable wells. Residence follows utility. Families migrate within the city over generations, abandoning apartments when channels shift and inhabiting old offices, bathhouses, store chambers, and turbine galleries. An address is often given by the nearest functioning system rather than by a street: below the third warm pipe, behind the western overflow, two levels above the green cistern.

Households are seldom composed only of relatives. They gather around obligations. A dwelling may contain siblings, apprentices, an elderly valve reader, two tram workers, children whose parents are stationed at the rim, and someone who has the right to sleep beside the boiler because they wake at the slightest pressure change. Such arrangements are not considered temporary. Shared maintenance makes a household as surely as ancestry does.

No one person owns water. One may own a roof tank, a set of filters, a length of gutter, or the labor required to keep a cistern clean, but the water itself remains part of the city’s circulation. To hoard it beyond an allotted interval is both illegal and shameful. The gravest ordinary accusation is not theft but obstruction: that someone allowed a channel to clog, built over an undocumented hollow, concealed seepage, or tied a private mechanism into a public flow.

Their most respected people are listeners, tasters, measurers, and keepers of recurrence.

Water tasters travel with nested cups, mineral strips, glass vials, and small brass instruments. They do not merely decide whether water is safe. They identify its route by flavor: iron from a particular gallery, lime from a fresh crack, the faint bitterness of roof moss, soot from an old boiler district. Skilled tasters can detect when two underground streams have begun to mingle. Before drinking from an unfamiliar source, they touch it to the lips, wait, smell the empty cup, and record the result whether or not anything seems wrong.

Channel keepers memorize the city beneath the city. Their maps are layered, translucent sheets showing present flows over extinct ones. The oldest among them can stand in an ordinary kitchen and describe the buried quarry wall below, the sealed stairs behind the stove, and the direction in which spilled water would travel if the floor cracked. They mark changes in blue-black ink that spreads slightly on damp paper, so their archives resemble weather systems.

Anchor crews live nearest the cliffs. They are broad-belted, laconic people who inspect retaining chains, bridge sockets, turbine roots, and platforms over the cloud. Their tools are attached to them by cords, and they tie nothing with a knot that cannot be released one-handed. Their vocation has produced its own etiquette. An anchor worker never says that a fastening is safe. They say it held at dawn, or held under western wind, or has survived two winters and remains under observation.

Mechanists tend the hidden exchanges by which one force becomes another. They think in consequences across distance. To them a closing gate is not a single event but part of a chain involving a rising tank, a descending counterweight, a ventilator opening elsewhere, and a warning bell that must sound before the pressure reaches an old joint. Their diagrams look less like machines than genealogies. Some can identify districts by vibration through the soles of their feet.

Gardeners cultivate pauses in the water. They grow edible mosses, bitter greens, silver-rooted grasses, mushrooms, dwarf fruits, medicinal ferns, and paper-leaved vines. Their work is partly horticulture and partly negotiation with shade, condensation, waste heat, mineral seepage, and wind. They may tend twenty small plots scattered through five elevations because no single chamber provides everything a crop needs. A gardener’s day is a vertical journey from cold roof beds to warm fungal vaults and back again.

Children are given runoff toys: small wheels, gates, cups, and channels fitted together in courtyards after rain. Through play they learn delay, overflow, blockage, and balance. They race leaf boats only in designated gutters, since an innocent obstruction can travel farther than expected. Before being allowed to cross the city alone, a child must know the nearest high route, the meaning of the western bells, and at least two ways home that do not share a tunnel.

They also learn silence. Not solemn silence, but attentive silence—the pause before answering, during which one distinguishes a pipe knock from a footstep or a cable note from an alarm. Plateau conversation has room for these interruptions. A person may stop speaking in the middle of a sentence, tilt their head, and continue once the sound has resolved. Nobody considers this rude.

Speech itself is quiet and exact. People identify uncertainty openly. They say a leak is likely, a wall appears settled, a sound has recurred seven times, a crack was dry at noon. Claims of certainty are reserved for direct measurements or long repetition. Boasting is regarded as a kind of structural weakness: an unsupported weight placed too far from its anchors.

This does not make them humorless. Their jokes are dry, cumulative, and often concerned with systems failing in predictable ways. They tease newly paired lovers about needing a second anchor. An indecisive person is said to be waiting for a third winter. Someone who repeats gossip without checking it has “drunk from an unmeasured crack.” They tell stories in which grand ambitions are undone by small neglected drains.

Meals are warm, sour, and mineral-rich. Grain is dried in exhaust lofts and cooked into dense cakes. Mushrooms, fermented greens, pale root vegetables, cliff herbs, moth-fed fish from a few heated tanks, and occasional bird eggs form much of the diet. Broths are kept simmering near furnace walls, changing over days as ingredients are added. Drinking water is rarely served plain to guests; it is warmed with herbs, partly to honor the source and partly because cold cistern water can hurt the teeth and stomach.

Hospitality begins with drying. A host takes wet outer layers, hangs them where their runoff will be collected, and offers warmed cloth for the face and hands. Only then is there drink. To waste the water shed from a visitor’s coat would be an insult to both visitor and house.

Their rituals are concerned less with beginnings and endings than with incorporation.

When a child is named, water from the household’s principal source is mixed with a drop from a public cistern and touched to the child’s tongue. When apprentices complete their training, they add their first independent measurements to the permanent records and must defend them against older workers’ questions. Partnerships are marked by exchanging anchor cords and jointly maintaining a catchment for one full wet season.

The dead are not buried deeply, because soil is scarce and underground space belongs to circulation. Bodies may be cremated using recovered industrial heat, or placed in ventilated stone chambers until only bone remains. Ash is mixed into ceramic markers, filter housings, or tiles set where the person worked. Bone is ground into pale aggregate for small objects, never structural walls; the living insist that no one should be made to bear weight forever. Names endure in maintenance language. A valve may retain the name of the woman who redesigned it, a stair the name of the child who first noticed water beneath it.

Those who die in falls are mourned differently. Their names are spoken at the cliff platforms, followed by a measured interval of listening. If a deep report rises from below during that interval, it is recorded without interpretation.

The city’s sparse animals occupy an unusual place in civic life. Storm birds are never caged. Their arrival is treated as weather made flesh, and shutters are left cracked so they can shelter in roof spaces. Pale moths are tolerated in machinery rooms unless they threaten cloth insulation. Cistern crustaceans are protected by law in the oldest reservoirs, partly because they may reveal contamination and partly because their origin is unresolved.

The larger dusk animal—if it is one animal—is called by many district names. Roof-long, gray-tail, quiet one, gutter fox. People leave scraps beneath overhangs and near warm vents, then carefully avoid watching. A confirmed sighting must include direction, duration, weather, and at least one second witness. Most reports fail these standards. This has not diminished belief in it.

There are individuals who spend their lives at the limits of accepted knowledge. Cliff listeners descend to the old platforms with drums of stretched membrane, weighted strings, pressure tubes, and recording needles. They classify the sounds below by depth, sequence, weather, and echo. Some argue for ice, some for rockfall, some for lower settlements. A few believe the reports come from within the mountain rather than beneath it.

They are not dismissed as mystics, but neither are they granted conclusions. The city gives them instruments, shelter, and archive space. Their duty is to keep listening without forcing an answer.

Other inhabitants seek obsolete water. They open sealed galleries, crawl through silted conduits, and sample ancient pockets exposed by new cracks. They sometimes return with blind organisms, unfamiliar mineral films, corroded tools, or air that extinguishes lamps. Their profession is dangerous not because the unknown is considered hostile, but because it joins known systems in unforeseen ways.

Every resident participates in maintenance. Markets pause when runoff exceeds the street capacity. Tram passengers disembark to clear fern roots from a grille. A teacher may interrupt a lesson because condensation has begun forming in the wrong corner. Repair crews leave tools in public recesses, tagged with the route by which they must be returned. Theft is rare; neglect is not, and much of civic governance consists of determining whether neglect was careless, unavoidable, or inherited.

Authority belongs to councils of systems rather than districts alone. Water, wind, heat, transport, cultivation, anchorage, and habitation each send representatives. Their debates are notoriously slow because every proposed change has remote effects. A new roof may shade a garden, alter runoff, increase load above a tunnel, and deprive a downstream wheel of the brief surge that opens a hospital vent. Laws are therefore often provisional and seasonal.

The city has no single calendar. It keeps counts of winters, major spill events, long dry intervals, anchor replacements, and the returns of particular bird flocks. Personal age may be given conventionally, but important memories are located by conditions: the year the eastern quarries held water until midsummer; the three-winter fog; the season when orange algae appeared in the upper pipes; the rain in which all seven western bells sounded.

Music favors resonance and delay. Instruments are built from pipes, glass bowls, stretched wire, dripping vessels, and ceramic plates. Performances may extend across buildings, with one player sending a tone through a cistern gallery and another answering from a courtyard above. The city’s best-known songs cannot be performed in dry weather because they depend upon active channels. During heavy rain, whole neighborhoods become accidental instruments, and people open doors to listen.

Their visual art is often temporary. Mineral washes are painted where seepage will alter them. Metal sheets are polished so moving mirrors draw light across engraved scenes. Moss is trained over porous stencils, producing images that darken, brighten, and vanish with moisture. Permanent monuments are regarded with mild suspicion. Anything claiming to resist the mountain entirely is either dishonest or badly designed.

Yet the inhabitants are not resigned to disappearance. Their continuity lies in revision. They expect their rooms to change purpose, their streets to be rerouted, their work to be softened out of recognition. They preserve not forms but relations: where water must be allowed to go, how force must be shared, which warning follows which sound, who is responsible when the old route fails.

Those who leave the plateau often find lowland life disturbingly careless. Rivers seem impossibly abundant. Buildings rise too high without visible chains. People speak over mechanical noises. Water runs from taps with no one naming its source. Some emigrants adapt; others become engineers, surveyors, or obsessive keepers of household drains. Many eventually return, bringing seeds, glass, medicines, metals, or stories that are received politely and checked against conditions.

At evening the inhabitants emerge briefly onto covered walks and upper terraces. Work coats steam near vents. Lamps appear behind ribbed glass. Gardeners close warm frames; listeners descend toward the rim; children crouch over the last active gutters, adjusting tiny gates with numb fingers. From household to household, people pause as the cistern bells begin.

No bell tells the whole city the same thing. Each marks an arrival somewhere beneath them, water completing a route through stone, brick, root, rust, and darkness. The residents listen for their own bell and for the ones on which theirs depends.

Then they move.

A valve is turned. A vat is covered. A lamp brightens as a counterweight drops. Someone wakes and pulls on boots. Someone else opens a channel cut long before their family came to the plateau. Far away, behind walls no living hand has fully mapped, old water enters a new course.

This is what lives there: not a people who have conquered the mountain, nor one merely surviving upon it, but a population drawn into its unfinished geology—watchful, interdependent, and gradually rounded by the same forces that soften iron, concrete, and stone.
