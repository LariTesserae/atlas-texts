The people of the drowned crown are neither fish nor merely people reshaped for water. At rest, half concealed in a root curtain or seated within a woven niche, they can be mistaken for slender growths of the city itself.

Their bodies are long through the torso and short in the leg, built less for running than for folding, turning, and holding position in moving water. The shoulders are broad and supple. Arms reach almost to the ankles, with wide hands whose six fingers are joined by a fine, transparent web. Their feet are similarly spread, useful for steering but more often curled around ropes, branches, and carved handholds. Even in sleep, something in them remains attached.

Their skin is smooth where currents pass over it and faintly ridged along the spine, flanks, forearms, and calves. The ridges are sensory lines: delicate organs that perceive pressure, approaching bodies, the beat of fins behind a wall, and messages struck into the tree. A citizen may know who is crossing the next chamber before seeing them. Old surveyors can lay a palm to bark and distinguish a feeding ray, a pruning crew, and the dangerous strain of a branch beginning to split.

Their colors are quiet but various. Most bear shades found in the city’s living architecture—gray-green, bark brown, pale blue, the muted violet of shadowed leaves. The young are often nearly translucent at the hands and face. With age, pigment gathers in whorls around joints and sensory ridges. Scars blanch into branching white lines. Gardeners deliberately stain their fingertips with plant dyes so that their signs remain legible in dim water.

Along the sides of the neck are several narrow breathing folds, protected by flexible plates of cartilage. When the people are calm, these open and close so gently that they resemble the mouths of small flowers. Exertion spreads them wide and shows the dark red tissue beneath. The city considers this private in the way another culture might consider nakedness private; formal collars do not obstruct the gills but veil their motion with fringes of fiber, bead, or leaf skin.

They also breathe to a lesser degree through richly supplied membranes beneath the arms and along the ribs. This is why their clothing hangs loose and leaves broad areas uncovered. Garments are not made for warmth alone but for modesty, identification, tool-bearing, and control of drag. A common garment is a cross-woven harness with narrow panels over chest and hips, weighted at the lower edge so it does not float across the face. Workers wear tight wraps of softened root fiber. Officials trail carefully pruned tassels whose knots declare office and district. Children wear bright back-straps by which an adult, turtle, or guide line can retrieve them.

Hair, where it grows, is coarse and sparse. Long loose hair is dangerous among roots and tools, so adults braid it close, bind it in gum, or permit small filter-feeding animals to crop it. Facial hair occurs as short sensory filaments around the mouth and jaw. These quiver at minute changes in current, giving attentive faces an expression of perpetual listening.

Their eyes are large, dark-pupiled, and shielded by a clear inner lid. They see color well in the green-lit day but rely increasingly on vibration after dusk. Their ears are small sealed pits. Sound reaches them through bone, throat, and rib cage as much as through the head. Music is therefore worn and touched as often as heard. Lovers press sternums together to share humming notes. Infants quiet when held against the resonant root walls. A lie spoken aloud may pass unnoticed, but an uncertain hand upon a message root is difficult to disguise.

They speak in several ways at once.

At arm’s length, conversation is made from low throat tones, mouth clicks, changes in gill posture, and quick signs of hands and shoulders. Names usually have both a voiced form and a signed one. The sign-name may refer to a childhood habit, a scar, a family graft, or the particular curl of a home branch. Across open water, speech becomes broader: whole-arm gestures, reflective palm paint, patterned flashes from shuttered worm-lamps. Over greater distances, the people drum roots and branchwood, transmitting formal phrases through the city’s body.

Consequently, privacy is understood less as silence than as disconnection. A private conversation takes place suspended away from walls and guide ropes, in open water where vibrations disperse. Serious confidences are exchanged while drifting. Courts, by contrast, require every participant to keep one hand or foot on living wood, so that hesitation and interruption can be felt by all.

The people do not swim constantly. Unanchored motion costs energy and makes precise work difficult. They travel by pulling themselves along ropes, pushing from branch to branch, or catching a current with small capes stretched between wrist and hip. Skilled swimmers move in slow, economical coils. Only children dart. To hurry without cause is considered both childish and vaguely alarming, the behavior of prey.

Their young are born soft, pale, and poorly buoyant. For the first years they are carried in ventral baskets or tethered inside household chambers. Their gill folds develop gradually, and nursing infants take a rich secretion from glands beneath the parent’s collarbones. Several adults may nurse one child. Kinship therefore follows tending more strongly than birth: the person who fed, tethered, taught currents, and maintained the child’s sleeping basket is a parent in law.

Children learn the city first by taste and touch. They are taught the sap signature of their home branch, the roughness of safe guide ropes, the vibration meaning *hold*, and the bitter trace that warns of diseased wood. Before they are trusted to travel alone, they must find their way home with their eyes covered. Many ride old turtles not only for safety but because turtles preserve routes the tree has nearly erased. A child’s first independent journey is commemorated by tying a new shell marker somewhere along the route. Decades later, adults sometimes find their childhood shells embedded in bark.

Households are not defined by enclosed dwellings. A family may occupy a sequence of chambers distributed along one cultivated limb: a sleeping arch, a fermentation hollow, an orchard niche, and a communal grooming screen, each separated by open water. Possessions are tethered in nets or pressed into living cupboards that open to familiar touch rhythms. What cannot be attached is not regarded as securely owned.

Wealth is measured less by quantity than by maintained relationships: rights to a productive plant strain, the service of trained animals, access to warm fissures, remembered grafting techniques, reliable messenger routes, and the labor of descendants willing to tend one’s dwelling. Someone may own a copper needle and still be considered poor if their walls are sick.

Their oldest profession is the tending of the host.

These gardeners, surveyors, judges, and physicians form no single guild, because the same knowledge underlies all four roles. They read swelling buds, fungal stains, tensions in wood, the direction of sap, and the minute distortions produced by distant weight. Their tools are simple: bone probes, shell scrapers, cords marked in knots, little wedges of stone, and living slips grown to reveal whether a cut is safe. Their conclusions can alter inheritance. If a chamber is constricting, no pedigree preserves it. If a branch has turned toward another household’s light, compensation may take a generation of redirected growth.

A surveyor’s authority is tempered by time. Important judgments are seldom delivered at once. A disputed wall may be marked and observed through an entire cycle of bright and dark currents. The tree is permitted to testify by growing.

Float-pruners hold comparable prestige. They travel among pearl-like gas organs with thorn knives and catch-nets, choosing which bladders to harvest and which to leave. Their work demands an exact sense of force. One careless cut can drop a laden scaffold; one neglected cluster can wrench a nursery from the tree. Experienced pruners have broad scars along the arms where torn floats dragged them through branches. Their apprentices learn first by managing the tiny bladders used on children’s baskets and funerary wrappings.

Other specialists tend blue-worm lamps, breed messenger fish to recognize new sap trails, milk the placid round stock, guide burden eels, soften wood with cultivated rot, or descend toward the mountain’s exposed ridges for stone. The stone divers are among the few citizens whose lives contain abrupt danger. They travel below the familiar branches into colder water and return with their skin darkened by pressure bruises. Their songs are very slow, conserving breath and telling distance through pauses.

Meals are gatherings around tethered boards or edible gardens. Food arrives as strips, paste, pods, living fronds, and fermented pulp squeezed from stoppered vessels. Chewing is done with small rear teeth; the front of the mouth is adapted for drawing soft food from husks. Salt is prized. Mineral seep rights cause more quarrels than hunting grounds.

There is little slaughter in the city center. Fish are culled, but usually in outer chambers where blood will not bring predators among homes. Many household animals are eaten only after natural death, a practice that produces elaborate recipes for souring and long fermentation. Guests are honored with freshly opened nut pods, warm milk-secretions, or fruit taken from the lintel of the host’s own dwelling. To eat a building’s fruit without permission is not mere theft. It is an intrusion upon the household body.

The people sleep anchored in woven baskets, singly or in clusters. Their breathing slows until the gill folds barely move. Because sound travels so far, complete quiet is neither expected nor desired. Familiar knocks, shrimp clicks, grazing scrapes, and the creak of the tree assure sleepers that the world remains in place. Sudden silence wakes an entire household.

They fear stillness more than noise. A passage emptied of fish, a wall from which shrimp have fled, a current that ceases unexpectedly—these are warnings. Predators may be near, but so may sickness in the tree. When danger comes, citizens flatten against screens or fold into bark hollows. Spears are short and heavy, meant to be braced rather than thrown. Defenders attack eyes, gills, and sensory organs. They do not pursue beyond the cultivated limbs.

Their ceremonies are acts of shared pressure.

At adulthood, a person chooses or confirms a public name by striking it upon a district root. The pattern must be distinct, transmissible, and not easily confused with warnings. At partnership rites, the participants graft two young shoots together and undertake responsibility for whatever chamber, arch, or fruiting niche the union eventually becomes. Some grafts fail. Such partnerships may be dissolved without shame. A thriving graft, however, can bind descendants who never met those who planted it.

The dead are returned to the tree without haste. The body is washed by cleaner shrimp, wrapped so the limbs remain slightly open, and placed in a prepared hollow. Relatives often add a cutting from the dead person’s dwelling, a worn tool, or a cord bearing the knots of obligations fulfilled. Useful metal is almost never buried. The hollow is tended until it seals.

For years afterward, kin visit the forming knot and touch its grain. The dead are not believed to persist as distinct voices, yet neither are they thought absent. They have entered weight, growth, alignment, and resonance. A branch containing many ancestors sounds different when struck. Older citizens claim they can hear the difference. No one laughs at them.

Their religion, if it can be separated from gardening, centers upon response.

They do not worship the surface, though it is the source of light. Few imagine reaching it. To them it is a remote brightness, beautiful and thin, where water ends and therefore life becomes impossible. The abyss below inspires greater attention. It supports the mountain, receives all that falls, and returns altered sounds after long delays. The people avoid giving it a face or intention. Images of the deep are considered presumptuous.

Instead, they speak of the Lower Answer: the conviction that nothing sent through the world is wholly lost. A blow enters wood. A body enters grain. A name enters the roots. A note descends through mountain and darkness. What returns may be delayed, changed, or too low to understand, but the world has received it.

The stone bell embodies this belief. During great ceremonies, citizens gather in the central sphere around the inverted grove. They anchor themselves in widening rings. Children cling to adults; turtles settle along the lower limbs; messenger fish are kept from the square so their flashing patterns do not distract. At a signal felt through the seven civic roots, the swimmers strike the water with paddles.

The bell’s note passes through them before they hear it. Gill folds flare. Lamps tremble in their shells. Animals become still. For an instant the inhabitants cease to be separate bodies and experience themselves as one pressure moving through the city.

Then they wait.

Sometimes nothing returns.

Sometimes, far beneath, the lower note arrives.

The oldest listeners say it is not always exactly the same. They have recorded changes too slight for an untrained body to notice: a longer interval, a roughened edge, a second pulse hidden beneath the first. These variations are preserved in knotted cords and in the spacing of shell rings around the civic chamber. Some believe they measure shifts in the mountain. Some say they are echoes from older drowned forests. A few maintain that something living below has learned the ceremony and is answering deliberately.

Those few are not dismissed, but neither are they encouraged.

For there are creatures below the cultivated light.

Now and then a burden eel refuses a downward route. Great browsing fish rise from the dark with circular wounds or patches of skin blanched as if pressed against cold stone. Surveyors detect knocks inside the trunk that do not correspond to wind, growth, or tools. Once in several generations, blue lights appear beneath the lowest roots, spaced too evenly to be animals and moving upward against the night current.

The city responds as it responds to all dangers: without haste, according to forms polished by repetition. Outer gates contract. Children are tethered. The root drums speak a pattern older than any living citizen. Bone spears are distributed from sealed niches. The bell is not struck.

Then, usually, the lights withdraw.

Life resumes its slow work. Walls are trimmed. Floats are counted. Rot is cleaned from joints. Fish pour through windows. A surveyor marks the changing curve of an arch that will not close for another sixty years. Two siblings quarrel over a warm sleeping niche. A child misses a handhold and is caught by an old turtle. Someone adds a white shell to the knot of a grandparent. Someone plants a room they will never inhabit.

Through all of it, the people remain in contact with the tree: hand, foot, flank, rope, tool, voice.

They live not upon it but within its decisions. Their city is never finished enough to possess and never ruined enough to abandon. It narrows around them, opens beyond them, incorporates their dead, and carries every stroke of their labor down into the mountain.

They call themselves, in the low close-range voice, a word made by closing the mouth and humming through the bones of the face.

Its hand-sign is one palm laid against living wood, while the other opens toward the dark.
