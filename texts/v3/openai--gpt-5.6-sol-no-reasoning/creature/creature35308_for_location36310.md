The basin is inhabited by a single custodian, though at first it is easy to mistake her for another arrangement of limestone.

She lives in the least ruined of the three squat shelters near the central springs. “Lives” is perhaps too vigorous a word. She occupies it as water occupies a hollow: wholly, quietly, without seeming to arrive or depart. Her back has taken the curve of the roof slab. Her knees remain drawn close beneath her chin, and one shoulder rests in the polished depression left by many earlier shoulders—or by her own, over a span too long to reckon.

She is small enough to crouch there comfortably. At a distance her skin appears chalk-pale, but closer inspection reveals layers of mineral color: iron at the knuckles, gray along the spine, a faint yellow translucence at the throat. The wet has smoothed every sharpness from her. Hair, if it is hair, hangs in a few stiff cords against her neck, fused with calcite and clay. Drops collect at their ends and fall at intervals unrelated to her breathing.

Her eyes are broad, dark, and seldom fully open. They have adapted less to darkness than to reflection. She does not look directly at things. She finds them in the tremor they make upon water, in the interruption of a sheen, in the dim doubling of a form beneath a pool’s surface. When someone enters through the cleft, she may know first from the altered rhythm of condensation on the walls.

She eats almost nothing that can be recognized as food. She draws the translucent film from the warm pools with two fingers and touches it to her tongue. She takes the tiny flies when they settle within reach. More often she presses her mouth to a seam in the stone and remains there for hours, drinking seepage grain by grain, along with whatever salts or minute life the water carries. Her needs have narrowed to what the basin can provide without changing.

The tools in the niche are hers, though she did not make all of them. The chipped blade serves to loosen a crust that obstructs a runnel. The hammerstone is used rarely, and only with taps so light they might be mistaken for pebbles settling. With the stiffened cord she lowers bowls into narrow wells, measures depths, or tests whether hidden water still moves. She repairs nothing into newness. She only returns each thing to the condition in which it can continue.

Every pool has a level she knows. Every drip has an interval. She can tell by sound when a hairline channel has begun to close with mineral, and by warmth when rain has entered the buried fissures beyond the walls. Then she unfolds herself.

The movement is slow but not weak. One hand appears beneath the shelter roof, then an arm, then a narrow head bowed to avoid the slab. She places her feet on the flattened stones without looking down. Her toes are long and widely spaced, able to grip wet rims and calcite bulges. She never dislodges more than a grain or two. Crossing the crowded basin, she seems not to travel through it but to emerge successively from its pale surfaces.

At a blocked channel she kneels and clears only enough deposit to restore the old thread of water. If a curb stone has shifted, she leans against it until it settles back. If a bowl’s crack seals and the vessel begins to fill too high, she reopens the seam with the flint point. The nearly full bowl must remain nearly full. That is its proper state.

Perhaps she is the last of the people who built the shelters. Perhaps she is what one of them became after choosing not to follow the others out through the cleft. Yet there are hints that she is older than the handwork. The curbs accommodate her paths too neatly. The smallest shelter resembles not a house made for her but a roof placed over a hollow she already used. The shell-gritted bowls may have been offerings, tools, or attempts to imitate the cups in which the stone itself held water.

Nor is it certain there is only one custodian.

Sometimes a pale hand is seen beneath a ledge while she remains visible at the central spring. Sometimes the answering drop from inside an empty shelter comes too regularly to be water. The shoulder-polished stones preserve bodies of different heights. In the black pools, reflections occasionally retain a crouched figure after the figure above has moved away.

If there are others, they occupy the basin as hidden seepage does: divided by stone, joined underneath.

They do not speak. Their communication, if it deserves the name, is conducted by small alterations of rhythm. A finger dipped into one pool may produce an answering ring in another. Three taps on a curb are returned by a click from beneath the floor. A bowl is shifted the width of a thumbnail and, days later, a cord appears rewound in the niche. Nothing is announced. Nothing needs to be.

The custodian shows no hostility toward visitors. She withdraws into the fitted stone and watches through reflected light. Thirst may be answered by a bowl placed upon a stepping stone. Injury may draw her nearer, especially if blood threatens one of the clear springs. Then she cleans the wound with warm water, binds it in the old cord if she must, and guides the injured body toward the cleft.

But anyone who attempts to widen the entrance, dismantle a curb, drain a pool, or carry away the tools will find the basin subtly changed. Familiar footholds become impossible to locate. Water films stone that was dry a moment before. The cleft appears behind the intruder, then across the basin, then nowhere. No violence occurs. There is only delay, misdirection, and the mounting certainty that every surface is observing.

Eventually the stolen object is set down.

A drop falls.

The way out becomes obvious again.

Left alone, the custodian returns each object to its niche. She crouches beside the nearly full bowl and waits until its inflow and escape are once more equal. Then she folds herself beneath the low roof, her skin paling among the stacked limestone, her breath indistinguishable from warm vapor.

She is not the basin’s ruler, nor quite its prisoner. She is its remaining adjustment: the minute living pressure that keeps a finished place from changing, tending water that neither rises nor falls, preserving structures that neither stand whole nor collapse.

Everything there has settled.

She is what continues the settling.
