The hollow is inhabited less by separate creatures than by a whole enduring commonwealth.

Its oldest residents are the great trees, twelve or so immense, widely spaced beings whose roots divide the buried floodplain among them. They stand alone but live joined beneath the peat, exchanging water, minerals, sugars, and chemical warnings through grafted roots and fungal threads. Moss occupies their bark; lichens stipple the upper limbs; ferns root in damp forks. Bees inhabit their cavities, beetle larvae tunnel through their deadwood, woodpeckers open those tunnels, and rooks crowd the crowns. Each tree is at once organism, dwelling, reservoir, roadway, and ruin.

Below them lives a low, continuous society of plants. Sedges hold the wettest ground in dense islands. Cranberry, pennywort, creeping willow, grasses, mosses, and liverworts spread through every interval, covering soil, water, fallen timber, and old stumps indiscriminately. Duckweed occupies the pools so completely that frogs surface through a green skin. Fungi lace the entire floor, mostly unseen, entering roots and dismantling leaves, returning every fallen thing to circulation.

Animal life is abundant but dispersed. Frogs inhabit the temporary pools and drowned channels, calling in coordinated bands before dawn and through the damp hours. Newts and aquatic larvae remain beneath the duckweed. Grass snakes wait at the margins. Voles tunnel through the woven root mattress, while shrews follow their passages in pursuit of worms, beetles, and soft-bodied larvae. Mites and springtails graze among moss stems; ants travel fixed routes along roots and fallen branches. Bees commute between recurring patches of bloom, and cicadas and crickets fill the canopy with their hard, sustained machinery.

Birds occupy the vertical spaces. Blackbirds forage close to the floor. Woodpeckers strike the resonant trunks. Rooks rise and settle communally in the crowns. Owls inherit the aisles after dusk, while bats patrol the clear air between the trees. Most of these inhabitants are heard more often than seen. Their bodies remain hidden in bark, peat, foliage, tunnels, cavities, and water, though their combined activity makes the hollow sound continuously occupied.

Nothing presides over this population. There is no central creature, yet the hollow behaves like one: the trees its columns and reservoirs, fungal filaments and vessels its pipes, insects its moving parts, birds its percussion, frogs its engines, peat its memory. Its inhabitants consume one another, shelter one another, pollinate, excavate, decompose, and rebuild. Death produces no vacancy for long. A fallen limb becomes a moss ridge, a stump becomes a garden, a carcass becomes insects and roots.

Even the round clearing at the center is inhabited by what appears absent from it. Frogs wait beneath the pool’s duckweed. Minute animals work under the moss. Bats cross the open air. Roots extend into it from the surrounding trees, and fungal threads pass through the dark ground below. The clearing is not empty; it is the hollow’s breathing space.

What lives there is therefore a stable multitude: ancient trees and brief-winged insects, hidden fungi, amphibians, burrowers, birds, mosses, and countless decomposers, all replacing themselves without substantially changing the form of their world. No individual lasts, but the arrangement does. The true inhabitant is the process itself—green, wet, sonorous, and old—using innumerable temporary bodies to remain where it is.
