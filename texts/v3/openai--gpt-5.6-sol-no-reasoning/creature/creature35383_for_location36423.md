They are called the Tenders, though there is no sign bearing that name and perhaps no one beyond the basin knows it.

They live in the thickness of the district.

The visible rooms above the workshops—the swept platforms, the cups drying upside down, the bedding rolled into niches—are only stations where they briefly emerge. Their true dwellings occupy the doubled walls, abandoned flues, bridge passages between buildings, and upper portions of chambers whose floors have sunk beyond use. A door that appears to open into a boiler house may turn immediately upward into a narrow stair. A cupboard may conceal a warm corridor threaded between steam pipes. Behind tiled cisterns are dry ledges, sleeping shelves, kitchens no wider than a person’s outstretched arms, and listening rooms where the brick carries news from distant pumps.

The Tenders are human in outline, but the basin has impressed itself upon them. They are slight, smooth-moving people with long hands hardened by heat and alkaline water. Their skin is pale where clothing covers it and stained at the extremities: rust at the fingertips, green-black mineral shadows around the nails, chalk-white bands across the wrists. Many have old scald patterns on their arms, glossy as fired glaze. Hair is kept short or wrapped close beneath oiled cloth. Loose strands are dangerous near belts, vents, and flywheels.

They go barefoot on interior routes, feeling vibration through soles toughened like leather. Outside, they wear high wooden pattens or narrow boots with metal ribs, made to enter warm standing water without sinking into the mud below. Their clothes are pieced together from waxed canvas, faded linen, rubberized aprons, and fine copper mesh. Every garment has ties rather than buttons. Buttons fall into machinery. Every belt carries chalk, a valve key, folded cloth, wax, and a small ceramic whistle that almost no one uses.

Their eyes seem large in the dimness. It is not that they see unusually well, but that they look by waiting. Steam shifts. Reflections rearrange themselves. A gauge needle quivers. Their most important sense is pressure.

Children learn the district first through the palm. They are taught to lay a hand against pipes and distinguish clean flow from sediment, steam from vapor lock, a working pump from one merely shuddering in sympathy. Later they learn through the feet: which tremors belong to the deep pistons, which to a settling vault, which to the central reservoir beginning to lift. The most experienced can press an ear to wet brick and name the underground gate that has opened three courtyards away.

They do not count time by hours. Their days are divided by pump sequences, ventings, and the recurring silence after a swelling of the reservoir. A meeting may be set for “after the seventh western blow,” a meal kept warm “until the lower turbine descends,” a child expected home “before the eastern roofs exhale.” Clocks exist, but their faces fog, their springs corrode, and no two remain in agreement. Pressure is more reliable.

The Tenders speak little while working. The district overwhelms ordinary conversation, and raised voices waste breath in the hot air. Instead they use shutters, bells, hammers, and changes in flow. One tap on a rail asks whether a passage is clear. Two slow taps warn of heat. Three irregular taps mean that brick has moved. Handbells behind sealed gates do not summon people; they request adjustments from distant stations. An answer may come as a brief dimming of lamps, a slowing belt, or the sudden gulp of water vanishing down a drain.

Their spoken language survives in the rooms above the workshops, where fans turn and the walls are padded with old cloth. It is quiet and exact. They possess many words for water according to where it has been: water newly fallen, water held in brick, water condensed from steam, water risen from below, water that has passed through roots, water that carries metal, water that moves opposite the slope. They rarely use one word to mean all of it.

There are few of them, fewer than the abundance of cups and beds suggests. The basin was built for a larger population. Empty places are maintained anyway. A sleeping niche may be aired for someone dead generations ago. A cup may remain on its rack because it belongs to a station rather than a person. The Tenders do not readily discard assigned objects. In a settlement that continually sinks, possession is less important than position: this wrench belongs by this valve; this bowl beneath this leak; this cloth over this vent. People, too, are identified by their stations and routes.

They eat what the district can grow without animals.

The isolated trees provide waxy fruit with dense, almost translucent flesh. Root beds in abandoned laundries yield pale tubers, water greens, and mats of edible fern. Fungi are cultivated in warm brick galleries on compressed vegetable fiber. Mineral yeast gathers as a cream-colored skin in certain ceramic troughs and is skimmed, pressed, and baked into sour cakes on boiler plates. They make bitter infusions from young leaves and a dark syrup from fruit left to condense in copper pans.

Nothing is pollinated by wing or mouth. The Tenders carry pollen themselves with soft brushes, or direct faint puffs of compressed air through flowering rooms. Some plants no longer open unless they feel the scheduled tremor of a nearby pump. Others release seed only when struck by warm rain. Gardens are therefore treated as extensions of the machinery: pruned, timed, pressured, and listened to.

Yet the vegetation is not entirely theirs.

The plants in the surrendered lower levels move too deliberately. Their roots tighten before pressure gauges change. Vines withdraw from rising water before warning bells are struck. Pale growths inside drain mouths orient toward faults in buried pipes and sometimes seal them with fibrous knots. The Tenders cultivate these responses without pretending to understand them. A certain tree may be connected by root to half a district. A fern bed may serve as an alarm. Thin vines are trained across brick fractures because they recoil when the masonry begins to shift.

The Tenders refer to such plants as the Quick, distinguishing them from ordinary greenery. They do not call the Quick alive, since in their understanding all roots are alive. They call them attentive.

No animals survive in the basin because the district excludes them. Whether this is caused by the water, the heat, the deep reservoir, or some old intervention is unknown. Birds approaching through the vapor turn aside. Insects brought in with fruit become still within minutes, though the fruit remains wholesome. Rats once existed in the oldest accounts, but those records end with descriptions of an upward flood and a season of white steam. The Tenders do not mourn animals personally. Most have never seen one. They know them as impossible shapes in inherited drawings: bodies that carried their own warmth, mouths that moved without speech, creatures that crossed ground without first testing its pressure.

Children find this difficult to believe.

They imagine birds as detached leaves and fish as roots that have learned to swim. The idea of insects troubles them most: thousands of tiny independent lives occupying the air seems indecently noisy.

The central reservoir governs them, though they do not worship it in any simple sense. They maintain its wall with ceremonial care because every brick there has been touched by generations. Dates and flood lines are preserved even when surrounding mortar is replaced. Before descending to a gantry mechanism, a worker places both wet hands on the polished parapet. This is partly custom and partly measurement: the warmth, vibration, and faint pull of the water can be felt through the brick.

No Tenden­der claims to know what lies under the reservoir.

Their diagrams show chains descending farther than any measured shaft. The gantry arms do not draw water upward. They restrain something, or maintain a balance, or repeatedly take up slack caused by the basin’s slow descent. The oldest chains are not iron. When exposed during rare maintenance, they appear dull and pale, without rust, and are warm even when submerged.

When the black surface swells at its center, the Tenders emerge all at once.

Then they can be seen in the streets: narrow figures passing beneath awnings, splashing through reflected green light, climbing ladders and leaning their full weight on wheel valves. They move in groups whose coordination resembles habit more than command. Older workers take the listening stations. The strongest attend the sluices. Children pull condensation cloths from the lower pipes and carry warning tiles to upper rooms. Those trained in rootwork hurry to the grates, where they speak softly and cut or bind the Quick before its tightening tears masonry apart.

The shutters open not from alarm but necessity. Each window becomes an eye on a gauge, a signal point, or an escape into the level above.

As water rises backward through the streets, the Tenders chalk its height wherever they stand. The mark is dated later, once the danger has passed. If the water reaches an old line, they draw the new one through it rather than above it, making a knot in the record. Some interior walls are covered with these intersections. At a distance they resemble devotional borders; close up, they are the district’s memory of near failures.

During the swelling, certain Tenders descend.

They enter sealed gates at the reservoir wall and climb down through galleries already half surrendered to the mud. They carry no flame. Light is conducted to them through green glass rods or reflected down wet shafts by hinged mirrors. Their task is to free counterweights, clear root masses from relief channels, and listen at the buried tanks for what they call the Returning Knock.

The knock does not belong to any pump.

It rises through the flooded foundations: one impact, then another after an interval too long to be an echo. Sometimes it seems directly below. Sometimes it moves around the basin. The old records disagree on whether it is a warning, a demand, or merely the sound of deeper structures settling beneath the known ones.

A Tenden­der who hears it does not answer.

That is the first rule taught to every child, before valves, before pressure marks, before the names of water: if something knocks from beneath the ordinary depth, keep both hands away from the wall.

The second rule is never to let all three gantries stop together.

Their entire society is arranged around that prohibition. Meals, sleep, births, repairs, mourning, and instruction yield to the turning of the gantries. At least one arm must always be rising or falling. If a chain jams, workers cross the district even through scalding water. If a counterweight fractures, furniture, bricks, tanks, and sometimes whole sections of roof are dismantled to replace its mass. The machines are not sacred, but continuity is.

Death is handled without burial. There is no stable earth, and nothing animal may be given to the water. The dead are dried slowly in the heat above the boilers, wrapped in mineral cloth, and placed in sealed wall cavities with their tools. A small ceramic plate records their station and the pump sequence during which they died. Over time the district grows literally inhabited by its former keepers. Walls become thicker. Corridors narrow. The dead help hold up the rooms of the living.

Perhaps that is why the settlement feels occupied even when no one can be seen.

There are inhabitants in the roofs, inhabitants behind the tiles, inhabitants sealed inside buttresses. There are roots moving through their old routes and machines repeating rhythms learned under vanished hands. There are chalk calculations renewed by descendants who no longer know the original units. There may be something below the reservoir for which the entire district is only a valve.

Still, the Tenders do not think of themselves as prisoners or survivors. To them, the basin is not a ruin. It is a functioning place with some rooms above water and some rooms below, some residents breathing and some built into the walls. Subsidence is not catastrophe but direction. The district moves downward; therefore life moves upward. A doorway is raised. A stair is cut. A bed is placed in a former window. The abandoned ground floor becomes a cistern, then a root chamber, then a depth remembered only by sound.

After the reservoir subsides, the Tenders disappear quickly. They close shutters, erase temporary signals, rinse tools in designated troughs, and carry wet clothes into the heated passages. Someone records the highest water line. Someone apportions the new rust. Children gather fallen petals before they clog the smaller drains. In a narrow room, bowls are filled with hot fern broth. The workers from below return last, silent and mud-dark to the shoulders.

One of them may have heard the knock.

No one asks until the pumps have slowed and the district reaches its brief interval of quiet. Then the oldest listener turns an enamel cup upright and sets it on the table. If the returning worker drinks, there was only settling brick.

If they leave the cup untouched, a wall will be opened before morning, another hidden passage brought into use, another level abandoned.

By the time the next valve opens, the Tenders will already have adjusted.
