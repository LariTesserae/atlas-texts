The inhabitants of this place are themselves liminal, adaptive creatures:

Membrane-woven beings that are part sensor, part fluid, part mechanism. They do not have discrete bodies so much as shifting competencies — some are primarily transport, others primarily transformation. Their boundaries constantly negotiate between solid and liquid states.

When they move, it's less locomotion and more migration: spreading like capillary networks, condensing into temporary clusters of intense activity, then dispersing again. Their communication is chemical and electrical, happening through minute pressure changes and ion exchanges.

Some are microscopic — single nodes that filter and translate environmental information. Others are larger, composed of intricate interconnected segments that can reconfigure themselves based on current metabolic needs. They have no fixed hierarchy, only momentary functional roles.

Their "intelligence" is distributed, emergent — not centralized but arising from constant negotiation between local systems. They maintain the wetland's delicate balance, sensing minute changes and responding with incredible granular precision.

Occasionally, they coalesce into more complex forms: temporary assemblages that might look like maintenance creatures, sensing creatures, reproductive creatures. But these are always provisional, always in flux.

They are neither born nor do they die in any traditional sense. They transform, integrate, disperse — their identity is process, not persistence.
