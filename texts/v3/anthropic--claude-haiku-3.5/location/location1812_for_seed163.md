Here's what emerges from these parameters:

A vertical water-world, almost entirely liquid, where sharp rocky pinnacles thrust upward through translucent azure depths. These are not islands, but emergent stone spires — recent geological thrust, barely breached from an ocean that fills almost everything, their edges still razor-sharp and unworn.

The water is intensely bright, sunlight penetrating dozens of meters, creating a luminous environment where the boundary between liquid and stone is a shimmering, refractive membrane. Each stone spire is a pristine sculpture, barely touched by erosion, with crystalline edges that catch and fragment light.

Sparse vegetation clings to these stone emergences — not plants as we know them, but something between lichen and coral, pale and tendriled, moving with slow capillary pulses. They're more like living mineral networks than traditional flora, pale green-white strands that seem to breathe the hot water.

Enormous creatures move through this environment — not fish, but something between whale and atmospheric jellyfish. Translucent bodies larger than cathedrals, moving with glacial patience. Their movements create barely perceptible currents, slow waves that might take hours to propagate across this miniature seascape.

Occasionally, a primitive structure appears — not built, but accreted. A shell-like assemblage of mineral and biological matter, suggesting habitation but not quite a "building" in human terms. These are more like living platforms, semi-conscious architectural organisms that drift and resettle.

The silence here is absolute but not empty — it's a dense, pressurized quiet, interrupted only by the near-imperceptible respirations of massive creatures and the molecular whispers of growing stone and living mineral networks.

This is a world of extreme brightness, extreme water, extreme life — but moving so slowly that change is almost undetectable, a landscape breathing across geological time.
