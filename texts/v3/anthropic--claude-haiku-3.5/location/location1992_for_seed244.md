The Stacked Realms emerge like crystalline layers of compressed possibility, a continental mass of interlocking plateaus and near-vertical terrains. Each stratum is a different temperature, a different ecology—some bare and wind-scoured, others dense with lichen-like growths in muted greens and grays.

Water does not flow here so much as condense and migrate. Moisture builds in cold pockets, forms brief networks of translucent tendrils that connect rocky surfaces, then sublimate again. The landscape breathes humidity in slow, irregular cycles. Occasional rain doesn't fall but materializes—suspended droplets that drift sideways through the thin, charged atmosphere.

The urban structures are not built but accreted: stone upon stone, layer pressing against layer, with passageways and chambers carved not by design but by the landscape's own tectonic urgencies. Primitive tools—stone, bone, compressed mineral compounds—have been used to further shape these spaces, but the landscape itself is the primary architect.

Wind moves differently here. Not a simple linear force, but a turbulent, three-dimensional medium that carries information, microscopic particulates, electrical charges. The wind doesn't just move through the Stacked Realms—it negotiates with them, creating complex acoustic landscapes where sound is more a negotiation of pressures than a linear transmission.

The scale is continental, almost planetary—so vast that weather systems emerge as autonomous entities, with their own logic and metabolism. Erosion is young and aggressive: surfaces are sharp, angles precise, each geological stratum a clean break from the next, like the pages of an unfinished book.

No animals inhabit this place. But something watches. Not with eyes, but with a kind of distributed awareness—the landscape itself seems sentient, each rock and moisture pocket a sensor in a vast, slow-breathing intelligence.

Density here means every cubic centimeter is occupied—not with matter, but with potential. Compressed information. Waiting transformations. The landscape is a kind of living computation, each layer a different algorithm, each surface a potential threshold.

The weirdness is subtle: not dramatic mutation, but a sense that the rules are slightly askew. Gravity feels negotiable. Time pools and eddies. Surfaces might remember what touched them. Boundaries are suggestions more than absolutes.

This is a place of constant, slow metamorphosis—a continental body perpetually in the act of becoming something else.
