The Lithic Expanse emerges as a vast, muted terrain of compressed mineral strata and attenuated industrial remnants. Here, low-lying plains of compressed salt and pale mineral sediments stretch across a dimly illuminated horizon, creating a landscape that feels both ancient and suspended.

Occasional skeletal structures—perhaps once factories or processing stations—rise like fragmented bones from the mineral ground. These are not buildings in any conventional sense, but industrial artifacts: rusted metal frames, partial gantries, segments of pipelines that emerge at oblique angles, weathered by countless slow cycles of ambient erosion. They stand sparsely distributed, their geometries suggesting some long-forgotten extractive purpose.

The atmosphere is warm but not oppressive, with a sense of contained stillness. Very little moves. What vegetation exists appears as rare, wire-thin growths—more like mineral tendrils or crystalline filaments than recognizable plants. They emerge from salt-crusted ground in minimal clusters, pale and translucent, absorbing whatever minimal moisture exists through complex root-like networks.

Fauna is essentially theoretical. Occasionally, something shifts at the extreme periphery of perception—a minimal rustling, a slight electromagnetic disturbance, a momentary refraction of dim light. But these are more like environmental artifacts than creatures.

The landscape feels densely occupied, but not by life as commonly understood. Every microscopic layer seems saturated with compressed information: geological memory, industrial residue, electromagnetic traces. The ground itself feels like a complex archive, each mineral stratum holding compressed histories of transformation.

Industrial echoes persist—not as active machinery, but as lingering electromagnetic signatures. Faint electrical potentials drift through the mineral substrate, suggesting past mechanized processes that have been gradually absorbed into the landscape's slow metabolism.

Sound is essentially theoretical. Perhaps a very occasional low-frequency resonance, more felt than heard—a distant industrial heartbeat, a mineral frequency, a geological sigh.

The entire expanse feels like a complex mechanism in extremely slow motion, a landscape that breathes across geological time scales, where change happens so gradually it's almost indistinguishable from stasis.
