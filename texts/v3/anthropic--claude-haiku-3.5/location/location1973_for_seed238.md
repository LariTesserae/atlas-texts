A humid grove, barely larger than a cathedral's footprint, emerges from a recent geological upheaval. The terrain is soft and barely formed, with surfaces that seem almost tentative — fresh stone eruptions still wearing sharp edges, not yet softened by time or weathering.

Water permeates everything. Not rivers or lakes, but a persistent moisture that seems to breathe through the landscape. Rocks weep continuously, not with dramatic cascades but with constant, quiet seepage. Surfaces glisten. The atmosphere itself feels condensed, like breathing inside a warm, damp lung.

Vegetation crowds every possible surface. Moss-like growths in impossible chartreuse and deep emerald press against stone, draping and layering in dense, intricate patterns. Strange plant-forms that blur the line between fungus and flora spread in complex networks. Some seem to pulse very slowly, expanding millimeter by millimeter across stone surfaces.

The heat is oppressive but not dry — a wet heat that clings, that feels almost alive. Everything seems on the verge of transformation: stones looking momentarily soft, plants that might be breathing, surfaces that could be membrane-like.

No animals inhabit this space. But there's a sense of potential animation, of something just about to become. The silence is not empty, but pregnant — filled with microscopic shifts and slow, patient metabolisms.

The light is dim and filtered, creating a sense of eternal twilight. Surfaces gleam not from brightness, but from their own internal moisture and strange luminescent qualities.

This is a place of emergence, of something very new and very ancient simultaneously — a landscape that feels like a first breath, suspended in a moment of becoming.
