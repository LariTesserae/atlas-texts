Here's what emerges from those parameters:

A vertical world of wet stone and sudden movement. Razor-edged peaks slice through a condensing atmosphere, where water doesn't fall so much as hover — suspended between liquid and mist, clinging to obsidian-like rock faces that seem momentarily frozen, then suddenly shift.

The elevation is extreme: kilometers of near-vertical stone landscape, with surfaces that flow like slow liquid yet maintain impossible angles. These are not mountains as we know them, but living geological structures that breathe and reconfigure. Water seeps constantly, not as streams but as a kind of ambient moisture that emerges from stone pores, creating temporary cascades and vanishing networks.

Creatures move through this environment with impossible agility. They're not quite fauna as we'd recognize — more like quick-silver motion-beings, part animal, part energy pattern. Some skitter across rock surfaces leaving momentary luminescent trails. Others pulse and merge with the stone itself, becoming indistinguishable from the landscape's constant reconfiguration.

No plants exist here. Instead, mineral structures pulse with a kind of proto-biological energy — crystalline formations that respond to temperature and moisture, expanding and contracting like living tissue. The stone itself seems sentient, with a slow metabolism measured in geological time yet experiencing rapid, turbulent changes.

Scattered throughout are artifact-remnants: simple stone tools, fragments of primitive constructions that look both ancient and newly formed. They might have been created by the motion-creatures, or by some previous inhabitation, or might be spontaneously generated by the landscape's own strange logic.

The light is remarkably bright — not sunlight, but an ambient luminescence that seems to emerge from the stone and creatures themselves. It creates no shadows, instead suffusing everything with a sense of constant, slightly unsettling revelation.

Sound is sparse but intense: occasional high-frequency vibrations, like distant whale songs translated through stone, punctuated by the liquid-sharp sounds of the landscape's constant micro-transformations.

This is a world of perpetual becoming — not stable, not chaotic, but in a state of continuous negotiation between form and formlessness.
