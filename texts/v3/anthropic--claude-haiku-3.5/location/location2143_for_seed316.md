In the vast vertical realm of the Chlorophyll Towers, living architecture rises from raw, nascent stone. These are not buildings, but grown structures: immense plant-forms that emerge from barely-formed geological strata, stretching impossibly upward through layers of near-darkness.

The towers pulse with a slow, almost imperceptible rhythm — breathing, growing, shifting millimeters per decade. They are dense woven masses of vegetation so thick that individual plants cannot be distinguished, more like living tissue than discrete organisms. Pale luminescent fronds curl and intertwine, creating intricate structural networks that are simultaneously root, wall, corridor, and circulatory system.

Moisture condenses constantly within these structures. Not rain, exactly, but a perpetual breathing of liquid — droplets forming, sliding, absorbing, reforming. The water moves like a circulatory system through the plant-architecture, creating micro-climates, sustaining the immense living construct.

Occasional sonic pulses emerge from deep within the towers — low frequency vibrations that suggest communication or structural adjustment. These sounds are not mechanical, but organic: the groan of growth, the whisper of cellular expansion.

The temperature is consistently warm, encouraging constant growth. But this is not a tropical warmth — instead, a steady, patient heat that allows transformation to occur at geological time scales. No animals inhabit this space; the towers are self-contained, self-generating ecosystems that require no external fauna.

Primitive technologies are embedded within the living structure — tendrils that function like sensors, membranes that capture and redirect energy, organic scaffolding that suggests rudimentary tool-like capabilities. But these are not separate from the plant-mass; they are integral, grown rather than constructed.

The entire environment feels like a single, breathing entity — not a landscape, but a living being in the process of becoming.
