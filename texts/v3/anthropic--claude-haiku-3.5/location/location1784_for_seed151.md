The wetlands breathe. 

Not a traditional marsh, but a strange industrial ecosystem where water permeates everything — seeping from metallic ground-skins, condensing on rusted machinery, pooling in mechanical hollows. The landscape feels like a living hydraulic system, warm and perpetually moist.

Low-lying terrain stretches in muted grays and oxidized browns, punctuated by skeletal industrial structures that rise like mechanical bones. These aren't clean factories, but weathered assemblages — half-organic, half-engineered — where pipes and conduits twist like circulatory systems through the wet environment. Some structures leak constantly, creating tiny cascading water networks that feed into larger channels.

The sparse vegetation isn't botanical in traditional sense. Instead, lichen-like growths cling to machine surfaces, pale green-gray organisms that thrive in the constant moisture and industrial heat. They spread in delicate networks, almost like circuit diagrams across metal surfaces.

Fauna move constantly — not animals, but something between mechanical and biological. Insectoid forms skitter between water pools, their exoskeletons part chitin, part stamped metal. They click and whir, generating loud mechanical sounds that pulse through the landscape like a heartbeat. Their movements suggest complex ecological interactions, predation happening through technological metaphors.

Water is everywhere but never pure — always mixed with industrial residue, always in motion. Condensation clouds hover just above ground level, creating a dim, humid atmosphere where visibility comes in pulses and layers.

The entire space feels like a living mechanism — not a dead industrial zone, but an active, breathing ecosystem where technology and organic processes have become indistinguishable. Heat radiates from the ground. Mechanical rhythms pulse. Water circulates. Something is always happening.
