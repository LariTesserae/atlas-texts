Here's what emerges from these parameters:

A high plateau of condensing stone, where water seems to breathe through the landscape rather than flow. The rock itself appears almost porous, pale ochre and ivory-white, with surfaces that constantly glisten and weep moisture. These are young mountains, recently thrust upward, with sharp angular edges still holding the memory of their geological birth—crisp fracture lines, unweathered surfaces, planes that intersect with geometric precision.

Bright light saturates everything, so intense that surfaces gleam and blur at their edges. The brightness creates a kind of vibrating whiteness, where stone and atmospheric moisture merge into a luminous membrane. Occasional sparse vegetation clings to shallow mineral depressions—pale lichen, thin grasses the color of bone, small succulents that seem more mineral than plant.

The landscape breathes with water. Not rivers or lakes, but a constant ambient moisture: rocks that sweat, surfaces that condense, thin veils of mist that form and dissolve in moments. The stone itself seems almost alive, exhaling and inhaling atmospheric water in slow, patient cycles.

Sound dominates—not from creatures, but from the landscape's own voice. A constant low humming, like stone singing to itself. Water droplets striking rock surfaces create a percussive rhythm. Wind moving across the angular plateau creates whistling tones that rise and fall with glacial patience.

Occasional scattered remnants suggest previous inhabitation—a single standing stone with faint markings, a partial wall whose purpose is no longer legible, fragments that hint at prior presence without explaining anything.

The scale is expansive: you can see far, but what you see is complexity rather than distance. Intricate mineral surfaces, water-breathing stone, light that transforms everything it touches.

This is a landscape of emergence, of something slowly becoming—not yet settled, still in its initial formations, bright and wet and strange.
