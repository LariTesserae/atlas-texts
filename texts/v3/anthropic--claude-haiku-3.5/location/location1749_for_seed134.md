Here's what emerges:

A vertical world, almost a throat of stone and moisture. Imagine a narrow stone chimney, perhaps 30 meters tall but only 2-3 meters wide at its widest points, carved by water but not yet smoothed — sharp-edged, recent, with raw stone surfaces that look freshly torn.

Water doesn't flow here so much as weep and condense. The stone sweats constantly. Moss-like growths in deep blue-green collect moisture, creating tiny hanging gardens that drip rhythmically. The stone itself seems almost alive — porous, breathing, absorbing and releasing water in slow pulses.

Tiny creatures inhabit this vertical ecosystem: translucent arthropods with multiple leg-clusters that grip stone surfaces, color-shifting to match wet rock and lichen. They move with precise, flickering motions, hunting microscopic prey that lives within stone pockets.

The temperature is intense — not burning, but persistently hot. This creates a constant micro-climate of rising humid air, condensation, slow biological processes. Water doesn't pool, but transforms: becoming mist, then liquid, then absorbed, then mist again.

No human structures interrupt this space. It feels primordial — a pocket of early-world formation, a prototype environment where complex systems are just beginning to interact and stabilize.

The soundscape is hushed: occasional droplet-strikes, minute creature movements, the soft breathing of stone and moisture.

An ecosystem in miniature, dense with life yet contained, fragile, intricate — a world within a world.
