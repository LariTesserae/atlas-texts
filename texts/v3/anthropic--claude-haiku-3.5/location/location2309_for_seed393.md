In the Fabricated Wastes, everything is constructed, yet nothing seems purposeful. The landscape stretches—a sparse, warm expanse of manufactured terrain that feels simultaneously ancient and newborn.

Geometric platforms of compressed mineral compounds rise in irregular terraces, their edges sharp and unweathered. Each platform is a different shade of pale—bone-white, ash-gray, faint ochre—interconnected by narrow bridges of woven metallic mesh that flex almost imperceptibly in slow, patient oscillations. These bridges connect platforms that seem randomly placed, as if assembled by a logic just beyond comprehension.

No living creatures move here. No wind disturbs the stillness. The light is even, casting no significant shadows, creating a uniform luminescence that makes distance difficult to gauge. Occasional crystalline structures—not quite buildings, not quite machines—punctuate the landscape. They're hand-crafted from simple metals and mineral composites, their surfaces etched with minimal functional patterns.

Sparse vegetation clings to platform edges: lichen-like growths in muted silvers and pale greens, more mineral than organic, spreading in thin, geometric patterns. They don't seem to breathe or grow so much as slowly reconfigure themselves across decades.

The entire environment feels like a prolonged experiment, or the remnant of a civilization's initial exploration—a prototype landscape where function has been prioritized over beauty, where construction is both the purpose and the result.

Occasionally, a distant structure will emit a low, almost subliminal tone—not quite a sound, more a vibration that suggests communication or maintenance, though no clear source is visible.

The Fabricated Wastes do not invite; they simply exist, meticulously assembled yet fundamentally alien.
