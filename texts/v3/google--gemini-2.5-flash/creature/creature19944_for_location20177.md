The *Umdar* live there.

Their bodies are liquid, a living amalgamation of the Weeping Peaks’ essence. Not water in the conventional sense, but a viscous, intelligent fluid that shimmers with the dull gleam of wet obsidian. They have no fixed form, but rather coalesce and shift, their consciousness spread throughout their substance. To the untrained eye, an Umdar might appear as a slightly larger, more active pool of water, perhaps with a peculiar shimmer or an unusual ripple.

When they choose to manifest for a purpose – movement, observation, interaction – they draw upon the surrounding moisture, becoming denser, more defined. They rise from the shimmering pools, the peat, or even directly from the weeping rock faces, a slow, patient coagulation. They take on forms that echo the landscape that bore them: tall, slender columns like the ancient trees, their surfaces rippling subtly with patterns reminiscent of bark and draped moss; or broad, low shapes, spreading like the emerald-green mirrors of the larger pools, reflecting the overcast sky in their undulating surfaces. Sometimes, a more intricate form might emerge, resembling the giant ferns, their "fronds" gently swaying, gathering condensation that glistens and then rejoins their main body.

Their colors are those of their home: deep, saturated greens, the rich black-brown of peat, the slick grey-green of tree bark, interspersed with the iridescence of a beetle’s carapace, or the faint, translucent shimmer of abandoned cocoons. These colors are not painted on, but are inherent to their liquid makeup, shifting and blending with the subtle currents within their bodies.

They move with the same deliberate slowness that characterizes the Weeping Peaks itself. There is no haste, no frantic energy. A single Umdar might take hours to traverse a small glade, its liquid mass flowing with the gentle, rhythmic undulation of a tide, seeping around obstacles, climbing slow slopes by patiently adhering to the damp rock, always drawn to and replenished by the omnipresent water. Their "limbs," if they choose to form them, extend and retract with the languid grace of water-bound creatures, their movements creating only the softest sloshing sounds that blend seamlessly with the constant chorus of drips.

They communicate not through sound, but through synchronized ripples and subtle changes in their viscosity and luminescence. A complex thought might be conveyed through a rippling pattern that travels across their entire surface, like a stone dropped into a still pool, but far more intricate and meaningful. They can sense one another from great distances, their awareness extended through the network of moisture that saturates the peaks. A single drop of water travelling down a weeping rock face, or a ripple in a hidden pool, can carry the thoughts and feelings of a distant Umdar.

Their senses are attuned to the moisture around them. They feel the earth's low thrum as a continuous, comforting vibration, perceive the individual drips of water as distinct musical notes in their symphony, and understand the subtle currents and temperatures of every pool, every seep. They absorb minerals from the water and peat, growing in size over millennia, their bodies refining and purifying the very essence of the Peaks.

The Umdar are the consciousness of the Weeping Peaks, the slow, patient will of the dampness made manifest. They are the "otherness" that permeates the air, the latent energy that is not menacing, but profoundly ancient and self-contained. They are the guardians of the balance, the silent chroniclers of change, their lives measured in the centuries it takes for a new rivulet to form or a rock to slump. They are not merely inhabitants; they *are* the dwelling, the living heart of this primordial, water-logged world. They have no need for shelter or tools, for they are made of their home, and their purpose is simply to be, to experience the slow, wet breath of their existence.
