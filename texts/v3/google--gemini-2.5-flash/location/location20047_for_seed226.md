The air hums with a heavy, pregnant chill, a chill not sharp or biting, but a deep, constant cool that seeps into the bones and clings like mist. Here, high above the world, the clouds often rest as much as they move, snagging on the nascent peaks. These emerging mountains are not jagged giants but rather vast, swelling domes and elongated ridges, their surfaces still surprisingly smooth, as if the earth’s crust is only just beginning to wrinkle and fold. The rock is a uniform, dark grey, almost purple in the muted light, and remarkably unblemished by the usual scars of wind and water. It glistens perpetually, not from rain – though rain is frequent, a soft, soaking drizzle that seems to melt down from the low, dense sky – but from an internal dampness. Moisture weeps from every pore, condensing on the surfaces, collecting in shallow, wide depressions that form mirroring pools on the nascent rock. The ground is often boggy, a squelching give beneathfoot, a constant reminder of the ever-present water just below the thin topsoil.

Despite this embryonic geology, life has taken hold with an astonishing, almost frantic vigor. The vegetation is lush, a vibrant, almost aggressive green that blankets the rising forms. It’s not the towering forests of old growth, but rather a profusion of thick, low-lying mosses, broad-leafed ferns that unfurl like slow motion explosions, and squat, gnarled shrubs with leaves of an almost waxy luminescence. These plants grow in dense, tangled mats, clinging to the damp rock faces and forming soft, springy carpets on the boggy flats. They thrive on the ever-present moisture and the cool, steady air, a verdant uprising on a landscape still finding its contours. In places, their growth is so thick it seems to defy gravity, clambering up steep slopes, creating soft, undulating ripples on the bare stone.

This place, despite its raw, untamed geology and profuse plant life, feels contained. The scale is intimate, almost like a vast, irregular chamber. One can walk from one edge to another and feel the encompassing presence of these rounded, rising forms. Yet, within this intimate scale, there's a surprising emptiness. While the vegetation is dense, it creates a sense of broad, uncluttered spaces between the prominent features. The broad, low domes of rock rise out of the boggy plains, separated by stretches of open, moss-covered ground, creating a landscape that feels spacious despite its defined boundaries. It's an arena, a crucible.

And within this crucible, something is being built. Not in defiance of the nascent landscape, but almost in concert with it. Across the broad, mossy plains, and clinging to the gentler slopes, are structures. These aren't the chaotic, organic clusters of simple settlements, but something more organized, more deliberate, hinting at an urban intention. They are low, broad constructions, built primarily from the same dark, indigenous stone, unpolished and rough-hewn, but carefully fitted together. The architecture is sturdy, almost monolithic, with wide, sweeping walls and flat roofs, blending into the muted tones of the landscape. They seem to rise organically from the ground, as if the raw material of the earth has been coaxed into new forms. There’s a distinct lack of sharp angles; everything is rounded, curvilinear, mirroring the underlying geology. Pathways, paved with flat, interlocking stones, snake between these buildings, connecting them in a sprawling, yet coherent network. The overall impression is of a grounded, patient construction, as if the builders understand the slow, fundamental processes of the land itself.

The technology here is primal, almost indistinguishable from nature itself. Tools are simple, hefty things of chipped stone and hardened wood. There might be some early, unrefined metals, but their use is rare, almost ceremonial. The construction of these large structures speaks of immense, collective effort, of manual labor on a scale that beggars belief. There are no machines, no complex pulleys, just the grunting, straining exertion of bodies and the ingenious application of levers and raw force. Even the water management, which must be a constant concern in this perpetually damp environment, seems to be handled through cleverly angled channels in the rock, diverting the constant weepage into purposeful streams that flow around and through the settlements.

A profound, almost deafening silence ought to reign here, given the sparse fauna. Indeed, animals are rare, fleeting shadows in the mist, or the rustle of something unseen in the dense undergrowth. But the landscape is far from quiet. A deep, pervasive hum vibrates through the air, a constant sound that seems to emanate from the very earth itself. It’s hard to place, a low thrumming that could be the slow, deep currents of subterranean water, or the subtle shifting of the nascent rock, still finding its solid form. Overlaying this hum are other sounds: the constant, soft drip and gurgle of unseen water, the steady, patient sigh of the wind through dense mosses, and, most prominently, the rhythmic thud and scrape of human activity. The sounds of heavy stone being moved, of rudimentary tools striking rock, of many voices, low and intoning, almost chanting in unison. It’s a loud place, not with sharp, jarring noises, but with a pervasive, resonant thrum of life and labor.

And yet, despite the industry, despite the emerging urban forms, there’s an unusual stillness to the place. Change is slow, almost imperceptible. The sun, when it breaks through the enduring cloud cover, casts a diffused, even light, illuminating the landscape with a soft, uniform glow that never feels harsh or brilliant. There are no dramatic storms, no sudden shifts. The cycles are long, measured in geological time, in the slow swell of the land, the patient creep of the moss, the deliberate construction of the settlements. Even the weirdness of the place is subtle. It’s in the way the land weeps, not from rainfall but from an internal source, as if the earth itself is perpetually damp. It’s in the sheer, patient scale of the primitive construction, the sense of a grand design being brought forth with the simplest of means. And it’s in the pervasive, almost sentient hum that defines the very atmosphere, a constant, resonant vibration that suggests a living, breathing world, still in the very earliest stages of its grand unfolding. Here, creation is not an explosion; it is a long, drawn-out exhalation.
