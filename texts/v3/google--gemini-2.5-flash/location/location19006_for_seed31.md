The air itself weeps. Not rain, not mist, but a pervasive, almost viscous dampness that clings to everything, a constant, gentle exhalation from the very stones. This is a high place, a sprawling plateau or perhaps the shoulder of a colossal, broken mountain range, where the world is still in the sharp, unblunted throes of birth. Everywhere, angular forms jut forth – raw, jagged outcroppings of a pale, almost phosphorescent rock, their faces scored with fresh fractures. There's no smooth erosion here, no ancient, wind-worn curves. Instead, edges are keen, points needle-sharp, as if some gargantuan hand had recently cleaved the earth apart and left the pieces to settle.

The sheer scale of it is overwhelming, an expanse that stretches beyond any immediate horizon. One looks out over a vast, undulating tapestry of these stark, pale formations, punctuated by the startling vibrancy of the vegetation. Despite the raw newness of the stone, life has found purchase in a surprisingly lush, yet sparse, manner. Deep emerald mosses cling to every crevice, creating soft, velvety patches that contrast sharply with the severe rock. They shimmer with the perpetual damp, their color intensified by the constant moisture. There are no great forests here, no tangled jungles. Instead, the plants grow in distinct, almost cultivated clumps – broad-leafed ferns unfurl in perfect, unfurling spirals from sudden fissures, their fronds glistening with condensed water droplets. Strange, succulent-like plants with thick, waxy leaves gather in shallow depressions, their forms echoing the angularity of the rocks around them. The ground beneath is not soil, but a fine, gritty detritus of pulverized rock, constantly damp, almost bog-like in places, where the water table seems to be just beneath the surface. Small, shallow pools of impossibly clear water collect in natural basins, reflecting the pale, diffused light of the sky.

The overall impression is one of immense, quiet warmth. The air is muggy, still, heavy with the scent of wet stone and blooming, earthy moss. There is no wind to speak of, no rustle of leaves, no breath of atmosphere. The silence is profound, almost oppressive in its completeness. The only sound, if one strains to hear it, might be the almost imperceptible drip of water from a weeping overhang, a slow, rhythmic *plink* echoing in the vastness. Everything is static. The water in the pools is perfectly still, the leaves of the plants unmoving. Time here seems to have paused, a moment caught and held in amber.

Against this backdrop of elemental rawness, a nascent urbanity has taken root, not as a sprawling city, but as a scattering of coherent, deliberate structures. These are not built *into* the landscape, but *from* it, using the readily available, freshly-fractured rock. Low, broad buildings of perfectly cut, rectangular blocks rise from the high ground, their pale material perfectly matching the surrounding terrain. There's a pragmatic exactness to their construction. Walls are thick, unadorned, fitted together without mortar, so tight are the joins. The roofs are flat, collecting the constant condensation, perhaps for some as yet unknown purpose. Pathways, also made of perfectly laid stone, connect these scattered structures, forming a rudimentary network across the uneven platforms. There are no spires or grand arches, nothing ornate. Everything speaks of simple, robust functionality.

The technology here is primitive, though incredibly precise. One can imagine the tools used to cleave these blocks – not metal chisels, but perhaps wedges of harder, tougher stone, driven by heavy wooden mallets. The cuts are too clean for anything less than significant, directed force, but the overall impression is one of painstaking manual craft. No sign of wheels or advanced mechanisms. Every block, every carefully placed stone, speaks of individual effort and communal organization.

There is no animal life, no flutter of wings, no scuttling insects, no distant calls. The silence belongs entirely to the landscape, the dripping water, and perhaps, the hushed movements of whatever beings constructed these pale, still dwellings. The world is a perfectly balanced diorama, a snapshot of creation before change, where the wet rock weeps and green life flourishes in ordered patches, all under a still, warm, eternally quiet sky. It is a place of profound, almost disturbing, equilibrium, where everything *is* and nothing *changes*.
