The air tastes of wet iron and ozone.

It is a low, heavy place, a bowl carved into the earth’s crust where the sky presses down like a physical weight. The light here never really brightens; it exists in a state of permanent, bruised twilight, dim enough to blur the edges of things, yet bright enough to catch the glint of water on everything. There is no sun to be seen, only a thick, sweating cloud layer that glows with a faint, electrical luminescence—the exhaust of a world that breathes through machinery.

This is the Flood-Grove of Saint-Vert, though it is not a grove of trees, but of metal and moss.

The scale is intimate, almost claustrophobically so, bounded by high cliffs of black, weathered basalt that weep constant tears of groundwater. The floor of the bowl is a scattered, sparse collection of industrial structures—cathedral-sized engine houses, isolated pumping stations, and tall, slender chimneys that rise like dead trees from a marsh. They are not crowded together; they stand in solitary dignity, separated by wide, shallow pools of still, oily water. It is a city built for ghosts, or perhaps for machines that prefer solitude.

Despite the emptiness of the life we recognize, the place is loud.

The noise is a constant, rhythmic thrumming, a deep bass note that vibrates in the teeth and bones. It is the sound of the Great Compactors, massive piston-driven towers scattered across the landscape. Each one is a masterpiece of industrial engineering, riveted iron plates streaked with rust and verdigris, standing four stories tall. They operate without cease, their huge steel heads slamming downward with a thunderous *crack* that echoes off the canyon walls, compressing the very air.

The heat is stifling, a tropical sauna amplified by the waste heat of the engines. It sits at a humid ninety degrees, the air so thick it feels like you could swim through it. Condensation drips from every rivet, every pipe, every overhanging eave. The ground is a soupy muck of black mud and strange, metallic root systems.

Here, the vegetation has gone feral. It has adapted to the din and the dimness with a terrifying enthusiasm. Massive ferns, their fronds shaped like serrated saw blades, unfurl from the joints of the machinery. Vines as thick as a man’s torso, covered in glistening, sticky hairs, strangle the iron piping of the walkways, slowly crushing the metal in their grip. The green is deep, dark, and luminous—a bioluminescent moss that clings to the wet iron, pulsing softly in time with the Compactors’ rhythm. It is a lush, choking paradise. The plants are not passive decorations; they are the reclaimers, slowly dismantling the city molecule by molecule.

There are no birds. There are no insects. The silence of biology is absolute. The movement here belongs solely to the non-living: the hiss of steam venting from a relief valve, the rhythmic shuddering of the treads on an automated tram that circles the perimeter on rusted rails, and the relentless drip, drip, drip of water.

The water is everywhere. It wells up from the ground, it condenses from the hot air, it leaks from the machines. It pools in the craters left by the heavy machinery, creating a mirror-surface network of lakes. In this dim light, the water looks like black glass, until a Compactor slams down nearby, sending concentric ripples shivering across the surface.

The strangeness of the place lies in the interaction between the living and the dead. The plants seem to be feeding on the vibration. Where a pipe shakes violently from the pressure within, a cluster of huge, fungi-like growths clings tighter to the metal, vibrating in sympathy. The ecosystem is one of resonance. The moss glows brightest where the noise is loudest. The vines grow thickest where the heat is greatest. The rules are simple: consume the vibration, endure the wet, and wait for the iron to rust.

An automated maintenance skiff, a small barge with a single, flickering lantern, chugs across one of the dark pools. It moves with a clumsy, heavy grace, leaving a wake of oily rainbows. It stops at a dripping intake valve, a robotic arm extending to wipe the moss away from a sensor gauge. The gesture is futile; within seconds, the moss begins to creep back over the cleaned metal.

This is a place of settled erosion. The structures are not collapsing; they are being absorbed. The iron is not rotting away; it is turning into soil. The city is becoming a geology, a stratum of the future. It is loud, wet, and hot, a temple to a process that does not care for observers, only for the slow, grinding conversion of energy into matter. The rain begins to fall again, a warm, heavy mist that turns the distant clang of the pumps into a muffled, underwater heartbeat.
