The air tastes of cold iron and wet limestone. This is the Under-Veins, a hollow vastness buried deep within the crust of the world, a space too large to be called a cave and too structured to be called a mere fissure. The scale of it is deceptive; from a distance, the darkness at the edges implies a confined tunnel, but as you move, the walls retreat into a gloom that swallows sight, revealing a regional expanse of subterranean plain.

Here, the light is normal—not the dim bioluminescence of the deep earth, but a steady, sourceless illumination that permeates the rock like a memory of the sky. It paints everything in flat, distinct shades of grey and bruised purple, highlighting the strangeness of this place: it is packed.

The density of the space is its most striking feature. There is no horizon line, only a gradual fade, yet every inch of ground between the viewer and that fade is occupied. This is not the chaotic clutter of nature, but the claustrophobic precision of the urban. The "city" here is built, but not by architects in the traditional sense. It is an accretion.

The terrain is raw, embryonic. Erosion has not touched this place; the sharp edges of the basalt columns and the jagged spikes of the obsidian floor remain as they were when the earth first cracked. It is a landscape of knives and edges, unsmoothed by time or water. And yet, rising from these raw, unfinished wounds in the earth are structures.

They are primitive, heavy constructions of stacked stone, lashed with crude, green-copper bindings that have not yet oxidized. The tech level is early—levers, pulleys, massive stone rollers arranged in circles that grind against the bedrock with a slow, terrifying momentum. The city does not sprawl outward; it climbs inward. Towers of dry-stacked shale rise hundreds of feet, merging with the stalactites that hang from the ceiling, creating a dense forest of stone where there is no air between the buildings. Bridges of rough-hewn timber connect the towers in a chaotic web, a saturated infrastructure that leaves no room for a single open plaza.

Water is a scarce but constant presence. It does not flow in rivers; it weeps from the rock. The dampness index is 1.0—intermittent. Pockets of water collect in hollows and depressions, slicking the walkways, making the primitive stone stairs treacherous. The condensation drips from the overhead infrastructure, a rhythmic, quiet plinking that is the only sound in the silence. The air is cool, enough to raise gooseflesh, but the chill is damp, clinging to the skin like a wet woolen shroud.

Vegetation is sparse, struggling to survive in the shadows of the stone towers. Here and there, a pale, stunted moss clings to the north-facing sides of the masonry, and a few black, leafless trees twist upward from cracks in the pavement, grasping for the flat light. They are bonsai-like, dwarfed by the sheer density of the construction around them, trapped in the gaps between the buildings.

Despite the silence—sound is low here, swallowed by the soft rock and the heavy acoustics—the city is active. This is an ecosystem in motion, but the motion is geological and mechanical rather than biological. You can see the "dynamic" parameter in the shifting of the massive rollers. Huge grindstones, thirty feet in diameter, turn slowly in subterranean wells, crushing ore or grain, the mechanism powered by the slow, relentless drip of water filling bladder-valves made of gut and leather. It is a clockwork of immense, lethargic processes.

Fauna is rare. You might see a single, flightless bird hopping across a high railing, its feathers the color of dust, or the flash of a pale rodent skittering into a crack between the bricks. There are no herds, no flocks, only solitary survivors navigating the labyrinth of a world that is still under construction.

The feeling is one of being inside a factory that the earth forgot to turn off, or a city that is being born prematurely. The masonry looks fresh, the dust still settling, as if the builders laid down their simple hand tools and vanished only yesterday. The rules here are dictated by the raw geology: the buildings cannot go out, they must go up, and the rock is sharper than the law. To walk these streets is to navigate a world that is still deciding if it wants to be solid, where the silence is heavy enough to touch, and the damp air carries the metallic taste of something new, hard, and unfinished.
