Before there is a place, there is a process. You are suspended in the embryonic crust of a world that has not yet decided if it will be a continent or an ocean. The elevation is extreme—you are kilometers above the theoretical sea floor—but the water is absolute. There is no shore. There is no surface. There is only the deep, cool immensity of a vertical flood, a hydrostatic abyss that stretches to the limits of the horizon in every direction, generating its own weather, its own continental pressure systems, its own storm-choked sky of water.

The temperature sits in the narrow band of a permanent, bruising chill—just above freezing, yet never crystallizing into ice. The water does not breathe; it presses. You are submerged in a high-altitude deluge, a column of atmospheric-water so dense it possesses the crushing weight of a deep sea, yet behaves with the chaotic, hyperactive turbulence of a gas giant's weather system. Massive internal rivers, hundreds of kilometers wide, shear past each other in the dark, generating hyper-currents that roil and fold the ocean into itself. It is a bazaar of hydrodynamics, a continuous, violent exchange of momentum. 

Despite the overwhelming volume of water, the space feels profoundly empty. The density is near-void. There is almost nothing per unit of volume—no organic matter, no particulate snow, no marine snow drifting down from above. The fauna is an absolute zero. No fish. No leviathans. No microbes. The vegetation is merely a sparse, accidental scattering of geology: thin, branching tendrils of unformed rock that jut out like dead coral from the vertical cliff faces, mineral weeds growing in the cold, stripped clean of any biological attempt.

The light here is normal, yet profoundly alien in its rendering. It is not the bioluminescent glimmer of the deep sea, but the flat, scattered, sunlit grey of a rainstorm—a storm that happens to be thousands of kilometers thick. Light penetrates in broad, diffuse shafts, illuminating the immense, moving walls of current. It catches the edges of the rain—the constant, surrounding velocity of water rushing past—turning the mid-water into a kinetic tapestry of sweeping, liquid shadows.

And the sound. The sound is deafening. It is not the muffled, pinging quiet of submarine films. It is a roaring, continuous, structural bellow. The sheer scale of the place—vast enough to spawn its own cyclones—means the acoustic energy of the turbulent water is catastrophic. You do not hear the water; you are assaulted by the physics of it. The low-frequency groan of water crushing against unformed stone shakes the skeleton. The high-frequency hiss of shearing currents vibrates in the teeth. It is the sound of a planet being forged in a cold, high-pressure wash.

The geology is raw, pre-formation, emerging. This is not a finished world; it is a construction site. The high-elevation plateaus and towering, submerged mesa are made of a pale, porous primordial stone—pumice-like, but dense, interlaced with veins of something that should not yet exist. The tech here is not built; it is emergent. It is post-modern, an engineering that current physics cannot fully describe, bubbling up from the planet's mantle like a geothermal necessity. 

Within the raw rock, stretching for hundreds of kilometers, are lattice-structures. They are not constructed artifacts; there are no makers, no tools, no architecture of intention. They are a natural product of this world's unfinished physics—a nanoscale or cosmic technology that grows like crystal under the extreme pressure of the turbulent flood. These lattices hum with an unfamiliar resonance, actively shaping the water that forces its way through them. They filter the flood. They redirect the continental currents into geometric, impossible spirals. Where the raw rock breaks apart under the hyperactive erosion of the shifting currents, these technological veins are exposed—not as metal or wire, but as a living, mineralized logic gate, a mineral computation occurring on a planetary scale.

The weirdness of the place settles into the realization that the water and the tech are the same system. The flood is not just drowning the landscape; it is the medium through which the landscape is compiling itself. The turbulence is the algorithm. The sound is the processing. You are floating in the cold, roaring birth-fluid of a machine that is learning to be a continent, submerged in the sky of a world that is violently, loudly, and endlessly becoming.
