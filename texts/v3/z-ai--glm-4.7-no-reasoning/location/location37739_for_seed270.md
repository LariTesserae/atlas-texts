You descend into it through a maintenance hatch that someone left open—or perhaps its hinges simply dissolved into the same mineral fatigue that claims everything here. The space below is a pocket, a void carved from intention: a former pump station, a reliquary for pressured systems, a room perhaps forty meters square and only six high. Low ceiling. Low land in concept. A box pressed into the flank of something larger that has forgotten it exists.

No water. The air itself is dry as calcined bone—humidity a memory, vapor a concept that cannot find purchase. What you notice first is the sound. It arrives before your eyes adjust: a churning, a clicking, a thousand small mechanisms exhaling in overlapping rhythms. The space is loud in the way a clock factory is loud—not with violence, but with accumulated insistence. Every surface thrums. The air carries vibration the way other atmospheres carry scent.

Your vision resolves. The room is dense. Impossibly, chokingly dense. Every cubic meter holds presence, holds weight, holds *life*—though you must widen your definition of that word until it nearly breaks.

They are everywhere.

Not vertebrates. Not anything your ancestry prepared you to recognize as fauna. They are chitinous and mineral, copper-shelled and iron-jointed, moving on cilia of oxidized wire and breathing through spiracles lined with filter-mesh that was once factory grating. The room swarms with them—insects is the closest word, but even that feels like a concession to familiarity. They range from a centimeter to the length of your forearm. Their bodies are constructed: you can see the rivets, the stamped segments, the serial numbers etched into thorax plates by machines that have been dead for centuries. Someone made the first of them. Then the first learned to make the rest.

It is overwhelming. You stand at the hatch and they flow around you like you are a stone in a current—not avoiding you with fear, but with indifference. You are terrain to them. You are humidity they cannot use, warmth they can parasitize briefly as they cross your shadow, mass that blocks a pheromone trail they follow to some destination you cannot parse. There are millions. Tens of millions. The walls crawl. The ceiling crawls. The floor is not visible beneath the carpet of them, moving in waves that suggest purpose, communication, a distributed intelligence that exceeds any individual the way a conversation exceeds a word.

The light is normal—hanging industrial fixtures, caged bulbs, the kind of illumination that makes no poetry of itself. It casts shadows that the creatures wear like camouflage, their manufactured carapaces catching and breaking the light into a thousand metallic glints. The room gleams despite its age. Despite its exhaustion.

Because it is old. So old that age has become a quality of the space rather than a measure of time. The erosion is absolute—the final form of things, reached at the end of patience. The walls are smooth where they were once ribbed with conduit channels; the conduits have been harvested, their traces just ghost-grooves in ferrocrete. The floor is worn to a polish by the passage of infinite legs over centuries, its surface a mirror of compressed movement. Whatever this facility once pumped or processed or pressurized, it has been hollowed by time into a shell. And the shell has been filled.

There are remnants of the builders. A control desk against one wall, its console dark, its switches fused into a single corroded ridge. A section of pipe, wide as a torso, emerging from one wall and terminating in midair—cut, not broken, by a process that left a rim sharp enough to slice. Safety signage in a language that uses the same warning colors humans use, which tells you something about the universality of hazard, or perhaps about the species that designed the first of these creatures. Occasionally, between the flowing masses, you glimpse a tool, a fitting, a fragment of maintenance infrastructure—all of it colonized. The creatures nest in junction boxes. They breed in relay housings. They have metabolized the insulation, the grease, the chemical residues, and from those industrial materials they have built themselves.

That is the strangest thing, the thing your mind keeps circling back to: they are of this place. Not invaders, not squatters, but *naturalized*. The room produced them. Its chemistry, its warmth, its mechanical residues, its electromagnetic bleed from whatever power source still faintly feeds the lights—all of it conspired across geological time to generate life from industry. Or something like life. Something that moves, and eats, and reproduces, and responds to stimuli, and fills every available niche until the niche is identical with the organism that fills it.

The coolness helps them. You feel it—a steady, mild chill, like autumn in a basement. It is the temperature of stored energy, of reactions that do not rush, of metabolism that has calibrated itself to patience. They do not hurry. Even the sound they make is not frantic but rhythmic, mechanical, the audio signature of ten million small engines running at idle. They tick. They purr. They click in patterns that might be language, might be echolocation, might be nothing more than the incidental music of exoskeletons contacting each other in a space too small for silence.

Something drops from the ceiling onto your shoulder. You feel the weight of it—light, precise, like a coin falling. It walks along your collar with legs like needle-nose pliers, investigates the seam of your sleeve, finds nothing it needs, launches itself back into the flow. It did not startle. You did not startle it. You are environment. You are weather.

The room is active in ways beyond the motion of its inhabitants. You notice air currents—thermal exchanges from the mass of bodies, from the mild heat of the lights, from whatever process still warms the walls just above the coolness of the air. There is weather here: micro-weather, pocket-weather, a convection cell that has existed for a thousand years inside forty square meters. It carries the pheromone-equivalents on which the creatures communicate, and it carries the faint ozone smell of their metabolism, and it carries dust that is actually shed carapace fragments, and it carries something else—a chemical signature you cannot name, but which tastes like *purpose*. The room smells like a factory that has become a body.

You could stay here. The thought arrives uninvited. You could sit against that worn wall and let them flow over you until you were part of the current, until your warmth was absorbed and your moisture was harvested and your organic matter was slowly, patiently, over seasons and years, broken down into mineral components that some future generation of these creatures would incorporate into their shells. You would become industrial. You would become eternal, in the only way anything can be eternal in a place this old: by joining the process that has already outlasted everything that built it.

You climb back through the hatch.

The sound follows you for a long time.
