The air is a wall. You step into this place and it presses against you—thick, wet, hot, and screaming. Not screaming in metaphor: the sound is literal, a biological roar that rises from every surface, every hollow, every cubic inch of green-black space. It is a sound without center, without source, a frequency made of ten thousand voices layered until they become white noise and then something beyond white noise—a pressure, a vibration in the chest, a hum in the teeth. Insects. Amphibians. Things that click and things that drone and things that produce sounds you have no name for, all calling, all overlapping, all deafening. The quiet here would be death. Nothing is silent. Even the rain, when it comes, is swallowed into the wall of sound, just another voice in the choir.

And the rain does come. Not as steady fall, not as dependable weather, but as sudden exhalations—bursts that last minutes or seconds, that sweep through the canopy in visible sheets and then vanish, leaving behind a steam that doesn't rise so much as suspend. The moisture hangs. It beads on every surface. It condenses on leaf and bark and stone and on your own skin, and it never fully dries before the next breath of wet arrives. The place is not underwater but it might as well be—the humidity is so complete that the air and the water have lost their clear distinction. You can taste it. You breathe it in and your lungs feel crowded.

It is low here. The land presses down toward some slow river you can hear but rarely see—a wide, brown, coiling thing that breathes with the rain, swelling and receding, changing its banks by centimeters each day, sometimes by meters when the downpours align. The terrain is flat in the way that only young land is flat—not worn down by time but simply not yet built up. The features are fresh, sharp-edged, new. Ravines cut sudden and steep through the red laterite, their walls still crumbling, still finding their angle. Mudbanks collapse into the river with soft roars. Root systems hang exposed where soil has slid away, gripping air. The land is still becoming itself. It hasn't decided what it is.

And the vegetation—God, the vegetation. It is not a covering; it is an occupation. Every horizontal surface, every vertical surface, every surface that tilts at any angle at all is claimed. The trees stand close as lovers, closer—trunk pressed to trunk, canopy woven into canopy, root tangled with root until no single tree is an individual, until the forest is one continuous organ. Below them, the understory is a vertical thicket of fern and palm and woody vine, each layer fighting upward, each gap filled the moment it opens. Epiphytes colonize every branch. Moss runs over every surface like a slow green fire. Lianas drape and climb and strangle, pulling trees into arches, bridging gaps, creating walls where walls shouldn't be. The density is suffocating. You cannot walk through this place—you cut, you crawl, you exist in the small spaces the forest forgot to fill, and those spaces are closing.

But it is the animals that make this place overwhelming. Not because they are large—some are, but that is not the point. It is their *number*. Their *presence*. They are everywhere. Above you, howler monkeys rage and branches crack under their weight. Around you, insects move in such numbers that the leaf litter seems to breathe, seems to shift like a living carpet. Ants traverse in columns wide as your hand, carrying fragments of leaf, carrying dead things, carrying the forest piece by piece into their chambers. Beetles gleam like wet metal in any gap of light. Spiders' webs catch across your face, across your arms, and the spiders themselves—some small, some impossibly large—watch from their corners with multiple patient eyes. Below the water line, in the black river, things move that create wakes, that displace the surface in patterns you cannot read. Snakes? Fish? Something else? The water gives nothing back but reflection.

And in the darkness—because it is dark, deeply dark, a darkness that feels colored—the life only intensifies. The canopy above is nearly total. What light exists filters down in isolated shafts, pale columns that pick out a single leaf, a single flower, a single falling spore, and then is swallowed. Most of the forest floor exists in a greenish dusk, or in no light at all. You navigate by touch and by sound, but the sound is no help—it is too much, too layered, too constant to parse. And in the deep dark, away from even the filtered shafts, your hand finds bark that is wrong—bark that pulses, bark that is warm, bark that yields like flesh under pressure. Or you don't find it. Maybe you imagined it. The weirdness of this place is subtle. It is not another world. But the rules bend. The geometry of roots suggests patterns that don't repeat don't terminate. The calls of unseen animals modulate in ways that seem like language—not your language, not any language you know, but *language*, syntax, response. Something is being said, constantly, by many throats, and the meaning hovers just beyond reach.

The river is the only open space, and even it is not truly open—the banks press in, the trees lean over, vines dangle into the water like fingers testing temperature. But on the water, if you were on the water, in some hollowed log with a hand-carved paddle—because this is a place of hands, of stone edges, of fire-toughened wood, of the first tools a mind can wrap around and shape—on the water you could see the scale of it. The forest goes on. It extends beyond sight, beyond sound, beyond any boundary you can conceive. It is not a grove or a stand or a wood. It is a nation of green, an empire of the living, and it has no frontier. The river bends and the forest is there. The river bends again and the forest is there. It was there before you and it will be there after and right now, in this moment, it is so fully *there*—so present, so loud, so wet, so hot, so swarming—that you cannot imagine absence. Cannot imagine silence. Cannot imagine dry.

Something moves in the water near the bank. Something large. The wake spreads and spreads and does not stop.

The howlers begin their morning call, though it might not be morning—time is hard to read in a place without sky. The sound builds. It layers with the insects, with the frogs, with the clicking and the droning and the whistling and the buzz-saw whine of cicadas so loud they seem to be inside your skull. The forest does not quiet for rain, does not quiet for night, does not quiet for anything. It is always at full voice. It is always at full occupancy. It is always, always becoming.

A leaf falls. It is caught before it reaches the ground.

You are not the protagonist of this place. You are a small, warm, water-bearing thing, and you are being noticed.
