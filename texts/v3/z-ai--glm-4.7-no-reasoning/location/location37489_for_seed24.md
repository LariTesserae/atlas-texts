The darkness here is not the absence of day—it is a substance, a medium. It pools in the low places and hangs in the still air, thick enough that you feel you could press your palms into it. This is a lowland basin, a shallow scoop in the geology where warmth gathers and nothing disperses. The temperature sits at a constant, heavy simmer, radiating upward from the mineral floor in slow, invisible waves. It is a dry warmth, entirely devoid of humidity. There is no water here. Not hidden in pores of rock, not lingering as vapor, not bound in chemical ice. The air tastes of calcined dust and old silence.

The space is bounded, a room-sized hollow carved by eons of patient wind. Perhaps a hundred paces across, perhaps less; the darkness makes distance uncertain. The terrain is nearly flat, a vast floor of packed, desiccated silt and exposed bedrock, worn smooth by an equilibrium reached long before memory. The erosion parameter here is fulfilled by sheer antiquity. The walls of this basin—low, sloping rises of sedimentary strata—are rounded and softened. There are no sharp edges left in the world. Everything has settled into its final, most comfortable shape. The stone does not crumble; it merely persists, holding the warmth like a kiln.

Between the stone pillars and the packed earth, there is space. Immense, empty space. The density is desperately low. You can walk for minutes and encounter nothing but the same flat, warm dust. The sparse vegetation that survives here does not cluster or crowd. It exists in isolation, each specimen separated by a breathless expanse of nothing.

And what grows here is barely vegetation at all. There are no leaves to photosynthesize in this oppressive dark, no green to be found. Instead, there are lithic growers—slow things that are half-root, half-mineral. They are dark, matte absorbers of heat, rising from cracks in the bedrock like stubby fingers. They might be a handspan tall, branching once or twice into blunt, waxy knobs. They do not drink; they synthesize. Fed only by the ambient warmth of the stone and the faint, slow decay of the dust itself, they exude a faint, oily sheen in the gloom. They are primitive things, pre-cellular in their simplicity, bound to the spot where the warmth is most even. They do not move. They do not change.

Nothing moves. The fauna count is an absolute zero. There are no grazers, no burrowers, no insects clicking in the dark. The sound parameter is a flatline. Step, and your footfall is swallowed instantly by the soft dust. Speak, and your voice dies against the rounded stone without an echo. The acoustic properties of the basin are perfectly dampened; the space is an anechoic chamber built by geology. The silence is not an absence of sound, but a presence—a low, perceptible hum of nothing happening, a pressure against the eardrums.

There are no constructions. No cairns, no scratched symbols, no discarded tools. Even the primitive technology parameter feels like an overstatement; no hand has ever been here to shape a flint or twist a fiber. The only fabrication is the occasional loose chert, split by thermal stress ages ago, lying where it fell. It is untouched, undeveloped, a landscape that has never been a resource.

The darkness makes it timeless. Without light to mark a day, without water to mark a season, the dynamic parameter collapses to zero. This place is in frozen equilibrium. The warm air does not circulate; it sits. The dust does not drift; it is packed. The lithic growers do not bud or wither; they simply maintain their state of minimal, heat-driven existence. If you were to leave and return in a thousand years, the stubby mineral branches would be in the exact same positions, having accumulated perhaps a millimeter of slow, aqueous-less growth.

It is a room at the bottom of the world, sealed by darkness, heated by the earth, empty of everything but the faint, scattered proof that warmth alone is enough to make something endure.
