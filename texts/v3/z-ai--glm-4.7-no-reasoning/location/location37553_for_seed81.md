To stand in the Upper Reach is to stand inside the throat of a vast, empty machine. The elevation here is staggering; the air thins into something that barely qualifies as breath, smelling of oxidized iron, sulfur, and the flat ozone tang of continuous electrical discharge. It is hot—a deep, radiating, mineral heat that rises from the metal streets and presses against the skin like a fever. The sun is a rumor, hidden behind a permanent ceiling of thick, condensing smog and atmospheric ash that filters the light down to a perpetual, bruised twilight. Everything is cast in shades of deep rust, bruised violet, and the dull, glowing orange of embers buried in ash.

This is a city—an organized, layered construction of staggering scale—yet it is utterly devoid of life. There are no plants. Not a single blade of genetically engineered grass, no clinging lichen, no spore or seed. The biology of the old world left no footprint here. This is a mineral kingdom, a constructed ecology of iron, slag, basalt, and vitreous coal. The architecture is industrial Brutalism pushed to the point of the surreal: massive hab-blocks rise like sheer cliffs of riveted steel, their windows dark and hollow, leading to miles of empty corridors. Subsurface pumps and refining towers dominate the plateau, arranged in geometrically perfect lines that stretch far past the visible horizon. The scale is regional, a settlement built for millions, but the density is absolute zero. The hab-blocks are silent. The plazas are vacant. There is only the infrastructure, humming along without an operator, a skeletal automation running on forgotten protocols.

And yet, it is wet. The heat of the city meets the cold altitude, breeding a phenomenon the absent engineers likely called "the Weep." The atmosphere is super-saturated; moisture condenses relentlessly against every vertical and horizontal surface. It does not rain. Instead, the city sweats. Water beads on the rusted flanks of smokestacks, running in continuous, silent rivulets down the sides of blast furnaces. The precipitate is strange—tepid, faintly luminescent, carrying a high mineral content that leaves trails of crystallized salt and copper sulfate as it dries. Piping infrastructure, exposed and thick as train tunnels, runs along the avenues like exposed arteries. At the stress fractures, the Weep escapes in pressurized jets, arcing over the empty streets to fall hissing onto the hot pavement, generating low-hanging steam that clings to the ground like a carpet of grey moss. The ground itself is a lattice of iron grating; beneath your feet, you can see dark, rushing water channeling through the sub-levels, an entire river system trapped in the city's guts. 

The sound is a contradiction. A city of this scale, running at this industrial capacity, should be deafening. Instead, it is mostly quiet. The acoustics of the empty spaces swallow noise. The rhythmic thudding of subterranean pistons is felt in the marrow of the bones rather than heard. The hiss of escaping steam and the low, bass thrum of the electrical grid create a constant, tectonic drone—a lullaby of mechanics. Footsteps echo for blocks, sharp and lonely, before being swallowed by the damp air. Occasionally, the silence is broken by the groan of thermal expansion, a ship-breaking shriek of metal settling under the heat, reverberating through the empty canyon streets and fading into a long, sorrowful whine.

The fauna is nearly nonexistent, but not entirely absent. In the upper vents, where the heat plumes meet the cold exterior air, gyroscopic drones float on the updrafts like mechanical jellyfish. They are battered, missing optics, trailing frayed copper wiring. They seem to be cleaning the particulate from the air, or perhaps simply riding the thermals on dying batteries, executing a maintenance loop left over from a population that no longer produces waste. Down at street level, the only movement is the flow of water, the venting of steam, and the slow, automated march of cargo-haulers—blind, wheeled titans that rumble along fixed rail-lines in the gloom, delivering raw ore to smelters that have not seen a human shift in centuries. 

The weirdness of the Upper Reach settles into your mind the longer you stay. It is the profound cognitive dissonance of a metropolis built for lives that are nowhere in evidence. The city is not dead; it is terribly, relentlessly active. The smelters burn. The pumps churn. The grid hums. The water cycles through the pipes, heated and cooled and condensed. The machine is maintaining itself, waiting for a command that will never come, processing resources into slag that is automatically carted away and dumped over the plateau's edge. It is an orphaned ecosystem of fire, metal, and water—a world that has outlived its creators, weeping steam into the dark, running endlessly hot, high, and hollow.
