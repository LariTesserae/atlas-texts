A high plateau, ancient beyond reckoning, where the stone has wept for so long it no longer remembers being dry.

You arrive in darkness—complete, palpable, a medium rather than an absence. Not the darkness of caves, which is merely underground, but the darkness of a place that has simply opted out of light. No stars. No sky-glow. No bioluminescence offers concession. The dark here has texture; it presses against the eyes like velvet, like deep water without the wet.

And yet the place is warm. Humid, almost tropical, the kind of warmth that makes skin feel soft and breathing feel like drinking. The heat rises from below—geothermal patience, a slow exhalation from stone that has been cooling for a hundred million years and is in no hurry to finish. This is the end of erosion's long conversation: a plateau worn down to its essential self, near-flat, near-featureless, a table of absurd antiquity. The mountains that once stood here were ground to dust before your species learned to make fire. What remains is the floor of a world, the final geometry, smoothed and settled into a form that will persist long after younger landscapes have crumbled into their own forgettings.

Water lives everywhere just beneath the surface. You cannot see it in the dark, but you feel it—underfoot, the ground has the slight give of saturated earth, though it supports weight. Water presses up; water condenses out of the air itself. The humidity is so complete that droplets form on any surface cooler than breath: on skin, on fabric, on the few structures that rise from the plain. They form and slide and form again. The air is a slow waterfall falling sideways. The stone—you understand this from the sound of your footsteps, from the occasional brush of your hand against outcroppings—is porous, hoarding water like a living sponge. Weeping rock. The entire plateau is an aquifer turned inside out, a landscape that has given up trying to contain its water and now simply exudes it.

The space feels like a cathedral, if cathedrals were carved from the absence of cathedrals. The scale is intimate—you can reach out in any direction and eventually touch a wall, a post, a standing thing—but the emptiness between those touchable things is vast, disproportionate, a void distributed in small portions. You are always near a boundary, yet the boundaries never arrive. The darkness eats perspective. You could be in a room the size of a chapel or a clearing the size of a hamlet; the distinction has been rendered meaningless. There is here, and there is a few steps away, and between them: nothing visible, nothing present, just warm wet dark and the sense of enormous room.

But the place is not truly empty. It is sparsely occupied in the way that a cathedral is sparsely occupied: a few figures, great distances between them, the negative space as meaningful as the positive. Scattered throughout the darkness—intuited more than seen, though your hands find them—are structures. Not ruins. Not quite. They are too purposeful for ruin, too intact for abandonment, yet too few and too strange for habitation. The largest is the size of a small room, perhaps three meters across, its surface cool and faintly ribbed under your fingertips. The material is like nothing you know: not metal, not ceramic, not stone, but something that conducts warmth reluctantly and refuses to hold water. Droplets bead on its surface and roll off in slow, audible streams. The structures taper at their bases, widening upward—inverse pedestals, or mushrooms of engineered substance, flaring into collecting shapes overhead. They feel like instruments. Like ears. Like things built to catch something from the dark air and condense it into purpose.

The technology—if that word applies to artifacts so far beyond your comprehension that theyC they might as well be natural features—operates at scales your physics cannot parse. Your hand on the structure's surface feels only smoothness, but you sense, with some faculty deeper than touch, that the material is active at the nanoscale, or perhaps at a scale below nanoscale, in the interstices of matter itself. Something is being done. Processes slower than chemistry, more patient than erosion, are running in the walls of these few scattered forms. They are machines at the end of their operating cycles, still faithfully executing commands issued before the plateau was flat, before the water table rose, before the sun that once shone here burned out or was hidden. They are the last functional artifacts of a civilization that understood durability as a theological principle.

The vegetation does not need light. This is the first wrong thing, the thing that marks this place as other-than-home. The lushness is overwhelming—you feel it pressing against your shins, your waist, your chest as you move—but you cannot see it, and the inability to see what you can feel gives the darkness an organic thickness. The plants here run on something else. Heat, perhaps. The geothermal warmth that rises from the deep stone. Or the water itself, mineral-rich and ancient, carrying energy in its flow. Or something the structures emit, some radiation below your perception, feeding the biome in exchange for... what? The vegetation is soft, unarmed, without thorns or tough bark. Nothing here has needed to defend itself. Without light, there are no seasons, no droughts, no sudden freezes. The growing is continuous and slow, a vegetal patience that matches the plateau's own. You brush through what feels like ferns, like moss standing knee-high, like vines that hang in curtains across your path—though you cannot confirm these comparisons, only the tactile impression of frond and stem and leaf, all of them wet, all of them warm, all of them alive in a way that light-grown plants are not: quieter, more persistent, as if photosynthesis were a hobby and this, this dark wet warmth, were the true work.

And the fauna. The sound is loud, and the sound is them.

A continuous low roar, like wind in a forest, but there is no wind—the air is still, stagnant, the warm breath of the earth barely moving. The sound comes from throats. From bodies. From the rubbing of legs and the clicking of mandibles and the boom of air sacs and the subsonic hum of enormous slow hearts. The fauna is abundant beyond counting, and you hear them all, layered in registers from the nearly ultrasonic down to vibrations you feel in your chest rather than your ears. Insects—or things occupying the insect niche, warm and wet and six-legged—chorus in the unseen canopy of fern-frond and hanging vine. Something larger moves with liquid heaviness to your left, its steps slow and deliberate, its breathing a bellows-rhythm that syncopates against the insect drone. Something else calls in a register below voice, a sound like a drum struck once and let resonate for thirty seconds, fading into the general hum. The darkness is full of bodies. You are never alone here; you are merely unable to see your company.

They ignore you. This is the second wrong thing. In a place this dark, prey animals should startle at your footfalls; predators should investigate. Instead, the life here operates on chemical time, on olfactory maps and thermal gradients, and you—warm, wet, mammal-smelling—read as just another feature of the landscape, a temporary obstruction, a stone that breathes. The creatures flow around you. Their sounds do not alter at your passage. You are irrelevant to an ecosystem that never evolved to notice things like you.

The weirdness is subtle. This is not a place of overt impossibility—no floating rocks, no reversed gravity, no doors to elsewhere. The wrongness is in the arrangement, in the particular combination of things that should not coexist but here have coexisted for so long they have become a single organism. Lightless lushness. Warmth without sun. Water without source. Technology without users. The weirdness is in the way the plateau feels *completed*, as if every process here has reached its terminal state, as if the erosion didn't just flatten the mountains but flattened time itself, compressing past and present into a single sustained moment that lasts for geological ages. Nothing is arriving. Nothing is departing. The water cycles in place, condensing and falling and condensing again. The plants grow and die and grow in the same dark. The animals are born and eat and die in sounds that never change. The structures compute their slow computations. The heat rises. The wet dark persists.

It is a landscape at the end of its becoming. It has been this way since before the word *ancient* had anything to be ancient about. It will be this way after.

You stand in it, warm and blind and soaking, listening to the roar of invisible life, your hand on a structure that is doing something incomprehensible in the dark, and you understand that you are not visiting this place. You are merely the latest condensation on its surface—a droplet that will roll off, that must roll off, while the plateau goes on weeping its slow water, growing its dark plants, humming its animal chorus, and waiting for nothing at all.
