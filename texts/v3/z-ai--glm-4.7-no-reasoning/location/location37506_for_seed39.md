At nearly six thousand meters, the world has not yet decided what it wants to be. This is a landscape of raw, embryonic geology—a place where the earth's bones are still cartilaginous, pushing upward in vast, unarticulated ridges. The elevation is extreme, the altitude staggering, yet the peaks ahead lack the jagged, finished resolution of ancient mountains. They are swollen, bulging formations of slate and mudstone, still squeezing themselves out of the tectonic dark, their faces unscarred by deep time. Erosion has barely begun its work here; there are no carved valleys, no scalloped cirques, only shallow, tilting depressions where the crust has buckled and sighed.

And into these nascent folds, water bleeds. The atmosphere sits heavy and cold, a pall of dim, anemic light that filters through a permanent lid of altostratus. The sun is a rumor, a bright smudge that neither warms nor illuminates. At this altitude, the cold is a physical weight, settling into the lungs and stiffening the fingers, yet the water refuses to freeze into glaciers. Instead, it seeps. The rock here is weeping. Water presses up from just beneath the scree, saturating the fractured stone until the mountainsides sweat. Every shallow depression becomes a squelching moor of saturated shale; the ground is a cold sponge, oozing rust-colored runoff from the iron-rich strata. There are no streams, no rivers—only a pervasive, surface-level weeping, a landscape-wide dampness that soaks the boots and chills the bone. 

The moisture supports life, but only just. Vegetation is sparse to the point of absence—no alpine meadows, no cushions of moss. Instead, the saturated gravel is colonized by vast, sprawling networks of black filamentous lichen, shot through with veins of phosphorescent blue. They cling to the wet rock like synaptic networks, drawing life from the mineral bleed. Where the water pools in the shallow tilts of the plateau, thick mats of anaerobic algae bloom, dark as old bruises, shifting sluggishly when the near-freezing water bubbles up from below.

Yet, for all this mineral emptiness, the place is heaving with fauna. The cold dampness breeds life in the cracks. Air beetles—hard-shelled and sluggish—emerge in dense, clicking swarms from the fissures where warm water vents into the air. They crawl over the weeping rocks in a living crust, picking at the metallic salts. Where the beetles gather, the sky thickens. Long-winged fulmars and high-altitude skua ride the thin, dim air, their calls a raw, scraping echo that cuts through the constant, low hiss of the wind. They drop like stones into the wet scree, gorging on the insect bloom before pushing off again, their wings labored in the rarefied atmosphere. Below them, in the saturated hollows, herds of tundra-grubs—blind, segmented mass-grazers the size of dogs—move in single-file lines, their chitinous plates clicking rhythmically as they suction the algae mats from the freezing mud. There are predators, too: high-striding stalkers with elongated limbs and insulating, oil-slick feathers, picking their way silently through the wet rubble, scanning for a broken profile in the dim.

But it is the sound that violates the expectation of this high wilderness. Beneath the call of the birds and the wind's dreary pull, the ground itself hums. The hum of industry. There are no buildings here, no smokestacks, no roads, no structures of any kind. The density of the place is near-void; stand in the center of this vast, open plateau, and you are alone in a sprawling, wet emptiness. Yet the earth is electrified. Deep in the newly born strata, a mechanical ecosystem thrives. Subterranean engines—vast, automated boring machines and automated extraction lattice-works—churn day and night. They were sent ahead to shape the unshapable, to mine the metal-heavy mud before the mountains fully rise. 

The only evidence of this industrial presence is acoustic and thermal. The bedrock transmits a deep, subsonic thrum that vibrates the fillings in your teeth and makes the standing water shiver with concentric ripples. From the dark pools, occasional bursts of steam vent with a mechanical, pneumatic exhalation—*chasssh*—venting the heat of buried turbines into the freezing air. The water that seeps from the rocks runs warm in places, carrying the faint, acrid tang of lubricants and copper oxidation. The machines are devouring the mountain from the inside out, their presence felt only as a trembling in the soles of the feet and the occasional, startling mechanical gasp that echoes across the empty miles.

It is an intermediary world, caught between states. A geology still in the womb, being hollowed out by automated industry it will never see. A freezing, weeping expanse of stone and damp, where the sky is a perpetual dusk and the ground squelches beneath the predatory stride of blind, chitinous grazers. Nothing here is finished. The water has not yet found its riverbed. The rock has not yet found its peak. The machines have not yet found their limit. Everything is active, pressing, emerging—caught in the cold, wet act of becoming.
