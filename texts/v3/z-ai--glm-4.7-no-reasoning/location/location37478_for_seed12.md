The air here is heavy, not with pressure, but with saturation. It sits at a vertiginous elevation, a high plateau suspended above the cloud layer of the world below, yet it behaves more like the bottom of a sea.

This is the High Sargasso, a place where the altitude is great enough to touch the stratosphere, yet the geography is defined by water. Not a chaotic ocean of crashing tides, but a still, warm, atmospheric inundation. The water parameter is absolute (2.9), but gravity is gentle. Vast quantities of freshwater are suspended in the air, a permanent aerosol so thick that breathing is less about inhalation and more about filtering oxygen through a sieve of warm mist. Visibility is low (1.1), a dim, golden twilight that never truly brightens, diffused by the perpetual moisture.

The ground is raw. Erosion is nearly nonexistent (0.4), giving the landscape a jagged, embryonic cruelty. The stone is sharp and angular, as if the mountains were spat out of the earth’s core only yesterday. There are no smooth river stones, no rounded hills, no beaches. The bedrock is a chaotic, frozen explosion of dark basalt and glassy obsidian, rising in sudden, sheer towers and collapsing in jagged scree fields that have not yet been worn down by wind or rain. It is geology in its infancy—raw, undeveloped, and possessed of a terrifying indifference to life.

Yet life persists, and it is lush (1.8). Because the stone is so sharp and the air so wet, vegetation does not grow *out* of the ground so much as it *drapes* over it. Massive, thick-matted vines and epiphytes create a dense (1.6) carpeting over the jagged rocks, softening the razor-sharp edges into pillows of vibrant green and deep crimson mosses. The flora here has evolved to ignore the soil; the trees are aerial, their root systems dangling like open-weave nets, catching the floating moisture and the organic detritus drifting on the wind.

Clinging to these makeshift forests, growing upwards from the vertiginous cliff-faces, is the city (1.6). It is a primitive, urban sprawl (1.6/0.6 tech), a tangled architecture of woven reeds, hollowed giant-bones, and stone lashed together with wet leather. It feels less built and more accreted—like a coral reef of civilization that has grown around the raw bones of the mountains.

The structures are densely packed, layer upon layer, hugging the sides of the great obsidian spires. There are no wide streets, only narrow, dripping catwalks and rope bridges that sway in the slow, patient currents of the air. The technology is simple: hand-forged hooks, bone needles, heavy stone pestles. There is no metal here that cannot be found in a raw nugget; the people work with the raw materials of the high altitude. Waterwheels—which look more like slow-turning paddle wheels—spin in the air currents, driving simple lift mechanisms that haul buckets of water up from the denser clouds below, even though water is already everywhere.

The atmosphere is warm (2.5), a humid tropical heat trapped at high altitude. It is a greenhouse in the sky. The silence (1.3) is profound. Because the air is so dense with water, sound does not travel; it is swallowed by the mist. Shouts from one tower to another are muffled to whispers by the time they travel ten yards. The dominant sound is the slow, rhythmic *drip, drip, drip* of water condensing on the stone and the vegetation, a constant, liquid heartbeat that marks the passage of time.

Movement is slow (1.1). The fauna here (1.2) is rare and strange (1.8). The creatures are adaptations to the buoyant, wet air. One might see a "Sky-Drifter," a creature resembling a manta ray but made of gas-bladders and translucent skin, flapping lazily through the mist. It moves with a glacial grace, filtering the water in the air for plankton and airborne spores. There are no darting birds, no quick mammals. Everything here glides. The predators, if they exist, are ambush hunters that drift motionless for hours, indistinguishable from the hanging moss.

The weirdness of the place lies in the interface between the raw, unyielding stone and the soft, engulfing water. The world feels unfinished. The mountains still look like they are cooling from creation, smoking slightly in the humidity, while the city and the forest cling to them like a desperate lichen. The water is the architect; it determines the shape of the buildings, the slant of the roofs, the posture of the trees.

At night, the darkness is total, save for the soft, bioluminescent glow of the moss and the rare, distant lightning storms that flash silently far below in the abyssal oceans of the lower atmosphere. Here, in the High Sargasso, time is measured by the slow growth of the vines across the raw glass of the obsidian, and the imperceptible rising of the water level as the world slowly, inevitably, submerges into the sky.
