The air tastes like a brass trumpet that has been left in the sun.

It is warm here, a stifling, respiratory heat, but not the wet heat of a swamp. The moisture is gone, abstracted away, leaving the atmosphere thick with the flavor of metal and the static charge of constant motion. This is a high place, a shelf or plateau perched in the upper layers of the world where the sky is a dim, bruised purple, perpetually lit by a source that cannot be seen—only felt as a pressure against the skin.

You stand in a grove, but it is not a grove of wood. The vegetation is lush, aggressive, and entirely mineral. Imagine forests of spun glass and hollow copper reeds, growing so dense they threaten to lock together, yet the density of the space is surprisingly low. There is room to move, room to breathe, if the air would only hold still. The ground is a crust of compressed, oxidized dust, cracked in spiderweb patterns that hum with a low-frequency vibration.

This is the Singing Veldt.

The noise is the first and overwhelming fact of existence here. It is not a cacophony; it is a symphony of friction. Because there is almost no water—0.1 on the scale, a theoretical rounding error—sound does not travel in waves of pressure through liquid air. It travels through the ground, through the stems of the plants, and through the solid, chitinous bodies of the fauna. It is loud, a resonant thrumming that vibrates in your teeth.

The fauna are the architects of this din. They are abundant, swarming the mechanical stalks in a riot of biological industry. They resemble glass-winged beetles the size of dinner plates, and lizards with scales of brushed iron. They do not drink; they consume vibration. They cling to the towering, hollow stalks of the copper-flora, scraping their legs against the metal stems in rhythmic patterns, generating high-pitched shrieks and low basso profundo groans that travel for miles. It is a chaotic, turbulent marketplace of energy—sound is their currency, and their food.

The flora reacts to this. The "trees" here—massive, slow-growing crystals of silicon and carbonate—have evolved to amplify the noise. Their interiors are hollow, acting like organ pipes. When the beetles scrape a certain rhythm on the bark, the tree resonates in response, shaking its leaves of thin foil. This shaking sheds layers of metallic dust, which falls to the ground. The fauna then scavenge the dust, processing it to build their own shells. It is a closed, frantic cycle of consumption and construction: noise begets motion; motion beats the metal; metal becomes food; food becomes noise.

The turbulence is physical. The ground shifts constantly. Small earthquakes, caused by the sheer volume of acoustic energy trapped in the crust, shudder through the veldt every few minutes. Cracks open and seal themselves within seconds as the vibrations settle the dust. It is a Solaris-like instability, but slower—a landscape that is never quite finished forming, dissolving and recombining in a slow, jittery dance.

The light is dim, filtered through a perpetual haze of metallic pollen. Everything is cast in shades of deep indigo and rust-red. Shadows are long and blurry. There is no sharp sun, only a general glow that seems to emanate from the horizon where the land drops away into a void of clouds.

Scattered amongst this vibrating forest are the built remnants of something older. The tech is primitive, seemingly cobbled together from the very materials the forest provides. You see low, rough-hewn shelters constructed from slabs of slate and bound with copper wire. They look like hamlets, small and isolated, perched on the rare stable outcroppings of rock. Inside these structures, the vibrations are dampened by walls packed with soft, absorbent moss—the only non-metallic plant life here, a pale, gray sponge that grows in the deep shadows.

These shelters are not homes in the human sense. They are workshops. You can see the faint marks of hand tools on the stone, simple chipping and grinding. The inhabitants, if they exist, are gone now, or perhaps they have simply adapted—become part of the fauna. Their artifacts remain: simple hammers made of heavy iron, windcatchers designed to howl when the air moves, signaling the swarm.

The ecology is defined by scarcity of one element and abundance of another. Water is a myth, a ghost story told by the silence. The creatures here fear silence; to stop moving is to cool down, to become brittle. In a world without hydraulic cushioning, stillness means death. So, the veldt screams. It howls with the wind that whips through the canyons of glass, it clatters with the millions of iron-feet of the beetle swarms, and it resonates with the very earth itself, ringing like a bell that has been struck and never stopped ringing.

You stand in the center of a small clearing, perhaps the size of a cathedral nave. The air is dry, warm, and filled with glittering dust that tastes of blood and batteries. Around you, the copper reeds sway without wind, shivering from the sheer volume of the world they inhabit. The sound is so loud it feels like silence, a solid wall of pressure pressing against your ears, waiting for you to join the rhythm.
