The air is thick enough to swim in. It hangs warm and dim, a grey-green suspension of vapor and spores that blurs the distance between the ground and the sky. There is no horizon here, only a gradual fading of the vast, vertical architecture into a hazy gloom.

This is the High Bogs, a continental plateau lifted so high that the clouds below are like another ocean, but the air here is not thin. It is heavy, saturated, utterly wet. Water does not fall so much as it condenses out of the breath of the place, weeping from every surface. The ground is not ground in the traditional sense; it has not been solid rock for epochs. The erosion here has reached its final, ancient logic: the stone did not just wear away; it surrendered.

Everywhere you look, the world is alive. The rating for vegetation is misleading because it implies separation—plants here, dirt there. But this is a total living mass. The substrate is a writhing, interlocked mat of root-hairs and sponge-moss that has digested the mountains. Underfoot, the sensation is less like stepping on earth and more like stepping on the hide of a sleeping leviathan. The terrain is rounded, smoothed by millions of years of slow, aqueous digestion. Hills rise in gentle, overwhelming swells, vast and soft, covered in a fur of ferns and creeping algae.

The elevation is immense, high enough to generate its own isolated weather, yet the landscape is flattened in the way that only the very oldest things can be. The sharp peaks were ground down eons ago, replaced by these endless, rolling domes of biological matter. It feels as though you are walking on the back of a world that has finished growing and has begun to merely exist.

Light is a precious commodity here. The sun is a myth, a brighter patch in the ceiling of perpetual mist. The illumination comes from the diffuse glow of the canopy above—a thick, interwoven roof of broad-leaved giants that filter the brightness down into a dim, emerald twilight. Shadows are soft, lacking edges.

Because of the dimness and the heat, sound travels differently. The air is dense, muting the world. It is mostly quiet, a silence that feels heavy and pressurized. But when you stop moving and listen, you realize the silence is actually a chorus of very small things. The slow drip of water weeping from the pores of bark. The wet rustle of a million leaves settling. The distant, low-frequency thrum of some enormous creature moving through the sponge-masses, a vibration felt in the soles of the feet rather than heard by the ears.

The fauna is abundant but subtle. There are no skittering rodents, no loud birds. Instead, the ecosystem is dominated by slow-moving grazers and arboreal sluggards. You see shapes draped over the massive branches—limp, multi-limbed things that mimic the hanging moss, soaking up the humidity. In the bogs between the root-hills, schools of small, blind fish navigate the shallow, tea-colored waterways that thread the sponge-ground. They move in unison, flashing silver in the gloom, reacting to the ripples of your passing with a delayed, fluid intelligence.

It is a strange place, ruled by a biology that feels alien. The plants here do not just grow; they construct. There are structures that look like buildings—towering spirals of calcified wood and hollow vines, vast archways of fused branches that bridge the gaps between the floating islands of root-mass. These are the "scattered" remnants, but they are not built by hands. They are grown by the world itself, architectural hives for insects or fungi, forming cathedrals of cellulose that stand empty and dripping in the grey light. Occasionally, you find a smooth stone hemisphere, half-buried in the moss, perfectly rounded and polished. Not a tool, but a egg, or perhaps a geological tumour expelled by the earth long ago.

There is no technology here. The concept of a tool would be laughable to the things that live in this soup. Everything is biological, temporary, and reabsorbed. The rule of the world is entropy and reclamation. Nothing is wasted; everything is eventually incorporated into the root-mat.

The dynamic is agonizingly slow. You could watch a flower bloom for a week and see no movement. The changes happen across seasons that last for decades. The water rises inch by inch over a century, drowning a valley, which then becomes a lake, which then becomes a bog, which then solidifies into new land. It is a landscape of patience.

The water is everywhere. It slicks every surface. It drips constantly from the vast, woven canopy, creating a fine, constant mist that clings to skin and cloth. The taste of the air is sweet and cloying, like overripe fruit and wet earth. To breathe here is to drink.

You stand on a rise, looking out over the endless, dim expanse. The land rolls away in waves of green and grey, punctuated by the dark, silent silhouettes of the giant plant-towers. In the distance, the weather of the plateau is forming—a thick, heavy bank of fog rolling in like a tide, silent and inevitable. The air grows warmer as the mist arrives. The sounds of the slow grazers fade into the dampness. It is a world that has forgotten how to be dry, forgotten how to be hard, and has simply decided to become one vast, breathing lung.
