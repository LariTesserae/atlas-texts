The sun does not move here. It hangs at zenith, a blinding, white pinprick in a sky the color of polished bone, casting shadows that are absolute and razor-sharp. There is no dusk, no dawn, and no passage of time discernible by the position of the light. The heat is a physical weight, a humid, suffocating blanket that presses the air down onto the surface of the world.

The world below is entirely constructed. It is a vast, low-lying basin, a regional sprawl of hand-made geology covering hundreds of square miles. There is no "earth" beneath your feet, only a mosaic of brick, tile, terracotta, and dressed stone, fitted together with a precision that borders on the obsessive. This is the Great Plain, built by hands that have long since turned to dust, or perhaps by hands that never stopped building, though they are nowhere to be seen now.

Because the erosion is young—negligible, really—the edges of things retain the harshness of their creation. The brick pathways are not smoothed by feet; the corners of the stone retaining walls are still sharp enough to cut skin. It looks as though the construction crew finished their shift only an hour ago, yet the patina of age suggests the work is ancient. It is a frozen freshness. A new world that has been old for a thousand years.

Water is the master of this architecture. The environmental reading (1.9) is misleading; it is not simply "wet," it is "weeping." The entire constructed plain is designed to channel water, but it does so with a sorrowful slowness. Every flat tile has a slight lip to it; every brick is mortared with a gap that weeps clear, cool condensation. The air is so saturated with moisture that water constantly beads and drips from the undersides of bridges, the rims of canals, and the joints of the artificial cliffs.

The vegetation (2.5) treats the built world as its geological substrate. Vines as thick as a man’s torso do not grow from soil but from the cracks between massive, interlocking basalt blocks. Ferns unfurl from the mortar joints of cyclopean walls. The trees here are immense, grounded in shallow terraces carved from the stonework, their roots wrapping around the architecture like constricting snakes, slowly crushing the masonry in a slow-motion embrace that will never actually succeed, because the stone does not crumble. The lushness is aggressive, a riot of emerald and viridians that feels painted onto the gray and brown of the city-plain.

Yet, despite the abundance of life, the place is sparse (1.3) in its arrangement. You can stand in the center of a plaza the size of a town and see only three distinct trees, each perfectly isolated, surrounded by open, tiled flags. The density is low; the land does not crowd itself. There is a sense of cosmic hospitality, or perhaps a terrifying loneliness. There is enough room for everyone, and no one is here.

And then there is the fauna (2.2). It is abundant, but wrong. The creatures here have adapted to a world that refuses to change. They are not streamlined; they are geometric. There are birds with plumage of folded parchment that fly in erratic, stop-motion bursts, as if the animation of reality is skipping frames. Lizards with scales of shattered tile skitter across the brickwork, moving with a jerky, robotic cadence.

Deer-like creatures graze on the moss growing between the cobblestones, but they possess too many joints in their legs, moving like puppets operated by a nervous novice. They are not afraid. They watch with eyes that are solid spheres of obsidian, reflecting the unblinking sun. The ecology is a trap; it is a food chain that has been paused. Predators stalk prey in a slow, circular dance, neither moving to strike nor fleeing, caught in a tableau of eternal hunt.

The technology that built this place is primitive (0.9)—no steel, no glass, no plastic. Just stone, clay, wood, and bronze levers. Yet the engineering is impossibly advanced. Aqueducts carved from single logs, hollowed out by tools we cannot imagine, carry water across miles without a single support pillar, defying gravity through some trick of friction or acoustic levitation that suggests a physics we have forgotten. Simple pulleys the size of houses hang motionless in the air, their ropes of braided hair taut, lifting nothing but the air itself.

The sound (0.6) is the most unsettling aspect. It is mostly quiet. In a hot, wet, lush place full of animals, there should be a cacophony. Here, the silence is thick and textured. The water weeps, but it does not splash; it flows with a viscosity that suppresses sound. The animals move without rustling the leaves. A bird might open its beak to scream, but the sound arrives late, a thin, hollow echo that seems to come from a different room. When two stones rub together in the phantom wind, they grind with a distinct clarity that feels like it is happening inside your own teeth.

The weirdness (2.6) seeps in like the condensation. This is a reality breakdown. The geometry of the place does not always align. If you walk in a straight line, the horizon may tilt. The sun seems to get closer and farther away without changing size. Distance is elastic; a tower that looks a mile away might take three days to reach, or you might find yourself standing at its base after a single step. The "stable, frozen" nature of the dynamic (0.4) means this broken state is not chaotic or collapsing; it is a fixed error. The glitch has been petrified. The world has crashed, and the blue screen of death is this bright, hot, silent garden of stone.

Here, nothing changes (0.4), not because it is peaceful, but because the mechanism of duration is jammed. A drop of water hangs from the lip of a fountain, suspended, vibrating but never falling. A flock of geometric birds hangs in the sky, arrested in mid-flight. It is a museum of a moment, stretched out into an infinite, agonizing eternity. You walk through a gallery of instants, frozen into permanence, surrounded by the lush, wet, artificial beauty of a world that stopped trying to make sense a long time ago.
