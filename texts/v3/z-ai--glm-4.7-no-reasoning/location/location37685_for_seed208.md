The sound arrives before the shape of the land does. It is not a sound that ears hear so much as one that fills the chest cavity and vibrates the back teeth—a low, unending, polyphonic roar that makes the concept of silence feel like a half-remembered dream. This is the sound of the world breathing out, of ancient water pushing through primordial stone.

We stand on a coastal plain of impossible age. The elevation is so low that the horizon feels like a geometrical error, a faint bruise where the dark sky rests against the dark earth. There are no mountains, no hills, not even a meaningful ridge. The erosion here is absolute; it has reached the end of its long mathematics. The landscape has been ground down to its final, exhausted state—a flat, terminal monotony of dark, waterlogged bedrock exposed to the open air. It is a primordial surface, stripped of any pretense of youth, settled into a permanence that predates the memory of the planet.

It is cool here, a damp, heavy cold that clings to the skin like a wet cloth, born of the near-surface water. The land is saturated to the point of weeping. The bedrock—an amalgam of dense, black slate and rusted, porous pumice—exudes moisture. Every surface is slick. The atmosphere is so thick with condensation that the dim light seems to drip. There is no rain, yet the air is raining; water condenses out of the fog in heavy, erratic droplets, striking the stone with sharp, percussive clicks that merely decorate the overarching, deafening roar. The water table is the ground. To walk is to feel the rock flex slightly underfoot, to hear the squelch of water being displaced from micro-fractures that haven't known dryness since the world cooled.

There is no plant life. Not a single blade of grass, not a fossilized spore. This is a mineral world, stripped of green. Yet, it is far from barren. The absence of vegetation has allowed the fauna to occupy every niche, evolving in strange, lateral directions away from anything recognizable as terrestrial biology.

The dominant presence is a vast, scattered assemblage of pale, chitinous grazers. They look like the bastard children of sea slugs and armored millipedes, ranging from the size of a dog to the size of a small boat. They are bone-white and bioluminescent, emitting a sickly, grayish glow that provides the only local light. They move in slow, undulating lines across the weeping rock, using rows of silicate-crushing mandibles to scrape the algal-bacterial slimes and oxidized minerals from the stone. The scraping of millions of mandibles on slate is a vast, shivering rasp, a rhythmic grinding that underpins the great roar of the wind and water.

Above them, the air is crowded with dense, erratic clouds of flyers—creatures like leathery, eyeless manta rays, navigating the thick fog through echolocation. They emit rapid, high-pitched clicks that cut through the low frequencies, a staccato chatter that adds a frantic top layer to the soundscape. When they dive to pluck a grazer from the rock, the wet snap of their wings is like a whip crack, and the prey emits a sudden, pneumatic hiss of escaping internal gases. The ecosystem churns in a slow, brutal cycle of scraping and predation, an engine of life running on rock and water.

The dimness is profound. The sky is a perpetual twilight of heavy, waterlogged clouds that diffuse what little light reaches this flat world. Visibility is a matter of a few dozen paces before shapes dissolve into the wet, gray murk. In this gloom, the sparse, primitive structures of the place emerge like the ribs of some dead leviathan.

They are not buildings in any meaningful sense, but cairns and wind-breaks—scattered monuments to desperation. Built from the only available material, they are jagged stacks of the black slate, mortared together with the crushed shells of the grazers and the sticky, gelatinous slime they secrete. The tech here is raw and immediate: heavy stones lashed with braided sinew of unknown origin, rough hafts of petrified wood, and primitive, percussive tools left leaning against the wet thresholds. There are no metals. Everything is hand-wrought, crude, and subject to the rot of the omnipresent water. The structures are scattered across the plain at vast intervals, lonely outposts connected by nothing but the trampled paths of the pale grazers and the deafening howl of the wind.

The weirdness of the place creeps in through the gaps in the gloom. It is in the geometry of the grazers, whose bodies seem to flicker and elongate when viewed through the dense fog, as if their edges are not entirely fixed in space. It is in the stones themselves, which occasionally hum with a deep, tectonic resonance completely divorced from the wind—a low, moaning drone that sets the teeth on edge and makes the standing stones vibrate to the touch. It is in the fact that no matter how far one walks toward the faint, bruise-colored horizon, the distance never closes. The plain stretches on, a flat, weeping table of ancient rock, populated by pale, glowing horrors and the faint, primitive marks of something that tried, and failed, to build a shelter against the endless, deafening wet.
