The land sits low and heavy, a broad expanse of grey-blue mudstone and exposed shale shelves that extend beyond sight, unbroken by hill or ridge. This is a young geology—slabs still edged sharp from their emergence, strata tilted and raw, not yet softened by time. The sky above is a low, seamless pall of cloud, and the light that reaches the ground is dim, diffuse, and grey, casting no shadows. Everything feels close, as if the clouds press down upon the very surface of the stone.

Despite the open scale, the space feels profoundly crowded. The density is not of objects, but of saturation—of substance itself. The mineral world here is layered, thick, and over-present. Every surface weeps. Water does not flow in channels here; it exudes. It pushes up from hairline fractures in the shale, running in thin, constant sheets down the tilted slabs. It condenses out of the air, beading on the cold rock faces. It hangs in the atmosphere as a persistent, heavy mist. The ground is sodden, the water table so near the surface that to press a hand against the mudstone is to feel it yield slightly, like a soaked sponge. Pools of still, iron-tasting water collect in the uneven dips between slabs, their surfaces trembling with the constant drip and seep.

Nothing grows here. There is no green, no root, no stem—only the mineral, the fungal, and the strange. Across the weeping rock, a vast and intricate latticework of glassy, black mineral deposits has crystallized. It forms dense, interlocking shelves and needles, spreading over the stone like a recursive mineral algorithm. Some formations resemble shallow, broken bowls; others rise into jagged, fractal clusters that crowd together, nearly touching, layer upon layer of dark, reflective surfaces. They are not alive, yet they feel occupied—structured with a logic that does not belong to any recognized biology. They are too regular to be mere crystal, too chaotic to be construction. The air around them feels thick, as if their presence displaces more than just physical space.

And from them, a sound. A loud, ceaseless, mechanical-seeming resonance: a low, vibrating hum that rises and falls in slow, tectonic breaths. It is the sound of water under pressure being forced through narrow, resonant cavities in the rock—a subterranean wheeze and drone that vibrates in the chest. It reverberates off the dense clusters of black mineral, amplified, echoing in overlapping waves until the entire landscape seems to tremble with a single, drawn-out chord. There is no silence here. The sound is so constant it has texture, a weight that presses in from all sides.

Occasionally, within the deeper pools of standing water, a pale, soft-bodied thing—resembling a blind, limbless amphibian, but with too many gill-slits and a body that seems to phase slightly out of focus at the edges—surfaces to expel a burst of gas, then sinks again into the dark. It is the only visible movement for leagues, and it happens rarely, leaving long, tense intervals where the only action is the slow, patient seep of water and the interminable hum.

Dotted across this crowded, mineral expanse are the faint marks of something prior. Not ruins, exactly—the structures are too partial for that. A single, oblong ring of fitted stones, each block shaped with a precision that has no origin in this landscape, rises barely a foot above the mudstone, filled with water that is colder than the rest. Elsewhere, a single metal post, smooth and utterly uncorroded, stands upright in a crack in the shale, vibrating faintly in harmony with the ground-hum. There is no tech here, no tool, no device—only these scattered artifacts, half-consumed by the young rock, their purpose unreadable. They are like punctuation marks in a language that cannot be parsed.

The world changes slowly. The water rises and falls by fractions over hours. The mineral lattices grow by millimeters across decades. The sharp edges of the shale will take eons to round. This is a landscape of patience, of pressure, of dense, heavy presence. It is a place that does not care for you, that was not made for you, that operates on rules just adjacent to the comprehensible—a young, weeping, shouting rock, crowded with the strange, sitting low under a dim and heavy sky.
