You become aware of the grade first. The world does not open; it climbs. You are at the apex of a vast, primordial incline, an ancient shield-mountain that has spent hundreds of millions of years weathering into a tabletop of staggering elevation. The air is thin and carries the bite of a permanent, damp coolness—a temperature that doesn't chill the bone so much as it slows the blood, hovering always just above the point of frost. 

Here, at the roof of the world, water does not fall so much as condense. Thick mists roll in like breath from the valleys below, pressing against the stone and leaving everything dripping. Intermittent rain arrives not as a downpour, but as a persistent, fine smearing of moisture. The stone underfoot is ancient, its edges softened by eons of patient erosion. It is the oldest stable state of the earth—granite and gneiss ground down until it is nearly flat, a final form that feels less like a peak and more like the floor of a faded sky.

Upon this immense, near-level plain, a city has grown, and it has grown dense. The settlement clings to the high plateau in a desperate, layered sprawl, a warren of near-touching structures built from the same gray-green rock they stand upon. Because the space is flat but the winds are savage, the city built inward and downward. The buildings are squat and massive, featuring walls three meters thick at the base, communal rooftops that serve as thoroughfares, and narrow alleys that are more like crevices. It is a town-sized hive, saturated with presence, where the boundary between one dwelling and the next is merely a shared wall of un-mortared, fitted stone. 

Yet, this is not a dead place, nor a primitive one. Thrumming beneath the cobblestones and through the guts of the city runs an industrial nervous system. It is an architecture of steam and iron, adapted to the damp and cold. Coal smoke does not rise freely here; the heavy mist traps it, weaving it into the perpetual twilight. The light is dim—a perpetual dusk of gray-amber and rusted yellow, filtered through fog and the glare of low-voltage electrical filaments. Glass insulators on wrought-iron poles crackle with a faint, wet static, carrying power to the subterranean factories where massive, slow-moving engines punch and stamp, their rhythmic thudding the mechanical heartbeat of the plateau. 

The vegetation has claimed this industry. It is a lush, opportunistic ecology of mosses, liverworts, and ferns—a green so dark it is almost black. It drapes the smokestacks, carpets the slanted roofs, and chokes the gutters. The damp feeds it, and the ambient heat of the factories nurtures it. Root systems have fractured the ancient cobblestones; thick pads of moss muffle the footsteps of the few workers who move between the shifts. It is a layered saturation: stone, then iron, then moss, then mist.

Fauna is rare, but what survives here is strange. The ecosystem is in motion, driven by the damp and the heat exhalations of the city. You see the "vent-worms"—pale, finger-length creatures that live in the rusted grates of the factory exhausts, feeding on the sulfur-rich condensation. Occasionally, a cloud-winged moth, translucent and the size of a dinner plate, breaks from the mist to spiral around a failing streetlamp before vanishing back into the obscurity. There are no birds; the air is too thin for their songs. 

Instead, the dominant sound is a wet, mechanical respiration. It is mostly quiet, but it is the quiet of a held breath. The drip of condensation off a mossy iron girder. The muffled, subsonic throb of the stamping presses felt through the soles of the feet. The hiss of steam escaping from a cracked valve, instantly cooled back to water by the mountain air. It is a soundscape of pressure and patience.

The weirdness of the place settles into you slowly. It is the dissonance of extreme elevation flattened into an endless plain, crowned by a city that feels trapped in the throat of the mountain. There is the strange sensation that the city is not built *on* the rock, but *extruding* from it, a mineral secretion of the plateau itself. And there is the rust. Everything iron here is perpetually oxidizing, bleeding orange and red into the green moss and gray stone, so that the town looks like a living wound on the ancient earth. 

The factories produce something—gears, rivets, stamped plates—but they are always immediately consumed by the rust and the moss, requiring constant replacement, an endless mechanical metabolism fighting a losing battle against the primordial damp. The plateau, having spent epochs eroding into its final, flat rest, is now slowly growing again, accreting a living crust of industry and vegetation, an active engine buried in the green-gray quiet at the edge of the sky.
