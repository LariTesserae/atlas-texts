A wound in the earth, long healed over. A sinkhole or a collapsed cavern, now an open crater the size of a modest chapel, its throat opening into a vast, hollow belly of stone beneath the surface. The sky above is a blinding, unbroken blue, and the sun strikes the pale limestone rim so fiercely that the edge burns white. But down here, below the surface, the light changes. It falls in a thick, singular shaft, pooling on the cavern floor like a luminescent spill, while the extremities of the underground chamber recede into a hush of dust and shadow.

The space is enormous yet enclosed—a single, breathing pocket of emptiness. The walls are smooth, swept clean by ages of slow drip and settling air. There is almost nothing here. A few boulders, rounded and patient, sit where they fell millennia ago, their surfaces softened by a balance of erosion that has long since stabilized. The rock is dry to the touch, yet the air hangs damp and close. Condensation weeps from invisible seams high in the dark, gathering into a solitary, wandering trickle that finds its way down the cavern wall, feeding a shallow, concave basin in the floor no larger than a washbasin. It is the only water for miles—a seep, a dampness, a held breath of moisture in an otherwise thirsty stone throat.

Around this seep, a sparse congregation of pale, jointed stalks has taken root in the cracks of the rock. They are leafless, almost translucent, drawing what little sustenance they can from the intermittent moisture and the fierce, singular beam of light that ignites them from behind. They look like the bones of some ancient, slender creature, standing in a quiet ring.

But the emptiness is a deception. The chamber teems.

In the brilliant column of light, thousands of translucent bodies drift and spiral. They are the size of a thumbnail, perhaps smaller—flat, geometric, spinning like living tesserae. They have no discernible heads or legs, only sharp, crystalline edges that catch the light and fracture it, throwing tiny prisms against the cavern walls. They are silent, or nearly so. The sound they make is the sound of a thought: a faint, dry rustle, like the shifting of sand, or the turning of a thousand tiny pages. They fill the light shaft densely, yet they leave no mark on the space. They do not touch the walls or the floor. They exist only in the illumination, drifting in an unfathomable, slow orbit around the bright center of the room.

Where the light fades, other shapes cling. In the dim margins, shelled things—large, dark, and deliberate—cling to the concavities of the rock. They are perfectly still, their low, dome-like bodies armored in a dull, keratinous black. They do not move, but they are not dead; they are waiting, with a patience that outlasts the erosion of the stone itself. Their simple technology of survival—shell, grip, slow metabolism—is the only craft this place has ever known.

The world here operates on gradients so weak they are nearly imperceptible. The pool of water rises and falls by millimeters over the course of weeks. The light shaft inches across the floor from morning to evening, and the spinning creatures follow it, drifting as one body from the eastern wall to the west, never straying into the dark. The shelled watchers might shift a single limb over the course of a day. There is no wind. There is no sudden sound. The silence is profound, textured only by the occasional, reverberating *tok* of a droplet falling from the high ceiling into the stone basin—a sound that arrives like a small, sharp knock on the door of the void, and then dies away, swallowed by the vast, empty architecture of the earth.

It is a place that has found its final shape. Nothing is being built. Nothing is being worn away. It simply holds itself: a bright, damp hollow beneath the world, spinning with quiet, glassy life, keeping its slow, strange time.
