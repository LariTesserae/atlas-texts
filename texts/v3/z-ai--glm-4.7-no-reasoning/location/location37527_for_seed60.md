The air here is heavy, not with heat alone, but with the sheer weight of moisture. It sits in the low basin like a physical object, a humid blanket that smells faintly of ozone, crushed mint, and the metallic tang of slow-moving gears.

This is the Sunken Gallery, a place where the distinction between "growing" and "built" has eroded away long ago.

The elevation is deceptive. You are low, below the tree line of the surrounding world, enclosed by what feels like crater walls, but they are not rock. They are root. The entire floor of this depression is a single, knotted organism, a dense, liver-colored carpet of mosses that have thickened into meat. There is no soil here, only plant-substrate, spongy and yielding. Every step sinks an inch into the warm, breathing flesh of the ground.

Water is everywhere, yet it never pools into mud. It is wept. The terrain consists of rolling hummocks and pillows of vegetation, and from every crevice, every pore of the plant-matter, a constant, slow weeping of water occurs. It is a marsh without standing water; the ground is simply saturated to the absolute limit of physics. The water trickles in a million silver rivulets, vanishing back into the plant-matter before it can gather momentum. It forms beads on the surface of leaves like mercury, rolling and merging, an endless cycle of condensation and absorption.

The vegetation rating is nearly absolute. There are no distinct trees, no trunks, no open sky between canopies. Instead, the space is packed with towering stalks of translucent, fibrous plants that resemble calla lilies the size of cathedral pillars. They grow so densely that you cannot see more than ten feet in any direction. To move through the Sunken Gallery is to push through a fleshy, warm resistance. The plants here have integrated the inorganic. Vines run along the ground like copper wire, their structure reinforced with ferric deposits drawn from the deep earth. Leaves shimmer with a bioluminescent patina that glows with the low, steady hum of a dynamo.

This is the "industrial" aspect of the world, but it is not a factory made by men. It is a factory made by evolution. Scattered through the dense thickets are the remains of a previous civilization—or perhaps they are exoskeletons shed by the flora itself. Rusted iron pipes, the size of a man's thigh, emerge from the living ground and ascend into the green gloom, piercing through the canopy. They vent a steady, rhythmic plume of steam. They pulse.

The sound is loud but monotonous. It is the sound of a giant breathing. A low-frequency thrumming vibrates through the soles of your feet, emanating from deep beneath the root-mass. It is the sound of water being pumped, of sap coursing under high pressure, of metal expanding in the warmth. Occasionally, a wet *thwack* echoes through the grove—a seedpod exploding, or a piston of vegetation firing. The air is filled with the hiss of escaping steam and the constant, dripping *shhh* of the weeping walls.

There is almost no animal life, and what exists is rare and strange. You might see a "Copper-Wing," a bird-like creature that seems to be made entirely of sheet metal and feathers, perching motionless on a rusted pipe. It does not chirp; it clicks, a tiny, ticking metronome in the overwhelming green din.

The light is normal, filtered through the translucent, glass-like upper leaves of the canopy. It takes on a verdant, aquarium quality. Shadows are soft and indistinct because the light bounces off the wet, reflective surfaces of the plants.

The weirdness lies in the stillness of the motion. Everything is moving, but it is all slow. The giant lily-pillars sway with a glacial grace. The pipes drip in complex, mathematic rhythms. The ground itself rises and falls as if the entire basin is the lung of a sleeping leviathan. It is a "post-rain" landscape that never stops raining; the condensation drips from the upper machinery of the canopy onto the soft, packed floor below, creating a mist that hangs perpetually at waist level.

It feels like being inside the clockwork of a jungle. The boundaries of the area are sharp—where the dense wall of pillars meets the sheer cliff of the crater—but within, it is a maze of warm, wet, industrial biology. The scent is intoxicating—a mix of wet rust and sweet, overripe nectar. You feel watched, not by eyes, but by the pressure of the life around you, a packed, saturated existence that tolerates your presence only as a minor fluctuation in its slow, rhythmic processing of the world.
