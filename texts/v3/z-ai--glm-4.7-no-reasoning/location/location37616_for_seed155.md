The sky up here is a pale, washed-out steel, the color of old water, hanging low over a plateau that feels less like land and more like the deck of a ship made of flesh. This is the High Bough, the Ferrous Verge, a place where the concept of "ground" has been entirely negotiated away by biology.

It is cool—a persistent, misty chill that settles into the joints and never leaves. The air tastes of ozone and wet moss. There is no true earth beneath your feet. At an elevation that would leave ordinary mountains gasping for snow, the planet has expressed itself as a single, continuous organism. The terrain is vegetable, but vegetable in the way a mountain is stone: massive, structural, and indifferent.

This is the Living City.

The vegetation parameter here is absolute. It is not merely that plants grow here; it is that architecture has been subsumed by them. The "buildings" are hollowed-out xylem towers, vast woody columns that once served as the arteries of some planetary tree-god. Now, they are occupied. Iron pipes, rusted through with decades of exposure, are wrapped tightly in serpentine vines, the metal acting as a trellis for the chlorophyll. The distinction between a water tower and a massive, swollen cactus has vanished; the cactus *is* the water tower, its flesh pressurized and humming with hydraulic force.

It is an urban environment, but one designed for a population that either vanished or was transformed. Streets are canals of damp, slippery bark, wide enough for heavy industry yet currently occupied only by the slow drip of condensation. The density is sparse, a strange paradox given the "total" vegetation. The city is huge—a regional sprawl of organic spires and fungal plazas—but it is empty of crowds. There is a profound sense of vacancy. You could walk a main avenue for an hour and see nothing but the swaying of fern-fronds the size of sails and the distant, rhythmic clanking of the unseen machines.

The erosion is ancient. The stone isn't worn down by wind; it has been smoothed by centuries of friction with living matter. The metal gantries connecting the towers don't look bolted on; they look grown through, the metal having been eaten and excreted by the plant-life until the steel and the cellulose are molecularly married. This is the final form of the world, a state where decay has been arrested by a suffocating abundance of growth. It is the end of life, but in a fertile, senile way—nature sitting on its front porch, waiting to die, but still watering the flowers.

Moisture is constant but not overwhelming. It is a dampness of the "damp cloth" variety. The air hangs heavy with fog that clings to the rusted gear-work protruding from the "walls." Water doesn't flow in violent rivers here; it sweats from the walls of the towers, pooling in shallow, quiet depressions before being sucked back up by the root-systems that serve as sewage and plumbing.

The sound is what breaks the silence. It is loud, a low-frequency industrial thrumming that vibrates in the teeth. Somewhere deep within the super-structure of the city, massive engines are turning. They are biological engines—great chambers of muscle and nerve tissue stimulated by electricity—but the noise is pure factory: the shriek of metal on metal, the hiss of steam vents, the repetitive *chunk-whoosh, chunk-whoosh* of massive pistons processing the air. The city is digesting itself, refining its own sap into fuel, a process that requires deafening, invisible machinery.

Fauna are rare. There are no birds, at least not in the sense of flying creatures that sing. The occasional movement in the corner of your eye is likely a semi-autonomous pruning drone, or perhaps a slow-moving, arboreal sloth-thing that feeds on the electrical cables. Life here is solitary. If there are predators, they are static—traps made of trigger-hair and rapid-closing petals that lie across the avenues like open manhole covers.

The weirdness lies in the seams. The reality breakdown is subtle at first. The light is normal—daylight, filtered through the canopy—but sometimes the shadows detach. You will look at a distant tower and see that it is looping, its top folding into its base in a way that hurts the eyes. The geometry of the place is non-Euclidean; streets that seem straight converge on points that are kilometers away in an instant. Time feels viscous. Standing near a large, pulsating vein of the city, you feel your own heart rate slow to match the rhythm of the pumps, dragging you down into the geologic timescale of the plant.

It is an active place, but its activity is the slow grinding of tectonic plates. Branches shift position over the course of days, the groaning of the wood sounding like hulls of ships breaking up in a storm. The industrial pulse drives this motion, a heartbeat that pushes nutrient slurries through pipelines the width subway tunnels.

This is the Green Gomorrah. A high-altitude garden of rust and chlorophyll, damp and cold, humming with the labor of machines that no one built, waiting for a master who has long since dissolved into the mulch.
