The air is heavy with the taste of copper and the tang of cold, stagnant water. This is the Low Sump, a depression in the world’s crust where the sky seems to sit only a few feet above your head, perpetually pressed down by a ceiling of bruised, violet-gray clouds.

It is a wet place, undeniably. The water parameter here is high, but it lacks the freedom of a river or the openness of a sea. Instead, the moisture is intrusive. It weeps. The ground is a compacted, silty shale, fractured into innumerable plates like the skin of a dying reptile, and from every fracture, water oozes. It rises up through the boot-soles, it beads on every metal surface, it drips incessantly from the overhangs of the scattered, rusting machinery that dots the landscape. The atmosphere is so thick with condensation that it feels less like breathing air and more like inhaling a cold, wet mist. The rocks themselves seem to sweat, slick and dark, covered in a film of iridescent oil that shimmers in the gloom.

But there is no green here. The vegetation parameter is a null set. There are no reeds, no moss, no algae to soften the hard lines of the world. Biology has been excised from the plant kingdom. Instead, the "flora" is purely mineral and chemical. Where a swamp might see cattails, here there are growths of rust—tall, fragile spires of oxidized iron that rise from the silt, fragile as glass and stained the color of dried blood. Clusters of white salt crusts form in delicate, fractal patterns that mimic frost, and the weeping rocks build up stalagmites of grey calcite that look like fingers reaching out of the mud. It is a garden of corrosion, quiet and still.

The scale is intimate, almost claustrophobic. The Sump feels like a room that has been forgotten. You can see the boundaries—a ridge of volcanic glass to the north, a wall of tumbled, eroded concrete blocks to the south—giving the space the feeling of a cathedral nave, albeit one dedicated to a drowned industry. The ceiling of cloud is low, lit by a diffuse, dim glow that suggests a sun burning far away through a thick blanket of smog.

Yet, within this small, dark room, the density is overwhelming. The space is packed. It is a clutter of verticality. Old industrial pilings, slick with black rain, stand like the petrified trunks of iron trees. They are spaced so closely that movement requires weaving through them. The density is auditory, too; the quiet is not true silence. It is a "mostly quiet" filled with the proximity of a thousand small sounds—the plink of water hitting metal, the hiss of steam escaping a vent, the click of hard chitin on stone.

Because there is life here, though it is not the life that dominates the silence. The fauna is abundant, but it is a life suited to the wet, the dark, and the industrial. This is the ecology of the Scavengers.

Swarms of small, crab-like creatures inhabit the rust-reefs. They have shells of dull brass and operate with a frenetic, mechanical energy, scuttling over the wet stones in ceaseless search for organic detritus or chemical deposits. They are everywhere—in the water, on the pipes, clinging to the ceilings of the broken concrete bunkers. Larger things, too, move in the shadows. Eel-like shapes with exoskeletons of riveted steel plates undulate through the shallow, silty pools that cover the floor. They move with a fluid, predatory grace, hunting the brass-crabs. It is an ecosystem in motion, a predator-prey cycle playing out in the half-light, fueled by the decay of the old world.

The tech level is industrial, but it is a ghost of industry. The structures here are scattered, partial remains of a factory floor that sank into the earth. Massive, toothed gears lie half-buried in the mud, their edges worn smooth by time, slowly turning as the water shifts their weight. Pipes, thick as a man’s torso, arc over the landscape like the ribs of a whale, dripping condensation. There is no electricity anymore, but the machines possess a residual energy—heat exchangers still groan with thermal expansion, and pistons gasp rhythmically, driven by the hydrostatic pressure of the groundwater. It is a landscape that is processing itself, grinding down under its own weight.

Erosion has settled this place into a strange equilibrium. The metal is not rotting away into nothingness; it is transforming. The rust formations are stable now, a part of the geology. The concrete has been smoothed by the endless flow of water, rounded and soft, no longer sharp. It is a world that has stopped fighting its environment and succumbed to it, achieving a wet, heavy balance.

There is a strangeness to it, a weirdness that permeates the mist. The water doesn't just obey gravity here; at times, it seems to coil, defying the wind, drawn up toward the rust spires by magnetic static. Occasionally, the brass-crabs will synchronize, thousands of them clicking their claws in a rhythmic wave that travels through the swarm, a communication that sounds like a typewriter typing the language of the dead.

And always, the sound. The plinking. The dripping. The wet slap of a foot—or a claw—in the mud. The distant, hollow echo of water falling into a deep reservoir somewhere in the dark. It is a small, dense, wet room of a world, teeming with mechanical life and decaying industry, cold and dark, but aggressively alive in its own way.
