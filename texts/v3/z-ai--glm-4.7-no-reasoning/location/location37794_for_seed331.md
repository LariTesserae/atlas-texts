The light here is not merely bright; it is comprehensive. It spills from a sky that lacks the harsh gradation of blue, instead presenting a smooth, pearlescent white dome that seems to emit illumination equally from every point. Shadows exist, but they are soft, diluted things, lacking the bite of contrast. The air is thick and warm, holding the heat of a slow breath. It feels heavy against the skin, a humid blanket that smells faintly of ozone and decaying peat.

You are standing in a low, wide bowl of a landscape, a terrain that has settled over eons into its final, most comfortable shape. This is the ancient end-state of geography: the mountains have long ago worn themselves into hills, and the hills into gently rolling swales. There are no sharp edges here. The stone, visible in outcroppings that punctuate the green, is rounded and polished, as if the world itself has been tumbled smooth by the tides of time. It feels less like a wilderness and more like a ruin that has been allowed to succeed.

The water is constant but intrusive. It does not gather in vast lakes, but weeps from the earth. The ground is spongy, a deep matrix of black soil and saturated moss that gives slightly underfoot. Small pools, no larger than puddles but seemingly bottomless, dot the landscape like mirrors. From the weeping rocks—rounded boulders that sit alone in the clearings—water traces silent paths down their flanks, perpetually condensing from the heavy air or sweating from the stone’s cooling pores. The sound of water is visual rather than auditory; you see the glitter and the flow, but the air swallows the noise.

Vegetation is lush but sparse, a paradox of the place. The plants that do exist are solitary giants. They are not crowded, but spaced with mathematical precision, each claiming a wide territory of root-system. The flora consists of fleshy, spiraled fronds and trees with trunks like woven bone-white mats, supporting canopies of broad, translucent leaves that turn the white sunlight into a cool, green gloom beneath them. There is just enough distance between these giants to make the landscape feel empty, a cathedral hall populated by a few solitary pillars.

The emptiness, however, is an illusion. The air is thick with life. Fauna is overwhelming, an invisible, teeming pressure. If you stand still, the surface of the water shatters with the frantic skipping of minute, multi-limbed skaters. The bark of the white trees ripples with the migration of translucent beetles that flow like sap. The ground itself seems to move—a carpet of shifting, iridescent centipedes and millipedes that churn the mulch, their chitinous plates clicking against one another in a sound that is felt through the soles of the feet rather than heard by the ears. It is a silent riot of biology, a frantic, writhing world that exists on a micro-scale, overwhelming in its quantity but minute in its individual form. Nothing here is large enough to challenge a human, but everything here is numerous enough to bury you.

Scattered through this green, humid quiet are the artifacts of a physics that forgot its limits. They are not buildings, but geometries left behind. A perfect sphere of obsidian, three meters tall, hovers just above the surface of a stagnant pool, rotating slowly without friction. It is motionless in the air, suspended by a field that distorts the space around it. A few yards away, a tetrahedral frame floats, its edges glowing with a faint, violet light that leaves no afterimage in the eye. These structures are sparse, isolated islands of impossible engineering in a sea of biological decay. They do not rust or moss over; the overwhelming insects refuse to crawl on them. They are the clean, silent bones of a dead god, ignoring the wet and the warmth, asserting a logic that transcends the erosion of the valley.

There is no wind. The only motion is the slow drift of the hovering artifacts and the frantic, chaotic vibration of the insects. The silence is profound, a high-frequency hum that seems to come from the light itself. It is a place of stagnation that feels alive, a terminal landscape where water has wept the mountains into sand, and the only things remaining are the infinite, small hungers of the soil and the perfect, uncaring shapes of a higher math.
