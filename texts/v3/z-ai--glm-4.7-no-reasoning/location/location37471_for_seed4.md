The scale is deceptive. To an observer standing miles away, there is nothing here but a dark, empty shelf of granite, a young wound in the earth where the stone has only recently split and risen from the crust. The rain falls, cold and incessant, sliding off the sharp, un-weathered edges of the rock. But to step down into the fissure is to leave the world of stone and enter a room of flesh.

This is the Gullet, a micro-canyon no wider than a town square but deeper than the height of three men. It is a pocket ecosystem, a city built by things that do not know they are building.

The air is heavy with water (1.9), a cold (0.4), clinging mist that tastes of iron and decay. It is never truly dry here; every surface is slick, weeping a constant, thin film of moisture that feeds the riotous green. The light is near-absent (0.1), a bruised purple twilight that persists even at noon, filtered through a thick canopy of woven vines that seal the ceiling like a thatched roof. Down in the Gullet, the sun is a myth.

The vegetation (2.8) is not just present; it is the architecture. There is no bare ground. The floor, the walls, the pillars that support the ceiling—all are plant matter. The "buildings" (1.9) here are mounds of compacted sphagnum and fungal shelves, hardened by age and chemical secretion into a substance sturdy enough to support the weight of a crawling creature. These mounds are arranged in concentric circles, spiraling down toward a central, black pool. They are windowless and doorless, but they are occupied.

It is an urban sprawl of the primitive (1.1). There are no furnaces, no steel, no wheels. There is only stick and bone and mud. Thin, peeled branches sharpened to points form crude palisades around the larger mounds. Ropes of braided kelp hang lines strung with drying fish-skin and damp moss. It is a city of pre-metal desperation, a shanty town constructed by beings with hands but no fire.

And those beings are everywhere (3.0).

The fauna is overwhelming, a crushing density of life crammed into this small, dark space. The sheer biodiversity is claustrophobic. The walls of the Gullet are not stone; they are a writhing tapestry of sleeping bodies. Thousands of small, chitinous things cling to the vertical surfaces—pale, eyeless crabs; multi-limbed arboreal mammals that wrap their tails around the roots to sleep; sleek, wet salamanders the size of dogs that pant in the cool air.

This is the rule of the Gullet: *Do not fall.*

To slip and fall into the gaps between the sleeping mounds is to be consumed. The ground, where it is visible between the mounds of woven moss, is not solid earth. It is a moving carpet of beetles, worms, and scavengers, constantly churning through the detritus that falls from above. The density is sparse (1.2) in terms of open space, but the "presences"—the living things—are packed tight. There is room to walk, if you are careful, but there is no room to be alone.

The soundscape is quiet (1.0) yet horrifying. A constant, low-level hum of wet movement—squishing, clicking, the soft rustle of scales on moss—and the intermittent, sharp *snick* of a trap closing or a predator testing its jaws. There is no howling, no roaring. The creatures here hunt by stealth and vibration. The silence is watchful.

The erosion is young (0.7). The granite walls that frame this green hell are sharp enough to cut. They have not yet been rounded by centuries of rain. This gives the place a jagged, unfinished feel, as if the ecosystem is rushing to fill a vacuum that opened only yesterday. The rock tears at the vegetation, and the vegetation fights back, sending crack-roots deep into the fresh fractures, shattering the stone further.

The weirdness (1.6) lies in the motion. The city is active (2.1). The "buildings" breathe. The larger mounds are actually the nests of the colony-builders, massive termite-like organisms that secrete a hard, ceramic-like paste from their mouths to reinforce the mud walls. As you walk through the narrow, muddy streets, you can feel the walls throb with the internal metabolism of the hive. There is a synchronization here—the traffic of the creatures follows invisible currents. When the rain intensifies, the entire population seems to shift, a tidal wave of fur and shell retreating deeper into the spiral structures.

It is a world without sun, where the water is the air, the plants are the houses, and the animals are the mortar. It is a dark, wet room, buried in the cold earth, filled with the quiet, heavy breathing of a million things trying not to be eaten.
