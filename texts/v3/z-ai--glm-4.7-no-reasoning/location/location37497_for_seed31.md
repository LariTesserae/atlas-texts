The air is heavy, not with heat, though the temperature is warm and skin-comfortable, but with suspension. It is a place of eternal yesterday.

This is a high plateau, thrust upward from the world below by forces that have not yet had the time to soften their work. Because the erosion is low, the edges of the plateau are terrifyingly sharp. The cliffs do not slope; they sheer, vertical walls of grey-white stone that look like they were cracked open mere hours ago. The rock is young, jagged, and rough, lacking the rounded shoulders of ancient mountains. It cuts the sky with geometric precision.

The scale is immense. Standing on the edge, one can see for miles, a sprawling openness that swallows the gaze. The ground spreads out in a flat, vast expanse, punctuated only by the sudden, dramatic geometry of the stone cities and the stark protrusions of un-weathered pillars.

Water is the defining condition here, but it is a water that does not flow. The rivers are still, glass-smooth canals that reflect the sky with perfect clarity. Because the land is young, the water has not yet carved deep valleys; instead, it rests in shallow, wide basins and runs in channels that seem unnaturally straight, as if drawn by a ruler. The ground is perpetually wet—a marsh-like consistency without the mud. The soil is packed hard by the dampness, covered in a low, resilient sheen of standing water that ripples only when the wind shifts. Moss grows in thick, vivid sponges on the stone, vibrant and lush, fed by the constant weeping of the rock faces where groundwater seeps unceasingly from the fissures.

There is vegetation, but it is sparse and architectural. There are no towering forests to clutter the view. Instead, the flora is isolated: single, massive fern-like trees that stand alone in the openness, their roots gripping the jagged stone. They are spaced with mathematical regularity, leaving vast negative space between them. The green is deep and saturated, contrasting violently with the pale stone, but there is a sense of balance. The emptiness is as present as the life.

In the center of this vast, wet garden stands the settlement.

It is a town, or perhaps a small city, built entirely of the surrounding stone. It is urban in its organization—dense streets and clustered structures—but primitive in its execution. There is no mortar that relies on chemical curing; the buildings are constructed through immense, dry-stone masonry. Blocks are stacked with such precision that they appear monolithic. The technology is strictly manual: hand tools, simple levers, and the friction of stone on stone. There are no metal reinforcements, no glass windows, only openings covered by heavy, woven reeds or simple wooden shutters.

The city feels dormant. It is built to last, squat and sturdy, huddled against the open sky. Yet, the streets are empty. The silence is absolute.

This is a world without fauna. There are no birds in the sky, no insects humming in the moss, no distant calls of mammals. The ecology is entirely botanical and geological. The only movement is the slow growth of the moss and the inexorable, microscopic creep of the water down the stone faces.

The "rules" of this place are dictated by its lack of dynamism. It is a frozen equilibrium. The clouds hang in the sky, drifting so slowly they appear painted. The water level in the canals never rises or falls; the overflow is exactly matched by the evaporation or the seepage. The temperature holds steady, a perpetual warm afternoon. Nothing rots, because nothing dies naturally; the vegetation simply grows until it reaches its limit and then halts, waiting.

The light is normal and clear, casting sharp shadows from the jagged rock formations. But without the sound of life, the light feels tactile, like a physical weight. You can hear the "sound" of the silence: a faint, ringing pressure in the ears, and the distant, soft drip of water striking stone—a rhythm that has been the same for a thousand years and will be the same for a thousand more.

It is a place of immense, quiet potential, like a held breath that never needs to be released. It is beautiful in its sterility, a high, wet, sharp-edged garden that refuses to change.
