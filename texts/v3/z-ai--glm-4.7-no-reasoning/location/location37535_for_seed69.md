This is the Deep Canopy—that continental roof of the world where elevation and atmosphere conspire to trap warmth and water in an eternal, suffocating embrace. The vast plateau sits miles above any sea, yet it weeps. It has always wept.

Standing here, you feel the altitude in your chest, the thinness of the air, but you do not see the sky. You do not see the sky at all. Above you is only the Black Canopy—a hundred-meter ceiling of interlocking flora so dense that it chokes out the sun. Light is a foreign concept here, a memory of a place that does not exist. The darkness is absolute, a physical weight pressing against your eyes. You navigate by the glow of the bioluminescent and the furious heat of the machines.

The wet warmth condenses on the underside of the leaves, forming a perpetual, heavy rain that never passes through a cloud. It drips, it streams, it weeps from the porous rock faces. The stone here is ancient, its jagged edges worn down by eons of this chemical drip, leaving behind curves and hollows, basins of tepid black water and walls of slick, dark basalt that sweat as if alive. The atmosphere is so saturated that breathing feels like drinking; the air is a warm, viscous soup.

And in this darkness, the world is screaming.

The sound is tremendous, a bone-rattling industrial symphony that never ceases. Because woven into the roots and the vines and the sweating stone are the Engines. They are vast, continent-spanning networks of iron and steel, rusting and weeping oil, yet still turning. No one remembers who built them. Perhaps they grew here, like a mechanical fungus. They are scaled production on an impossible level—great pistons the size of redwoods hammer into the earth, extracting geothermal heat to power electrified webs of copper wire that spark and snap in the perpetual damp. The factories do not produce goods; they produce pressure. They produce motion. They keep the plateau alive, cycling the heavy water, grinding the atmosphere, their exhaust venting as superheated steam that feeds the canopy above.

Amidst this mechanical gigantism, life has not just survived; it has erupted. The ecology is layered, dense, saturated. Every square inch of rock or pipe is colonized. Thick, leathery vines—black as tar and veined with pulsing, bioluminescent sap—strangle the iron supports. Fungal shelves the size of city buses jut from the ancient stone, their gills glowing a sickly, electric blue, casting the only illumination in this sunless world. In that trembling azure light, you see the architecture of the weird: flora that does not photosynthesize, but *electrosynthesizes*, feeding off the massive currents arcing through the wet metal.

The fauna is abundant, and it is terrifying. Shapes move in the blue-black murk, clicking and thrumming with electrical signatures. There are things with too many legs that scuttle along the high pipes, maintaining the ancient machinery with mindless, industrious precision. In the pools of weeping water, blind leviathans surface, their skin covered in insulating nodules, their mouths wide enough to swallow a shipping container. Predator and prey do not hunt by sight or sound here—they hunt by reading the magnetic fields disturbed by movement, by tasting the electrical discharges of fear in the water. The ecosystem is a violent, churning circuit, powered by the grid and the heat of the earth.

Structures—human structures, orAor whatever came before—exist only as pathetic scars. A single, half-swallowed bunker of concrete, its roof torn off by a growing root the thickness of a train carriage. A rusted catwalk leading nowhere, suspended over a boiling, mechanically aerated lake. A solitary iron door set into a cliff face, its handle worn smooth by a million years of dripping condensation. These are not settlements; they are artifacts, swallowed whole by the dense, mechanized biology.

The landscape is vast beyond comprehension. You could walk for weeks and never leave the same Engine sector. The weather is localized and violent—a storm here means a pipe bursting, venting a geyser of scalding white vapor that condenses instantly into a torrential downpour, flooding the walkways and sending the blind, clicking predators into a feeding frenzy. The ground trembles constantly with the heartbeat of the pistons below.

This is a world in motion, a world in balance, but it is a terrifying balance. The old rock holds the weight of the water and the iron; the machines keep the roots fed; the roots hold the machines together. It is an ecosystem of steam and blood and rust and electricity, running in the dark, a mechanical god dreaming in the wet heat, forever weeping, forever turning.
