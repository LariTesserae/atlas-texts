A vast ridge of embryonic stone tears upward into the warm, suffocating dark. This is a place being born—or forcing itself into being—through the violent, ceaseless assertion of raw geology. The elevation is extreme; you stand on the spine of a mountain range that does not yet fully exist, a towering scaffold of pressure and heat that pushes so high it seems to scrape against the thick, blind weight of the atmosphere. 

There is no water. Not a trace of moisture, not a vapor, not a drop of rain has ever fallen here. The world is anhydrous, a sealed furnace where chemistry happens without solvent, where minerals melt and fuse directly in the dry heat. The warmth is total and radiating, rising from the active stone beneath your feet and pressing down from the lightless sky above. It is a warmth that feels like a physical grip, sweatless and airless, a temperature that belongs to a kiln rather than a climate.

Beneath this warmth, the terrain is in a state of frantic, continuous emergence. The erosion parameter is almost non-existent because there has been no time for decay; the wind hasn't yet smoothed the edges, and water has never carved a channel. Instead, the landscape is raw and unformed—a churning topography of pressure ridges, thrust faults, and heaving escarpments. The stone is blistered, pushed up in colossal, pillow-like folds of obsidian and dark, vitrified basalt. It is a volcanic glass world, but the glass is still soft, still cooking. 

This is the source of the turbulence. The land is not still; it is caught in a hyperactive cycle of Solaris-like creation and destruction. The ground lifts in slow, imperceptible surges, only to fracture and drop away in sudden, shuddering collapses. Great slabs of the ridge sheer off into the invisible depths, exposing glowing, magma-veined interiors that immediately cool into new, jagged geometries. The space is regional in scale, an entire province of this forming world, yet it feels claustrophobic. The density is sparse—there is a terrifying amount of emptiness between the features. You walk on a narrow, rising causeway of cooling stone, and to either side, the ground drops away into absolute, lightless chasms. The emptiness is an active participant here, a vast volume of dark, superheated gas that swallows space and sound.

What little light exists is bioluminescent, born from the stone itself. It is not the light of a sun or a flame, but a faint, sickly phosphorescence bleeding from the cracks of the cooling rock—a dim, ochre glow that only serves to outline the sharp, unnatural silhouettes of the ridge. It illuminates nothing, merely emphasizing the overwhelming darkness that presses in from all sides.

There is no flora, no fauna. No root survives this heat, no cell holds water here. Yet, the world is not entirely dead. It supports a simple, non-recognized biology—a fungal-mineral half-life that thrives on the boundary of creation and destruction. In the deepest crevices, where the fresh stone cracks and hisses, patches of lithotropic fuzz cling to the walls. It is a strange, rust-colored powder that blooms instantly when exposed to the superheated gases venting from the rock, metabolizing the sulfur and iron, only to die back into inert ash the moment the vent shifts. It breathes with the mountain, a scattered, mindless ecology of heat and mineral.

Amidst this furious geology, there are marks. The built environment is vanishingly faint, scattered and mysterious. Partial remains are sunk into the stone—not ruins of a city, but the footings of single, isolated structures, now actively being swallowed by the emerging ridge. A single, sloping wall of a material darker than the basalt, utterly impervious to the heat, juts from the cliff face at a sickening angle as the mountain grinds past it. Further along the spine, a perfect circle of drilled holes perforates a fresh lava flow, an artifact caught mid-birth, the rock welling up around the strange indentations. There is no tech here, no tools left behind, only the skeletal impressions of something that tried to anchor itself to this embryonic world, and was subducted by its turbulent growth.

The soundscape is sparse but profound. Because the air is so dry and the space so open, the acoustic properties are strange—damped, yet carrying. There is no wind. Instead, there is the deep, rhythmic subsonic boom of the mantle pushing upward, felt in the marrow more than heard by the ear. Above this constant pressure, the only distinct sounds are the sharp, violent *cracks* of obsidian cooling and fracturing, echoing once across the vast emptiness before being swallowed by the dark. Occasionally, a high, thin hiss escapes from a new fumarole, a gas jet screaming into the void for a few seconds before the stone shifts and chokes it off.

To stand here is to stand inside an engine. It is a world of extreme vertical scale, of oppressive heat and absolute dryness, where the laws of geology are accelerated into a waking nightmare. The ground beneath you is a temporary arrangement, a fleeting shape in a continuous, violent extrusion. It is a dark, raw, and turbulent world, perpetually on the verge of becoming, and just as perpetually tearing itself apart.
