The air is thick and moves like syrup. It is not merely humid; it is soupy with suspended spores and the damp, respiration-heavy heat of an enclosed lung.

We are in the Pulp.

The "landscape" here does not stretch to horizons. The parameters of scale are deceptive; we are high up, yes, nearly two thousand meters above the theoretical base of this world, but the distance to the next wall is barely the length of a city block. This is a "hidden" place in the literal sense: it is a single, vast, enclosed chamber, a geological bubble hidden within the strata of a dying world. It is a pocket universe of stone.

The ceiling is lost in a gloom of low-hanging clouds, gray and roiling with a warmth that feels feverish against the skin. The light is dim, a perpetual twilight equivalent to a stormy hour before dawn, yet the temperature pushes against the limits of comfort—a tropical, sweating heat that clings to everything.

Because of the extreme age—2.9, deep time, the settled final form of a world—the floor is not jagged. Erosion has had billions of years to work its will. Everything that could be sharp has been ground down. The floor is a smooth, undulating plane of packed, red-brown dust and polished stone. There are no pebbles, only sand and boulders worn into perfect, low-slung eggs.

And it is dry. The water parameter is a ghost. There is no standing water, no rain, no rivers. This is the great paradox. The vegetation is lush—a riot of creeping vines, fleshy succulents, and towering, trunk-like fungi—but they drink nothing. They are xerophytes evolved for a void, sucking moisture from the air itself or tapping into the deep, mineral oils that seep through the cracks in the ancient stone. The chemistry here is non-aqueous; the sap of the plants is thick and clear like glycerin, sticky to the touch, sealing their moisture inside against the desiccating atmosphere.

The density of the construction makes the chamber feel like a terrarium house. It is urban, but not in the sense of a modern metropolis. This is "primitive" urbanism: a favela of stone and bone that has grown organically over the millennia. Structures are carved directly into the giant, smoothed boulders or stacked using slabs of stone without mortar, balanced with a pre-industrial precision. There are no metals; the technology is restricted to hand tools, levers, and the use of massive animal bones as beams. It looks like a necropolis slowly being reclaimed by a jungle, though the city is alive.

The city is not for humans, nor does it seem empty. It is built for scale—the scale of the overwhelming fauna.

This is the cacophony. The sound is loud, a wall of noise that hits you the moment you visualize the place. It is the sound of a million legs clicking against stone, the chittering of mandibles, the hollow thrum of wing beats, and the low, bowel-shaking growls of the apex predators moving through the narrow streets.

The fauna is overwhelming. The ground is a moving carpet of life. Armored beetles the size of dogs scuttle through the alleyways, their shells clicking with a sound like typewriter keys. In the air above, larger things glided—ray-like creatures with leathery skins navigating the warm updrafts, their silhouette distinct against the dim light.

Because the space is "pocket-scale" yet "high elevation," the architecture clings to the sides of the pit. We are standing on a network of scaffolding and bridges woven from giant sinew, looking down into the dim center. The ecology is vertical. Predators hang from the ceiling like bats, dropping down on the climbers below. Prey species have evolved to be climbers, scaling the sheer, polished rock walls with hooked claws, fleeing into the maze of the hewn stone dwellings.

The dynamic is active. It is rush hour in the jungle. A hunt is happening nearby. You can hear the struggle—the crashing of a heavy body through the stone shanties, the screech of something being caught, the sudden swarm of scavengers rushing in to claim the scraps.

The atmosphere is heavy with the scent of spice and rust. The plants here are defensive; the "lush" greenery is comprised mostly of thorny briars and neurotoxic ferns that pulse faintly in the dim light. When the wind blows through the openings in the high rock vents, it carries a pollen that glitter, making the air look like it is filled with gold dust, but inhaling it induces a lethargy that keeps the urban prey animals docile until the moment of the strike.

It is a place that feels like a tumor on the world—a hot, dry, loud pocket of hyper-aggressive life trapped in a bottle of ancient stone. It is crowded, sweaty, and dangerous, a vertical city of beasts where water is a myth and the blood of the living is the only coolant the engine knows.
