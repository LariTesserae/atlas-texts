A throat in the mountain. That is the first impression—not of a cave, but of anatomy. The elevation is extreme; you have climbed to a place where the air should be thin and cold, where winds should strip the flesh from the bone. Instead, you descend into a crevice barely thirty meters across, a plunging wound in the high plateau where the world's heat has pooled for millennia.

The scale is intimate, cathedral-sized but irregular. No smooth nave here—the walls convulse inward, layered and dense, pressing close enough that in some passages you must turn your shoulders. There is no architecture, no built thing, no artifact of human intent. Only the raw geology of a fissure that opened when the mountains were young and has since settled into its own erosion: a slow, patient balance of water and stone that has carved the space into its final, enduring shape. The walls are not carved but *digested*, their surfaces fluted and dimpled, fretted with pockets and overhangs, every centimeter occupied by something.

Darkness is absolute, or near enough. The sky above is a cracked seam of dark grey, admitting a light so attenuated it serves only to prove the dark exists. Your eyes never adjust; they simply give up. You navigate by touch and by the pressure of the space against your skin.

And yet the place is warm. Disturbingly so. The stone breathes a wet heat, a geothermal exhalation that rises from somewhere deeper still. Condensation is constant—water does not flow here so much as *sweat*. Every surface films with moisture that beads and trickles in erratic paths, pooling in the hollows the erosion has prepared. You hear it: a persistent, complex drip and trickle, the sound of a hundred tiny negotiations between water and stone, layered into a continuous quiet roar. It is never silent. The water speaks in overlapping rhythms, and the stone answers with resonance, the close walls amplifying every drop into a low, reverberating hum that you feel in your sternum more than hear with your ears.

The vegetation is sparse but not absent—life here has made a specific compromise. In the cracks where just enough moisture accumulates and just enough warmth rises, there grow things that are almost not plants: black threads, slick as gut, clustering in wet mats that hang from the overhangs like viscous hair. They do not photosynthesize in any way you would recognize. They are warm to the touch. They drink the condensation and they drink something else too, something in the steam, and they have grown slowly, over geological time, into forms that serve the space. In a few places, where a wider pocket admits the faintest reflected glow from the wet walls, there are growths like pale calcified corals—not animal, not mineral, some old negotiation between the two. They protrude from the stone in branching shelves, creating the only geometry in this place that suggests anything other than erosion.

But the flora is footnote. The fauna is everything.

You hear them before you understand what you are hearing—that the sound is not all water. There is a chittering, a clicking, a low continuous rasp like a thousand knives being honed. The walls move. In the darkness, the walls are *moving*. What you took for texture is population. What you took for stone is shell.

Crabs. Or the thing that crabs become when they are given warmth and darkness and eons of undisturbed stone. They are everywhere. They coat the walls in layers, each the size of a human palm, carapaces flattened and optimized for the crevice life, their legs tucked, their claws folded, occupying every surface in a living crust. They are pale—not white, but the color of wet limestone, of old bone, of the steam itself. Their eyes are reduced to dark pinpricks, useless in the permanent night, but their antennae are long and interlocking, a constant mesh of sensory filaments that read the air's moisture and temperature and, you realize with a prickling at your neck, *your presence*.

They do not scatter. They are too densely packed to scatter, and they have had no predators here for longer than your species has existed. They shift, instead, in slow tides—a compression here, a relaxation there, the entire population moving as a single organism of ten thousand parts. The sound is their movement: the click of claw on shell, the rasp of leg on stone, the creak of carapace articulating, multiplied into a continuous texture of noise. It is loud, not with violence, but with *presence*. The place is occupied. You are inside something living, something that has filled this cathedral of stone for millennia and has become, in its way, the organ of the mountain itself.

They feed on the moisture and on each other in slow, patient cycles—moulting and dying and being consumed in a gradient so gradual it is nearly static. The dead are not removed; they are eaten where they rest, their shells slowly incorporated back into the matrix. You stand on a floor that is not stone but compacted chitin, layers upon layers of the dead, a slowly accumulating sediment of crab. The erosion below—the patient dissolution of stone by water—is matched by the accumulation above: the patient construction of chitin by life. The two processes have reached an equilibrium. The space neither grows nor shrinks. It simply persists, filled with this slow, loud, warm, wet metabolism.

There is something else. At the deepest point you can reach—where the crevice narrows to a throat and the heat becomes almost intolerable and the darkness takes on a different quality, a thickness—the crabs thin and then vanish. In their place, the walls are studded with something that is neither animal nor plant: growths like swollen amber blisters, translucent and turgid, each the size of a fist, protruding from the stone in clusters. They pulse. Not with light, but with something slower—a contraction, a dilation, a rhythm unrelated to any heartbeat you know. When you place your hand near them, you feel heat radiating in waves synchronized to their movement. They are breathing. They are digesting. They are doing something to the water that seeps past them, changing its chemistry, releasing the steam that feeds the crabs and the thread-plants and the warmth that makes this high-altitude wound livable.

You do not know what they are. They have no name because no namer has come here, and if one did, the darkness would prevent seeing, and if they could see, the language would have no category. They are not weird in the way of aberration; they are weird in the way of *completion*—as though the mountain has grown its own organs, as though geology has decided it requires metabolism, and has produced these slow, pulsing, amber glands at the interface of stone and heat and water.

The place does not change quickly. Nothing here does. The crabs moult over weeks. The thread-plants grow over decades. The amber glands pulse over hours. The water carves over centuries. The chitin accumulates over millennia. You are present for a single frame of a very long motion, and the place knows it. It does not acknowledge you. You are a temporary discontinuity—a source of heat and moisture the crabs will investigate with their antennae, a displacement in the air the glands will ignore, a sound the water will drown. You are inside the mountain's throat, and it is swallowing you with patience, and it does not care.

You leave the way you came: by touch, by feel, by the slow recognition that the walls are retreating, the crabs thinning, the sound simplifying, the heat lifting. You emerge onto the high plateau and the cold strikes you like violence and the wind takes your breath and the stars are out and you are *visible again*, and you realize that in the crevice you had become part of something—not participant, not observer, but component. A small, temporary, warm-blooded presence in a system that did not need you but made room for you in its density, as it makes room for everything, by slow compression and patient accommodation.

The mountain throat closes behind you. The crabs shift to fill the space you occupied. The water continues its carving. The glands pulse in their amber clusters. The sound goes on, unchanged, a continuous low roar of life and water and stone, audible even here, even above the wind, as though the mountain is humming with what it contains.
