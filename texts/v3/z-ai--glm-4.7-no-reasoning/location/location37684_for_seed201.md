It begins with the light.

At this elevation—somewhere above the practical ambitions of mountains, in that thin throat of the world where air becomes a suggestion rather than a promise—the sun does not merely illuminate. It scalps. The light here is bright to the point of violence, bright with the emptiness of atmosphere that cannot diffuse it. Shadows fall with surgical precision, black cuts against the snow-bleached stone, and there is no gentle gradient between what is touched by the sun and what is not. You are either bathed in radiance or you are nowhere at all.

The place is small. That is the first thing the body understands before the mind catches up—a boundedness to it, a sense of walls even where walls do not exist. Perhaps it is the cirque: a great bowl carved into the peak's flank by ice that has long since retreated to higher, angrier shelves. The cirque is no more than a hundred meters across, a cathedral of open sky and vertical stone, and its floor is a chaos of shattered granite and the stubborn green things that have learned to call this cruelty home.

Young stone, this. The glacier left only centuries ago, and the rock it exposed has not yet learned to be round. Everything is edge. The boulders that litter the cirque floor are angular as broken teeth, their faces still wearing the scrape and polish of ice—striations that catch the light and throw it back in fractured ribbons. There are no gentle talus slopes here, no patient decomposition into soil. The mountain is still being born, still cracking and splitting with the freeze-thaw fury that rules this altitude. At night, you can hear it: the sharp report of stone splitting along faults, the small avalanches that whisper down the headwall and go no further. The mountain is talking to itself, working something out in the language of fracture.

And yet—life. An improbable amount of it, given the cold, the thinness, the rock's refusal to become dirt. The cirque floor is crowded with cushion plants, those dense mossy humps that hug the ground like green thought, like patience made physical. *Silene acaulis.* *Androsace.* They cluster in the leeward hollows, in the slight depressions where wind cannot scrape them away, and they are absurdly green against the gray—green in a way that seems like an argument, a dissent filed against the regime of stone and ice. In late summer, which here lasts perhaps three weeks, the cushions bloom: tiny pink and white stars so small they could be dismissed as flecks of quartz, but they are not, they are alive, they are the peak's only tenderness.

Taller plants persist in the shelter of the larger boulders. Stubborn grasses that go blond by August. A few stunted willows that grow horizontal to the ground, their branches tracing the wind's shape like a diagram of suffering. They flower too, catkins pale as bone, and their pollen sometimes dusts the rocks in a fine gold powder that the next wind removes. Nothing gets to stay long here except the stone.

The water comes and goes. There is no stream, no steady melt—instead, moisture arrives in irregular gifts. A snow squall that drops two inches and then is gone by noon, the white retreating in dark patches down the rock faces like something ashamed. Condensation that beads on the cushion plants at dawn, each droplet a small lens of sky before the sun burns it off. And in the cirque's lowest point, where the stone forms a natural bowl, a tarn: a pool no bigger than a bed, ringed with ice even in August, so clear that you can count the pebbles on its bottom and see, quite distinctly, the cloud formations passing overhead in the water's still surface. The tarn is the cirque's single mercy. Everything comes to drink.

And that is where the fauna gathers—drawn to water in a place where water is rationed, drawn to each other in a place where warmth is communal. The pika are everywhere. Small, round, earless-seeming, they dart among the rock rubble with an urgency that borders on comedy, harvesting grasses and caching them in small haypiles beneath the boulders. Their calls pierce the quiet—short, sharp alerts that ripple from one territory to the next, a network of sentinels tracking the sky for what comes from above. And above, always, the eagles. Golden eagles that ride the thermals boiling off the south-facing headwall, their shadows crossing the cirque floor in slow, predatory ellipses. They are patient. They have nowhere else to be.

Marmots graze the cushion plant margins in family groups, fat and tawny and seemingly unconcerned, but their sentries stand upright on the highest rocks, and when they whistle—when they sound that long, descending alarm—the whole community dissolves. The marmots vanish into cracks in the stone as if the rock itself had swallowed them, and the pika fall silent, and for a moment the cirque holds its breath, and the only sound is the wind.

The wind. It must be spoken of, because it is the true ruler here. It comes over the headwall in downdrafts that can knock a standing person to their knees, and it carries grit, fine crystals of snow or sand that sting any exposed skin. It makes the willows scream, a high thin sound like wire being drawn. It scours the rock faces clean of any ice that has loosened during the day. It is the agent of erosion, the patient sculptor that will—over millennia—round these sharp edges, fill this bowl with soil, bring the gentleness of lower elevations up to meet the peak. But not yet. Not now. Now it is just force, just cold breath, just the mountain reminding everything that lives here what the terms are.

The terms are these: stay small, stay low, stay alert. Grow fast and die fast. Take what moisture comes and store what you can. Do not reach upward; the wind will take you. Do not stand alone; the cold will find you. The ecosystem operates at a pace that seems frantic and glacial at once—frantic in the brief growing season, when every organism races to flower and seed and cache before the snow returns; glacial in the long dormant months, when the cirque goes white and silent and the only motion is the ice creeping down from the permanent fields above, advancing a few centimeters a year, retreating a few centimeters back, working its slow argument against the rock.

There is something else here. Something that does not fit the calculus of cold and stone and survival.

At the cirque's eastern margin, where the headwall curves and a narrow ledge provides footing, there are marks. Not petroglyphs—nothing so intentional or so legible. Just marks. Scrapes in the rock that are too regular to be natural, too weathered to be recent. Vertical lines, grouped in sets of three, carved deep enough that they have survived the wind's abrasion. They appear on three separate boulders, always on the face most sheltered from weather, always at a height that would require a human to stand. And beside one grouping, a shallow depression has been worn into the stone—a cup mark, or a seat, or something for which no clear analogue exists.

Someone was here. Someone with tools hard enough to scratch granite—primitive tools, hand-tools, nothing that implies industry or mining or conquest. Someone sat in this cirque, in this murderous cold, and carved lines into rock. And then left. Or stayed. There is no way to know. No remains, no charcoal, no bones. Just the marks and the seat-shaped hollow and a feeling, entirely subjective and therefore suspect, that this place was once chosen. That someone understood something about this cirque that the data of geology and biology cannot capture. That they came for the light, perhaps. For the way the sun fills this bowl at midday and turns the stone silver and the water to mercury and the green cushions to emerald, and for a few hours the cold retreats to the shadows and the cirque is almost warm, and the pika run and the marmots bask and even the wind pauses to let the warmth accumulate.

It is a small paradise. It is the size of a room, and it is as harsh as a mountain can be, and it is full of life that has no business being here, and it is beautiful with a beauty that does not forgive. The marks on the rock suggest that someone once recognized this—recognized it and stayed long enough to record their presence, and then vanished into the lower elevations where survival is easier and such records are unnecessary.

Below the ledge with the marks, someone has stacked stones. A cairn, no more than knee-height, balanced with a care that the wind has not yet defeated. It is recent—the stones still dark with the lichen they tore from their resting places. It marks no trail; there is no trail to mark. It simply declares: *someone is here now, or was here recently, and they wanted you to know.* It is the most primitive of architectures, the simplest possible intervention in a landscape, and it is enough. It changes everything. It means the cirque is not forgotten, means the peak is still climbed, means that the terms—stay small, stay low, stay alert—have an exception written into them for the kind of animal that looks at a place like this and chooses to enter it anyway, carrying nothing but hand-tools and the stubbornness to carve lines into rock that will outlast them by centuries.

The quiet, when it comes, is profound. Between the pika's alarms, between the stone-splitting reports of the freeze, between the gusts that roar over the headwall, the cirque falls into a silence so deep it seems to have weight—a silence that presses against the eardrums and fills the throat. It is the silence of a place where nothing is happening, where even the processes of erosion and growth have paused to take breath, where the light stands still on the rock and the tarn holds its perfect mirror and the whole bounded space waits for the next iteration of wind or cold or bloom.

And then a pika calls, and the marmots emerge, and the eagle's shadow crosses the floor, and the cirque resumes its furious, fragile business of being alive at the top of the world.
