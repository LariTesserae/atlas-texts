The heat here is a physical weight, a heavy, wet blanket that presses down from above and rises up from the rot-rich earth. It is an absolute darkness, broken only by the rare, dim phosphorescence of dying moss or the sickly, intermittent sputter of a failing electric cable swinging somewhere high in the gloom. This is the Bottomlands, a sprawling, sunless basin where the elevation never dares to rise, and the air is too thick to breathe without effort.

Despite the oppressive heat, the world is not on fire; it is stewing. The water parameter suggests a deceptive dampness—there are no open oceans or rushing rivers here, but rather a state of perpetual condensation. The air is so saturated with moisture that it sweats from every surface, forming constant, heavy beads that slide down rock and chitin alike. The ground is a sponge, a packed, semi-solid mat of decomposing matter that squishes underfoot, releasing pockets of hot, humid gas with every step.

The vegetation is lush but aggressive, fighting for every square inch of space. Because the density is overwhelming, there is no concept of "undergrowth" versus "canopy"—it is all one solid mass of plant life. Massive, broad-leaved ferns press directly against the trunks of towering, moss-draped cycads. Vines as thick as a man’s torso strangle the branches above, creating a woven ceiling so tight that it blots out whatever faint light might exist at the zenith of the cavernous space. The green is deep and dark, almost black in the absence of sun, Photosynthesis here happens with a desperate efficiency, fueled by the volcanic heat rising from the earth's crust rather than solar radiation.

And then there is the fauna. Overwhelming does not begin to describe it. This is a world of pure biological density. In the dark, the air vibrates with the movement of living things. The ground is a carpet of insects, beetles with shells like polished obsidian, millipedes that move in winding rivers of legs, ants that march in armies that take hours to pass. Because the scale is open, extending far beyond the immediate view, the sheer volume of life is incomprehensible.

But the sound is mostly quiet. This is the paradox of the place. With so many creatures, one would expect a cacophony, but the dynamics are slow and the atmosphere acts as a muffler. The millions of legs scuttling over the rot blend together into a low, steady white noise—a constant, dry rustling, like silk rubbed against silk. There is no birdsong, no chattering monkeys, only the slow, heavy exhalations of massive, docile herbivores that move through the dense thicket like ghosts, their bodies brushing against the leaves with a slow, rhythmic *swish-hush, swish-hush*.

The erosion parameter tells us this place is established. The rocks are rounded, worn smooth by centuries of humidity and the friction of countless crawling bodies. The terrain is settled, a network of low hills and damp sinkholes that have remained unchanged for millennia. The weather, such as it is, is a slow cycle: the heat builds, the humidity spikes until the air cannot hold it, and then a warm, heavy mist falls—not a storm, but a gradual saturation that slickens the already-slick surfaces.

The strangest element, the one that clashes with the primitive, overgrown facade, is the technology. It is industrial, mechanized, but dead. Massive, rusted iron pipes, some as wide as a house, burst from the jungle floor and disappear into the darkness above. They are silent, cold to the touch despite the heat, leaking a slow, oily drip that the insects seem to avoid. Occasional junction boxes, corroded by the damp, hum with a faint, dying electric current—the only source of light in the abyss. These are not ruins of a city; they are the infrastructure of a machine that consumed this landscape long ago and then died, leaving the biology to reclaim and overgrow the steel skeletons.

The rules of this world are simple: adapt to the crush, or be crushed. Space is the most valuable currency, and every vertical surface is claimed. The temperature demands lethargy; movement is slow, deliberate. Predatory strikes happen with lightning speed, but life otherwise moves at a glacial pace to conserve energy in the stifling dark.

It is a place of claustrophobic vastness. You stand in a pocket of air barely wide enough to spread your arms, yet you know the jungle extends like this for hundreds of miles in every direction—a dark, hot, infinite cellar of life, humming with the silence of a billion crawling things.
