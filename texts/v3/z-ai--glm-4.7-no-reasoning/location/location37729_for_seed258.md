The plateau hangs. It does not sit upon the earth; it is suspended in the breath of a warmer, higher climate, a place where the atmosphere is so saturated that water condenses out of nothing, beading on every surface without the excuse of rain. This is a landscape in utero—a geography that has not yet decided to be stone. Erosion has barely touched it. There are no carved valleys, no ancient river channels, no worn edges. It is raw, embryonic geology, but the geology is not rock. 

Everything here is made, yet nothing here is manufactured. The built environment is absolute, a total architecture of living mass, but it is achieved without a single tool, without a single machine. There is only the plant. Vegetation is not a feature of the landscape; vegetation *is* the landscape. It is the substrate, the structure, the skin, and the bone. 

We walk on a vast, interlocking pavement of rhizome and root—thick, Calloris-like tissues that fuse at the seams, forming a continuous, springy floor that stretches across the high plateau. There is no dirt beneath us, only deeper layers of woody parenchyma, descending into a plumbing of colossal sap-conduits. The space is open, a regional expanse of living architecture, but it is sparsely populated by presences. The biological architecture fills the volume, yet the entities within it are scattered, separated by vast, echoing distances of green geometry. Fauna is a rarity, a rumor; an occasional pale, limbless grazer slipping between the buttresses, leaving a trail of mucous that is instantly absorbed by the thirsty floor. 

Water is everywhere, pulled from the humid, warm air by the vegetable superstructure itself. Weeping is a structural necessity. The overarching canopy—composed of billions of arching, load-bearing stolons—is perpetually condensing the fog. Water droplets form at the apices, swell, and fall in a continuous, synchronized percussion. This is the sound of the place: a deafening, rhythmic dripping, a hydro-mechanical heartbeat that reverberates through the hollow stems. The sound is loud, a white noise of fluid transfer, a thumping, aqueous din that fills the ears and vibrates in the soles of the feet. It is the sound of a factory with no machines, only the relentless, biological industry of osmosis.

There is a profound strangeness to the static nature of this world. It is a frozen equilibrium. Nothing germinates; nothing decays. The plateau exists in a state of perpetual, time-invariant noon. The light is steady, diffuse, and ordinary—a flat, warm illumination that casts no harsh shadows but gives everything a saturated, chlorophyll-heavy depth. The air is still. There is no wind, only the slight, thermal sighs of the mass transpiring. The dew-point is a physical law enforced by the architecture itself. 

Because the plant is the architecture, the space is defined by topiary on a civil engineering scale. Vast chambers of living cellulose open into corridors of bundled vascular bundles. There are halls where the roots have grown together into weight-bearing columns, sweating moisture from their barkless skins. There are empty atria, tens of meters across, where the only furniture is the steady, vertical rain of condensation falling from the vaulted, leafless ceiling into a central, throat-like catchment of absorbing fibers. The emptiness is balanced by the saturation of the medium—the space is sparse in inhabitants, but the environment itself is dense with life, thick with the smell of warm sap, tannin, and the ozone-tang of static water.

It is a world that is just coming into being, yet it never changes. It is an emergent fortress of xylem and phloem, locked at the exact moment of its maximum expansion. There are no seasons to force a shedding, no droughts to force a retreat. The high plateau simply breathes in the wet warmth, sweats out the excess, and holds its shape—a massive, living-made tundra of green tissue, weeping endlessly into its own depths, roaring with the sound of water recycling through a body that is as large as a valley, and as permanent as a mountain that has forgotten how to erode.
