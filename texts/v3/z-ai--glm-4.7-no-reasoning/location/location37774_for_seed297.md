The sky is a perfect, static sheet of white, like a sheet of paper left in the sun too long—bleached but not dark. It casts a flat, shadowless illumination across the horizon. There are no clouds here, only a diffuse, ambient brightness that seems to emanate from the air itself. The light is normal in intensity, but it lacks direction; it has no source and no terminator, creating an eternal, high-noon stillness.

You stand on a vast, open plain, a regional expanse that stretches until the curvature of the world bends the features upward. The elevation is low, a basin of sorts, but the ground does not feel like dirt or stone. It feels like the skin of a sleeping giant.

The terrain is young, violently fresh. The earth here has not settled into the complacency of old hills; everything is sharp. The ground is fractured into jagged plates, tessellated polygons of obsidian and glass that press against one another like the scales of a black reptile. In the gaps between these sharp plates, the land falls away into sudden, geometric crevasses—neat, angular fissures that plunge into a darkness the light cannot penetrate. There is no dust, no sand, no grit of erosion. The edges are knife-blade keen, untouched by the blunting hand of wind or rain.

Because there is no water.

The desiccation is absolute, yet the landscape violates the rules of dryness. It is a desert of moisture, void of the hydrogen oxide, yet dense with an impossible vitality. The vegetation here is lush, a suffocating mat of growth that does not drink. Vast, writhing networks of silver vines and translucent, crystal-leafed shrubs cling to every available surface. They grow in layers, high-saturation thickets that blur the line between plant and mineral. They do not wilt; they do not droop. They stand rigid, caught in a perpetual state of explosive bloom.

The temperature is warm, a humid heat that presses against the skin like a heavy wool blanket, yet the air is perfectly dry. It is a "dry humidity," a phantom sensation of moisture that the flora seems to thrive on. They are not photosynthesizing; the flat light offers no energy. Instead, they draw sustenance from the hum—the deep, infrasonic vibration that permeates the void.

This is the technology of the place. It is beyond modern, beyond the physics of steam or circuitry. It is a landscape of nanoscale resonance and locked-time fields. The world is not dead; it is merely paused in a state of frozen equilibrium. The very atoms of the ground and the plants are engaged in a high-tech stasis, a containment field that arrests entropy. The "youth" of the terrain is a product of this: the landscape is constantly being refreshed, re-knitted, its sharp edges maintained by a trillion invisible machines that reject the concept of "weathering."

It is silent. Mostly quiet. The wind does not blow, for there are no pressure gradients to drive it. The air is a static medium. If you stand perfectly still, you might hear the faint, electric hiss of the maintenance field—a sound like the ocean held inside a shell, but synthesized, cold, and precise.

The fauna is rare, and what exists here is not animal in the way biology understands it. Occasionally, a thing moves through the crystal thickets. It might look like a deer, composed entirely of floating glass shards held together by magnetic fields, or a bird that flies by refracting light rather than flapping wings. They do not eat or sleep. They are glitches in the rendering, temporary coalescences of the system's processing power. They leave no tracks on the glass plates.

The weirdness lies in the breakdown of reality between the organic and the artificial. The "plants" are not grown; they are assembled. If you were to cut a stem, it would not bleed sap; it would vent superheated plasma or collapse into a pile of inert carbon dust. The colors of the vegetation are hyper-real, shades of violet and neon green that vibrate in the eye, colors that no natural pigment could produce.

The open scale of the place is deceptive. You can see for miles, but the distance feels flat, like a diorama. This is because the world is not fully "there." It is a projection, a stabilized pocket universe where the dynamic nature of time has been excised. Things never change. The crystal leaf that is falling from a bush right now has been falling for a thousand years and will fall for a thousand more, caught in the middle of its descent, never quite touching the ground.

It is a lush, sharp, vegetated hell of perfection. It is a garden that does not grow, only exists. It is beautiful in the way a scalpel is beautiful: precise, clean, and utterly indifferent to life. There is no water to nurture, only the relentless, humming architecture of a world that refuses to decay.
