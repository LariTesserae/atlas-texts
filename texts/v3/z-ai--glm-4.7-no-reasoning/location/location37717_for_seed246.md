The throat of the world has been cut, and it sings a deafening, ancient song.

You are standing in the upper resonating gallery of a city that died before humanity learned to chip flint. The elevation is immense—you can feel it in the thinness of the air, the way the sky presses down like a lid of dark, ice-laden glass—but you cannot see the horizon. You are enclosed. This is a cathedral-scale void, a single vast chamber hollowed out of the summit of an primordial mountain. 

The erosion here is the defining fact of existence. This place has reached the absolute end of its life cycle. There is no rubble, no sharp debris, no potential left to be worn away. The walls, the arches, the sprawling infrastructure of this prehistoric urban center have been ground down by eons of moisture and wind into the smooth, rounded contours of river stones. The architecture has settled into its final form: a topology of gentle swells and shallow depressions, resembling a massive, petrified throat or the inside of a colossal, fluted shell. The original material—whatever stone or substance this city was carved from—is gone, leaving only a dense, iron-hard conglomerate that looks like packed ash and bruised purple twilight.

It is cold. Not the biting, wind-whipped cold of a peak, but a heavy, damp cold that sinks into the marrow. Water is everywhere, though there are no streams or pools. Moisture condenses constantly out of the frigid, saturated air. It beads on the gentle bulges of the eroded architecture, weeping down in perpetual, silent tears that feed the lush overgrowth. The air tastes of ozone, wet stone, and the copper tang of old blood.

And it is loud. Unbearably loud. The sound is the reason this place exists in its current state. A deep, tectonic bellow—like a didgeridoo crafted from the mantle of the earth—booms through the chamber. It is the mountain breathing, a subterranean wind forced up through fissures in the bedrock, amplified by the acoustics of the dead city's rounded infrastructure. The roar is so constant it becomes a physical pressure, vibrating in your teeth and blurring the edges of your vision. Within that drone, overtones cascade: high, keening whistles that shift pitch with the humidity, and the rhythmic, percussive thump of air-pressure pockets collapsing against the stone. 

In this deafening, damp cold, life has run riot. The vegetation is intensely lush, but it is a cold-adapted, parasitical lushness. There are no leaves, no green. The surfaces are choked with thick, overlapping mats of glassy, white fungi and trailing veils of silver lichen. Bulbous, gelatinous growths—translucent and veined with pale amber—cling to the ceiling, pulsing faintly in time with the acoustic shocks. Thick cables of vine, tough as sinew and black as wet oil, drape across the smooth avenues, anchoring the fungal forests to the deeply-aged walls. When the condensation drips from the vines, it hits the fungus, and the impact produces a sharp, cracking hiss that momentarily cuts through the sub-bass roar.

Fauna is rare, but what exists is perfectly adapted to the void. In the shadows of the rounded alcoves, you might catch the glimpse of something pale and many-legged, scaled in translucent keratin, moving with the slow, deliberate grace of a creature that never needs to hurry. There are perhaps a dozen of them in this entire vast space, feeding on the amber-pulsing fungi, navigating by the vibrations in the stone. They are nearly deafened themselves, blind, relying entirely on touch and the seismic rhythms of the chamber.

The strangeness of the place creeps up on you. It is in the acoustic geometry. The city was not built for shelter, or worship, or defense. The smooth, fluted channels, the perfectly parabolic basins carved into the floor, the spiraling erosion of the pillars—all of it forms a massive, organic instrument. The city was grown and carved to be played by the wind. The infrastructure is the mechanism. 

And the place is active. It is not a dead ruin; it is an ongoing process. The wind cycles shift, and as they do, the resonating frequency of the chamber changes. The fungus swells or retracts with the barometric shifts, breathing in and out. The condensation builds to a critical mass, then falls in a sudden, heavy rain that drums against the bio-mats, sending ripples of motion through the vines. The few pale creatures migrate from alcove to alcove, chasing the warmer microclimates created by the shifting acoustic nodes5 standing waves. 

You stand in the center of this grove-or-room sized space, isolated in the near-void of the chamber. The urban density is there in the layered construction, but it has been hollowed out by time until there is almost nothing per volume—just vast, cold air, the overwhelming roar of the stone-throat, and the relentless, wet creep of the silver vines. It is a place at the end of its timeline, stripped of all noise but its own voice, singing its final, eternal song into the damp dark.
