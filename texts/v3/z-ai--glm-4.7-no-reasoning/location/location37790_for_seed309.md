You descend. The passage down is not cut but *grown*—a throat of interlocking mycelial arches and slick creepers that tighten as you pass, releasing you only when you reach the subterranean cathedra. What opens before you is a chamber roughly the size of a cathedral nave, but there is no stone here. No earth. No mineral substrate at all. There is only plant, and the things that crawl upon it.

This is the Floor—a vast, shallow basin far beneath the surface, and it is entirely colonized. The ground beneath your feet is a dense mat of rhizomatic wood-vine, spongy and thick, compressed by centuries of growth into something that bears weight like timber. The walls, the colonnades, the vaulted ceiling—everything is alive, or was alive, or is the product of something living. Towering fungal trunks serve as pillars, their brackets fusing overhead into a lacework canopy that blocks the sky you never see anyway. Between them, in great scattered gaps, hang the infrastructures of industry.

The light is perpetual and dim. Bioluminescent lichen clings to every vertical surface in scabrous patches, emitting a sickly amber glow that pools in the crevices and leaves the high reaches dark. But the brightest light comes from the works: copper pipes and iron gutters laced through the living architecture, carrying steam and hydraulic fluid, glowing at their joints with heat. Glass tubes full of cultured algae run in linear racks along what must be called streets—though they are merely the negative space between the massive root-buttresses of the greater plants. The algae-tubes pulse with green light, casting shifting cellular shadows on the fungal walls.

It is warm here, close and humid, the temperature of a greenhouse, of a lung. The dampness is not standing water but a universal condensation. Every surface weeps. Moisture beads on the pipes, runs in trickles down the mycelium, drips from the overhead lattice in irregular rhythm. There are no streams, no pools—only this endless exhalation of water from the air itself, drawn up through the great taproots from some deep aquifer below. The air tastes of iron and chlorophyll and the sour tang of fungal spore.

This place is old. The erosion is not of wind or water but of life itself, grinding against life. The fungal pillars show the settled weathering of centuries: their bark is scarred, hollowed, bored into by generations of insects and epiphytes. The pipes are not new either—they are riveted iron, blackened with scale and verdigris, sagging where the living wood has shifted beneath them over decades, bent to accommodate the slow growth of their hosts. The factory-grass—engineered reeds with taproots that drink from the condensation channels—grows in orderly rows in the wider spaces, harvested by automated sickles that clang and snick on timers you cannot see.

And the sound. God, the sound. It is a roar that you feel in your chest more than hear with your ears. The mechanical layer: the chug and wheeze of hydraulic pumps buried in the root-mass, the high screaming whine of bone-saws in the processing alcoves cutting fungal wood into substrate blocks, the rhythmic clattering of conveyor belts made of chitin-linkage running on tracks grown into the floor. The biological layer: the constant susurrus of feeding, of mandibles grinding, of tens of thousands of bodies moving and breathing and clicking. And beneath it all, a low, almost subsonic thrumming—the heartbeat of the great mycelial body that underlies the entire chamber, pulsing nutrients through its network at the pace of geology.

Because this place is not empty between its structures. It is *sparse* in the way a cathedral is sparse—you can walk between the pillars, you can see the far wall dimly—but the negative space is occupied by fauna. Overwhelmingly. The air is thick with movement. Moths the size of your hand spiral around the algae-tubes, their wingbeats a papery flutter beneath the mechanical din. Armored isopods the length of your forearm scuttle along the pipe-trunks, cleaning the condensation with their mouthparts, clicking as their segments articulate. Above, in the dark vault, something large moves—a colony of gliding web-spiders, their silk cables laced between the fungal brackets, swaying with the vibration of the machines. The floor crawls. Every crevice hosts a nest, a burrow, a shell. You cannot stand still without something landing on your shoulder, investigating, tasting the salt of your skin with a proboscis like a heated needle.

The fauna is not incidental. It is the workforce. It is the system. The isopods are the maintenance crew; they are drawn to the pipes by pheromone signals the factory releases through its exhaust vents. The moths are pollinators, maintaining the algae cultures in the tubes. The spiders harvest their silk for the conveyor linkages. And deeper, in the processing vats where the fungal wood is broken down into cellulose pulp, there are maggots—great pale legless things the size of dogs, bred for heat, writhing in masses around the digestion tanks, keeping the reaction temperatures stable with the furnace of their own metabolisms. The industry here is not mechanized alone. It is *chimerical*—half engine, half organism, the boundary between livestock and component entirely dissolved.

The weirdness of this place is not in any single impossible thing but in the totality of the integration. There is no separation between the built and the grown, between the living and the manufactured. The pipes are not *in* the fungus; the fungus has *grown around* the pipes, incorporating them, feeding on the condensation they provide, dying where they overheat and being replaced by new growth in a slow, balanced cycle. The machines do not destroy the ecology; they are its weather, its seasons, its selection pressure. The creatures here have evolved *for* this environment—evolved to live in the vibration frequencies of the pumps, to feed at the cycle of the harvest, to breed in the intervals between maintenance shutdowns.

And it is active. Everything is in process. Steam vents from a ruptured pipe across the chamber, hissing white into the amber dimness, and already the isopods are swarming toward it, drawn by the heat and the moisture, their shells clicking like a thousand castanets. A conveyor belt stutters, jams on a fragment of chitin, and somewhere an alarm begins to sound—a bio-alarm, a sac of acid-pulp ruptured by pressure, emitting a high keen wail that sends the moths scattering. The fungal pillars are growing, pushing the pipes outward by millimeters a year, and somewhere a bracket snaps with a report like a gunshot, and a section of gutter sags, and the condensation stream alters its course, and the lichen in that zone begins to die from too much water, and the maggots in the nearest vat slow their writhing as the temperature drops by half a degree, and the processing overseer—a human, you realize, the first you've seen, pale and gill-necked, adapted to the damp and the dark—shuffles out of an alcove to adjust a valve, naked and unbothered, her skin mottled with the same bioluminescent lichen that coats the walls.

She does not look at you. She has work to do. The system does not pause for visitors. The system is, in fact, barely aware you are not another component.

And the floor shifts beneath your feet as the mycelial network adjusts its nutrient flow, and you feel the pulse again—that subsonic heartbeat—and you understand that you are standing not in a factory, not in a forest, not in a city, but in an organ of something larger, something that extends kilometers in every direction through the dark earth, a living industrial organism the size of a town, processing, growing, breathing, eating, producing, and the chamber you stand in is merely one chamber of its body, one alveolus in its lung, and it does not know your name, and it will not remember you were here, and the isopod on your shoulder has already decided you are not food, and the moth has already passed, and the alarm has already stopped, and the work continues.
