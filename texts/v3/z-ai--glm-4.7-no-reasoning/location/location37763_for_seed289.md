There is no horizon, only an intensity of brilliance that whites out the concept of distance. You are below the surface, but not in the dark. The elevation is negative—a microscopic pocket, a subterranean blister buried deep within the strata of a world that has forgotten you exists. It is a room-sized anomaly, a cavity in the raw, unformed rock.

The geology is embryonic. The walls are not "rock" in the sense of ancient, craggy stone, but rather a dense, translucent slurry that has not yet decided if it wants to be quartz or clay. It is under construction. The erosion score is negligible because the material hasn't existed long enough to be worn down. It is raw geology, wet and malleable, extruding itself in jagged, half-formed spikes that glow from within.

And it is packed. The density is suffocating. There is no "empty space" here. Every cubic millimeter is occupied by a presence, a structure, or a lifeform. It is a saturated solution of existence.

The dampness is pervasive, scoring a 0.7—a constant, weeping moisture. Condensation slicks everything. The water doesn't drain; it has nowhere to go. It sits in still, mirror-smooth pockets, trapped by the sheer density of the room.

Vegetation is sparse but aggressive. There are no forests, no canopies to filter the blinding light. Instead, the flora is solitary and monumental. Huge, singular stalks erupt from the floor, thick as pillars, covered in a bark that resembles polished bone. They stand like statues in the confined space, their leaves few but massive—single, wide fans that catch the light and refract it into blinding rainbows. They are not growing; they are simply *being*, occupying their allotted volume with immutable stubbornness.

Between these bone-trees, the fauna is abundant. This is the strange part. The space is loud—2.1 out of 3 loud. But the sound is not the rustle of wind or the chirp of birds; it is a relentless, low-frequency thrumming, a vibration that rattles your teeth. The floor is a carpet of chitin. Millions of small, hard-shelled creatures—beetles the size of fists, millipedes with iridescent, oil-slick segments—crawl over one another in a constant, friction-filled dance. They are packed tight. They move in a single, writhing layer across the wet stone, a living tarmac of clicks and clacks.

There is no technology. No tools. The "built" score is low, but not zero. Scattered amidst the living carpet are artifacts, though they are not constructions. They are "scatters"—bones of massive things, long since turned to stone, or perhaps strange, geometric nodules of metal that precipitated out of the walls naturally, looking for all the world like ruined doorways or rusted hinges, yet entirely biological in origin. They sit like faint marks on the landscape, half-buried in the living tide of insects.

The weirdness lies in the stillness of the motion. Despite the deafening noise of the crawling carpet and the flowing water, the dynamic is zero. The world never changes. The insects move, but the swarm does not shift. The droplets fall, but the water level never rises. The light pulses, but the shadows never lengthen. It is a frozen equilibrium. If you were to leave this room and return a thousand years from now, the beetles would be crawling over the exact same bumps in the stone; the water droplet would be poised at the exact same angle on the tip of the stalagmite. It is a system in perfect, agonizing lockstep.

You are standing in a bright, wet, microscopic pocket of raw creation, buried beneath the earth, packed tight with screaming life that is going nowhere. It is a room that has finished happening before it even began.
