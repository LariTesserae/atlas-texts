The heat comes first—not the clean, baking heat of a desert, but something sodden and pressurized, a warmth that has absorbed too much moisture and now sweats it back into the air. The atmosphere is a thermal sponge. You breathe in and feel the water enter your lungs before the air, thick and slightly sweet with the mineral tang of dissolved stone. 

The land sits low, barely above the water table, and the water has had its way here for a very long time. Erosion is not a violent process in this place; it is a patient, ancient negotiation. The terrain has been worn down to its essential truths: shallow braids of warm water spreading across flat slabs of porous rock, weeping cliffs of compressed ash and clay that bleed steady rivulets from hidden seams, and pools of standing water so still they look like dark glass until a bubble of warm gas breaks the surface from below. It is a landscape of seeps and weeps, where the rain does not fall so much as condense directly onto the skin of the earth.

And yet, despite the water, the place feels emptied. Vegetation is a rumor here—scattered, leathery knots of pallid weed clinging to the cracks where the weeping rock offers a grip, their roots more gall than anchor. There is no animal life. No insects skim the water; no birds call. The only movement is the slow, gravitational crawl of water over stone, the rise of heat-shimmer, and the occasional settling of a saturated cliff face surrendering a bushel of wet clay to the flats below. It is an ecosystem of erosion and humidity, running in quiet, endless loops.

Into this saturated vacancy, a city has been built. It stands as a stark imposition of geometry upon the formless wet, a sprawling expanse of low, heavy structures and elevated walkways stretching outward into the gloom. But it is a city without tech, without tools, without the faintest spark of industry. The construction is organic—built from the saturated earth itself. Great blocks of indurated mud, walls of packed calcite and woven fungal mortar, streets paved with the flat, heavy bones of some extinct leviathan. The architecture is layered, dense, a labyrinth of blocky ziggurats and enclosed courtyards, all designed to endure the relentless damp. There is no metal to rust, no wood to rot. The city is made of the same stuff as the swamp, just forced into right angles and load-bearing arches.

But the city is unpeopled. The density of construction is betrayed by the sparsity of presence. There is vast space between the buildings, and within them, cavernous rooms sit hollow, their doorways gaping like dark, wet mouths. The settlement extends past the immediate, a regional sprawl of organized construction reaching into the obscurity of the humid haze, but it is saturated with emptiness. It is a metropolis of absences, a ghost town built not by the dead, but by the displaced.

Because here, at the edges, reality is melting. 

The weirdness does not announce itself with sudden horrors; it is a slow, atmospheric decay, a breakdown of the rules that govern distance, light, and form. The darkness is profound, but it is an active darkness—a dimness that seems to swallow light rather than merely lack it. Bioluminescent lichen cling to the base of the mud-brick walls, casting a feeble, bruised blue glow, but the light only reaches a few feet before the humidity devours it. The darkness is wet. It clings to the skin. 

In this gloom, the geometry of the city begins to lie. A street that appears straight slowly curves in on itself, leading back to the same weeping rock formation you passed an hour ago. Doorways, when approached from an angle, reveal rooms far larger than the exterior of the building could possibly contain—vast, echoing cisterns of warm, still water, with steps leading down into the dark depths where the water meets the porous earth. Staircases descend from empty plazas into the shallow bays, continuing down beneath the surface of the water, the steps slick with algae, disappearing into the murk, implying a submerged city beneath the city, still organized, still constructed, but utterly fluid.

The silence is profound, but it is not dead. It is an active quiet, heavy with the pressure of the low elevation and the heat. The only sounds are the ones the city makes as it endures: the slow, rhythmic *drip* of water from the lip of a calcite overhang; the deep, almost subsonic groan of a mud-brick wall shifting as its core absorbs another gallon of atmospheric moisture; the occasional, unsettling *crack* of the earth, like a joint popping in a giant's knee, as the water table swells and recedes with the hidden tides. 

The processes of the world grind on. The water weeps from the rocks, the heat condenses on the walls, and the empty city settles deeper into the mire, its right angles softening, its straight streets melting into the meanders of the swamp. It is a place of transition, where the boundaries between the built and the born, the solid and the liquid, the real and the impossible, have been worn tissue-thin by the long, patient erosion of an impossible rain.
