The plateau goes on forever. Not metaphorically—the highland truly possesses a horizon that never resolves, a continental shelf of elevated atmosphere where the sky presses down like a wet wool blanket, perpetually grey, perpetually condensing. This is a world still becoming. The bedrock beneath is raw, embryonic—soft calcareous stone and un lithified mud, not yet carved by rivers, not yet given shape by time. It is a geological fetus, vast and unformed, and it is drowning in growth.

Everything here is plant. There is no soil, no bare stone, no open air—only a continuous, immanent body of vegetation that fills space the way water fills a bowl. The substrate itself is root-mass layered on root-mass, a living chert of compressed rhizome and lignin that extends downward until it meets the soft, giving rock, which it dissolves and replaces. To stand here is to stand on turgor pressure alone. The ground beneath your feet would sink if the hydraulic architecture weren't so perfectly pressurized—a continent suspended on osmosis.

And the water. It is everywhere and in everything, not as standing pools but as a condition of existence. The atmosphere is at dew point. Always. There is no rain because rain requires air dry enough to accept condensation into distinct drops. Here, the moisture simply *is*—beading on every surface, running in sheets down the vertical masses of stalk, weeping from the undersides of leaves that never dry, seeping from the pores of the ground itself. The vegetation breathes it in and sweats it out in a continuous loop, and the pressure of all that transpired water is what holds the architecture up. You could call it rain if you needed a word, but it's more like existing inside a lung that never exhales completely.

The scale breaks perception. The highland stretches in every direction, and because the light is so dim—this thick, vaporous luminance that filters through ten thousand cumulative layers of photosynthetic surface above—the distance fades into green-grey murk at perhaps a hundred meters. Beyond that, there is only the sound, and the sound is *enormous*. A roar. A continuous, physical-cavity-shaking roar. It comes from the growth itself. Every cell in this planetary organism is elongating, dividing, pumping. The sound of a single stalk expanding is imperceptible. The sound of a continent of stalks expanding, their fibers creaking, their xylem vessels drawing water with hydraulic force, their leaf-unfurling membranes peeling apart wetly, their root-tips grinding through soft calcareous stone—it deafens. The air vibrates at frequencies you feel in your molars. Subsonic groans of tectonic mass being displaced by biomass. The click and pop of millions of vacuoles pressurizing simultaneously. A wet, rhythmic thrumming that is the heartbeat of photosynthesis operating at continental intensity.

There are no animals. No eyes, no mouths, no legs. The ecology is pure—plant consuming light and water and mineral, plant consuming plant, plant parasitizing plant in an endless round of autotroph turning heterotroph turning saprotroph turning substrate for the next autotroph. There are vines that are also trees. There are leaves that are also roots. There are organs with no taxonomic name—gas bladders the size of buildings, anchor-tendrils that drill through living wood, fruiting bodies that exude not seeds but *chemical blueprints*, molecular instructions that reconfigure the surrounding tissue into copies of themselves. It is an ecosystem without consumers, without movement, without death as animals understand it—only assimilation, transmutation, and the endless press of new growth shouldering aside the old.

And yet it is not primitive. Not merely. There is an engineering here that exceeds explanation—something operating at scales both nanoscopic and continental, something that understands physics in ways that haven't been discovered yet. The hydraulic systems are too efficient. The structural load-bearing capacities of the tallest stalk-clusters violate material science—their tensile strengths, their flexibility under wind load, their ability to channel water uphill without pumping. At the molecular level, the cellulose lattices show arrangements that don't appear in any terrestrial biology: self-assembling crystalline structures that reconfigure in response to humidity gradients, lignin polymers with conductive properties, chloroplasts operating in light conditions that should be below their compensation point but somehow aren't. Something designed this. Or something *is* designing this, continuously, in real-time, at the speed of growth.

The process is visible. You can watch it happen. In the dim wet light, if you focus past the immediate roaring surface, you can see the plateau *accreting*—edges extending into the raw geological nothing, roots pioneering into soft stone that hasn't yet hardened, converting mineral to biomass in real-time. The highland is growing. It is colonizing the planet's crust, replacing lithosphere with biosphere, and it has been doing so for longer than any timescale that permits objection. Weather systems form and dissolve within its bulk—convective cells driven by the transpiration of a continent, warm wet air rising from the biomass and condensing immediately back to the surface in a closed loop. It generates its own climate. It *is* its own climate.

The strangeness is quiet. Nothing here violates the laws of thermodynamics—energy enters as light, exits as heat and chemical potential, moves through the system in accordance with everything understood about gradients and entropy. And yet the *efficiency* is impossible. The waste is nearly zero. The information density in the molecular signaling—chemicals exuded from one tissue that reprogram another, that trigger developmental cascades across kilometers of living substrate—suggests a computational capacity, a parallel processing network operating through the medium of growth itself. The vegetation isn't just alive. It's *calculating*. Each cell a processor. Each hormone gradient a data stream. The plateau is solving something—optimizing something—at a speed and scale that dwarfs cognition.

There is no horizon. There is no sky. There is only the green-dark, the wet, the press of living material in every direction, and the deafening sound of a world becoming itself, cell by cell, hydraulic pulse by hydraulic pulse, pushing outward into the formless dark, filling every volume, converting every substrate, an engine of photosynthesis and turgor and molecular computation that will not stop until the planet is entirely, irreversibly alive.
