The air here is thick enough to swim in. It is a hot, humid suspension that sits in the lungs like a heavy meal. Up here, at the jagged crowns of the world, there is no silence and no stillness—only a relentless, churning urgency.

You are standing on a cathedral made of bone-white fungus and woody vine, but you do not stand on a mountain. You stand on a construction. The ground beneath your feet is not stone; it is "Old Growth," a densely packed, lignified pavement laid down by generations of planetary architects. This is a high-altitude city of flora, a sprawling, open network of biological terraces rising above the cloud layer, built by a planet that does not know the meaning of "stop."

The scale is overwhelming. Great archipelagos of constructed plant-mass stretch out into the distance, separated by yawning, mist-filled chasms. These platforms are vast—regional in size—flat-topped mesas that weren't eroded by wind but were grown upward in a frantic geological sprint. Because the erosion here is nil, the edges are razor-sharp. The boundaries of the world are geometric, sheer cliffs of living matter dropping away into the wet, white void below. It looks young. The scars of its vertical growth are still visible, raw green fissures weeping sap that hasn't yet hardened into bark.

Despite the overwhelming mass of vegetation, the density is paradoxically sparse. It is not a jungle where you must hack through a wall of leaves. It is an open city of towers and buttresses. Giant, solitary stalks—some as thick as skyscrapers, others slender as needles—rise from the paved bio-floor like the pillars of a deserted temple. Between them lies open space, wet and airy, allowing the harsh, normal light of the sun to strike the floor unimpeded.

And it is loud. The sound is a constant roar, a white noise of biological combustion. The heat (2.6) drives the system like an engine; the world is burning through its own energy. Every surface is sweating. The weeping rocks you pass are not geological but glandular—great clusters of succulent tissue mounted on high plinths, weeping gallons of purified water into runnels carved along the streets. The water flows everywhere, a frantic plumbing system ofrivulets and gutters, constantly replenished by the condensing atmosphere.

There is no animal life to chitter or call. The roar comes from the plants themselves. It is the sound of rapid expansion—cellular division on a macroscopic scale, the groaning of fiber as thick trunks twist tighter, the rhythmic *thrum* of spore cannons firing into the wind, and the violent *pop* of seed-pods bursting under the solar pressure. The atmosphere is alive with drifting pollen and spores, turning the air into a shimmering, golden haze.

The tech level is zero, yet the complexity of the structures suggests a sinister intelligence. There are winding stairs carved into the sides of pitcher-plant coliseums, and bridges formed by the intertwined tendrils of crawling lianas that look suspiciously load-bearing. It is a pure nature ecosystem, but one that has evolved the habits of infrastructure. The "houses" here are hollowed-out bulbs of enormous flowers, lined with waxy, insulating floors.

But the defining feature is the turbulence. The landscape is not static; it is unstable. A tower of moss and cellulose you saw a moment ago might suddenly shudder, outgrow its anchor, and collapse into the abyss, while a new spire erupts from the ground in a fountain of wet earth and green slime. The ground shifts. The "roads" open and close. It is a Solaris-like madness of creation, an anthill-energy applied to geology. The rules of this world are simple: grow, occupy, replace.

You walk carefully. The surface is slick with the condensation of the hot air meeting the cooler interiors of the massive plants. The smell is intoxicating—a mix of rotting vegetation, ozone, and the sweet, cloying scent of rapid blooming. There is nowhere to hide from the sun and nowhere to hide from the growth. You are an observer in a factory that is manufacturing a jungle at breakneck speed, high above the old world, waiting for the floor beneath you to decide it wants to be a tree instead.
