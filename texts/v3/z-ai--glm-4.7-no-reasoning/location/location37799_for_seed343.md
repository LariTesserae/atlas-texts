Here, the sky is not so much above as it is simply *absent*. You stand on the rim of the world, or perhaps the world’s roof, an elevation so extreme that the air has lost its ambition to be thick. It is thin, humming, and brutally clear.

This is the High Loam, a plateau lifted eons ago into the upper troposphere, settled now into its final, ancient form. The erosion parameter here isn’t about wearing down, but about wearing *smooth*. The jagged bones of the earth were ground away a million years ago. What remains is a vast, rolling tableland of slate-grey stone and accumulated soil, stretching to the curvature of the horizon—an open, regional emptiness that feels like the inside of a lung.

It is deceptively empty. The density is near-void; you could stand in the center of the Loam and spin in a circle with arms outstretched without touching a single solid object. The space between things is absolute. It is a hollow place, a cathedral-sized vacuum where the wind sweeps through unimpeded.

But the emptiness is a lie of perspective. The scale of the life here demands distance to be understood.

The vegetation is total, a "all-living-mass" that doesn't grow *out* of the ground so much as it *is* the ground. There is no distinct line where the soil ends and the plant begins. The substrate is a sprawling, infinite knot of spiraled vines and mats of moss-like matter that have fused over geologic time into a singular, planet-spanning hide. It is tough, fibrous, and warm to the touch.

And because the density is so low—the vegetation spread out in kilometers-wide, creeping solar collectors—this living mat supports an ecology of overwhelming size.

The fauna is deafening, a roaring cacophony that strikes you like a physical blow the moment you arrive. In the distance, floating on the thermals that rise from the warm, sun-baked earth, are Great Levitan-Herons. Their wingspans are measured in furlongs; they are vast, tattered leviathans of feather and gas-bladders that drift through the thin upper atmosphere like storm clouds. When they call—a low, resonant *boom* that vibrates in your chest—it echoes across the plateau for minutes.

Below them, haunting the vast open spaces, are the Stilt-Walkers. To see one is to mistake it for a moving hill. They stand on spindly, jointed legs that rise fifty meters into the air, their bodies small in comparison, allowing them to stride effortlessly over the creeks and fissures that cut through the living-mat. They are silent, graceful giants that move with a slow, geological patience, browsing on the tops of the towering, fungal "trees" that dot the landscape.

The water level is low but persistent. The climate is damp in the way a breath is damp—intermittent. Clouds snag against the peaks of the mega-flora, condensing into heavy droplets that slide down the massive stalks and collect in the "pockets" of the terrain. These pockets are vast, bowl-shaped depressions in the living mat, filled with still, black water that reflects the normal, high-contrast light of the sun. The water is brackish and ancient, stewing in the warmth. It hums with the noise of billions of tiny insects—the only small things here—microscopic larvae that squirm in the slick, creating a constant, low-frequency drone that underpins the bird-calls.

And amidst this biological roar and sprawling life, the traces of the past are faint.

Scattered across the open plain, kilometers apart, are the Built Remnants. They are primitive, barely recognizable as structures. A circle of stacked stones, too regular to be natural, eroded by wind into shapes like vertebrae. A single, rusted iron spike driven into the living-mat, protruding only a foot, surrounded by a halo of white where the plant-life refuses to grow. These are the marks of early tool-users, perhaps ancestors who tried to tame the verticality and failed. They used simple-fabrication, hand-hewn rock and beaten metal, tools that look like toys next to the Stilt-Walkers.

The atmosphere is warm, a gentle, enveloping heat that rises from the decomposing vegetation beneath your feet. The air smells of ozone, wet dust, and the sweet, heavy scent of nectar carried on the wind from miles away.

The dynamic of the place is a slow, biological grind. The plant-as-architecture grows and folds over itself, burying the stone ruins inch by inch. The Stilt-Walkers migrate in a slow, seasonal drift across the valley. The Levitan-Herons mate in the sky, their mating dances creating turbulence that kicks up dust-devils on the plain. It is an ecosystem in motion, but at a scale that makes the frantic hustle of lowlands seem irrelevant.

Here, you are an intruder of insignificant size. The ground you walk on is alive and warm. The silence you crave is impossible; the world screams with the sounds of giants eating, breeding, and drifting in the warm, thin light. You stand between the stalks of an infinite forest, on the back of a sleeping god, squinting at the horizon where the ancient, smoothed mountains fade into the haze. It is a place that has already been here for a billion years, and will be here for a billion more, indifferent to the footprints you leave in the moss.
