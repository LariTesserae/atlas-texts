# The High Thunder Plain

The world ends here—or rather, the world *thins* here, stretched over altitude until it becomes something gauzy and near-transparent, a place where the atmosphere seems more suggestion than substance.

---

## Terrain

You stand on a vast high plateau, so elevated that the horizon curves visibly, bending away in all directions like the rim of a shallow bowl. The surface beneath your feet is ancient—a pavement of metamorphic rock exposed over eons, schist and gneiss layered in shades of gunmetal and tarnished silver. The rock is nearly flat, tilted only slightly, scoured by winds that have blown since before the mountains that once stood here wore down to this single stubborn plain.

This is all that remains of a mountain range older than most living things. The peaks are gone, the valleys are gone, the rivers that carved them are gone. Only the root remains—this tabletop of stone floating above the world, two or three miles above where lowland creatures believe the earth properly exists.

The plateau extends for miles in every direction, a broad and open stage. There are no cliffs at its edges—not sheer ones, anyway. Instead, the land falls away in immense ragged talus slopes and degraded escarpments, gradual enough that you could walk to the rim without realizing how close you are, and then suddenly the world opens beneath you and drops for thousands of feet through fog layers and cloud decks into whatever green or brown or blue country lives far below.

Here on the plateau itself, there is almost nothing. That is the defining quality. The space between objects is enormous. You can walk for an hour and pass a single standing stone, a single shallow depression, a single crack in the pavement. The emptiness has weight. It presses against you—not loneliness, but a kind of spatial pressure, the awareness of how much nothing surrounds you and how far you are from anything that could catch you if you fell.

---

## Water

Water exists here, but barely, and never where you'd look for it.

Condensation is the primary ocean. Every morning, if the temperature drops enough, a skin of moisture appears on the rock—thinner than a sheet of paper, a mere wetness that you can feel under your palm but could never collect. It burns off within an hour of true sunrise. The stone darkens briefly, then lightens again, and the water is gone as if it were never there.

In the shallow depressions—some natural, some carved by ice over ages long past—pocket water lingers longer. These pans are rarely larger than a tabletop, sometimes as small as a cupped hand. After the rare rain, they fill. Rain here comes perhaps a dozen times a year, moving in fast grey curtains that the wind drives sideways across the plateau with startling violence, and then it is gone, and the sky is bright again, and the water in the pans begins its slow retreat into the air.

Some of the deeper depressions hold water for weeks. These are the wells of the plain, the only reliable surface moisture, and everything that lives here arranges itself around them in a geometry of thirst.

There is no flowing water. No streams, no rivers, no springs. The plateau is too high and too hard for water to dig into it anymore. What rain falls either evaporates or sits in its little stone cup until it evaporates too. The water cycle here is a short, local, nearly closed loop: sky to stone to sky, with a brief pause in between.

---

## Light and Sound

This is a place of extreme exposure, and the two things that define it most are light and sound—both present to an almost painful degree.

The light is brutal. At this altitude, the atmosphere is thin, and it filters almost nothing. Sunlight arrives raw and full-spectrum, bleaching the rock to near-white at midday and casting shadows so sharp they look cut from paper. There is no soft diffusion, no gentle ambient glow. You are either in direct sun—in which case every surface blazes and you feel the radiation on your skin like a physical weight—or you are in shadow, where the temperature drops instantly and the grey stone seems to absorb what little light reaches it.

When clouds pass between you and the sun, the change is instantaneous and total. The entire plateau goes from incandescent to somber in the time it takes a cloud to cross the solar disk. And then just as quickly, the light returns, and the stone blazes again, and you squint against it.

But sound—sound is the true inhabitant of this place.

The wind never stops. Never. It is the permanent condition of the plateau, the baseline reality against which all other phenomena are exceptions. At this elevation, with no trees to break it, no hills to divert it, no structures to slow it, the wind moves across the rock face with an unobstructed momentum that is difficult to comprehend until you stand in it. It is not a gentle breeze or a stiff gust. It is a continuous roar, a wall of moving air that presses against you constantly and produces a sound so pervasive that it ceases to be heard in the ordinary way and becomes instead a condition of existence, like gravity, like temperature.

You feel it in your chest before you process it as sound. A low, resonant vibration that rattles your sternum, overlaid with a higher, screaming component that tears at the edges of hearing. The wind finds every crack and gap in the rock and forces air through them at velocities that produce whistling, howling, shrieking—a whole choir of aerodynamic sounds, each one born from a specific fissure in a specific stone, each one constant as long as the wind blows and the crack remains.

Standing stones—there are a few, isolated and mysterious—sing. The wind wraps around them and produces tones that rise and fall with the wind's intensity, eerie sustained notes that seem almost designed, almost intentional, as if the stones were placed here to make this exact sound in this exact wind. They were not placed. They are the last remnants of veins of harder rock that resisted the erosion that removed everything else. But the sound they make does not feel accidental.

In the brief lulls—which come rarely and never last more than a few seconds—the silence that replaces the wind is shocking, a negative pressure, an absence so total that your ears reach for sound and find nothing. And then the wind returns, and the roar resumes, and the plateau goes back to being what it always is: deafening, exposed, scoured.

---

## The Living

And yet things live here. Many things. That is the surprise of the plateau, the fact that refuses to square with the emptiness and the harshness: this place teems with life, if you know how to look.

The dominant large creatures are the *thrask*—a name I give them because they have no other, or none that any living person knows. They are something like goats and something else entirely. Large as cattle, broad-bodied, with low centers of gravity and legs that end in pads of dense callus rather than hooves—adaptation for the stone pavement, which would destroy a hoof over time. Their coats are the exact color of the wet rock: dark grey going to black at the limbs, lighter on the barrel, with a rough shaggy texture that the wind flattens to one side constantly, so that every thrask looks perpetually leaning, even when standing still.

They travel in small bands—five, eight, a dozen—moving between the water pans in patterns so regular they might be ritual. They walk into the wind by preference, their broad bodies cutting through it, their eyes half-closed against the blown grit. They can go days without drinking, getting moisture from the lichen they scrape from the rock and from the condensation they lick from the stone in the early mornings. When they reach a water pan, they drink deeply and quickly, their throats working in visible pulses, and then they move on, because standing still at a water source is how you become visible to things that hunt.

And things do hunt. The *krest* is the apex presence here—a creature I cannot describe adequately because adequate description would require you to have seen something like it, and you have not. Imagine a bird. Now remove everything that makes a bird light or fragile. The krest is a flying predator the size of a working horse, with a body dense as bone and wings that span twice its length, membrane stretched between elongated finger-bones, leathern and dark. It does not soar—there is too little air here for soaring. It *falls with purpose*, dropping from heights above the plateau in controlled descent, its wings making small adjustments, its eyes—pale yellow, set forward—tracking movement on the rock below with an accuracy that seems mechanical.

When it strikes, it folds its wings entirely and hits the stone at a speed that should kill it, and does not. Its legs absorb the impact through joints that flex in ways joints should not flex, and its prey—if it has chosen well—is already dead, taken in the moment of contact by claws that punch through hide and into the organs beneath. If it misses, it runs across the stone on folded wings, accelerating with a loping stride that looks awkward and is not, until it reaches the plateau's edge and throws itself off, dropping into the thicker air below to climb again and begin the hunt anew.

The krest is silent. In all this roaring wind, in all this howling stone, the krest makes no sound. It arrives without warning and leaves without farewell, and the only sign of its passage is a thrask that does not rise from the ground, or a band of thrask that has moved suddenly closer together and stands facing outward, watching the sky.

Smaller creatures fill the gaps. Lizards—flat, dark, heat-seeking—scurry between rocks when the sun is strong and disappear into cracks when it is not. They move with a speed that seems impossible in the cold, but their bodies are built to harvest heat from the stone, and when the rock is warm, they are warm, and they are fast. Insects—small, hard-shelled, wind-resistant—crawl on every surface. They are the base of everything, feeding on the lichen and the organic debris that the wind carries and deposits in every micro-depression, and everything else feeds on them or on things that fed on them.

The thrask eat the lichen too, and small plants that grow in the cracks, and sometimes each other's dead. The krest eat the thrask. Smaller raptors—true birds, sharp-winged, riding the updrafts at the plateau's edge—take the lizards and the insects. And when anything dies on the open rock, the wind and the insects and the small raptors reduce it to bone and then to nothing in a span of days, and the plateau is clean again, as if nothing had lived there at all.

---

## Ecology and Rhythm

The plateau's ecology is a sparse web with many connections—a system where almost every species interacts with almost every other, because there are too few species for the relationships to be indirect. Remove the lichen and everything collapses. Remove the insects and the lizards die and the small raptors die and the krest must hunt only thrask and the thrask are overhunted and then the krest die too. Remove the wind and the condensation stops and the water pans evaporate faster and the thrask cannot drink and they leave or die and the krest follow.

It is a stable system—has been stable for a very long time—but it is a narrow stability, a balance maintained on a knife-edge where every element is load-bearing. There are no redundancies, no backup populations, no alternative food sources. Everything does one thing and does it completely, and the system works because each thing's one thing connects precisely to the next thing's one thing, and the chain closes into a loop that has turned, without noticeable change, for centuries or millennia.

The plateau does not have seasons in the way lower places have seasons. The temperature variation is too small, the day-to-day change too gradual. It is always cool here—cold at night, cool during the day, never warm, never hot. The sun provides light and radiation but not meaningful heat; the thin air cannot hold warmth. The rock absorbs what it can and radiates the rest away as soon as the sun sets, and the nights are bitter, and the creatures endure them by finding shelter in the cracks and the shallow depressions and the lee of standing stones, pressing their bodies against stone that is warmer than the air, waiting for the sun to return.

Rain comes when it comes—driven by systems far below, pushed up the plateau's slopes by forces that have nothing to do with the plateau itself. The rain is a visitor, not a resident. It arrives, it falls, it leaves, and the plateau returns to its default state of dryness and wind and light.

## The Standing Stones and the Weird

There are perhaps twenty standing stones on the plateau, distributed across its extent in a pattern that is not random but is also not any pattern that makes obvious sense. They are not aligned with compass points or astronomical events. They are not equidistant from each other. They are not arranged in circles or lines or spirals or any geometric figure that a human mind would produce.

They are tall—twice the height of a person, some of them, though others are barely above waist-height. They are narrow, some barely a foot thick, and they lean at angles that suggest they should have fallen long ago but have not. The wind has carved them into shapes that are almost but not quite aerodynamic: teardrops, blades, twisted columns that thicken and thin as they rise. They are all the same material—a dense dark stone, darker than the pavement, shot through with veins of something that catches the light differently, something that might be mineral and might be something else.

The weirdness of the plateau lives in these stones.

The sounds they make in the wind, first of all, are *wrong* for their shapes. A stone of that profile should produce a certain range of tones when wind passes over it at a given speed. These stones produce tones that do not correspond to their profiles. They produce overtones that should not exist, harmonics that fall in the gaps of the natural series, notes that seem to come from inside the stone rather than from the air around it. Standing among them in a strong wind, you hear a chord that has no business existing—a sound that is almost musical but is not quite, that resolves almost but not entirely, that reaches toward something and fails to reach it, leaving you with a sense of almost-pattern, almost-meaning, almost-purpose.

And the thrask treat the stones with a deference that is hard to interpret. They will alter their route to pass near a standing stone, and when they are near it, they slow down, and sometimes they stop, and they press their flanks against the stone and stay that way for a time, eyes closed, breathing slowly. They do this singly or in groups, and they do not do it at every stone, and they do not do it at the same stone every time, and no pattern to their choices is discernible. It might be shelter—they are pressing against the lee side, out of the wind. But they sometimes press against the windward side, which provides no shelter, and they stay there just as long.

The stones are warm to the touch, warmer than the surrounding rock, warmer than the air, warmer than they should be given that they stand in constant wind and intermittent sun. The warmth is slight—a few degrees, no more—but it is consistent, and it does not diminish at night, and it does not vary with the season. The stones are warm because they are warm. That is all that can be said.

Nothing grows on them. The lichen that covers every other surface on the plateau—flint-grey, crustose, nearly indistinguishable from the rock itself until you look closely—does not colonize the standing stones. The insects do not walk on them. The lizards do not sun themselves on their bases. The small raptors do not perch on their tops. The stones stand in the ecosystem the way a hole stands in a fabric: by their absence, by their refusal to participate, by the clean border that separates them from everything that lives.

---

## The Feeling of the Place

To stand on the high thunder plain is to stand at a kind of boundary—between the earth and the sky, between silence and sound, between the living and the merely present. The wind pushes you. The light exposes you. The emptiness surrounds you. And beneath your feet, the rock goes down and down and down, solid all the way, ancient all the way, indifferent all the way.

Nothing here cares about you. The thrask will not alter their path for you. The krest will note you as a shape on the rock and will categorize you as *not thrask* and will ignore you. The insects will crawl over your boots if you stand still long enough, but they do this because your boots are warm and have texture, not because they know you are there.

The plateau has been here, more or less as it is, for longer than anything alive can measure. The mountain range it was once part of rose and fell. The climate that formed it changed and changed again. Species emerged and vanished and emerged again. And the plateau remained—wearing down, yes, losing meters of thickness per millennium, but remaining, persisting, outlasting everything that tried to change it.

It will outlast you too. It will outlast your species, probably. It will stand here, loud and bright and nearly empty and thoroughly alive, making its sounds in the wind, and the wind will not stop, and the light will not soften, and the thrask will walk to the water pans and the krest will fall from the sky and the standing stones will hum their impossible hum, and this will go on until the plateau itself finally wears away and the last of this ancient root returns to the lowlands from which it came, and something else takes this place, or nothing does, and the wind blows over nothing and makes no sound, and even the silence is gone.

But that is far ahead. For now, the plateau is here, and you are on it, and the wind is pushing you, and the light is on you, and somewhere a thrask is calling—a low, resonant grunt that carries through the roaring air with impossible clarity—and somewhere else, a krest is climbing the sky, and the standing stones are humming, and the world is thin and bright and full of sound, and you are very, very high up, and very, very small.
