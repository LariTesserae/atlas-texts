The world is not a globe anymore, nor is it a seabed; it is a suspension. The elevation is absolute zero—not on a map, but in relation to the light. We are deep, deep down, beneath the crush of a darkness so total it feels like a physical weight, a heavy velvet shroud pressed against the eyes. This is the Below.

Here, the water does not behave like liquid; it behaves like time. It is everywhere (2.7), filling every void, saturating every cubic inch of space with a cold, sluggish clarity. The temperature is low enough to slow the blood (0.5), a biting chill that seeps through scales and skin alike, but it is not frozen. The water remains liquid, ancient and unmoving, a preservative as much as an environment. It is dark (0.4), the kind of darkness found inside the heart of a glacial crevasse, lit only by the occasional, agonizingly slow pulse of bioluminescence that feels more like memory than vision.

Despite the silence—the sheer, crushing quiet (0.4)—the place is screaming with movement. This is the paradox of the Turbulent Deep (2.8). The currents here are not water flowing from point A to point B; they are geological churnings. The ocean floor is not a bed of mud, but a grinding, shifting expanse of Erosion (2.8). This is the world in its final, settled form, flattened by eons of pressure until the mountains have been worn down to stumps and the valleys filled with silt. There are no sharp edges here. Every stone has been tumbled for a million years until it is perfectly, frictionlessly smooth. The terrain is a vast, undulating plain of gravel and rounded boulders, packed so tightly (2.9) that there is no distinction between "ground" and "object." It is a solid mosaic of sediment, interlocked pieces of a shattered world pressed together by the weight of the ocean.

There is no room to swim between the stones. The density is absolute. It is a bazaar of geology, where every square inch is occupied by the presence of the past.

And yet, it shifts. Beneath this packed, ancient floor, the heat of the dying planet’s core creates a hyperactive instability. Huge sections of the seabed—hectares of packed stone—suddenly liquefy or slide. It is a Solaris-like chaos played out in slow motion. A hill of smooth granite might suddenly decide to be a valley, collapsing inward with a thunderous, booming vibration that travels through the bones long before it reaches the ears. The ground is constantly recreating itself, eroding and reforming in a chaotic loop, yet never changing its essential nature: flat, worn, and old.

Vegetation is a cruel joke here (0.7). There is no forest, no kelp forest waving in the current. Life has been stripped down to its absolute bare minimum. A single, thread-thin strand of pale, ghostly white algae might cling to the crest of a smooth black boulder, stretching inches upward to catch the nonexistent sun. It is sparse, skeletal, barely alive. It does not photosynthesize; it chemosynthesizes the faint heat leaking from the grinding tectonic plates.

The fauna, too, is rare (1.3). There are no schools of fish, no great leviathans. The ecosystem cannot support abundance. The creatures here are solitary, blind nomads navigating the tight, packed maze of the seabed. They are primitive things (0.8)—armored, low-slung, lacking finesse. Imagine a smooth-sided crab the size of a dinner table, its eyes gone, its claws evolved into bulldozing scoops for scraping the nutrient-rich slime off the shifting rocks. It moves with a painstaking slowness, conserving energy in the cold. It is a biological machine of simple tech: pressure, leverage, and patience.

There is no built world (0.2). No ruins of Atlantis, no sunken ships. The water is too ancient, the erosion too complete. Anything made by hands has long since been ground into the silt that packs the floor. This is a place devoid of intelligent artifact. It is a wilderness pure and simple, untouched by tools, though not untouched by violence—the violence of time and pressure.

The scale is open and regional (2.0). You could swim for days—weeks, years—and never see a change in the topography. It is a rolling desert of submerged stone, extending endlessly in all directions. The sensation is one of profound agoraphobia combined with claustrophobia; the space is infinite, yet the packing of the stone is so dense that there is nowhere to hide, nowhere to rest that isn't shared by the crushing weight of the adjacent world.

To be here is to witness the end of geology. The mountains are gone. The sharp angles are gone. The water has won, flattening the world into a dense, dark, turbulent prairie of stone. The only sound is the low-frequency groan of the planet settling, a sound so low it vibrates in the teeth, and the occasional, soft *clack* as a billion smooth pebbles shift against one another in the chaotic dance of the deep.
