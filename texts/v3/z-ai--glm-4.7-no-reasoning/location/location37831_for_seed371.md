A throat of basalt, newly torn.

The place is a ravine—hardly more than a wide crack in the spine of young mountains, the kind that has not yet learned to soften its edges. The walls rise sheer and close, cathedral-narrow, cutting off the sky to a bruised slit far overhead. This is a geology of abruptness: the rock is columnar, fractured into hexagonal pillars that jut and overhang like the ribs of some vast, fossilized beast. There is no talus, no accumulated scree at the base. The stone is too new for that. It splits, it reveals, and it stands—fresh-faced and sharp as broken glass, its surfaces still carrying the slick, vitreous gleam of recently-cooled magma or the razor-strations of glacial carve that paused only moments ago.

And into this raw throat, machines have been lowered.

Not built here. *Lowered.* Winched down from the rim by cables that still hum with tension, set onto ledges carved by precision charges, bolted into living rock. The technology is industrial—late-era, heavy-gauge, the kind that runs on coal-gas and spark and the kind of brute rotational force that makes the whole ravine shudder. There are compressor stations clamped to the walls like bloated ticks, their feed lines running in bundled tangles down toward the floor. There are extractor heads—great augers and bore-tips—grinding constantly into the stone at the ravine's far end, seeking something. The sound is enormous: a compounded roar of diesel knock, hydraulic whine, and the deep, bone-transmitted grinding of steel teeth eating fresh basalt. It never ceases. It fills the ravine so completely that silence has become a thing you can only imagine, like a color you once saw in a dream.

The air is wet, but not generously so. This is not a place of streams or pools. The moisture comes from the machines themselves—from their exhaust, their cooling jackets, their venting steam—and from the strange condensation that forms on the cold rock faces when the warmed air of the ravine meets the stone's deeper chill. Everything drips. The walls weep a constant thin sheen of water, and from the cable stays and the bolt-heads and the exposed flanges, droplets fall in erratic rhythms, adding a percussion of tiny plinks to the greater mechanical thunder. There are no puddles on the ravine floor—only a dark, consistent dampness that keeps the stone black and makes the spilled grease and cutting fluid iridescent.

Against all expectation, the place is overgrown.

Not with the patient, deep-rooted flora of old mountains, but with something faster, something opportunistic and aggressive. The vegetation has found every crack, every seam, every place where the new rock offered a purchase, and it has exploded into that space with a ferocity that feels almost hostile. Thick vines—dark-green, nearly black in the ravine's perpetual dusk—coil around the compressor housings and drape from the cable runs like strange bunting. Mosses and lichens have furred every surface that doesn't vibrate too violently, and even some that do; there are pads of vivid chartreuse growth creeping up the extractor legs, spreading in real-time, colonizing the warm metal as if the heat itself were a nutrient. Ferns unfurl from the wall-clefts in dense profusion, their fronds thick and le"athery and always slightly trembling from the machine-vibration, so that the whole ravine seems to breathe with a vegetative shudder. The roots go deep, exploiting the fresh fractures, widening them, pulling the rock apart faster than the bore-heads can. There is a war here between the plant and the drill, and it is unclear who is winning.

The light is almost absent. What little there is comes from the machines—the hot orange glow of running exhaust manifolds, the blue-white stutter of arc-lamps mounted on the extraction rig, the dim red of warning indicators on the compressor stations. These sources do not illuminate so much as they *etch*—catching the wet gleam of rock, the gloss of a vine-leaf, the spray of water from a vent, and rendering each in hard, isolated detail while the rest of the ravine sinks into a black so thick it feels like velvet pressed to the eyes. Shadows are unstable. The vibrating lights throw moving darks across the vegetation, so that the ferns seem to crawl and the vines seem to tighten around their metal hosts.

There is no animal life here. No insects in the moss, no birds in the slit of sky, no rodents in the crannies. The sound and the vibration and the wet darkness have driven everything that breathes and moves of its own volition far from this place. And yet the ravine does not feel empty. It feels *crowded*—overfull, saturated to the point of distress. The machines occupy one register of presence; the vegetation another; the rock a third; the water a fourth. They are all in constant, turbulent interaction, and the energy of their collision fills the space like a gas under pressure. Things are happening here at every scale simultaneously: water beading on a leaf-tip, a vine tendril tightening around a pipe, a bore-head grinding another centimeter into stone, a root system swelling in a crack and levering a flake of basalt free, a compressor cycling and venting and cycling again. There is no stillness anywhere. Even the darkness seems to move.

The place has the feel of a wound that cannot close—too raw, too active, too saturated with competing processes to scab over or heal. The machines grind on. The plants grow on. The rock breaks and breaks and breaks, fresh faces revealed to the wet air and immediately claimed by the first spores that settle there. And the sound—the sound goes on and on, a single sustained chord of industry and growth and geology, reverberating off the close walls, shaking the water from the stone, shaking the darkness itself, as if the ravine were not a place at all but a process, a happening, a brief and violent negotiation between the deep earth and the things that have come to chew upon it.
