The water is relentless here, but it is not violent. It moves with the slow, deliberate certainty of a mechanism that has been running for centuries. This is the Low Refinery, a hamlet-sized depression in the world where the elevation dips just low enough to catch the heavy, warm breath of the atmosphere. The air is thick—so dense with moisture that inhaling feels like drinking—and it tastes of wet iron and ozone.

The scale is intimate, almost claustrophobic, bounded by the curling walls of the crater. You can stand in the center and see the edges where the land rises up, but those boundaries are blurred, erased by the sheer volume of green.

Because the vegetation is total. There is no bare earth. Every square inch of the ground, every pipe, every gear tooth, and every crumbling brick has been claimed by the plant-mass. This is not a garden growing *on* a city; it is a city that has become a biological substrate. The structures are architectural, built of rusted steel and riveted iron, but they serve as the skeleton for a massive, consuming flora.

The dominant species—let's call it the Boiler-Vine—has a vascular system that mimics plumbing. Thick, translucent tubes curl around the smokestacks, pulsing with a sap the color of dark motor oil. The leaves are broad, rubbery affairs, sized to catch the low, normal light filtering through the perpetual haze. They overlap in tight, tessellated patterns, creating a living roof that shudders when the wind blows.

The water saturates everything. It drips constantly from the underside of the mechanical gantries, creating a rhythmic *plink-plink-plink* that competes with the low thrum of the place. It weeps from the rusted joints of the pipes, trickling down to join the shallow, sluggish canals that run through the "streets." The ground underfoot is spongy, a dense mat of roots that have woven themselves into the grates and walkways, creating a surface that feels dangerously alive, shifting slightly as you walk.

The noise is loud but steady. It is the sound of industry slowed down to the pace of biology. Huge flywheels, half-swallowed by moss, turn with a groaning, metallic sigh, driving pumps that push sludge through the heart of the grove. There is a constant hiss of steam escaping from valves that have been sealed shut by creeping lichen. The condensation on the pipes falls in heavy sheets, creating a roar of white noise that dampens all other sounds.

It is warm here, a humid, stifling heat that smells of compost and copper. The temperature drives the engines, but the engines also seem to drive the ecosystem. The exhaust from the smokestacks—rich in carbon and heavy particulates—feeds the canopy. In return, the plants secrete oils that lubricate the gears. It is an industrial symbiosis, a settled form of erosion that has reached a perfect equilibrium.

There is no visible human life, nor is there much fauna. The city is empty of citizens, yet it is full of presence. Occasionally, a small, hard-shelled beetle skitters across a wet control panel, or a frog the size of a fist croaks from inside a hollow piston, but these are rare. The dominant life is the green-iron hybrid itself.

The structures are two or three stories high at most, giving the space the feel of a cathedral nave. Old habitation units, their windows blown out and glass replaced by opaque membranes of fungus, line the main canal. Bridges connect the upper levels, but they are slick with slime and dangerous. The layout is organized, unmistakably urban, with distinct "districts" for processing, storage, and power generation, but the divisions are eroding. The power plant is slowly becoming a thicket; the warehouses are now terraced gardens of carnivorous pitchers.

The light is normal, unremarkable daylight, but it has to fight to get in. The canopy filters the sun into a dim, green twilight. What beams do penetrate illuminate the water vapor rising off the machinery, creating shifting, ghostly pillars of mist in the aisles between the factories.

Everything happens slowly here. The movement of the sun across the sky is the fastest thing to witness. The big gears might rotate once every ten minutes. The vines creep visibly, perhaps an inch an hour. The water level in the canals rises and falls with a glacial rhythm, dictated by some deep, subterranean tide. It is a place of patience. You feel that if you were to stand still for an hour, the vegetation would begin to incorporate you, first wrapping your ankles in cool tendrils, then sealing you into the rust and root to become just another component in the eternal, humid machine.
