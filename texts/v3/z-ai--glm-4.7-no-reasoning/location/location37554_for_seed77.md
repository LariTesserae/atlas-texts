A plain that stretches to the curvature of a horizon that should not exist at such low elevation, yet it does. The ground is not ground. There is no soil, no stone, no sediment—no natural substrate at all. Everything underfoot is made. The world is a total construction, a fabricated continental shell lying flat and low under a vast, temperate sky.

The heat is the first thing that registers. It presses down from above and radiates up from below, a thick, oppressive warmth that never breaks. The air shimmers. And yet, the landscape is not barren in the way a desert is barren. It is sparse in a different fashion—a low-saturation place where made objects are scattered across immense distances, balanced with emptiness, separated by long stretches of bare constructed surface.

The surface itself is the mystery. It is smooth, almost seamless, a pale grey-white material that could be ceramic, could be polymer, could be something without a name. It was never quarried. It was never poured. It simply *is*, present as the fundamental bedrock of reality here—but it is not rock. It is made. Walk far enough and you find the seams. Not cracks—erosion has barely touched this place, it is too young, too newly formed—but joints. The surface is tessellated, though the pattern is hard to discern from any single vantage. The tiles are enormous, some dozens of meters across, some hundreds, fitted together with tolerances too fine for any tool. There is no grout. No gap. Just a hairline trace where one panel of made-material meets another, visible only when the light catches it at a shallow angle.

And the light is normal. That is perhaps the strangest thing. The light is ordinary daylight, diffuse and slightly hazy, as if filtered through a thin high cloud cover that never resolves into clouds. It casts shadows. It makes the heat visible. There is no source—no sun, no lamp, no glow—just illumination, present everywhere at once, as though the sky itself is the emitter. The sky is a flat, hot white, fading to a pale amber at the horizons, and it goes on indefinitely.

Between the tesserae of the floor, in the seams, there is moisture. Not standing water, not pools, but dampness—a persistent condensation that beads along the joints, darkening the material at its edges. The humidity is not enough to cool the air. It only makes the heat wetter, closer, more felt. Occasionally, from somewhere in the architecture of the plain, water emerges. A thin trickle runs along a seam for a kilometer or more, a thread of clear liquid barely a centimeter deep, moving with glacial patience toward some unseen drain or reservoir. You can kneel and touch it. It is cool for a moment, then body-temperature, then indistinguishable from the heat of your own finger. The water does not accumulate. It moves, or it evaporates, or it is reabsorbed by the material below.

The vegetation—such as it is—grows from these wet seams. It is sparse, almost hesitant. The plants are not like plants elsewhere. They have no leaves, no branches. They are structures: pale fibrous columns that rise from the joints in the floor, thin as wire, some ankle-high, some waist-high, a very few reaching overhead. They branch only once or twice, at sharp angles, and their tips swell into small tight nodes, hard and dry and closed. They do not photosynthesize in any way you would recognize. They seem to subsist on the moisture in the seams and the ambient heat, converting thermal energy into structure through some process that has no biology you can name. Their roots—if they are roots—run inside the seams, invisible. They do not penetrate the made-material. Nothing penetrates it.

There are no animals. No insects, no birds, no sounds of movement or breath. The quiet is vast, but it is not absolute. The place is active; it is simply active without life. 

Listen long enough and you begin to hear the processes. The tessellated floor expands and contracts with the heat. The sound is subsonic, felt more than heard—a low cyclic throb in the bones of the legs, a pressure that rises and falls over minutes or hours. The seams shift. A joint that was hairline-thin in the morning becomes a millimeter wide by midday, and the condensation in it spreads, darkening a longer stretch of the floor. By late cycle, the joint has closed again, squeezed tight, and the moisture has been forced upward, beading into a ridge of tiny droplets along the seam like a seam of glass beads. This is the weather of the place: not storms, not wind, but the slow breathing of the constructed surface itself. Thermal cycling. Expansion. Contraction. The world is flexing.

And the vegetation responds. Over the course of a long hot stillness, the pale columns swell slightly, their nodes tightening. When a seam opens and the humidity spikes, they soften, become almost pliable, and a few of them—very few—release a fine powder from their nodes, a pale dust that hangs in the still air and settles along the seam. Seed, or spore, or instruction. It drifts a short distance and settles into the moisture in the joint. By the next cycle, there may be a new wire-thin column rising from the seam, a centimeter high, barely visible. This is the ecology: material, moisture, heat, and the slow propagation of made-vegetation through the cracks in the made-world.

The scale is difficult to accept. You can walk for hours and the landscape does not change. The same flat constructed plain, the same scattered pale columns rising from the seams, the same thin threads of water, the same hot white light. But gradually you notice that the pattern of tessellation is shifting. The tiles are growing larger. You are moving, without ascending, toward something—or the geometry of the plain is such that the construction is denser in some regions than others, the seams closer together, the vegetation slightly thicker. Or perhaps the opposite: you are moving toward a region where the tiles become so vast that the seams disappear entirely, and the floor becomes a single unbroken plane, and there is no moisture, no vegetation, nothing but the made-surface stretching away under the flat hot light, perfectly smooth, perfectly dry, perfectly empty.

In the other direction—so you are told, so you infer from the way the seams multiply and the columns thicken—the construction becomes more fragmented. The tiles shrink. The joints widen into channels. The water flows faster, pools deeper. The vegetation grows denser, clusters into stands, thickets of those pale wire structures, their nodes splitting open in the humidity, releasing their dust in visible clouds. The sound of the place changes: the flexing of the surface becomes audible as a low creaking, a groan of material under stress, and the water makes a sound too, a thin trickling, almost a whisper, as it runs through the channels. It is still hot. It is still made. But it is more alive, in the only way this world can be alive.

There is no center. No edge. No structure visible above the plain—no towers, no walls, no architecture in the conventional sense. The construction is all beneath your feet, extending down to an unknown depth, and above you is only the featureless emitting sky. The world is a floor and a ceiling of light, and between them, heat, and moisture in the cracks, and the pale vegetation drinking from the seams, and the slow pulse of the material expanding and contracting, expanding and contracting, the heartbeat of a place that is still being born.

It is not finished. That is the final impression. The erosion parameter is low because there is nothing to erode—the place has not existed long enough to weather. But more than that: the construction feels *ongoing*. The seams are not yet fully sealed. The tessellation is not yet complete. The material is still settling, still finding its final tolerances. The moisture that condenses in the joints is not a feature of a finished world but a symptom of an unfinished one—water pooling in the gaps of a structure that has not yet closed itself. And the vegetation, sparse and strange, is not an invasion of the made-world by nature but an emergent property of the made-world itself, growing from its cracks according to whatever logic built the floor in the first place. The plants are made too, or they are a consequence of the making, or they are what happens when a constructed surface left unfinished encounters heat and moisture and time.

This is the place: a vast hot low flat plain of made-material, tessellated, jointed, breathing slowly in the heat, damp in its cracks, growing a sparse pale ecology from its seams, silent but for the groan of its own expansion, under a flat white sky that emits light without a source. It is a world under construction. It is a world that may never be finished.
