To be here is to stand inside the throat of a mechanical flood.

There is no sky. Above, below, and on all sides exists only the water—heavy, warm, and the color of bruised iron. It does not fall; it occupies. The atmosphere is entirely liquid, a thick, post-flood hydrosphere where the boundary between air and sea dissolved long ago, leaving only a suffocating, buoyant pressure. You do not drown here, because the rules of respiration have broken down alongside everything else. The water pushes against you like a living membrane, dense and near-touching, vibrating with an occluded warmth that feels feverish against the skin.

It is dim. The light that filters down from whatever fictional surface exists above is swallowed whole by the density, leaving only a bruised, bioluminescent twilight—a sickly chartreuse fading to deep magenta in the troughs of the currents. You cannot see the edges of this place. It extends outward past the limits of perception, a regional abyss of layered saturation.

And yet, it is profoundly, violently alive with flora. The vegetation is lush beyond the constraints of biology, a riot of chlorophyll and strange pigments that defies the liquid crush. Massive hydrothermal vines, thick as industrial smokestacks, coil around each other in paranoid helices, racing upward into the dim murk. They are choked with overlapping shelves of gelatinous leaves and blooming sacs of gas that pulse like exposed organs. Everything grows at a speed that feels like panic. The flora does not sway; it writhes. It climbs, it strangles, it overwrites itself in a continuous loop of growth and decay, forming a dense, near-solid lattice of green and black matter. There is no space between the branches. To move is to push through a yielding, tearing wall of wet leaves and spined stems.

There is no animal life. Not a single fish, nor crustacean, nor microbe swims through these waters. The ecology is a closed, insane circuit of hyperactive vegetation cannibalizing itself in the absence of predation or pollination. And yet, the place teems with movement. The plants flex and reach with mechanical intent. Flora behaves like fauna. Feeder-fronds snap shut on drifting spores with the kinetic violence of jaws; search-tendrils tap and click against the hulls of scattered structures, seeking input, seeking reaction. The border between plant and machine has rotted away entirely.

Scattered through this living wall are the remains of the built world—faint marks and occasional artifacts, swallowed by the green and the dark. A single, tilted smokestack protruding from a thicket of pneumatic vines. The skeletal frame of a factory floor, its grating now serving as a substrate for explosive fungal blooms. The curved, rusted hull of an industrial compressor, half-buried in the silt, its copper intestines pulled out and wrapped in photosynthetic coils. These are not ruins; they are anchors. The architecture is being digested and repurposed. 

Because the technology here is not dead. It is industrial, and it is dreaming. The machines did not stop functioning; they mutated. Somewhere in the deep, engines still turn, powered by the geothermal heat of this young, sharp-featured landscape. The elevation is violently low—a crushing sub-aquatic basin—but the geology is freshly torn, a new wound in the earth. Ridges of razor-sharp, un-eroded basalt jut from the silt, too young to have been smoothed by the water. Through these fractures, the industry breathes. Hydraulic pumps suck in the thick water and exhale it as superheated steam, feeding the lushness, driving the storm. Electrified cables, stripped of their rubber skins, spark and twitch in the water, sending out recursive waves of charge that make the muscles seize and the teeth ache. The factories are still producing, but they are assembling nothing—just extruding endless, useless miles of metal wire that are immediately subsumed by the climbing vines, creating a chimeric lattice of steel and root.

The noise is deafening. It is a physical weight, a sonic turbulence that threatens to shake the skeleton apart. The low, rhythmic thumping of subterranean pistons forms the heartbeat of the abyss. Over it, the high-pitched shriek of steam venting from cracked pressure valves cuts through the water like a saw. And then, the flora—the horrific, hyperactive flora—contributes its own din: the wet, explosive pop of gas-bloom sacs rupturing under the pressure; the grinding tear of cellulose being forced through metal grating; the subsonic hum of thousands of electrified roots vibrating at frequencies that blur the vision and loosen the thoughts. It is an anthill of mechanical and botanical energy, a bazaar of deafening, purposeless production.

This is a place of acute reality breakdown. The turbulence is not merely physical; it is ontological. The water behaves with episodic malice, suddenly losing its viscosity, dropping you a foot before snapping back into thick syrup. You turn a corner around a rusted girder and find a patch of water that is boiling cold, freezing the vines instantly into black glass, only for the temperature to violently invert a second later, flashing into scalding heat. Space folds. A distant smokestack appears simultaneously inches from your face and a mile away, flickering between the two states like a broken hologram. The plants grow in impossible geometries—fronds that branch into non-repeating, aperiodic tiling, leaves that display the shadow of a four-dimensional object cast into three dimensions, making the brain stutter as it tries to parse the angles. Time stutters, too. A vine will retract into a seed, and then explode outward again in a second, looping its lifecycle in a chaotic, Solaris-like manifestation of a world debugging its own existence.

You are inside a submerged, industrial stomach. The water is the acid. The vegetation is the lining. The machines are the peristalsis. And the deafening, chaotic roar is the sound of a broken world endlessly, violently digesting itself.
