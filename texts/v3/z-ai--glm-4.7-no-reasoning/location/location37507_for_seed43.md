The air is heavy and tactile, a humid breath that clings to the skin. Down here, beneath the surface of whatever world exists above, the light is flat and shadowless, filtered through layers of grime and distance until it resembles the glow of a dying hearth. It is warm, a stifling, blanket warmth that smells of wet rust, yeast, and the sharp, musky scent of crowded life.

This is the Belly.

To call it a cavern would imply a geological history that does not exist here. There is no natural stone. Every surface—the floor, the jagged ceiling, the jutting pillars that support the crushing dark above—is fabricated. It is an architecture of refuse. Thousands of years ago, or perhaps millions, something Above built this hollow and filled it with their debris. Over eons, the trash has compacted and fused, heated by the depth and pressure, until the decay of soft plastics and foams hardened into a synthetic sandstone. It is a built environment, yes, but built by negligence rather than design. The "stone" is layered like sediment, strata of blue wire, grey foam, and brown ceramic, all eroded into smooth, rounded shapes by the slow, patient grind of time.

The space is the size of a grand cathedral, perhaps a small hamlet, defined by boundaries that feel claustrophobic despite the ceiling’s height. The walls press in not because they are close, but because they are busy. This is a dense environment, a saturation of matter where a dozen different things compete for every cubic inch of air.

Water is the constant, intermittent rhythm of the place. It is not a flood, but a persistent, nagging dampness. Condensation slicks every wall, gathering in the crevices of the artificial stone until gravity forces it to drip. The sound of water is everywhere—the *plink, plink, plink* of droplets hitting the pooled floor, the gurgle of sluggish streams that have cut canyons through the compacted trash. The water itself is oily and iridescent, swirling with chemical rainbows, but it supports life.

And what life it is. The fauna here is abundant, aggressive, and loud. The silence of the deep earth is shattered by a cacophony of chittering, splashing, and calling. This is an active ecosystem, a biological engine running at high speed.

The floor is a moving carpet of carapace. The primary inhabitants are the Molt-Beetles—fist-sized insects with shells that mimic the blue and grey of the walls. They swarm over the terrain, scraping algae and moss from the damp surfaces. Where they walk, the ground seems to ripple. They are prey to the Slink-Rats, sleek, many-limbed creatures that dart between the jagged outcroppings of rusted metal protruding from the walls.

Vegetation is sparse, struggling to take root in the synthetic, metal-rich soil. But where it finds purchase—a crack in a "rock" that is actually a fused mass of glass bottles—it explodes outward. Massive, fleshy ferns unfurl, their leaves thick and waxy to hold in moisture. They grow in isolated, vibrant groves, oases of deep green that contrast violently with the dull greys of the architecture. Vines hang from the ceiling like thick, hairy ropes, dipping into the standing water below to drink, creating natural curtains that section the space into rooms.

The erosion here is established, settled. The sharp edges of the original construction have been worn away. The dangerous spikes of rebar sticking out of the "cliffs" have been polished smooth by the passing of a million insect bodies and the dripping of a billion raindrops. It is a place that has found its balance, a comfortable rot.

It is a place of primitive technology and survival. The humanoids—pale, sightless scavengers called the Dredgers—move through the noise with practiced ease. They use simple tools: sharpened lengths of pipe, woven nets made from plastic fibers stripped from the walls, and clay pots fired from the mud of the riverbed. They don't build; they rearrange. They carve hollows into the softest parts of the compressed trash walls for sleeping, and they hang cured meat from the metal ribs that jut from the ceiling. They are part of the density, moving like fluid through the solid rock of the world.

The weirdness of the place is subtle, found in the materials. Occasionally, a beetle will burrow too deep into the wall and hit a pocket of "time"—a trapped cache of ancient, dry air that hisses out smelling of sterile chemicals and ancient preservatives. Or a light will flicker in the distance, not a reflection, but a bioluminescent fungus growing on a patch of pure, energy-rich battery sludge, pulsing with a heartbeat rhythm.

But mostly, it is the sound that defines the landscape. It is loud. The scuttling of a thousand thousand legs on stone, the dripping of water from a hundred meters up, the screech of a predator, the low thrum of the Dredgers chanting to keep the dark at bay. It is a symphony of the enclosed, a roaring, vibrant, damp echo chamber where everything is eating, or being eaten, or waiting to rot into the next layer of the floor.
