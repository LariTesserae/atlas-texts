A vast, high plateau stretches under a sky that seems more like a ceiling—low, iron-dark, swallowing light rather than distributing it. The warmth here is a pressure, a thick atmospheric weight that presses down on the stone, but it brings no comfort. It is the warmth of exhausted friction, of colossal engines turning somewhere out of sight, venting heat into a world that never sees the sun. True darkness rarely falls here; instead, there is only a permanent, bruised twilight, a dimness where shadows lose their edges and distances become impossible to judge.

The terrain is an ocean of weathered rock, ancient and settled. The erosion parameter runs deep—this is not a landscape in the process of being carved, but one that has already been carved, long ago, and now rests in its final form. The stone is smooth in the way that only millennia of patient wind can achieve, its surfaces worn down to undulating swells and shallow troughs. It feels almost organic, like the hide of some dormant beast. There are no sharp edges left, no recent fractures. The plateau is a continent of rounded stone, so vast that it generates its own weather—though that weather is mostly a slow, damp exhalation.

Water is scarce, yet it defines the experience of the place. There are no rivers, no lakes. Instead, moisture accumulates in the low places—the shallow troughs between the stone swells—and condenses out of the heavy air when the temperature shifts even slightly. Pools appear and vanish over the course of hours. The rock is never dry, always tacky underfoot, always beaded with a fine dew that never quite evaporates in the gloom. Occasionally, a thin rain falls, warm and mineral-tasting, but it is less like weather and more like the plateau itself is sweating. The dampness gets into everything. It thickens the air. It makes the darkness feel tangible, a wet wool pressed against the eyes.

There is no construction here. No buildings, no roads, no artifacts. And yet, the landscape is unmistakably industrial. The technology is not built upon the land; it is emergent from it, or perhaps subterranean within it. Deep below the stone, vast engines turn—whether mechanical or geological is impossible to say. Their presence is felt rather than seen: a low, almost subsonic tremor that vibrates through the soles of the feet, a heat that rises from cracks in the rock, and most of all, the *sound*. It is not loud. The parameters of this place forbid loudness. But there is a constant, quiet chuffing, a rhythmic exhalation of steam from fissures that dot the plateau like pores. The vents release plumes of warm vapor that catch the dim light and become luminous for a moment—ghostly, pale shapes that drift and dissolve. This is the only movement in the sky. The sound is the sound of a world breathing through iron lungs.

Against this sparse, mechanical backdrop, life is shockingly abundant. The fauna have adapted to a niche that should not exist—warm, dark, damp, and untouched by any hand. They are unusual creatures, shaped by the constraints of permanent twilight and the strange ecology of the vents. Many are eyeless or nearly so, relying on vibration and chemical sensing. There are pale, soft-bodied grazers that lick the mineral dew from the stone, their forms low and wide, like furless slugs the size of cattle. They move in slow, deliberate herds from vent to vent, following the warmth. Above them, in the haze, glide things with leathery wings and keen olfactory organs, riding the updrafts from the fissures. They are silent hunters; the air carries scent well in the damp, and sound is nearly useless in the flat acoustic deadness of the weathered rock.

The most remarkable creatures are the vent-dwellers themselves—organisms that have formed a symbiosis with the plateau's mechanical exhalations. They cluster around the fissures, their carapaces dark and metallic, seemingly grown from the same ore as the stone. They feed on the chemical rich steam, and when they move, they click and whir in a way that mimics the sound of the engines below. It is unclear if they are mimicking the machines or if the machines are mimicking them; the boundary between the biological and the industrial here is thin, eroded away over the same aeons that smoothed the stone.

The ecology is active, a slow but constant churn. Predator and prey move in quiet cycles across the vastness. The herds drift; the hunters follow; the vent-dwellers cluster and disperse. There is no stillness, but there is also no urgency. Everything moves at the pace of the plateau itself—gradual, inevitable, driven by processes too large and too old to rush.

The sparse vegetation contributes almost nothing to the visual landscape, but it is there—tenacious, strange. In the deepest of the stone troughs, where condensation pools and lingers longest, grows a kind of lichen that is not quite plant and not quite fungus. It is dark, almost black, and it spreads in branching patterns that recall circuitry or the cracks in a glazed pot. It feeds on the mineral water and the faint, ambient heat, and when the steam plumes wash over it, it fluoresces—a brief, sickly bioluminescence, amber and green, the only color in the grey-black world. These flashes of light come and go without warning, making the darkness feel alive, watchful.

This is a place of paradoxes. It is untouched by any builder, yet it feels manufactured. It is natural, yet its processes are industrial. It is vast, yet it feels enclosed by the darkness and the low sky. It is alive with fauna, yet it feels desolate, the life more like an infestation than an inhabitance. Standing on the smooth, warm stone, feeling the rhythmic throb of the engines far below, watching the pale steam drift and the eyeless grazers lumber past in the dim, one gets the sense that this is not a place meant for living things. It is a place that is simply *running*—a continental machine, performing some ancient, forgotten function, and the life upon it is incidental, like mold growing in the pipes of a factory. The world does not care that you are here. It barely knows you are here. It is too large, too old, and too busy with its own deep, quiet work.
