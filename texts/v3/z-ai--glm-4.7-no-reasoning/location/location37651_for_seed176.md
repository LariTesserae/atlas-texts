# The Weeping Stack

It stands in a mountain cleft where three ridges converge — a single tower of young basalt, maybe forty meters at its widest, sheer on three sides, broken on the fourth where the rock face sheared away and left the interior guts exposed. New rock. The kind of new that hasn't yet learned to be rounded. Edges still carry their cooling cracks, columnar joints sharp as mill teeth. The stone is dark — grey-black with olivine green in the fractures — and it weeps.

Not metaphorically. Water presses up through every seam. The mountain behind it is a catchment, and the water table sits high, forced against the stack by the weight of stone and the narrowness of the gap. It emerges under pressure, not seeping but pushing, finding every joint and flaw the cooling basalt left behind. Threads of water run down the faces in patterns that shift week to week as new channels open and old ones mineralize shut. There is always sound. The stack *rings* — a low, wet, constant tonal hum from the water resonating in the rock's internal cavities, overlaid with the higher splash and chatter of surface runoff. It sounds like a factory that has been flooded and left running.

---

The vegetation came early and came dense. Spores and seeds caught in the wet crevices and found everything they needed: warmth from the sun-struck south face, constant moisture, mineral nutrients leaching fresh from the young stone. Now the stack wears a second skin of growth. Mosses first — dark, sodden, thick as carpet, drinking straight from the rock — then ferns in the deeper cracks where organic matter has begun to accumulate, their fronds overlapping and layering, each shelf of green shading the one below. Small trees have taken hold on the broader ledges: mountain kopakopa with their roots splitting stone already, only decades old and already committed to dismantling what built itself here. Vines thread from crack to crack, stitching the green together. On the interior, where the sheared face opened the stack's chambers to air, the growth is thickest of all — a hanging garden of drip-fed foliage, roots dangling free where they've found no purchase yet, still reaching for the pooled water at the base.

Nothing moves fast here. The ferns uncurl over days. The moss creeps millimeters a season. The water cuts its channels in increments too small to watch. Even the sound, for all its volume, has a steadiness that makes it feel less like event and more like condition — the acoustic equivalent of humidity.

---

At the stack's base, where the ground levels into a shallow bowl before rising to the ridgelines, the water pools. Not a lake — too shallow, too full of growth — but a wetland bench, a sponge of saturated earth and standing water between hummocks of grass. The water is clear and cold and smells of fresh basalt, of iron not yet oxidized, of the mineral breath of new land. Steam lifts off it on warm mornings when the sun hits the water before the air. Condensation clings to every surface within the cleft; the place breathes wet.

And here, at the water's edge and half-submerged in it, are the marks.

---

Someone built here. Not a settlement — a project.

The remains are scattered and partial, industrial in character, scaled to the space. A pump housing, cast iron, corroded to orange where the water touches it but still holding its shape, bolted to a stone ledge with fasteners that have fused to the rock with rust and mineral deposit. A length of pipe, ceramic-lined, running from the pool into the stack's exposed interior, where it connects to — or once connected to — a network of smaller pipes and valves that still rattle faintly when the water pressure surges. A generator housing, stripped of its copper but still massive, sitting on a poured concrete pad that is itself now cracking and moss-grown. Cable runs along the rock face, ceramic insulators still intact, wire long since stolen or rotted. A single building, barely a room, its walls half-collapsed, its roof gone, its interior full of ferns growing from the accumulated detritus of ceiling tiles and rodent nests and leaf litter. On the wall that still stands, a stenciled number: **7**.

The scale of the thing suggests extraction. They were pulling something out of this stack — water, probably, or mineral solution, or heat. The pipes run inward. The pumps face the rock. Whatever they were after, it was inside.

They left. The equipment is abandoned, not maintained. But the water still moves through some of the pipes. The pressure still operates valves that still swing open and shut on cycles timed by flow, not by hand. The stack still hums. The system they built is broken everywhere and dead in its purpose but not entirely still — it twitches, it cycles, it makes its small mechanical noises in the wet dark inside the rock where nobody goes. A machine dying very slowly, maintained by water, ignored by everything green.

---

Fauna is scarce. A few wetland birds work the shallows — pīwaiwaka flicking between hummocks, a matuku standing still among the reeds for hours at a stretch. Invertebrates in the moss: springtails, small beetles, the nymphs of insects that require clean cold water. But nothing larger. The place is too small, too enclosed, too loud. The sound deters. The constant resonant hum and splash fills every frequency a territorial call might use. Animals pass through but don't stay.

Instead: the vegetation itself acts as fauna. The ferns shiver when the water pressure pulses. The hanging roots inside the stack sway with the drip. The moss ripples — not with wind, which barely reaches this cleft, but with the slow subsurface movement of water seeking new paths. Everything green here is kinetic, just barely, just enough to notice if you watch long enough. The place stirs at the speed of growth and water.

---

Stand at the entrance to the cleft and the stack fills your vision. It is small enough to comprehend — you can see all of it at once, the tower and the wetland and the collapsed building and the exposed chambers with their hanging gardens — but too dense to absorb. Every surface is occupied. Every crevice hosts something. Stone, water, moss, pipe, root, rust, fern, wire, lichen, concrete, vine — layered and interleaved and pressed together so tightly that nothing sits on top of anything else; everything is *within* everything else. The industrial and the geological and the biological have merged into a single composite material. You cannot say where the pump ends and the root begins. You cannot say whether the pipe is holding the rock together or the rock is holding the pipe in place. You cannot say if the moss is consuming the iron or the iron is using the moss to reach the water.

This is a young place. The rock is young. The growth is young. The abandonment is young. Everything here is in its early stages of becoming whatever it will become next — the stack is still learning to erode, the vegetation is still learning to exploit the erosion, the water is still learning the paths the vegetation opens, and the rusting machines are still participating in all of it, their shapes still recognizable, their function still legible, their dissolution still far enough off that they remain *things* and not just *mineral deposits*.

Give it a century and the pipes will be veins of ceramic in the stone. Give it a millennium and the stack will be a rounded hill with a wet core. Give it ten thousand years and the cleft will fill, the water will find another way, and this will dry into a green ridge like any other, with nothing humming inside it.

But that is not what it is now. Now it is a young loud wet dense small place where new rock weeps and dead machines rattle and everything is just beginning.
