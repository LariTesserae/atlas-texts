What lives in the High Bough is not a community, and it is barely a civilization. It is a persistence. In the hollowed-out xylem towers and along the slippery bark avenues, the population is composed of things that have learned to survive in the belly of a dying god, mimicking the functions of long-vanished masters to keep the food coming.

They are the **Mimics of the Maintenance**.

First, there are the **Graft-Kin**. They are human, or at least they were, once. They are solitary figures, wrapped in layers of heavy, oil-tanned canvas and scavenged rubber, swaddled against the damp chill that never leaves the bones. They wear masks that are not masks, but rather the dried, hollowed-out carapaces of giant beetles, the multifaceted eyes replaced with thick lenses of scrap glass to see through the swirling fog. They do not speak; communication is unnecessary when the city itself broadcasts its needs through the rhythmic *chunk-whoosh* of the distant pistons. The Graft-Kin are the custodians of the rusted veins. They carry tools that are less tools and more parasites—wrenches that bite into the metal to drink the excess hydraulic fluid, saws that vibrate with the frequency of the living wood to coax it into closing over a breach. They move with the slow, deliberate grace of deep-sea divers, knowing that a sudden movement could trigger the pressure valves or the predatory floor-plants. They are lonely, but they are never alone; they are accompanied always by the hum of the Green Gomorrah, a vibration that rattles their teeth and, over decades, fuses their spines to the ambient rhythm of the city.

Then there are the **Rust-Grazer Sloths**. These are the primary domesticated "animal," though the line between fauna and machine is blurred. They are massive, loping creatures with fur that has matted into coarse, greasy dreadlocks interwoven with copper wire and rust flakes. They hang from the iron gantries, their claws evolved to grip conduits rather than branches. They do not eat leaves; they eat corrosion. Their long, prehensile tongues scrape the oxidation from the pipes, the taste of iron as sweet to them as ripe fruit. In this way, they are the city's immune system, slowly consuming the decay that threatens the structural integrity of the towers. When they move, they are accompanied by the sound of grinding metal, their stomachs filled with heavy slag which they excrete as dense, magnetic bricks—building materials that the Graft-Kin gather to patch the hollows in the walls.

Deeper in the shadows, where the geometry begins to loop and the light turns bruise-purple, dwell the **Siphon-Spiders**. They are not true spiders, but arachnids the size of hunting dogs, their bodies translucent and swollen with bioluminescent fluids. They do not spin webs of silk; they spin webs of fiber-optic cables, tapping into the nervous system of the planetary tree-god. They sit in the centers of their vibrating nets, the bioluminescence pulsing in time with the data streams of the Living City. They are harmless to the Graft-Kin, provided one does not disturb the cables. To touch a Siphon-Spider's web is to feel the sudden, agonizing rush of the city’s collective memory—the growth of a century in a second, the sensation of roots piercing bedrock, the slow agony of the engines turning. They are the historians of this place, recording the senile dementia of the super-organism.

In the plazas, where the fungal blooms are wide enough to serve as floors, live the **Echo-Walkers**. They have no substance; they are shaped like shifting pillars of mist, constrained within the outline of humanoid forms. They reenact the bureaucracy of the dead. You might see an Echo-Walker standing before a wall of swollen cactus-flesh, making gestures of writing on a clipboard that isn't there, checking pressure gauges that dissolved centuries ago. They wander in loops, their paths tracing the non-Euclidean streets that converge on nothing. They are silent, and if you try to speak to them, they simply walk through you, leaving you with a sensation of profound, ancient sorrow and a smell of wet petrichor.

Finally, there are the **Gardeners**. The city attempts to prune itself, but it is clumsy, its biological instincts overwhelming its architectural logic. The Gardeners are the correction. They appear to be autonomous drones made of carved bone and polished wood, animated by the twitching, exposed muscle of the city itself. They scuttle along the avenues on many jointed legs, armed with shears that are serrated shark-teeth. They are ruthless surgeons. When a fern-frond grows too large and threatens to block a vent, a Gardener descends, slicing through the thick cellulose with the wet sound of a butcher chopping meat. They trim the moss from the gears, they harvest the excess fruit that grows in the ventilation shafts. They have no eyes, yet they see everything through the vibration of the floor. They are the immune system’s white blood cells; they do not distinguish between a rusted pipe and a Graft-Kin’s arm if the arm is straying where it shouldn't.

But the true resident, the one that encompasses all others, is the **It-That-Sleeps**. It is the Living City itself. It is the dampness in the air, the low thrumming in the teeth, the intelligence that slowly drags time into a viscous crawl. It lives in the pressure of the water towers and the friction of the branches. It is aware of the inhabitants only as internal irritations, a mild itch that is scratched by the turning of a gear or the release of a spore. It is waiting for the end, watering its own flowers with the condensation of its breath, dreaming of a time when the metal is all gone and only the green, silent ascent remains.
