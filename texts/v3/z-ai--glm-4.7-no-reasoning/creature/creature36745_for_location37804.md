Something lives here, though it does not have a name for itself, and would not recognize the concept of having one.

It is not the forty stalks, though they are its most visible organ. It is not the water, though that is its blood. It is not the heat, though that is the slow combustion of its metabolism. It is an organism in the way a tide pool is an organism: not a single life, but a life-shape that emerges from the sum of its constraints.

It is old, but not ancient. It was born when the hatch sealed and the inventory number was forgotten and the crate settled into its rust. It was born when the water found the crack and the heat found it had nowhere to go and the bulb, against all expectation, kept burning. Its birthday is not a day but a process—the slow moment when neglect became autonomy, when maintenance failed and equilibrium found a new shape in the absence of intervention. It has been alive for as long as the drain has been clogged. It will die when the bulb finally darkens, or when the crack heals, or when someone remembers that this space exists and opens it up to the world's dry indifference.

Its body is the concrete and the steel and the air held within them. Its skeleton is the poured walls, still sharp-edged, still doing their job of containing. Its skin is the film of water on the floor—the persistent gloss that never dries, that accepts footprints and erases them with the patience of a tide. Its breath is the cycle of condensation: moisture drawn from the hot air against the cool pipes, swelling, detaching, falling with that sharp click like a fingernail tapping glass, then evaporating back into the thickened atmosphere only to condense again. It breathes in and out at the rate of water's patience, which is to say: it breathes so slowly you cannot see it happen, but you can see the evidence—the sheen on every surface, the dark delta on the eastern wall, the black pool in the clogged drain that is its stillness, its deep silence, the place where its breath pools and waits.

Its nervous system is the vegetation. Those forty stalks—threadlike, dark-green-reading-as-black, warm to the touch—are not inhabitants of the pocket. They are the pocket reaching toward something it needs. They are its fingers, extended in the slow gesture of a thing that has learned that the world will not come to it, and so it must go to the world, even if going means growing at a rate measured in centimeters per year, even if reaching means straining toward a single caged fixture whose light is flat and indifferent and probably should have failed seasons ago. The warmth of the stalks—that strange, impossible warmth, warmer than the air around them—is the warmth of something doing work. They are transmuting heat and mineral water and the dregs of industrial light into themselves, into the deep green of their chlorophyll, into the pale tips where new growth waits in its colorless embryo state. They are making something from almost nothing. This is what lives here: the act of making, occurring in a place where nothing was meant to be made.

Its memory is the crate. The half-collapsed metal box against the western wall, stamped with a number that leads to nothing, rusted into the floor at its base. The crate does not contain anything anymore—just darkness and the ghost-smell of oil or solvent, some industrial essence that has long since volatilized into the wet heat. But it is the proof that a hand was here once. It is the fossil of intention, the artifact of a time when this pocket was still a room, still had a purpose, still existed in someone's spreadsheet or mental map of the building. The crate remembers being carried. It remembers being set down. It remembers being opened and closed. It remembers being useful. And then it remembers being forgotten—the longer, slower memory of standing empty while the water crept in and the heat built up and the hatch sealed and the vegetation began its blind ascent. The crate is what the pocket was before it became what it is. It is the pocket's former life, preserved in rust and flaking green paint.

It dreams, though its dreams are not like human dreams. It dreams of the engine—not the mechanical source somewhere beyond its walls, but the vibration itself, the low roar that rounds the edges of thought and settles in the back teeth and the sternum. In its dreams, the vibration is a voice, and the voice is saying something in a language older than purpose, a language made of frequency and amplitude and the slow cycling of thermal energy. It dreams of the water finding new paths—new cracks, new seepage lines, new deltas of darkness spreading across its concrete skin. It dreams of the drain unclogging, and the black pool draining away, and the loss of that stillness that is its deepest breath. It dreams of the bulb failing, and the light going out, and the stalks reaching in darkness toward a warmth they can no longer see, and the slow, patient dying that would follow—the chlorophyll fading, the tips curling, the forty fingers withdrawing back into the wall. It dreams of the hatch opening. It dreams of the intrusion of dry air, of footprints that do not fill in, of the gradient that would carry its heat away and leave it cold and empty and just a pocket again, just a forgotten cavity with no life in it and no reason to exist.

But these are only dreams. The drain remains clogged. The bulb continues to burn, its dark scorch mark notwithstanding. The crack remains open, and the water continues to find it, and the mineral solution continues to seep through, and the roots continue to drink from the wall's failure. The engine continues to turn. The heat continues to accumulate. The stalks continue to grow.

What lives here is a slow, hot, wet machine that runs on the waste of something larger. It produces nothing that the world wants—no fruit, no flower, no shade, no beauty recognizable as beauty. It produces only the incremental growth of forty stalks of something unnamed, and the persistent film of water on the floor, and the steady accumulation of thermal energy that has nowhere to go. It is a closed loop. It is an ecosystem in miniature, a chapel of self-sufficiency, a religion with no congregation and no god and no scripture except the constant drip and the constant roar and the constant reach toward light.

It does not know you are there.

This is the most important thing to understand about what lives here. It does not know you are there because it does not know there is a *there* for you to be in. It does not have a category for *you*—for human, for intruder, for witness. It has the crate's memory of hands, but those hands were part of a world that no longer exists as far as the pocket is concerned. The pocket knows only itself: its walls and its water and its heat and its light and its forty stalks and its clogged drain and its engine-roar. It is complete. It is sufficient. It is a whole world, sealed and running, and your presence in it is an event too brief and too foreign to register against the slow timescale of its processes. Your footprints will fill in before the water has fully beaded on your skin. Your body heat will dissipate into the air before the stalks have grown a millimeter. You are a perturbation so small that it falls below the threshold of what this system considers real.

And yet.

And yet, for as long as you stand here, you are part of it. Your breath joins its breath. Your heat joins its heat. The moisture that rises from your skin joins the condensation cycle—joins the thick, candied air, finds the cool pipes, forms droplets, falls in that sharp click-click-click that cuts through the engine-roar. You are a temporary tributary, a brief inflow of water and carbon and warmth that will be absorbed and redistributed and forgotten. You are part of the pocket for as long as you are in the pocket, and then you are not, and the pocket continues, unchanged, unremembering, unguilty.

This is what lives in a place that has no reason to exist: something that has learned to exist without reason. Something that has found equilibrium in the absence of purpose, that has built a fragile, sufficient world from the waste heat and the seepage and the light that should have failed. Something that does not know it is forgotten because it does not know there is anyone to forget it. Something that does not know it is small because it does not know there is anything larger. Something that does not know it is strange because it does not know there is anything normal.

It is a pocket. And what lives in a pocket is whatever can fit—and what can fit, in a pocket this size, is a process, not a creature. A slow, patient, blind process of water finding cracks and roots finding water and stalks finding light and heat finding nowhere to go. A process that has been running for longer than bulbs are supposed to burn, and may run for longer still. A process that does not need you, that has never needed anyone, that will continue its slow, hot, wet work long after you have climbed the ladder and pulled the hatch shut behind you and returned to the world of breezes and purposes and things that know they are being seen.

What lives here will not miss you. What lives here does not know how.
