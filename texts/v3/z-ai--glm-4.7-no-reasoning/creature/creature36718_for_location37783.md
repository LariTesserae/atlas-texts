What lives here is not a collection of animals, but a single, distributed metabolism. The entities that inhabit this white, grinding hell are less "creatures" than they are components—biological cogs shaped by the pressure of the high, cold air into forms that mimic the mechanical.

To understand them, you must first look at the **Grazers**, the great moving jaws of the plateau. They are multi-legged monstrosities, low-slung and heavy, armored in matte galvanized steel that has grown ring by ring like the layers of an oyster shell. Their underside is soft, a pale, vulnerable flesh that secretes a solvent to melt the black-leafed crust, but their tops are impregnable shields of dull alloy. They move on articulated limbs that end in not wheels, but in clustered, piston-like "feet" that lock into the uneven stone. They do not walk with the fluidity of a deer; they clunk forward with the inexorable, linear progression of a stamping press. They communicate by striking their chitinous legs against the bedrock—ping, ping, ping—a sonar ping that echoes through the sheer valleys, ensuring no two Grazers ever collide in the dense fog.

Then there are the **Conveyors**, the swarming, winged things that darken the air. They are large, the size of hawks, but built like jet-age drones. Their wings are not membranous skin but rigid, serrated blades of chitin that slice through the thick, freezing air with a whistling shriek. They possess the industrial beauty of a assembly line robot; their bodies are segmented, plated in overlapping scales that slide over one another with a sound like a zipper. They do not eat; they fuel. They possess specialized proboscises that drill into the deep, spongy moss or the exposed cooling fins of the larger plants, extracting the supercooled, sugary sap that acts as hydraulic fluid for their internal systems. When they swarm, they do not flutter; they synchronize. A cloud of Conveyors moves as a solid object, a shifting tessellation of bodies that flows over obstacles like mercury, maintaining a strict centimeter of distance between neighbors. They are the couriers of the plateau, transferring pollen—which looks like fine, conductive metal dust—from one fungal tower to another, sparking tiny arcs of static electricity as they work.

The stationary life is even stranger. The **Fungal Towers** are not merely plants; they are capacitors. Growing in jagged, crystalline arrays up the vertical escarpments, they possess a silicified outer shell that is hard as glass. Inside, their vascular system is not filled with water, but with a conductive, silver gel. They grow towards the light, not to photosynthesize in the traditional sense, but to capture the intense, sterile radiation of the white sky and convert it into electrical charge. The "leaves" are heatsinks, radiating the excess energy away as a shimmering heat haze. When the Grazers strip a Tower too low, the Tower discharges a stunning burst of current—not as a defense mechanism, but as a biological reset, cauterizing the wound and welding the rock around its base to anchor itself more firmly against the plateau's violent winds.

And in the pools of frigid, viscous fluid that collect in the rock's wounds live the **Dredgers**. These are small, scuttling things, resembling a cross between a crab and a water pump. They are covered in fine, hair-like cilia that vibrate at high frequencies, keeping their bodies from freezing in the supercooled water. Their purpose is filtration; they act as the kidneys of the engine, processing the chemical runoff of the greater organisms. They take in the heavy metal-rich water and excrete small, smooth pellets of pure ore. These pellets are not waste; they are used by the smaller, cliff-dwelling "mason" organisms to patch cracks in the stone or to reinforce their own shells. The Dredgers move with a frantic, twitching energy, a sharp contrast to the slow, grinding majesty of the Grazers, ticking like watches in the silent damp.

But the true architects of this wrongness are the hidden ones, the **Weave-Vines**. They are the nervous system of the plateau. Growing across the ground, stitching the disparate biomes together, these vines are black, thick cables that look suspiciously like insulated wiring. They pulse with a slow, visible peristalsis, pumping the silver sap from the Towers to the Grazers, and from the Grazers back to the Towers. They possess thorns that are actually micro-syringes, tapping into the bark of any tree or the shell of any insect that touches them, integrating them into the grid. If you were to pull up a Weave-Vine, you would find it does not have roots, but rather a branching, fractal network of contacts that burrows into the raw granite, drawing minerals directly from the stone itself.

There is no "nature" here, in the romantic sense. There is no struggle for survival in the way a lion hunts a gazelle. There is only **function**. The Grazers do not "eat"; they *harvest*. The Conveyors do not "fly"; they *circulate*. The Towers do not *grow*; they *process*. The living things of this high, dead engine are indifferent to their own existence. They endure the blinding light and the freezing damp with the stoicism of a rivet in a hull. They are locked in a pattern of such density and complexity that they have become indistinguishable from the factory they inhabit. They are the machine's ghosts, haunting the throat of the world, working with the loud, rhythmic clatter of life that has forgotten how to be anything other than a process.
