Something immense and patient lives here, though it does not think in any way you would recognize. It lives in the aggregate—the sum total of all this wet, feverish flesh and chitin and cellulose, pressed together so tightly that no space remains between beings. You look for a creature at the top of this food chain, but there is no top. There is only a lateral smear of consumption, a horizontal frenzy. The thing that lives here is the Biome itself, and it is awake.

It does not sleep because the heat forbids it. The heat is its metabolism, its engine, the furnace that keeps the chemical reactions cascading endlessly. Every chirruping insect leg, every pulsing throat of a pale amphibian, every drip of enzymes from a fungal gill—these are not separate lives. They are the electrical impulses firing across the nervous system of a single, vast, territorial entity that refuses to acknowledge the concept of individual death. When the slug-thing the length of a city bus is pulled apart by a swarm of shale-carapaced feeders, the Biome does not lose anything. It merely redistributes. The mucus hardens into chitin. The chitin is ground down by mandibles into the gray mud. The mud sprouts phosphorescent threads that fruit and are eaten. The circle does not turn; it writhes.

The dominant consciousness, if you could call it that, is a low, continuous hum of chemical signals hanging in the saturated air. Pheromones of alarm, of ripe decay, of sexual readiness—they do not dissipate. They accumulate, layering into a thick, invisible fog of intention that presses against the skin like the humidity itself. To breathe here is to inhale the desires of a trillion mouths. The air tastes of iron, sulfur, and the sharp, ozone tang of unending biological electricity. It is a mind made of spores and scent trails, processing information not in thoughts, but in the directional flow of a million swarms shifting toward a rotting leviathan, or away from a sudden vent of superheated steam.

And within this mind, there are the Architects. They are the closest thing to a spine the Biome possesses. Blind, pale, and possessed of a chemical intelligence that borders on the mechanical, they are the insect-things that build the mounds. They do not see the obsidian shards jutting from the earth; they feel them with their antennae, taste the mineral composition of the volcanic glass, and bind it. They are the masons of the raw wound, spinning chitin and regurgitated cellulose into massive, dark cathedrals that breathe. Their mounds are the lungs of the place—labyrinthine structures that vent the toxic gases of the lower muds and draw in the thin, diffuse light from the sepia sky. Inside these structures, the temperature is stabilized. Fungi are farmed. The soft, pale grubs of the ruling caste are tended. The Architects do not rule by force; they rule by infrastructure. They make the only dry places in a world of slick edges and warm mire, and all other life comes to them, eventually, seeking shelter from the exhausting, suffocating wet. They are the princes of the perpetual present.

But even the Architects are merely symptoms. The true sovereign of this place is the Hunger. It is the only constant. There are no full bellies here, only varying degrees of empty. The oversized ferns unfurl not to seek the sun—a rumor too vague to chase—but to catch the oily drizzle, to filter the nutrients from the dead water before it sinks back into the sponge. The giant, bloated slugs slide through the mud not with purpose, but because stillness is death. If they stop, the swarming ground will claim them in hours, covering them in a shivering blanket of iridescent carapaces that will hollow them out from the underside. Life does not predate life here out of malice; it predates because the alternative is to become the mud. Everything is feeding. Everything is being fed upon. The landscape is a dinner table where the meal is always in progress, and the guests are also the courses.

This is who lives here: a world-sized mouth, wrapped in a skin of dark vines and sharp black rock, breathing out a thick, wet breath that smells of ancient swamps and fresh blood. It does not care about you. It does not care about anything. It has no future to build toward, no past to remember. Erosion has been defeated by the sheer, sticky density of life; the sharp rocks remain sharp because the Biome holds them in place, wrapped in roots as thick as torsos, bound by the mucus of giants. It exists in a fever dream of perpetual ingestion, an eternal Tuesday of the Permian, an unbroken spell of genesis that never quite figures out how to stop. It is a success story written in a language of slime and screams, thriving in a nursery that refuses to become a garden, ruled by a mind that is nothing more than the deafening, bass-heavy roar of a billion hearts pumping hot blood into a hungry, waiting dark.
