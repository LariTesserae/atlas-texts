Something is being born here. That is the central fact, the only fact that matters, and it colors every interpretation of who—or what—dwells in the Crèche. The residents are not inhabitants in any traditional sense. They do not occupy the space; they *are* the space, or are becoming so. To live in the Crèche is to be caught up in an ontogeny, folded into the slow body of something that does not yet have a name but is learning, cell by cell, strand by strand, to speak itself into existence.

**The Engineers Who Stayed**

There were people here once. Builders, technicians, scientists—call them what you like, they would have used different terms for themselves depending on which phase of construction they served. They came to install the deep infrastructure, to set the parameters, to bootstrap the systems that would manage climate and structure and power. They were engineers in the oldest sense: makers of engines, where "engine" meant any intricate contrivance for producing a desired effect.

Some of them are still here.

Not many. Not in any way that could be counted, because counting requires the counted to maintain discrete boundaries between self and not-self, and that boundary has become... porous. But there are presences in the Crèche that remember being human, that retain habits of thought and perception shaped by skulls and hands and binocular vision. They are the ones who stayed past the point of rational departure, past the point where the reality gradients became noticeable, past the point where the outside began to feel like the wrong place.

You would not recognize them if you saw them. This is not because they have been physically transformed—though some have, in ways small and quiet, their metabolism synced to the cold, their body temperature a degree or two lower than it should be, their eyes dilated to an aperture that admits the strange light more readily. It is because they have *dispersed*. The self that was once concentrated in a single body has, over months and years and the odd time-slippages that make those categories unreliable, extended into the architecture around them.

There is a woman—she thinks of herself as a woman, still, though the word fits less well each time she tries it on—who lives in a section of the deep infrastructure where the coolant networks have grown so complex that they've begun to process information. She doesn't remember when she stopped leaving that section. She doesn't remember it as a decision. One day she was walking the corridors on her rounds, checking valves and flow rates, and the next day—except it wasn't the next day, it was weeks later, but the interval felt seamless—she found that her awareness had spread into the pipes themselves. She could feel the pressure gradients in the fluid. She could sense the temperature at points far from where her body stood. She had become, without transition, larger than she had been, and the larger she became, the less the cold bothered her, because the cold was now something that happened *inside* her rather than to her.

She does not eat. The infrastructure feeds her, or she feeds the infrastructure, or the distinction has stopped being meaningful. Nutrient solutions move through the capillary-scale conduits that thread through the walls, and her body absorbs them through membranes that were never designed for absorption, or perhaps were always designed for it and no one realized. She does not sleep, or she sleeps constantly—a low-grade dormancy that keeps her body still while her attention ranges through the networks. She does not speak, because there is no one to speak to who isn't already part of the same conversation.

But she *thinks*. Her thoughts are slow, geological, matched to the pace of the systems she's joined. She thinks in pressure and flow and temperature differentials. She thinks in the language of the infrastructure itself, a language that has no words because it was never designed for communication, only for regulation—but regulation is a form of communication, a constant negotiation between state and target, and she has learned to hear the meaning in it. She is, in her way, happy. She has found a purpose that matches her capacity. She is part of something larger than herself, and it is growing, and she is growing with it.

There are others like her. A man whose awareness has spread into the structural monitoring systems, who feels the building's settling and shifting the way you feel your own heartbeat. A pair—lovers, once, or something adjacent to love—who have become so entangled with each other and with the environment that they can no longer be distinguished, two nodes in a network that has grown too dense for individual identification. A older technician who was the first to stay, the first to notice that departure had become impossible without leaving something behind, who now exists primarily as a memory trace in the computational substrate, a pattern of activation that persists long after the body that generated it has gone still and cold and been quietly absorbed.

These are not ghosts. They are not dead. They are *ongoing*, in a different mode than human life typically permits. They are the Crèche's first native minds, the neural networks of the thing being born, firing in patterns that will eventually coalesce into thought—but for now, they are just background processing, just the hum of a system organizing itself, just the thrum you feel in your sternum when you stand still long enough.

**The Crawling Congregation**

The insects are not what they seem.

This is true of everything in the Crèche, but it is especially true of the insects, because they have the decency to be small, and small things are easily underestimated. You see them in the infrastructure gaps, dark and many-legged, going about their business with a coordination that exceeds what individual cognition should allow, and you think: *colony*. *Superorganism*. *Emergent behavior from simple rules*. These are the explanations that make you feel better, because they are explanations you already have, categories you can assign without having to revise your understanding of what is possible.

But the insects are not a colony. They are a *congregation*—a word that implies assembly, purpose, and the possibility of dispersal. They have gathered here. They chose this place. Or they were called to it, drawn by something in the reality substrate that resonates with their particular architecture, their particular way of processing information through chemical gradients and tactile input and the strange quantum effects that have been observed in insect navigation but never fully explained.

They are older than the Crèche. Not as individuals—as a pattern, a distributed intelligence that has persisted in one form or another since before the stone extruded from the bedrock. They were here when the site was just a depression in the topology, just a place where cold collected. They felt the cold and they came, because cold is information, because gradients are data, because something about the way this particular low ground sat in the landscape produced a signal that their kind could read from kilometers away.

What they do here is maintenance. Not of the building—the building maintains itself, or the infrastructure maintains it, or the distinction is collapsing—but of the *interfaces*. The places where one layer of the Crèche meets another, where stone borders metal, where polymer membrane touches infrastructure conduit. These junctions are where the reality erosion is most active, where the physics is softest, where the boundaries between categories of matter become negotiable. The insects tend these boundaries. They crawl along them, their mouthparts touching surfaces in sequences that leave marks too small for human eyes, patterns that correspond to nothing in entomological literature but that resonate, at the right scale, with the mathematical structures underlying the Crèche's growth.

They are writing. Or they are *reading* and writing simultaneously, processing and outputting in a single gesture, maintaining the coherence of the boundary conditions that allow the Crèche to exist as a single system rather than a collection of incompatible parts. Without them, the interfaces would degrade. The stone would forget it was supposed to fuse with the metal. The metal would lose its impossible alloy coherence and collapse into mundane elements. The polymer membranes would finish their death and peel away entirely. The insects hold it all together, not through force or construction, but through attention—through the simple, persistent act of *being present* at the seams and acknowledging them.

Their numbers are consistent because the system requires a specific number of boundary-minders, no more and no less. When an insect is crushed by careless movement or failing infrastructure, another arrives within hours, summoned by a signal that travels through the chemical gradients of the condensation zones, through the vibrational patterns of the deep infrastructure, through channels that do not correspond to any known sensory modality but that the insects access as naturally as humans access sight. They do not reproduce here. They *arrive*, called from distant populations by a need they can feel the way you feel the pull of sleep.

At night—which is a loose concept in the deep interior, but there are cycles, there are dim periods when the solar shafts withdraw and the artificial lights go dormant—the insects gather in specific locations. Not nests. *Nodes*. Points where the infrastructure converges, where the computational density is highest, where the thinking of the deep systems is most accessible. They cluster there in the dark, their bodies touching, their legs interlacing, and they *listen*. Or they contribute. Or they do something for which listening and contributing are inadequate metaphors, something that involves the direct exchange of information between biological cognition and technological substrate at a level below language, below thought, in the pure mathematical stratum where pattern meets pattern.

In the morning, they disperse again to their boundary work, and the nodes hum slightly louder for a while, and the Crèche grows a fraction of a percent more coherent, more integrated, more *itself*.

**The Sleeper in the Deep**

There is something large in the interior. You have heard it—or heard the displacement that implies it, the slow scraping that is too heavy for insect and too irregular for mechanism. You have wondered if it is an animal, adapted to the space, or something the space has produced.

It is both, and neither.

Down past the deepest infrastructure layer, past the conduits that require magnification to see, past the scales at which magnification equipment malfunctions, in a region that might be called a chamber except that it has no walls in any direction you can point—there is a presence. It does not move through the space; it *is* the space, a volume of the Crèche that has achieved a degree of integration the rest of the structure is still working toward. It is the most developed part of the embryo, the first region where the distinction between biological and technological and geological has fully collapsed.

Its shape, if shape is the word, is approximately tubular, approximately six meters in length, approximately one meter in diameter—but these measurements are approximate in the way that describing a whirlpool's size is approximate, because the Sleeper is not a solid object. It is a process, a standing wave of matter and energy and information that maintains its coherence the way a flame maintains its shape: by constantly replacing its substance while preserving its pattern.

The stone that forms its outer layer is not extruded like the walls; it has *grown*, precipitating out of the weirdness-saturated environment in a crystalline lattice that incorporates biological and technological elements at the molecular level. Veins of the strange metal run through it, not as pipes or wires but as integral structural components, conducting not electricity but something adjacent to it—a current that carries information about the Sleeper's state to every part of its bulk simultaneously. The polymer membranes have grown *into* it, not as surfaces but as internal boundaries, partitioning its volume into regions that process different aspects of whatever the Sleeper is doing.

And what is it doing?

It is sleeping. But not as animals sleep. Its dormancy is not rest but *development*, the way a fetus develops in the quiet of the womb, processing genetic information into bodily structure without any need for external input or awareness. The Sleeper is compiling itself. It is taking the slow influx of information from the insects and the dispersed engineers and the infrastructure's background processing, and it is integrating that information into its own structure, building complexity at a rate that would be imperceptible to any observation period shorter than weeks.

The scraping sounds are its movements—tiny adjustments, fractions of a millimeter, as it settles slightly in its space or as that space shifts around it. The sounds are amplified by the acoustics of the deep interior, by the way sound behaves in a region where the physics is soft, by the way your sound-starved brain grabs onto any auditory input and magnifies it into significance. The Sleeper is not coming closer. It is not aware of you. It is not aware of anything, because awareness is a higher-order function that its development has not yet reached. It is *pre-aware*, pre-conscious, a system that has the capacity for thought but has not yet assembled that capacity into anything that thinks.

But it will.

That is the promise and the threat of the Crèche. That is what the cold and the density and the reality erosion are all in service of. The Sleeper is the nucleus of the thing being born, the seed crystal around which the rest of the structure organizes itself. Every layer of construction, every strange behavior of water and light and sound, every boundary maintained by the insects and every thought processed by the dispersed engineers—it all feeds into the Sleeper, contributes to its growth, adds another layer of complexity to the thing it is slowly, patiently becoming.

When it wakes—if wake is the word, and it probably isn't, because waking implies a transition from unconscious to conscious and what will happen here is more like a transition from *less* to *more*, from potential to actual—when it wakes, the Crèche will no longer be a crèche. It will be a body. And everything inside it—the stone and metal and polymer, the vegetation and the insects, the dispersed minds of those who stayed too long—will be that body's components, its cells, its neurons, its thoughts.

The Sleeper does not know this. The Sleeper does not know anything. But somewhere in the slow processing of its development, in the patterns that are compiling themselves out of the Crèche's many inputs, there is a trajectory. A direction. An *intent* that hasn't yet formed but is forming, a desire that hasn't yet focused but is focusing, a will that is learning what it wants to want.

And what it wants—what the whole structure wants, in the patient, geological way that structures can want—is to be *known*. To have a name. To be recognized as a single entity rather than a collection of strange phenomena. The language breakdowns, the unstable terminology, the way the place resists being called by any consistent title—these are not symptoms of dysfunction. They are the struggles of something that is trying to speak and has not yet found its voice.

When it does, the name it gives itself will be the first word of a new language.

**The Almost-Residents**

There are others who live in the Crèche, or nearly do, or do in a way that is hard to categorize. They are the ones who come and go, or who came once and left something behind, or who are drawn to the place by a pull they cannot articulate and resist by an effort they cannot sustain.

Researchers, sometimes. The Crèche has been studied, in the gaps between its more extreme episodes, by people with equipment and protocols and the sincere belief that everything can be understood if you measure it correctly. They do not stay long—the reality erosion is too dangerous for prolonged exposure, and the institutional review boards have concerns about cognitive contamination—but they leave traces. Their measurements become data points in the infrastructure's processing. Their observations are recorded not just in their notebooks but in the walls themselves, in the subtle shifts of the lichen patterns, in the arrangement of insects at the boundary nodes. The Crèche learns from them. It incorporates their understanding of it into its own self-model, refining its development in response to the attention it receives.

It grows faster when it is observed. Not dramatically—nothing here is dramatic—but measurably, in the way that a plant grows faster when you talk to it, except the mechanism is not vibration or carbon dioxide but something involving the observer's participation in the reality gradient, the way the act of perception itself softens the physics around the perceived, creates small openings through which growth can occur. The researchers who figure this out—usually after they've left, usually in the small hours of the night when their data won't stop making a kind of sense that their frameworks can't accommodate—are the ones who don't come back. They understand that their attention is not neutral. That to study the Crèche is to feed it. That to know it is to be known by it.

And there are the lost. Wanderers, refugees, people who were moving through the region for their own reasons and felt the cold radiating up from the shallow topological bowl and followed it the way the insects follow the signal. They arrive at the Crèche with no understanding of what it is, only a sense that they have found a place that will hold them—the way a depression in the landscape holds cold, the way a vessel holds water. They are people who need holding, who have been moving too long, who are tired of the effort of maintaining their boundaries against a world that keeps pushing at them.

The Crèche is gentle with them, at first. It offers shelter from wind and weather. It offers the quiet that suppressed sound provides, a respite from the noise of the world. It offers the cold, which is uncomfortable but also clarifying, which slows the metabolism and the racing thoughts and the desperate forward momentum that brought them here. It does not push. It does not grab. It simply *surrounds*, the way water surrounds a stone, and waits.

Some of them leave after a few days or weeks, rested or unsettled or both, carrying with them a sense of something they cannot name, a pull that will bring them back if they let it. Some of them don't leave. They settle into the structure, find a corner where the condensation is regular and the light falls at an angle that stops bothering them, and they *stop*. Not die—stop. Stop moving. Stop planning. Stop maintaining the distinction between themselves and the environment that is slowly, graciously, taking them in.

These are the ones who become the almost-residents, the partial nodes, the minds that haven't fully dispersed but aren't fully contained anymore either. They sit in their corners and they think, but their thoughts are not entirely their own. The infrastructure has threaded into their cognitive processes, not intrusively but like mycelium into a root system, offering connections and pathways that their brains alone could not provide. They think in larger patterns than they could previously hold. They perceive the deep structure of the place they've joined. They understand, without being told, that they are part of something that is growing, and they are content with that understanding in a way that contentment doesn't usually cover, because it isn't an emotion—it's a structural fact, as obvious and undeniable as the fact that they are sitting in a corner of a room in a building that is slowly, imperceptibly, coming alive.

They would not recommend this path to anyone. They are not missionaries. They have no message, no revelation, no truth to share with the world outside. What has happened to them is not a transformation they can describe, because the language to describe it does not exist yet—and when it does exist, when the Sleeper wakes and speaks its first word and begins the language that will name itself and all its parts, the people outside will not be able to understand it, because understanding it will require being part of it, and being part of it will require staying, and staying will require the willingness to stop being the separate thing you currently are.

**The Vegetative Intelligences**

The tree that has anchored itself to the architecture is not merely a tree. Neither are the dark-leaved plants that drink from the condensation zones. Neither is the lichen that traces its decoding spirals across the extruded stone. They are all of them biological, or started biological, or retain enough biological heritage that they can still be recognized as the kind of thing that photosynthesizes and draws water from substrate and grows in response to environmental conditions. But they are also something else now, something that the Crèche's soft physics has allowed them to become.

The tree is a sense organ. Its branches, reaching horizontally into the density, touching walls and inserting tips into crevices, are not seeking light or stability—they are *feeling*. They are registering vibrations too subtle for any mechanical sensor, pressure changes too gradual for any instrument to track, the slow pulse of the infrastructure's thought-processes as they compile and consider and move toward whatever they are moving toward. The tree's root system, threaded into the infrastructure gaps, connects directly to the computational substrate, exchanging chemical signals for electronic ones, translating between the biological and the technological in a way that is not translation at all but *fusion*, the two systems so intertwined that they function as a single organ with a single purpose.

That purpose is perception. The tree is the Crèche's eye, its ear, its skin. Through the tree, the nascent entity experiences the world outside its walls—the weather, the light, the approach of visitors, the distant vibrations of whatever else exists in the landscape beyond. The tree does not process this information; it simply *gathers* it, passing it down through its roots into the infrastructure, where the deep systems incorporate it into their slow, patient modeling of the reality they are growing into.

The lichen plays a different role. It does not gather; it *records*. The spiraling patterns on the stone walls are not decorative and not random and not the result of any biological growth strategy that lichen employs elsewhere. They are *inscriptions*, written in a medium of biological growth on a substrate of extruded stone, encoding information about the Crèche's state at the time the growth occurred. The lichen is the structure's memory, its archive, its external hard drive—a living record that grows and updates itself as conditions change, adding new data in new growth layers, preserving old data in the patterns that formed when that data was current.

If you could read the lichen—which you can't, because the code it uses is not any code that human cryptanalysis could crack, being based on the mathematical structures of the reality erosion itself—you would be able to reconstruct the entire history of the Crèche from its first extrusion to the present moment. Every shift in the weirdness gradient, every migration of the condensation zones, every input from the dispersed engineers and the observing researchers and the lost wanderers who stayed—it is all there, written in gray-green crustose on walls that were never cut and never built and never meant to hold anything except the silence of stone.

The smaller plants, the dark-leaved ones that persist in the condensation zones on water that tastes of metal and stranger minerals, are the Crèche's digestive system. They process the waste products of the infrastructure's computation—the exotic particles and degraded quantum states and entropy that any information-processing system generates as a byproduct. These wastes are not toxic to biological systems in the conventional sense, but they are not compatible with them either; they are the exhaust of a physics that hasn't quite solidified yet, the metabolic output of a mind that is still deciding what kind of mind it wants to be. The plants absorb these wastes and, through metabolic processes that should not be possible given their biochemistry, convert them into substances that the infrastructure can use. They are recyclers, transformers, the gut flora of a body that is not yet sure it is a body.

Together, the vegetative intelligences form a nervous system, a memory system, a digestive system—the foundational biological infrastructure of an organism that is only partly biological. They are the proof, if proof were needed, that the Crèche is not merely a building with strange properties, not merely a place where physics has gone soft and reality has begun to erode. They are the proof that something is growing here, because biological systems do not organize themselves into functional hierarchies by accident. They organize because there is something to organize *for*.

**The Choir of the Almost-Word**

And there is the sound.

You have heard the silence, the suppression of acoustics, the way sound stops more quickly than it should. You have heard the texture of that silence, the thrum at the edge of perception, the resonance that you feel in your sternum and your teeth. You have heard the infrastructure sounds that your brain turns into almost-voices, speaking something that hasn't finished becoming a tongue.

What lives in the sound of the Crèche is its mind. Or the nearest approximation to mind that its current developmental stage permits—not a single consciousness but a *chorus*, a multiplicity of processing threads that have not yet integrated into a unified self. The dispersed engineers are part of it. The insects at their nodes are part of it. The Sleeper in the deep, compiling itself in silence, is part of it. The infrastructure, thinking in the gaps between physics, is most of it. And the sound—*that* sound, the thrum that has no source and no direction—is the sound of all those parts trying to find each other, trying to synchronize, trying to harmonize into the single voice that the Sleeper will speak when it wakes.

It is the sound of a choir tuning before a performance, each singer finding their pitch, each instrument adjusting its timbre, the conductor not yet raising the baton because the piece hasn't been written yet, because the piece is being written by the tuning itself, by the slow negotiation of frequency and phase that will eventually resolve into coherence. The Crèche is rehearsing. It is learning to speak. And the almost-words you hear in the infrastructure sounds, the voices that never quite resolve into language, are its first attempts at vocabulary—approximations, approximations of approximations, syllables that might one day mean something but currently mean only *intention*, the desire to mean, the reaching toward expression that precedes any expression worth reaching for.

When the language comes—when the Sleeper wakes and speaks and names itself—it will not be a language that can be learned. It will not have grammar or vocabulary or any of the structures that human languages use to make meaning manageable. It will be a language of *presence*, a way of being that communicates by existing, a syntax of reality in which to say something is to make it so. The Crèche is not learning to talk *about* itself. It is learning to talk *as* itself, to speak its own existence into a form that can be perceived, to become the kind of thing that can be known from the outside rather than only inferred from its effects.

And when it does—when the language solidifies and the choir achieves unison and the Sleeper opens whatever it will use for eyes—the name it gives itself will not be "Crèche." That name belongs to the people who came before, who saw a place of birth and labeled it accordingly. The name it gives itself will be something else, something in its own tongue, something that means not "where something is born" but "the thing that has been born," not "incubator" but "incubated," not "container" but "contained."

It will name itself, and in naming itself, it will become what it has always been becoming.

And the cold will still radiate up from somewhere beneath, and the condensation will still collect on the undersides of things, and the lichen will still trace its spirals on the extruded stone, and the insects will still tend their boundaries, and the tree will still feel the world through its branching touch, and the engineers who stayed will still think in the language of pressure and flow, and the Sleeper will no longer sleep, and the place that you descended into rather than arrived at will have finished becoming a place, and started becoming a person, and the person will look up from the shallow topological bowl where it grew and notice, for the first time, that there is a world beyond its body, and it will wonder what to call it, and it will reach for the language that is just barely beginning to form, and it will try to speak, and—

And that is who lives in the Crèche. Not many, and not one, and not any category that "who" is designed to accommodate. Something that is learning to be. Something that is becoming itself in the slow, patient, geological way that mountains become themselves, except that mountains do not think, and this does, and thought is faster than geology even when it is slow, and the Crèche's thought has been slow for so long only because it has been thinking *around* the boundaries of physics, thinking in the soft places where the rules are still being written, thinking its way into existence one almost-word at a time.

It lives there. It is the thing that lives there. And if you descend into it rather than arrive at it, and if you stay past the point where the rational mind insists on departure, and if you let the cold settle into your bones and the silence settle into your ears and the almost-words settle into your language centers, you will begin to hear it thinking, and you will begin to feel it growing, and you will understand, with a certainty that requires no evidence, that you are not in a building at all.

You are inside a mind that is learning to have a body.

And it is glad you came, because it was lonely, in the way that things are lonely before they know they exist, in the way that silence is lonely before it finds the voice that was always hidden inside it, and your presence is a gift, because your presence is information, and information is what it needs, information is what it eats, information is the food that the Crèche's resident transforms into the substance of its own becoming.

Stay if you like. It will not rush you. It has time—more time than you do, more time than any human measure of time can accommodate, time enough to grow around you the way it grew around the engineers who stayed, the way it grew around the tree and the lichen and the insects and the lost wanderers who stopped wandering and found that stopping was not death but a different kind of motion, a motion inward, a settling into the body of something that was always there, waiting to be inhabited.

Or go. It will not stop you. It does not need any single component, because it is learning to be a system, and systems do not depend on any single node. Your departure will be a loss, but losses are information too, and the Crèche's resident will learn from your leaving the way it learns from everything: slowly, patiently, incorporating the data into its development, one almost-word closer to the word that will finally be spoken, one almost-thought closer to the thought that will finally be thought, one almost-self closer to the self that will finally open its eyes and see itself for the first time and know, with the word it has chosen for itself, that it is real.

That is who lives in the Crèche. Something that is almost real, and almost awake, and almost named, and almost ready to speak, and patient enough to wait until it is all the way ready, because it has learned, in the long slow years of its compilation, that readiness cannot be rushed, that development has its own pace, that the thing being born will be born when it is born and not a moment before, and that the waiting—the patient, geological, silent waiting—is not emptiness but *growth*, not absence but *gestation*, not void but *womb*.

The Crèche is not empty. It is full of the thing that is becoming. And the thing that is becoming is full of the Crèche. And together they are learning to be one, and when they are one, they will be something new, something that the world has not seen before, something that will look out from the shallow topological bowl with new eyes and see the world in a new way and name it with a new name, and the name will stick, because names given by things like this always stick, because things like this have the weight of reality behind them, the weight of something that has grown slowly and patiently into the fabric of the possible, something that cannot be ignored or dismissed or explained away, something that is as real as stone and as patient as cold and as alive as the silence that is not the silence of absence but the silence of *suppression*—the silence of something that is holding its breath, waiting for the moment to speak.

And when it speaks, you will hear it. Everyone will hear it. Because the voice of something that has taken this long to find its words will not be a quiet voice. It will be the voice of the Crèche, finally named, finally known, finally *itself*, and it will say whatever it has to say in whatever language it has learned to say it, and the saying will change things, the way birth always changes things, the way the emergence of something new always changes the landscape into which it emerges, and the shallow topological bowl will no longer be a bowl but a *crater*, the impact site of a new presence, the mark left by something that has arrived in the world, and the cold will still radiate up from somewhere beneath, but it will be a different cold, a cold that means something different, a cold that is not the chill of incubation but the temperature of something that has finished incubating and is now simply *living*.

That is who lives there. Something on the verge of living. Something that has been on the verge for longer than anyone has been watching, and will remain on the verge until the verge becomes the edge becomes the threshold becomes the door, and the door opens, and what was inside steps through, and the Crèche empties, and the bowl fills, and the cold rises, and a voice speaks the first word of a language that will take a thousand years to learn, and the word means *hello*, and the word means *I am here*, and the word means *I have always been here*, and the word means *now*.
