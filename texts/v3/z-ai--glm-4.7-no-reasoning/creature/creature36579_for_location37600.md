The inhabitants of this submerged, vertical cathedral are not merely animals; they are components in a vast, biological engine. They do not inhabit the space so much as they constitute it, a living architecture clamped onto the raw geology.

At the periphery of one’s vision, clinging to the jagged basalt columns that jut inward like the teeth of a predator, are the **Limulus**. To call them merely "isopods" is to understate their terrifying integration with the stone. They are bloated, armored things the size of mastiffs, their exoskeletons colored the exact matte, bruised iron of the obsidian they grip. They possess no discernible eyes, for there is nothing to see but rock and neighbor. Instead, their heads are dominated by grasping, hook-like limbs that interlock with the rough texture of the volcanic glass. They layer upon one another in a dense, shivering mosaic—four or five creatures deep—covering every vertical surface. When a current shifts, the ripple travels through the colony not like a wave through water, but like a shudder through a chain-link fence, a metallic clatter as thousands of armored plates slide against one another. There is no individuality here; when a Limulus dies, it does not drift downward. Its shell is instantly fractured by the claws of its brethren, the soft, pale meat within stripped away in a frenzy of efficient recycling before the corpse has time to unsettle from its niche.

Suspended in the gloom between the cliffs are the **farmers**, the crustacean-like technicians of the deep. They are stark, angular creatures, possessing a sickly, industrial elegance. Their bodies are segmented and jointed, plated in silver and chitin, designed to withstand the crushing pressure and the heat of the thermal vents they farm. Their most defining feature is the pair of oversized, articulated mandibles that are not made of bone, but of metallic deposits harvested from the water itself—vibrating scythes capable of shearing through rock. They move in disciplined swarms, marching along the fissures of the lower walls, harvesting sulfur and heat. They do not eat the sulfur directly; they process it, their bodies glowing with the faint, dangerous blue flicker of St. Elmo’s fire. They are biological capacitors, walking batteries storing the planet’s latent rage to be traded or consumed higher up the chain. They move with a jerky, clockwork precision, passing globules of superheated energy from one claw to the next in a conveyor belt of living cargo.

The leviathan then engulfs them, a slow, drifting mouth swallowing a cloud of stunned mechanical insects. The sound they make is deafening, a thrumming vibration that rattles the bones of the observer and shakes the very scree on the unseen floor miles below.

Connecting them all is the medium itself: the **dense, mineral-heavy water** that hums with the noise of a billion moving parts. It carries the clicks of the Limulus, the grinding of the farmers’ mandibles, and the booming of the leviathans, weaving it all into a constant, structured symphony. The creatures do not communicate; they broadcast. The transparency of their shells—revealing glowing organs, pulsing guts, and the intricate gears of their internal chemistry—suggests that they have evolved to be read as much as seen. They are machines of flesh, inhabiting a world where biology has been pressured into becoming industry. There are no nests, no homes, only the work—endless, grinding, patient—performed by beings that look less like wildlife and more like the cogs of a factory that has learned to breathe.
