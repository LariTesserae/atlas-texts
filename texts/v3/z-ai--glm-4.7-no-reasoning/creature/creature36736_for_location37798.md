They are small, soft, and impossibly vulnerable against the backdrop of this roaring, fleshy colossus. To call them "inhabitants" implies a degree of dominion they do not possess; they are not the masters of this depression, but its parasites, its quiet itch. They are the **Osmotics**, a people shaped by the crushing weight of the sun and the suffocating generosity of the air.

They stand no taller than a human child, though their proportions are elongated and ectomorphic. There is no sharp angle to their anatomy. Evolution here has sanded away the ridges of bone and the edges of joints, smoothing them into a streamlined, gelatinous continuity. Their skin is the defining feature of their existence: a membrane of translucent, pearlescent grey that feels slick and cool to the touch, like the skin of a mushroom. It is not a barrier; it is a net. Across their surface, a lattice of raised, hexagonal ridges serves as the primary interface for survival. Through these ridges, they drink the atmosphere. They do not breathe in the ragged rhythm of a lung; they simply *are*, their cells perpetually bloated with the volatile humectancy of the broth they inhabit. Their blood is not red, but a thick, sap-like emerald, heavy with the same nutrients that feed the trees.

Because the air vibrates with the cacophony of a billion biological processes, sound is a useless medium for intimacy. They possess no vocal cords. Their faces are smooth, skull-like masks of cartilage, lacking noses or ears. Where a mouth should be, there is only a permanent, sphincter-like seal—a remnant of a digestive tract that became obsolete eons ago. Instead, they communicate through **bioluminescence and tremor**. Across their backs and along the lengths of their spindly, multi-jointed arms, veins of phosphorescent fluid pulse with slow, complex rhythms. A shift from blue to amber indicates curiosity; a rapid, staccato flashing of jagged red is a scream of danger. To "speak," they press their bodies against the living wood of the mega-flora, sending vibrations deep into the rhizomatic cortex, letting the ground itself carry their words.

Their morphology is dictated by the terrifying "Vegetation Score" of their home. They are evolved climbers, evolved to live not on the ground, but in the dangerous verticality of the spires. Their limbs are too long for their torsos, ending in digits that are not fingers, but delicate, hook-like rasps capable of slicing through the thick, fleshy bark of the tree-towers. They can leap incredible distances, swimming through the heavy air with the grace of flying squirrels, their skin flaps billowing out to slow their descent.

They dress in the skins of the lesser failures of the jungle. Their clothing is minimal, constructed from the cured, leathery petals of the pulse-flowowers or the hollowed-out carapaces of the giant, swimming insects. They wear nothing that impedes the contact of their skin with the mist. A "mask" is a rare piece of technology, a slab of dense, porous chitin carved to fit over the face, used only when venturing near the hotter, more volatile volcanic vents where the spore count is toxic even to them.

The Osmotics live in **The Grafts**. Their villages are not built; they are surgically incited into being. Using crude, jagged knives made from the sharpened beaks of apex predators, they wound the massive vertical trunks of the mega-flora. Into these wounds, they insert spores and hormones, persuading the plant to grow in specific, unnatural shapes. A doorway is a permanent callus forced into a circle; a platform is a massive shelf of induced wood-growth that weeps a natural resin to seal the cracks. Their homes are hollows within the muscle-trees, warm and humid, lined with soft, downy mosses they cultivate for bedding.

Their technology is a horror of botany. They practice **flesh-welding**. If a bridge is needed, they do not weave ropes; they splice together the living vines of two separate spires, stretching them taut until the vascular systems merge and the two trees become one, a suspended archway of living tissue that pulses and flexes with the wind. They make tools by "infecting" branches with calcifying bacteria, causing the wood to harden into stone-like density before snapping it off to use as a maul or a hammer.

Socially, they are twitchy and hypervigilant. The constant sensory overload—the vibration, the light, the osmotic pressure of the nutrients—keeps their nervous systems in a state of high-frequency tension. They move with a jerky, insectoid speed, constantly twitching their heads to taste the air currents. Sleep is communal and shallow; they huddle together in piles inside their hollows, their bioluminescent bodies syncing up to pulse in a single, slow rhythm, a beacon of calm in the chaotic night.

They view the world not as a place of resources, but as a single, hostile, breathing organism that must be placated. They do not "hunt" the swimming manta-rays of the sky; they perform "harvests," drifting on woven platforms to catch the clumsy grazers, dissecting them with a reverence that borders on ritual, returning the inedible parts to the floor as an offering to the roots. They are the midwives of chaos, trimming the fungal foam, directing the growth of the vascular arches, living terrified, awe-struck lives in the belly of a biology that has forgotten how to be still.
