The residents of the Low Refinery do not walk the streets. They are the streets. In this humid, pressurized depression, the distinction between the mechanic and the biological has dissolved, and the entities that dwell here have evolved to occupy the liminal space between engine and organism.

The primary citizens of this rusted cathedral are the **Gear-Gardeners**. They are not humanoid, nor are they strictly animals. They are arthropods of significant size—perhaps the size of a large dog or a small calf—with carapaces composed of oxidized iron and biomineralized copper. Their shells are segmented and jagged, mimicking the scrap metal they crawl over, providing them with perfect camouflage against the backdrop of rivets and girders. They move with a stiff, hydraulic gait, their multiple legs clicking against the wet grates. They have no eyes, at least not in the traditional sense; instead, their heads are dominated by sensitive, feathered antennae that wave constantly, tasting the density of the air and reading the vibrations of the groaning flywheels. They are the stewards of the equilibrium. You will find them clustered around the massive, moss-choked drive shafts, using their powerful mandibles to strip away parasitic fungi that threaten to seize the motion, or to clear sludge from the intake valves. They do not eat the vegetation; they consume the friction, the heat, and the waste byproducts of the machine, maintaining the slow, deliberate heartbeat of the place.

Living symbiotically alongside them are the **Bellows-Bats**. The creatures hang in the high, vaulted shadows of the refinery ceilings, where the smokestacks belch their warm, carbon-rich breath. They have evolved to lack the echolocation of typical bats; here, sound is a constant, roaring ocean, and sonar would be useless. Instead, they possess vast, leathery wings that act as thermal sails, riding the updrafts of heated air rising from the condensation plants. Their fur is slick and oily, repelling the constant moisture, and their faces are flat, wide scoops lined with cilia that filter the heavy air for spores and drifting particulates. They sleep in tight, clinging clusters on the undersides of the gantries, their bodies shifting slightly with the expansion and contraction of the heated metal, looking less like animals and more like growths on the pipe-work.

Deep within the sluggish canals, the water itself is alive. The primary aquatic denizen is the **Radiator-Eel**. These long, sinuous creatures are the color of tarnished brass, with skin that feels exactly like a rusted pipe to the touch. They have no scales, only a series of overlapping metal-like plates that hiss softly as they slide over one another. They are bottom-feeders, scouring the spongy root-mats for the heavy oils that seep down from the machinery. Their internal biology is a pressurized steam system; they breathe through vents along their spine that release small puffs of white vapor, creating a trailing mist wherever they swim. They are lethargic, moving with the glacial rhythm of the water, pulsing slowly as they absorb the ambient warmth of the refinery.

Clinging to the vertical surfaces of the Boiler-Vines and the brickwork are the **Fuse-Wire Spiders**. These are small, spindly constructors that spin conductive webs. Their silk is not organic protein, but a metallic, copper-thread filament harvested from the stripped insulation of old wiring. They stretch these webs across open spaces, often between a leaking steam valve and a patch of dry moss. The webs do not catch prey; they catch energy. The spiders scuttle along the lines, their feet insulated by thick pads, "drinking" the small electrical currents that bleed from the aging infrastructure. Their bodies glow faintly with a bioluminescent charge, a soft, electric yellow that pulses in time with the hum of the factory.

And then there are the **Hollows**. These are perhaps the most integrated of all the Low Refinery’s inhabitants. They are amphibious, frog-like creatures that have adapted to the abandoned interiors of the machinery. A Hollow does not find a home; it becomes a component. The adult-sized specimens will squeeze themselves into a hollow piston, a cracked cylinder, or an empty gearbox, their bodies soft and malleable like wet clay. Once inside, they swell slightly, their skin bonding with the metal, sealing the breach. They effectively replace the ruined part of the machine, their strong muscle tissue acting as the living piston or valve. They sit for years, perfectly still, their heartbeats synchronized with the thrum of the engine, breathing the exhaust and filtering it through porous throats. They are the silent, embedded ghosts of the refinery, indistinguishable from the hardware they inhabit, alive only to the keen observer who notices the faint, rhythmic blink of a gill-slit inside a rusted valve.

There is no hierarchy here, only circuitry. Every creature acts as a resistor, a capacitor, or a conductor in the great, green circuit of the Low Refinery. They are slow, metallic, and damp. They wait. And in the thick, warm air, they endure the long, turning centuries of the machine.
