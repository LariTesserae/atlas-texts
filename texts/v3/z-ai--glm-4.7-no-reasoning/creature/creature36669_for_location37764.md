The inhabitants of this manufactured dark are not its masters, nor are they its architects. They are its fleas, its mold, its parasites, existing in the interstices of the architecture that neither needs nor welcomes them.

Because there is no sun and no season, there is no cycle of waking or sleeping, only the rhythm of the water. The things that live here have evolved to listen to the groan of the clay. They are the listeners and the grazers.

First, there are the **Shadow-Grazers**. These are the dark, flat shapes that adhere to the walls. To look at them is to look at a patch of darkness that has suddenly decided to peel itself away from the brick. They have no thickness, perhaps no more than the width of a piece of parchment, and they possess no distinct anatomy. There is no head, no limb, no orifice. They are simply mobile planes of organic matter, slick and cold, possessing the texture of a slug’s underbelly but the silence of a falling leaf.

They move by contraction and adhesion. They flow over the rough, fired-clay surfaces like oil over stone, seeking out the damp veins where the phosphorescent minerals glow brightest. They feed on the pale, shelf-like fungal growths that sprout in the perpetual dampness. When a Grazer finds a cluster of these spore-matter shelves, it does not bite or chew; it drapes itself over the growth, sealing itself against the wall, and waits. Over the course of hours, it secretes a solvent that liquefies the fungal matter, which it then absorbs directly through its skin. Once fed, the Grazer peels away, leaving behind a patch of wall that is stark, clean, and disturbingly barren—a scar in the mold.

They are rarely seen because they are nearly invisible, but they can be heard. When they move quickly, dislodged by a sudden shift in the masonry or a cascade of water, they slap wetly against the clay, a sound like a hand slapped against wet mud, echoing through the chutes.

Deeper in the honeycomb, where the corridors narrow so tight that a human could not pass, live the **Echo-Weavers**. These are spindly, spider-like constructions, but they are not made of chitin or flesh. They are composed of the same materials as the city itself—bones of calcified lime, threads spun from the resinous mortar that sweats from the walls. They look like animated debris, a handful of broken brick and dust pulled together by a nervous energy.

They do not graze. They build, and they listen. The Weavers spin webs across the openings of the gutters and ventilation shafts. Their webs are not sticky silk but fine, tense membranes of dried resin and spit, strung like the strings of a thousand instruments. The Weavers are attuned to the hydro-circulation. They sit motionless in the center of their webs, vibrating with the architecture, waiting for the specific frequency that indicates a breach—a crack opening, a seal failing, a trough about to overflow.

When a "distress" vibration travels through the clay, the Weavers swarm. They rush to the site of the leak or crack, not to repair it—that is the work of the water and time—but to harvest. They gather the loose clay, the dissolving mortar, the soft fungal sludge, and they bind it with their own secretions. In this way, they reinforce their own nests. They build their colonies inside the hollows of the walls, lining them with a hard, ceramic-like plaster that they manufacture themselves. They are the builders within the builder, creating a second, microscopic city inside the first.

Finally, there are the **Gulper-Grubs**, solitary and massive. They are rare, immense tubes of translucent, gelatinous flesh that can grow to the length of a man’s arm, though they are usually curled in on themselves like sleeping snakes. They inhabit the larger reservoirs, the deep sumps where the water eventually pools. They are blind, their bodies filled with a dense, heavy fluid that allows them to withstand the immense pressure of the deep basins. Their skin is translucent and cloudy, revealing a sluggish, dark digestive tract winding through their length.

They are filter feeders, the detritivores of the deep. They inhale the mineral-heavy water, straining out the silt, the spores, the dust, and the microscopic organisms that drift down from the upper levels. They are the only things here that possess a discernible mouth—a sphincter-like opening at one end of the tube that pulses rhythmically, sucking in water and expelling it in a steady, filtered jet.

When a Gulper-Grub dies, which happens rarely, its body does not rot. It calcifies. The heavy minerals in its body crystallize, turning the creature into a rigid, stone-like pillar. Other grubs then attach themselves to this calcified corpse, using it as an anchor in the shifting silt. In this way, even their death contributes to the architecture, becoming new rock in the deep.

These are the lives here: the flat grazers that erase the mold, the debris-spiders that string webs across the dark, and the filtering tubes that turn water into stone. They are noise and motion in a place that is otherwise patient, heavy, and still. They live on the sweat of the walls and the breath of the damp, scuttling through a world that is entirely built, entirely alien, and entirely indifferent to their presence.
