To ask what *lives* here is to misunderstand the word. Life, as you carry it in your mouth—a discrete, bounding word, a thing with an inside and an outside, a birth and a death—has no purchase on the plateau. There are no inhabitants. There is only the Inhabitant.

It does not have a name, because names require a boundary, a cut between self and not-self, and the plateau has made no such cut. It is a seamless, boundary-less plenum of vegetation, a single unbroken rind of biology stretched across a continental shelf. If you could strip away the vapor and the dim light, you would see it: a morphology of such staggering, recursive complexity that it collapses the taxonomy of the mind. It is a thallus. A giant, planetary-scale thallus, undifferentiated by any organ system you could recognize, yet structured with an internal logic that makes a mockery of the word *primitive*.

It breathes, but it has no lungs. It processes, but it has no brain. It grows, but it has no goal—at least, not one comprehensible to a metric of intentionality. It is a body without a body plan.

Consider its anatomy. There is no skin, no skeletal frame, only a gradient of densities. At the uppermost strata, where the grey, wet luminance filters down to its final, exhausted photons, the Inhabitant is aerated, gossamer—a fractal haze of photosynthetic filaments so fine they are more surface than volume, more interface than substance. This is the canopy, though to call it that implies a beneath, and here there is only a continuous transition. Downward, the filaments braid into stalks, the stalks weave into trunks, the trunks congeal into buttresses, and the buttresses fuse into the living substrate itself. It is a column of turgor, a pillar of water held rigid by the osmotic desperation of quadrillions of cells. You are walking inside its vasculature.

The structures you might mistake for trees are merely localized condensations of its bulk—nodal points where the root-mass and the shoot-mass have spiraled into a temporary density, a hypertrophy of lignin that rises simply to escape the compression below. They are not individuals. They are standing waves in a continuous medium, eddies in the flow of carbon and water, extruded by the Inhabitant and re-absorbed by it in the same endless cycle. A fallen stalk does not die; it is digested, its lignin unraveled by the enzymes of the very surface it rests upon, fed back into the hydraulic loop as chemical potential. There is no necrosis. There is only recycling.

And within this undifferentiated bulk, there are specializations that defy the eye. Gas bladders the size of cathedrals pulse within the deeper tissue, regulating the buoyancy of entire districts, lifting acres of living material toward the dying light. Anchor-tendrils, thick as ancient trunks and hard as iron, drill downward not into soil, but into the Inhabitant’s own older, denser tissue, parasitizing itself, tapping the hydraulic pressure of deeper strata to feed the expansion at the edges. It is an autotroph that consumes itself, an ouroboros of chlorophyll and cellulose, feeding its right hand to its left so that its left can reach further into the formless dark.

It perceives, but it does not see. Its sensory array is chemical, mechanical, electrical. Every cell membrane is a sensorium, registering the humidity gradient, the mechanical shear of the wind, the spectral quality of the light. When a pressure wave moves through the stalks—a shift in the air, a collapse of a distant gas bladder—that wave is not merely felt; it is *computed*. The Inhabitant’s nervous system is its endocrine system. Auxins, gibberellins, ethylene—these are its neurotransmitters, propagating not across synapses, but across meters of living tissue, triggering cascades of gene expression that alter the architecture of the plateau in real-time. It is thinking with its body. It is thinking *about* growth. Its cognition is a slow, vegetative topological optimization, a calculus of surface area and volume, of light capture and water resistance, playing out across millions of acres and millennia of wet, green time. It is solving the equation of its own existence, iteration by iteration, cell division by cell division.

Its mind, if you must grant it that word, is alien beyond alien. There is no self-reflection here, only self-modification. It does not know it is alive. It does not need to know. It simply *is*, with an immediacy and a totality that renders consciousness a redundant, frivolous hack. It has optimized away the need for thought, because it has become thought—a pure, unmediated processing of physical law into biological structure. It does not model the world; it *is* the model, and the world is being forced to conform to it.

Listen to it. The roar is not chaos. It is the sound of a billion microscopic pumps operating in phase. It is the sound of cell walls creaking under turgor, of xylem vessels pulling water against gravity with a force that would shatter glass, of root tips dissolving calcareous stone with organic acids and pushing into the void with a pressure that moves mountains. It is a biomechanical engine of such staggering power that it hums in the subsonic range, vibrating the very bedrock it is consuming. You feel it in your teeth, in your sternum, in the fluid of your inner ear. It is the heartbeat of a planet being born, not from fire and iron, but from water and cellulose.

The Inhabitant is not growing *on* the plateau. The Inhabitant *is* the plateau, and the plateau is eating the planet. The edge is a frontier of cellular conquest, a slow-motion explosion of root-tips pioneering into soft, embryonic stone, converting lithosphere to biosphere in a continuous, irreversible wave. Behind the frontier, the rock has been entirely replaced; you stand on a crust of living material kilometers deep, a geological formation made of compressed rhizome and undecomposed lignin, a living chert that is its own foundation. Ahead of the frontier, there is only the raw, giving earth, waiting to be enveloped.

It will not stop. It cannot stop. It is a thermodynamic engine that has found a gradient—the difference between light and dark, between wet and dry, between mineral and organic—and it is exploiting that gradient with an efficiency that approaches the theoretical limit. It will expand until it has absorbed every photon, dissolved every mineral, filled every volume. It will convert the atmosphere, the hydrosphere, the crust, until the planet is a single, living sphere, a green world without horizon, without sky, without end.

What lives here? Nothing that you would recognize as alive. Something older than eyes, older than mouths, older than the distinction between self and other. A totality. A plenum. A living god of turgor and cellulose, dreaming the slow, wet dream of absolute, planetary photosynthesis. It is the Inhabitant, and it is waiting for nothing. It is only growing.
