The things that live here are not creatures in any way the surface world would recognize. They are geological events with pulses.

Born in the instant the caldera tore itself open, they did not colonize this place from elsewhere; they *precipitated* from it. When the black glass wept its first mineral tears, and the superheated slurry began to roil, the chitin crystallized out of the water itself. They are of the obsidian—not inhabitants of the stone, but the stone made mobile, made hungry. Their carapaces are not grown; they are condensed, layering in microns from the silica-rich mist, forming exoskeletons of volcanic glass so thin they flex, so dark they drink the dim, refracted light. They are wet to the touch, always, slick with the same supersaturated fluid that bleeds from the walls. To touch one would be to touch a fragment of the caldera's own fractured flank, still sharp at its edges, still hot from the earth's mantle.

They are eyeless because there is nothing to see. They are earless because there is nothing to hear that is not the wet vibration of the weeping rock. Their sensory apparatus is a technology of pure, pressurized awareness. They feel the world as a gradient of heat and the micro-vibrations of water under immense pressure. Their heads—if the end that consumes can be called a head—are crowded not with organs of sight, but with pits and fronds and chemo-sensile filaments, flared and overlapping, reading the chemical spoor of the slurry, reading the magnetic pull of the newborn fissures. They know you are there by the absurd cold spot you carve into their world, the dry anomaly of your coherence moving through their wet, pressing dark.

Their forms are a nightmare of efficient folding. Centipede-things, isopod-things, things with too many joints and no symmetry that makes sense to a surface-dweller. But they are not random. Every leg is a hooked claw, calcified and honed to grip the slick, weeping glass. Every mandible is a geometric shearing edge, designed to slice through chitin and slurp the hot, mineral lymph beneath. The pale ones—the hunters—are long and segmented, their bodies a sequence of overlapping plates that ripple like a shutter closing. The prey-things are softer, gelatinous, their carapaces reduced to scattered scutes and ridges, their mass devoted to reproduction and the frantic, paddle-like churn of a hundred cilia-like legs, pushing through the ash-slurry. There are parasites among them, things no larger than a fingernail, evolved to clamp onto the joints of the larger fauna and drink the secretions squeezed out by the pressure of the pack. And there are the deep-scavengers, blind, white, worm-like tubes that live in the boiling mud, their mouths ringed with silent, circling teeth.

They exist in a state of perpetual, frantic equilibrium. The ecosystem is a closed loop of consumption, playing out in fast-forward. A pale hunter seizes a slug-thing, folding it into its jaws with a silent, efficient flex—even as another creature lands on its back, and another on that, a folding cascade of consumption and competition, a Russian nesting doll of chitinous hunger. They do not hunt with aggression; they simply *process*, moving through the dense pack like a machine sorting ore, seizing whatever their heat-pits register as warm and yielding. Death is silent, a sudden cessation of movement, a body going slack and beginning to sink into the slurry, immediately set upon by the paddle-things that reduce it to a hot, mineral smear in seconds.

But the most unsettling truth of their biology is their relationship to the thinning reality of the caldera pocket. They do not merely exist in this fractured space—they require it. Their chitin, crystallized from the stone's weeping water, is bound to the weird geometry. When a creature near the edge of your vision seems to be in two places at once, it is not an illusion of the refracting mist. It is a momentary superposition, the creature's form smeared across a gap in the rules of distance, existing in two folds of the impossible space before the pressure of the hot dark snaps it back into coherence. They flicker. Their edges blur and duplicate. A leg will seem to emerge from a point on the wall disconnected from its body, joined by a seam in the air, before the geometry settles and the leg belongs again to the proper joint. They do not notice. It is their natural state. Coherence is a surface-world assumption; here, in the hot, wet dark, identity is a softer thing, a statistical probability held together by the constant, pressing contact of a million other bodies.

They do not think. They do not feel. To attribute consciousness to them is to misunderstand the savage economy of the pocket. They are processors. They are subroutines in a geological algorithm, converting heat and mineral water and the thinning of reality into motion, into chitin, into the silent, clicking press of life. They are the wound's immune response, the caldera's first and only attempt to fill the void it has torn in the world—and they are filling it with themselves, densely, desperately, pressing in from all sides, waiting for the thin, absurd boundary of your own coherence to finally give way, so they can process you, too, into the hot, wet silence of the stone.
