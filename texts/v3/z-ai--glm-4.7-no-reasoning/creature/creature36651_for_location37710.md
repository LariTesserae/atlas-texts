They do not think of themselves as the staff of Vash. To do so would require a concept of their own transience, a sense of themselves as temporary, and they are not temporary—they are slow. They are as slow as the stone. They have lived here long enough that their own edges have softened, their own contours have absorbed the damp, and they move through the fog with the same mindless certainty as the water finding a joint. If the town is a scaffold, they are the tension in the ropes—not the architect, not the payload, but the simple, continuous pull that keeps the shape from slackening.

They are not old. Their faces, when you see them, are unlined by worry or by years in the way the lowlanders count years. But they are weathered in a stranger fashion: smoothed, like the corners of their buildings, by the constant abrasion of an environment that has not yet hardened. Their skin has the pale, porous quality of the soft stone, as though it too might weep if pressed. Their hair grows in dark, flat masses, held close to their skulls by the condensation that never quite evaporates. They wear layers of heavy, untreated fabric—woven from the tough, pale stems of the vine that colonizes the eastern row—absorbent and dense, never fully dry, carrying the smell of wet mineral and living green. They are never warm to the touch, but they do not seem cold. They have acclimated to a temperature that hovers just above discomfort, and their bodies have adjusted by simply ceasing to expect heat.

Their hands are remarkable. That is where their relationship to the place becomes visible. Their palms are broad, the calluses not hard and brittle but thick and leathery, stained permanently dark from handling the young stone. Their fingers are thick, almost clubbed at the tips, shaped by a lifetime of gripping soft rock that resists not by hardness but by consistency. They work the way water works—finding the weak point, applying persistent pressure, returning again and again to the same crack until it yields. They can read the stone the way a sailor reads water: by subtle variations in the sound it makes when struck, by the way moisture beads on its surface, by the smell of the minerals still dissolving within it. When they cut a block from the ridge, they are not breaking rock. They are persuading it. They are asking it to be a shape it has not yet decided to be, and they are patient enough to wait for it to agree.

There are fewer than forty of them, and they do not pair or cluster in the ways that people do when they are building a community. They do not have families in the traditional sense—they have lineages of task. One teaches another how to read the seeps, how to find the place where the water table is pressing hardest against the surface, and that knowledge passes not through blood but through proximity, through years of working the same stretch of wall, of harvesting the same patch of well-catch. They sleep alone, in the empty rooms of the half-occupied buildings, on cots made of bent wood and vine-cord. They do not decorate. They do not own things beyond their tools and their layers of damp clothing. Their rooms are cells—functional, bare, waiting for something they do not expect to see.

They do not speak much, and when they do, their voices are low and carry poorly over the constant sound of water. They have developed a language of gesture to supplement it—hand signs that indicate the quality of the stone (ready, yielding, overripe), the state of the well (clear, mineral, disturbed), the approach of weather (though weather here is so constant that the signs for it are largely academic). They can communicate across the wide gaps between buildings without raising their voices, making shapes in the fog with their hands, reading each other's intentions by the posture of their shoulders against the gray.

They do not gather for meals. There is no central hearth, no communal table. They eat alone or in pairs, consuming the things they harvest: the fibrous cores of the ferns, the waxy leaves of the vine stripped of their coating and boiled in stone basins over slow-burning peat that they cut from the marsh-edge. The well-catch leaves, they do not eat. Those they lay at thresholds, or weave into cords, or press into the mortar as it cures, as though the plant's faint glow carries some property that the stone requires. They are not deliberate about this in a way that suggests ritual; they are matter-of-fact, the way one is about adding water to a mix. It is simply what one does with well-catch. The plant is a reagent. They know this without being told.

They do not wonder about what is below. This is the most difficult thing for a visitor to accept: they are not curious about the process in the deep rock, the vast thing becoming itself in the dark. They are aware of it the way one is aware of breathing—not as a subject of contemplation, but as a rhythm that underlies all action. When the sound of the water changes—when a new channel opens below and the susurration shifts in tone—they notice, and they adjust. They might reinforce a wall that has begun to settle differently, or redirect a path that the deforming stone has displaced. But they do not ask what is happening down there. The question feels, to them, like asking why the heart beats. It beats because it does. The stone grows because it does. Their job is to keep the scaffold stable while it does.

This is why they cannot answer when asked when Vash will be finished. They are not waiting for completion. They are not working toward an end. They are *maintaining*—a continuous, present-tense activity that has no conclusion. The town is not a project; it is a practice. They are practicing the shape of the place, holding it in configuration while the substrate makes itself, and they will continue to practice it for as long as the substrate requires, which may be generations, which may be forever. They do not experience this as futility. They experience it as purpose. The emptiness, the overbuilt infrastructure, the buildings that wait for residents who have not come—these are not signs of failure. They are the shape the town must hold in order to do what it does. A bowl is useful because it is empty. Vash is useful because it is not yet full.

There are children in Vash, though not many. Three, at present. They are not born in the way that suggests the residents have paired and reproduced in the normal fashion; they appear, small and damp and silent, at intervals of years, found in the mornings curled at the rim of the well, or asleep in a harvested patch of well-catch, or simply standing in a doorway with no memory of having arrived. The residents accept them without question. They are given a set of tools appropriate to their hands and set to work: clearing paths, carrying stone, learning the language of gesture and the patience of water. The children do not ask where they came from. They do not ask what is below the well. They learn the work, and they become, gradually, indistinguishable from the adults—smooth-skinned, slow-moving, porous at the edges, more feature of the landscape than person.

The visitors who come to Vash—and they do come, rarely, drawn by rumor or accident or the wrong kind of curiosity—always want to know who the residents *are*. What are they? Are they people who found this place and chose to stay? Were they placed here? Did they grow here, like the moss, like the well-catch, adapted to the specific chemistry of the young stone? The residents do not answer this question, and their silence is not evasive but genuinely blank. They do not have an origin story. They do not have a founding myth. They do not have a word for what they are that translates into the languages of the lowlands, where people are separate from their places, where the ground is finished and the buildings are set upon it like objects on a shelf.

Here, the ground is not finished. The buildings are not set upon it; they are *of* it, grown from it, maintained in a state of dynamic equilibrium with it. And the residents are of it too—shaped by the constant damp, by the sound that never ceases, by the slow deformation of the stone they shape and reshaped. They are the human expression of the same process that makes the valley deepen and the well-rim rise and the ferns grow thick and black in the thin light. They are not tending the machine. They are part of the machine. They are the part that can hold a tool and make a decision and walk to the eastern boundary to check the vine, and they do these things not because they have been instructed but because the system, left to itself, produces people who do these things, the way it produces moss, the way it produces the sound of water, the way it produces the faint glow at the center of the well.

They do not have faith. Faith requires doubt, and they do not doubt the process. They do not have hope. Hope requires an imagined future, and they do not imagine; they maintain. They do not have fear, because fear requires the possibility of failure, and the concept of failure does not apply to a geological event. The stone will become what it becomes. The town will hold the shape or it will not. The residents will tend it for as long as there are residents to tend, and when the last one is gone—if that day comes—new ones will appear, small and damp and silent, at the rim of the well.

This is what lives in the Infant Ward of Vash: not a people, but a function. A slow, steady, human-shaped function that keeps the scaffold standing while the vast, quiet, terrifyingly patient thing below makes itself in the dark. They are the hands of the process. They are the eyes at the rim of the well. They are the keepers of the well-catch and the layers of soft stone and the sound that has no source and no end. They are as much a part of the geology as the water and the moss and the rock that still breathes, and they will remain until the rock decides what it is, or until the ridge crumbles, or until the sound stops—which is to say, they will remain. The fog closes around them. The water runs. They work.
