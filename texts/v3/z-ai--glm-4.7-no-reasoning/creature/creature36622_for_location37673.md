They are the Offal-Flies of the Vertical, though they resemble insects only in the frantic, vibrating economy of their movement. They are not organic in the way biology textbooks—those dust-gathering relics of a planet-bound past—would define life. They are chitinous miracles of salvage, small creatures the size of a human hand, evolved within the dark, wet ribs of the megastructure.

Their carapaces are sculpted from the very environment they inhabit: a jagged, geometric tessellation of hyper-carbon and memory-metal. They look like living shrapnel, like shards of a shattered hull that learned to crawl. The edges of their bodies are laser-sharp, refusing to dull, designed to slice through the viscous drag of the humid air. There is nothing soft about them; they are creatures of hard angles and reflexive surfaces, colored the bruising purple of the cooling fluids they drink.

Sight is a sense they discarded long ago. In a place where the light is flat and drowning in fog, eyes are useless vulnerabilities. Instead, they perceive the world through a lattice of vibration-sensitive spines running along their backs. They feel the texture of the silence. They can read the tectonic groan of the expanding walls, distinguishing the safe, rhythmic breathing of the structure from the dangerous, high-pitched whine of a stress-fracture. They taste the iron in the air through pores in their underbellies, tracking the chemical scent of ozone and the electrical signature of the force-fields that hum invisibly around them.

They live in the spaces between the pipes, clustered in the "shanties" of maintenance drones where the heat is most concentrated. Here, amidst the bundles of pulsing, bioluminescent blue fluids, they graze. Their mouthparts are microscopic needles, capable of piercing the insulation of the ancient cables to sip the thermal energy leaking within. They are electrovores, drawing their sustenance not from food, but from the waste heat of the world-sized engine. A single droplet of the gravity-defying fluid provides them with calories for a week; they gorge until their abdomens glow with a faint, radioactive light.

Their movement is dictated by the breath of the vent. When the fans throttle up and the air pressure drops, they huddle flat against the metal, locking their claws into the nanometer-scale imperfections of the grating, becoming indistinguishable from the rust and the grime. But in the lulls, when the micro-storms of hissing vapor settle, they skitter. They run in lightning-fast bursts, blurs of motion across the slick black ice, chasing the shadows cast by the drifting steam.

Reproduction is a solitary, violent affair. When a female—a larger, duller specimen—reaches critical energy saturation, she seeks out a depression in the floor grating where the condensation pools. She lays a cluster of eggs that look like ball bearings, perfectly spherical and heavy. She then abandons them to the freezing water. The eggs rely on the chemical harshness of the environment to harden their shells; the sterile, antiseptic nature of the water is their incubator. Only the strongest embryos can crack the casing, fueled by the faint electrical current running through the conductive metal of the floor.

They are the sole witnesses to the cathedral’s decay. They watch the pale, eyeless crustaceans—simple, stupid things—clean the filters, and the Flies eat the crustaceans when they stray too far from the warmth. They are the apex of this tiny, hidden world, the kings of the interstice. They know the taste of the stratosphere just inches beyond the hull, and they know the safety of the deep, humid dark. They do not know why the structure breathes, or who built it. They only know that when the walls groan and the pressure shifts, it is time to move, to climb higher up the ribcage of the Leviathan, forever ascending the infinite shaft in search of the next warm exhaust port.
