What lives here is not what is usually meant by "life." There are no watchers in the tall grass, no hunters in the shadows, because the grass is mineral and the shadows are permanent. The residents of this hollow are things that have forgotten how to be distinct from the world they inhabit. They are the slow children of equilibrium, beings for whom movement is a geological event and thought is a chemical impulse.

The most evident inhabitants are the architects of the iridescent films. To a passing eye, they are merely a scum on the water—rainbows smeared on black glass—but up close, the skin of the water is a metropolis. These are mats of cyanobacteria and extremophilic algae, bound together in a gelatinous, self-generated slime. They do not bloom or die in seasonal rushes; they exist in a continuous, metabolic hum. They drift across the surface of the shallow pools like slow tectonic plates, merging when they meet, digesting one another if the chemical balance demands it. They are not individuals; they are a diffused intelligence of pigment and exudate, sensing the dim light through the thickness of their own colonies. They breathe the humidity, exchanging gases with the thick air so slowly that a single breath for them might span a human day.

Beneath the skin of the water, in the anoxic darkness where the sediment is soft and the stone yields like wet clay, live the miners. These are microscopic worms, pale as the limestone itself, eyeless and sensing the world through the bristles along their flanks. They are not burrowers in the traditional sense; they are chemical navigators. They follow the faint gradients of acidity, tracing the invisible veins of iron oxide that run through the substrate. They eat the stone—not directly, but by farming the bacteria that dissolve the mineral matrix. As they move, they leave behind trails of finer silt, microscopic furrows that fill with water and harden over decades into new veins of stone. They are the force behind the "room-sized hollow," the relentless, microscopic jaws that have eaten the center out of the earth, shaping the bowl by infinitesimal degrees.

Then there are the cavity-dwellers. The pillars and fins, riddled with holes and pockets, house a creature that has no name in any language of the surface. It is a soft, gelatinous entity, shaped like a flattened pear, translucent enough that you can see the dark pulsing of its gut. It clings to the underside of the stone ledges, submerged in the perpetual dampness. It has no shell; its protection is the chemistry of its flesh, which is so saturated with the same minerals as the water that predators find it indigestible—rock eating rock. It anchors itself with a single, muscular foot, and it filters the water that drips from the condensation above, extracting the faint nutrients carried down from the air. It does not hunt; it waits. When a drop of water finally detaches itself from the lip of the rock and falls, the creature reacts with a slow, rhythmic pulsing, absorbing the impact. It is a creature that lives for the drip, measuring its life in the irregular ticks of condensation.

In the spaces between these living things, the air itself is populated, though not with insects. No wings buzz here; the air is too heavy for flight. Instead, the moisture supports drifting spores—dormant, sleeping things that float like microscopic dust motes in the dim light. They are the seeds of fungi that might have colonized a world more dynamic, but here, they have lost the imperative to germinate. They simply persist, suspended in the thermal plateau, waiting for a change that will not come. They are a ghost population, a potential life frozen in amber-light, neither alive nor dead, just drifting.

And finally, there is the faint, impossible sense that the stone itself is aware. The ridges of clay, the compacted silt, the iron oxides—they do not breathe, but they remember. The artifacts—the hand-cut flint, the post-hole ghost—are not merely preserved; they are being digested. The water moves through the grain of the stone, carrying microscopic organisms that dissolve the sharp edges of the flint, turning the distinct geometry of human effort back into rounded, natural lumps. The stone is reclaiming the intention, erasing the history until the tool is just another odd-shaped pebble. In this sense, the "life" of the place is a process of forgetting. The formations, the pillars, the fins—they are all engaged in a slow, unthinking erasure of difference, grinding down the sharp corners of reality until everything is as smooth and patient as the clay.

What lives here is a single, continuous organism of stone, water, and slime. The blind worm in the dark, the shining mat on the surface, the clinging thing under the ledge—they are merely organs in a body that has no defined shape. They do not compete; they compartmentalize. They maintain the temperature, they filter the light, they digest the mineral. They have achieved the peace that the place embodies: a state where there is no hunger, only metabolism; no fear, only existence. They are the keepers of the hollow, and they are indistinguishable from it.
