To live here is to never know the sound of your own footsteps. Or rather, to never hear them over the low, tectonic groan of water shifting in the air, the slow tear of a vine growing a fraction of an inch thicker, the deep, bellows-like exhalation of a giant shifting its weight from one century to the next. The inhabitants of this place do not walk; they dock. They do not run; they fade.

They are creatures of the green skin and the black glass. 

Consider first the *Lithochorytes*—the stone-drifters. They are the kaiju of the vertical forest, though "kaiju" implies a violence that they entirely lack. They are mountains of flesh that have elected to be alive. A fully grown Lithochoryte spans the height of a young obsidian spire, draped across the jagged edges with a padding of muscle and fat so dense it could cushion a falling boulder. Their hide is not skin; it is a topography. It is thick, leathery, and deeply furrowed, acting as a substrate for an entire secondary ecosystem. Mats of neon-sharp moss grow along their shoulders; symbiotic orchids with translucent, aquamarine veins bloom in the creases of their joints, drawing nutrients from the creature's slow sweat. Plate-like ferns anchor themselves behind their ears, their fronds rotating lazily to catch the ambient moisture. 

The Lithochoryte does not eat in any traditional sense. It possesses a wide, lipless mouth at the end of a thick, tapering neck, which it opens and points into the slow-moving curtains of suspended rain. It drinks the world. It will sit like this, jaws agape, drinking the horizontal tide for weeks. Its eyes, when they open, are milky and blind to detail, sensing only the shifting gradients of the luminous haze. It blinks, and the membrane slides across with the heavy, wet sound of a sail unfurling. It takes an hour. When it moves, it does so by extending a limb the size of a redwood trunk, the muscles bunching with a patience that makes geology look hasty, finding a new grip on the sharp granite, and pulling its massive bulk a few feet further up the cliff-face. It has no predators. It has no prey. It is a Buddha of the wet spires, existing only to be a canvas for the forest.

Beneath them, threading through the labyrinth of biological barriers, are the *Nereids*—the water-weavers. If the Lithochorytes are the mountains, the Nereids are the rivers. They are sinuous, elongated creatures, vaguely serpentine but with far too many limbs—six jointed arms lined with overlapping, plate-like scales that mimic the shingled leaves of the vegetation. They are the size of ancient pythons, but their density is strange; they are slightly buoyant. They do not slither along the stone. They swim through the air.

A Nereid will launch itself from a high overhang, not falling, but catching an updraft. It rides the strange magnetic currents that pull the beach-ball-sized globules of water upward. It wraps its plated arms around a floating sphere of rain, and the water absorbs its momentum, carrying it spiraling up into the canopy. They are the architects of the hanging gardens, inadvertently pruning the massive vines as they use them to swing from one water column to the next. Their scales are constantly slicked with a layer of condensation; they gleam with the internal, diffuse light of the place, looking less like animals and more like animated rivulets. When they sleep, they coil inside the horizontal rivers that flow along the granite ledges, breathing the water through their skin, entirely indistinguishable from the current itself.

And then there are the *Aero-Mycelia*, though calling them creatures stretches the definition of the word. They are the minds of the mist. They are vast, gossamer networks of bioluminescent fungus that drift in the upper atmosphere, tethered to the obsidian peaks by threads so fine they are invisible to the naked eye. They are the ones that drink the ascending water, catching the floating globules in their vast, drifting nets. When a beach-ball of water hits a Mycelium web, it bursts, releasing a slow cascade of spores that drift downward like glowing snow. In the aquamarine twilight, these spores catch the diffuse light, creating brief, swirling nebulae of gold and green around the jagged teeth of the peaks. They do not think, but they react; when the atmospheric tide shifts, they contract, pulling the water vapor closer to the rock, altering the flow of the rain itself. They are the weather-makers, the invisible hands shaping the atmospheric tide.

What is striking about the life here is the silence of their interactions. There is no mating dance, no territorial roar, no frantic scramble for survival. The world is too saturated with life-giving water and light for competition to have evolved. When two Nereids meet at the junction of a hanging water pillar and a vertical vine, they simply flow around one another, their scales brushing with a sound like wet leaves parting. When a Lithochoryte reaches a ledge occupied by an Aero-Mycelium tether, it simply breathes the spores in, adding a faint luminescence to its already glowing orchid coat for a few decades, before exhaling them back into the mist.

They are solitary monarchs of a still life. To be a creature here is to accept that gravity is a negotiation, that the ground is a suggestion, and that water is the only true constant. They are not shaped by the struggle to survive, but by the sheer, dense, suffocating abundance of it all. They are the inhabitants of the pause, breathing in time with the slow rotation of the water columns, living in the warm, green eternity where the only mark of time is the slow, grinding erosion of rock that refuses to erode.
