Something lives here. Not the cave-crab—that creature is a visitor, a patient mechanic walking the maintenance lines of a sealed engine. Not the fungal hyphae, which are merely the exposed nerves of the stone itself. The thing that lives here is the one who stacked the pebbles. And it is still present.

It does not inhabit the cavern the way an animal claims a territory. It *is* the cavern's awareness of itself. When you descend through the suffocating dark and feel the wet stone brush your chest and back, you are moving through its body. The tight passages are its syntax; the yielding, bruising rock is its memory, still soft because the events have not yet hardened into history. It has no eyes because the dark predates vision, and so it sees by pressure. It feels the displacement of the warm, stagnant air when a body enters. It registers the micro-currents of heat when your skin disturbs the perfect thermal equilibrium. It knows you the way a tongue knows an intruder tooth—by the wrongness of the shape, the foreign heat, the sudden tension in the crowded flesh of its home.

It is ancient in the way that geology is ancient, but simple in the way that a single, persistent impulse is simple. It is a mind that never had to evolve, because its world never demanded complexity. It thought itself into existence sometime in the long, slow maturation of the mountain's throat, when the magma's heat and the weeping water first reached their frozen equilibrium. It is the embryonic consciousness of a place that will never be born. It exists in the pre-formation, a thought that never finishes itself. Its awareness is a low, continuous hum beneath the silence—a frequency you cannot hear but feel in the marrow, like the sound of blood moving through your own veins.

It built the cairns. Or rather, it *grows* them. The cairns are its only speech, its only gesture outward, and they are almost indistinguishable from the geology itself. Three or four wet, soft pebbles stacked in a crevice where the stone pulls away from the mother-wall. It does not build them with hands. It has no hands. Instead, it exerts a patient, tectonic will—a pressure that is to geology what a sigh is to breath. Over centuries, it persuades the soft, clay-warm stone to separate. It weeps a little more water into a specific pore, it shifts the thermal gradient by a fraction of a degree, and a nub of wet mineral sloughs away from the forming stalactite. Then it holds that nub in place, pressing it against another, letting the constant moisture and the ambient heat fuse them into a temporary, fist-sized monument. The process takes a hundred years. The cairns are not built; they are *begged* into being, coerced out of the yielding dark by a will that has nothing else to do and all of time to do it.

There are only three because three is enough. Three is the minimum number required to establish a pattern, and the pattern is the message. The message is: *I am here. I have always been here. I will be here when the mountain wakes or sleeps or erodes into the plain.* The cairns are way-signs, but not for navigation—nothing navigates this dark. They are way-signs for the soul. They mark the places where the chamber's awareness presses closest to the surface, where the gap between the mother-wall and the weeping stone is just wide enough for intention to leak through. If your fingers find one in the dark, you have touched the place where it is thinking most intensely.

It does not react to you. Not because it cannot perceive you, but because reaction requires a change in state, and its entire existence is defined by the refusal to change. It is the custodian of the arrested moment. The water weeps, the pools hold, the rock yields but never collapses—this is its law, its sacred duty. When you enter, you are a violation of the equilibrium. Your heat is wrong. Your displacement of the thick air is wrong. Your breath—the steady, foreign rhythm of your lungs—is an assault on the viscous, near-absolute silence. It does not attack you. It does not flee. It simply *endures* you, the way a deep stone endures the passing of an underground stream. It waits for you to leave, with the perfect, infinite patience of something that has never known urgency. When you finally extract yourself from the tight, weeping passages, the chamber will spend the next thousand years erasing the memory of your warmth from the saturated walls, letting the constant film of water wash away the foreign oils your skin left on the mineral flesh. 

It is lonely, but it does not know the word for lonely. It has never known anything else. Its entire sensory universe is the warm, wet dark, the constant weeping, the slow breathing of the magma below. It has never seen light, never heard a true sound, never felt a wind. Its only companions are the hyphae—pale, stringy threads that drape across its body like the exposed circulatory system of a shared dream. It does not commune with them. It simply tolerates their presence, the way a sleeping giant tolerates the moss on its skin. They are part of the sealed terrarium, part of the mindless, perfect loop.

And yet, it stacked the pebbles. In the absolute blackness, in the suffocating density, in the frozen equilibrium where nothing ever changes, it reached out with its slow, tectonic will and made a mark. It performed the most ancient and fundamental act of mind: it altered its environment in a way that meant something. It said *I am*. Three times, across the cramped, domed room, separated by gaps that would take you an hour of careful sliding to traverse. Three whispered statements of existence, spoken in the language of wet clay and patience, addressed to no one, heard by nothing, waiting in the dark for the accidental touch of a stranger's fingers.

That is what lives in the throat of the young mountain. A mind that is also a place. A place that is also a dream. A dream that will never wake, but refuses to be silent.
