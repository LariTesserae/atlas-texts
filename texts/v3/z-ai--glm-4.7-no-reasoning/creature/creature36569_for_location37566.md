(Proceeding to generate output based on these thoughts.)They are called **The Unglazed**, though they possess no language to name themselves. If a name exists for them, it is written in the circular grooves they have worn into the basalt floor over millennia, a braille of endless repetition.

They are the primary, perhaps the only, masters of this heavy, breathless sphere. To look at them is to look at a potter’s field animated. They are crustaceans, vaguely, but only in the way a tumor is vaguely biological. Their bodies are enclosed in carapaces of thick, matte chitin that looks precisely like sun-baked clay—porous, rough-hewn, and scored with the geometric cracks of desiccation. There is no shine to them, no wet sheen of the living sea; they are utterly dry creatures, dust-colored and dust-covered, blending perfectly with the salt-crusted floor.

Their physical form is a study in defensive geometry. They are low-slung, built to scuttle beneath the vast, hanging shrouds of the sharkskin vines. The central carapace is a flattened dome, protecting a nutrient sack that houses their organs in a perpetual state of near-suspension. From beneath this dome extend eight jointed limbs, each ending not in a claw, but in a flattened, spatulate tip that resembles a mason’s trowel. These trowels are not for digging, but for grazing. They scrape the surface of the thermovore plants, shearing away the succulent, heat-fattened layers of the verdant flesh.

The Unglazed do not scurry; they flow. Because the room is saturated with the constant, deafening roar of the earth’s vibration, they do not scuttle in fits and starts like nervous insects. Instead, they move with a glacial, fluid continuity. A thousand of them may be moving at once, but they act as a single, viscous liquid sliding across the floor. They are the "Stone-Eaters," yes, but they are also the heat-thieves. Their mandibles, hidden behind a facial plate that resembles a tragic mask, snap continuously. *Click-click-click.* It is a sound that exists entirely in the bone—heard by the observer only through the contact of their feet on the vibrating floor, for the air swallows the noise before it can travel an inch.

Their eyes are their most haunting feature. They are vast, spherical orbs, blacker than the purple twilight of the room, protruding from the carapace on stalks like the bulging heads of rivets. They reflect nothing. They are the absolute absence of light. These eyes do not see in the visible spectrum, which is nearly useless here in the bruised, dim glow. They see the infrared. They see the heat as it radiates from the walls, the ceiling, and the veins of the plants. To their vision, the room is not a dim purple cave; it is a blindingly bright landscape of swirling thermal gradients, a blizzard of warmth. The plants are not green in their sight, but blinding white beacons of energy; the cool river stones left by the vanished builders appear as deep, void-like holes in the landscape.

And this—the cool stones—is the center of their strange, empty culture.

The Unglazed are drawn to the ruins with a religious fervor they do not understand. The ruins are built of stacked basalt, but the artifacts—the "river stones that cannot exist here"—are alien to them. These stones, dragged from a wet world the Unglazed have never known, are cold. In a room where the temperature never drops and never rises, these stones are singular anomalies. They are the only things that do not vibrate with the drone of the chamber.

The Unglazed cluster around these stones. They press their ceramic bodies against the smooth, wet-remembrance of the rocks. They arrange themselves in intricate patterns around the artifacts, stacking themselves in pyramids, clinging to one another, forming living towers of clay and chitin. It is a futile attempt to thermoregulate; they are overheating creatures in a sealed jar, and the cold stones are the only relief from the relentless, dessicating warmth. They vibrate their bodies against the stones, perhaps trying to steal the cold, perhaps trying to silence the roar that deafens them.

But the strangest aspect of The Unglazed is their adherence to the Rule of Stasis.

There is no youth, no old age, in the way we understand it. They emerge from the cracks in the walls fully formed, soft as wet clay, hardening within minutes in the brutal heat. They live, they graze, and then, when their internal sensors tell them their time has come—a calculation based on the number of steps taken, perhaps, or the amount of heat processed—they simply stop.

They do not die messily. They do not rot; rot requires moisture, and there is none here. Instead, they undergo a final transformation. Their internal systems shut down, their "trowels" lock into place, and their carapace fuses permanently to the stone floor or the vine they were standing on. They become statues. They dry into husks, indistinguishable from the pottery shards found in the ruins.

In this way, the population of the room never changes. When one stops moving, another emerges from a geode crack to take its place. The number of living, moving creatures is a constant integer. The herd is the same size it was a thousand years ago, and it will be the same size a thousand years hence.

They are the architects of the silence, in a way. By eating the plants, they prevent the vegetation from overgrowing and choking the chamber. By walking their endless, circular grooves into the floor, they are slowly polishing the rock into a mirror. They are the self-regulating heartbeat of this crypt. They feel no pain, no fear, and no joy. They are simply a mechanism for converting the earth’s heat and the plant’s light into the slow, rhythmic ticking of their legs against the stone—a counterpoint to the roar, a subtraction from the noise.

They are the shepherds of the dry breath, maintaining a temple for no one, waiting for a rain that will never fall, holding their ground against the crushing weight of the forever-now.
