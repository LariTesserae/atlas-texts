Here, life is not something that merely survives; it is something that accumulates. The inhabitants of the Bottomlands are defined less by what they eat and more by how much they can endure. They are creatures of the crevice, the seam, and the squeeze.

The first impression is the chitinous tide. The floor is not earth; it is a shifting pavement of biology. The primary biomass here is the beetle—specifically, the *Obsidian Rollers*. These are not skittering, nervous creatures. They are the size of dinner plates, their shells domed and glossy, absorbing the faint, sickly phosphorescence of the moss to glimmer dully in the gloom. They move in a ceaseless, low-geared grind, pushing massive spheres of compacted detritus. They are the sanitation engineers of the deep, their mandibles clicking with the sound of scissors cutting through wet cardboard. When you step, you do not step on dirt; you step on a layer of shells that crunch and then give way, pressing into the soft, hot mud beneath.

Weaving between the beetles are the rivers of legs. The *Linear Millipedes* stretch out to lengths of ten or fifteen feet, their bodies composed of hundreds of segments, each armored in slate-grey plating. They do not scuttle; they flow. Their movement is hydraulic and relentless, a conveyor belt of hooked legs that hook into the rot-sponge of the ground. They are silent executioners of the smaller fungal blooms, stripping the slime-coated bark of the massive cycads in wide, diagonal swaths. Where they pass, the plant life is shaved clean, leaving behind smooth, pale wood that looks like polished bone in the dark.

Above them, in the vertical space, the *Stilt-Walkers* dominate. These are spindly, heron-like figures that have evolved to navigate the tangled ceiling. Their legs are elongated, shock-absorbing stalks that allow them to stride over the carpet of beetles without ever touching the ground. Their feathers are not airy; they are modified into leathery, overlapping scales that shed the constant moisture. They move with the ghostly *swish-hush* described by the environment, their long, needle-like beaks probing the air for the flutter of moth-wings. They are statuesque and alone, each stilt-walker maintaining a territory of only a few square yards, perched precariously on the vines that strangle the iron pipes.

The true giants, however, are the *Heap-Beasts*. They are the massive, docile herbivores that haunt the deeper sinkholes. Imagine a hippopotamus, but flattened and spread out, low-slung to the ground to distribute their immense weight through the rot-sponge. They possess no distinct shape; they are ambulatory hills of flesh, sagging and wrinkled, covered in a thick, velvet-like moss that acts as camouflage. Their eyes are tiny, protected by heavy ridges of fat and gristle, useless in the dark. Instead, they navigate via vibration and smell. When a Heap-Beast exhales, the hot steam from its nostrils creates a temporary pocket of clarity in the humid air, smelling of sweet, fermenting peat. They move with the tectonic slowness of the geology around them, pushing through the "wall" of ferns and cycads, simply too heavy to be stopped by thorns or vines. The ecosystem tolerates them; the insects part like the Red Sea before their bulk, and the vines creak and groan but hold, burdening the weight of these living islands.

But it is the interaction with the dead technology that produces the strangest adaptations. The *Pipe-Lampreys* are a species of amphibious, worm-like creatures that have colonized the massive, rusted iron pipes. They are the color of the rust—oxide orange and dull brown—and possess suckers lined with diamond-hard teeth used to rasp away at the metal itself. They do not eat the metal; they eat the specific bacteria that thrive on the rust, a microscopic farm cultivated by the slow, oily leak of the machine. They cluster around the junction boxes where the dying electric cables hum, basking in the faint, imperceptible warmth of the resistance. They are blind, their faces dominated by the circular mouth, pulsing slowly as they cling to the vertical surfaces of the industrial skeletons, mimicking the bolts and rivets until they decide to move.

In the spaces between the massive pipes and the living wall, the *Silt-Spinners* make their homes. These are spiders, but not the delicate kind found on the surface. Their bodies are the size of a human fist, bloated with water. They spin webs not of silk, but of a viscous, glue-like mucus harvested from the condensation on the pipes. These webs hang like heavy, translucent curtains, dripping with collected moisture. They do not flutter; they sag. When prey flies into them—a rare, bioluminescent moth or a lost cricket—the web does not bounce; it simply envelops the victim in a heavy, suffocating wetness. The spider approaches slowly, calmly, injecting a dissolving fluid while the humidity of the air aids in the digestion.

Even the silence is a living thing here. It is enforced by the *Silencers*, sleek, jaguar-sized felines with fur so dark it seems to be made of negative space. Their pads are oversized, covered in a soft, spongy tissue that muffles their impact on the sponge-ground. They do not roar; they lack the vocal cords for it. They communicate through a series of low-frequency vibrations that travel through the ground, rumbles felt in the soles of the feet rather than heard by the ears. They are the lightning in the slow world, the only things capable of sudden violence. When they strike, it is a blur of motion, a sudden cessation of the rhythmic *swish-hush*, followed by the wet sound of consumption, and then the return of the endless, humid hum.

This is the population of the Bottomlands: a crushing, suffocating tapestry of scavengers, grazers, and rust-eaters. They are pressed together, shell against shell, breath against breath, moving in a slow, dark dance that has been going on for millennia, uncaring of the iron bones that jut from their world, indifferent to the lack of sun. They are the children of the heat and the rot, thriving in the damp, dark crush of the infinite cellar.
