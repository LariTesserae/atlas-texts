To live here is to be a mechanism of maintenance, though none of the inhabitants would call it that. They are the keepers of the hum, the shepherds of the slow-spinning copper, and they are shaped as much by the 0.8 twilight as they are by the iron they inhabit.

There is a hierarchy of dampness here.

At the bottom, perhaps four feet below the iron grating where the visitors walk, are the Waders. They are not human, or if they ever were, they have been unwritten by the dripping dark. They are spindly, pale things, standing waist-deep in the sluggish black mirror of the sump. Their skin is translucent, showing the dark, slow pump of blood beneath, matching the viscosity of the water they tend. They wear no clothing, only a thick, coating of grease and river-slime that protects them from the oily water. Their eyes are large and lidless, adapted to catch the faint, ghostly bioluminescence of the moss.

They do not speak; the air is too thick for idle chatter, and the sound of a voice would be a sacrilege against the *plink*. Instead, they communicate through a complex system of taps and vibrations, rapping their knuckles against the iron support pillars of the grating above. They are the custodians of the filtration. Using long, rake-like fingers made of bone or scavenged metal, they comb the sludge, clearing the intake valves of the great engines that pulse beneath the floor. They move with a glacial grace, disturbing the water as little as possible, conscious of the frogs that watch from the rails above.

Above them, on the lattice of iron grating, walk the Stewards. These are taller, heavier beings, wrapped in heavy, oil-cured leathers that have absorbed the smell of iron and old stone over generations. They are hunched creatures, their posture molded by the low ceilings and the need to constantly check the gauges that dot the walls like the eyes of insects. They carry tools that are worn smooth by millennia of use—wrenches with handles polished to black glass, hammers whose heads are rounded nubs from repetitive, gentle tapping.

The Stewards are the interface between the living and the machine. They do not operate the engines with haste; nothing here is hurried. They place a hand on the warm flank of a converter to feel its heartbeat, a vibration that travels up their arm and resonates in their chest. They wipe the condensation from the glass of the pressure gauges with soft cloths, reading the needles not for data, but for the *mood* of the room. They are monks of thermodynamics, worshipping the gradient, the transfer of heat. Their faces are obscured by heavy ventilation masks, not to filter out smoke—the air is clean, albeit heavy—but to warm the air they breathe. To breathe the cool, raw damp of the cavern too deeply is to risk a chill that never leaves the bones.

Then there are the Gardeners. They are smaller, perhaps the size of children, though they age with the slowness of the flywheels. They are the ones who tend the pale, translucent ferns and the ghost-moss. They move along the catwalks with the agility of spiders, clipping a leaf here, encouraging a root to grow around a rivet there. They cultivate the vegetation not for beauty, but for silence. The moss absorbs the high-frequency hisses of steam; the ferns catch the falling droplets before they can hit the metal with too much noise. The Gardeners are covered in pockets, filled with spores and samples, and they move with a profound, tiptoeing reverence.

Finally, there are the Observers. They sit cross-legged on the broad, flat housings of the massive generators, warmest places in the city. They are covered in the frogs.

The relationship between the Observers and the frogs is symbiotic in a way that defies biology. The Observers are perfectly still, their skin rough and warty from the humid air, resembling the stones they sit upon. The frogs—massive, dinner-plate-sized creatures with eyes glowing like weak embers—climb over them. The frogs are not pets; they are parasites that have evolved into partners. They eat the insects that are drawn to the Stewards' lamps and the Gardeners' moss, keeping the city clean. In return, the Observers provide a mobile landscape of warmth.

The frogs possess an intelligence. When a pressure valve begins to leak, emitting a sound too high for the human ear, the frogs sense it. They shift. The vibration of their webbed feet on the metal signals the change. The Observer, feeling the shift of the weight on their lap, will stand up slowly, the frogs sliding off with wet slaps, and go to alert the Stewards.

No one eats here. The hunger is for stillness. They subsist on nutrient pastes derived from the distilled water of the sump, flavorless and grey, eaten in silence while watching the copper flywheels turn their imperceptible inch.

This is a society of the pause. They value the interval between the drip and the plink. They define themselves by their ability to exist within the humid, iron-tasting lung of the world without disrupting it. They are the antibodies of the machine, the wet, silent keepers of the hamlet, waiting for the planet to remember itself, content in the meantime to watch the wheels turn in the dark.
