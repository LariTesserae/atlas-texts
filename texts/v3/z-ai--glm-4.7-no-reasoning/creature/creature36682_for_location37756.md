They do not walk upon the world so much as they inhabit its skin.

The people of the Canopy Works are a species of verticalist. Their ancestors were bridge-walkers and steel-riggers, or perhaps the management caste who once directed the flow of resources from high towers, but that distinction has dissolved in the humidity. Now, they are defined by their relationship to the fall. To live here is to trust the rivet, the weld, the cable. It is to know, in the sinews of the calf and the grip of the hand, the difference between a grating that will hold and a mesh that will yield.

Physically, they are shaped by the climb. The "lowland thinker" would find them alarming—their calves are overdeveloped, corded with the constant negotiation of stairs, their sense of balance preternatural. They possess a quiet, grounded heaviness; they do not scurry. To scurry is to risk the slip. They move with a deliberation that the monkeys mock but the trees respect. Their skin is toughened by a lifetime of abrasion against steel that is always either too hot or too slick, textured by the oils of rust and the resins of crushed leaves. They smell of salt air, wet iron, and the sharp, green scent of crushed fig leaves.

They are not numerous. The Works could hold thousands, perhaps tens of thousands, in its hollow bones, but the current population is scattered—a diaspora within a single structure. They cluster in the "residential tiers," roughly thirty to fifty decks up, but even there, density is low. You might walk for ten minutes along a catwalk, past open doorways leading into apartments carved out of old shipping offices or electrical substations, seeing only the occasional flash of movement—a curtain drawn back, a silhouette against a lamp—before encountering another soul.

Their society is an oral economy of maintenance. They have no currency, only credit measured in labor and knowledge. The highest status is not assigned to those who own the most space, but to those who know the history of the failure points. There is a woman on Deck 44 who is listened to with reverence because she knows the lineage of every leak in the primary pressure manifold; a man on Deck 38 who is sought after because he can interpret the mood of the engine-pulse, predicting a thermal shift hours before the gauges twitch. They are engineers by default, gardeners by survival mandate. Their children learn to read before they learn to read; they learn the color codes of the pipes—red for pressure, blue for return, yellow for waste—before they learn their alphabet.

Their dwellings are palimpsests. A living room might be a former cargo hoist bay, the machinery long since stripped away, replaced by a floor loom and a table made from a ships’ wheel. Walls are rarely solid; they prefer fabric partitions—canvas, burlap, woven reeds—that allow the air to circulate. Privacy is a luxury of the lowlands; here, you live in the sound of your neighbors. You hear the child crying three decks down through the ventilation grate; you hear the rhythmic thud of a baker kneading dough on the level above. This aural transparency has bred a culture of remarkable discretion. They learn not to listen, or at least, not to react to what they hear.

They dress in textiles that breathe. Cotton, linen, and the loose-woven meshes they manufacture from local fibers. Colors tend toward the functional—russets, ochres, and dark greens that hide the grime of the work, though the young will sometimes adorn themselves with the bright plumage of fallen parrots or the iridescent wings of beetles, woven into their hair or sewn onto their sleeves. Footwear is the most critical article of dress. Boots are handmade, resoled dozens of times, with soles of rubber scavenged from old seals and hoses. To lose a boot is a crisis; to walk barefoot on the grating is to risk a slice that the humid air will turn septic within hours.

Diet is a function of the microclimate. They are omnivores of opportunity. The upper tiers provide fruit—figs, papayas, citrus that grows in the crates of the old solariums. The mid-levels support root vegetables and hardy greens grown in the deep soil of the solid decks. Protein is mobile: they trap the rats (which are hunted with a pragmatic cruelty), they fish the deep pools for the blind, pale fish that live in the filtered darkness, and they keep chickens in coops that hang suspended beneath the catwalks, keeping the birds safe from the climbing predators. Their cuisine is dominated by stews and curries, one-pot meals that maximize fuel efficiency, flavored with the hot peppers and ginger that thrive in the railing boxes.

They are haunted by the Below. The twenty decks beneath their living territory are taboo, spoken of only in whispers. The stories say that the machinery there is still running, but that it has changed—that the heat has cooked the oil into something that smokes, and that things live in the dark that are neither rat nor monkey. They fear the silence far more than the noise. The Works produces a constant thrum, a low-frequency heartbeat; when a sector goes quiet, it is a sign that the system is dying, and that the water, or the light, or the structural integrity, will soon follow.

And yet, they are content. This is the hardest thing for an outsider to grasp. They do not see themselves as trapped in a rusting cage. They see themselves as the immune system of a living thing. There is a profound intimacy in their relationship with the structure. They know the texture of every handrail they touch daily. They know the way the light hits a specific patch of deck at 4:00 PM, turning the rust into gold. They have named the currents of air—The Breath of the Furnace, The Sigh of the Vane.

They are a people who have stopped waiting for rescue, having long ago realized that rescue is a concept for people who are lost, and they are not lost. They are exactly where they are supposed to be. They are the keepers of the rivets, the tenders of the vines, the listeners to the pulse. When they die, their bodies are lowered not into the dark below, but up—up to the highest open platforms, placed in woven baskets, to be taken by the birds and returned to the sky that feeds the Works. It is their final act of integration: to become part of the cycle, to let their substance feed the figs that will feed their grandchildren.

They are the Canopy’sKeepers. They are the corrosion, and the polish. They are the reason the mountain stands.
