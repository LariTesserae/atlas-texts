Here, the currency of existence is not water, but dust and resin. To live in this suspended ribcage is to be a creature of architecture and slow, deliberate friction.

The primary citizens of this space are the **Weavers**. They are not the builders of the room—that labor belonged to the vanished giants—but they are its inheritors and maintainers. They resemble wood, but they are flesh. Imagine an insect, perhaps the size of a small dog, whose carapace is not chitin but polished, cured hardwood—mahogany and ash articulated by flexible joints of pale, dry sinew. They move with the terrifying, stop-motion grace of a praying mantis, but slowed by a factor of ten. You watch one for an hour, clinging to the vertical reeds of the wall, and see it lift one leg once. Their legs end not in claws, but in chisel-like points, and they are eternally carving. They shave microscopic curls from the wicker walls, shaping the chaos into geometric perfection. They do not eat the wood; they sculpt it. They hum—a low, resonant thrum that vibrates through their wooden bellies and harmonizes with the structure itself. They are the caretakers of the geometry, ensuring the walls continue to weave in directions that make sense, fighting the entropy of the fraying corners.

Beneath the Weavers, occupying the dense, choking thickets of floor-vegetation, are the **Foragers**. These are the creatures that give the air its drifting, golden haze. They are soft, shapeless things, vaguely resembling slugs or caterpillars, but covered in a coat of dense, shaggy fur the color of dried amber. They have no obvious legs; they move by rippling their undersides, leaving behind trails of shimmering, static-rich oil. They are the eaters of the dead. When a leaf falls, when a timber snaps, when a Weaver finally succumbs to time and falls, the Foragers dissolve it. They secrete a solvent that breaks down organic matter into a fine, nutritious dust, which they then exhale into the air. It is this dust that feeds the towering cork-screw trees and the needle-mosses. They are silent, blind workers, throbbing softly as they process the history of the room, turning the past into the atmosphere.

Among the hanging vines and the vaulted ceiling dwell the **Drifters**. These are the "bees" of this ecosystem, though they are not insects as we know them. They are leathery sacs, perhaps the size of a human head, filled with a lighter-than-air gas. Their wings are crumpled, parchment-like appendages that beat in a lazy, rhythmic pulse. They do not fly; they swim through the thick, velvet air with a sluggish buoyancy. Their bodies are striped in warning colors—ochre and charcoal—but they possess no stingers. Instead, they are pollinators of time. They land on the slow-blooming flowers, their fuzzy bodies collecting the heavy, sticky pollen, and transfer it to the next node. As they move, they emit a faint, melodic chime, like two crystals tapping together, produced by plates rubbing together on their undersides. It is the only sharp sound in the room, a delicate bell-note marking the passage of a season.

Clinging to the high arches, where the light is filtered and dim, are the **Gaze-Lizards**. They are the observers. Their scales are indeed like polished ceramic—cracked glazes of matte white and deep celadon. They are ambush predators of a sort, though they hunt nothing physical. They feed on attention. When an intruder stands in the room, the Gaze-Lizards rotate their eyes to lock onto them. They do not blink. They absorb the bioelectric buzz of a watching mind. As you stare at them, you feel a draining sensation, a subtle lightheadedness. They grow brighter the longer they are watched, their ceramic shells glowing faintly with absorbed energy. In the absence of observers, they enter a torpor, turning dull and gray, merging with the clay-daub until life returns to the room.

Finally, there are the **Idols**. Are they alive? The description says they breathe, and this is the truth of their existence. They stand shoulder-to-shoulder with the breathing thickets, indistinguishable from the artifices of the builders. They are humanoid, tall and thin, carved from the same dark, fire-hardened timber as the pillars. They have no mouths, only wide, unblinking eyes carved into the wood. But if you press your ear against their chests, you hear a slow, rhythmic clicking—the expansion and contraction of their internal fibers. They breathe once every few hours. They do not eat. They do not move. They simply stand, anchored by roots that dig deep into the suspended platform, acting as the nervous system of the room. They feel the vibrations of the Weavers’ carving, the hum of the Drifters, and the footsteps of the intruder. They are the room’s immune system. If the balance shifts, if the dampness were to somehow rise, it is the Idols who would begin to scream—a sound of grinding wood that would shatter the amber silence—and summon the other inhabitants to war.

This is the menagerie of the jar: sculptors of wood, dissolvers of matter, swimmers of dust, eaters of sight, and trees that think. They are a closed loop of consumption and creation, thriving in the dark, dry warmth of a lung that isn't theirs, trapped in the amber of a moment that never ends.
