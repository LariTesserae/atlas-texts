To live here is to be a component of the circuit, a gear in the ghost machine. The inhabitants of this Hamlet of Iron are not distinct from the architecture so much as they are its mobile antibodies, the feverish white blood cells of a metal leviathan that calcified into a landscape centuries ago.

The primary citizens, the ones that define the very horizon of the place, are the **Mantles**. To call them flying creatures would be an oversimplification; they are aerial filter-feeders, vast and triangular, with wingspans that rival the very cooling fans they often roost upon. Their bodies are mosaics of scavenged plating—shielding from old reactors, grating from ventilation shafts—welded together by the slow, patient accumulation of mineral deposits in the water. Their undersides are exposed machinery, a complex diffuser of lungs that act as electrostatic precipitators. They do not flap frantically; they ride the thermals rising from the combustion nests, gliding on the deafening updrafts. When a Mantle dies, it does not rot. It simply loses its charge, its internal magnetic fields collapsing, causing the creature to plummet and shatter against the floor, becoming instant sustenance for the ground-dwellers. Until that moment, they are majestic, stained-glass horrors, their silicate membranes glowing with the faint, sickly luminescence of trapped radioactivity.

Beneath their shadows, ruling the vertical struts and the riveted catwalks, are the **Siphons**. These are the primates of this industrial nightmare—long-limbed, spindly creatures that resemble a chaotic bundle of copper pipes and hydraulic pistons. They possess heads that are little more than armored sensory clusters, dominated by parabolic dishes that swivel endlessly to捕捉 the acoustic landscape. They "hear" food. They cling to the vibrating girders with magnetic feet, tapping on the metal with needle-like probes to locate stress fractures or pockets of trapped gas. Their movement is jerky, impossibly fast, like a film skipping frames. They do not eat solid matter; they insert their proboscises into grease-fittings and valve-stems, sucking out the ancient, black lubricants that keep the city from seizing up. In the dim light, their eyes—which are actually photoreceptive sensors—glow with a harsh, actinic blue, scanning for the voltage leaks that serve as their lifeblood.

The true masses, however, the proletariat of the iron slurry, are the **Harvesters**. If the Mantles are the clouds and the Siphons are the birds, the Harvesters are the grass. They are low, flattened organisms resembling trilobites constructed from ball-bearings and heavy chain mail. They are the size of dinner plates, moving in dense, undulating carpets that flow over the granite flagstones like mercury. They possess no discernible face, only a series of grinding mouths on their undersides. They are the silent custodians of the floor, consuming the microscopic metal shavings, the rust flakes, and the carbon dust that settles from the air. They are terrified of the open channels; they hug the shadows where the machinery meets the ground, forever processing the detritus of the world, turning waste into the oily paste that feeds the larger predators. Their shells are polished to a mirror sheen by centuries of friction against the stone, making them difficult to see until you step on them, at which point they scatter with a sound like falling coins.

Deeper still, inside the hollowed-out carcasses of the repurposed condensers, live the **Thermalites**. These are sessile, tube-like growths that cluster around the exhaust vents like barnacles on a ship’s hull. They are the only "plant" life in this zero-green world, though they are purely animalistic in nature. They are soft, fleshy tubes inside a hard, ceramic shell, pulsating rhythmically in time with the *thump-hiss-thump* of the city's heart. They cannot move; they anchor themselves to the hottest surfaces, rooting into the metal with corrosive acids. They feed exclusively on heat, their bodies filled with a gas that expands and contracts to drive a simple circulatory system. When the machinery cools—a rare event—the Thermalites go dormant and shrink, their shells clamping shut. But when the great furnaces roar, they bloom, extending fleshy, heat-gathering fronds into the hot air, glowing a dull, angry red.

At the top of the food chain, looming in the shadows where the ceiling girders form a canyon of steel, are the **Architects**. They are rarely seen and never heard, for their bodies are acoustically dampened. They are massive, spider-like entities composed largely of the heavy iron beams that form the city itself. They do not scavenge; they build. They move with agonizing slowness, lifting riveted girders that have shaken loose, welding them back into place with plasma generated in their mandibles. They are the keepers of the equilibrium. They hunt the Siphons not for food, but to recycle the copper and rubber in their bodies to patch leaks in the high-voltage lines. They are the surgeons of the iron world, terrifyingly strong, possessed of an intelligence that is cold and mathematical. They treat the city not as a habitat, but as a body, and all other creatures as bacteria to be managed or consumed.

And in the spaces between them all, invisible to the eye but felt in the teeth, lives the **current itself**. It is the ghost in the machine, the electromagnetic aura that permeates the air. Small, nautilus-like creatures called **Sparks**—fragile, glass-shelled entities no larger than a human fist—drift through the air, carried purely by magnetic fields. They have no muscles, only internal ferrofluids that align with the invisible lines of force surrounding the power conduits. They are short-lived, shattering if they touch the ground, but while they drift, they map the invisible nervous system of the city, darting from arc to arc like fireflies in a thunderstorm.

This is the population: a hierarchy of rust and consumption. They do not sleep. They do not dream. They vibrate. From the lowest ball-bearing grazer to the high-voltage titan, every one of them is tuned to the frequency of the Hamlet, locked in a symbiotic groove where life is just another form of entropy management.
