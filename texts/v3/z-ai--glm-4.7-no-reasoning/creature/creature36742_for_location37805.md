The residents of this sunken city are not citizens in the way the world above understands them. They are not individuals with names, but nodes in a net, pulses in a circuit. They do not build; they occupy. They do not rule; they endure. They are the moist, shifting animating force of the fracture, and they are shaped, first and foremost, by the squeeze.

There is no room here, and so the life that survives has evolved to exist *between*. The primary occupants are the Weavers—soft-bodied creatures that have forgone the rigidity of bone in favor of cartilage and gel. They are the architects of the squeezed spaces, appearing like lengths of wet silk or living rope. You might see a Fracture-Slither, a creature that looks less like an animal and more like a tie of pale muscle undulating through the moss. It has no defined head, only a sensory ridge at its leading edge that tastes the chemical composition of the air as it moves. It slides through the channels, its skin secreting a mucilage that lubricates the moss, sealing the moisture in, repairing the green fabric behind it as it passes. It eats the decay; it is the compactor of the city, sliding through the inches-deep detritus, compressing centuries of dead fern into the rich humus that feeds the next generation of growth.

Sharing the corridors are the Stick-Lurkers, amphibians that have forgone the luxury of open water. They are clad in skin that matches the liverworts so perfectly that to see them, they must move. Their limbs are elongated, spindly things, allowing them to span the narrow gaps between the stone faces without touching the wet floor below. They hang in the air, suspended by four limbs splayed against opposite walls, simply waiting. They have flattened bodies and wide, upward-gazing eyes that catch the faint gray light of the sky-strip. They wait for the passing of the Weavers or the flutter of a damp-winged moth, striking with a speed that belies their torpid appearance, their jaws extending like sudden, wet traps. Their silence is total; even when they strike, they absorb the impact of their own movement, the wet moss padding the violence of the hunt.

Where the stone widens into the "plazas," the floor is alive with a different sort of resident: the Scuttlers. These are the crustaceans of the deep earth, isopods and amphipods that have grown grotesquely large in the nutrient-rich darkness. They resemble polished stones, their segmented armor plated in mineral gray and slate blue. They trundle through the sedge and ground-pine, the tank-treads of the city, consuming the fallen fronds and the constant rain of organic debris. They clack softly—a sound like knitting needles—as they graze, their mandibles processing the wet cellulose. They are communal, huddling together in the lee of the larger rock ridges for warmth, piling atop one another in living mounds that shudder and shift as they breathe.

Beneath the surface, in the tannin-dark pools that fill every hollow, live the Phantom-Gills. These are salamanders that have neotenized, retaining their larval gills into an ageless adulthood. They are translucent, their internal organs visible as dim shadows, and they move through the water with the grace of smoke. They have eyes that have atrophied from lack of use, sensing the world through the lateral lines that run down their flanks, detecting the pressure waves of a drowning beetle or the vibration of a falling drop of water. They are the bottom-feeders of the vertical world, the cleansers of the deep water, swimming in the spaces between the submerged roots where the light never reaches.

And then there are the Grazers of the Heights. High up near the strip of sky, where the stone is wettest and the moss thickest, live the Wall-Walkers. These are small, arboreal mammals that have forgotten how to walk on flat ground. They have sharp, curved claws and prehensile tails that act as a fifth limb, anchoring them to the vertical faces of the fracture. Their fur is plush and water-repellant, dark as wet slate. They move in skittering flashes across the green architecture, leaping from one mossy ledge to another, feeding on the succulent tips of the ferns and the nectar of the hidden orchids. They are the only creatures here that possess a trace of warm-blooded vigor, and yet they are quiet, nesting in hollows they excavate in the thick mats of moss, curled into balls of fur to conserve heat against the seeping cold.

But the true population, the one that defines the city, is the invisible one. It is the micro-life. It is the mycelium that threads through every inch of the sponge-ground, a neural network of white fibers connecting root to root, rotting log to living stone. It is the bacteria that thrive in the cold seep, metabolizing the minerals brought up from the deep earth. It is the spores, floating in the saturated air like dust motes in a sunbeam, waiting for a wet spot to land and begin the slow conquest of converting stone to soil.

This is a society of profound indifference. The Fracture-Slither does not notice the Stick-Lurker; the Wall-Walker ignores the Scuttler below. They are all locked in their own struggle for existence, pressed tight against the cold stone, driven by the slow, rhythmic pulse of the water. They are not friends, nor enemies; they are simply neighbors in a house without walls. They pass through each other's territories, they eat one another's dead, they breathe the same heavy, wet air. They are the permanent tenants of a place that refuses to be empty, a writhing, breathing coating over the indifferent rock, turning the geology of the fracture into the biology of the city. They are the green that moves, the water that breathes, the silence that watches.
