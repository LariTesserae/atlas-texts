Here, there is no word for "permanent." There is only the next binding, the next stone, the next breath that crackles in the throat before it can become speech. The people who live here—if "live" is the correct verb for this endurance—have no name for themselves that would survive the telling. They are known, if at all, by the architecture of their exhaustion.

They are the Menders. Or perhaps the Eaten. Or simply the Ones-Who-Hold.

Physically, they are compressed by the altitude and the absolute cold into something dense and tireless. They are not tall; the space between the walkways is mere hand-spans, and they have evolved—or surrendered—to a stature that fits the honeycomb. Their bodies are thick-barreled, short-limbed, and wrapped in layers of cured hide from the very arthropods that devour their homes, skins that are constantly patched with lichen-paste and sinew. Their hands are their defining feature: immense, calloused things, the fingers broad and flat from a lifetime of wedging slate and chipping tufa. The nails are worn to the quick, blackened with ice-blood and stone dust, joints swollen and gnarled from the relentless repetition of binding and stacking. They have the hands of creatures who have never known a tool more sophisticated than a wedge of bone and a length of gut.

Their eyes are a surrender to the light. After generations in the blinding, hyper-sharp noon, their pupils have narrowed to permanent, rheumy slits, and the sclera are yellowed, mapped with burst capillaries from the glare. Many are functionally blind, navigating by the vibration of the meltwater in the rock, the subtle shifts in air pressure when a hide-door is lifted, the cold breath of a gap where a wall should be. They do not need to see the milky arthropods to know they are there; they feel the sublimated heat-halos as a faint, sickly warmth against their skin, a trespass in the deep freeze. 

They live in a state of perpetual, anthill-energy reconstruction, and it has bred a particular psychology. They do not plan. Planning requires a future, and here, the future is a wall that will be eaten in a cycle. They operate on a reflex of immediate failure: see the gap, fill the gap; feel the slack, tie the knot. The settlement is not built to a design but to an impulse. It is a shantytown of muscle memory. When a dwelling collapses—silently, in a slow-motion puff of ice-dust—the Menders do not mourn or deliberate. They simply turn, like a single organism, and begin to re-stack. The scaffolding goes up. The lashings are tied. The arthropods begin to gnaw. The water seeps into the joints. The slate cracks. The structure will fall again. This is not tragedy; it is respiration.

Their society is organized not by hierarchy but by proximity to the weeping stone. The innermost hollows, where the micro-channels of meltwater run closest to the surface and the ice-curtains are thickest, belong to the elders—not elders of age, but elders of endurance. Those who have survived the most collapses, whose hands are the most twisted, are given the relative warmth of the subterranean bleed. Here, they sit with their backs to the wet rock, feeling the subsonic thrum in their spines, and they chip bone pins with a rhythm that matches the mountain's pulse. The young, the quick, the ones with the most strength to waste, cling to the outer walls and the walkways, constantly re-tying the scaffolding that bridges the gaps, their hands torn by the frost-rimed gut-sinew.

They do not speak. The cold freezes sound at the source, and they have long since abandoned the attempt. Their communication is a kinetic language, a choreography of the constant rebuild. A tap of a bone pin against a slate edge signals a fault line. A tug on a shared lashing draws attention to a fraying. A hand pressed flat against a neighbor's chest, feeling the heartbeat and the vibration of the meltwater simultaneously, means *hold here, this joint is failing*. Their silence is not empty; it is dense with the tactile grammar of survival.

They eat the parasites, when they can catch them. The milky-scaled arthropods are cracked open, their endothermic heat sucked greedily from the belly pods before the flesh freezes solid on the tongue. The pale lichen is scraped from the rock in bloody, mineral flakes. The frost-hardened tubers are prized, cracked between stones, tasting of starch and grit. Every meal is a theft from the swarm, and the swarm retaliates by eating the sinew bindings in the night. The Menders wake to find a walkway collapsed, a hide-door slack, and they begin again.

There is no religion here, no prayer to the cold or the light. But there is a reverence, a single sacred truth that permeates their writhing, bazaar of uncoordinated labor: the water. They feel it in their teeth, in their marrow—the subsonic thrum of the mountain bleeding out. They know, in the way a body knows its own heartbeat, that the water is carving the cathedral from the inside out. They are not fighting the cold, or the light, or even the arthropods. They are fighting the water's patience. The water will always win. It will seep, press, and flash. It will swell the joints and crack the slate and reduce the tufa to pitted hollows.

But the Menders are the counter-argument. They are the resistance to the erosion. Not a successful resistance, but a constant one. They are the flesh of the mountain, rebuilding itself as it is consumed. They cling to the weeping stone, stacked and layered, hand-working the crude early tools, bracing against the assault. They are the fever in the deep freeze, the futile, hyperactive equilibrium that keeps the settlement from dissolving into the undulating hollows of the exhausted terrain.

They are the ones who hold. And they will hold until the water carves the last hollow, and the mountain breathes its last subsonic thrum, and the perpetual noon shines on a place where nothing lives, and nothing writhes, and nothing builds.
