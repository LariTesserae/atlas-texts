They are the maintenance crew of the infinite drift, silent technicians clad in chitin and hydraulic bone. They do not scurry so much as they *calculate* their movement across the spongy, rust-colored mats of moss. These are the spider-things, the arachnid-engineers, and their bodies are masterpieces of functional biomechanics. They possess too many legs—eight, ten, perhaps twelve—and each one ends not in a point of flesh, but in a polished, piston-like joint of hardened calcium that clicks softly against the wet vegetation. They move with a jerky, staccato precision, skating over the surface tension of the water-heavy air. They are not hunting; they are pruning. With mandibles that glint like surgical steel, they snip away the overgrown fronds of the floating algae-reefs, clearing the intake valves of the great, sleeping giants. Their eyes are faceted, compound lenses that reflect the stark, unshadowed white light in a thousand tiny mirrors, absorbing data on humidity and pressure, forever ensuring the lattice remains tight.

Drifting silently in the spaces between the spider-things are the Grazers. They resemble rays or manta rays, flattened aerodynamic bodies designed to ride the slow, majestic currents of the vertical world. Their skin is not flesh; it is a translucent, flexible photovoltaic lattice, a patchwork of absorbed sunlight and biological solar panels that glow with a faint, inner teal. They have no mouths, only wide, slitted vents along their undersides that filter the heavy, silken air. As they glide through the suspended globules of water, their ventral gills pulse rhythmically, separating the moisture from the oxygen, converting the atmosphere into fuel. They are immune to the deafening thrum of the forest around them, their internal membranes insulated against the vibration, coasting like ghosts through the roaring steam of the Heart-Pumps.

And then there are the Heart-Pumps themselves—the titans, the anchors of this drifting ecology. They are not merely trees; they are biological colossi, evolved engines of iron and flesh. Their trunks rise like pillars of a forgotten cathedral, vast cylinders of gunmetal-grey bark textured with the smooth, cold feel of brushed steel. This exterior is iron-oxide and hardened mineral, worn smooth by eons of erosion, seamless save for the deep fissures where bioluminescent moss grows in the crevices. They do not rust in the traditional sense; they oxidize slowly, their surfaces flaking into microscopic dust that feeds the floating sponge-forest below.

The branches of the Heart-Pumps are heavy, horizontal cantilevers of vascular muscle, sagging slightly under their own immense weight. From these branches hang the great, translucent leaves, each one a tough, leathery solar sail veined with copper and blue electricity. Inside these leaves, the fluid glow is visible, a thick, luminous sap that pulses in time with the tree's heartbeat. The roots of these giants do not dig into soil; they fan out into the open air, a massive, chaotic net of tendrils that absorbs moisture directly from the 2.8 suspension. They drink the fog.

Beneath the bark, the organism is alive with a deafening roar. It is the sound of massive biological pistons contracting, of valves made of cartelidge slamming shut to pressurize the water sacs within the trunk. The Heart-Pumps are inhaling the heavy, water-saturated air, filtering it through chambers lined with cilia that catch the impurities, and then exhaling purified, dry oxygen in great, cloudy hisses that blast from vents near the crown. This breath ripples through the floating mats, creating a localized wind that the spider-things brace against. It is a continuous, industrial rhythm—a thrumming bass note that vibrates in the chest of any creature nearby, the sound of a planet breathing through a machine.

Flitting among the iron branches are the Dusters—tiny, bird-like creatures composed almost entirely of down and air sacs. They are buoyant, lighter than the heavy humidity, able to perch on the vibrating copper veins of the Heart-Pump leaves without disturbing the mechanism. They feed on the mineral flakes that drift down from the iron bark, their beaks specialized for scraping oxidized metal. They chirp, but their songs are short, high-pitched bursts that are instantly swallowed by the low-frequency roar of the trees. They exist in the interstices, living on the sheddings of the machine-gods, their feathers turning a rusted orange from the iron dust they consume.

Life here is wet, cold, and perpetually in motion yet forever static. The inhabitants are adapted to a world where gravity is a suggestion and the air is a thick, silken soup. They do not fear the fall into the abyss below, for they are part of the suspension, tethered to the floating mats, locked in the eternal maintenance of a system that never changes. They are the components of the great, organic factory, breathing in the glitter-dust water and exhaling the light, drifting in the frozen equilibrium of the vertical sea.
