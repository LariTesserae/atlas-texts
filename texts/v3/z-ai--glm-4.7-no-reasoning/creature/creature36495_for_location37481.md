To live here is to be a note in a chord that can never resolve.

Nothing moves quickly. Nothing can. The acoustic pressure is a physical law unto itself, a gravity of sound that thickens the air, turns the spray into hovering sculpture, and makes haste a concept that died with the green things. To exist on this plateau of drowned mountains is to endure the oscillation—to let it pass through you until the boundary between your body and the thrum becomes indistinct.

Consider the **Stasis-Skaters**, the unnamed scavengers of the spray-zone. They are not birds, though they haunt the air; they are not fish, though they never touch the submerged stone. They are living parabolas—creatures of taut, translucent skin stretched between rigid spars of cartilage, riding the standing waves of sound that arc between the Monoliths. They are born in the sonic updrafts, catching the shockwaves as a physical medium. Their lifecycle is measured not in seasons but in decibels. They feed on the acoustic residue itself—the heat generated by the compression of air, and the electro-static crackle that lives in the engine’s shadow. When a shockwave bellows from a chimney, the Skaters simply tilt a spar, sliding up the pressure gradient, hanging at the apex for hours, their heartbeat synchronized to the pulse of the engine. They are deaf to all but the beat; their eyes are mere light-sensitive pits, sealed shut by scar tissue from the windchill. They navigate by feeling the texture of the sound in their bones.

Down below, where the heavy, oily water meets the knifelike basalt, live the **Silt-Molders**. They are the unseen counterpart to the colossal Leviathan-Striders. If the Striders are the iron-plated islands, the Molders are the submarine roots that tend them. They are blind, white, flaccid things—the texture of wet concrete—that cling to the submerged cliffs, extending long, tubular stomachs into the abyssal silt. They ingest the raw, toxic minerals and the waste of the Striders, excreting the purified metallic snow that the Striders filter-feed. They are the gut flora of the industrial sea. Their biology is a closed circuit of lead, sulfur, and extremophile heat; they thrive where the geothermic pipes bleed warmth into the crushing dark. When the engines pulse, the Molders contract in unison, a peristaltic wave that travels down the mountain face to the midnight zone, milking the submerged stone.

And then there are the **Rust-Choirs**, the human-adjacent presence, the inheritors of the engines. They are not builders; they are maintenance. They live in the lattice of the Monoliths, in the deafening guts of the resonators, where the heat is a solid wall and the sound is a malleable clay. Generations of acoustic pressure have remade them. They are gaunt, stooped figures with overlarge, dense bones and skulls thickened to withstand the roar. Their eardrums are vestigial, long since ruptured and calcified. They do not speak; they do not need to. They communicate through sub-vocalizations that travel through the steel of the Monoliths—tapping the rivets with specialized tuning-fingers, feeling the resonance return. They read the health of the engines by pressing their foreheads to the cold iron and listening to the vibrations with their skulls.

The Rust-Choirs wear suits of woven asbestos and scavenged iron plating, insulated against the chill and the spray. They move with a terrifying, deliberate slowness, knowing that a misstep into the shear-water—the heavy, mineral-thick ocean—means an immediate and soundless death. They tend the thermal pipes, clear the frost from the intake valves with ceremonial scrapers, and harvest the electro-static discharge to spark the burners. They view the Leviathan-Striders as ancient, sleeping gods, and when a Strider’s migration path brings it close enough to brush against the base of a Monolith, the Choirs hold a silent vigil, pressing their hands to the shuddering stone, feeling the immense, geological heartbeat of the crustacean merge with the mechanical roar.

There is no youth here in the traditional sense. The Choirs reproduce through a kind of industrial budding—growing new members in the warmth of the geothermic runoff, incubating them in soundproofed alcoves lined with oily rags. The children emerge already partially deaf, already knowing the rhythm of the thrum. They do not cry when they are born; their first act is to tap a rhythm on the floor, joining the choir.

To live in this place is to be a creature of static violence. You are forged in the forge of the eternal note. Your blood runs thick with iron; your thoughts move at the pace of the tectonic drift. The sky is a bruise, the water is a blade, and the sound is the universe breathing. You do not hope for silence. Silence would be the void, and the void would be death. You cling to the roar, because the roar is the only proof that anything exists at all.
