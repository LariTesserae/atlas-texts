To live in the Sump is to be a component. Nothing here possesses an interiority that we would recognize as private; everything is a gear tooth interlocking with the jaw of the machine. This is not an ecosystem; it is a closed equation, written in rust and voltage and screaming.

Consider the rust-mites. They are the most populous residents, the dark matter of this place. To call them the size of dogs is to describe a ship by its tonnage—it misses the horror of their geometry. They are low, flat, and sprawling, built to skulk beneath the curvature of massive pipes. Their bodies are not flesh; they are a nightmare lattice of biological iron and carbon, grown rather than spun, resembling something between a tick and a tank. They have no eyes. In a place where light is a failing spark and a dull fungal vomit, vision is a liability. Instead, their faces are broad, sweeping dishes of chitin, studded with millions of microscopic setae that read the vibration of the air—the specific frequency of oxidizing metal, the tremor of a heat-bat taking flight, the hum of a glass-bramble shifting its charge. 

Their mandibles are the sound of this place. They are not mouthparts; they are industrial tools. They are hardened steel, self-sharpening, interlocking in a spiral pattern like the auger of a milling machine. When a rust-mite feeds—which is always—it does not chew; it grinds. It presses its face against the skin of a pipe and engages, peeling away layers of oxidized metal in long, curling ribbons that it funnels into a digestive tract that is more smelter than stomach. The sound is a continuous, drizzling hiss, like steel wool dragged slowly across a microphone. They do not stop. They do not sleep. They are driven by a chemical imperative that demands the reduction of the great iron world into waste, and they pursue it with the mindless, flawless dedication of an enzyme dissolving a cell.

Above them, hanging in the dead air like obscene fruit, are the heat-bats. They are the counterweight to the mites, the predators that give the loop its shape. Where the mites are low and grinding, the bats are suspended and shrieking. Their leathery wings are not for flight in any avian sense; the air is too thick, too viscous to slice. Instead, they are sails and anchors, allowing them to ride the slow convection currents rising from the warm engine housings, or to lock their hooked talons into the thick rubberized sheathing of dangling cables. 

Hanging, they resemble stalactites of tar. They cluster until the cable sags, a dense, writhing pendulum of dark flesh. Their faces are voids, eyeless and pale, dominated by deep, convoluted thermal pits—wrinkled maws of hypersensitive tissue that paint the world in gradients of heat. To a heat-bat, the Sump is not dark; it is a blazing chiaroscuro. The great engine housings are blinding suns of residual warmth; the rust-mites are dim, scurrying embers; the glass-brambles are sharp, cold negatives in the shape of lightning. 

When they shriek, it is not to communicate. It is echolocation weaponized by physics. The sound they produce is a metallic scraping, a frequency perfectly tuned to the strange acoustics of the heavy air. It does not travel far, but it resonates. The shriek hits the particulate fog, hits the flat planes of the brambles, hits the metal walls, and builds a standing wave. The bats feel the return vibration in their wing-joints and their jaw-hinges; they do not listen, they *feel* the shape of the room. And in doing so, they create the very walls they navigate. Their sound is the mortar of this place. 

And then there are the glass-brambles, the flora that is also a trap. They do not grow; they extrude. They are the Sump's excretory system, its kidneys. When the rust-mites strip the pipes, they expose the raw alloy beneath, which reacts with the copper-tasting fog to produce a heavy, toxic exhaust. The brambles drink this. They are crystalline straws, thrust into the floor, drawing the gas down into their silicate root-networks where the raw electrical current arcing through the floor breaks the gas down, neutralizes it, and excretes the inert dust that hangs in the air. They are immune to the heat-bats, too sharp and cold to be eaten, and useless to the rust-mites, holding no iron. They simply endure, humming with the voltage that sustains them, looking like a frozen explosion of broken glass.

But the truest resident of the Sump is not a creature at all; it is the Loop itself. 

Nothing here has a lifespan. Time is an arrow that was fired into a solid wall a century ago, and it is still vibrating in place. A rust-mite does not age; it simply accumulates a patina of the very dust it helps create, until one day its mandibles grind down to nubs and it stops, becoming a fixture, a tiny hill of metal on a pipe, soon to be eaten by its own grandchildren who see it only as a rich deposit of oxidized iron. A heat-bat does not die; it hangs and shrieks until the kink in the cable it grips finally shears under the collective weight of generations, dropping the cluster into the dark maw of an open pressure door, where the current of falling air carries them back to the warm engines to begin the cycle of hanging again. 

There is no progress in the Sump, no evolution, no history. The weirdness of the place is the weirdness of a photograph that breathes. It is a mechanical purgatory populated by things that believe they are alive, simply because they are consuming. They are not the masters of the Sump; they are its inmates, trapped in a perfect, screaming circuit, turning the rust into dust, the dust into gas, the gas into crystal, the crystal into hum, the hum into shriek, and the shriek into rust, forever and ever, in the warm, dry, electric dark.
