To live in the Under-Veins is to live as an extension of the stone. The people here—if they can be called that, for they share more DNA with the basalt and the slate than with the soft, surface-dwelling things that breath warm air—are the Gelembic. They are not a nation, not a tribe, but a colony of masons trapped in the reflex of building.

They are born from the damp and the cold, emerging not from wombs but from the deep crevices where the weeping water pools, slick and heavy with minerals. They are slight, wiry things, forged by claustrophobia. A surface man would break his skull on the jutting obsidian within an hour; the Gelembic navigate the landscape of knives by moving like water themselves. Their skin is the texture and hue of wet limestone, mottled with deposits of bruised purple where the flat light hits them wrong. Their spines are hunched, not with age, but with the constant expectation of low ceilings and overhead bridges. Their hands are their most vital organs—massive, calloused, disproportionately wide, with fingers like the gripping legs of cave crickets, capable of finding purchase on a vertical shale face where no ledge exists. They do not walk; they clamber. Their gaits are a jerky, sideways scuttling, eyes flicking upward to track the drip line of condensation.

They possess no eyes, only shallow depressions beneath brow ridges of hardened calcium, shielded by a nictitating membrane of translucent stone. They do not see the grey and purple flatness; they feel it. They read the tension in the green-copper bindings, the vibration of the massive stone rollers grinding thirty feet below, the subtle shift in air pressure when a bladder-valve fills and drops its weight. Their language is not spoken—the heavy acoustics would swallow it—but tapped. They strike the bedrock with iron chisels, sending staccato rhythms through the stone that vibrate through the bones of the listener. *Chink-chink-chink.* It is a language of industry, of warning, of structural integrity.

Their society is an anthill without a queen, a mechanism without an operator. They do not govern; they accrete. The city climbs inward because the Gelembic do not know how to stop. They are driven by a biological imperative as rigid as the geology that surrounds them: *fill the void*. When a gap between towers grows too wide—perhaps thirty feet of open air, a terrifying abyss to their minds—they feel a physical itch, a cold hollow in their chests. They must span it. They lash rough-hewn timber, they stack the shale, they bind it with copper that will never oxidize in the airless damp. They work until the gap is closed, until the density is 1.0, and then they climb higher, chasing the stalactites, merging their constructions with the hanging teeth of the earth. 

They do not sleep in beds. They do not rest in plazas—there are none, because a plaza is a failure of purpose, an unbearable emptiness. They sleep lashed to the scaffolding, hanging like the pale, stunted moss from the north-facing walls, their bodies slick with the condensation that plinks rhythmically against the leather bladder-valves below. They are always working, always building, even in their torpor. A sleeping Gelembic will unconsciously chip at the stone beside it, smoothing a jagged edge, preparing a surface for a block that has not yet been laid.

They are herbivores of a sort, grazing on the sparse vegetation, but they harvest the pale moss and the black, leafless trees not for sustenance alone, but to clear the way for the next course of masonry. They eat the raw shoots and the damp bark with the same mechanical indifference with which they lay a cornerstone. When they find a flightless bird—the dust-colored survivors of the deep—they do not hunt it. They ignore it, unless it perches on a critical load-bearing point, in which case they gently, meticulously wall it in, incorporating it into the structure. The bird becomes a fossil, breathing its last in the dark, another binding agent in the great accretion.

The Gelembic do not know who gave the first command to build. They do not remember the architects who laid down the hand tools and vanished. They do not possess a concept of "finished." To them, the Under-Veins are not a city being born prematurely; they are a body growing around a skeleton. The rock is sharper than the law, and so the rock is their law. They are the flesh of the factory that the earth forgot to turn off—pale, damp, tireless, building a labyrinth that will never be completed, climbing inward toward a center that does not exist, in a space that is still deciding if it wants to be solid. And they will keep stacking the stone until it decides.
