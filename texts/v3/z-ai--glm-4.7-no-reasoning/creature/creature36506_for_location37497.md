They are tall—too tall for the weight of their heads, yet they do not stoop. They stand upright, their spines rigid as the vertical cliffs that define the plateau’s edge. Their skin is the color of the wet stone, a pale, porous grey that seems to weep a constant, thin film of moisture. It is not sweat; it is an osmotic weeping, a physical transpiration that keeps them permanently slick, ensuring they never dry out in the perpetual, suspended afternoon.

Their limbs are elongated, ending in hands that are not quite hands. The fingers are tapered and hardened, calcified into living chisels capable of prying apart the fissures in the rock or smoothing the rough-hewn blocks of their city. They move with a fluid, gliding economy of motion, their feet wide and flat to distribute their weight across the damp, packed earth, leaving no prints, only the faintest displacement of the standing water that sheens the ground.

There is no gender among them, only a singular, architectural intent. Their faces are smooth, devoid of the ridges of expression—no brows to furrow, no mouths to speak or eat. They do not consume. The biology of the plateau has negated the need for intake; the air is thick with suspended humidity and perhaps they absorb their sustenance directly through their skin, drinking the atmosphere like the moss drinks the stone. They have eyes, but they are deep-set and dark, like the still, glass-smooth canals, reflecting the sky with a perfect, unblinking clarity.

They are the architects of the stone city, but they are not builders in the human sense of urgency. They do not hurry. A single block might be positioned over the course of years, lifted with the slow, rhythmic application of simple levers and the friction of their own immense strength. They work in silence, communicating through a language of vibration and pressure. When two of them meet, they might press their calcined forearms together, standing motionless for hours, exchanging information through the resonance of their bones, a silent data transfer that ripples invisibly through the stagnant air.

Inside the city, the streets are empty of movement but full of presence. They rest within the dry-stone structures, standing or reclining on stone slabs, entering a state of hibernation that resembles death but is actually a deep, meditative pause. The woven reeds over the windows are not for privacy, but to filter the light into a softer, dimmer hum, allowing them to withdraw from the brilliance of the plateau’s unyielding sun.

They are the custodians of the "Eternal Yesterday." Their purpose is to maintain the equilibrium. They patrol the banks of the straight, ruler-drawn rivers, clearing a fallen leaf or a displaced piece of moss with surgical precision. If the water level shifts by a fraction of an inch, they are there, adjusting the flow, ensuring that the overflow matches the evaporation exactly. They are the immune system of the plateau, attacking any hint of decay or chaotic change with a ruthless, quiet efficiency.

Because they do not die, they do not reproduce. The population is static, fixed at a number that perfectly matches the available space. They are ageless; the young look identical to the old, for there are no young and no old. They simply *are*, crystallized moments of consciousness that emerged fully formed from the damp rock and will remain until the stone itself turns to sand.

They possess a culture of stillness. Their art is the arrangement of space. They do not paint or carve statues; they arrange the negative space between the massive fern-like trees. They will spend a decades turning a single stone pillar until it catches the light at a specific angle, creating a shadow that stretches for miles, a silent semaphore visible only to the horizon. The beauty they create is not for enjoyment, but for alignment—aligning themselves, their city, and their world with the immutable laws of the plateau.

To see them is to see a reflection of the landscape: stark, wet, and terrifyingly sharp. They carry the silence of the place within them. When they move, the air moves with them, heavy and suspended. They are the breath that is held, the guardians of the static potential, waiting in the warm, skin-comfortable heavy air for a change that will never come.
