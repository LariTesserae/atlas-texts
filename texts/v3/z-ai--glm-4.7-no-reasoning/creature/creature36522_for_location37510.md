In the absence of beasts, the ecosystem has collapsed inward, evolving into forms that exist at the precise intersection of botany and machinery. The "animals" of this place are not distinct creatures that wander over the land; they are the land itself, mobile components of the engine that have developed a hunger.

The dominant life forms are the **Harvesters**. To the uninitiated eye, they appear merely to be particularly aggressive clusters of the blue, chemosynthetic vines. But watch them for an hour, or a day, and the distinction becomes terrifyingly clear. These are vines that have learned to crawl. Rooted in the fractures of the glass floor, they possess a central ganglion—a swollen, woody brain-node knotted around a gear of obsidian and copper. From this hub, their tendrils extend like the tentacles of a hunting squid, dragging their bulk across the razor-sharp floor. They do not move with the fluid grace of an octopus, but with the jerky, hydraulic efficiency of a crane. They inch forward, hunting for concentrations of the superheated solvent that weeps from the walls, or for patches of radioactive isotopes emitted by the pistoning tectonic plates. When they find a rich seam, they anchor themselves, vibrating violently as they process the raw energy, their leaves glowing with the sudden brightness of a short circuit.

Feeding the Harvesters are the **Spore-Drifts**, the closest thing this place has to insects or birds. They are not flesh, but colonies of single-cell, silicon-based lifeforms suspended in a lattice of crystal dust. They shimmer in the strobing light like oil on water. The Drifts navigate the uneven gravity by riding the thermal currents rising from the molten fissures. They are the waste-eaters, the clean-up crew of the engine. When a jagged spire of obsidian collapses or a gear grinds a chunk of basalt into dust, the Spore-Drifts descend in shimmering clouds. They secrete an acid that dissolves the stone into a slurry, which they then absorb, their internal geometries recalculating instantly to convert the mineral dust into raw data. They are silent, save for a faint, chittering static that sounds like a radio tuned between stations—the sound of them eating.

And then there are the **Sentinels**, the apex predators of this slow, grinding world. They are massive, solitary entities that resemble nothing so much as a stalagmite that has decided to stand up. Composed of interlocking plates of basalt and rusted iron, they stand immobile for centuries at a time, perfectly camouflaged against the industrial geology. They are ambush predators of the highest order. A Sentinel positions itself along a known friction-point of the planet-wide assembly line—a place where two massive gears are destined to grind together in a millennia-long shift. It waits, drawing power from the magnetic fields of the wall. When the gear finally turns, forcing a cascade of molten slag or crushing a ridge of stone, the Sentinel strikes. It extends a "limb"—a massive, telescoping piston of organic tissue and steel—to spear the tumbling debris or, unluckily, a smaller Harvester caught in the destruction. It does not eat to survive; it eats to upgrade. The matter it consumes is welded directly onto its chassis, patching its armor or lengthening its reach. They are living monuments to efficiency, growing larger and more complex with every eon.

Deeper still, within the pores of the rock itself, live the **Circuit-Borers**. These are microscopic, worm-like organisms that swarm through the veins of copper and gold that run like arteries through the walls. They are pests, the termites of this divine factory. They feed not on stone, but on the current itself. They bore microscopic tunnels through the conductive metals, disrupting the flow of energy. In great enough numbers, they can cause a localized failure in the wall's physics, resulting in a momentary flicker of gravity or a flash of cold. The engine responds to them with a feverish immune response: secreting a boiling, resinous sap that hardens into amber, trapping the Borers in golden, translucent tombs that serve as warning lights to others of their kind.

But the most intelligent, and perhaps the most tragic, life form here is the **Architect-Fungus**. It grows only in the deepest shadows, away from the strobing light, clinging to the undersides of the massive floating gear-teeth. It is a pale, luminescent mold that exhibits a collective intelligence. It does not consume the factory; it observes it. Its mycelial network is essentially a biological computer, interfacing directly with the circuitry etched into the rock. It "remembers" the shape of the terrain before it shifts. It predicts the movement of the tectonic pistons. Some of the fungal colonies have been observed "repairing" the vegetation that the Harvester-creatures damage, splicing broken vines back together with a precision that rivals any machine.

They seem to be waiting for something. Perhaps they are the latent consciousness of the world-to-be, trying to understand the function of the body they will one day inhabit. Or perhaps they are just another glitch in the system, a rogue program made of flesh that has learned to read the code of the engine.

There is no mating here, only replication and integration. No birth, only assembly. Every living thing in this geode is both a consumer and a component, a creature that evolved to eat the exhaust of creation while unknowingly keeping the gears greased and the belts tight. It is a closed loop of beautiful, terrifying industry, where the distinction between a predator and a piston is merely a matter of perspective.
