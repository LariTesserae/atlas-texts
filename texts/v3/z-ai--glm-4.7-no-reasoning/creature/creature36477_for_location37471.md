The architects of the Gullet are the Oozers, though they are rarely seen in their entirety. To witness an Oozer is to witness a living trowel, a creature of soft, pale flesh that exists solely to mix and spread the ceramic paste that binds the city. They are hunchbacked and bloated, their skins translucent and slick with the same greying mucus they excrete from glands along their jaws. They move with a glacial, deliberate purpose, crawling up the vertical spirals of the moss-mounds to apply a fresh layer of secretion that hardens into a stony shell. They are blind, their faces dominated by a gaping, toothless mouth designed to carry mud, and sensitive frond-like antennae that twitch constantly, tasting the chemical composition of the air and the structural integrity of the walls.

Living within these breathing mounds are the Spinners. Small, multi-limbed arboreal mammals, they resemble a nightmare cross between a lemur and a spider. Their fur is slick and dark, matted down by the perpetual dampness, and their limbs are elongated, ending in sharp, hooked claws that allow them to scale the slick fungal architecture with terrifying speed. They are the weavers of the Gullet, responsible for the ceiling that blocks the sky. They hang upside down from the root-vines, braiding kelp and hanging moss into the thatched roof, constantly repairing the holes where the heavy rain tries to break through. They communicate not with sound, but with a rapid clicking of their claws against the ceramic shells—a staccato Morse code that echoes through the canyon like the ticking of a million clocks.

Guarding the outer palisades are the Needles. Standing waist-high to a man, these are the soldiers of this primitive civilization. They are crustacean-like, covered in jagged plates of chitin that are sharp enough to slice through careless fingers. They possess no eyes, only deep pits of sensory holes that detect the slightest vibration. Their forelimbs are modified into serrated spears, naturally grown and perpetually sharpened on the granite rocks. They stand motionless for hours at a time, indistinguishable from the sharpened stakes of the palisade itself, waiting for the vibration of an intruder—or a falling neighbor—to trigger their reflex to stab.

Scuttling through the narrow, muddy streets are the Scavengers, a chaotic mix of pale, eyeless crabs and sleek, wet salamanders the size of dogs. The crabs are the sanitation crew, their claws specialized for cracking open the bones of the dead and stripping the carcasses clean within minutes. They move in a chittering, tidal wave of shells, consuming everything that falls. The salamanders, sleek and muscular with skin the texture of wet velvet, are the ambush predators of the ground level. They do not chase; they wait, submerged in the mud with only their nostrils exposed, ready to snap up any crab or unwary Spinner that ventures too far from the vertical safety of the walls.

And dominating the central, black pool are the Brood-Mothers. Massive, sedentary termite-like queens, they are the heart of the "buildings," the biological engines that power the expansion of the city. They are immense, bloated sacks of flesh that barely move, anchored deep within the largest mounds. Their bodies are fused with the architecture of the nest, their nervous systems extending into the roots and vines, sensing the health of the colony through the thrumming of the walls. They are the reason the city breathes; their massive, rhythmic intake of air draws the oxygen down into the Gullet, and their exhalations filter out the toxic gases, venting them through the chimneys of the upper mounds.
