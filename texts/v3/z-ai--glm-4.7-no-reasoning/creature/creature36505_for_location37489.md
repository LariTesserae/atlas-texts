Something lives here.

Not many things. Not in the way that life crowds and clamors in places warmed by suns and watered by rain. But the description is certain: there are lithic growers, scattered across that desiccated floor like dark thoughts in a heavy mind. They are what live here. They are all that lives here. And so they deserve more than a glance—they deserve the full weight of attention that any survivor of such a place has earned by simply enduring.

Consider them first as forms. They rise from cracks in the bedrock—these must be ancient fissures, joints in the stone where thermal stress long ago parted the strata just enough for something to find purchase. The growers exploit these weaknesses with a kind of patient opportunism that has no strategy behind it, no intention. They simply found warmth welling up from below, or concentrated between the faces of cracked stone, and they stayed. A handspan tall, perhaps less; the darkness makes measurement uncertain, and they do not advertise themselves. They are dark, matte things, absorbers rather than reflectors, their surfaces the color of the shadow they inhabit—or perhaps slightly darker, swallowing what little visual information might exist in this place. They branch once or twice, producing blunt knobs that terminate without flourish, without flower or fruit or any gesture toward reproduction as we commonly understand it. There is a waxy quality to their surfaces, an oily sheen visible only as a subtle difference in the texture of the darkness around them—something slightly greasier, slightly more present than the dry dust and the smooth stone.

They are half-root, half-mineral. This is the crucial fact, the axis around which their existence turns. They do not draw water from the ground because there is no water to draw. They do not photosynthesize because there is no light to harvest. They do not consume other organisms because there are no other organisms to consume. They exist on warmth alone—on the slow, constant radiance of the mineral floor, and on what the description calls "the faint, slow decay of the dust itself." This is thermosynthesis, a metabolism of heat, and it is perhaps the most austere form of life imaginable. They take the thermal gradient between stone and air, the molecular energy of warmth itself, and they transmute it into the only thing they produce: more of themselves. But barely. A millimeter per thousand years. A growth rate that makes geological erosion seem hasty.

And what are they, really? The description calls them "pre-cellular in their simplicity," and this is a phrase worth sitting with. They may not have membranes in any recognizable sense—no lipid bilayer enclosing a cytoplasm, no nucleus containing genetic instructions. They may be mineral accretions with organic properties, or organic structures with mineral properties, the distinction having collapsed in this place where the usual categories have no purchase. Perhaps they began as crystals that learned to self-replicate, or as protoplasmic residues that mineralized around their own persistence. Perhaps they are something older than the distinction between the living and the non-living, a transitional form that never needed to evolve further because it found a niche where evolution offers no advantage. In a place of frozen equilibrium, change is not survival—stasis is.

Each one is alone. This is important. They do not cluster; they exist in isolation, separated by breathless expanses of nothing. They cannot communicate because there is nothing to communicate. They cannot compete because there is nothing to compete for. They cannot cooperate because there is no threat to cooperate against. They are solipsists by circumstance, each one a universe unto itself, knowing nothing of its nearest neighbor except perhaps—perhaps—a faint thermal signature across the dark distance, a warmth slightly different from the warmth of the stone. Or perhaps not even that. Perhaps each one is entirely ignorant of all others, entirely sufficient to itself, a closed system of minimal metabolism and zero relation.

They do not move. They do not change. The description states this twice, and it bears repeating until the full weight of it settles in. In every other place where life exists, movement is its signature—the twitch of muscle, the reach of root, the turn of leaf toward light, the pulse of sap, the beat of flagella. Here, movement has been abandoned as a strategy. There is nowhere to move to, nothing to move toward, nothing to move away from. The warmth is everywhere and constant. The darkness is everywhere and constant. To move would be to expend energy for no gain, and energy is too precious here to waste on gesture. So they are still. Not the stillness of a stalking predator, or a waiting seed, or a meditating mind—those are all stillnesses that contain the promise of future motion. This is a stillness that contains no promise. They are as still as stone because they are nearly stone, and stone does not aspire to move.

And yet—they have a kind of presence. They rise from the floor. They branch. They exude. These are actions, however slow, however minimal. To rise is to assert verticality in a horizontal place, to oppose the flatness of the packed silt with a stubby gesture of height. To branch is to complexity, to become something more than a simple protrusion, to differentiate into knob and stem and base. To exude—that oily sheen—is to transform internal chemistry into external substance, to mark the boundary between self and not-self with a faint secretion. These are the gestures of life, stripped down to their barest possible expression. They cannot be reduced further without disappearing entirely.

So: who lives here?

The lithic growers live here. They are not impressive. They are not beautiful in any conventional sense—dark, matte, stubby things that look more like accidental formations than intentional organisms. They are not numerous; you could count them all in an hour of careful walking, and the count might be less than a dozen, or less than half a dozen, depending on how far the basin extends into the darkness where you cannot see. They are not dynamic; they will not reward your attention with motion or growth or response. They will simply be there, as they have been for uncounted ages, absorbing warmth and existing at the absolute minimum threshold of what can be called existence.

But they are here. In a place of total darkness, total silence, total stillness, total absence of water and life and change—they are here. They have found a way to persist on warmth alone, to build bodies from mineral and oil, to maintain themselves against the entropy that should by all rights have dissolved them into the dust long ago. They are the proof that the universe does not require much to make something endure. A crack in the stone. A constant temperature. Enough time for patience to become indistinguishable from success.

They do not know they are alone. They do not know anything. But if you stand in that basin, in that heavy simmer, in that anechoic dark, and you let your eyes adjust until the darkness becomes not a wall but a medium—and if you look down at the floor, and you see one of those stubby, branching, dark-matte shapes rising from the bedrock with its faint oily sheen—then you are looking at something that lives. Something that has always lived here, for values of "always" that make human civilization seem like a brief eccentricity. Something that will still be living here long after you have left, and long after every place you have ever known has been ground to dust or swallowed by sea or burned to ash.

Something lives here. It is not much. It is enough.
