The residents of this sunken, folded pocket are not entities in the way the surface understands them—distinct creatures with skins and boundaries. Here, life is a continuum, a gradient between the mineral and the animal, the plant and the architecture.

The primary inhabitants are the **Thrombophytes**, the blind vines that constitute the forest. They are not merely plants; they are the architects and the atmosphere of the place. To call them "flora" is a convenience that fails to capture their agency. They possess a form of distributed intelligence, a collective consciousness rooted in the dense tangle of their rootless stems. They are white and translucent, lacking the chlorophyll that demands the sun; instead, their bodies are filled with a clear, viscous sap that pulses with a rhythm agonizingly slow—a beat that might occur once per hour. They "see" through chemistry, tasting the water for the faint metallic tang of fresh basalt wounds. When a new fracture bleeds mineral dust into the water, the thrombophytes swarm toward it, not by moving quickly, but by prioritizing growth in that direction. They are the geology’s digestive system, turning the sharp edges of the earth into soft, pale flesh.

Intertwined with the thrombophytes are the **Caulifers**, the spiraling arms that give the void its structure. These are stouter, thicker than a human torso, with a skin like polished, wet bone. They act as the load-bearing beams of this submerged cathedral. Their purpose is structural as much as it is biological; they grow to bridge gaps, to lock the chaotic tangle of the thinner vines into a rigid lattice. If you were to cut one (though the basalt water makes cutting nearly impossible), it would not bleed sap but a dense, fibrous paste that smells of ozone and old salt. They are the "bones" of the room, and they hum constantly. The sound you feel in your teeth—the subsonic vibration of the rock—is amplified and modulated by the caulifers, turning the geological groans of the earth into a resonant, low-frequency song that keeps the pocket suspended in its own weird topology.

Then there are the **Limbless Drifters**, the fauna of the deep. They are rare because the space is so full, but they exist in the interstices, the "rooms" formed by the vine-walls. They have no defined shape, resembling a blob of congealed jelly more than a fish. They possess no eyes, for there is nothing to see, and no distinct limbs, for there is no need to swim against the current. They are filter-feeders of the highest order, drifting through the viscous water like ghosts. Their bodies are permeable membranes; they simply allow the cold, heavy brine to pass through them, absorbing the mucus shed by the thrombophytes and the microscopic organisms that thrive on the mineral dust. They are warm—startlingly so—creating a localized heat signature that betrays their presence in the freezing dark. They do not breathe; they osmose. They have no brain, only a dense nerve net that allows them to sense the texture of the water, ensuring they do not drift into the crushing grip of a caulifer.

Living deep within the densest mats of vegetation are the **Siliceous Siphons**. These are smaller, parasitic organisms that look like delicate, white sea-anemones made of glass. They anchor themselves firmly into the joints of the caulifers, drilling into the woody tissue with microscopic, rotating files made of silica. They do not kill their hosts; they merely tap into the slow circulatory system of the vine, stealing the nutrient-rich sap. In return, they secrete a enzyme that keeps the surrounding water clear of parasites that might otherwise harm the thrombophytes. It is a symbiotic relationship built on theft and protection, functioning on a timescale of centuries.

And finally, there is the **Itch**, a presence barely perceptible. It is not a single organism, but the ecosystem itself—The microscopic life, the bacteria, and the spores that saturate the water, turning it into a "living plasma." This is what makes the water feel thick, what makes the darkness feel like it has a texture. The Itch is the metabolic heat of a trillion tiny organisms consuming the rock, consuming the water, consuming the dead skin of the drifters. It is the engine of the pocket, the relentless, hungry force that is slowly, surely, converting the entire hollow into a single, solid block of living matter.

Together, they are not a community of separate individuals, but a single, sprawling superorganism. The vines are its flesh, the drifters its blood cells, the siphons its white blood cells, and the Itch its immune system. They do not communicate; they *are* the communication. The looped nature of the space—the way the right wall becomes the left—is a physical manifestation of their biology: a system that has folded in on itself, infinitely recycling its own matter, waiting for the moment when the pressure becomes too great and the cycle begins anew.
