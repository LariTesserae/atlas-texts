They are the Architects of Rust, the Children of the Thrum. In the Flood-Grove of Saint-Vert, where the air is too thick to breathe and the light is a mere suggestion, "life" does not scurry or flutter; it spreads, anchors, and consumes.

The primary citizens of this heavy, wet bowl are the **Iron-Ferns**. They are not delicate things. Each frond is a jagged plate of steel-hard biological matter, springing from the earth—or directly from the iron itself—in tightly wound screws of serrated green. They do not photosynthesize in the traditional sense, for the sun is a myth here. Instead, they are *phonosynthetic*. Their broad, saw-blade leaves capture the deafening bass notes of the Great Compactors, vibrating violently in the wake of each piston’s crash. This kinetic agony is what they drink. You can trace the health of a colony by the buzz in the air; the loudest, most abrasive patches of noise are where the ferns stand tallest, their edges rimmed with a vibration-induced bioluminescence that bleeds a pale, sickly green into the humid dark.

Clustering in the shadows of these giants are the **Copper-Lichens**. They are a crust, a living rash that blooms across the wet basalt and the riveted flanks of the engine houses. They possess a collective intelligence that borders on the hive-mind. When the maintenance skiff passes, dragging its wake of oily rainbows, the lichen ripples. It is not a reaction to the water, but to the disturbance in the magnetic field. They "see" through the electrical turbulence of the storm clouds above and the current running through the tram rails below. They feed on the slow oxidation of the metal, excreting a mild acid that accelerates the decay of the pipes they cling to, turning the industrial skeletons into the rich, black sludge from which new roots will drink.

Dominating the vertical spaces are the **Stranglers**, immense vines as thick as a man’s torso, hanging from the overhead eaves and dripping a viscous, clear nectar. They are the hunters of the grove, though they hunt nothing that moves. They hunt heat. Where a pipe vents scalding steam, the Stranglers coil tightest, their flesh warm to the touch, pulsing with the thermal rhythm of the machine. Their surface is covered in sticky, barbed hairs that catch the drifting spores and metallic dust swirling in the air, slowly incorporating the inorganic debris into their own structure. Over decades, they calcify, turning partially into stone and metal, becoming the structural bones of the new ecosystem.

Beneath the surface of the black, glass-like lakes, things are stirring. In the water, the line between plant and machine is entirely erased. Here live the **Piston-Polyps**. Rooted in the silt of the lakebed, they are stalk-like organisms with heads of smooth, metallic nodules that resemble ball-bearings. They are sensitive to pressure. When a Compactor slams down, sending a shockwave through the ground and water, the Polyps recoil instantly, pulling their metallic heads deep into the safety of their fleshy stalks. As the ripples fade and the vibration settles, they bloom again, their nodules clicking softly together, harvesting the microscopic iron particles suspended in the water. They are the filter feeders of the deep, slowly cleaning the water of the city’s leeching blood, turning poison into heavy, inert pearls that sink into the mud.

And then there are the **Resonance-Fungi**, massive, shelf-like growths that sprout from the joints where metal meets metal, where the friction is greatest. They are soft and fleshy, covered in a fine, downy fur that trembles perpetually. They do not grow outward; they grow *into* the vibration. They act as dampeners, effectively "eating" the shaking that would otherwise tear the machines apart. In exchange, the machinery provides them with the perfect perch to catch the falling condensation. They are the symbiotes, the lovers of the machine, ensuring the Great Compactors can continue their rhythmic suicide dance just a little longer.

There is no animal life here that we would recognize as sentient. The "people" of this place are the processes themselves—the slow, digestion of the iron, the rhythmic beating of the engines, and the relentless, creeping growth of the green.
