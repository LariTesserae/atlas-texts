To live in the City of Damp Stone is to be on the verge of not living at all—or at least, not living as the lowlanders understand it. There is no bustle here, no commerce, no governance that requires voices raised in argument. The population is a number that has likely remained exactly the same for centuries, hovering somewhere between a dozen and two dozen souls. They are not inhabitants in the active sense; they are a part of the stasis, human elements that have been weathered down to their most essential, quiet forms.

You would not notice them at first. They do not move through the streets in the way people move elsewhere. There is no walking with purpose, no hurry, no evasion. Their motion is a slow, deliberate negotiation with the slick, reflective stone. They walk with a gait that is almost a glide—feet placed flat to maximize friction against the damp glaze, bodies leaning infinitesimally into the constant, prickling chill of the wind. They wear garments of undyed wool, felted and dense, coated in a lanolin so thick that the persistent mist beads on their shoulders and rolls off in tiny, erratic rivers. The cloth is the color of the clouds, the color of the goats, the color of the lichen-bleached rock. They blend in by choice and by consequence; a figure standing still against a drystone wall is less a person than a temporary thickening of the masonry.

Their faces are a geography of the city itself. The cold is a sculptor, and it has carved them into the same forms as the rock: high, angular cheekbones that catch the weeping water and channel it down channels of deep-set eyes. Their skin is wind-tight, roughened to the texture of basalt, lacking the flush of warm blood. They possess a pallor that is not sickly, but mineral—the grey-white of wet granite when the sun breaks through. Their hands are enormous, knotted things, the knuckles swollen and the fingertips worn flat from a lifetime of gripping cold chisels and testing the friction of stone. The dampness is in their lungs, in their bones; they breathe out plumes of vapor that merge immediately with the mist, their internal heat surrendered without resistance to the air.

They are the builders and the maintainers, though "maintaining" is perhaps too active a word for what they do. They are *sustainers* of a balance. The city is in equilibrium, and their work is not progress, but preservation. They do not build new structures; they only re-seat a stone that the ice has heaved slightly out of line. They do not dig new channels; they only clear a runnel where a tuft of wiry grass has taken root. Their technology is entirely tactile and manual, and they prefer it that way. The friction of rope, the leverage of wood, the weight of stone—these are not limitations to them, but the proper vocabulary of a world that has rejected complexity. A wooden lever is a tool that can be understood by touch, by the grain and the give of it. Iron would be an intrusion, a metal that rings too loudly and rusts in the damp. They work in silence, or near to it, communicating with gestures and the occasional low hum that harmonizes, unconsciously, with the wind in the ventilation shafts.

Their relationship with water is sacramental. They do not drink from the small pools that gather in the crannies where buildings buttress against the cliff; those are left to the rock and the lichen. Instead, they collect the weeping water in stone cups, catching the thin tears that glide down the gables. They drink it cold and pure, tasting the minerals of the ridge, the ghost of the mountain’s bones. They know the rhythm of the drips intimately. A longer pause between the *plink* from the eaves means the mist has thinned; a quicker tempo means condensation is heavy. They do not measure time in hours, but in the filling of a reservoir, the drying of a runnel, the subtle shift in the echo of water falling on wet stone.

They are solitary, but not lonely—or rather, their loneliness is so complete and so longstanding that it has become a form of company. They dwell in the sturdy, primitive structures, one or two to a building, and they go for days without seeing another person. When they do encounter one another on an arched bridge of rough-hewn granite, they do not speak. They pause, acknowledge the other's presence with a slow blink of pale, water-clear eyes, and then continue on their way. There is no need for words. They all know the same few truths: the cold, the damp, the weight of the stone, the drop of the precipice. Words are for places where reality is uncertain; here, reality is a drystone wall, fitted so precisely it seems to have grown. It does not require commentary.

Occasionally, one of them will stand on the edge of the city, where the land falls away into the endless clouds, and look out at the sparse presences and vast emptiness. They do not feel the vertigo that a visitor would. They feel the density—the specific, measurable density of their small island of construction against the sea of empty air. They understand themselves as a cluster of warmth and intention, however faint, clinging to the spine of the world. And they understand that the spine does not care for them, that the equilibrium they maintain is an allowance, not a right. The mountain goat navigating the sheer vertical face three hundred feet away is their kin in this: both of them are clinging to the vertical, both of them are silent, both of them are gray.

The deepest aspect of their psychology is their relationship with time. They have no future, in the narrative sense. There are no plans to expand, no projects to undertake, no generations to follow who will do differently. They are living in the epilogue, and they know it. This does not despair them. Despair requires a narrative—a sense that things should be otherwise. They have no such sense. The city has finished its story, and they are the final sentences, written in a hand so deliberate and weathered that the letters have become part of the page. They are simply *being*, with a stillness that is not passive, but achieved. It takes a rigorous, intentional quiet to maintain a place of perfect stasis. They are the monks of a religion with no deity, only the ongoing, exacting, unending work of remaining in balance with the damp and the cold and the slow, uncaring weathering of the world.
