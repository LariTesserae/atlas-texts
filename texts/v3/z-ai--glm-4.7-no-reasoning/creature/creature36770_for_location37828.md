To live in the Pulp is to never know silence, and to never know thirst—at least, not the kind water can quench.

We begin with the Builders, though "builder" is perhaps too generous a term for something that operates on the logic of a termite colony wired into the adrenal glands of a dying god. They are the architects of the bone-and-stone favela, the ones who carved dwellings into the monolithic, egg-smooth boulders and balanced megalithic slabs without mortar. They are bipedal, but only in the way a praying mantis is bipedal; their posture is a perpetual, jack-knifed crouch. Their hides are calcified, overlapping plates of keratin the exact red-brown of the packed dust, rendering them nearly invisible when they freeze. They have no eyes to speak of—only twin arrays of vibrating, feathery filaments that rise from their brow ridges, reading the soup-thick air for pressure changes, chemical signatures, and the specific, terrible frequency of an apex predator's lung-clearing growl.

They do not speak. They chitter. They communicate in a staccato of mandible-clacks and the striking of their own carapaces, a language of percussion that blends seamlessly into the endless, rhythmic typewriter-clatter of the dog-sized beetles that serve as their livestock, their pests, and their emergency rations. The Builders do not work metal; they work leverage and patience. Their hands are four-jointed, ending in hooked chisels of bone. They harvest the giant sinew of the fallen to weave their scaffolding, and they drill into the flesh of the towering fungi to tap the viscous, glycerin-clear sap. They drink it. They seal their dwellings with it. They mix it with the red dust to make a crude, sweat-curing concrete. Their existence is an act of perpetual, claustrophobic masonry. They are born on the scaffolding, they hang from the woven sinew bridges as infants so they do not fall prey to the carpet of swarming scavengers below, and they die on the scaffolding, their bodies left to tip over the edge, falling into the dim, rushing center of the pit where their chitin will be harvested by the next generation to patch a roof.

Below the Builders, in the crushing, moving carpet of the base level, live the Gluttons. These are the armored beetles, the great, scuttling detritivores. They are the urban infrastructure. They are the size of mastiffs, but built like tanks, their low-slung bodies sweeping the streets clean of organic debris. They have no malice, only an appalling, indiscriminate appetite. When a hunt concludes—when some screaming, climbing prey is dragged from its crevice by a pair of scything mandibles—the Gluttons are the audience. They cluster in a clicking, shoving mass of polished black and brown shells, waiting for the apex predator to drop a limb, a splatter of ichor, a chunk of neurotoxic fern that was torn in the struggle. Then they boil over the spot, a riot of mandibles and segmented legs, and in seconds the stone is bare, polished anew by their gizzards. They are the street-sweepers of the Pulp, and their shells provide the raw plates from which the Builders craft their hand-tools and shields.

In the fever-warm gloom above the sinew-bridges, riding the thermals of the enclosed lung, are the Skimmers. They are the false rays, the leathery, eyeless shadows that blot out the dim, perpetual twilight. Their wingspan is the width of an alleyway, and they navigate by the micro-eddies in the syrupy air, feeling the displacement of a climbing creature seven meters below. They do not hunt the swift; they hunt the weary. The air of the Pulp is their greatest weapon. It is thick with the golden, glittering pollen of the pulsing, neurotoxic ferns—a chemical weapon deployed by the flora to protect its thorny briars, but which the Skimmers have learned to weaponize. A Skimmer will glide in slow, tightening spirals above a cluster of prey, fanning the pollen with its wingbeats until the lethargy hits, until the prey’s hooked claws loosen from the polished stone, until it slips, silent and dazed, into the open air. Then, the Skimmer simply tilts, intercepts the falling body in mid-air, and swallows it whole, gliding onward with a visible, writhing lump distending its gullet.

And then there are the things that hang from the ceiling. The Drop-Bears of the Pulp, though "bear" is a grotesque understatement for something that hangs like a massive, fleshy stalactite in the roiling, feverish clouds. They are the apex predators—the Bowel-Shakers, the source of the deep, subsonic growls that vibrate through the stone dwellings and make the Builders' scaffolding shudder. They have no eyes, for there is no light worth seeing by. Their bodies are a horrific intersection of bat and sloth and ambush spider: six limbs ending in rending hooks, a face that is nothing but a gaping, circular maw lined with rotating, bone-shearing teeth. They exude a scent of rust and spice—a pheromonal marker of territory that also serves to mask the chemical signature of their descent. They do not fall; they lower themselves on thick, secretion-slicked cords of their own sinew, dropping with a sudden, horrifying acceleration. In the Pulp, you do not look up, because looking up means exposing your throat to the thing falling from the gloom. The Builders know this instinctively; they keep their heads tucked, their vibrating filaments trained ever upward, feeling for the displacement of air that signals a drop.

The prey species, the Climbers, are the nervous energy of the Pulp. They are lithe, multi-limbed things with gripping pads and desperate, over-evolved survival instincts. They live in the maze of the hewn stone dwellings, the narrow crevices too small for the Gluttons, too fast for the Skimmers' pollen. They are the ones who make the Pulp feel like rush hour—a constant, scrambling flight from surface to surface. Their blood is cool, the only cool thing in this enclosed tumor, and when they are caught, the sudden release of their ichor against the hot, dry stone creates a brief, fragrant steam. It is the only relief from the heat the Pulp ever knows. The whole ecosystem pivots on this moment: the steam of a Climber's life, sizzling on the ancient rock, providing a fleeting, mineral dampness that the glycerin-sapped vines and neurotoxic ferns immediately reach for, their thorny tendrils unfurling to drink the condensation before the dry air steals it away.

To live in the Pulp is to live inside an engine that runs on blood. There is no water. There is no weather. There is only the endless, humid recycling of flesh into stone, and stone into flesh, all of it trapped in a geological bubble, roaring in the dark.
