What lives here is not a collection of organisms, but a single, patient metabolism. 

If you look for inhabitants in the way you look for creatures in a forest—expecting eyes, expecting flight, expecting the frantic arithmetic of predator and prey—you will conclude that this place is mostly empty. You will see the ferns and think *plant*, seeing only the stillness. You will see the crustaceans and think *animal*, seeing only the glint. But to survive here is to cease being a separate thing. What lives in this pocket has surrendered its edges. 

The dominant life is the chamber itself, which has become a body.

Consider the ferns. They are not individuals. They are a single rhizomatic mass that has crept through the false soil for millennia, cloning itself into a forest of genetically identical fronds. There is no competition here because there is only one of them. It does not grow toward the light; it *is* the light's destination, a meter-high thicket of leathery dark green that has learned to drink the air itself. Its roots are not seeking nourishment—they are the circulatory system of the floor, holding the silt in place, keeping the iron from sloughing into the water. The fern does not live *in* the chamber; it is the chamber's lungs, a membrane that breathes in the machine's exhaust and sweats out the oxygen that the fungi and the unseen things consume. It has been unfurling in absolute stillness for so long that it has forgotten how to bend. It does not need to. There is no wind to test it, no herbivore to take it. It simply persists, adding a layer of lignin each century, becoming less like a plant and more like architecture.

Consider the fungi. They are the chamber's gut. They do not bloom and fruit in the way of surface mushrooms, offering themselves to the air. They are rubbery, pale, weeping things that stay close to the mulch, recycling the dead fronds almost before they have finished dying. They are the reason the water never rots. They are the reason the biomass remains constant. They are a slow, cold fire that burns the chamber's waste back into substrate, and they have been burning so long that they have merged with the sediment, becoming a half-centimeter of compacted life that is neither quite alive nor quite dead—a humus made of previous selves.

Consider the tiny, blind crustaceans in the surface film. They are nearly theoretical. A millimeter long, existing in the two-dimensional tension between water and air, they are the only fauna that has allowed itself to be seen, and even then, only as movement, never as form. They have no eyes because there is nothing to look at. They have no predators because there is nothing large enough to hunt them and nothing small enough to bother. They live in the meniscus the way ticks live on a hide—ingesting the chamber's condensation, excreting into the chamber's water, their lives so perfectly scaled to the available niche that they are practically a chemical process. They are the chamber's thoughts, tiny and repetitive and invisible, occurring just below the surface of perception.

And then there is the thing in the walls.

It is the only creature here that still retains a will, however small. It has adapted to the acoustic chaos not by becoming deaf, but by becoming indistinguishable from the machine. Its call exists at exactly the frequency of the thrumming, hiding its voice inside the vibration. It has made itself an artifact. You will never see it because it has become part of the chamber's sound. It lives in the interstices—the gaps between steel plates, the spaces between pipes—because the interstices are the only places left that are not completely saturated with the chamber's presence. It is clinging to the margins, to the thin walls between the machine's body and its own. It is the thing that remembers what it was like to be separate. It calls out into the deafening hum, and the hum answers, and it tells itself it is not alone. Perhaps it has been calling so long that it has forgotten what it is calling *for*. Perhaps it is calling to the machine itself, waiting for a response that sounds different from its own voice. It will never get one. The machine does not know it is being spoken to. The machine only thrums.

What lives here, then, is a community of the assimilated. The fern has become furniture. The fungus has become soil. The crustacean has become a chemical reaction. The thing in the walls has become a sound. They have all been here so long that they have been absorbed into the chamber's final form. They are not living *in* an environment; they *are* the environment, expressing itself in slow, wet increments.

They do not notice you because you are still an event. You still have edges. You still displace the water, still breathe in gulps, still cast a shadow in a place that has forgotten what shadows are. You are a sudden, temporary disequilibrium—a warm body moving through a space that has perfected the art of not moving. To them—collectively, metabolically—you are not a presence. You are a question the chamber does not have the vocabulary to answer, an interruption too brief and too loud to be anything but noise.

When you leave, and you pull the hatch closed behind you, the thing in the walls will hear the seal engage. It will feel the vibration shift, just slightly, as the last gradient disappears. And then it will go back to calling, hiding its voice inside the machine, waiting in the interstices for something that will never arrive, in a room that will never change, in a darkness that has been wet and warm and loud since before the word *dark* meant anything at all.
