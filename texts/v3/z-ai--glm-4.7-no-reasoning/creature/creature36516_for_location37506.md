The things that live here are not born of the sun; they are born of the heat and the hum. They are creatures of the interstice, adapted to a world that is equal parts geology and machine, biology and industry.

At the bottom of the food chain, where the iron-rich mud meets the air, live the **Osmotic Grazers**. These are the tundra-grubs, massive, segmented tubes of chitin that slide through the saturated shale like thick, blind sausages. They are deaf to the wind, for they have no ears, but their undersides are lined with thousands of microscopic cilia that vibrate in harmony with the subsonic thrum of the engines below. They navigate by the shaking of the earth, following the resonance of the warm-water vents. They move in solemn, single-file processions, their front ends agape, rasping tongues scraping the dark, bruise-colored algae from the rocks. When a vent exhales its pneumatic *chasssh*, the grazers pause, their bristled flanks trembling, soaking the waste-heat into their cold-blooded systems before resuming their slow, suctioning march.

Crawling over the backs of the grazers, and infesting the scree like a moving carpet, are the **Salt-Scavengers**—the air beetles. They are hieroglyphs of survival, their shells etched by the abrasive wind into intricate, geometric patterns that mimic the fractured slate beneath them. They possess a unique, metallic biology; their blood is rich in copper, leached from the minerals they consume, giving them a faint, bio-electric charge. When they swarm, the air around them crackles with static. They feed on the oxidized runoff, clustering around the seeping iron stains like flies on an open wound, mandibles grinding the rust into paste. Their wings are vestigial, useless nubs, for the air here is too thin to lift anything that carries armor. Instead, they rely on the thermal updrafts created by the hidden machines, ballooning into the sky on pockets of warm industrial exhaust to drift to new feeding grounds.

Patrolling the perimeter of the grub herds are the **Silencers**—the high-striding stalkers. They are elegant horrors, possessed of spindly, elongated limbs that allow them to stride over the squelching mud without sinking. Their bodies are wrapped in oily, iridescent feathers that repel water and deaden sound, allowing them to move without the tell-tale crunch of scree. Their eyes are massive, pale orbs that gather every photon of the dim, anemic light, seeing the world in slow-motion monochrome. They do not hunt by speed, but by vibration; they stand perfectly still, waiting for the rhythmic clicking of a grub to break syncopation, then strike with a swift, surgical puncture to the soft segment between armor plates. They are the only creatures here that seem to fear the hum of the deep machines, often pausing to tilt their heads, listening anxiously to the bedrock, as if aware that the world they walk upon is being digested by something far larger than themselves.

Above them all, the sky belongs to the **Wind-Eaters**. The fulmars and skuas here have evolved beyond mere flight. They are creatures of the pressure differential, their wings spanning immense distances to catch the scant currents of the upper atmosphere. Their chests are reinforced with keels of bone that act as resonance chambers, amplifying their harsh, scraping calls so that they can communicate over miles of empty, dim plateau. They ride the thermals generated by the cooling vents of the underground machinery, circling the "hotspots" invisible to the eye. When they dive, they fold into aerodynamic bullets, plummeting through the thick cloud cover to snatch the fat, metallic beetles from the rocks. They are indifferent to the dampness, their plumage coated in a hydrophobic oil secreted from a gland near the tail, which smells faintly of ozone and machine grease.

And then there is the life that is not life, yet moves with purpose: the **Internal Ecology** of the machines themselves. The subterranean engines, though automated, have acquired a parasitic ecosystem. Within the warm, dark labyrinth of the bore-holes, colonies of **Thermal-Fungi** bloom. They are mats of gray, fiber-like mold that thrive on the heat of the turbines and the trace lubricants that mist the air. They feed on the rubber seals and the insulation of the wiring, a slow, silent corrosion that the machines cannot scrape away. In turn, **Vent-Crushers**—pale, eyeless crabs that have adapted to the zero-light, high-pressure interior of the mountain—scurry along the intake pipes, feeding on the fungi. They are completely blind, their shells translucent and soft, warmed by the pulse of the engine. When a machine vents its steam, it occasionally ejects these crabs onto the surface, where they freeze instantly into statues of ice, picked at by the birds before the sun—or the lack of it—can melt them away.

This is a hierarchy of vibration and heat. Every creature here is an interpreter of the mountain’s trembling. They live in the rhythm of the pump, the grinding of the bore, the hiss of the vent. They are the incidental biology of a world that is being manufactured, feeding on the overflow of a process that cares nothing for them, thriving in the warm, wet shadow of the earth’s incomplete bones.
