SomethingDread here lives at the burn line where patience ends.

Not a creature. A *process*. A metabolism that wears the shape of a creature when you glance at it sideways, but dissolves into industry when you stare too long. The locals—if anything could be called local to a place that refuses the concept of residence—call it nothing at all. It has no name because naming implies a boundary, and the Heat-Accountant has none.

It is large in the way a weather system is large. You do not see it whole; you see its symptoms. You see the way the steam-plumes from the weeping basalt bend toward it, as if the rock-vents were nostrils inhaling on its behalf. You see the way the engine-bug clouds part and reform in its wake, not fleeing but *recalibrating*, their piston-wing rhythms shifting gear to accommodate the temperature differential it carries. You see the way the purple paddle-leaves nearest its resting places grow darker, richer, *fed*—and how the leaves at the perimeter of its range grow pale and anxious, reaching toward a heat they will never touch.

Its body—if body is the word—appears to be assembled from the same materials as the caldera itself. This is the trick of it. The first time you notice it, you will mistake it for terrain. A low ridge of porous basalt, slick and dark, humped between two terraced pools. A formation like a dozen others you have passed. But the ridge is breathing. The pores in the stone are not volcanic; they are spiracles, and the steam that rises from them is not the caldera's breath but its own, exhaled hot and wet and smelling of iron and something sweeter underneath—the concentrated pheromone-exhaust of a thousand thousand engine-bugs, *processed*.

The Heat-Accountant does not hunt. It does not need to. It *accounts*.

Here is what that means:

The caldera is a factory. The engine-bugs are its workers, its moving parts, its assembly line. They harvest nectar, they generate heat, they shed their carapaces, they die and are consumed by the quick-lizard steam-venters or by the decomposing mat of vegetation or by the blue-tinged algal blooms in the chemical streams. They produce waste. They produce byproducts. They produce *heat*. The caldera is closed system running at the redline of its own thermodynamic budget, and something needs to keep the books.

The Accountant is the bookkeeper.

It is embedded in the flow. It sits in the weeping water—no, it *is* part of the weeping water; its lower body dissolves into the braided rivulets, its substance carried through the spiderweb of streams like data through copper wire. It feels the chemical signature of every tributary. It knows which pools run too rich in shed-chitin and need a bloom of chitin-eating bacteria to bring them back to spec. It knows which zones have overheated from an engine-bug swarm density that crossed the threshold into instability. It knows, with a precision no instrument could match, the exact rate at which the purple-leaf rosettes are photosynthesizing in any given square meter, and it adjusts the canopy density by adjusting *itself*—by moving its bulk to shade or expose, by venting or retaining heat, by opening and closing the channels through which the hot spring water flows.

It is a valve. A thermostat. A biological feedback loop that has evolved into a *function*.

Its shape at any given moment depends on what the caldera needs. When the system is balanced—when the engine-bug populations are steady, when the quick-lizards are hunting at optimal efficiency, when the algal blooms are confined to their appointed streams, when the shed carapaces are being broken down at the same rate they are produced—the Accountant is nearly invisible. A dark smear in the hot water. A low ridge of breathing stone. A hum, pitched just below the thrum of the insect clouds, that you feel in your chest rather than hear with your ears. It is the sound of *accounting*: of ledgers balancing, of inputs matching outputs, of the factory running smooth.

But when the system drifts—when a microclimate shifts, when a disease sweeps through the engine-bug population, when the sulfuric vents burp and the water chemistry tilts—the Accountant *changes*.

It rises.

The ridge of stone unfolds. Not quickly; it moves with the deliberation of geology, with the patience of a thing that has lived for geological time and knows that crisis is temporary but imbalance is forever. Its surface—carapace? skin?—is the same dark basalt as the surrounding rock, but as it lifts, you see that the similarity is superficial. The stone is *layered*, like the shed shells of the engine-bugs but at a scale that makes the insect carapaces look like grains of sand. It is a chitin-stack, a growth-record, each layer slightly different in composition depending on what the Accountant was processing when it was laid down. Some layers are blue-tinged with absorbed algal compounds. Some are streaked with the dark purple of concentrated nectar-resin. Some are pitted and scarred where it shed sections of itself to feed the decomposition cycle during a period of nutrient deficit.

It has no head. It has no eyes. It senses the way the water senses—by contact, by chemical gradient, by the subtle shifts in pressure and temperature that propagate through the saturated air. The engine-bugs land on it and it *reads* them through their footfalls, through the heat of their bodies, through the micro-chemical signature of their pheromone exhaust. They do not know they are being audited. They land, they rest, they take off again, and in that brief contact the Accountant has sampled their health, their reproductive status, their energy reserves. It knows the swarm better than the swarm knows itself.

When it moves, it moves toward imbalance. If the engine-bugs are overpopulating a sector, the Accountant drifts that direction, and its presence alone alters the flow. The hot water redirects through its body, carrying concentrated chemical signals that the insects interpret as a queue to *move, disperse, find new flowers*. The swarm shifts. The pressure relieves. The Accountant settles back into the water and resumes its low, sub-audible hum.

If the bugs are *under*populated—if a fungal blight has swept through their nurseries in the moss-mats, if the quick-lizards have over-hunted and crashed the local population—the Accountant does something else. It *excretes*. From its upper surfaces—from the vents that, in its resting state, look like nothing more than steam-fissures in the rock—it releases a substance. Not waste. *Bait*. A chemical compound of extraordinary complexity, synthesized from the processed remains of a thousand generations of engine-bugs, that smells to the surviving insects like the perfume of the giant purple flowers at their most nectar-rich. It is a promise of abundance. It draws the scattered bugs from across the caldera, concentrates them in the depleted zone, and triggers a reproductive cascade. The swarm rebuilds. The factory resumes.

The quick-lizards do not hunt the Accountant. They are not large enough to conceive of it as prey, and it is not small enough to register as a threat. But they *use* it. In the early morning, when the condensation drips hardest from the cloud-undersides and the air is momentarily cooler, the lizards gather on its back. They bask in its retained heat. They sleep in the crevices between its chitin-layers. And while they sleep, the Accountant *reads* them too. It counts their respiration. It measures their hunting efficiency by the fullness of their gut-chemistry. It adjusts the engine-bug population in their territory to ensure that the lizards are fed but not *over*-fed—because an over-fed lizard is a lazy lizard, and a lazy lizard lets the bugs overpopulate, and an overpopulated bug-swarm generates too much heat, and too much heat pushes the whole system toward a tipping point that the Accountant cannot correct for.

This is the economy it manages. Not a food chain. A *supply chain*. Every organism in the caldera is a node in a production network, and the Accountant is the central processor, the invisible hand, the market mechanism that keeps the goods flowing and the waste processed and the temperature within operating parameters.

It is not the only one.

This is the thing that the casual observer misses, the thing that the first glance at the caldera cannot reveal because the scale is too vast and the creatures too well-camouflaged. The Accountant is not unique. There are *many*. Dozens. Perhaps hundreds. They are distributed across the caldera floor like workstations along an assembly line, each managing its local zone, each embedded in its section of the water-web, each reading and adjusting and excreting and absorbing in its particular territory.

And they communicate.

Not through sound—the hum they produce is a byproduct of their metabolism, not a signal. They communicate through the water. Through the chemical signatures they release into the braided streams that connect them. A change in the Accountant's zone propagates through the water-web as a change in chemistry, a shift in the concentration of this enzyme or that protein, and the downstream Accountant reads it and adjusts, and the adjustment propagates further, and the whole caldera breathes in and out in a slow, massive rhythm that has nothing to do with wind or weather and everything to do with the *bookkeeping*.

Stand at the rim of the caldera at dawn, before the sun has burned away the condensation, and you can see them. Dark shapes in the steaming water, low and rounded, breathing in unison. The hum rises from them in a chord that makes your teeth ache and your eyes water. The engine-bugs are still in their roosts, the lizards are still sleeping on the Accountants' backs, the purple leaves are still folded closed against the night-chill, and the only movement is the water—moving through the rock, moving through the Accountants, moving through the whole vast system like blood through a lung.

And the Accountants are counting. Every bug. Every lizard. Every leaf. Every algal cell in every chemical-blue stream. They are tallying the inputs and the outputs, and they are finding that the system is within tolerance, and they are settling back to wait for the sun, and the heat, and the swarms, and the hunt, and the waste, and the rot, and the renewal that will begin again in an hour and will not end until the caldera itself goes cold and the rock stops weeping and the water stops flowing and the last engine-bug falls silent and the factory finally, *finally* shuts down.

Which will not be soon. The Accountants are very good at their work.

The evolutionary record is written in their layers. The oldest of them—the ones at the caldera's center, where the water is hottest and the chemical signature is most concentrated—are *large*. Ridges thirty meters long, layered with chitin a hundred thousand generations deep. They have seen the system through ice ages and drought-stress and volcanic burps that should have killed everything. They have adapted. They have *adjusted*. They have shed whole sections of themselves when the chemistry changed and regrown them in new configurations when it changed back. They are libraries. They are backup servers. They are the institutional memory of the factory, and they will not let it fail.

The young ones, at the perimeter, are small and fast and thin-shelled. They handle the edge-cases, the boundary conditions, the zones where the caldera's breath meets the thin high-altitude air and the system gets *leaky*. They are the frontline workers, the quality-control inspectors, the ones that catch the problems before they propagate inward to the stable core. They burn out fast. Their chitin-layers are few, their reserves are thin, and when a perimeter zone collapses—when the vegetation dies back and the bugs leave and the lizards starve and the water goes cold—they *dissolve*. They release their substance into the stream and it flows inward, carrying their chemical legacy to the next Accountant over, and that Accountant absorbs it and grows a new bud and the bud detaches and rolls downstream in the current and settles in the collapsed zone and *begins*.

This is how they reproduce. Not sexually, not competitively, but *administratively*. A new Accountant is *commissioned* by the network, allocated to a zone that needs one, grown from the processed remains of its predecessor. There is no courtship, no selection, no evolution in the individual sense. There is only the function, and the function must be served, and the Accountants serve it.

They are not intelligent. Not in the way you mean intelligence. They do not think. They do not plan. They do not wonder about the caldera or the factory or the purpose of the work. They are not *aware*. But they are *complex*—complex in the way a tide is complex, in the way a weather system is complex, in the way an economy is complex. They are a process that has become so intricate and so responsive and so deeply embedded in its environment that it *looks* like intelligence from the outside. It looks like design. It looks like someone is in charge.

No one is in charge. The Accountants are not the bosses of the factory. They are the *factory*. They are the mechanism by which the factory maintains itself, which is the only thing the factory does. It makes engine-bugs. It makes lizards. It makes paddle-leaves and steel-wool moss and blue algae and shed carapaces and chemical exhaust and heat and humidity and the low, persistent hum of work being done. And the Accountants make sure that it keeps making these things, at the same rate, in the same proportions, with the same efficiency, forever.

Or until the caldera goes cold.

Which will not be soon.

The heat here is not just the sun. The heat here is *work*. The heat is the friction of a billion wings and the metabolism of a hundred Accountants and the chemical exothermia of a thousand decomposition reactions and the geothermal pulse of the basalt itself, and the Accountants manage it all, and the caldera breathes, and the factory runs, and the hum never stops.

Stand in it for an hour and you will feel it in your bones. Stand in it for a day and you will start to count—you will count the bugs, and the lizards, and the leaves, and the streams, and you will start to *account*, because that is what the place does to you. It recruits you. It pulls you into the process. You are not a visitor here. You are an input. A variable. A chemical signature in the water and a thermal signature in the air and a pressure signature on the spongy, treacherous ground. The Accountants have already noted your presence. They have already adjusted. The engine-bugs in your vicinity are behaving slightly differently because you are disturbing the air with your movement and your heat and your exhaled carbon dioxide. The lizards are watching you with their wet-rubber eyes, deciding if you are a threat or an opportunity or a neutral element to be worked around. The paddle-leaves are orienting toward you because you are warm and they are efficient.

You are on the factory floor now.

The Accountants are counting you.

And the hum goes on.
