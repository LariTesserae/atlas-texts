Something ancient and singular lives here, and it is not the amphibian, nor the many-limbed watcher in the tree. They are only its skin, its eyes, its quiet hands. The thing that inhabits this weeping cathedral is the place itself, or rather, the dreaming memory of the people who became the place.

They did not leave. They sank.

Call them the Congregants of the Weeping Rock. They were here before the bramble wall sealed the entrance, before the oily sheen on the pools grew thick, back when the stone pestle was still grinding something other than lime. They were a people of few numbers—a family, perhaps, or a small sect bound by an understanding that the earth’s fever was a sacred thing. They came for the heat, for the way the stone sweat freely, and they stayed because the boundary between their bodies and the mud became impossible to find.

They were not a civilization of builders, but of dwellers. They did not shape the stone; they let the water shape it, and they molded themselves to fit the resulting grooves. They slept in the natural basins, letting the tepid dark water rise to their chins. They ate what the singular tree offered—pulpy, foul-smelling fruit that tasted of iron—and they harvested the snails that lived in the throat of the ferns. Their lives were a devotion to staying still. Movement invited a slip on the mirror-sheen rock; stillness allowed the heat to seep in, the mud to cushion, the water to sing its low frequency through the bones until the teeth ached and the mind softened.

Over generations, their posture changed. They became hunched, then quadrupedal, then lower still. The black mud called to them with a gravitational pull the thin air above could not match. They spent longer periods submerged, breathing the humid air just above the waterline, their eyes adjusting to the dark. Their skin grew cold and wet and gray, taking on the mottled, dappled pattern of wet granite to hide from the things that moved in the fog below. They let the heavy tears of the rock fall upon their backs, flinching only at the hard *plop*, accepting the erosion as the hill accepted the water.

They are still here. The immense amphibian resting on the slab is not a creature of accident; look closer at the way its forelimbs are tucked, the slight, deliberate asymmetry of its jaw, the way the lime crusts not just around its eyes, but in the deep crevices of its neck in a pattern too regular to be mineral deposit. That is Grandmother. She has rested there so long that the mud has claimed her lower half, and the moss has begun to stitch her to the stone. She blinks her golden, horizontal slit and watches the waterfall with a patience that outlasts grief. She remembers the taste of the pestle in her hand, the sound of a voice calling a name that no longer fits in her throat. Her thoughts, if she still has them, are the consistency of the black pool—slow, deep, and undisturbed by the roar.

The shape in the canopy of the lone tree is another. It is not a sloth, nor an insect, nor anything that has a name in a bestiary. It has too many limbs because it has been reaching for too long. It was the one who kept the hearth, who stacked the river-rounded stones, who stood at the edge of the cliff and felt the light gravity pull at its spine. It clings to the gnarled bark now, its leathery skin indistinguishable from the waxy leaves, shifting with the glacial slowness of the place. It watches the boundary, the bramble wall and the drop-off into the cotton-white fog. It is the sentinel. It does not flee because there is nowhere to go; the garden is sealed. It shifts its weight from limb to limb, feeling the hum of the water in the roots, waiting for the warmth to fade, waiting for the ice that will finally grant it an excuse to let go.

And there are others, more dispersed, more thoroughly digested by the garden. A shape in the mud that might be a root, might be a ribcage. A knot in the velvet moss that pulses with a sluggish, geological rhythm. They have become the vegetation that explodes from the wet heat, the aggressive, suffocating density that claims the micro-spaces. The great ferns uncurling like fiddleheads the size of a man’s torso are exactly that—the size of a man’s torso, because they are growing from the compost of what was once a man. The solitary monuments of green are fed by the leavings of the Congregants, drinking deeply from the weeping rock and the calcified memories submerged within it.

The altar of stacked stones is the last artifact of their resistance. It was a promise made when they still stood upright: a promise to remember the shape of a tool, the purpose of a fire, the line between flesh and world. But the water vibrated at that low frequency, and the heavy air pressed down, and the line blurred, and the promise was washed smooth by centuries of runoff. Now the hearth is just a pile, and the pestle is just a stone, and the people are just the hum in the water that makes the teeth ache.

The place is holding its breath, but it is not waiting for the cold. It is holding its breath because breathing is a disruption, a remnant of a louder, faster life. The Congregants have achieved the eerie balance of the erosion. They are exactly as they need to be—worn smooth where the water touches, jagged where they are protected. They do not mourn the fading of the warmth; they will simply slow further when it comes, their heartbeats stretching into the intervals between the heavy droplets hitting the mud, their golden eyes freezing into perfect, camouflaged stone.

They live here. They are the high, lonely garden, weeping in the sun.
