They are a people of the slow beat and the iron lung, shaped by the plateau as surely as the moss is shaped by the damp. To call them "human" is accurate only in the anatomical sense; they have become something else—something geological and thermal.

Physically, they are dense. You would expect starvation at this altitude, but there is a heaviness to them, a gravity in their movements that suggests their bones have grown thick to anchor them against the thin air and the savage winds. They are short of stature, compact and barrel-chested, evolved—or perhaps engineered—over generations to maximize oxygen intake. Their chests seem permanently expanded, ribs flared wide to house lungs that process the thin, mist-choked air with ruthless efficiency. Their skin is the color of the wet stone: a pale, sallow gray, often flushed with the ruddy hue of constant exposure to the heat of the foundries. It is a tough, leathery hide, less prone to chapping than to the subtle, perpetual stain of iron oxide. The older ones look like statues carved from rusted meteorites—their hands and faces permanently etched with the orange dust of the machines, the pigment settled into the lines of their palms and the creases of their eyes until it seems part of their flesh.

They dress in layers of felt and oil-treated canvas, materials that absorb the constant moisture rather than repelling it. There is no attempt at bright color; their garments are the black of deep moss, the gray of rain clouds, and the brown of wet earth. They wear heavy-soled boots with iron rings riveted into the heels, so that when they walk the cobblestones, they do not so much tread as *clack*—a sharp, percussive sound that cuts through the damp silence. You hear them coming long before you see them, the rhythmic clicking of their metal-shod feet echoing in the narrow crevices like the tapping of a blind man’s cane.

Their eyes are adapted to the perpetual twilight. The pupils are huge, swallowing the iris, dark pools that reflect the faint amber glow of the streetlamps and the dying arc-weld light of the factories. They possess a terrifying night vision, but in direct sunlight—a rarity here, seen only during the clearest moments of the noon—they are nearly blind, squinting and pained, pulling heavy hoods forward to shield their gaze. They navigate by vibration and temperature as much as by sight.

Culturally, they are defined by the "Great Metabolism." They do not view the city as a place they built; they view it as an organism they maintain. The rust is not an enemy to be defeated, but a predator to be fed. Their entire existence is a Sisyphean cycle of fabrication and decay. They are obsessed with the integrity of the seal. Because the air is thin and cold, the preservation of heat is a sacred law. A draft is a sin; a leaking steam valve is a crisis. Their social hierarchy is based on thermal efficiency: those who tend the boilers and the deep engines are the priesthood of the plateau, guarding the subterranean heart that keeps the blood of the city from freezing.

Their language is a guttural, dialect-heavy mix of ancient tongue and industrial onomatopoeia. They do not shout; the damp air swallows sound. Instead, they speak in low, thrumming registers, utilizing the resonance of the stone walls to carry their voices. A conversation in the alleyways sounds like the muttering of the plumbing itself—hisses, pops, and rhythmic grunts. They have a thousand words for different states of wetness: for the mist that clings, for the rain that smears, for the sweat that condenses on cold iron, and for the dangerous slick of black ice that forms when the factory exhaust meets the night air.

They are solitary yet crowded. Because the buildings share walls and heat, privacy is physical, not auditory. A family might live in a single room carved out of the granite, sleeping on pallets suspended over warm machinery to stay alive. They sleep in shifts, mirroring the rhythm of the stamping presses below. When the machines pulse, they pulse; when the engines pause for cooling, the city falls into a synchronized, exhausted slumber.

Their diet reflects the ecosystem. There are no crops here; the soil is too shallow and the sun too weak. They are tenders of fungus and keepers of the vent-worms. Massive, humid caverns beneath the factory floors serve as mushroom farms, growing fleshy, gray-capped fungi the size of dinner plates in the warmth of the exhaust vents. The worms—those pale, finger-thick creatures found in the grates—are not vermin to them, but protein. They harvest them with iron tongs, roasting them over small coal burners until they crunch like crisp pork. They drink water that has been filtered through layers of charcoal and moss, tasting faintly of peat and iron.

Perhaps the strangest aspect of their psychology is their relationship with the "Grade." They live on a tabletop mountain, but they possess a primal fear of the edge. They know that the world climbs up to them, but they never look down. The city is built inward, away from the cliffs that drop into the void. To them, the center of the city is the center of the universe—a place of maximum safety, surrounded by the breathing engines. The edges are where the mist thickens into nothingness, where the wind screams not from the sky, but from the abyss.

They are a people holding their breath. They live in the throat of the mountain, exhaling steam and iron, waiting for the moment the rust finally wins. Until then, they scrape the oxidation, replace the rivet, and listen to the heartbeat of the machine, content in their warm, damp, green-gray purgatory at the roof of the world.
