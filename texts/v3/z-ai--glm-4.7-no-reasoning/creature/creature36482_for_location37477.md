They are not the masters of this place, for there are no masters here; they are the occupants of the spaces between the drops, the inheritors of a world that has decided to rest. In the Old Flat, where the sun hangs ripe and the air hangs heavy, life does not soar or scurry so much as it *percolates*. It is a damp, teeming insistence, a biological froth bubbling up through the geological silence.

The most visible architects of this saturation are the great stone-carpenters. Pale, monstrous tortoises, their shells sculpted into domes that mimic the very whalebacks of the landscape, heave themselves through the muck. They move with the agonizing slowness of the tectonic plates themselves, pushing aside the spongy moss like living plows. Their shells are not the bright, clean greens of younger worlds, but are blackened and pitted, velvet-coated with a thick, slick fur of algae and lichen. To look at one is to see a mobile island, a walking micro-continent complete with its own ecology of weeping moss and tiny, skittering beetles. They are silent observers, blinking heavy eyes at a sun that refuses to set, their presence marked only by the slow, rhythmic *crinkle-squelch* as they navigate the shallow cups of stone.

Beneath the canopy of the iron-barked corkscrew trees, in the shadows where the bruised-purple leaves trap the humidity, dwell the rot-eaters. These are the sloth-things, pale and spectral felines without the sharpness of a predator. They have evolved away from speed and venom, for there is nowhere to run and nothing to fight but the mud. Their fur is long and dragging, soaking up the water until they look like animated mops, heavy with the weight of the air. They move in a slow drift, a blur of grey against the grey rock, feeding on the soft, fungal growths that pillowed on the damp stone. Their eyes are large and watery, adapted to the dim, refracted light of the perpetual haze, and they watch the world with a mild, amphibious disinterest. They do not stalk; they simply amble into the presence of their meal and take it, as if the environment offers it up in exchange for their quiet company.

But it is the hidden infrastructure that truly owns the basin. If you were to peel back the wet, opaque layer of the standing water, the bottom would seem to writhe. The mud is alive. It is a churning city of crustaceans and worms, a subterranean metropolis where the skyscrapers are the splayed roots of the corkscrew trees. Millions of freshwater crabs, their shells the color of bruises, march in endless lines across the submerged rock floors. They are the janitors of the Old Flat, the tireless processors of decay, clicking their claws in a constant, wet Morse code that vibrates up through the soles of one’s boots. They filter the water, they aerate the muck, and in their endless abundance, they form the living foundation of the food chain.

Above them, claiming the thin layer of air between mud and sky, are heavy-bodied birds that have forgotten how to be graceful. They are not the sleek herons of other lands; they are lumbering, loud things with feathers that absorb water rather than shed it, weighting them down so they fly low, skimming the surface like skipping stones. Their legs are long and skeletal, designed to wade through the shallow saucers of the land. They stand motionless for hours, statues of grey and ochre, their long necks coiled like springs, waiting for the flash of a silver minnow. When they strike, it is a sudden, violent punctuation in a sentence of slow commas, a splash that shatters the mirror of a puddle and sends ripples rolling out to meet the horizon.

And then there is the noise. It is the sound of the air itself being rent. The cicadas here are not insects; they are a force of nature. They cling to the smooth flanks of the whalebacks, buried in the slick, dark streaks of algae, their bodies vibrating with such intensity that the rocks seem to hum in sympathy. They are the electric current of the landscape, invisible but palpable, a sonic wall that presses against the skin.

But the true kings of the cacophony are the Bullfrogs. They are the size of dinner plates, indeed, ancient and bloated beings that sit in the centers of the permanent puddles like lords in their keeps. Their skin is the texture of wet sandpaper, tough and warty, absorbing the oxygen directly from the water. When one calls, it is a deep, resonant *bonk* that feels like it should come from a brass instrument rather than a throat. It echoes off the stone hills, bouncing from whaleback to whaleback, triggering a chain reaction—a dialogue of bass notes that rolls across the Old Flat like thunder. They do not fear; they merely proclaim their existence, loud enough to be heard over the geology.

In the spaces between these grander forms, the air is filled with the floating detritus of life. Dragonflies with wings like stained glass hover in the warm, woolen air, their movements frantic and blurred compared to the slow rock around them. They are the frantic punctuation marks, the only things that seem to understand the concept of "now" in a place that operates on "forever." They mate and die in the span of a single afternoon, their falling bodies adding to the rich, black sludge that feeds the roots of the iron trees.

There is nothing human here. The mind cannot find a foothold of industry, no fence post or crumbling wall to anchor the imagination. There is only the wet breath of the earth and the things that breathe with it. The lives here are cycles of eating, floating, and humming, played out on a stage of smooth grey stone under a heavy, patient sun. They are the children of the sogginess, creatures that have learned that to survive is to yield, to become soft, and to let the water hold you up.
