To live in the Open Works is to be a contradiction: one must be slow enough to outlast eternity, yet active enough to process the ceaseless death of the world. There are no hearts beating here, only pumps and pistons; no lungs filling with air, only bellows drawing in the heavy, ozone-tainted draft. The inhabitants are not visitors in this corrosion; they are its immune system, its parasites, and its inheritors.

They are the Gear-Stalkers.

At a distance, they are almost invisible, a trick of the low-light environment. They stand taller than a man, perhaps seven or eight feet at the shoulder, but they possess no rigid, distinct silhouette. Their bodies are conglomerations of scavenged plate and iron, fused together by a biology that understands metallurgy better than anatomy. Their "skin" is not living tissue, but a carapace of interlocking steel scales, harvested from the dissolving conveyor belts and riveted to a sub-dermal layer of dense, fibrous muscle that feels like braided cable to the touch.

They move with a disjointed, hydraulic grace. Their legs are reverse-jointed assemblies of rusted pistons and bone, the joints lubricated by the same black, brackish water that weeps from the ceiling. When they walk, the sound is lost in the 2.6 drone of the facility—a heavy, rhythmic clanking that mimics the ambient machinery, allowing them to walk unseen amidst the grinding gears. They do not scurry; they march. Their motion is a series of locking and unlocking joints, a mechanical efficiency that wastes no energy.

Their heads are perhaps the most unsettling aspect of their physiology. Resembling a diving bell or a blast shield, the face is a smooth, curved plate of lead-infused glass, dark and reflective. They do not see in the visible spectrum. The sodium-violet arcs that crawl across the ceiling are useless to them. Instead, they possess thermal pits and vibration-sensitive tendrils that hang from their lower jaw like a beard of copper wires. They "hear" the shape of the world, sensing the stress fractures in the girders and the flow of electricity through the dormant circuits.

Because the predation index is 0.4, they do not hunt flesh. They hunt function. They are the maintenance crew of a ruin that refuses to die.

Their primary food source is the bioluminescent fungus that clings to the warm underbellies of the pipe bundles. The Gear-Stalkers use long, prehensile tongues— serrated and tipped with a solvent that can melt through inch-thick plating—to scrape the glowing mold from the pipes. In consuming it, they ingest the rare earth metals and industrial lubricants that fuel their own internal furnaces. One can often see them in the distance, clustered around the thicker pipes like grazing cattle, their glass faces illuminated from within by the ghostly violet glow of their digestion.

They are social, but in the way of components in a machine. They communicate not through vocal chords—which would be drowned out by the tectonic noise—but through a complex language of acoustic knocks and ground vibrations. They will strike a metal girder with a specialized limb, sending a low-frequency pulse through the structure that can be detected for miles. A "conversation" looks like two statues rhythmically drumming on the world, a synchronized tapping that travels through the rust and steel.

Reproduction is a matter of industry. There are no sexes, only fabricators. When a Gear-Stalker reaches the end of its operational cycle—when the rust has penetrated too deep into the joints—it will instinctively seek out one of the "calcification vats," the great chemical silos that have become hills of slag. There, it will dismantle itself, feeding its usable alloys and organic cores into the vat’s intake valves. Weeks later, the vat—a biological reactor repurposed from industrial waste—will "birth" a new, smaller unit. The young are born with a pristine, galvanized shine, which dulls to the familiar orange and brown within days as the damp air oxidizes them.

Then there are the Scavengers, smaller, skittering things that exist in the spaces between the floorboards. Where the Gear-Stalkers are the shepherds of the fungus, the Scavengers are the insects of the rot. They are crab-like entities, no larger than a dog, composed entirely of the translucent, fiber-optic vines mentioned in the ecology reports. They have burrowed into the corpses of ancient maintenance drones, animating the rusted shells with their own writhing, nervous systems. They scuttle along the underside of the grating, safe from the Stalkers, feeding on the mineral runoff and the tiny flakes of iron that rain down like snow.

Finally, there is the entity known only as the Hum. It is debatable if it is alive, but the score of 1.8 weirdness suggests it possesses agency. The Hum is a mass of magnetic consciousness that drifts through the upper galleries, where the sodium-violet arcs are strongest. It has no body. It is a storm of static electricity and ferrous dust, held together by a magnetic field generated by the tectonic machinery. It "eats" by dismantling anything that crosses its path, atomizing metal and absorbing the energy. The Gear-Stalkers freeze when the Hum passes, locking their joints and dimming their internal bioluminescence, mimicking the statues of slag that populate the landscape until the storm drifts on.

In this place, the water level is high, the darkness is thick, and the noise is absolute. The inhabitants are forged from these very pressures. They are the children of the Open Works, beings of iron and oil, tending to a world that is no longer building anything, simply enduring—groaning, weeping, and living on in the crushing dark.
