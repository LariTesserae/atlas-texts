To dwell here is to be part of the water cycle; the inhabitants do not so much live within the plateau as constitute its moisture. They are soft things, mostly, devoid of the rigidity that light imposes on biology. In the absence of sun, the skeleton is a redundancy, an evolutionary expense that has been trimmed away over the epochs. What moves through the warm, dark air is muscle and fluid, contained within skins that feel like the velvet of the darkness itself—smooth, pliable, yielding to the touch.

The largest of them are the floaters, vast and sentient bladders of gas and warm gelatin, drifting through the blackness like islands in an archipelago. They have no distinct shape; they are simply *mass*, contorting slowly to fit between the ancient, ribbed structures. You do not see them, but you hear their passage—a sound like heavy canvas rubbing against stone, followed by the wet sigh of air displaced. They move by osmosis and diffusion, grazing on the mists that condense on the artifacts. Their internal chemistry is a mirror of the plateau’s geology: slow, heat-driven reactions that generate a low, thrumming vibration, the subsonic heartbeat that underpins the entire soundscape. They are the whales of the deep earth, eyeless and mouthless, absorbing sustenance through their entire surface area. To touch one is to feel a fever-hot bag of water, pulsing with a glacial rhythm, indifferent to your intrusion.

Beneath these leviathans, in the tangled, knee-deep mats of the spongy "soil," live the treaders. These are the things that brush against your legs, soft and multitude. They occupy the niche of the grazer, yet they possess no jaws. Imagine a six-pointed star made of wet dough, the width of a dinner plate, that contracts and expands to walk. They have no legs, only hydraulic protrusions that extend and retract, squeezing their bodies through the dense mesh of the vegetation. They feed by rasping—their surfaces are coated in a microscopic roughness, like sandpaper made of velvet, which they use to scour the nutrient-rich slime from the porous rocks. They travel in herds that feel like a single fluid entity; when you stand still, they flow around your boots like parting water, their movements silent save for the faint *hiss* of friction against wet leaves.

The noise—the roar that defines the dark—belongs to the shakers. They are the engineers of this invisible world, clustered around the fluted structures like acolytes at an altar. They resemble insects, if insects were grown from the same soft clay as the treaders. They have carapaces, but these are flexible, made of chitin that has been softened by the eternal humidity until it resembles tough leather. They cling to the vertical surfaces of the ancient machines, their long, multi-jointed limbs anchored securely. Their bodies are equipped with stridulating organs, not the tiny chirps of crickets, but massive, resonating plates that run the length of their abdomens. By flexing their thoraxes, they scrape these plates together, producing the deafening, continuous drone that vibrates in your chest. It is not a song of mating or territory; it is a song of fuel. The vibrations shake loose microscopic particles from the artifact walls, a harvest of synthetic dust that the shakers sweep into filter-mouths with their front legs. They are eating the machines, molecule by molecule, a process so slow that the machines will outlast them by a million years.

And then there are the silk-spinners, the architects of the claustrophobia. They hang in the void between the structures, unseen until you walk into their webs. But these are not sticky webs; they are slick and strong, woven from a secretion that hardens into a bioplastic resembling fishing line. They create nets in the open air, filtering the falling condensation, catching the spores and dust that drift in the warm currents. The spinners themselves are elongated, tapering things, like living spindles of wet glass. They hang upside down from their anchor lines, motionless for hours, vibrating slightly with the ambient drone of the shakers. They are the trappers of the dark, passive and patient. To touch one is to encounter a surface that is terrifyingly hard and smooth compared to the surrounding softness—a needle hidden in the velvet.

All of them share the same temperature. The plateau is a thermodynamic equilibrium; there are no cold-blooded hunters seeking the heat of prey, nor warm-blooded prey fleeing the chill. Everything here is blood-warm, stone-warm, the same tropical heat as the air. This thermal unity is why they ignore you. In a world of thermal gradients, vision is irrelevant; heat is the only truth. But here, where heat is omnipresent and uniform, the thermal sense is blind. You do not stand out as a beacon of life; you are merely another warm lump in a landscape of warm lumps. The shakers do not stop their scraping because your body heat is no different from the heat radiating by the geothermal vents. The floaters do not swerve aside because your scent is lost in the complex, mineral perfume of the wet stone.

They are ancient, too. The genetic drift here has slowed to a crawl. The shakers you hear vibrating against the machine are likely virtually identical to the ones that vibrated against it when the plateau was young enough to remember the concept of "young." They are not evolved; they are distilled. Every defense mechanism that wasn't strictly necessary has been leeched out of them over the eons. No thorns protect the ferns because nothing eats the ferns but the mindless treaders. No venom coats the spinners' fangs because there are no predators to fight. They exist in a state of sublime, hollowed-out efficiency.

They are the mechanisms of the plateau, just as the ribbed towers are. The animals breathe in the humid air and exhale the carbon dioxide the ancient vegetation needs; the treaders excrete waste that feeds the floating islands of moss; the shakers grind the machines into dust that becomes the earth. You are walking through the digestive tract of a world that has been digesting itself for a hundred million years, and it has no room in its stomach for a visitor. You are not food; you are not a threat. You are merely a pebble, temporarily suspended in the flow, until you step aside, or leave, or fall, and the current of warm, wet life closes over the space where you stood, erasing the ripple of your passing as if it never happened.
