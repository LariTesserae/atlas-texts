They do not call themselves anything, because to name oneself is to imply a distinction between the namer and the world, and here, there are no distinctions. There is only the Tidelands, and the things that move through its warm, wet arteries are simply the way the Tidelands perceive itself.

They are the **Current-Readers**.

They are not biological in the way a taxonomy textbook would understand it. They possess no internal skeletons, for the Tidelands offers support on all sides; they do not need to carry their own structure. They are modular, soft-bodied entities composed of a dense, muscle-like hydrostat wrapped in a thick, leathery dermis of grey and teal. They resemble nothing so much as a cross between a manta ray and a conductor’s baton—flattened, elongated, possessing a central "head" cluster that splits into three trailing tails, each tail frayed into thousands of sensory cilia.

They are perfectly adapted to the noise. They "hear" not through ears, but through their skin, which is sensitive to the micro-vibrations of the constructed world. A Current-Reader feels the hum of a pump three decks away as a tickle against its flank; it feels the stress-fracture of a ceramic wall as a deep, subsonic ache in its gut. They swim through the air and the water alike, buoyed by internal gas bladders that regulate their depth within the dense, humid atmosphere, trailing their cilia through the spray to taste the chemical composition of the haze.

Their society is not built on hierarchy, but on **Flow**.

They live according to the cycles of the water. When the Tidelands "exhale"—the great venting of warm vapor from the deep fabrication vents—the Current-Readers gather in the plazas, suspended in the updrafts, their bodies bloated with the gas harvested from the vents. This is their rest state. They hang in the pale light, translucent sails unfurled, drifting in a stationary cloud that resembles a slow-motion swirling of smoke. Here, they exchange data. They do not speak; they press their sensory clusters together, interfacing directly through the membrane, sharing the map of the changing world.

To the Current-Reader, the architecture is not a static obstacle. It is a puzzle that is constantly rewriting itself. They carry in their collective memory a model of the Tidelands that is more accurate than any blueprint—a fluid, four-dimensional map of *where the water will be next*. They know that Corridor 4B is not a corridor today; today, for the next six hours, it is a high-pressure sluice. They know that the Vertical Gallery on Level 9 is currently "dry" (meaning only ankle-deep), and that is where the Grazers—the flat, adhesive slime-eaters—will be congregating.

Thus, the Current-Readers are the shepherds of chaos. When the great surge cycles begin—the tidal breathing of the city—they do not flee. They ride the leading edge. A swarm of them will gather at a choke point, their bodies streamlined, gripping the mineral walls with suckers that can hold against tons of pressure. They wait for the water hammer, the violent pulse of fluid that clears the tubes. As the torrent hits, they release, surfing the shockwave through the narrow channels, using the force to travel miles in minutes, moving from the deep dark levels to the sunless plazas above without expending a calorie of their own.

They are the predators of the drift, but they are also the maintenance crew.

They feed on the biofilm and the Grazers, stripping the mineral scum from the valves and intake ports that the Tidelands uses to regulate itself. In doing so, they become an immune system. If a section of the city begins to calcify, if the accretion of mineral slime threatens to seal a vent permanently, the Current-Readers sense the change in the harmonic pitch of the water flow—the high-pitched whine of restriction. They converge. They eat. They scrape the stone clean with radula-lined mouths, returning the geometry to its intended tolerances.

Their young are not born; they are **shed**.

When a Current-Reader becomes too large, too heavy to ride the subtle updrafts, or when their sensory membrane begins to thicken and lose sensitivity to the fine vibrations, they undergo a metabolic shift. They sink. They migrate to the deepest levels, the "Roots," where the water is warmest and the pressure is highest. Here, they anchor themselves. Their cilia fuse with the mineral substrate. Their bodies dissolve, their cells breaking down and reconstituting into the gelatinous matrix that feeds the new hatchlings spitting from the thermal vents.

They are not individuals who die. They are currents that run their course and return to the ocean.

In the plazas, when the light filters down through the haze and hits the suspended swarm, the Current-Readers cast shifting, geometric shadows on the wet walls below. To an outsider, they look like debris caught in a draft. But to the Tidelands, they are the nervous system. They feel the pulse of the pumps; they anticipate the seal of the gates; they ride the turbulence. They are the ones who know that the horizon is not a line, but a cycle, and they are content to be caught within the turning of the wheel.
