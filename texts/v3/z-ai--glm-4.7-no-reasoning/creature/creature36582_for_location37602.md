Here, in the Sump, the distinction between the worker and the worked has long since evaporated. The citizens of this wet, iron-wound valley are not merely inhabitants; they are components, interchangeable biological gears lubricated by the constant humidity and driven by the rhythmic thrumming of the floor.

They are a pale, gangly people, evolved for the vertical labyrinth. Generations of navigating the jagged spires and extruded iron buttresses have stretched their limbs, giving them a simian reach suited for swinging on rusted cables or clinging to the slick, black stone. Their skin is the color of the bruised twilight—translucent and grayish, thickened by the chemical haze into a perpetual sheen of sweat. They do not wear clothing in the traditional sense; instead, they are swathed in layers of tattered, oil-soaked canvas and strapped with leather harnesses, hooked onto the machinery they serve to prevent a fatal fall into the steaming mire below.

Their eyes are large, black, and unblinking, adapted to the dim, sullen illumination of the arc-lamps. They possess a tapetum lucidum that catches the flickering electric discharge, making their gaze shimmer like wet stones. Sight is secondary here, however. The dominant sense is hearing. Their ears are oversized and ridged, capable of pivoting to isolate the *thump-hiss* of a hydraulic press or the high-pitched whine of a voltage leak amidst the industrial roar (1.8). They do not speak with voices; they communicate through a complex series of knocks against the pipes, using the iron skeleton of the city as a resonator to transmit messages through the vibration itself.

They are silent, efficient, and singular in purpose. There is no laughter, no conversation, no art. They move in a trance state, their actions synchronized with the "dynamic score" of the Sump (0.3). If a valve must be turned at the precise second the steam rises in a curling pattern, a hand is there to turn it, not by conscious thought but by genetic memory. They are the immune system of the machine—clearing blockages, tightening rivets, and sweeping the vermin from the vents.

And the vermin are their constant companions. The relationship between the people and the pests is one of grim pragmatism. The rats, sleek and muscular from swimming in the warmer discharge channels, are regarded less as animals and more as mobile waste disposal units. The citizens do not shriek at the sight of a dog-sized rat; they merely step aside, or in leaner times, wield their heavy wrenches with a casual, practiced lethality. The roaches, skittering across the girders, are plucked from the metal and consumed without hesitation, their chitinous shells providing the only crunch in a diet of soft, synthetic gruel dispensed from the walls themselves.

There is no hierarchy among them, for a hierarchy requires ambition, and ambition implies a desire for change. In the Sump, change is impossible. The rust is held in perfect stasis, and so is the society. They are born in the crevices of the cooling vents, amidst the pale yellow ferns and black mold, and they die slipping on the rainbow-slicked mire, their bodies falling into the crushers to be processed alongside the raw ore. They do not mourn; they merely replenish the workforce.

At night, though the electric lights never dim, they retreat into the hollows of the jagged rock formations, curling together in piles to share body heat in the oppressive warmth. They sleep with the rhythm of the turbines, their chests rising and falling in time with the pistons, dreaming of nothing but the hum of the wire, the hiss of the steam, and the eternal, blessed stability of the cage.
