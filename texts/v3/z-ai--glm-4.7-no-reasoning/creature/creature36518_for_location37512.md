The people of the High Flood do not walk so much as they ooze. They are extensions of the root-mat, soft-spoken and deliberate, moving with the viscosity of the sap that runs beneath the bark of their floors. Their skin is not the dry parchment of lowlanders but a porous, living sponge—pale and translucent, tinged with the blue-green of the deep water they float upon. They are perpetually damp, glistening with a fine layer of mist that never evaporates, their hair hanging in heavy, slick ropes that drape over shoulders like wet moss.

They dress in layers of permeable fabrics spun from the hollow fibers of water-plants, loose and billowing garments that trap pockets of warm air against the chill. There is no metal here; it rusts too quickly in the humid breath of the forest. Instead, their tools and adornments are organic: needles of carved bone, hooks made from the curved beaks of climbing fish, and weights of smooth, polished stone that serves to anchor them against the ceaseless tugging of the wind. They do not wear shoes. To bind the foot would be to lose the sensitivity required to read the vibrations of the bridge—the subtle thrum that warns of a weakening strut or a heavy predator passing in the depths below.

Their eyes are large and dark, adapted to the diffused, green-glass light of the aquarium-world. They see in gradients of shadow and refraction, scanning the water below not with fear, but with a predator’s patience. They are quiet, even when the city is loud; they have learned to speak in the pauses between the thunderous drumming of the rain, their voices a low, melodic hum that vibrates in the chest rather than assaulting the ear.

They are communal parasites living in symbiosis with the giants. The "houses" are not built, but revealed—hollows bored into the sides of the great trees, expanded carefully so as not to kill the host, lined with the soft, water-repellent down of seed-pods. In the lower tiers, where the spray is constant and the air is thick with the roar of the water, the laborers live. They are the stouter, broader members of the species, their hands rough and calloused from hacking back the aggressive undergrowth that constantly threatens to choke the walkways. They tend to the "gutters," the complex lattice of vines that channels the runoff, ensuring the floodwaters flow off the edges and not into the living rooms.

Higher up, in the dry hollows near the canopy, dwell the elders and the weavers. These individuals are thinner, more spindly, their limbs elongated from a life of climbing and reaching. They tend to the city’s few forms of agriculture: gardens of moss and edible fungi that grow in the crooks of branches, and the strange, luminous beetles they cultivate for their honey. They move with a ghostly grace, stepping out onto the swaying bridges of woven roots as if the vertigo were a comfort, rather than a terror.

The relationship between the people and the fauna is one of easy familiarity. They do not domesticate the climbing fish with legs, but they do not startle when a scaled, finned hand grips their ankle while they sit on the edge of a walkway. They simply brush the creature aside or offer it a scrap of moss. The iridescent insect clouds are a nuisance that is endured with stoic swipes of the hand. The great amphibious mammals that watch from the hollows are seen not as neighbors, but as lazy roommates—snoring, shifting masses of fur that one must step over on the way to the communal fire pit.

And they do have fire, small and smoky, burning piles of dried driftwood scavenged from the "edge of the world." It is the only true source of heat in this cool, suspended cathedral. They gather around it at dusk, though the light never truly fades, pressing close to share warmth. Here, they strip off their wet layers and steam in the rising heat, their skin flushing pink. It is a time for mending nets and weaving new floorboards, tasks performed with a slowness that borders on meditation.

There is no concept of "fast" here. A child does not run; they amble. A warning is not shouted; it is passed along like a slow ripple through a crowd. If a bridge collapses, it is not a tragedy, but an event—a return to the water. The people simply climb back up the aerial roots, shake themselves off like dogs, and begin the slow process of weaving a new path. They are a people in equilibrium, perfectly balanced on the thin skin of the world, content to drift in the green, suspended twilight where gravity is a suggestion and the water is the only solid truth.
