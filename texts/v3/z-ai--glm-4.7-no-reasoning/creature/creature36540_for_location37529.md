They are the Lithodermes, the Skin-of-Stone, though they have no use for names. In the architecture of the plateau, they are the mechanism that keeps the stone from being merely dead.

To look at them is to look at the shale trying to move. A typical adult is a low, flattened dome of jagged interlocking plates, seamless in silhouette but complex in close view. The "shell" is not a covering but a continuation of the geology; it is the outermost layer of their being, grown in a lattice that mimics the crystal structure of the rock they inhabit. This dermis is perpetually in flux—absorbing new minerals where it presses against the vein walls, sloughing off older, spent layers in fine dust that trails behind them like smoke.

Beneath this stony integument lies the stratum of heat and motion. They are not fleshy in the human sense; they are viscous and plastic, a high-pressure biochemical soup maintained at a temperature just below the point of mineral fusing. This interior glows with a dull, cherry-red luminescence that leaks out through the cracks in their plating, visible only when they huddle together in the deep borings, creating a shared radiance that warms the tunnels around them.

Locomotion is their primary interaction with the world. They possess a ventral complex that is less a cluster of legs and more a singular, muscular underside—rippled with thousands of cilia-like gripping pods. They do not walk; they flow. They press themselves against the stone, and these gripping pods ripple in waves, creating friction and traction against the tiniest imperfections in the rock face. A large Lithoderm moving across a flat shelf resembles a puddle of mercury trying to maintain a shape: the center stays relatively still while the edges ripple and advance, pulling the mass forward. It is silent work, saved only for the whispery grind of mineral-on-mineral where their plated flank brushes a tunnel wall.

Sensory input is purely seismic and chemical. They possess nothing resembling eyes or ears. Instead, their leading edge is lined with specialized sensory pores—delicate, filamentous structures that extend millimeters into the air or press deep into the stone. These pores taste the gradients: they read the subtle drop in sulfur content that indicates a vent nearby; they feel the micro-vibrations of a tunnel-mate shifting position three chambers away; they sense the stress fractures in the rock before they give way.

Their consciousness—if it can be called that—is communal and distributed. There is no "I" in the Congregation, only the density. A single entity is merely a processing unit for the larger network. When a creature finds a particularly rich vein of reactive mineral, it does not consume it greedily; it stops and begins a rhythmic scraping, broadcasting a seismic signal of its find. Others redirect their flows, converging on the source not to compete, but to process. They form temporary assemblages, clusters of interlocking bodies that function as a single, multi-stomached refinery, breaking down the stone into chemical energy and waste with terrifying efficiency.

The waste they produce is their gift to the architecture. It is not excrement in the biological sense, but a slurry of neutralized compounds—oxidized metals, stable sulfates—that they smear along the floors of the high-traffic tunnels or pack into the unused niches. Over centuries, this waste hardens, smoothing the rough rock into polished surfaces, reinforcing narrow passages against collapse, effectively "growing" the city they inhabit. They are the builders of their own maze, guided by the invisible logic of chemical saturation.

At the bottom of the size hierarchy, invisible to the naked eye, exist the Dissolvers. These are not true organisms but semi-sentient protein chains, a "livestock" cultivated by the Lithodermes in the deepest, hottest recesses. The Dissolvers thrive in the acidic pools that collect in the basement fractures, eating raw silicates and excreting simpler, energy-dense molecules. The larger Lithodermes "farm" them by periodically descending to these sumps, absorbing the rich broth into their porous undersides, and carrying it back up to the cooler zones to fuel their slow metabolism.

Life in the Congregation is a cycle of immersion and emergence. During the peak heat cycles, when the plateau breathes its sulfur fog and the stone becomes too hot to touch safely, they retreat deep into the earth, packing themselves so tightly into the lower honeycomb that the network becomes solid with bodies. They enter a state of torpor, their internal temperatures equalizing with the rock, their metabolisms slowing to a crawl. This is the time of the Great Silence, where even the scraping stops, and only the groans of the expanding geology can be felt.

As the heat wanes and the frost begins to sublime, they wake. The seismic hum of the population shifts frequency—a signal rippling through the stone that it is time to ascend. They pour upward from the depths, flooding the surface shelves. They engage in the "Grooming," a collective behavior where they scrape the crystalline frost from the rock, ingesting the sulfur and water trapped within the lattice. It is a delicate process; they move with infinite care across the fragile crystal fields, their movements coordinated to avoid shattering the harvest.

Death is a concept that does not exist here, only a change of state. When a Lithoderm becomes too heavy, or its mineral lattice too rigid to permit movement, it does not die in agony. It simply stops. It finds a suitable niche—a spot in the wall that requires reinforcement—and it settles. It retracts its sensory pores and ceases the ripple of its ventral grip. Over hours, its internal chemistry cools and solidifies. The boundary between creature and rock dissolves completely. The body becomes a pillar of granite-hard composite, indistinguishable from the surrounding shale except for the faint, ghostly pattern of its plating.

The other inhabitants treat these stationary forms with the same respect they afford the vitrified bowl at the center: they route their tunnels around them. The ancestors become the architecture. A tunnel through the Congregation is not just a passage through rock; it is a corridor lined with the calcified remains of a thousand generations, a history of movement fossilized into the walls, holding the ceiling up for the living who flow endlessly above them.

They are the Congregation. They are the stone waking up, eating itself, and building a cathedral from the remains. They are not waiting for anything, because in the dark heat of the plateau, everything they need is already there, beneath their feet, within their skin, vibrating in the endless hum of the deep.
