To live here is to live as a component within a vast, self-sustaining engine. The intelligence that built this place—the architects who folded space, raised the hematite sky, and engineered the atmospheric density—have departed, but their ghost remains as physics. To exist on the High Veldt is to be a gear that does not grind, a pulse that does not echo. You are not a visitor; you are a local node, and the transition into belonging is seamless, inevitable, and absolute.

The humanoids who dwell here—if "human" is even the right word for what millennia of deep-time adaptation have wrought—are tall, their proportions stretched to accommodate the thinning of the air. Their skin is not flesh in the traditional sense, but a living mesh of coppery dermal plating and porous, dark clay. It breathes, drinking the oppressive humidity directly through capillaries that open and close in hexagonal patterns, mirroring the tessellated earth. They do not sweat; they osmose. The heavy, damp warmth is not a burden to them—it is a medium, a rich nutrient broth that keeps their internal hydraulics supple. Their body temperature is not their own; it syncs with the radiant heat of the stone, making them invisible to the thermal gaze of the manta-predators above. They are room-temperature creatures, moving through the blast-furnace day with the cold, steady patience of limestone.

They do not walk; they glide. The high friction of the environment would shred ordinary feet, but their soles are lined with magnetic repulsors—a biological mimicry of the monolith’s gravity-defiance. They skim inches above the cracked dirt, tracing the invisible magnetic tracks that guide the water spheres. When they pause at a weeping vein of water, they do not cup it in their hands. They place their mouths to the limestone and inhale the moisture directly from the rock, drawing the water up through the porous stone as if they were an extension of the aquifer itself. 

Their eyes are vestigial, sealed shut beneath smooth, hard ridges of brow. In a world where shadows defy the sun and light is a physical weight, vision is a liability. Instead, they navigate via a crown of pale, bioluminescent nodes that ridge their foreheads and collarbones, pulsing with the same soft, ice-blue light as the sensory organs of the mantas. Through these nodes, they read the electromagnetic topography of the veldt. They feel the hum of the photovoltaic trees in their teeth; they hear the silent, devastating strike of a predator as a sudden, sharp spike in the local static. They communicate not with breath—breath is too precious, the air too thick—but through localized shifts in their bio-electric field. A greeting is a harmonic resonance; a warning is a sudden dip in voltage, felt instantly by the herd.

And they are a herd. They call themselves the *Conduit*, though the word is not spoken but transmitted. They live in the sweeping hollows of the weathered limestone, fitting their long, rounded bodies into the organic dips of the ancient, sleeping creature's bones. They do not build shelters; the way-stations are their only architecture, but they do not enter them. They simply gather beneath the skeletal arches of nanocarbon, plugging themselves—physically, via fibrous, root-like tendrils that extend from their spines—into the earth's grid. 

This is their deepest purpose: they are the battery cells of a dormant network. When the fierce, bright sunlight pounds down upon the veldt, the photovoltaic trees drink it, humming with stored power. But trees cannot move. The *Conduit* move. As they sleep in the hollows, the energy harvested by the forest pulses through the ground, up through their spines, and is refined in the organic furnaces of their bodies. They are living transformers, stepping down the wild, ferocious voltage of the sun into the steady, deep-thrumming current that keeps the monoliths hovering and the way-stations glowing. When they wake, they carry this charge within them, distributing it across the plateau as they roam, a walking grid maintaining the machinery of a departed god.

They tend to the runners and the mantas not as shepherds, but as sysops. When a runner's chrome hide is fractured by a misstep, a member of the *Conduit* will kneel beside it, pressing their clay palms to the wound. Nanocarbon fibers weave from their skin into the creature's, patching the breach with a scar of dark moss. When a manta descends, sated, and rests on a thermal vent, the *Conduit* brush their glowing nodes against its wings, reading its health in the amperage of its pulse. They do not mourn the taken; the violent flash of a predator’s strike is merely a transfer of energy, a deletion of a file, a natural correction to the system’s equilibrium.

At night, when the hematite sky dims and the server-ceiling of stars blinks on, the *Conduit* stand upon the ridges. They do not look up; they have no eyes. Instead, they open their nodes, pointing their faces at the data ports of the cosmos. They download the starlight. In the profound, 0.4 silence, the plateau becomes a cathedral of circuitry. The hovering monoliths pulse in slow, rhythmic time with the stars. The trees hum in bass frequencies. The water spheres roll silently up the hills, and the chrome runners flicker in the distance.

And the *Conduit* stand there, breathing in the warm, wet ozone, their heartbeats locked in perfect, terrifying synchronization with the earth. They are flesh that has become metal; they are biology that has become code. They are the curators of an empty museum, the vital, organic sparks in a world of silent, perfect engineering, holding the light of a forgotten empire in their veins, waiting, in eternal stillness, for the architects to return and log back on.
