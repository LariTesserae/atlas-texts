To live here is to be part of the circuit. You do not inhabit the Garden; you *conduct* it.

The beings that linger in this sunken cathedral are not distinct in the way animals are distinct in the air above. They are extensions of the mechanism, biological components wired into the industrial ghost. They are the things that have learned to eat the electricity, to taste the vibration of the buried flywheel, and to translate the slow, bodily heat of the water into movement.

There are three kinds of life here. There are the **Architects**, the **Scavengers**, and the **Ghost-Current**.

### The Architects

They are what you might mistake, at first glance, for a particularly robust variety of vine. They drape the central shaft like bunting, thick as a man’s torso, anchored deep in the silt where the foundation of the old city meets the true seabed. They are the masters of the iron lattice.

If you were to touch one—and the water encourages you to touch, its warmth lulling, its density slowing your hand until it drifts like a dream—you would feel not the fibrous indifference of cellulose, but the rhythmic thrum of a muscle. The Architects are animals that have mimicked plants so perfectly they have become plants. They possess no eyes, for eyes are useless in the amber gloom; they possess no mouths, for they feed not on matter but on the differential current that leaks from the rusted conduits.

Their bodies are filled with a conductive gel, a pale, milky plasma that glows faintly with the blue of bioluminescence. They grow along the paths of least resistance, wrapping around riveted steel beams, hugging the ceramic pipes that still carry the ghost of a signal from the depths. As they grow, they tighten. This is their function: they are the living clamps, the tendons of the submerged city. Where the old iron has warped under the immense pressure of the water, an Architect has wrapped itself around the joint, contracting its long, tubular body to hold the structure together. They are the reason the settlement has not collapsed; they are the scaffolding that has replaced the mortar.

They are blind, rooted, and immensely strong. They communicate through the water itself, sending low-frequency pulses that ripple through the biofilm—a slow, seismic language of *stress* and *support* and *hold*.

### The Scavengers

Where the Architects are static and immense, the Scavengers are small and numerous. They are the清理者—the agents of entropy that ensure nothing is wasted.

They resemble crustaceans, but simplified, stripped down to the essentials of scavenging in a packed world. They have no legs for walking; legs are useless where there is no solid ground. Instead, they possess a multitude of hooked claws, allowing them to cling to the vertical forests of root-mass or scuttle upside down along the undersides of the rusted plate-steel ceilings.

Their shells are not calcified, but metalliferous. They incorporate the iron and copper of their environment into their own bodies, armored in the rust of the city they consume. They are the color of the water—murky, bruised orange—and difficult to see until they move.

The Scavengers are the immune system of the Garden. They do not hunt living prey; there is nothing to hunt. Instead, they seek the *breakdown*. They swarm over a leaf that has finally succumbed to its own weight, tearing it into manageable chunks that are instantly seized by the waiting filter-feeders. They clean the biofilm from the lenses of the long-dead sensors. They burrow into the silt, constantly turning it over, ensuring that the nutrients are distributed, that the decay that feeds the roots is accelerated.

Most unsettlingly, they groom the Architects. You will see clusters of these iron-shelled crabs picking delicately at the hides of the great tube-worms, removing parasites, clearing the vents through which the Architects draw their conductive fluids. It is a symbiosis of maintenance. The Scavengers keep the circuit clean; the circuit provides the warmth and the stray voltage that the Scavengers’ primitive nervous systems rely on for navigation.

### The Ghost-Current

The strangest inhabitants are not animals at all, but a colonial organism that lives in the water column itself. It is visible only when it moves—a shimmering distortion in the murk, like heat haze on a road, but cold.

It is a living net. Millions of microscopic organisms, polyps drifting on tendrils of silk-thin mucus, connected by a shared electrochemical sensitivity. They form vast, drifting webs that fill the gaps between the buildings, occupying the "streets" that the roots have not yet claimed.

The Ghost-Current is passive. It does not swim. It drifts. It is a living sieve. It filters the spores, the dust, the microscopic debris that floats in the perpetual twilight, turning the murk into clarity, feeding on the suspended life.

When the buried mechanism pulses—once per second, perhaps slower—the Ghost-Current glimmers. The colonial organisms are attuned to the electromagnetic field. When the field spikes, the net contracts, bunching up like a drawn purse, capturing whatever nutrients are in the water. When the field relaxes, the net expands, drifting back toward the empty channels.

To swim through the Ghost-Current is to pass through a curtain of static electricity. It clings to your skin, a tingling, alien sensation. It is not alive in the sense of having a heart or a brain, but it is alive in the way a lightning storm is alive—a coordinated event, a temporary alignment of forces, feeding on the energy that leaks from the machine below.

### The Fate of the Stranger

There is no predator here that will tear you apart. The Garden does not hunger for blood; hungers are crude and quick, and this place is slow.

If you were to stay, the Architects would simply grow around you. They would sense the heat of your body as a variation in the ambient warmth, and they would slowly, gently, extend their tendrils to bracket your limbs. They would not crush you; they would support you. They would incorporate you into the lattice, holding you in place because you are a mass that requires support.

The Scavengers would come, eventually. They would not bite; they would taste. They would sample the soft detritus of your skin and eyes, testing to see if you were structure or substrate. Finding you soft, they would begin the gentle process of abrasion, wearing away the boundaries.

And the Ghost-Current would drift through your open mouth, filling your lungs not with water, but with the living spores of the garden, settling into the warm, wet dark of your internal spaces.

You would not die. Not quickly. You would simply become another node in the circuit. Your heat would feed the Architects; your minerals would feed the Scavengers; your electrical impulses would be absorbed into the great, silent hum of the place. You would become a statue of warm flesh in the dark, a relay in a machine that has been running for centuries, processing *more*, filling the void, closing the circuit.

You would be, at last, part of the Rule. You would be full.
