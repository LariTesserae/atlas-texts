They are a people shaped by the squeeze—narrow-shouldered and hunch-backed, evolved to slide through the compressed arteries of their city. They are not tall; height is a liability here. To be tall is to scrape your head on the sagging, waterlogged beams of the upper stories, to risk the falling slate that slides slickly off the steep, unnatural angles of the roofs. They are dense, solid creatures, possessing a gravitas that matches the high-density mud they walk upon.

Their skin is the color of the wet stones: a pallid, greyish undertone flushed with the blue of poor circulation and perpetual cold. It is rarely exposed. They wrap themselves in layer upon layer of rough-spun wool and tanned hides, garments that have felt the spray of the weeping cliffs so often that they have stiffened into shells. The clothes smell of woodsmoke and the iron tang of the water, scentless to the wearers but overpowering to an outsider. They wear boots of cured, oil-soaked leather that come up to the knee, laced tight with sinew to keep the black slime from breaching the barrier.

Their faces are dominated by their eyes—large, and adapted to the gloom, they catch the strangled light and reflect it back like the puddles on the floor. The whites of their eyes are faintly yellowed, the irises a dark, watery green. They see perfectly in the dim twilight, their pupils wide black discs that seem to swallow the meager illumination. Beneath these watchful eyes, their noses are small and pinched, an evolutionary defense against the smell of old spit and rot, though their mouths are often set in a permanent, tight frown of exertion.

They are not a speaking people. The silence is deceptive, yes, but they do not break it with idle chatter. Their language is a dialect of gestures and low, resonant hums that vibrate through the wooden beams they lean against. To shout here is to waste breath, and breath is precious when the air is heavy and wet. When they must communicate across a gap of mud, they use a series of sharp clicks and whistles, mimicking the sound of dripping water to mask the noise.

Their hands are their most distinct feature—broad, spade-shaped, and scarred from the abrasive grey rock. The fingernails are thick, ridged like shells, and stained black by the tannins of the earth. They are constantly moving. These people are defined not by who they are, but by what they prevent. They are the bailers, the bracers, the patchers. They live in a state of permanent, low-level emergency.

You will see them wading through the alleys, hip-deep in the soup, dragging long poles of iron-tipped ash. They use these to test the ground before they step, probing for the soft spots where the saturation has turned the earth to liquid. When a building groans—a deep, wet moan of timber under stress—they swarm. They move with the coordinated efficiency of ants, heaving rough-hewn logs into place, lashing leather straps with a tightening that screams in protest, pegging joints that refuse to hold. They fight the geometry of the city, forcing the bulging structures back into line.

They eat little, and what they eat is scavenged from the dark corners of the world. They harvest the leathery fungi that sprout from the mortar, boiling it into a grey slime that provides warmth more than flavor. They hunt the heavy, flightless birds that roost in the eaves, trapping them with snares made of gut, catching the oily creatures as they hop blindly through the mud. The meat is tough and rank, but it burns long in the belly.

Their relationship with the water is one of exhausted enmity. They treat the creeping puddles not as a natural phenomenon, but as a living, malicious pest. When the mineral deposits climb the pilings, they scrape them away with jagged rocks, scraping the "rust" off the wood until it bleeds fresh sap. They bail the cellars without cease, using buckets made from the hollowed-out shells of giant, freshwater snails found in the deeper mines. The rhythm of their lives is the *splash-thud* of the bucket hitting the sump, the endless, Sisyphean labor of keeping the dry dry.

They are a superstitious folk, wary of the Weirdness that permeates the air. They avoid looking directly into the black mirrors of the cliff faces, fearing what might look back. They mark their doorways with symbols drawn in ash—spirals and tight circles meant to confuse the water, to trick it into flowing elsewhere. They believe that if they stop moving, if they stop bailing and bracing, the city will simply close its eyes like a drowning man and sink beneath the surface.

There is a softness to them, despite the hardness of their world. They touch often—a hand on a shoulder when passing in a narrow gap, a shared nod of acknowledgment when the "thump" of a collapsing mine echoes and the ground shifts beneath their feet. They are bound by the claustrophobia. They know that their neighbors are the only thing holding their walls up, and that if one tower falls, they all fall. They are ghosts in the machine of their own survival, pale and dripping, eternally holding back the weight of the wet world.
