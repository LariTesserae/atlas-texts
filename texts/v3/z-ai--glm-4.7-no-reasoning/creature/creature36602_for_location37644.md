The water is absolute here. It is not an ocean in the geological sense, but a single, bounded volume—a vast, dark aquarium of a room, fully submerged and enclosed.

This is the Sump. It is a cathedral-scale industrial chamber, roughly hemispherical, perhaps three hundred feet from floor to ceiling. The scale feels "small" only because the boundaries are visible: the curved walls, riveted with steel plates the size of garage doors, enclose the space entirely. There is no open horizon, only the crushing pressure of the deep and the looming presence of the architecture.

Everything is built. Every surface is artificial—steel, iron, concrete, and copper—yet nothing looks sharp anymore. The parameters of time and erosion have triumphed over engineering. The metal has been underwater for so long that it has lost its rigid geometry. The girders and support beams have eroded into smooth, flowing shapes, worn down by centuries of currents until the iron looks like water-rolled stone. Thick layers of rust coat the machinery, flaking off in heavy clouds of red sediment that drift through the water like blood in a syringe.

The temperature is uniformly cold, just above freezing. It is a chill that seeps through the material, slowing the movement of anything living within it. The water is thick and murky, a heavy soup of industrial decay and silt that limits visibility to a few dozen yards.

Life here is sparse in vegetation but abundant in fauna. There are no kelp forests, no coral reefs. The only plant life is a pathetic, sparse fuzz of greyish slime clinging to the upper reaches of the walls where the last failing lights flicker. But the water is thick with motion.

The space is loud, defined by a cacophony of industrial noise. The central feature of the room is a massive, subaquatic dynamo, a machine the size of a warehouse that still churns in the dark. It is a "Total" build of gears and pistons, rusted but functional, grinding away with a deafening, rhythmic thrum. The sound travels perfectly through the water, a low-frequency vibration that rattles the teeth and vibrates in the chest.

The fauna have adapted to this mechanical roar. Swarms of small, pale, eyeless fish dart through the water like shrapnel, unbothered by the noise, feeding on the bacterial mats that grow on the warm pipes. Larger, crab-like creatures with shells as hard as steel scuttle along the rusted grates of the floor. They are the vermin of this deep place, scavengers that thrive in the nooks and crannies of the machine, picking at the organic sludge that accumulates in the joints of the gears.

The lighting is dim and dying. Rows of glass-bulbed fixtures, bolted high into the ceiling, strobe weakly with a sickly, sodium-yellow luminescence. They barely penetrate the gloom, creating long, dancing shadows that stretch and distort across the eroded machinery.

The atmosphere is one of heavy, enclosed industry. It is a "Dynamic" system; the water is constantly circulated by the churning of the central dynamo, creating artificial currents that push the schools of fish back and forth in a hypnotic, endless loop. There is no escape. The doorways, if they ever existed, have long since rusted shut or been buried under tons of fallen debris. It is a sealed ecosystem, a room where the machine has become the environment, and the animals have learned to live inside the belly of the gear.
