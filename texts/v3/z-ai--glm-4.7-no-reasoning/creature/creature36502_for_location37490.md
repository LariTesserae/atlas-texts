Here, in the suffocating gloom where the air itself feels like a heavy, wet shroud, life does not merely exist—it infests. It is a biology of excess, a frantic claustrophobia of flesh churning against stone.

The baseline of this world is the **Crawling Carpet**. To look down is to lose one’s stomach in the sheer scale of the movement. The ground is not visible; it is buried under a shifting, palimpsest of scavengers. These are the **Litho-Crabs**, pale, sightless crustaceans the size of mastiffs. Their shells are not the hard, calcium armor of surface-dwellers, but soft and flexible, like wet leather, scarred by the constant friction of their kin. They move sideways in a jerky, synchronized panic, their thousands of clawed legs tapping a relentless, staccato rhythm against the limestone. They have no eyes; their faces are dominated by gaping, vertical slits that serve as both mouth and nose, constantly testing the heavy air for the scent of heat or decay. They are the vultures of the basin, clearing the space of anything that falls, their immense crushing claws capable of turning rock into gravel and bone into jelly.

Interwoven among the heavy treading of the crabs are the **Ribbon-Worms**. These are not mere worms, but ribbons of translucent flesh, yards long and as wide as a man’s torso, undulating in waves through the cracks of the crab migration. Inside their clear, gelatinous bodies, one can see the workings of a dark metabolism—multi-colored organs pulsing slowly, conveying crude nutrients. They move without legs, using microscopic cilia to grip the oily sheen of the stone, gliding over the backs of the crabs with a slippery, suctioning sound. They are parasites of opportunity, burrowing into the joints of the larger crustaceans with a drill-toothed mouth that spins like a corkscrew, feeding on the soft meat inside before retreating back into the safety of the crowd.

Above this melee, the air is so thick with life that it feels like swimming. The dominant flyers are the **Membrane-Skates**. Evolved in the absence of wind, they have lost the grace of the bat or the bird; they are flying leathery pancakes, essentially triangular mouths with wings. They are devoid of fur, their skin stretched tight over a framework of cartilage that glows with a faint, sickly green bioluminescence. They do not flap so much as they vibrate, their wings moving at a frequency that creates a low, humming buzz that adds to the roar of the basin. They hang in the air in dense, shifting clouds, waiting for a disturbance in the swarm below. When a larger creature stumbles, the cloud descends, a blanket of flapping wings and rasping tongues, stripping the victim in seconds.

And then there are the **Anchors**—the massive, hulking beasts that form the living islands of this ecosystem. They are the biological equivalent of the stone itself, creatures that look like armored slugs inflated to the size of a house, supported by hundreds of spindly, calcified legs that splay out like the roots of a fallen tree. Their flesh is grey and tough, corded with muscle that feels like granite under the hand. They are the "regenerators," the mobile mountains of meat. Because they are constantly being eaten, their biology is a miracle of speed; wounds knit visibly, flesh bubbles up to fill gouges within minutes, and their skin secretes a constantly renewing layer of slime that makes it difficult for parasites to gain a solid purchase. They move with the agonizing slowness of tectonic plates, pushing through the crushing crowd of smaller scavengers, indifferent to the thousands of bites taken out of their hides every hour. They groan—a deep, resonant sound that vibrates in the chest, a sub-sonic declaration of their massive presence.

Swimming through the stagnant, boiling pockets of water are the **Glass-Eels**. These are horrors of transparency, their bodies composed of a silica-like structure that refracts the minimal light, making them nearly invisible until they are upon you. They have no gills, but rather breathe through their skin, which is constantly abraded by the mineral-rich water. They lie in wait in the black pools, their bodies stiff and straight like discarded rods of iron, vibrating with an electric charge that stuns the unwary. When they strike, they move with lightning speed, their jaws unhinging to swallow prey whole, dissolving it internally with acids potent enough to etch stone.

Perhaps the strangest inhabitants are the **Geo-Phantsoms**. These are the part-mineral entities, the "animated silica" that drift on the peripheries of vision. They look roughly humanoid in outline, but jagged and asymmetrical, composed of shifting plates of crystal and shale. They do not eat; they feed on the vibration of the roar. Their bodies are covered in tympanic membranes, sensitive diaphragms that pick up the sound waves of the basin and convert that sonic energy into a chemical charge that fuels their slow, crystalline growth. They are fragile, shattering if touched, but they are constantly reforming, drawn together by the magnetic resonance of the deafening noise. They are the listeners, the ghosts of the noise, adding a layer of clicking, chiming chimes to the lower registers of the cacophony.

Everything here is hot to the touch. The internal fires of the million-million bodies create a palpable heat haze, a microclimate of sweat and steam that hangs over the oily stone. The smell is an assault—a perfume of rotten iron, salty brine, and the copper tang of blood that never dries. In this place, you are not a spectator; you are an intrusion of heat and noise, a soft packet of calories in a world of hard appetites. The crowd does not hate you, nor does it fear you. It simply acknowledges you as fresh fuel, a temporary obstruction in the endless, grinding machine of consumption.
