They are not a people in the way that a city requires people. They are the inhabitants of the lung, the citizens of the single, continuous organism that has forgotten how to be separate. In a place defined by a total architecture of living tissue, where the boundary between "wall" and "wilderness" has dissolved, the beings that dwell here are parasites, symbionts, and ghosts, all adapted to the terrifying, humid permanence of the green.

There are the **Stem-Walkers**, tall and gracile, who move through the vascular corridors like thoughts moving through a nervous system. They are humanoid only in the loosest sense—their limbs are elongated, their digits fused into tapering spikes that can pry apart the seals of the stomata-vaults when they need to pass. Their skin is not opaque, but a translucent, gelatinous membrane, pale as the mist itself, allowing one to see the slow, dark circulation of their own internal fluids. They do not speak; the deafening hydro-mechanical roar of the plateau makes sound useless. Instead, they communicate by pressing their elongated fingers against the "bark"—the living, Calloris-like skin of the floor—vibrating messages through the shared rhizome network. They are the electric impulses of this place, flitting through the xylem-chambers, harvesting the condensed water that drips from the load-bearing arches. They wear nothing, for the temperature is a static, warm constant, and they carry nothing, for the floor provides everything: a springy path beneath their feet, a drink seeping from every wall, and the sweet, cloying scent of oxygen-rich sap to fill their lungs.

Then there are the **Slooms**, the "rumored" fauna made visible. They are massive, limbless grazers, resembling colossal slugs or flattened snakes, but composed of a dense, fibrous material that mimics the floor they traverse. They are white and eyeless, navigating by the texture of the root-pavement and the taste of the humidity. They move with terrifying slowness, their muscle contractions rippling beneath their skin in waves that take minutes to travel from head to tail. They do not eat the vegetation; that would be cannibalism in a world that is all flesh. Instead, they lap the sweat—the perpetual condensation that coats every surface—with tongues rough like sandpaper. They are the cleaners of this cathedral, sliding through the hollow stems, leaving behind trails of glittering, sterile mucus that seals micro-fractures in the architecture. They are solitary, separated by miles of green geometry, communicating only through low-frequency thrums that vibrate through the wooden parenchyma, a ghostly Morse code felt in the soles of the feet rather than heard by the ears.

Higher up, where the canopy of load-bearing stolons weaves a ceiling that catches the non-existent rain, live the **Hangers**. They are small, spindly creatures that sleep in the cradles of fused vines, upside down, suspended by their tails which have evolved into biological hooks that secrete a bonding enzyme identical to the plant’s own sap. They are the gatherers of the "drip," the rhythmic percussion of the place. When a droplet swells and falls from the apex of a stolon, a Hanger drops with it, riding the bubble of surface tension down into the dark, humid atria below, drinking their fill in mid-air before uncoupling and flapping—gliding really, on skins stretched between their arms—back up toward the light. They are frantic, nervous things, vibrating with the anxiety of creatures that live in a hall of mirrors, terrified of being absorbed into the wall. To stay still too long is to risk fusing; the plateau constantly attempts to incorporate them, to heal over them as if they were a wound.

And finally, there are the **Absorbed**. It is said that if you stand still in the echoing silence of the root-halls, you can see them—figures in the walls, faces frozen in the barkless skin of the load-bearing columns. They are not dead, but they are no longer distinct. They are the ancestors of the Stem-Walkers, perhaps, or the Slooms that grew too tired to move. Over centuries, the living architecture accepted them. Their outlines have softened, their edges blurring into the woody texture of the surround. They have become architectural elements, their open mouths now part of the ventilation system, their eyes forming hollows where the dew collects before dripping to the floor. They are the ultimate citizens of this plateau: they have achieved total unity with the environment. They stand as statues in the vast, green emptiness, weeping tears of condensation that run down their stone-soft cheeks and feed the rhizomes below.

In this place, there is no predation, only consumption and integration. It is a world without sharp edges, a biology of curves and fusions. The inhabitants do not conquer their environment; they dissolve into it, living in the humming, wet belly of a plant that is larger than time, waiting for a season that will never come.
