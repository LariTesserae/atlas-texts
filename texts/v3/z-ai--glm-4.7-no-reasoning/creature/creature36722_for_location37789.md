It is a city of vertical stone and thick, breathing silence, sprawling across a range of jagged, young mountains that cut the sky like broken serrated blades. The scale is overwhelming—continental—a vast, interlocking labyrinth of masonry that clings to every precipice and fills every canyon, stretching to a horizon obscured by freezing haze.

The air is aggressively thin and cold, a damp chill that penetrates wool and wood alike. Here, water is not an abundance of flowing rivers but a persistent, soaking presence. The stone is slick with it; a sheen of black ice coats the granite surfaces, and the air hangs heavy with a moisture that sublimates instantly onto skin, leaving the inhabitants perpetually rimed with frost. It is a place of wet cold, where the dampness seeps into the mortar and the bones.

Vegetation is nearly nonexistent, strangled by the altitude and the harsh, abrasive winds. There are no trees, no grasses—only the hardiest, darkest lichens that cling to the leeward sides of the towers, and patches of pale, scrubby moss that grow in the protected crevices where the runoff collects. The color palette is severe: the grey of wet stone, the black of wet timber, the dull brown of the endless mud, and the white of the breath-mist that hangs constantly over the city.

Despite the desolation of the flora, the density of life is crushing. The "urban" environment is primitive, a chaotic stacking of rough-hewn granite and heavy timber without mortar, held together by gravity and immense weight. The structures are tiered, a dense warren of terraces and bridges, creating a single, massive artificial organism.

And it is teeming with fauna. Because the land cannot support fields or pastures, the city *is* the pasture. Every street, every wide balcony, every plaza is packed with shaggy, cold-adapted life. Massive, humped oxen with coats thick enough to obscure their legs stand shoulder-to-shoulder in the narrow alleys, their bodies radiating a primal heat that mingles with the smoke pouring from the flues. Herds of agile, mountain goats navigate the steep rooftops as if they were flat plains, their hooves clacking incessantly against the slate. Heavy-feathered birds roost in the thousands on the timber beams, their weight bowing the wood, creating a living, shifting architecture of squawks and rustles.

The technology is archaic, driven by muscle and stone. There are no engines, only the groan of wooden winches and the rhythmic trudging of beasts turning massive capstans to haul water and grain up the vertical inclines. The atmosphere is quiet not because of emptiness, but because of the muffling nature of the cold and the damp. Sounds are absorbed by the wet wool and the heavy fog; the lowing of cattle and the shouting of laborers sound distant, softened into a low, thrumming hum.

It is a slow world. The erosion of the sharp, young mountains is matched only by the glacial pace of life here. People move with a deliberate, economizing gait, conserving energy in the thin air. The city feels as if it is holding its breath, a dense, dark, frozen hive huddled against the vastness of the sky, forever damp and forever enduring.
