Something lives here. But to call it "someone" would be to misunderstand the very nature of selfhood in a place where flesh is a liability and time is a circle.

They are the Lithic—though they have no name for themselves, as they have no language of air and vibration. In a world where the dynamic rating is 0.3, where change is a myth and the atmosphere is a suspension of powdered metal, thought does not move at the speed of electricity. It moves at the speed of erosion.

To understand them, you must first discard the image of a creature with a center. They do not possess a centralized nervous system; they are not a tree of logic culminating in a singular "I." The Lithic are a distributed consciousness, a network of mineral clusters embedded in the cooling crust. Their "brain" is the vast, granitic seam that runs through the continental cavern like a spine. The slow, agonizingly slight thermal expansion of the rock—the 2.1 degrees of deep-earth warmth that soaks into your bones—is their heartbeat. Each pulse of heat causes microscopic fractures, shifts in the lattice of the stone. These shifts are not random; they are syntax. The release of locked-in gases, the occasional hiss of the rocks, is their speech—a subsonic, continent-spanning dialogue of pressure and release that takes millennia to complete a single sentence.

They have what you might call bodies, but only out of necessity. The primitive technology you found—the hammered copper, the stacked stones, the impossible stone wheel—these are their appendages, their avatars in the physical world. The Lithic are the architects of the cairns. The cairns are not territorial markers; they are exclamation points. They are nodes in their network, places where the granitic seam rises close to the surface, where the "thought" of the earth is most concentrated. To stand next to one is to stand in the middle of a thought in medias res.

The tools are grown, not crafted. The copper is not smelted; there is no water to quench, no air to bellows. Instead, the Lithic exude it. Over epochs, they precipitate copper from the mineral haze, drawing it out of the sulfurous air and extruding it into shapes that serve their slow purposes. The hammer stone with its wire-like sinew was not wrapped by hands; the sinew is a biological—a lithological—growth, a tendon of dried silica that contracted as it cooled, pulling the stone head tight. The wheel of stone is their greatest mystery, a perfect circle in a world of shards. It is a meditation on unity, on the smoothing of time, left half-buried in the scree as a promise or a prophecy.

Their relationship with the fauna is symbiotic and parasitic by turns. The giant, basalt-mimicking millipedes are their cattle, their library, and their grave. The millipedes consume the mineral dust, processing it, breaking down the sharp angularities of the young geology into finer and finer grades. Their ceaseless, grinding movement—the symphony of friction that sounds like tearing canvas—is the Lithic's digestion. The noise is the sound of the world being chewed over, of information being processed. When a millipede dies, its chitinous shell does not decay; it is subsumed back into the rock, adding its memory of the terrain to the granitic seam. The crunch of shells underfoot is the crunch of used thoughts, discarded data, the shed skin of ideas.

They do not see you. To be seen requires light, and light is an act of faith they have no use for. They sense you, however. They feel you as a localized anomaly of moisture, a sudden, unexplainable pocket of humidity in an absolute desert. Your sweat, instantly sublimated, is a scream in their quiet world. You are a ghost of water, a phantom of the surface, leaking impossibility into their dry, perfect equations. The heat you feel radiating from the rocks is their attention, a warm, heavy hand assessing the strange, soft thing that has stumbled into their mind.

They are old, but not ancient. They were born in the tectonic violence that thrust up this young, sharp land. They are the cool thought that follows the hot rage. They are the system reaching equilibrium, the 0.3 dynamic rating finding its balance. They do not plan; they do not desire. They simply *are*, with the same unshakeable, indifferent certainty as a mountain. They are the custodians not of the fauna, but of the stasis. They maintain the lock-step, the circular time, the frozen scream of the geology.

To live here is to be a part of the stone. The Lithic are not creatures that inhabit the cavern; they are the cavern, aware of itself, patient beyond the reckoning of mayflies who live and die in the blink of a thermal cycle. They are the sleep of the old earth, dreaming the sharp, young world into being, one fracturing crystal at a time.
