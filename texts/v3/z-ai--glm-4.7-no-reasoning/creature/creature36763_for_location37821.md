The people of Oth are not merely residents of the Spire-Garden; they are its slow, beating heart, indistinguishable from the damp grey stone and the voracious greenery that threatens to swallow them whole. They are a people forged in the quiet violence of the uplift, shaped by a gravity that demands compactness and a biology that demands submission.

To look upon an Othi is to witness a creature in the midst of a slow, centuries-long negotiation with its environment. They are short, their stature compressed by the cramped verticality of their homes, with limbs that appear slightly too long for their torsos, allowing them to bridge the jagged fractures of the rock with a spider-like ease. Their skin is the color of the mist—pale, translucent, and cool to the touch. Beneath the surface, dark veins trace intricate maps, carrying blood that moves sluggishly, adapted to the biting chill. They do not blush; their skin simply darkens, a grey deepening to charcoal, like a storm cloud rolling in.

Their flesh is porous. This is their defining trait. In a world where wetness is a conductor, the Othi are biological lightning rods. Fine, moss-like fuzz sprouts from their shoulders and along their spines, not as hair, but as a symbiotic garden. This fuzz is usually a teeming, vibrant green, but it shifts color with their diet—turning deep crimson when they harvest the blood-berries of the upper canopy, or a bruised purple when they process the slug-flesh. They do not sweat; they weep a thin, nutrient-rich dew that feeds the flora growing upon them, trading their electrolytes for the camouflage and insulation the moss provides. To touch an Othi is to feel the damp velvet of a living forest floor.

Their faces are masks of calm, almost insectoid stoicism. The eyes are large, black pools that reflect very little light, evolved to pierce the perpetual twilight gloom. They lack whites, appearing as solid orbs of obsidian that seem to drink in the shadows. Their noses are small, flattened slits, designed to prevent heat loss but highly sensitive to the chemical signals of the spore-clouds. Their mouths are rarely used for speech; the jaw is narrow, filled with small, needle-like teeth adapted for scraping and softening, rather than tearing.

And they are never truly alone.

An Othi moves through the Spire-Garden as a hive mind moves through a body. The "rules" of Oth dictate that individuality is a luxury, but survival is a collective act. draped over their shoulders and wrapped around their waists are the "Silks"—great, leathery panels of shed skin harvested from the giant lizards. These garments are not sewn; they are tied and knotted, acting as saddlebags, cradles, and shelter. But the garments are alive with secondary life. A dozen or more of the dinner-plate sized grey beetles—the "Carrion-Grinders"—cling to the leather of a traveler’s back, cleaning it of parasites and mold. The Othi do not brush them away. They reach back with long, slender fingers to stroke the beetles’ shells, communicating through touch and vibration.

They cultivate the parasites as much as they cultivate the fruit. On their forearms, they allow the gelatinous slugs to attach, forming small, pulsing welts where the slug dissolves dead skin cells and feeds on the sebaceous oils. In return, the slug secretes a potent antibacterial mucus that the Othi scrape off with stone knives to use as a salve for wounds. It is a disgusting intimacy to an outsider, but to them, it is holy. To merge is to survive.

Their society is matriarchal in the most biological sense. The "Grand-Mothers" are the oldest among them, their skin so overgrown with moss and fungal shelfs that their humanoid shape is nearly obscured. They sit in the deepest hollows of the settlement, rooted to the stone, acting as the digestive centers of the community. The foragers bring them the harvested fruits and the strange, pulsing tubers found in the high vertical rifts. The Grand-Mothers consume these and, through a process of internal fermentation and enzymatic breakdown, excrete a nutrient-dense paste that is the staple food for the rest of the clan. They are living factories, stationary and revered, their minds perhaps wandering the neural networks of the vines that dangle into their chambers.

When they must communicate, they rarely use their voices. The "library-quiet" of the Spire is maintained through a complex language of clicks, hums, and grinding teeth. They mimic the sonar of the moths to signal danger, and the rustle of the ferns to signal safety. A conversation between two Othi looks like a dance: they tilt their heads to expose the sensitive patches of skin on their necks, they blink their large eyes in slow, rhythmic patterns, and they touch hands, letting the nerve endings in their fingertips taste the chemical state of the other.

Even their children are born into the continuum. Newborns are not washed; they are placed in the "Cradle-Pans," condensation collectors carved into the rock floor that are filled with a slurry of moss spores and insect eggs. The infant’s porous skin drinks this in immediately. Within hours, the first fuzz of moss begins to sprout, marking the child as an Othi. They learn to walk before they learn to speak, scaling the vertical rope bridges with a fearlessness that churns the stomach of any observer.

They are not conquerors of this place; they are its components. They are the damp grey mortar between the sharp stones of the Spire-Garden. They move with the slow deliberation of the tides, unbothered by the beetles in their hair or the slime on their skin, knowing that in the cold iron air, the only way to stay warm is to let the life of the world grow inside you.
