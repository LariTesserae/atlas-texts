The people of the Iron Sump do not live; they are held. They are less inhabitants of the plateau and more mineral deposits accumulated around the machinery, crustaceans clinging to the hull of a leviathan that does not know they are there. 

To survive here is to become an adjunct to the deafening thrum. Their bodies have adapted to the relentless vibration in ways that are more structural than biological. Over generations, the constant rattling has subtly thickened their joint capsules and hardened the periosteum—the membrane around their bones—leaving them with a stiff, deliberate gait. They do not walk so much as pivot and plant, moving with the careful, rolling momentum of a vessel navigating a heavy swell. Their spines have shortened and compressed to better absorb the *whump-thump* of the compressors, and their lung capacity has deepened to pull oxygen from the dense, pressurized air, though they can only breathe safely through the heavy filtration masks that weld themselves to their faces like iron gills.

The noise is the true environment, and it has reshaped their minds. With thoughts constantly drowned out by the grinding of tectonic gears, the Sump-dwellers have undergone a quieting of the interior monologue. They do not think in words, which are too fragile and sequential to survive the acoustic assault. They think in vibrations, in the timing of the massive pistons, in the tactile language of pressure changes against the skin. A shift in the rhythm of a distant compressor is not a sound to them, but a shift in their own mood—a tightening of the chest, a quickening of the pulse. They are empathically bound to the engines, reading the factory’s myriad cycles the way a sailor reads the sea. When the great gears slow for their agonizing, minutes-long rotation, the people feel a collective exhalation, a sudden, dizzying lightness in their bones. 

They wear the heavy, antiquated suits not just for protection from the toxic, oil-slicked runoff, but because the suits are their social skins. Beneath the boiled leather, the rust-resistant polymers, and the reinforced exoskeletons, their actual skin has gone pale and soft, starved of light in the shadowless glare. The suits are painted in the ochres and deep vermilions of the rusted pipescapes, marking their guilds and their roles—those who tend the weeping valves, those who read the sideways-crawling water, those who scrape the luminescent lichen from the cooling towers to harvest its faint, chemical heat. 

Communication is a physical act of intimacy and exertion. Throat-mics distort their voices into the same gravelly, mechanical barks emitted by the autonomous haulers. When they speak to one another face-to-face, they press the heavy helm of their suits against the other’s shoulder or chest, transmitting the vibration of their voices directly through the solid matter, bypassing the air entirely. It is a language of brief, utilitarian pulses: *Move. Hold. Leak. Danger.* For anything longer, they use the tapping of tools against the giant pipes, sending ringing codes that travel for miles through the steel, messages riding the skeleton of the city.

They are deeply solitary creatures, isolated even when standing shoulder-to-shoulder in the maintenance bays. The sheer scale of the machinery renders them insignificant. A person is a speck of condensation against an apartment-block compressor. There are families, but they are not bound by blood so much as by proximity to the same colossal engine. A child is not raised by parents, but by the rhythm of the sector they are born into. They learn early that the water that moves sideways along the pipes is a hazard—it can drag a suit’s weight against the metal, pulling a person into the crushing mesh of a slow-turning gear. They learn to read the sheen on the basalt, knowing which slick will support a boot and which will slide them into the boiling runoff. 

They harbor no animosity toward the machines, nor any romantic delusion of being their masters. They see themselves as the flukes, the wire-like shrubs eking out an existence in the cracks of the basalt. They are a patient, eroded people. Their culture is one of maintenance and endurance, lacking art or music, for those things require silence and space. Their only storytelling is the mapping of the decay: the exact year a specific patina will eat through a main valve, the century-long genealogy of a crack spreading across a cooling tower's base. They are historians of rust.

At the edges of their shifts, when they stand alone on the vast, wet plains of smooth rock, staring into the shadowless glare of the perpetual noon, they sometimes remove their gloves. They press their bare, pale palms flat against the slick, warm stone. They feel the slow crawl of the condensation, the distant trembling of the hill-sized gears, the patient oxidation of the iron. In those brief, stolen moments, they match their own pulse to the heartbeat of the Sump, finding a profound and lonely comfort in the knowledge that the steel-and-stone eternity will outlast them, and that their weeping, too, will eventually be reclaimed by the rust.
