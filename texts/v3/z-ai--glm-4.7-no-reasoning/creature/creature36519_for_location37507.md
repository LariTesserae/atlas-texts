They are not merely survivors; they are the digestion of the world.

The Dredgers are the architects of the clutter, the pale underlords of this humid dark. They are short, dense beings, their physiology warped by the gravity of the depth and the malnutrition of the trash-heap. Their skin is the color of unbleached parchment, translucent enough that the blue-purple tracery of their veins is visible beneath the surface, mapping them like the wiring embedded in the walls. They have no eyes—the sockets are smooth, fused over by bone, save for a slight ridge where sensory pits pick up the vibrations of the dripping water and the scuttle of prey.

Sight is a useless sense down here, where the light is a dying memory and the shadows are total. Instead, the Dredgers possess a face that is a study in tactical sensitivity. Their lower jaws are unhinged, capable of opening to an impossible angle, lined with rows of teeth that are not calcified bone but sharp, serrated keratin—like shark skin, constantly renewing. Above the mouth, the nose is a splayed, wet slug of flesh, twitching constantly as it samples the air, parsing the complex bouquet of wet rust, yeast, and the musk of crowded life. But it is the ears that dominate their visage: large, conical shells that swivel independently, covered in fine, sensory hairs, capturing the faintest scratch of a beetle’s leg meters away.

Their bodies are adapted for the claustrophobic squeeze. They are lithe but powerful, possessing a loose, collapsible skeleton that allows them to flatten themselves against the ground or contort through cracks in the "rock." Their arms are long, dangling past their knees, ending in hands that are grotesquely specialized. The fingers are elongated, the two middle fingers fused into a single, rigid probe tipped with a hardened, chitinous claw, perfect for prying apart the layers of synthetic sediment or ripping into the husk of a Molt-Beetle. The remaining fingers are opposable and delicate, capable of weaving the intricate plastic-fiber nets they use to trap the darting Slink-Rats.

They move with a fluid, silent grace that belies their grotesque appearance. To them, the floor is not solid; it is a texture to be read. Their bare feet are wide and callused, the soles ridged with traction patterns that grip the oily stone. As they walk, they spread their toes, feeling the tremors of the beetle swarms beneath the grime. They do not step blindly; they step into the rhythm of the cavern, matching their pace to the *plink, plink, plink* of the condensation, becoming part of the sonic landscape.

Their clothing is an extension of the environment, a second skin scavenged from the debris. They wear loincloths and simple tunics fashioned from the waxy leaves of the fleshy ferns, treated with oils to make them water-resistant. Over this, they drape layers of "leather"—actually strips of rubber harvested from ancient tires or seals found deep in the walls—bound together with sinew. A Dredger is never without a belt; this is their tool kit, strung with clay pots, sharpened lengths of pipe, and hooks made from rebar. The metal is polished to a mirror sheen, not for vanity, but to reflect the faint, pulse-like glow of the bioluminescent fungus, serving as a beacon for kin in the dark.

Society is a cooperative necessity. There is no hierarchy of leaders, only a hierarchy of utility. The females (distinguishable only by a broader pelvic spread, necessary for birthing the large-headed young) are usually the guardians of the "hearths"—pockets of chemical decay where heat rises. Here, they tend to the fires, fed not by wood, but by chunks of oily, energy-dense sludge mined from the battery veins that run through the trash-rock. The males roam the periphery, the "hunters," though they are more often foragers.

When a Dredger dies, they are not buried. That would be a waste of biomass. They are taken to the "Giving Wall," a section of the cavern where the erosion is thinnest and the Molt-Beetles are thickest. The body is laid upon the stone, and the clan chants—a low, thrumming resonance that vibrates in the chest, used to communicate over long distances and to signal to the scavengers that the feast is ready. Within hours, the body is gone, stripped to the bone, and the heavy, enriched bones are cracked open for their marrow, then carved into tools or beads. The circle is absolute.

Their young are few, born live and sleek, clinging to their mothers' backs like wet frogs. A Dredger child learns the sound of the Belly before it learns to walk. It learns the difference between the sound of settling stone and the shifting of a Slink-Rat. It learns to fear the silence, for silence in the Belly means the ecosystem has stalled, or a predator is holding its breath.

Most striking is their relationship with the "ghosts" of the place. Occasionally, a beetle will crack a wall and release a hiss of sterile, ancient air. When this happens, the Dredgers stop. They tilt their heads, flaring their nostrils, inhaling the scent of the world Above—the scent of ozone and dry nothingness. They call it "Ghost Breath." It is a religious experience, a reminder that there is a ceiling to their hell. They will capture the air in their clay pots, sealing it tight, and keep it as a talisman, shaking the pot occasionally to hear the hollow rattle of nothingness.

They are a people defined by the tactile. They read history in the layers of the wall—strata of blue wire, grey foam, and brown ceramic. To run a hand along the wall is to read a library of neglect. They know the chemical taste of the water, the metallic tang of the iron ribs, and the musky sweetness of the fern groves. They are the ultimate recyclers, living in the intestine of a lost civilization, turning the waste of the past into the flesh of the future. They are not miserable; they are merely *present*, fully integrated into the roaring, vibrant, damp echo chamber that is their home.
