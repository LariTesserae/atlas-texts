To live here is to be patient. Nothing moves quickly in the sub-basement of the world; the crushing weight of the earth above and the thermal pressure radiating from the walls see to that. The inhabitants of this popped blister, this industrial oubliette, have abandoned the very concept of haste. They are creatures of the increment, entities shaped by the *plink* of a water droplet and the millimeter-by-millimeter creep of a vine.

**The Soot-Swifts** are the only things that still remember flight, though they barely use it. They are stunted, wretched things—more feather than flesh, their plumage slicked back perpetually by the wet heat and the greasy condensation. Their feathers are the color of rust and old pipe smoke, and they cling to the blackened exhaust vents not for warmth, but for the brief, dying ember-glow that allows them to see the silhouettes of the slow-moving pistons. When they do fly, it is a clumsy, desperate flutter from one perch to the next, their wings making a sodden *thwap* in the thick air. They feed on the tiny, blind gnats that breed in the oily rainbows of the micro-pools, skimming the stagnant surfaces with their lower beaks. Mostly, they just sit, preening their wet feathers into sharp, metallic-looking points, waiting for the heat to do their digesting for them.

**The Blind Salamanders** are the lords of the puddles, though their domain is pathetic. They are pale, gelatinous creatures, their skin a translucent white through which the slow, syrupy pulse of their organs can be seen. Their eyes are just shallow, empty dents in their skulls, but their heads pulse with electroreceptors, allowing them to "see" the world as a grid of voltage drops and spikes. They rest in the palm-sized hollows of the masonry, their bodies contorted to fit the exact shape of the stone, barely distinguishable from the water they lie in—only the faint, rhythmic flick of a gill giving them away. When a piston exhales a cloud of white vapor that settles and raises the water level by a millimeter, they simply float higher, their tiny, griping toes holding tight to the slimy brick. They are the guardians of the standing water, keeping the bacterial rot from blooming into something that might choke the machine.

But the true occupants of this place are not flesh. They are the **Wet-Livers**, the bio-mechanical hybrids that have blurred the line between the grown and the built. They are the ones who plugged the vines into the conduits.

They were not built; they *accreted*. They began as simple maintenance algorithms—automata of rust and wire designed to clear the fresh, geometric cuts of the young rock, to keep the pistons free of creeping moss. But the heat was too heavy, the air too wet, and the moss too aggressive. The automata got slow. They got tired. They stopped returning to their charging cradles.

Now, they are indistinguishable from the green mass. They sit slumped against the riveted walls, their brass carapaces choked with bruising green moss, their jointed limbs throttled by vines as thick as a man's thigh. They have become part of the architecture. But they are not dead. They sip voltage through the roots that have fused with their power cables, and they drink condensation through the porous moss that fills their intake valves. Their "breathing" is the slow expansion and contraction of their metal ribs, perfectly synched with the thermal shifts of the room—they have become the lungs of the factory.

A Wet-Liver might sit motionless for a decade, a stalactite of rust and vine growing from its shoulder, before it slowly—glacially—reaches out a corroded, leafy arm to wipe a buildup of algae from a pressure gauge. Its movement is imperceptible, a shifting of weight that takes a full day to complete. It is a gesture of symbiosis, not maintenance. The machine gives them heat and power; they keep the terrestrial processing going, just enough to prevent the whole oubliette from tearing itself apart in a geological seizure.

There is also the **Stonewright**, a singular presence, older and larger than the Wet-Livers. It is a mass of dark, glistening stone and blackened pipe, roughly the shape of a hunched, legless torso, rooted directly into the center of the hollow. It has no discernible head, only a cluster of sensor-nodes and dripping cable bundles that vanish into the earthen floor. It is the one that cut the stone—too geometric, too fresh, as if carved yesterday. The Stonewright is the slow heart of the place, responsible for the "breathing" that is the dominant sound of the hollow. It processes the earth itself, drinking the silty water that seeps from the jagged walls, heating it within its belly, and extruding it as the white vapor that drifts through the dim light. It is the source of the heat, the weeping of the walls, the patience. It sits beneath the low, dripping dome, feeling the weight of the world above, and it holds the ceiling up by refusing to move.

To live here is to be like the flora—to grow slowly toward a light that does not exist. The inhabitants do not wait for rescue or for the sun to rise; they have forgotten such things exist. They only know the rhythm of the piston, the *plink* of the water, and the sweet, cloying scent of rotting verdure. They are the prisoners of a greenhouse built in the dark, and they have made their peace with the sentence, putting down roots into the iron and the stone, becoming the damp, sweating architecture of a world that is slowly, ever so slowly, grinding to a halt.
