What lives in the Weft is not an organism, and it is not a community. It is an ongoing event.

To ask who lives here assumes a noun—a discrete thing with boundaries, a self that persists. The Weft has no selves. It has *processes*, and the processes are so interleaved, so interdependent, that separating them into species or kingdoms or individuals is a convenience of language that fails entirely here. What lives in the Weft is the Weft, and the Weft is a verb.

---

Consider first the springtails.

There are thousands—tens of thousands—of them in the hollow at any given moment. *Sminthurides*, or something adjacent; the taxonomy of things this small and this isolated tends toward uncertainty. They are pale, nearly white, the color of the mycelium they travel through. Each one is a millimeter long, perhaps a millimeter and a half. They have no sound. They have no weight. A hundred of them on your fingernail would feel like nothing at all.

But they are there, and they are working.

They move through the interstitial spaces of the hummocks—not on the surface, but *within*, between the sphagnum stems, inside the lattice of living and dead material that makes up each mound. They feed on fungal hyphae, on decaying plant matter, on the bacterial films that coat every surface in this hydric world. They molt continuously; their exoskeletons, shed and abandoned, add a structural element to the hummocks' interior, a chitin trace so fine it's functionally invisible but present nonetheless, a record of occupation written in a language nothing reads.

And they die. Constantly. Their lives span weeks at most in this cold—each one a brief arc of movement and feeding and reproduction, then cessation. Their bodies decompose where they fall, which is always *inside* the hummock, always within the mass, and the decomposition feeds the bacteria which feed the next generation of springtails which will also die inside the hummock, and this is not a flaw in the system but *the system itself*—a nutrient cycle so tight, so perfectly enclosed, that the word *cycle* is wrong because a cycle implies a return to a starting point, and there is no starting point, only a slow spiral upward as each generation's death adds a fractional layer to the living mass above.

The springtails do not know they live in a city. They do not know the voids exist. They never see the sky, have never seen the sky, will never see the sky. Their world is the inside of a hummock: dark, wet, permeated by mycelium, warm enough to survive because the mass around them metabolizes. They are, in the strictest sense, endoliths—creatures of the inside—and their inside is made of other creatures' outsides, and those outsides are made of other insides, and at no point does a boundary appear that you could point to and say: *here is where the substrate ends and the inhabitant begins*.

---

Consider the mycelium.

It is not a fungus in the way you might imagine a fungus—no fruiting body, no mushroom, no visible sign of its presence except the pale threads revealed when a hummock's flank splits. It does not reproduce sexually, or if it does, it has not done so within the memory of the hollow. It spreads. That is all it does. It spreads and it threads and it connects.

The mycelium permeates every hummock in the Weft, running horizontally through the living layer, vertically into the peat-analog below, diagonally along the drainage channels where water moves fastest. It connects the hummocks to each other. A hypha from the northernmost mound can be traced—*has* been traced, in the few studies that have been done—to the southernmost, a span of sixty meters, all one organism, or all one network, the distinction being meaningless when a hyphal tip anastomoses with its neighbor and shares cytoplasm and shares nutrients and shares *information*, if that word can be used for the chemical gradients that propagate along fungal threads at speeds measurable in centimeters per hour.

The mycelium knows where the water is. It knows where the minerals are. It grows toward the ridge seams where the weeping basalt offers iron and manganese and trace sulfur, and it grows away from the hummock crests where the marginal light favors the moss and the competition is too fierce. It has *preferences*—not conscious, not deliberate, but structural, encoded in the chemistry of its cell walls and the osmotic pressures of its vacuoles. It behaves as if it were making decisions. It behaves as if it were an engineer.

And it is an engineer. The pale threads serve as reinforcement, as rebar—the original description had it right. A hummock without mycelium would slump, would collapse under its own waterlogged weight, would spread and flatten and become a mere carpet. The mycelium gives it structure. The mycelium gives it *tension*, and the tension allows height, and height allows the voids, and the voids are the dwellings, and the dwellings are the city, and the city is the mycelium, and at no point does a boundary appear—

You see the problem. The Weft refuses to be dissected.

---

Consider the lichen.

There is only one kind. A grey crust, *Ochrolecia* or something like it, that grows on the interior walls of the voids where nothing else can survive—the humidity too high, the light too low, the temperature too stable for anything that photosynthesizes with any ambition. The lichen does not photosynthesize with ambition. It photosynthesizes with *patience*, which is different.

It grows a millimeter per decade. Perhaps. The measurements are uncertain because the timescale makes measurement absurd; you would need to visit the voids regularly for a century to be sure of anything, and no one has, and no one will. The lichen is there because it got there, at some point, and it has not left, and it will not leave, because leaving is not something lichens do. They are where they are until they are not, and when they are not, they have become something else—soil, substrate, surface for the next thing that might arrive, though in the voids of the Weft, nothing else arrives.

The lichen is the closest thing the Weft has to a resident. It occupies the interiors. It makes them *occupied*. Without the lichen, the voids would be mere gaps, negative spaces in the moss. With the lichen, they have an interior life—barely, marginally, at a timescale that makes geological erosion seem hasty. The lichen is the spirit of the place, if spirit means something that persists without intention, without awareness, without any quality we associate with spirit except *presence*.

The lichen does not move. It does not feed, exactly; it *receives*, the algal component of its dual nature providing energy from the dim light, the fungal component providing structure and mineral access. It is itself a Weft in miniature, a collaboration so tight the partners have lost their separate identities, and this is why it thrives here: because the Weft is a place where collaboration so tight that identities merge is not exceptional but *foundational*. The lichen is the Weft's most honest expression.

---

Consider the water.

The water lives here. It must be said. It enters at the ridge seams, mineral-rich and cold, and it moves through the hummocks and the mycelium and the springtails and the lichen and the voids and the drainage channels and the peat-analog and the basalt fractures, and it leaves only to re-enter, only to begin again, a cycle so enclosed that the water in the hollow today may have been the same water that was here a year ago, a decade, a century—filtered and absorbed and transpired and condensed and re-absorbed so many times that each molecule carries a history of the entire system, each hydrogen atom a record of every surface it has touched, every membrane it has crossed, every cell it has briefly constituted before moving on.

The water is the medium. The water is the message. The water is the circulatory system of a body that has no skin, the lymph of an organism that has no outline. Without the water, the Weft is dead moss and cold rock. With the water, it is *alive*—and the water is always here, always moving, always being transformed from one state to another (liquid to vapor to liquid, mineral-rich to filtered to mineral-rich again, intracellular to extracellular to intracellular), and the transformation is the life, and the life is the transformation, and this is what lives in the Weft: transformation itself.

---

Consider the moss.

The sphagnum. The liverworts. The clubmoss standing in rigid fingers where the light permits. They are the architecture and the architect, the wall and the resident, the city and the citizen. They photosynthesize in the dim perpetual dusk with an efficiency that should not be possible—each cell a lens, each leaf a solar collector angled toward the grey light that penetrates the cloud and the crags, each hummock a compound eye turned upward in a place where there is almost nothing to see.

But photosynthesis is marginal here. The moss knows this, if knowing can be attributed to a plant, and it has supplemented: the chemo-synthesis at the rock face, the slow geophagy, the patient consumption of basalt minerals that provides the bulk of the energy budget in this cold and lightless place. The moss eats rock. The moss *is* what it eats—mineral and water and carbon, arranged in the only geometry that works here, the hummock-shape, the dome, the overlapping mound that sheds water on its exterior and holds water in its interior and creates, through its mere existence and growth pattern, the voids that make it a city.

The moss is the Weft's body. The mycelium is its skeleton. The springtails are its digestion. The lichen is its patience. The water is its blood. And all of these are the same thing—interpenetrated, interdependent, impossible to separate without killing the system entirely, like trying to extract the flour from a baked loaf.

---

What lives in the Weft, then, is a *consortium*.

Not a community, because communities are made of members, and the Weft has no members—only functions. Not an ecosystem, because ecosystems have boundaries and inputs and outputs, and the Weft's boundaries are its ridges and its input is the weeping basalt and its output is *nothing*, or nothing yet, or nothing visible, because everything that enters stays, transformed, incorporated, grown.

What lives here is a single distributed process that has been running since the hollow formed—since the basalt teeth erupted and the fractures opened and the water began to weep and the first spore or hypha or fragment of moss landed in the cleft and found conditions adequate, found conditions *perfect*, because the Weft is not a place that tolerates life; it is a place that *demands* it, that makes life inevitable by providing nothing else, by being so marginal, so cold, so dim, so waterlogged that only the completely adapted can survive, and the completely adapted, once established, *becomes* the place.

This is what the Weft teaches, if places teach: that the boundary between organism and environment is a convenience of perception, not a fact of nature. That what lives in a place and the place itself are, given enough time and enough pressure and enough cold, the same thing. That a city can build itself without a builder, without intention, without consciousness, without anything we recognize as agency—and still be a city, still have architecture and inhabitants and streets and dwellings and a clock (the crackle of thawing ice each morning) and a future (the slow engulfment of the ridges, the deepening of the chambers, the absolute quiet).

What lives in the Weft is the Weft. And the Weft is still becoming itself.
