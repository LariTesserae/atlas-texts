The Ostrans do not walk. Not in the way you or I understand the verb—the placing of one foot before another, the hearty compression of soil, the negotiation with a surface that pushes back. To walk is to be of the ground, and the ground on the Rise of Ostra is an open wound, weeping and raw, still deciding if it belongs to the planet or to the void. The Ostrans belong to the city, and the city has divorced itself from the ground. And so the Ostrans drift.

They are tall. They must be. In a metropolis where the buildings are sparse and the boulevards are made of empty air, where a thousand structures are scattered across the footprint of a small nation, the body must be legible from a distance. They are long-limbed, attenuated, stretched like taffy by the loose grip of gravity and the sheer verticality of their home. Their proportions are unsettling to an imagination raised on Earth—a ratio of limb to torso that suggests the stick-figure drawings of children, or the shadows cast by saplings in winter. But there is nothing frail about them. To be fragile here is to be shattered. Their bones—if they are bones, and not some graduated form of the macro-molecular polymer that folds their towers into being—are latticed things, hollow and reinforced, pneumatic architectures that can withstand the sudden, violent updrafts that roar between the hovering monoliths.

They are wrapped in cloth. Or rather, in a material that behaves like cloth but remembers its shape. It flows behind them in ribbons and pennants, catching the thin, rhythmic breath of the wind, but it never tangles, never clings. It is programmed to respond to the air, to stiffen into an aerodynamic rudder when the gusts sharpen, and to relax into a drape when the air stills. The fabric is the color of the Rise on a clear day—a pale, anemic grey-blue—but at the hems, where the material thins, it flickers with the same dim, ultraviolet fluorescence that bleeds from the lichen-mats below. They carry the light of the ground with them, even as they float above it. It is the only marking they have.

Their faces are an exercise in restraint. The Ostrans have evolved—or engineered themselves—toward a kind of functional minimalism. The wind at three miles does not howl, but it breathes, and that breath is a cold that freezes moisture instantly on exposed skin. Their features are recessed, shielded beneath a brow that shelves like the escarpments of their home. Their eyes are narrow, dark slits—horizontal, unblinking—that filter the glare of the hard-light bridges and the white fog that churns below. You cannot read an Ostran's expression. Their faces are the faces of the towers they live in: translucent at the edges, jade-green, faintly opaque, offering only the ghost-marks of what lies inside. When they turn their heads, you can see, for a moment, the faint tracery of their nervous system illuminated beneath the skin—a branching, river-delta of pale light that pulses in time with the electric hum of the gravitational stabilizers. They do not breathe through their noses or mouths, at least not visibly. There is no steam-plume from an Ostran, no cloud of warmth exhaled into the freezing air. Their respiration is internalized, efficient, silent. They take in the air, crush it, extract what they need, and expel the remainder as a faint, heatless shimmer that distorts the light behind them like a mirage.

They are solitary, even in company. The city is designed for beings who treat distance as a triviality, and the Ostrans embody this philosophy in their social geometry. You will see two of them on a hard-light bridge, a half-mile of luminous force stretching between two hovering spires, and they will stand at opposite ends, facing outward, looking not at each other but at the vast, wet panorama of the Rise. This is not hostility. This is not even indifference. It is simply that an Ostran requires a certain volume of space around them—a hemisphere of empty air—to feel present. To stand close to another is to collapse the waveform of your existence. They communicate across these distances through subtle shifts in the ultraviolet trim of their garments, or through the faint, subsonic vibrations they send down through the hard-light beneath their feet, which travel the beams like signals through fiber optics, received not by the ears but by the bones. When they do speak aloud—which is rare, and reserved for what they call "thick moments," times of acute decision or grief—their voices are low, resonant, and sound, to a human ear, like the drip-drip-drip of water striking the weeping rocks, or the bass thrum of the migrating herds felt through the soles of the feet. It is a voice meant to be felt, not heard. A seepage, not a sound.

They are not predators. They are not prey. This is what makes them so strange amidst the biological thunder of the Rise. Below them, the six-legged grazers move in their thousands, nervous, frantic, steam pouring from their snouts, driven by the active pulse of a world that has not settled. Above them, the sky-wardens circle, wingspans blotting out the sun, diving with a violence that makes the air scream. The ecosystem of Ostra is a frenzy of blood and breath and panic, a landscape of chase and flight visible from miles away. And the Ostrans simply... hover above it. They watch. Sometimes, from the edge of a hard-light boulevard, an Ostran will look down at the migration, at the churn of fur and steam and fog, and you will see their head tilt—a slow, mechanical gesture, like the shifting of a telescope—and the dark slits of their eyes will widen, just barely, to take in the full scope of the biological engine below. There is something in that widening that might be admiration, or might be appetite, or might be the simple, aesthetic appreciation of a being who lives in a city that is grown, not built, and who recognizes in the frenzy of the herds a kindred chaos—a system that, like their own architecture, is still becoming, still deciding what it wants to be.

But they do not hunt. And they are not hunted. The sky-wardens will not approach the city. The grazers will not walk beneath the hovering spires. It is as if the gravitational nodes that anchor the towers emit a frequency that the fauna of Ostra cannot tolerate, a boundary written in force that separates the biological from the technological. The Ostrans live in the seam. They are the only creatures on the Rise that exist in both worlds simultaneously—the raw, wet, weeping world of stone and steam and blood, and the still, jade-green, humming world of the city. They are amphibious, in their way: able to breathe the freezing, glass-sharp air of the highlands, and able to breathe the processed, neutral atmosphere of their towers. Their lungs—or whatever serves as lungs—are dual-chambered, capable of extracting oxygen from both the saturated fog and the dry, recycled air of the interior spaces. This is why they can stand on the open hard-light bridges for hours, motionless, unbothered by the cold, while the wind pulls at their garments and the fog curls around their ankles like a cat. They are not suffering. They are synthesizing.

They do not sleep as we sleep. There is no lying down, no closing of eyes, no surrender to unconsciousness. To sleep is to be vulnerable, and to be vulnerable is to be part of the ecosystem, and the ecosystem is a place of teeth and talons and panic. Instead, they rest in place. They stand on the hard-light bridges or in the wide, empty boulevards of air, and they go still. Their UV trim dims. The branching light of their nervous system flickers and fades. Their respiration slows to a seepage so faint it barely disturbs the fog. And from a distance, in the pale light of the Ostran sky, they look like another set of the crystalline silicon trees that grow from the weeping rock below—tall, thin, still, extruded from the surface of the city, rooted not in soil but in force. It is impossible to tell, sometimes, whether an Ostran is awake or resting until you step close enough to feel the faint, electric warmth that bleeds from their skin, a heat so low it barely registers, a heat that reminds you they are not mineral, not silicon, not stone, but something alive, something that has chosen to live in a place that has not yet chosen to be a world.

They are few. A thousand structures, and perhaps only ten thousand Ostrans, scattered across a country-sized sprawl of hovering towers and empty air. They are an experiment in sparsity, in the architecture of distance. They do not crowd. They do not cluster. They do not build walls around themselves or close the doors of their towers. The towers do not have doors. They have thresholds—membranes of hard-light that can be thickened into opacity or thinned into nothing, controlled by the will of the inhabitant. Privacy is not a spatial condition on Ostra; it is a temporal one. You are alone when you choose to be alone, and you are present when you choose to be present, and the city—vast, humming, green, and half-empty—accommodates this without question.

And yet, for all their solitude, they are not lonely. Or if they are, their loneliness has a different shape than ours. It is not the ache of absence but the pressure of presence—the awareness that the city is alive around them, that the gravitational stabilizers hum in their teeth, that the weeping rocks drip a trillion tiny droplets into the crevices below, that the herds thunder and the sky-wardens scream and the fog swirls in its programmed patterns and the wind breathes its long, rhythmic respiration across the face of the Rise. They are alone in the way that a single note is alone in a symphony—distinct, separate, but inseparable from the sound of the whole. The Ostrans are the quiet notes. The high, sustained, almost inaudible tones that underlie the thunder and the drip and the hum. The notes that you feel in your teeth.

They will outlast the herds. They will outlast the sky-wardens. The biology of the Rise is a frenzy, but frenzies exhaust themselves. The ecosystem will settle, or it will collapse, and the grazers will thin and the warden's wings will falter and the steam will stop pluming from six-legged snouts. But the Ostrans and their city will remain, hovering above the wet, dark stone, growing new towers from the ghost-marks of the old, folding new structures out of the raw, juvenile geology below. They are the builders of a world that has not yet decided what it is. And they are patient. They have the patience of stone that has not yet eroded, of air that has not yet stilled, of a wind that does not howl but breathes—in, out, in, out—waiting for the Rise to finish becoming itself, so that they can finish becoming whatever they are meant to be next.
