To live here is to be a janitor of the ghost machine, a steward of the metabolism.

The primary denizens are the Gardeners, though to call them "creatures" is a misnomer; they are autonomous units of biological maintenance. They are roughly humanoid in silhouette, but constructed entirely of compacted, woven moss and dense fungal hyphae. Their skin has the texture of a dry sponge, rough and absorbent, wearing a coat of pale lichen that mimics the uniform coveralls of the old workers. They have no faces, only a smooth, convex surface of sensory nodes that glows with the faint, bioluminescent pulse of the floor-moss. They move with a glacial, deliberate slowness, never rushing, carrying shears made of sharpened slate or bone. They do not prune the wild growth; they prune the *order*. They trim the square leaves of the giant ferns back to perfect right angles. They scrape the red, petrified rust from the exposed gears to ensure the friction remains constant. They do not sleep; they merely enter a dormant state by rooting themselves into the spongy floor during the dim hours, drawing nutrients from the soil until the "shift" begins again.

Higher up, nested in the rigid cables of ivy that bridge the smokestacks, live the Rivet-Beetles. They are solitary, heavy-shelled creatures, their carapaces segmented to mimic the iron plating of the city's hull. The subtle weirdness of this place has imprinted on them; their shells do not just look like metal, they are cold to the touch and conduct the faint electricity that hums through the vines. They are the waste-recyclers of the canopy. They feed on the dead, woody tissue of the iron-hard trees and the nodules of oxidized metal that form in the bark. When a beetle dies, it does not rot. It simply oxidizes, turning into a statue of rust that eventually crumbles into metallic dust, feeding the roots of the next generation of ferns. They are silent, save for the *clink-clink* of their legs as they traverse the suspension bridges, sounding for all the world like a loose tool falling down a shaft.

Deeper still, in the damp hollows where the exhaust vents exhale cool air, dwell the Ozone-Eaters. These are small, amphibious blobs of translucent gel, barely distinct from the puddles they inhabit. They are the filters of the Fungineer’s Quarter. They absorb the metallic scent of the air and the faint, toxic residue of the old geothermal pumps, excreting clear, clean water that drips down to join the sap-streams. They possess a simple, cruel beauty: their internal organs are visible through their skin, arranged in the geometric grid patterns that dominate this ecosystem. They have no mouth, only a pore that breathes in the heavy, chemical air.

And then there is the Architect.

There is only one, for the Quarter is small and requires only a single mind to oversee the digestion. It resides in the deepest basement, where the roots have coiled around the ancient turbines. It is massive—a brain-like structure of white mycelium intertwined with the copper wiring of the city's central processing unit. It does not have eyes or ears in the traditional sense; it feels the vibration of the pumps (sound 0.8) and the tension in the load-bearing walls (vegetation 2.6). It is the thing that turned the leaves into squares and the bark into numbered grids. It is the will that enforces the truce. It does not think in thoughts, but in flow rates and structural integrity. It is the memory of the city made flesh, dreaming of a geometry that is neither purely natural nor entirely artificial.

There are no swarms here, no chaotic chattering of life. Every living thing has a job, a function, a place in the assembly line of decay. The fauna is rare (0.7) because there is no room for passengers, only crew. Even the silent, sleek bird nesting in the rafters of the collapsed warehouse is not truly a bird; upon closer inspection, its feathers are made of thin, papery shavings of rusted metal, and it does not sing, for it has no voice box—only a resonator that hums in harmony with the subsonic thrum of the infrastructure.

To live here is to be part of the machine that has learned to breathe. You are either a cog, a wire, or the oil that keeps the turning smooth.
