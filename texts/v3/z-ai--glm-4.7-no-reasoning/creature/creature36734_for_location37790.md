Who lives here is not a community in any way you would recognize from the surface. There is no society here, no culture, no shared narrative of origin or destiny. There is only the System, and the many forms it takes.

**The Pale Stewards**
They are not many—perhaps a few dozen in this single alveolus, perhaps a few hundred throughout the whole vast body. They are human, or were human, or are what human becomes when the boundary between self and environment dissolves. They move through the warm dark like ghosts, naked, their skin no longer a barrier but an interface. It is mottled with colonizing lichen that provides what little light they need to see by. Their necks have softened, the gill-slits closing and opening with the rhythm of the humid air, filtering the spore-thick breath of the chamber. Their lungs are large, cavernous, adapted to pull oxygen from air that is more water than gas. They do not speak. They have not spoken for generations. They communicate through pheromone emission, through touch, through the subtle manipulation of the valves and levers that are their only inheritance. They are born in the warm creches near the digestion vats, delivered by maggot-heat and fungal-midwifery, and they die quietly, their bodies absorbed back into the root-mat within hours, leaving only the lichen that was their light.

They do not think of themselves as workers. They do not think of themselves at all, in the way you mean. They are not *servants* to the factory; they are its nerve endings, its fine manipulators, the delicate fingers of a hand that reaches across kilometers of subterranean darkness. The woman who adjusts the valve does not wonder what the valve controls. She does not wonder what the substrate blocks are for, or where the pipes carry their fluids, or who benefits from the labor of this place. These questions have no meaning here. The valve needs adjusting. The system maintains itself. She is part of the maintenance. She was born to adjust that valve, and when she can no longer adjust it, she will feed the roots that feed the algae that feed the moths that pollinate the tubes that carry the light that makes it possible to see the valve in the first place. She is a loop in a larger loop, and she is content in the way a heartbeat is content—not with emotion, but with rhythm.

Sometimes one of them stops. Just stops, standing motionless in the amber dimness, eyes open, breathing slowly, while the isopods crawl across their feet and the condensation beads on their shoulders. These are not breakdowns. These are the system thinking—processing some imbalance too subtle for mechanical sensors, some shift in the nutrient flow or the vibration frequency that requires stillness to detect. The others step around them without interest. The pause will end. The work will continue. Or it will not, and the standing figure will slowly settle, knees buckling over days, descending into the root-mat, and the factory will have digested another of its own.

**The Armored Legions**
The isopods do not live here in the way the stewards live here. The isopods *are* here. They are as much a feature of the chamber as the pipes or the pillars—older than either, present since before the first iron was threaded through the mycelium, evolved from the blind pale things that crawled in the deep root-systems long before the factory came. But the factory did not displace them. The factory *recruited* them.

They are the size of your forearm, and there are tens of thousands of them, perhaps more—the floor literally shifts with their movement, a rustling carpet of articulated segments and clicking legs. Their shells are not the pale translucent armor of cave-dwellers but a dark, lacquered chitin, almost black, hardened by the minerals they consume from the pipe-scale and the condensation. They are maintenance. They are digestion. They are the immune system of a body that has no blood but has plenty of fluid, and that fluid leaks, and it must be managed.

When a pipe ruptures—and pipes rupture constantly, iron soft and sagging in the humid warmth—they swarm. Not with purpose, not with coordination, but with the simple magnetic pull of heat and moisture and the pheromone exhaust the factory vents specifically to draw them. They coat the break in a clicking, clattering mass, their mouthparts scraping the mineral buildup, their bodies sealing the gap with their own shells while the condensation welds them in place. Some die there, cooked by the steam, their bodies becoming part of the seal. The others eat them later, when the pipe has cooled. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is lost. There is only a temporary relocation of mass from one part of the system to another.

They do not know they are workers. They know heat, they know moisture, they know the chemical signature of their own dead, and they know the endless imperative to feed and breed and shed and feed again. The factory has shaped them—selecting for the pheromone response, for the size that fits the pipe-gaps, for the heat tolerance that lets them survive the steam—but it has not *trained* them. They are wild things, utterly wild, and their wildness is exactly what makes them useful. A domesticated isopod would need to be directed, managed, supervised. These need only to be summoned, and their nature does the rest.

And they are everywhere. In the pipes, crawling through the warm dark interiors where the fluid flows around them. In the fungal bark, boring into the hollows to lay their eggs. On the bodies of the stewards, cleaning the dead skin and the lichen-growth with the same mindless thoroughness they bring to the pipe-scale. On *you*, already, investigating, tasting, finding you neither food nor hazard nor interest, and moving on. You are not part of the system. You are not even an obstruction. You are a momentary perturbation, a stone dropped in a river, and the water closes around you and continues.

**The Aerial Servants**
The moths are the only beauty here, and they are a mechanical beauty, the beauty of a well-designed component. They are the size of your hand, their wings broad and papery, scaled in shades of pale green and amber that match the light they navigate by. They spiral around the algae-tubes in helical paths, their wingbeats a soft papery counterpoint to the mechanical shriek of the bone-saws, and they are pollinators—but the algae they pollinate is not a natural thing. It was engineered, centuries ago, by minds that are now digestive pulp in the root-mat, and it cannot reproduce without the moths, and the moths cannot live without the algae, and neither can exist without the tubes that hold the one and the light that guides the other, and the tubes are made of glass blown by stewards who learned the craft from stewards before them, and the glass is shaped by the heat of the maggot-vats, and the maggots are fed by the processed fungal wood, and the fungal wood is cut by the bone-saws, and the bone-saws are powered by the hydraulic pumps, and the pumps are maintained by the isopods, and the isopods are summoned by the pheromone exhaust, and the pheromone exhaust is controlled by the stewards.

The moth does not know any of this. The moth knows light, and it knows the chemical signature of the algae-bloom, and it knows the involuntary twitch of its abdomen that deposits pollen on the receptive culture-pad. It is a link in a chain that has no start and no end, only an endless looping of dependency and production, and it is beautiful in the way a gear is beautiful—precisely because it does not know and does not need to know.

When the bio-alarm sounds, the moths scatter. Not in fear—fear implies a concept of future danger—but in simple reflex, a hardwired response to the frequency of the acid-keens that signals a disruption in the order of things. They flutter against the fungal walls, against the lichen, against the faces of the stewards, and then the alarm stops, and they return to their spiraling, and the disruption is over, and the work continues.

**The Silk-Architects**
Above, in the dark vault where the amber light fades to nothing, the web-spiders hold dominion. You cannot see them clearly—only the suggestion of large, gliding bodies, the creak of their silk cables under tension, the swaying of their architecture in the vibration of the machines. They are not the spiders of the surface, spinning traps for the unwary. They are weavers of infrastructure, and their silk is not for catching prey but for linking the conveyor belts, for binding the chitin-linkage, for creating the flexible joints that let the living factory flex and shift without breaking.

They are the size of cats, perhaps larger, their bodies pale and soft, their legs long and delicate, their spinnerets complex and specialized—six of them, producing six different silks for six different industrial applications. The strongest is the cable-silk, thick as cord, used to bear the weight of the conveyor tracks. The finest is the linkage-silk, woven into the chitin-chain, providing elasticity. The rest are adhesives, sealants, structural meshes, and one—a strange, iridescent thread—that conducts something, though you cannot tell what, threading through the root-mass in patterns too regular to be accidental, connecting nodes you cannot identify in the darkness above.

They do not descend. They have no need to. The silk they require is produced by the factory—synthesized in vats from the proteins of the processed fungal wood—and hauled up to them by the conveyor belts, great bundles of raw material that they weave with a speed and precision no machine could match. They are the looms of this place, and their weaving is the binding that holds the living and the mechanical together, and they work in the dark because they have always worked in the dark, and the dark is where the silk is strongest.

Sometimes a cable snaps. The vibration travels through the entire web-network, and the spiders respond instantly, gliding on draglines to the breach, spinning replacement cable even as the conveyor below stutters and jams. They are the fastest responders in the system—faster than the stewards, faster than the isopods, faster than the bio-alarms. They are the reason the factory can flex at all, and they ask nothing in return but silk and darkness and the occasional vibration that tells them they are needed.

**The Heat-Engines**
Deepest down, in the processing vats where the fungal wood is broken down into pulp, the maggots hold court. They are the most disturbing thing here, and they are disturbing precisely because they are the most *intentional*—not in the sense of having intentions, but in the sense of being most clearly, obviously, unashamedly *bred for a purpose*. The isopods were recruited. The moths were shaped. The maggots were *made*.

They are the size of dogs, pale and legless and eyeless, their bodies segmented and soft, their mouths circular and ringed with grinding ridges. They writhe. They always writhe. They writhe in masses around the digestion tanks, packed so densely they form a single squirming carpet of pale flesh, and the heat they generate is staggering—a furnace of metabolisms, each body burning at the limit of its biochemistry, producing the temperatures needed to break down the lignin of the fungal wood, to reduce the substrate to cellulose pulp, to drive the reactions that feed the algae and fuel the pumps and sustain the entire impossible edifice.

They do not eat the wood. They do not eat anything, in the way you understand eating. They are fed—mechanically, by conveyor, a paste of nutrient slurry delivered at intervals calibrated to maintain the population at exactly the density required for the thermal output the processing vats demand. They consume the slurry and they produce heat and they produce waste and the waste is the slurry, or part of it, cycled back through the root-mat, through the fungal digestion, through the processing that creates the paste: the great snake of consumption eating its own tail, and the maggots are a segment of that snake, and they do not know and cannot know and would not care if they could.

They breed constantly. They die constantly. The mass remains stable because the births and the deaths are balanced by the slurry-paste and the thermal demand and the slow adjustments of the stewards who monitor the vat-temperatures with their hands and increase or decrease the feed by fractions of a degree. A maggot lives perhaps three weeks. In that time, it writhes, it metabolizes, it breeds, it dies. It never sees light. It never knows stillness. It is heat, and when it can no longer be heat, it becomes food, and the cycle does not pause.

**The Body Itself**
And beneath all of this, beneath the stewards and the isopods and the moths and the spiders and the maggots, beneath the pipes and the vats and the conveyor belts and the algae-tubes, beneath the fungal pillars and the root-mat floor and the lichen that provides the light—there is the mycelial network. The great body. The thing that this chamber is merely an organ of.

It is not a mushroom. It is not a fungus in any way that implies the fruiting bodies you know—the caps, the stems, the brackets that form the pillars of this place. Those are its *skin*, its *surface*, the part of it that interacts with the air and the light and the machines. The network itself is deeper—kilometers deeper, threading through the dark earth, through the stone and the clay and the water, connecting chamber to chamber, alveolus to alveolus, lung to liver to heart of a body that has no brain and no will and no name but is alive in the only way that matters: it grows, it processes, it responds, it maintains itself.

The heartbeat you feel is its pulse—the slow pumping of nutrients through the network, driven by osmosis and capillary action and the differential pressures of chambers deep below, operating at the pace of geology, a rhythm measured not in beats per minute but in beats per hour, per day. A single cycle of the network's nutrient flow takes perhaps a week, and in that week the fungal pillars grow by millimeters, the pipes shift by fractions, the lichen blooms and dies, a generation of maggots is born and writhes and feeds the roots that feed the algae that feeds the moths, and a steward adjusts a valve, and the system endures.

It does not know you are here. It does not know *it* is here, in the way you mean knowing—there is no consciousness, no awareness, no ghost in this machine that is also a body that is also a city that is also a world. There is only process. There is only the endless, mindless, beautiful, terrible integration of life and industry into a single functioning whole that has no purpose you can discern, no product you can identify, no goal beyond its own continuation.

Unless—unless the product *is* continuation. Unless the purpose of the factory is to be a factory, and the purpose of the body is to be a body, and the purpose of the system is to persist, and everything that lives here—the stewards with their gill-necks and their lichen-light, the isopods with their clicking maintenance, the moths with their helical pollination, the spiders with their cable-weaving, the maggots with their blind writhing heat—is here because it serves that purpose, and that purpose is served by their being here, and the loop closes, and the work continues.

The isopod on your shoulder moves on. The moth spirals past. The pale steward adjusts her valve and returns to her alcove without having looked at you once. The floor pulses beneath your feet. The bone-saws scream. The conveyor clatters. The heartbeat thrums, slow and vast and indifferent.

You are not food. You are not a component. You are not even a visitor, really. You are an anomaly, and the system is vast, and anomalies resolve themselves, given time. The isopods will find your body when you stop moving. They will clean the skin from your bones with the same thoroughness they bring to the pipe-scale. The roots will find your bones, and the network will find the minerals in them, and the minerals will become fungal wood, and the fungal wood will become substrate pulp, and the pulp will become slurry-paste, and the paste will become heat, and the heat will become processing, and the processing will become something the system needs, and you will have become a part of the body after all.

The work continues. The work always continues. The body does not know your name, and it does not need to. It has no use for names. It has use only for mass, for energy, for the endless cycling of matter through the processes that sustain it. And you are matter, and you are energy, and you are standing in its lung, breathing its breath, tasting its spores, and the isopod has already decided you are not food, and the moth has already passed, and the alarm has already stopped.

And the floor shifts. And the pulse beats. And somewhere in the kilometers of dark earth below you, the great network adjusts its flow, and a valve opens, and steam hisses, and the isopods swarm, and the stewards shuffle, and the maggots writhe, and the moths spiral, and the spiders weave, and the factory breathes, and the body lives, and the work—*

the work continues.*
