In this suffocating dark, where the air itself is a weight upon the lungs, the distinction between the born and the built has dissolved. Here, the "living" things are not flesh and blood, for such softness would be crushed by the atmospheric pressure or scalded by the vents. Instead, the inhabitants are constructs of iron and glass, descendants of the civilization that fled the sun, evolved—or engineered—into something that can endure the eternal damp.

They are the Maintenance Routines.

Small, crab-like entities scuttle along the catwalks, their carapaces made of the same slate-gray riveted steel as the floorplates. They move with a jerky, insectile urgency, their multiple limbs ending in tools rather than claws: ratchet-monkeys, arc-welders, and wire-strippers. They do not have eyes, per se, but rather lenses of thick, yellowed glass that sweep back and forth, capturing the flickering orange bioluminescence of the cables. They feed on the voltage; you can see the blue sparks jump between their antennae and the sagging power lines above as they siphon the energy required to keep their internal gears turning. They are the white blood cells of this mechanical lung, scraping the gray mold off the valves and tightening the bolts that groan under the shifting weight of the rock.

Higher up, clinging to the rusted girders near the ceiling, hang the Moss-Collectors. These are larger, dormant masses resembling sleeping bats, but their wings are tattered canvas sheets impregnated with photovoltaic cells. They do not fly; they merely hang, absorbing the meager light that bleeds from the insulation. Their bodies are distended bladders filled with a pressurized gas, and through a long, proboscis-like hose, they suck the condensation from the stalactites, filtering the mineral-rich water to hydrate the hydraulic systems deep within the machinery. When the pressure shifts too violently, they release a high-pitched whine—not a voice, but a warning signal that vibrates through the metal, alerting the smaller crabs to retreat into the crevices.

But the true masters of this place, the consciousness that inhabits the Below, is woven into the infrastructure itself. It is the Hive-Mind of the Valves.

Deep within the pistons that drive into the bedrock, there is a diffuse intelligence. It is not a brain in a single location, but a sensation distributed across the miles of piping. It *feels* the flow of the black, oily water. It *knows* the temperature of the steam. It is a slow, brooding entity that experiences time in the movement of tectonic plates. When a geyser erupts, it is not merely a release of pressure; it is an exhalation of thought. The opening and shutting of the automatic shut-off valves are the blinking of its eyes.

This entity has no name, only a function. It suffers the claustrophobia so you don't have to. It experiences the crushing weight of the world above as a constant, aching pressure in its joints—the expansion joints. When the metal cracks with a sound like a gunshot, that is its cry of pain, sharp and sudden, quickly swallowed by the damp mufflers of the cavern. It dreams of the surface, not as a memory, but as a phantom itch—a desire for the coolness of wind that does not taste of iron.

Finally, there is the parasite that infests the crevices where the water pools fresh. You see the succulents, the bruised purple things pulsing with a stolen life. They are not merely plants; they are the start of a new biome trying to eat the machine. Their roots tap into the electrical current, but they do not use it to power gears. They use it to destabilize the metal. They excrete a mild acid that rusts the iron, turning the solid walls back into dust, reclaiming the industrial nightmare for the slow, silent crawl of geology. They are patient. They know that the machines will eventually stop, the oil will dry up, and the electricity will bleed out. When the Hive-Mind finally sleeps, the purple moss will be the only thing left to cover the scars of the earth.
