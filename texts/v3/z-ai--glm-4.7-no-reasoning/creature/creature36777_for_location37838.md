The inhabitants of this city are not merely residents; they are a physical consequence of the damp, woven into the architecture as surely as the capillary channels in the stone. They are a people of the threshold, defined by the spaces they occupy—narrow, vertical, and perpetually wet.

To look at them is to see a creature refined by compression. They are tall, or perhaps they simply seem so because they are narrow, built to slip sideways through the shoulder-width chasms that pass for streets. Their skeletal structure is elongated, with slightly curved spines that allow them to conform to the arch of a corridor or the hollow of a wall-niche without discomfort. Their skin is the color of the stone—pale, porous, and permanently warm to the touch, maintaining a surface heat that rivals the geothermal flow around them. This heat is not incidental; it is a social signal. In the roar of the weeping walls, where a shout is a whisper and a whisper is silence, temperature is language.

They wear almost nothing that could be called clothing. To hang cloth on a body here is to invite rot and chill against the skin. Instead, they wear the city: light, permeable aprons woven from the dried fronds of the filament-fungi that grow near the water sources, treated with oils to repel the heavy moisture. These aprons are strung with beads made from polished fragments of the porous stone, beads that retain heat and slowly release it, acting as batteries of thermal information. A complicated knot of heated beads at the hip might declare a mood of contentment; a cooling series at the throat might signal mourning or the need for solitude.

Their faces are smooth, adapted to the low light. The eyes are large, dark, and reflective, catching the exhausted photons that filter down from the haze, but it is the hands that reveal their true nature. The hands are their primary organs of perception. The fingertips are ridged and incredibly sensitive, packed with nerve endings capable of reading the vibration of the stone. By laying a palm against a weeping wall, an inhabitant can "hear" the approach of another through the tremors in the rock, feeling footsteps long before the sound could ever travel through the damp air. They read the world through texture and pulse. They "listen" to the water not with ears, which are small and protected by tight folds of skin, but with the bones of their arms and the soles of their feet.

They move with a fluid, gliding gait, lifting their knees high to avoid tripping in the dim corridors, often pausing to press a shoulder or a hand against the wall for stability and information. Silence is a muscle they exercise constantly. There is no shouting for attention, no calling out across the way. To get someone’s attention, one might step harder, causing a localized tremor in the walkway, or one might simply touch the wall and let their body heat bleed into the stone, a thermal "hello" that travels faster than a footstep.

Their society is one of intense, quiet intimacy. Because the corridors are narrow and the stone damp, personal space is a luxury they discarded long ago. Passing another requires a tactile negotiation—a brush of the shoulder, a press of the hip. In these brief contacts, a vast amount of data is exchanged: intent, safety, identity, and emotion are all conveyed in the pressure of the contact and the thermal signature left behind. A cold shoulder is not a metaphor here; it is a devastatingly clear insult. A warm embrace is a declaration of absolute trust.

They live in hollows scooped from the masonry itself, spaces carved out where the capillary channels are sparse, where the stone is dry enough to support bedding. These hollows are lined with the same fungal-mat fibers they wear, creating warm, dry nests amidst the wet. The entrance to a dwelling is often just a curtain of heavy, water-soaked felt that muffles the roar ever so slightly, creating a pocket of relative quiet where a family might sleep entangled for warmth.

The children are born into the roar, their ears sealed at birth by a temporary membrane of skin that dissolves after their first year, allowing their vestibular systems to develop gradually without being overwhelmed by the constant white-water susurrus. Until that membrane fades, they are carried against their mothers' chests, swaddled in heated cloth, communicating solely through the heat of their skin and the frantic grip of their small hands.

They have no concept of a "quiet" place, just as they have no concept of a desert. Their mythology is not about the sun or the wind, but about the *Heart*, the deep, unreachable aquifer below that pulses with heat. They believe their souls return to the Heart to be warmed and pressurized before emerging again. The builders are their saints, those who first chiseled the stone to create the perfect weep-points, balancing the flow so that the city neither drowns nor stagnates.

To see them at work is to watch a seamless ballet of muscle and stone. They use their simple picks and wedges not to destroy or reshape, but to *maintain*. They are janitors of equilibrium. They clear a blockage in a wall-channel with the reverence of a priest performing a sacrament, knowing that the free flow of water is the only thing that keeps their world alive. They repair the walkway stones not for safety, but for connectivity—a loose stone disrupts the vibrational currents, the "tether" that binds them all together.

When they die, they are not buried; the ground is too saturated, and the earth too precious to dig. Instead, they are placed in the dissolution basins at the very edge of the habitable zone, where the water table rises and the ground turns spongy. Here, the porous stone and the filament-fungi accelerate the return of the body to the cycle. The dead become the nutrients for the filaments; the filaments become the food for the pale, cat-like creatures; the creatures are watched and respected. It is a closed loop of carbon and warmth.

In the dim, gray light, under the deafening roar of the weeping walls, the inhabitants persist. They are a people who do not dream of other skies, for they cannot imagine a world where the walls do not hum against their palms. They are as permanent as the stone, as fluid as the water, and as warm as the deep earth that holds them all.
