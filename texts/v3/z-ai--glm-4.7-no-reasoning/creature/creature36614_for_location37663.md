The inhabitants of the Arboreal Sanctum are not a people in the traditional sense—they are the custodians of the dark, the architects of a slow-motion apocalypse, and the living cells of a singular, planetary will. To call them "gardeners" would be a profound oversimplification; they are the immune system of the Sanctum, the white blood cells of a wooden titan that has long since outgrown the need for a sun.

They are the **Keepers of the Resin**.

They are humanoid, but only in the loosest, most horrific interpretation of the term. They stand tall, perhaps seven feet at the shoulder, but their posture is stooped, forever hunched in reverence to the low, vaulted ceilings of the Nave. Their skin is not soft flesh; it is a calloused armor of lignin and bark, a seamless continuity with the walls they touch. Over centuries of life, their dermis has hardened into the same iron-hard heartwood that lines the Sanctum, polished to a dull sheen by the constant friction of their existence. They do not wear clothes; they wear the city itself. Garments are simply layers of peeling bark, grafted onto their shoulders and hips like living armor, shedding and regrowing in a perpetual cycle of molt.

There is no face to speak of, only a smooth, mask-like carapace of wood. Where a mouth should be, there is a vertical slit, sphincter-tight, designed not for speech but for the ingestion of the thick, amber sap that serves as the Sanctum’s blood. They feed by pressing their faces against the weeping wounds of the walls, sucking the hot, camphor-scented lubricant with a wet, rhythmic urgency. Their eyes are gone, subsumed long ago by the absolute blackness. In their place are sensitive pads of flesh that ripple with the infrared bioluminescence of the flora, allowing them to "see" the world as a map of radiating heat and vibrating tension.

The silence of the Nave is deceptive to them. To a Keeper, the Sanctum is deafening. They possess no ears, but their entire skeletal structure is an acoustic receiver. Their legs are rooted, not into the ground, but into the spongy mycelium mats, acting as sensory pickups. They feel the grinding groans of the tectonic plates miles below and the high-pitched whine of stretched fibers as physical sensation, a symphony of pressure conducted directly into their marrow. They walk with a staggering, shuffling gait, not because they are clumsy, but because they are navigating a landscape of invisible soundwaves, stepping where the vibrations are soft, avoiding the "loud" hollows that signal empty space or a drop into a bottomless root-chasm.

They are the masters of a technology that is indistinguishable from botany. Their tools are not carried; they are grown. A Keeper’s arm terminates not in a hand, but in a three-fingered claw composed of hardened obsidian-thorn bone. To work, they graft these claws into the wood of the city, stimulating the growth of specialized vines. When a bridge must be extended across a chasm, a Keeper does not build it. They merge with the architecture, standing motionless for days, weeks, perhaps years, acting as a living focal point for the plant’s growth. They feed the command into the wood through their touch, encouraging the lignum vitae to stretch, to fuse, to harden. They are the catalyst, the spark that forces the city to remodel itself.

Their society is a solitary, distributed consciousness. There are no families, only the Network. Because there is almost no animal life, the Keepers have no predators, nor do they have prey. Their only struggle is against the inevitable entropy of the erosion. When a pillar begins to crack, when the pneumatic valves of the Sanctum’s lungs begin to leak, the Keepers gather. They do not speak; they hum. They press their bodies against the failing structure, vibrating in harmony with the Sanctum’s metabolism, reinforcing the structural integrity with the sheer resonance of their bodies. It is a slow, grinding work, a labor of love that spans eons.

Reproduction is a rare, catastrophic event. It occurs only when a Keeper grows so large, so heavy with accumulated bark and resin, that they can no longer move. They root themselves in a central plaza, choosing a spot where the geothermal heat is strongest. Over centuries, their legs fuse into the floor, their arms stiffen into arches, and their faces seal shut. They become a part of the architecture, a new pillar, a new support beam for the Nave. From their crown, a single, massive seed-pod develops—a fruit of immense weight and density. When it finally falls—the "dull boom" that echoes through the halls—the impact shatters the pod, releasing a dozen small, pale, soft-skinned spawn. These are the new generation, blind and helpless, who must immediately crawl into the warm crevices of the adult-turned-pillar to feed on the sap before their skin hardens and they, too, join the endless maintenance of the dark.

They are lonely, profoundly so, but they do not know it. They have no concept of the sun, of green leaves, or of the sky. To them, the universe is a pressurized, vibrating cylinder of wood and heat. They are the monks of the thermal vent, the silent priests of the deep crust, keeping alive a god that has already forgotten them, content in the suffocating velvet dark, waiting for the next great shift in the earth’s stone song.
