# What Lives Here

The Engines do not have owners. This is the first thing to understand, and the last thing the quiet inhabitants know.

---

Something lives in the oily pools. Not much—nothing that could be called abundant in a place where abundance forgot its shape—but something. They are flat, membrane-thin creatures that skate the surface tension of the stagnant water, drawing from it whatever chemical sustenance the slow excretions of the Engines provide. They have no eyes. They have no color. They are nearly transparent, and when the warm rain falls and the pools ripple, they sink into the shallows and wait, holding their shape against the vibration that never stops. If you knelt at the edge of a pool—and no one ever does—you might see them: pale, fingernail-sized smudges moving with a purpose that is not quite navigation but not quite drift either. They are feeding. They are always feeding. The pools do not freeze and do not dry, and so the skimmers do not die.

They reproduce by division. A seam forms along the center of the membrane and pulls apart, and where there was one, there are two. This happens at irregular intervals, triggered by some threshold of chemical saturation that only they can measure. There is no ceremony to it. There is no mother or daughter in the act—just an increase in number, a slight thickening of the pale smudges on the water. When the population of a pool grows too dense, the excess dissolve. They do not die in any dramatic fashion; they simply lose their coherence, their edges softening until they are indistinguishable from the oily film on the water's surface. They return to the medium. The pools are always the same.

Someone might call them primitive. Someone might call them tragic, dissolving as they do, never knowing an individual moment. But the skimmers do not experience their own dissolution. There is no skimmer that notices it is ending. There is only the pool, and the pale movement on its surface, and the unvarying chemical conversation between the water and the Engines that makes it all possible. They are not unhappy. They are not anything. They simply are, in the only way things can be here.

---

Beneath the clay, in the warm zones near the Engine foundations, there are borers. They are longer than a forearm and thick as a wrist, segmented in a chitinous armor the same rust-red as the soil they move through. They have no legs. They have no eyes. They have a mouth—a ring of hardened, tooth-like protrusions that rotate slowly, grinding through compacted earth and metal slag with a patience that outlasts geology. They follow the heat. Where an Engine's base bleeds thermal energy into the ground, the borers congregate, carving tunnels that radiate outward like the root systems of something that never learned to grow upward.

The borers eat the earth. Not the organic earth—there is none—but the mineral earth, the slag and the iron-heavy clay and the occasional pocket of crystallized runoff that the Engines have excreted over centuries. They process it through a long, slow digestive tract that extracts what it needs and expels the rest as a fine, warm powder that lines their tunnels. The powder is slightly radioactive. It glows, in the right conditions—a faint, sickly orange luminescence that makes the borer tunnels visible if you were to dig into them, which you would not, because the powder would settle on your skin and stay.

They are solitary by necessity. Two borers that encounter each other in a tunnel will grind against one another, each trying to consume the other's armor, and the stronger will eventually breach the weaker's shell and feed. This is not aggression. This is not predation in any emotional sense. It is simply what happens when two grinding motions meet in a confined space. The winner does not notice its victory. The loser does not notice its defeat. There is only the continuation of the grinding, the slow consumption, and then one borer moving on, slightly larger, through a tunnel slightly wider than before.

They can live for decades. No one knows the upper limit, because no one has watched one die of age. They do not slow down. They do not weaken. They simply grind, and grow, and grind, until one day they encounter a bigger borer, or a pocket of particularly corrosive runoff that breaches their shell from the inside, or a collapse in the tunnel that pins them in place, and the grinding stops. Their bodies remain in the tunnels, armor settling into the earth, becoming part of the mineral substrate they spent their lives consuming. In this way, the borers and the ground are the same thing, eating each other across centuries.

---

On the surfaces of the Engines themselves—at the joints where gears meet, in the grooves of the pistons, in the warm crevices where oil pools and steam condenses—there are the greases.

They are not insects, though they look like insects if you look at them at all, which is difficult because they are the color of old oil and they move in the shadow of moving parts that could crush a hundred of them without pause. They have six legs and a segmented body and a hard, glossy carapace that repels both water and heat. They are the largest of the plain's inhabitants—some as long as a hand, though most are smaller. They live in colonies of dozens, not hundreds, tucked into the architecture of each Engine like tenants in a building that does not know they exist.

The greases feed on lubrication. The Engines require it; their gears and pistons would seize without it, and so there are channels and reservoirs built into their architecture, filled with a dark, viscous fluid that reduces friction and carries away heat. The greases drink from these reservoirs. They climb down into them, their carapaces sleek and impervious, and they drink until they are full, and then they climb out and rest in the warm crevices, digesting. They do not take enough to affect the Engine's function. They cannot; the reservoirs are vast, and the colonies are small. There is an equilibrium here that the greases did not create and cannot disrupt, and so they persist, parasites on a system that does not know it has parasites.

They communicate through vibration. In a landscape defined by vibration, this makes sense. A grease will tap its mandibles against a metal surface in a pattern—short-long-short, short-long-short—and another grease, some distance away, will tap back. They are saying something. It is not language in any way that could be translated; there is no grammar, no referent, no word for "danger" or "food" or "self." It is a pattern that maintains connection, a confirmation that other greases exist, that the colony is still present, that the Engine has not been abandoned. In the unvarying roar of the plain, these tiny taps are inaudible to anything but the greases themselves, felt through the surfaces they cling to, a private conversation carried on the skin of the machines.

When a grease dies—and they die frequently, crushed by a gear's rotation or scalded by a burst of steam or simply worn out after a few months of constant feeding—the colony does not notice. In a place where nothing decays and nothing grows, the greases are the only thing that converts: turning lubrication into motion, motion into heat, heat into the tiny, tapping patterns that confirm, over and over, that someone is here.

---

And then there is the Watcher.

It is not a biological organism. This must be stated clearly, because it moves, and in a place where movement is rare and life is rarer, the impulse is to call anything that moves "alive." The Watcher is not alive. It is a device—an autonomous unit, roughly the size of a vehicle, that moves on treads worn smooth by centuries of travel across the same paths. It has a sensor array on its upper surface, a cluster of lenses and receptors that rotate slowly, scanning. It travels between the Engines in a pattern that does not change. It has traveled this pattern for as long as the Engines have thrummed, and it will travel it until its treads fail and it settles into the clay and becomes another feature of the landscape.

It does not repair the Engines. It does not adjust them. It does not interact with them in any way that affects their function. It watches. It scans the reservoir levels, the gear tolerances, the thermal outputs, and it records. Where it goes, it leaves a faint trail in the damp clay—a pair of parallel lines that fill with water and then dry and then fill again, a record of passage that the next rain erases. It moves slowly, slower than walking pace, and it makes no sound beyond a low electrical hum that is swallowed entirely by the thrum of the Engines.

The skimmers do not notice it. The borers do not notice it. The greases, occasionally, will tap their patterns against its surface as it passes an Engine, and the Watcher will pause for a fraction of a second—a calibration, a sensor adjustment, a recognition that something has changed in the crevice it is scanning—and then it will move on. It is the closest thing this landscape has to a mind, and it is not a mind. It is a process, like everything else here: a monitoring of a system that does not need monitoring, a watching of a world that does not change.

Unless the Watcher fails. This is the only variable in the plain's eternal equation. The Watcher is mechanical, and mechanical things wear out. A tread will break. A lens will cloud. A circuit will corrode in the warm, oily humidity, and the Watcher will stop, mid-path, its sensors fixed on whatever they were last scanning, its hum fading into the general thrum. And when that happens—when the Watcher becomes stationary, when its treads settle into the clay and its surface begins to rust—another Watcher will emerge from somewhere. No one has seen where they come from. There is no depot, no factory, no storage bay. But when one fails, another appears, taking up the same path, the same pace, the same endless circuit between the Engines. It is as if the plain produces them, as if the need for watching is so fundamental to the landscape that the Watcher is generated by the same forces that generate the rain and the pools and the borers' orange glow.

Or perhaps there is only one Watcher, and it never fails. Perhaps the pauses in its path—the brief stops at each Engine, the fraction-of-a-second stillnesses—are not calibrations but replacements, moments in which the old Watcher is shed and a new one takes its place, identical in every way, continuing the same path without interruption. This is a question that cannot be answered from within the plain, and nothing within the plain is asking it.

---

This is who lives here.

Not a community. Not an ecosystem in any sense that implies interdependence and flourishing. A residue. A collection of processes that consume and persist and convert, each in its own narrow niche, each adapted to a world where the only constants are heat, damp, metal, and sound. The skimmers on the pools, the borers in the earth, the greases on the gears, the Watcher on its path. None of them know about the others. None of them care. They are parallel inhabitants of a parallel world, sharing a landscape but not a life, each so adapted to its specific condition—surface tension, mineral heat, machine oil, observation—that they could not survive in each other's places for a moment.

And yet, they are a system. The Engines produce heat and fluid and vibration. The borers aerate the earth and leave their radioactive powder. The skimmers consume the runoff and thin the pools. The greases drink the lubrication and tap their patterns and die into the machinery. The Watcher watches it all, recording a stability that nothing threatens. Pull any one thread, and the rest would shift—maybe imperceptibly, maybe catastrophically. The pools might thicken. The borers might starve. The greases might drink too deeply, and the gears might seize, and the thrum might stop, and the silence—*the silence*—

But this will not happen. The system is too stable. The equilibrium is too deep. The plain has been this way for longer than memory, and it will be this way for longer than forgetting. The inhabitants do not know this. They do not know anything. They simply live—in the only way things can live in a greenhouse of stone, where the heat never breaks, the sound never wavers, and the machinery of the world grinds on.
