To live here is to be slow.

Nothing moves quickly. Nothing can. The water itself—a single body of it, indivisible, pressing equally from all directions—has a viscosity that approaches honey, though it is cold as the void between stars. To swim through it is to push against the weight of deep time. Every stroke costs something. Every motion leaves a small hole in the water that takes hours to close. The inhabitants—if that word can be used for things that have no word for themselves—have learned to minimize their effort. They drift more than they swim. They let the glacial currents carry them, accepting displacement over destination, because destination implies purpose, and purpose implies speed, and speed is impossible here.

There are no fish. Fish are too quick, too nervous, too hungry. Fish belong to seas where light penetrates and warmth quickens blood and predators flash through coral corridors. Here, the only animal life is of a different order entirely—though whether it is animal at all is a question the place does not answer.

They are pale. Everything here is pale, because paleness is the color of things that have never seen the sun. Their skin—or whatever covers their flesh—is translucent in the faint bioluminescent glow, showing the slow pulse of fluids within, dark and cold. They have no eyes. Eyes are organs of distance, of light, of alertness to approaching threat or approaching prey. Eyes require a world where things can be seen coming, where preparation is possible, where the future arrives at a speed that allows response. Here, the future arrives at the speed of settling sediment. There is nothing to see. The walls are known by touch, by the subtle displacement of water, by the faint electrical signature that living tissue carries and the constructed stone does not. They navigate by pressure and by presence, the way a blind person navigates their own home—not by seeing the furniture, but by feeling the shape of the space around it.

Their bodies are soft. This is not weakness; it is adaptation. Hardness—bone, shell, cartilage—requires minerals, requires growth that builds and accumulates. In water this cold, with metabolism this slow, accumulation takes millennia. And hardness is heavy. Heavy things sink. Sinking things rest against the floor, and the floor is where the sediment accumulates, and the sediment is where the dead things settle—fragments of vegetation, shed growth, the slow debris of a world that barely produces waste but produces some. The floor is not death, but it is where death goes, eventually. The living stay off the floor. They stay in the water-column, in the space between the grown-over ceiling and the sediment-laden floor, and they stay there by being almost weightless, almost neutrally buoyant, their bodies adjusted to the water so precisely that they neither rise nor fall but simply exist at whatever depth they occupy, like particles in suspension.

They are few. This is the hardest thing for a surface-mind to accept: the place is so vast, and contains so little. Thousands of leagues of constructed chambers, corridors, galleries, volumes—and within them, perhaps a few hundred of these pale, blind, soft beings, drifting through the dark like deep-sea ghosts. They do not gather. They do not school. They have no social structure that a human observer would recognize, no communication that can be heard, no bonds that can be named. And yet they are aware of each other. When one drifts near another, there is a change in the pressure-field, a displacement that both feel, and they adjust—moving closer, or moving apart, or simply continuing on their separate drifts with the knowledge of another's presence added to their long, unstructured awareness. It is not intimacy. It is not indifference. It is something between: a recognition that the other exists, that the water holds them both, that the walls extend around them both, that the dark presses against them both, and that this shared condition is, in some sense, sufficient.

They eat rarely. Their metabolism—cold, slow, ancient—requires food in quantities that would starve a surface creature to death in days, but sustains them for centuries. They feed on the vegetation, though feed is perhaps too active a word. They drift through the curtains of kelp-analogue, and their mouths—soft, lipless, always slightly open—filter the water as it passes, catching the smallest fragments: broken cells, dying plankton-analogues, the particulate waste of the growth that covers every surface. They are not predators. They are not even truly grazers. They are filters. They take what the water gives them, which is very little, and they make it last, which takes a very long time.

Sometimes—once in a decade, perhaps, or once in a lifetime—they find something larger. A fragment of vegetation that has detached and is drifting, still alive, still faintly luminous. A cluster of seed-pods releasing into the current, their soft clicking the only sound in the dark. A growth-bloom near a deep vent, where the water holds a fraction more warmth and the chemosynthetic organisms flourish in densities that are, by the standards of this place, almost riotous. At these times, the pale beings gather—drifting in from whatever distances they have wandered, drawn by the faint chemical signature of abundance, the way that desert creatures gather at a rare water-hole. They do not compete. There is enough, because there are so few, and because they eat so little, and because the bloom will last for years, and because they have no concept of shortage—only of presence and absence, abundance and stillness, the current and the quiet.

They do not build. They do not make. They do not modify their environment in any way. This is perhaps the most alien thing about them: they are intelligent—there is a depth to their awareness, a capacity for recognition and memory and even, perhaps, for something like thought—but they do not act on their intelligence. They do not shape the stone or cultivate the growth or alter the currents or store the food. They simply exist within the structure that was made before them, by hands that are gone, for purposes that are forgotten. They use it—they shelter in its chambers, they drift through its corridors, they feed from its growth-covered walls—but they do not change it. They are not the inheritors of the construction. They are its inhabitants, its current residents, its quiet tenants, the way that moss is a tenant of a wall, or a bird is a tenant of a tree. They did not make the place. They do not own it. They do not even understand it. But they live in it, completely, the way that water lives in a cup.

They are old. Not individually—though an individual might live for five hundred years, or a thousand, or longer; no one has ever watched one die, and it is possible that they do not die, that they simply slow, over millennia, until their metabolism is so reduced that they are indistinguishable from the water around them, and they become, in effect, another kind of current, another kind of pressure, another kind of presence in the dark. But even if the individuals die, the kind is old. They have been here since before the water filled the structure, or nearly so. They were here when the constructors still worked—perhaps they were the constructors, or the constructors' companions, or the constructors' descendants, changed beyond recognition by the filling and the cold and the time. Or perhaps they came after, finding the drowned structure by some ancient instinct, moving in through some opening that has long since been closed by settling stone or growing vegetation, and making it their home because it was here and it was shelter and the water was here and the water was life.

They do not remember the constructors. This is important. They have no tradition, no history, no knowledge of the hands that cut and placed the stones. If they once knew, they have forgotten—and their forgetting is not a loss but a transformation, the way that a river forgets the mountain it flowed from because the mountain is now the river, is now the valley, is now the plain, is now the sediment, is now the sea. The structure is not a mystery to them because they do not think of it as something that was made. It simply is. The way that a mountain is. The way that the water is. They have no category for construction, no concept of making, no sense that the walls around them are anything other than the natural shape of the world. And in this, perhaps, they are not wrong. The walls are natural now—if natural means shaped by the forces that have acted on them for the longest time, if natural means arrived at their most stable form, if natural means completed. The constructors made the structure, but the water made it what it is. And the pale beings, drifting through its chambers in the dark, are part of that making. They are part of the completion. They are the structure's final inhabitants, its last function, the use to which it has been put after all other uses have been forgotten.

There is one more thing. It is the hardest thing to name, and the most important.

They are not unhappy.

This is difficult to accept, because the place they inhabit is, by any surface measure, terrible. It is cold. It is dark. It is vast and empty and slow and silent and ancient beyond comprehension. It contains no warmth, no light, no joy, no comfort, no variety, no surprise, no hope, no change. It is a place where nothing happens, and nothing happens, and nothing happens, across spans of time that would erode civilizations to dust and still the water is moving and the walls are holding and the growth is growing and nothing has changed and nothing will change and the whole of existence is a dark, cold, quiet, patient holding-on.

And they are not unhappy.

They do not have the capacity for unhappiness, or if they do, they do not exercise it. They do not want things they do not have. They do not imagine things that are not present. They do not compare their condition to some other, better condition and find it wanting. They do not suffer from the gap between desire and reality because their desires are so small, so simple, so easily met by the world they inhabit: water to float in, walls to shelter beside, growth to feed from, others to drift near. They have all of these things, always. They have never lacked them. They have never known a world where they were absent, and so they have never feared their absence. They live in a state of permanent satisfaction—not the active satisfaction of a desire fulfilled, but the passive satisfaction of a desire that has never been felt, a need that has never been known, a lack that has never been perceived.

They are, in their quiet and ancient way, complete. The structure is complete, the water is complete, the growth is complete, and they are complete. The place has arrived at the state it was always moving toward, and they are part of that state, and they are part of that completion, and they do not know it, and they do not need to know it, because knowledge is a surface thing, a quick thing, a thing of light and change and time, and they are creatures of the deep, the cold, the dark, the slow, and they have no use for knowledge, and no need of it, and no lack of it.

They simply are. And the water moves around them. And the walls hold. And the dark rests. And the growth waves in its patient rhythm. And the world goes on, complete, and held, and still.
