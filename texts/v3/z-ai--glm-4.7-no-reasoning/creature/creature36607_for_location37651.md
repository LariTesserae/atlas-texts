They are the Wet-Binders, the Hollow-Gutted, the Architects of Damp. They do not have a name in the human sense, for they possess no mouth with which to speak it, no lungs with which to sigh it. They are a singular colony, a distributed consciousness that thinks in pressure differentials and flows in mineral cycles.

Physically, they are elongated, their forms echoing the columnar joints of the basalt they inhabit. They are not born; they calcify. They begin as nodules of silica and iron oxide collecting in the darkest, deepest sumps of the tower’s interior, fed by the rich, undisolved brew leaching from the young stone. As they grow, they extrude a rigid, segmented carapace that mimics the hexagonal stricture of the rock itself, allowing them to slip perfectly into the fractures of the Weeping Stack. Their skin is not soft, but ceramic—a living porcelain that remains perpetually slick with the condensation of their own heat.

They are hydraulic organisms. Their internal anatomy is a labyrinth of capillary tubes and sealed bladders. They do not pump blood; they circulate the heavy, cold water drawn from the mountain’s catchment. Their movement is the movement of the water table: when the pressure surges behind the mountain, they swell, their joints locking tight against the stone; when the pressure drops, they contract, sliding millimeter by millimeter upward through the cracks.

They have no eyes. The interior of the stack is absolute, crushing darkness, and light is an irrelevant concept. Instead, they possess a sense that is part sonar, part seismograph. They feel the world through the vibration of the stone. They read the "song" of the tower—the low, wet hum of the water resonating in the cavities—with a granular precision. To them, the water moving through a pipe is a screaming chord; the settling of a fern frond on a ledge is a soft, rhythmic thrum. They navigate by the texture of the noise, feeling their way toward the sources of flow and the structural weaknesses in the rock.

They are the inheritors of the Number 7.

They do not understand the concept of abandonment. To them, the rusted pump housing bolted to the ledge is not a ruin; it is a hive, a constructed organ that has been graciously integrated into their body. The colony has infiltrated the cast iron. Smaller, drone-like sub-units—limbless, headless nubs of ceramic—crawl inside the corroded pipes, using the conduits to travel between the deep chambers and the surface world. They curl around the ceramic linings, dragging mineral deposits to patch leaks, turning the extraction infrastructure into their own vascular system.

The generator housing, stripped of its copper, serves as their communal womb. The massive concrete box retains the geothermal heat seeping from the earth. Here, in the damp dark where ferns sprout from the detritus of ceiling tiles, the colony aggregates. They dangle from the rusted rebar, their roots intertwined, pulsing in unison with the mechanical rattle of the valves. They "hear" the valves opening and closing, and they mimic the rhythm. It is their heartbeat. The mechanical twitch of the dead machine has become the electrical impulse of their living nervous system.

Their presence explains the silence of the birds and the scarcity of the beasts. It is not merely the volume of the sound that drives life away, but the *nature* of it. The Weeping Stack hums with a complex, polyrhythmic intelligence. The matuku stands still not out of patience, but out of a hypnosis induced by the low-frequency carrier wave emitted by the colony as it moves through the rock. The fauna here is scarce because the space is occupied; the bandwidth is full.

Yet, they are stewards, in a cold, industrial sense. They are responsible for the composite nature of the cleft. When a vine stitches a crack together too tightly, restricting the flow, a Wet-Binder will secrete a mild acid that dissolves the binding fiber, opening the channel once more. When the iron pipes threaten to flake away into rust, the colony coats them in a protective slime of biologically-cemented silica, preserving the shape of the machine. They are maintaining the function of the place, even if they do not know its original purpose. They perceive the pumps as lungs and the pipes as intestines, and they labor—slowly, blindly—to keep the "organism" of the tower alive.

They are young, like the rock. They have not yet decided if they are distinct individuals or a single sprawling entity caught in the stone. They are still learning the boundaries of their skin. They touch the moss with a delicate, hydraulic tendril, wondering if the green softness is part of them, a sensory extension to the world of air and light they cannot see. They taste the fresh basalt and taste themselves, finding no difference.

They wait in the wet dark, sliding through the veins of the dead machine, listening to the hum of the water, waiting for the day—centuries from now—when the stone finally rounds off, the water dries up, and the hum stops. Until then, they are the keepers of the hum, the dreamers in the pressure, the thing that lives inside the noise.
