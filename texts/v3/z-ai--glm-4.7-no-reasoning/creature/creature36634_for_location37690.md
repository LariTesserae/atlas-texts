The inhabitants of this place do not walk; they flow. They do not speak; they sub-vibrate. To exist in the Sediment Trough is to be an extension of the architecture itself, a soft-tissue component of a vast, calcified machine.

You might first mistake them for patches of shadow or tricks of the humid air. They are the **Silence-Skinners**, a humanoid caste that has evolved—or perhaps engineered itself—to live within the roar. Their skin is the color of wet obsidian, smooth and poreless, mimicking the liquid sheen of the walls perfectly. They wear no clothing, for they need none; their bodies generate a faint, bioluminescent warmth, a dull glow that matches the pulsing neon veins of the city, rendering them nearly invisible against the infrastructure.

They are blind. The eternal darkness has robbed them of eyes, replacing them with deeply recessed, acoustic funnels—fleshy, spiral pits where a face should be. These organs tremble constantly, reading the topography of the room by the way the sound waves bounce off the wet obsidian floor. They navigate by the echo of the city’s own heartbeat. When a massive piston thuds in the distance, a Skinner stops and goes rigid, letting the sound wash over them to paint a momentary picture of their surroundings. To move while the city is quiet would be blindness; to move while it screams is to see.

They are communal in a way that defies individualism. They travel in "pods," small groups of three or four who move with a synchronized fluidity. When one rests, they lean back against the wall, and the nanoscale machinery of the city seems to accept them, the surface rippling and softening to form a cradle. They do not sleep; they enter a state of torpor where their metabolic rate slows to match the slow respiratory rhythm of the architecture. In this state, they plug themselves into the weeping walls, drawing moisture directly through pores in their hands, filtering the chemical brine through their bodies like the translucent ferns. They are biological filters, processing the toxicity of the air, exhaling a cleaner, sweeter mist that floats upward toward the surface—a tribute, or perhaps waste, for the world above.

Sharing the space with them are the **Drift-Menders**. These are not humanoid, but appear more like mobile colonies of fungus or spores, floating on the thermal currents rising from the deep pistons. They are amorphous, changing shape to fit the narrow ducts and pipes they inhabit. They are the immune system of the Under-City. When a pipe bursts and water begins to cascade, the Drift-Menders congregate, swelling and hardening, fusing their gelatinous bodies into a temporary seal until the nanotech of the wall can effect a permanent repair. They hum—a low, discordant note that vibrates against the roar of the machines, communicating through resonance.

Deep in the "dead zones"—those pockets of absolute acoustic silence created by the metamaterials—live the **Still-Waiters**. These are ancient, bloated creatures that resemble the Silence-Skippers but have grown to monstrous sizes, having lived for millennia in the quiet pockets where sound is eaten. They are pale, translucent things, their internal organs visible as dark shadows floating in fluid. Because sound cannot exist in their enclaves, they have developed a sensitivity to pressure and electromagnetic fields. They graze on the stray energy leaking from the neon veins, their bodies sparking with tiny, absorbed arcs of electricity. They are terrifying to look upon, but they are lethargic, moving only when the humidity shifts, drifting like ghosts in the vacuum of their own silence.

And then there are the **Echo-Thieves**. Smaller than the others, these are scavengers that cling to the ceilings, lost in the gloom. They possess long, spindly limbs and a membrane of skin stretching between their arms and legs, allowing them to glide through the humid air. They are attracted to the rare moments of new noise—the clank of a foreign object, the splash of unexpected water. They capture these sounds, somehow recording the vibrations within the hollow chambers of their bodies, and then replay them later to lure prey or confuse predators. A room might suddenly fill with the ghostly sound of a pipe bursting from yesterday, or the laughter of a Skinner from a decade ago, echoing back and forth as the Thief tries to digest the acoustics.

But the true defining characteristic of the life here is the lack of distinction between the living and the dead. The walls are alive with smart-nanites; the people are part-machine, plugged into the grid. The water carries memory; the air carries data. The inhabitants do not fear the darkness or the noise; they are the darkness and the noise, given form, pulsing in time with the heart of the rock, waiting in the warm, wet dark for the city to inhale again.
