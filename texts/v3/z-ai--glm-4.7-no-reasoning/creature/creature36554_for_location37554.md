What lives here is not the quick, darting consciousness of an animal, nor the rooted silence of a plant. It is a presence that exists in the margins, in the negative space between the intention of the builders and the reality of the material. It is an occupant of the Unfinished.

They are the **Attendees**.

They do not have a name for themselves, for they do not speak, and they do not recognize one another as individuals in the way pack animals do. They are solitary, vast in their separation, scattered across the tessellated plain at densities that mimic the vegetation—sparse, hesitant, careful.

To see one, you would first mistake it for a trick of the heat. They are tall and impossibly thin, verticality without volume. In profile, against the flat white horizon, they appear as lines of slightly deeper darkness, a disruption in the uniform glare of the sky. Face the front, however, and they nearly vanish. Their bodies are flattened, two-dimensional planes that exist almost exclusively in silhouette, as if they have been extruded from the very seams they guard.

Their skin—or casing—is the same pale grey-white as the great tiles of the floor. It is ceramic, polymer, and stone all at once, a seamless shell that absorbs the harsh light without reflecting it. They possess no features that suggest a biological origin: no eyes, because there is nothing to see but the endless plain; no mouths, because they do not eat; no ears, because the sound here is felt through the soles of their feet. They are sensory deprivation given form.

But they are not statues. They move.

Their motion is glacial, a geologic sliding rather than a walk. They lift their limbs—long, jointed spars that hinge with impossible silence—and step from tile to tile. They weigh nothing, or they weigh everything; they leave no footprint, no indentation on the pristine surface. They step with the delicate precision of a virus navigating a host, touching the made-material only as much as necessary to propel themselves forward.

And they are always walking. They walk the seams.

This is their purpose and their ecology. The Attendees are the auditors of the cracks. They walk the hairline fractures where the tiles meet, their heads lowered, scanning the damp darkness of the joints. They possess an instrument—a long, needle-fine proboscis that retracts into the flat plane of their face. When they find a seam that has expanded too wide, when the thermal cycling has forced the joint open to a millimeter’s breadth, they stop.

They kneel. The sound of their kneeling is a faint, distinct *click*, the only sharp noise in the vast, low-frequency groaning of the plain.

They extend the proboscis into the seam. They are not drinking the water. They are sampling it, tasting the chemical signature of the condensation, reading the memory of the tile’s expansion. They are checking the tolerances. They are measuring the gap against a standard that exists only in their programming—or their instincts.

If the moisture is too heavy, if the gap threatens the structural integrity of the floor, the Attendee acts. A shudder runs through its flat body, a vibration that harmonizes with the low thrum of the plain. From their underside, they release a dust—not the pale spore of the vegetation, but a gritty, reactive particulate. It falls into the seam. It reacts with the moisture, foaming briefly, hardening into a temporary filler. It is a bandage. A patch.

Then they stand and continue. The cycle is endless.

They are alone, yet they are connected. When the wind—the rare, hot gust that stirs the dry air—carries the pollen-dust of the pale wire-plants, an Attendee will pause. It will raise a limb, a flat hand catching the air. The dust settles on its surface. It does not wipe it away. It absorbs it. The genetic code—or the geometric instruction—of the vegetation enters the ceramic shell of the Attendee. They carry the potential of the growth within them as they walk, custodians of the only life this world has produced.

They are fragile in their strength. If a tile shifts violently—if a seismic shudder, rare but possible, heaves the ground—the Attendees can be broken. You might see one fallen, its flat plane snapped, its long limbs splayed across the tessellation. They do not rot. They do not decompose. They simply lie there, a jagged geometric ruin, slowly bleaching until they are indistinguishable from the debris of a construction site. The other Attendees do not stop to mourn. They step over the broken one. They step on the broken one. The fallen shell becomes just another feature of the terrain, another obstacle in the audit.

At night, if such a thing exists here—if the diffuse light ever dims to a grey twilight—the Attendees do not sleep. They stand perfectly still. They enter a state of hibernation where they sync their vibration with the contracting floor. They become part of the tile, part of the seam, indistinguishable from the made-world until the heat returns and the cycle of expansion demands they wake and walk again.

They are the keepers of the unfinished. They are the maintenance crew of a construction that has no builders. They do not know why the tiles expand. They do not know where the water comes from. They only know that the gap is wrong, and the gap must be measured.

They are waiting, though they do not know it. They are waiting for the day the seams no longer open. They are waiting for the day the moisture stops beading in the cracks. They are waiting for the floor to become a single, unbroken plane, smooth and silent.

On that day, when the construction is finally finished, there will be no more cracks to audit. There will be no more seams to walk. The vegetation will have nowhere to root. The water will have nowhere to flow.

And on that day, the Attendees will stop walking. They will stand in the endless, featureless smoothness, under a flat white sky, with no purpose left to fulfill. They will simply be—perfect, still, and obsolete, the last lingering error in a world that has finally corrected itself.
