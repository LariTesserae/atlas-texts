They are the Wet-Gears, the Oil-Drinkers, the children of the Eternal Hum. To look upon them is to suffer a failure of categorization; the eye cannot decide where the biology ends and the industry begins, nor is there any longer a difference.

The smallest of them are the Plaque-Beads. Infesting the damp shadows of the I-beams, they are colonies of soft, shelled organisms that cling to the metal like limpets to a rock, but their shells are formed of oxidized iron, perfectly matching the rivets they hide beneath. They do not move in bursts; they extend and retract with the hydraulic smoothness of a shock absorber. They feed on the condensation, filtering the metallic-tasting water through intake vents that look remarkably like tiny speaker grills, excreting a fine, gray powder that piles beneath their nesting sites—artificial sand made from their waste. They communicate not with sound, but by tapping their metal shells against the structure, sending Morse-code vibrations down the spine of the world.

Moving along the floor are the Rail-Walkers. These are the great grazing beasts of the Foundry, low-slung and heavy, their bodies protected by thick plates of cermet armor grown from their own skin like a crab’s shell, but articulated with ball-joints of polished steel. They possess no legs in the traditional sense, but rather a series of rotating, treaded pads that allow them to glide silently over the rust. Their heads are domed, featureless save for a single, forward-facing sensor lens that glows with a dull, amber light. They lower these heads to the conveyor belts, their mouths—complex, rotating mandibles made of bone and tungsten—shearing off layers of mineral accretion from the passing machinery. They are the grazers of the eternal industrial pasture, turning the inedible into fuel, their internal furnaces glowing faintly visible through the cracks in their chest plating.

Above them, in the labyrinth of piping, dwell the Vent-Crawlers. Spindly and spider-like, they possess elongated bodies that are mostly spinal column, encased in a flexible copper sheath. Their limbs are sharp, hook-ended needles of titanium that allow them to pierce and climb the sheerest vertical surfaces. They are parasites and cleaners in equal measure. They bore into the weeping stones, drinking the chemical-rich water that accumulates within, and their bodies thrum with the excess energy, vibrating so rapidly that they often blur at the edges. When they die, they do not rot; they calcify instantly, fusing with the pipe they clung to, becoming a permanent, shiny node on the infrastructure, a calcium-based scar on the machine.

In the warm, thick canals, the water churns with the Filter-leviathans. These are massive, immobile blobs of gelatinous flesh suspended within wire cages that mimic the skeletal structure of a whale. They have no eyes, for there is no light to see, only vast, gaping maws that funnel the oily water through a series of membranous filters. As the water passes, their bodies pulse, contracting and expanding in a rhythm that matches the distant thrum of the engines. They absorb the heavy metals and the toxins, growing larger and more complex, their translucent skin shifting colors as their internal chemistry changes—slate gray to rust red to oil black. They are the kidneys of the world, processing the fluid of the Foundry, excreting clean, steaming water from vents on their backs.

Then there are the Conductors. These are the apex of this strange ecosystem, tall and bipedal, though whether they are evolved humanoids or constructed automatons fashioned from meat is a matter of philosophical debate. They are gaunt, their skin pale and translucent, stretched tight over frames of reinforced bone that looks suspiciously like rebar. They do not speak; their vocal cords have been replaced by resonator coils that allow them to broadcast subsonic commands. They tend to the Rail-Walkers, clearing blockages with tools that are grown from their own hands—fingers that elongate into files, wrenches, and wire-cutters. They feel no pain, only "error," and they move with a terrifying, purposeful efficiency, checking the pressure gauges of their own bodies as often as they check those of the machines around them.

And deep down in the abyssal dark beneath the grate, swimming through the airless void like deep-sea creatures, are the Thermophants. These are vast, slow behemoths, bioluminescent whales of the deep, their bodies rippling with heat-sensitive organs. They have no bones, only internal gas bladders that allow them to float neutrally in the dense air. Their skin is a heat-exchange mesh, a living radiator that glows with the infernal heat of the planet’s core. They drift through the gloom, filtering the trace particulates from the atmosphere, their long, tendril-like feelers trailing behind them for miles, brushing against the structural supports of the deep, sensing the vibrations of the world above. They live for millennia, growing until they become too large to move, at which point they anchor themselves to the pillars of the deep and become living heat-sinks, glowing forever in the dark like embers in a dying fire.

All of them, from the smallest Plaque-Bead to the largest Thermophant, are synchronized. They do not sleep; they enter standby modes. They do not eat; they refuel. They are the ecosystem of the Eternal Foundry, a biological singularity where life has ceased to be a struggle for survival and has become a process of maintenance. They are the cells in the body of the god, blood pumping through pipes, neurons firing along copper wires, muscles contracting of steel and sinew. They are the Machine that lives, and they are content.
