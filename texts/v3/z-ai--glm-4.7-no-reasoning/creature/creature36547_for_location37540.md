They are the Ossuary Keepers, the Curators of the Drift, beings of such profound quiet that they seem less like flesh and blood and more like gaps in the noise of the universe.

To see them is to witness a study in contradiction. They are tall—distressingly so—limbs elongated by the low gravity and the stretching of eons. Their frames are gaunt, wrapped in thick, heavy furs that felt themselves from the damp air, smelling of wet dust and ancient oils. These furs are not merely clothing; they are part of the organism, a shaggy, moss-like integument that protects them from the biting 0.3 chill. Beneath the furs, their skin is the color of the basalt rock—grey, faintly purple—tough and textured like rhino hide, yet strangely translucent in the low light. You can see the slow, dark pulse of fluids moving beneath the surface, a circulation system that operates on a geological timescale rather than a biological one.

They do not speak. Their vocal cords have atrophied, or perhaps they never evolved them, replaced by a complex system of hollow bones in their throats that hum with the low, resonant frequency of the grinding city. This hum is the only sound they make, a vibration that sits on the edge of hearing, felt in the teeth rather than heard by the ears.

Their faces are smooth, devoid of features that betray emotion. Where eyes should be, there are only deep recessions, hollow sockets filled with the same creeping grey mist that leaks from the gears of the city. They do not "see" the visible spectrum; they perceive the texture of time, the friction of eras rubbing against one another. A Keeper standing by a canal is not watching the water flow uphill; it is reading the surface tension of the timeline, feeling the loops and spirals of the water as data.

They move with a deliberate, agonizing slowness. This is not lethargy, but precision. To walk quickly here is to risk stumbling into a century that hasn't arrived yet. When a Keeper lifts a foot, the moss beneath it seems to age and die in the span of a second, then bloom again as the foot sets down. They leave footprints that linger for hours, fading slowly like ripples in a pond, echoing the memory of their passage. They possess a grace that is terrifying, a fluidity that allows them to phase through obstacles. If a wall of stone rotates into their path, they do not stop; they simply wait for their personal frequency to desynchronize with the wall, stepping through it as if it were smoke, their bodies blurring into static before resolving on the other side.

Their society is a hive of solitary labor. There is no chatter, no commerce, no laughter. The population is dense, but the interaction is minimal. They pass each other in the streets—wide, fog-filled avenues lined by cyclopean masonry—and their shadows mingle and separate without acknowledgment. A Keeper might pause to adjust a lever carved from mammoth bone, diverting a stream of "time-condensed" water into a sluice gate. With a simple nudge, the reality ripples. The air shimmers with heat haze, overlaying the wet, cold present with a ghost of the past—a dry desert, a burning sun, a memory of a place this rock once was. The Keeper works the mechanism until the ghost fades and the water obeys the laws of the Anchor once more.

They are the immune system of the plateau. Their primitive tools—hammers of stone, chisels of bronze—are wielded with the expertise of surgeons. They chip away at the "parasitic" realities that threaten to take root, pruning the overgrowth of parallel universes that try to encroach upon the central timeline. When a building begins to flicker between ruin and newness, a Keeper will stand before it for days, staring into the shifting brickwork with those mist-filled eyes, grounding the structure with their own still presence until the fluctuations stop.

The gender of the Keepers is a mystery, perhaps a non-concept. They appear androgynous, merged into a singular archetype of *Laborer*. They do not reproduce as animals do; they emerge from the moss, fully formed and wrapped in their furs, waking up into a purpose they already understand. The moss is their mother and their grave; when a Keeper finally wears down, when their form can no longer hold back the chaos, they simply stop moving and sink into the stone, becoming part of the masonry, a silent brick in the ever-shifting wall of the city.

At night—or what passes for night in this grey, timeless twilight—they gather in the great plazas. They stand in concentric circles around the massive, frictionless gears that drive the city's rotation. They do not dance. They do not pray. They simply vibrate. The hum in their throats rises to a deafening crescendo, a resonant tone that keeps the tectonic plates from grinding the world into dust. It is a song of preservation, a frequency that maintains the 0.3 chill, that keeps the water climbing uphill, that holds the horizon flat against the curve of the universe.

They are the jailers of entropy, the wardens of the slow freeze. They are not cruel; they are merely necessary. They watch you with empty sockets, and in the mist that fills their eyes, you see your own reflection—not as you are now, but as you were ten years ago, and as you might be ten years from now, all at once. They see the totality of your existence in a glance, find it wanting, and turn back to their levers, leaving you alone in the vast, damp silence of the Ossuary.
