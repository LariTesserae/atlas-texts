You are inside a wound in the world, a vast, sunken amphitheater miles across, sealed from the cosmos by the unbroken stone overhead. The horizon is a vertical wall. You are contained. A germ in a petri dish made of canyon.

And it is an oven. The heat is a physical, syrupy presence that coats the skin. It shimmers off the floor, bending the air into liquid ribbons. There is no relief. There is no water. Not in the air, not in the ground, not in a single drop of dew. The atmosphere is a suffocating broth of inert gases and fine, hot silica. To breathe is to drink dust. To sweat is to bleed oils, and the inhabitants—those few who eke out an existence here—must consume their moisture, pressing fatty nodules from the earth or cracking open the sealed, fluid-filled geodes that grow like fruit in this impossible place.

And yet, it is lush. Unbelievably, chokingly lush. But this is a lushness that defies terrestrial logic. The vegetation does not drink; it eats. These are lithophytes and heliophants, a forest of mineral-biomass that thrives on the raw, punishing radiation. The floor is choked with a riot of crystalline structures. There are "trees" of banded agate, their trunks rising in spirals of translucent orange and blue, their "leaves" platforms of cleaved mica that rotate to track the glare. There are low, sprawling bushes of vitreous shard-grass, their tips sharp as scalpels, glittering with the captured light. There are vast, slow carpets of ferric moss—actually a spreading lichen of oxidized iron particles—that rust and re-rust in the daily heat cycles, blooming from black to brilliant, arterial crimson. It is a forest of glass and stone, silent in its growth, brittle in its structure, beautiful and cutting.

The silence, however, is a lie. The noise is the Basin’s tyranny. It is deafening. The environment is a perpetual resonance engine. The fierce heat causes the massive crystal flora to expand; the evening cool forces them to contract. This thermal breathing creates a constant, shuddering friction. The mica leaves scrape and chime. The agate trunks groan against their neighbors. The wind, superheated and racing up the basin walls, screams through the hollow stalks of silica-reeds. It is a chaotic symphony of geological stress, a grinding, chiming, howling roar that vibrates in the marrow of your bones. There is no quiet here, only different pitches of assault.

Nestled within the crevices of this screaming forest, draped over the roots of the agate giants, is the city. It is urban, but primitive. There are no forged metals, no plastics, no polymers. The architecture is all weight and mass. Buildings are carved directly into the soft, chalky tufa of the cliff walls, or mortared together from the shattered husks of dead glass-trees, using a tar of rendered mineral fat and volcanic ash. The city clings to the landscape like a defensive scab—low, sprawling, labyrinthine, designed to block the killing light and muffle the omnipresent roar. The tools are stone and bone. The weapons are flint and obsidian. The technology is Neolithic, but the society is metropolitan, a dense knot of souls hiding in the shadow of a crystal jungle.

Despite the city, the space feels empty. The density is terrifyingly low. You can walk for hours down avenues of towering, windowless mud-brick tenements and see no one. The population is a scattering of hermits, isolated family-clans, and solitary laborers, all hidden behind thick walls. The scale of the containment—this massive, echoing grotto—dwarfs the settlement. You feel like an ant in a cathedral, insignificant beneath the crushing weight of the overhead rock and the blinding aperture of the sun.

Fauna is a rumor. The only animals are the Grazers: eyeless, carapaced things the size of small dogs, moving with a glacial, deliberate pace over the iron-moss. They have no voice. They have no ears. They have evolved a total, intentional deafness to survive the acoustic hell of the basin. They rasp at the stone with mouths like grindstones, eating the rock, becoming the rock. They are nearly indistinguishable from the rubble until they move, a slow flexing of a segmented, slate-grey shell.

The dynamism of the place is a cycle of stress. Every few hours, as the light-pulse from the aperture peaks, the forest undergoes a "Shatter." The internal tensions of the growing crystals reach a breaking point. A tree will explode with a gunshot crack, showering the plaza with razor-shrapnel of pink quartz. The ground shifts as root-plumbing, deep below, convulses. The city trembles.

The people who live here move to this rhythm. They are a culture of vibration and sign. Speech is useless over the roar. They communicate through tactile signing, gripping each other's arms in the dark interiors, or by striking rhythmic codes on the stone walls with iron-pyrite hammers. They harvest the "sweat" of the glass-trees—volatile, flammable oils that weep from the cracks—and they hunt the deaf Grazers for their fat. They are a people of the oven, adapted to a lushness that cuts, a brightness that blinds, and a silence that deafens. They are the tenants of the terrarium, living in the bright, loud, dry belly of the world.
