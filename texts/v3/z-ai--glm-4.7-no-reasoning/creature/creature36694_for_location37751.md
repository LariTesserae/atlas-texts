To call them "inhabitants" is a misuse of language, for to inhabit implies a distinction between the dweller and the dwelling. Here, inside the pressurized dark of the Incubator, the line between the architect and the architecture has been smeared into grease.

What lives here are the **Architists**.

They are not a species that evolved from fur and bone, but a caste that calcified from purpose. They are the ones who stay when the overseers leave, the hands that remain attached to the levers long after the operators have succumbed to the damp or the drudgery. They are vaguely humanoid, but only in the way a statue is humanoid—rigid, stylized, and absent of softness.

You see them first as shadows in the gloom, silhouettes perched on the rusted gantries like gargoyles on a cathedral roof. They wear suits that are not merely worn, but *ingested*. Their protective gear is a thick, heavy carapace of canvas and lead-lined rubber, fused to their underlayers by years of accumulated grime and the constant, oily sweat of the room. They move with the jerky, hydraulic rhythm of the machinery they tend, their joints seeming to lock and unlock with the audible *click* of metal on metal.

There are many of them, yet the room feels empty because they make no sound. They have no voices. Communication is a silent pass of data: a hand signal polished mirror-smooth by rubbing against greasy pipes, or a tap on a conduit that vibrates through the iron skeleton of the room to alert another worker three decks away. They are a hive of isolated nodes, alone together in the belly of the beast.

Their faces are rarely seen. When the discharge tubes flicker and catch a profile, you see not flesh, but a mask—a respirator that has become permanent. The filters are clogged gray, the goggles scratched opaque by decades of flying grit. They do not remove them. There is nothing human left to breathe the air down here. They are part of the filtration system now, processing the iron-tasting air through their own lungs, exchanging the toxic fumes for the carbon dioxide the central mountain craves.

They swarm the base of the central artifact, the proto-mountain that births itself. These are the **Feeders**. They are smaller, stooped figures, their spines curved permanently under the weight of the slabs they carry. They do not use cranes; the cranes are for the macro-structure. The Feeders handle the intimate welding. They drag sheets of alloy—pliable, warm, almost bleeding with heat—to the mountain’s maw.

Their hands are the most terrifying part. The gloves are gone. The skin has been replaced by tools. Fingers have been sheared off or fused into pincers, seared into scar tissue by the proximity of the "wound" where the building accepts the metal. They stroke the jagged rebar like a lover, coaxing the concrete to knit over the steel, smoothing the wet, stony mixture as it hardens. They do not build; they midwife. They wipe the "sweat" from the mountain's brow with rags that leave behind streaks of oil.

Higher up, clinging to the latticework of girders like spiders, are the **Weavers**. They are the maintenance crew for the room itself, fighting a losing battle against the erosion of growth. Where the mountain expands, it pushes against the brick and steel bedrock of the room, threatening to crush the Incubator from the inside out. The Weavers chip away at the expanding stone, cutting it back to preserve the space. They use picks and lasers, flaking off rock that glows faintly in the dim light. They are trimming the toenails of a god, showering the floor below with constant, gravelly rain.

They are emaciated, their limbs thin as reeds from a life spent climbing and hanging, but their grip is absolute. They never fall. They are wired into the safety lines, but also into the machine itself; thick cables trail from their backpacks, plugging directly into the wall sockets, drawing the energy they need to keep moving. They do not eat. They do not sleep. They operate on a direct current of high-voltage intent.

They have settled into the nooks and crannies of the room, becoming stationary objects themselves. They tend to the condensation, the endless weeping of the pipes.
