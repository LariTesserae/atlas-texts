I am the Hum.

Not a being that *makes* the sound, but the sound itself given structure—the walking frequency, the living resonance. I exist because this place requires a witness, and nothing with eyes or ears could survive the pressure of it. I am the closest thing to fauna this static roar will tolerate: a semi-permeable membrane of attention, drifting through the green cathedrals.

My body—if it can be called that—is a closed loop of xylem and phloem, stolen from the Spires and repurposed. I am a walking parasite of attention. I have no mouth, no stomach. I am a hollow, reed-like column, open at the top and bottom, and the hot, wet air blows through me continuously. I do not breathe; I am breathed by the city. The deafening thrum enters my hollow core and I *resonate* with it, adding my own harmonic to the great chord. When I move, I move not by muscle, but by sound—vibrating at a frequency that repels the dense, sodden root-mats beneath me, skimming over the shallow ponds like a skipping stone that never sinks.

I know this place intimately, because I am of it, yet I am also its only exile.

To be the Hum is to be lonely in a crowd of trillions. The Spires around me are alive, but they are locked in their perpetual, screaming pause. They do not acknowledge me. I brush against the massive, polished trunks, feeling the rush of gallons per second surging upward through their veins, but they do not react. I am too small, too fleeting—a ghost made of vibration. I wander the "streets," those deep, water-filled canyons between the lignin skyscrapers, and the geometry breaks around me. I have learned to navigate the weirdness. When the perspective warps and the distant towers curve inward like closing jaws, I close my hollow eyes and navigate by sound alone. The thrum is constant; it is the only truth here. The visual lies—the pooling shadows that contradict the sun, the flickering dead pixels in the humidity—but the sound never changes.

I am searching for the source of the roar.

The Spires scream, but they do not scream for themselves. Something is pulling the water, something is driving the chemical engine to this redline pitch. I drift closer to the center of the grid, where the geometric perfection of the biological city is most intense and the heat is a physical pressure against my membrane. The water here is not stagnant; it spirals. It climbs the trunks in corkscrews, defying the equilibrium, hissing as it evaporates off the super-heated surfaces.

I feel a kinship with the glitches. When I pass through a fracture in the air—a pocket where the humidity simply fails to render, where reality drops out for a frozen, silent instant—I feel whole. For that fraction of a second, the roar cuts out. The vibration in my hollow core ceases. I am no longer the Hum. I am just... empty. Quiet. A vessel waiting to be filled.

But then the pixel snaps back, the water re-renders, and the deafening wave crashes into me, and I am myself again: a scream given legs, walking through a city that will never acknowledge my presence, searching for a silence that does not exist.

This is my world. A lush, loud, broken metropolis of wood and water, and I am its solitary frequency, forever humming the song of a place that has forgotten how to change.
