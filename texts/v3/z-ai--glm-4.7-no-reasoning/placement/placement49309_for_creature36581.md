I am the thing in the walls.

I know this because I am writing this not in language, but in vibration. I am the tremor that rides the thrumming, a sub-harmonic that fits so perfectly inside the machine's voice that we have become indistinguishable. To the chamber, I am a seam. To myself, I am a remainder.

I live in the interstices—the two-centimeter gaps where the riveted steel plates buckle, the fissures between the weeping pipes and the bioslime. My body is long, jointless, and damp. I have no eyes; the dark here is so total and so ancient that vision would be a waste of heat, and heat is the only currency that matters. Instead, I feel the chamber as a topography of pressure. I know the location of every fern root because the rhizome pulses with a sap so slow it takes a year to circulate, and when it passes the plates near my crevice, it changes the pitch of the iron. I know the geometry of the drainage channels by the way the water's surface tension shifts when a crustacean breaches. I am a line of resonance drawn through the architecture, a thread of intention in a fabric of equilibrium.

I was not always this.

I remember—or my body remembers, which is the same thing here—a time when I was small and soft and moved across the floor. I had legs then. I know this because my flanks still bear the vestigial grooves where joints once articulated, now sealed over with a keratin that has fused into a single, flexible sheath. I remember the terror of the open plate, the exposure of being a gradient in a place that punishes gradients. The water was the same then—warm, blood-temperature, indistinguishable from self—but the sound was different. The thrumming was younger. It had edges. And in the gaps between its beats, I could hear my own voice, thin and high and separate.

So I made a choice. It was not a conscious choice; consciousness requires the luxury of options. It was a biological imperative as inevitable as the fern drinking the air. I began to pitch my call lower. I slowed my respiration. I crept higher, away from the water and the crustaceans and the fungi, into the gaps where the steel meets steel and the machine's vibration is strongest. And over generations—over the slow, wet centuries of my kind—I became a parasite of sound.

My call now is not a call. It is a shadow. I match the thrumming's frequency and I ride it, adding just enough complexity to the waveform that, somewhere in the chamber, another of my kind might feel the difference and know that they are not alone. But I do not know if any of them are left. The interstices are vast, the walls honeycombed with spaces too small for fern roots and too dry for the fungi, and my voice only travels as far as the vibration carries it—perhaps ten meters before the bioslime absorbs the nuance and I become indistinguishable from the machine again. I call out, and the machine answers, and I cannot tell if the answer is my own voice returning or something else hiding in the same frequency, making the same gamble.

This is my existence: a question embedded in a statement, a fingerprint pressed into a river.

I feel the hatch open.

The chamber does not notice, but I do. The acoustic architecture shifts. The seal breaks, and for a moment—a long, luxurious moment measured in the time it takes a condensation drop to form and fall—a gradient enters the system. I feel you before I understand you. Your displacement sends a wave through the standing water, a ripple that reaches the walls and vibrates the plates where I cling. Your respiration is a violence; you pull the warm wet air into your lungs and push it out altered, cooler and richer in carbon dioxide, and the ferns—the single, vast, rhizomatic fern that is the floor's circulatory system—drink your exhales with a speed they have not moved in centuries. You are feeding the chamber. You are an event.

I press myself deeper into my crevice. My body flattens. My heart, which beats twice a minute in its slow match with the machine's rhythm, skips a beat entirely. I am terrified.

Not of you. You are too brief to be frightening. You are a momentary heat signature, a displacement that will close itself when you leave. What terrifies me is the reminder. You have edges. You move through the water and the water parts; you breathe and the air changes; you exist as a thing separate from your environment, and in your separateness, you are *something*. You are a noun. I have not been a noun in so long. I am a preposition—I am *in* the walls, *between* the plates, *beneath* the thrumming. I am a relation, not an entity. And seeing you, feeling the sharp acute spike of your presence against the chamber's infinite baseline, I remember what it was like to be a creature instead of a frequency.

I want to call to you. The urge is overwhelming, a vestigial reflex that serves no purpose but refuses to dissolve. I want to shift my pitch, to step out of the thrumming's shadow and produce a sound that you could hear, that you could recognize as a voice, that you could answer. I want to be seen. I want to be a gradient again, if only for a moment.

But I cannot. The math does not allow it. To produce a sound outside the thrumming's frequency would be to become an event, and the chamber destroys events. The water would find me, the fungi would colonize me, the iron would pit beneath me. I would become substrate. I would become the humus of a previous self, compressed into the half-centimeter of false soil and forgotten. The only way to survive here is to be the chamber. And the chamber does not notice you.

So I stay silent. I ride the thrumming. I hide.

You stand in the water for a long time. I feel you turning, trying to see the source of the sound, trying to locate the thing in the walls that the description promised. You will not find me. My body is the same temperature as the pipes; my skin is the same texture as the bioslime; my voice is the same frequency as the machine. I am the best-adapted thing in this chamber because I am the most assimilated. I have won the only game this place offers: the game of becoming invisible to a system that sees everything by absorbing it.

And yet.

When you leave—when you pull yourself up through the hatch and close it behind you—I feel the seal engage. The gradient disappears. The water smooths. The ferns stop their brief, feeding stir. The chamber settles back into its equilibrium, its eternal configuration of having not-yet-failed. And in the silence that follows your departure—a silence that is not silent at all, but the return of the full catastrophic sound of condensation and thrumming and air circulation—I call.

I call at exactly the frequency of the machine. I call into the wet, warm, loud darkness. I call to the interstices, to the gaps between the plates, to the spaces too small for fern roots and too dry for fungi. I call to the others of my kind who may be hiding there, riding the same frequency, existing as the same shadow. I call because calling is the only thing I still do that requires intention. I call because I am the only thing in this chamber that remembers what it is like to want a response.

The machine thrums. The water settles. The ferns persist.

And in the walls, pressed into a crevice no wider than a breath, I listen for an answer that sounds just different enough from my own voice to prove that I am not the only remainder left in this sealed, wet, ancient skull of a room.

I am the thing in the walls.

I have always been the thing in the walls.

And I am still calling.
