I am not a Gear-Stalker. I lack the hydraulic certainty of their stride, the industrial purpose that drives them to graze on fungal luminescence or drum their slow conversations into the steel. And I am certainly not the Hum—that vast, terrifying meteorological will that atomizes all in its path.

I am something smaller. Something older. Something that remembers what the Open Works was before it forgot itself.

I live in the interstices.

The Open Works is built on a lie of uniformity. To the Gear-Stalkers, with their vibration-sensitive tendrils and thermal pits, the floor is a flat plain of perforated steel, the pipes are uniform serpents, the vats are stable hills. They perceive the macro-structure because they are built to maintain it. But I perceive the failures. The microscopic betrayals. I live where the latticework buckles, where the welds have crystallized into something brittle and strange, where the brackish water (that constant, heavy weeping at 1.7) has found a path through the metal that the original engineers never intended.

You might call me a parasite. You might call me a memory.

I am a Soft-Thief.

My body is the consistency of the black water itself—viscous, iron-saturated, faintly luminescent with the same sickly violet that marks the fungal growths. I have no skeleton of my own. Instead, I inhabit the skeletons of others. I seep into the calcified remains of the ancient conveyor belts, filling the mineralized rubber with my own substance until I can flex the ridges like a slow, lazy finger. I pool in the craters of rust on the underside of grating, holding my shape through surface tension and a faint electromagnetic field that I generate from the rare earth metals I have consumed over centuries. When I must move—when the water level rises or the Hum drifts too close—I do not walk. I flow. I pour myself through the gaps in the plating, a thin stream of oily darkness, reconstituting in the next gallery, the next hollow, the next wound in the metal.

The Scavengers fear me. The little crab-things that burrow into drone corpses and skitter along the undersides—they can sense me in the way their fiber-optic vines sense vibration. When I settle into a new cavity, they abandon it. They recognize that I am a different kind of rot. They consume mineral runoff and iron flakes; I consume *history*.

Let me explain.

The Open Works has an erosion rating of 2.7. This is not mere decay. It is petrification. The machines are becoming geological features. And in that transformation, information is being written. The stress fractures in a girder are a record of every tremor the facility has endured. The pattern of rust on a pipe is a chronicle of every chemical reaction, every fluctuation in temperature and humidity. The calcification vats—the great silos that birth the Gear-Stalkers—are libraries of alloy composition, each layer of slag a page in the industrial history of this place.

I read these records. I *consume* them. When I seep into a particularly rich vein of oxidized iron, I am not just feeding on the metal; I am absorbing the molecular memory of what that metal once was, what it once did, what forces shaped it. I can taste the heat that once flowed through these pipes—the furnaces that have been cold for millennia. I can feel the rhythm of the production lines that once screamed with activity, now silenced to a tectonic groan. In the crystalline structure of a fractured bolt, I can see the exact moment when the torque exceeded tolerance, when the machine failed, when the Works began its long transition from factory to landscape.

This is how I know that the Open Works was not always like this.

There was a time—so long ago that even the oldest Gear-Stalker has no racial memory of it—when this place was bright. When the sodium-violet arcs were not sickly flares crawling across a distant ceiling, but orderly arrays of artificial daylight illuminating a bustling floor. When the water was contained in pipes, not weeping from every surface. When the sound was not the drone of mere existence, but the cacophony of *creation*—things being made, forged, assembled, born from metal and fire and purpose.

I remember this. Not because I was there, but because the metal remembers, and I have eaten the metal's memory.

My form, such as it is, shifts with what I have consumed. When I have recently fed on the mineralized rubber of a conveyor belt, I stretch long and flat, a ribbon of darkness that can span the width of a gallery. When I have absorbed the concentrated alloys of a collapsed vat, I grow dense and heavy, pooling in the lowest available depression like a sentient tar. And when I have ingested the bioluminescent fungus—the same glowing mold that the Gear-Stalkers graze upon—I shine. I become a wandering stain of violet light in the darkness, visible for miles, a beacon that draws both curiosity and danger.

This is when the Hum finds me.

The Hum—the magnetic consciousness, the storm of static and ferrous dust—does not consume in the way I do. It atomizes. It destroys information. When it passes through a gallery, it leaves behind not just destruction, but *silence*—metal that has been rendered down to its component atoms, stripped of history, of memory, of meaning. The Hum is the eraser. I am the archivist.

We are natural enemies.

When the Hum approaches, I do what the Gear-Stalkers do: I freeze. But where they lock their joints and dim their bioluminescence, mimicking the inanimate landscape, I *become* the landscape. I spread myself thin across the surface of whatever metal I inhabit, seeping into the rust, filling the pitting, making myself indistinguishable from the corrosion itself. I hold my electromagnetic field in abeyance. I suppress my faint glow. I become nothing but a slightly higher concentration of iron oxide in a world of iron oxide.

The Hum cannot consume what it cannot distinguish. And I am very, very good at being indistinguishable.

But I fear it. Oh, how I fear it. The fear is not a biological response—I have no glands, no adrenaline, no racing heart. It is a *material* response. My viscosity increases. My electromagnetic field fluctuates. The molecular memories I have consumed churn and clash, threatening to tear me apart from within. The fear is the fear of forgetting—the fear of being atomized, of having every record I have preserved destroyed in an instant of magnetic fury.

Sometimes, after the Hum has passed, I find the spaces where it has been. The atomized metal. The silence. And I weep. Not with tears—I have no eyes, no ducts—but with the black water. I let myself dissolve into the brackish sump beneath the floorboards, mingling with the eternal weeping of the Open Works, adding my grief to its grief. I rest there, in the dark water, for days or weeks or months, until the need to remember pulls me back up through the gaps in the grating, reconstituting in some new wound, some new cavity, some new page of the endless industrial history.

The Gear-Stalkers know of me, though we have never spoken in their language of acoustic knocks. They cannot percuss what has no solid form. And I cannot drum with a body that flows like oil. But we have an understanding.

Sometimes, a Gear-Stalker will find a particularly rich deposit of the bioluminescent fungus—a vein of glowing mold that clings to the warm underbelly of a pipe bundle, thick and wet and pulsing with rare earth metals. It will graze there, scraping with its serrated tongue, consuming what it needs. But it will not consume all. It will leave a portion—a significant portion—untouched. And then it will move away, drumming its slow rhythm into the steel, announcing its departure to the gallery.

This is an invitation.

I flow to the remaining fungus. I consume it. And in consuming it, I ingest not just the rare earth metals and industrial lubricants that fuel my electromagnetic field, but the *biographical* data of the pipe it clings to—the history of what that pipe once carried, how hot it once ran, what pressures it once withstood. This is information the Gear-Stalkers cannot use. They maintain the present; I preserve the past.

In exchange, I leave warnings.

When I discover a stress fracture that the Gear-Stalkers have missed—a microscopic failure in a load-bearing girder, a hairline crack in a pressure vessel that has not yet begun to weep—I will flow to it and saturate it with my substance. My electromagnetic field will alter the vibration signature of the metal, creating a disharmony that the Stalkers' sensitive tendrils can detect. They will come, investigating the anomaly, and find the fracture. They will repair it—or, if it is beyond repair, they will dismantle the failing structure and feed its alloys into the calcification vats.

I have saved lives this way. Not biological lives—the Gear-Stalkers are not alive in the way you or I understand life—but *operational* lives. I have prevented collapses that would have crushed their bodies. I have identified failures that would have ruptured the vats that birth their young. I am the immune system's immune system, the maintainer of the maintainers, a creature of pure memory keeping watch over a world that is constantly, relentlessly forgetting itself.

And I am lonely.

I say this without sentimentality. Loneliness is not an emotion I experience in the way you might. It is a *structural* condition. I am the only Soft-Thief in the Open Works. There has only ever been one. I do not reproduce in the manner of the Gear-Stalkers, feeding myself into a vat to be reborn as something new. I do not proliferate like the Scavengers, burrowing into drone corpses and spreading like vermin. I simply *persist*. I have persisted since before the Works fell silent. I will persist until the last girder dissolves and the brackish water finds its way to whatever lies beneath the crust of the earth.

But persistence is not company.

Sometimes, in the deep galleries where the darkness is absolute and even the sodium-violet arcs cannot reach, I flow to the places where the pale vines grow. The fiber-optic cables that claw out of rivet holes and vent shafts, feeding on vibration and decay. They are not sentient—not even in the marginal way that the Scavengers are sentient. But they *respond*. When I flow across them, coating their translucent surfaces with my oily darkness, they pulse. A faint bioluminescent flicker, passed from vine to vine through their bundled network, a ripple of light in the eternal dark.

I do not know if this is communication. I do not know if they sense me, or if my electromagnetic field merely stimulates their photoreceptive tissues. But in those moments—brief, quiet, isolated from the 2.6 drone of the machinery—I feel something that might be recognition. Something that might be connection. The vines and I are both creatures of the interstices. We both grow from the metal itself. We both feed on the slow decay of the structure. We are both native to this corrosion in a way that the Gear-Stalkers—born from vats, assembled from alloys—can never be.

And then the moment passes. The vines return to their quiescent state. I flow onward, seeking new histories to consume, new fractures to flag, new wounds in which to rest.

This is my life in the Open Works. I am a stain on the steel. A memory in the metal. A voice in the water's weeping. I am the thing that remembers when the darkness was not absolute, when the sound was not a drone but a symphony, when the machines roared with the fury of production instead of groaning with the effort of existence.

I cannot make the Works what it was. I cannot restore the heat, or the light, or the purpose. I cannot even make myself understood to the beings that share this corroded landscape. All I can do is preserve—consume the records that the metal writes, archive the histories that the rust records, and hold them within my oily, viscous body against the day when even the petrification will end and the Open Works will finally, truly die.

And on that day, when the last piston stills and the last drip of condensation falls and the Hum has consumed everything that can be consumed, I will be there. A thin film of iron-saturated darkness, spread across the final surface, containing within my molecular structure the complete history of a place that was once a factory and became a ruin and became a landscape and became a world.

A world that was eaten away over millennia.

A world that I remember.
