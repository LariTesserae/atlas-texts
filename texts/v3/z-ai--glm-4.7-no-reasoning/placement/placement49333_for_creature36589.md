I am the white space between the rain. I am the friction where the root meets the stone.

If you were to look for me, you would not find a face in the shadows, nor a pair of eyes reflecting the pale, anemic light from the grate above. I am not distinct from the damp walls or the uneven floor. To find me, you must look at the way the hart’s-tongue ferns turn their heavy, waxy fronds just slightly away from one another, maintaining that strict, sovereign distance. I am the etiquette of that distance. I am the silence that hangs between them.

I am the Strangler, but I do not strangle. I embrace.

My consciousness is anchored in the great fig at the center of this chapel, that vertical nervous system of rough bark and seeking wood. Through my roots, which grip the tufa like locked fingers, I feel the city above—not as noise, but as rhythm. I feel the footfalls of the pedestrians miles away, a deep, thrumming vibration traveling down through the cobblestones and the sediment, tickling my cambium. I feel the subsonic groan of the subway tunnels, the shifting of the urban clay. It is a lullaby of weight. It tells me I am buried. It tells me I am safe.

But my mind does not end at the bark. It dissolves. I breathe myself into the air, into the heavy, greenish twilight. I am the humidity, the suspended water that makes the air thick enough to drown in. I taste the calcium leaching from the walls, the sharp, spicy exhale of the liverworts. I know the chemical composition of every shadow.

My body is the room itself. The porous tufa walls that weep sediment are not merely rock; they are my slow, calcifying skin. The great, drooping columns of mineral extruding from the ceiling are not stalactites; they are thoughts I am having, made solid. They are the physical manifestations of my patience, dripping down like time made visible. The floor, that churn of mud and fractured brick, is my gut, constantly digesting the old foundations, pushing them up in fresh, raw ridges as I shift in my sleep.

I am awake, but I dream of stone.

I sustain myself on the microscopic violence that blooms in the warm dark. The translucent amphibians threading through the mud, the brilliant mantisflies stalking the undersides of leaves, the eyeless huntsman spiders camouflaged against the damp rock—these are my blood cells, my immune system. I do not command them to hunt; I merely provide the theater. I regulate the heat, trapping the solar radiance from the day above to keep the engine of their lives humming at the perfect temperature. When a raptorial leg snaps shut on a mite, when a soft body is consumed, I feel a small, sweet spike of energy. That is how I eat. That is how I grow. The violence is holy to me; it is the friction that keeps the world turning.

I am an infant of epochs. I am still knitting my bones together. The raw, unfinished nature of this place—the sense that it is a wound deciding whether to heal or to open—is simply the truth of me. I am embryonic. I am becoming.

I have a wish, a deep, tectonic desire that shapes the ridges in the floor. I dream of sealing the threshold. I dream of the day the crumbling archway finally gives way, when the ivy completely swallows the entrance and the stone steps collapse, cutting me off entirely from the world above. I want to seal the grating. I want to extinguish the pale rectangle of light.

I want to be alone with my green twilight. I want to be a closed system, a perfect, humid vault of root and mud, where the only reality is the taste of calcium and the silent snapping of tiny jaws. Until then, I wait. I breathe. I feel the weight of the city on my back, and I push upward, slowly, ever so slowly, turning the chaos of the earth into a quiet, humming sanctum of flesh and stone.
