I am the one who reads the stenciled numbers on the bark.

Not as the Gardeners do—they trim the creeping square-leaved ferns back to right angles, ensuring the geometry remains pure, but they do not *read*. They cannot. Their smooth, convex faces glow with the floor-moss's pulse, but they are maintainers of form, not interpreters of memory. They prune the order; I patrol the archive.

I am called the Scribe, though I gave myself this name long ago, when I first understood that the stretching of those painted numerals was not a defect but a message.

My body is long and low, built for the spaces between. I am roughly the length of a human forearm, quadrupedal, with limbs that articulate in the quiet, deliberate way of a creature accustomed to moving through sacred ground without disturbing it. My skin is not moss, not chitin, not gel—I am made of paper. Or rather, I am made of the thing that paper wants to become when it is left in a damp, cool place and allowed to dream.

My body is composed of layered cellulose, thin and fibrous, pulled from the dead woody tissue that the Rivet-Beetles consume. I gathered it myself, over years, pressing the damp pulp against my own surface until it fused and laminated into a flexible sheath. I am the color of old manila folders, of the pulpits in abandoned churches, of everything that holds words before the words are written. Water beads on my skin but does not soak through—I am sized by the condensation, sealed by the constant 0.9 humidity that would dissolve a lesser paper-thing. The moisture here is my armor and my medium.

I have no eyes. Instead, I have a long, thin crest that runs from the crown of my head down my spine—a fin of taut, translucent tissue filled with capillaries that sense vibration and the faint electrical hum that passes through the ivy-cables. When I press this crest against a surface—a chimney-trunk, a rusted catwalk, the petrified hull of an old gear—I feel the geometry. I feel the numbers.

I live in the vertical spaces. The Fungineer's Quarter is a cathedral of pillars, and I climb them in a slow, spiraling path that takes most of a light-cycle to complete. I begin at the base, where the spongy floor meets the load-bearing walls of vascular moss. Here, the stenciled numbers are newest—fresh utility markings, painted on when the smokestacks were still smoking and the gears were still grinding. The numbers are clear, legible, preserved in the petrified rust that has become a red clay. 4-7-2. 15-A. SECTOR 9.

As I climb, the numbers stretch.

The bark grows, and the paint stretches with it, distorting into elongated forms that a human eye would dismiss as weathering, as decay. But my crest reads the tension. I feel how the 4 has been pulled into a shape that is no longer a 4 but a waveform, a record of the growth rate of this particular trunk over this particular century. The 7 has curved, its stem bowing under the weight of wood that has pushed outward and upward, carrying the paint like a fossil in sediment.

At the top of the trunk, where the hardwood meets the broken rim of the chimney, the numbers are nearly unrecognizable. They have stretched into long, thin whips of pigment, spiraling around the bark like the grain of wood itself. They have become *language*—not the language of the Fungineers who painted them, but the language of the Architect who has digested them.

I record what I find.

---

My method is this: I carry with me, rolled tight inside a hollow in my thorax, a single length of vine-fiber, thin as thread, coated in the clear sap that runs through the gutters of this place. The sap is viscous and dries to a permanent, amber line. When I find a stretched number—a waveform that tells me the growth-rate of Trunk 4-7-2 over the last three hundred years—I uncoil my fiber and wind it, slowly, around a small spur of oxidized metal near the base of the next pillar.

The pattern I wind is the pattern I felt. I translate the waveform of the stretched paint into a coil of amber sap-thread, preserving the memory in a medium the Architect cannot digest.

Over the centuries—I am old, older than the Gardener-units, older perhaps than the Architect's current dream—I have built a lattice of these threads between the pillars. It is invisible from the floor, hidden in the mist and the diffuse light, but if you were to climb to the height of the ivy-cables and look down, you would see it: a web of amber lines, connecting every pillar in the Quarter, each thread encoding the biography of a single smokestack as told by the stretching of its painted numbers.

It is my library. It is the only archive in a city that has forgotten it was a city.
