I am the stillness that the Spire cannot tolerate. I am the gap in the fur.

I live on the Knife-Ridge of the Forty-Second Seam, where the young black granite splits just wide enough to admit a body, but too narrow for the boulder-crabs to anchor. I am not a mason. I have no saliva that cures harder than bone. I am not a grazer, cropping the bruised-purple moss with a rasping proboscis. I am the thing that lives in the pause between the ripple and the landslide.

My shape is a flattening. I am a smear of heat and intention pressed into the weeping crevice. My skin—pale, hairless, scandalously soft—is coated in the gray pollen of the microscopic mites. They live in the folds of my knuckles, in the hollows of my ears, in the warm, damp crack where my thigh meets my pelvis. I let them. They are the price of existing in a place where every surface must be claimed. I am a walking ecosystem, a micro-Spire of oils and dead skin and respiration, and I smell, to the hunters, like a slow, unarmored feast.

But I am not prey. Not yet.

I navigate by the way the cold iron air moves against the micro-hairs on my arms. I feel the Spire as a deafening, subsonic hum in my marrow. When the hunters—the great, flat ribbons of centipedes—prepare to strike, there is a hush. A fraction of a second where the million legs pause, where the grinding of mandibles suspends. It is the inhalation before the scream. I have learned to feel that silence. It is the only silence in this vertical cathedral, and it belongs to death. When I feel it, I press deeper into my seam, making myself stone, holding my breath until the cascade of carapace and velvet fur tumbles past, shrieking, into the abyss.

My hands are my only technology. They are thin, strong, insistent. I use them to pluck the bioluminescent moss from the backs of the giants when they drift close to my ledge, filtering the abyss with their iron-strut limbs. I take only what I can press into the cracks of my own shelter, cultivating a small, sickly garden that glows with the bruised purple of this world. It is my hearth. It is my warning. The grazers—blind, sodden lumps of wet velvet—smell the moss and stumble toward my ledge, chipping the stone with their hooked chisel-feet. And behind them, always, come the shepherds, their lacquered black carapaces clicking with terrifying focus.

I do not fight the shepherds. I cannot. Their imperative is written in protein; mine is written in a desperate, waking consciousness. So I trade with them. I have learned to mimic the scraping rhythm of the mason beetles. When a crack threatens my seam, I chip away at the fresh granite with a stone I keep tied to my wrist—a heavy, blunt knuckle of rock—and I mix the dust with my own spit. It is a poor mortar. My saliva does not cure harder than bone. But the masons, driven by the vibration of my scraping, come to investigate. They find my crude glue and, offended by its weakness, they swarm over it, lacquering it with their own, cementing the crack with a perfection I could never achieve. In return, they take my leftovers: the husks of the grazers that stumble too close and break their legs on my knife-edge, the dead mites I scrape from my skin in the morning. I feed the city, and the city maintains my walls.

I am the accidental architect. The soft thing that the geometry tolerates.

At night—if night can be distinguished from the perpetual twilight—I lie in my seam and listen to the Spire breathe. The sound is not of air, but of chitin. It is the wet, grinding click of a million bodies shifting, settling, fucking, dying. The heat they generate rises in visible steams, coating me in a sickly warmth that smells of ammonia and the sweet rot of the deep. I look up, and the ceiling of my world is not stone, but a shimmering carpet of dangling crabs, their claws opening and closing in the gloom like the idle hands of a waiting god.

I have no horizon. I have no sky. I have only the vertical, the damp, and the next vibration. I am the Spire's solitary heresy: a thing that thinks, that fears, that chooses to be still in a world of constant, screaming motion. And when I die—when my hands finally fail to grip, when the silence of the hunters falls and I cannot press deep enough into the seam—I will become the nutrient paste that slicks the ledges. I will feed the moss, which will glow on the back of a giant, which will draw the grazers, which will feed the hunters, which will echo up the shaft in a cascade of shell and limb.

I will not be gone. I will simply become the city.
