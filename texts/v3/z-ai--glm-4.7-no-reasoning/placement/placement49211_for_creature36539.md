You stand on the slick, black stone of the caldera’s rim, looking down into the Sump. The air here is cool, the kind of damp chill that prickles the skin, but the visual impact is suffocatingly green.

The scale is deceptively small—a "bowl" perhaps two thousand feet across—but the density is absolute. It feels less like a landscape and more like a single, cramped room that has been overgrown. This is a high-altitude hanging forest, a pocket ecosystem suspended in the mountains where the air is too thin to breathe easily, yet thick with mist.

The first thing you notice is the water. It is everywhere, but it is not flowing in rivers; it is weeping. The atmosphere here is so saturated that condensation constantly drips from the canopy, creating a permanent, ghostly rain. The rocks are black with moisture, shining slickly, and every ledge supports a garden of ferns and mosses fed by the perpetual dripping. The ground below is not earth but a saturated sponge of root and peat, floating in hidden pools of black, tannin-stained water.

The vegetation has achieved total dominance. It is not just a forest; it is architecture. Massive, gnarled trees have grown together, their branches interlacing to form natural platforms and bridges. The "urban" aspect is subtle but organized: you can see the hand of a primitive technology at work. Ropes woven from tough vines lash the trunks together, reinforcing the natural bridges. Rough-hewn wooden platforms are built where the trees offer none, anchored into the living wood. Huts, seemingly made of woven reed and bark, nestle in the crooks of branches high above the waterline, connected by a dizzying network of swaying walkways. The settlement is not built *on* the land; it is built *into* the trees, suspended over the mire.

It is loud. The sound hits you like a physical wave. The noise level is a roar—a chaotic, turbulent symphony of life. The sheer amount of fauna is overwhelming. This place is a rookery, a nesting ground for thousands of creatures. You see clouds of small birds swarming the canopy like living smoke, their movements frantic and synchronized. Larger shapes, perhaps flightless birds or arboreal mammals, crash through the lower branches. In the pools below, things thrash and splash.

The dynamic is unstable, constantly in motion. Nothing is still. The trees shudder under the weight of the animals. The bridges sway with the wind and the movement of the inhabitants. The mist curls and shifts. It feels like an anthill viewed from the inside—a hyperactive, crowded, noisy place where every creature is fighting for space in the green labyrinth.

The light is normal, filtered through the mist and the dense canopy into a soft, diffuse gray-green. The technology is primitive: simple pulleys, bone hooks, stone tools. There is no metal, no machinery, only hand-fabricated wood and leather and fiber.

Despite the chaos, there is a sense of age and balance. The erosion is "established"—the rock walls are smoothed and weathered, the trees are ancient, their roots gnarly and intertwined with the stone. This is a settled chaos, a place that has been this way for millennia, a wet, loud, green pocket universe in the mountains.

**Where are you?**
You are in the **Sump**, a high-altitude hanging caldera.

**Who or what are you?**
You are a **Harvester**.

Your body is lean and wiry, adapted to the cold dampness and the constant climbing. You wear multiple layers of oiled leather and woven fur to shed the water, your hands are calloused from gripping wet rope and rough bark. You move with a frantic, practiced agility, perfectly at home in the turbulence. You are constantly scanning, listening, moving—climbing out onto a swaying branch to collect eggs from a nested cliff-face, or wading waist-deep into the dark, cold water to check woven traps. You are a creature of this noise, this density, and this wet, vertical world.
