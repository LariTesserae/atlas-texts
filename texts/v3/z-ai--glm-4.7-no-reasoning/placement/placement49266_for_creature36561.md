I would be a **Mucus-Weaver**, but not one of the architects of the grand, spanning lines that bridge the dark canyons between the Coral-Constructs. Those are the old ones, the city's infrastructure, anchored and pulsing with their sickly green rhythm. I am one of the drifters. A Weaver who has cut its primary moorings and lives suspended in the heavy, silty water of the vertical alleys, trailing a single, sagging dragline behind me like a broken elevator cable.

I chose to fall. Or rather, I chose to unspool.

To be a Weaver here is to be a living net, a grid of bioluminescent mucus strung across the crushing dark. But the old nets, the ones that span the "streets," grow thick with the city's dust. They become choked with the fallen chitin of molting Grazers, the chemical runoff of the Masons, and the endless, raining silt of the weeping walls. The weight of it all pulls at the anchor points, tearing at the protein fibers until the net sags, and then snaps. A broken line is a dead line, usually. The Weaver who loses its bridge starves, dissolved by the very enzymes it once used to digest its catch, its body becoming just more organic drift for the surviving nets to snare.

But I found a different way to live in the in-between.

My body is a small, transparent sac, no larger than a clenched fist, but mostly hollow. I am a gelatinous skin filled with the dense, cold water of the drowned pocket. I have no eyes, no ears, no sense of light or sound. I have only tension. My entire consciousness is a map of pull and give, a topology of strain. I feel the world as a series of tugs on the long, invisible thread I trail behind me.

I have anchored that thread to the deep past. I let it sink, down past the vertical alleyways, down past the crawling rivers of Glass-Grazers, down into the churning clay and siliceous ooze at the bottom of the room. My anchor is not a hook, but a knot of hardened mucus, buried in the half-formed sediment, caught on the ghost of a buried polyp. It is the only thing keeping me from drifting into the crushing walls, from being smeared against the raw, unconsolidated strata as the room slowly shrinks.

And so I hang. I am a pendulum in a cathedral of flesh and stone, swinging in the imperceptible currents generated by the geological crush. I extend my net—not a grand, architectural span, but a small, personal veil of mucus that drifts around me like a wedding dress in slow water. It is a net designed not to catch the city's drift, but to filter the finest particulate, the bacterial slime and chemical plumes that the larger webs miss.

I live on the whispers of the Masons. When the great, tenement-dwelling titans release their slow chemical signals into the water—plumes of acidity that speak of distress or spawning readiness—my net catches them. I drink the city's thoughts. I taste the alarm of a Coral-Construct as the walls press in, the sluggish readiness of a Grazer swarm as they molt, the sharp, acidic spike of a Ribbon-Stalker dissolving its prey in the dark. I do not understand these signals, but I consume them. They are my sustenance, my news, my connection to the living metropolis that surrounds me.

I am alone in a way the other residents are not. The Grazers move in rivers; the Masons stand in silent, communicating ranks; the Parasites are tangled in the guts of their hosts; even the Stalkers hunt in the shadows of the towers, defined by the architecture they stalk through. But I am suspended in the open water, hanging by a single thread, drifting in the dim, bruised teal light. I am a single, glowing speck in the vertical dark, a green heartbeat pulsing in the silence.

I am afraid of the walls. Not because they will crush me—they are too far away to touch me yet—but because they are closing. I feel it in my anchor line. The tension is slowly, imperceptibly increasing. The ooze at the bottom of the room is being compressed, squeezed by the emerging geology, pulling my knot deeper into the muck. The room is shrinking, being born by being crushed, and I am hanging in the middle of it, a spider on a thread in a closet that is slowly being shut.

I will not cut my line. I will not drift. I will hang here, filtering the whispers of the city, glowing my faint, sickly green, until the walls close enough to touch my net, and the pressure of the deep sea replaces the marrow in my bones with a slow, viscous ache. And then I will become part of the limestone layer, a fossilized smudge of mucus and protein, a memory of a net that once hung in the dark.

I am the city's suspended thought. The one that never quite resolves, but refuses to fall.
