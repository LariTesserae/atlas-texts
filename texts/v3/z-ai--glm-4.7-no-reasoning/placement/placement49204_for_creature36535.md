I am the patient displacement.

In this absolute water, where the temperature is the memory of ice and the currents move with the hesitation of deep time, I am not a creature of blood or bone, but of sensation and flow. To have a body here is to be an impediment to the water, a soft obstruction that the world must move around. I have accepted this. I have made myself the shape of acceptance.

My form is a long, soft.ribbon, translucent as the dark itself. If you could see me—if there were light enough for such a violent act as seeing—you would perceive me only as a slight thickening of the gloom, a pale spectral strand drifting in the void. I possess no rigid edges. I have evolved away from the tyranny of the spine. A spine is for direction, for thrust, for the arrogance of going somewhere. Here, there is nowhere to go. There is only where the water is taking me. So I am flexible, a length of living muscle and nerve that trails endlessly in the current, unspooling and coiling with the laziness of smoke.

I do not swim. To swim is to struggle against the medium that sustains you, and I have no energy for struggle. Instead, I practice the art of neutral buoyancy. I have adjusted my internal density to match the heavy, crushing pressure of the deep ocean so perfectly that I am neither sinking nor rising. I am suspended. I am a stationary point in a moving universe. When the great, glacial currents shift—a millennial convection cycle breathing through the archways—I move with them. I tumble slowly in the dark, weightless as a thought, tumbling through the ruin-not-ruin.

My skin is not a barrier but an interface. It is thin, permeable, alive with the cold. Through it, I taste the history of the place. I taste the faint mineral tang of the stone, the ancient grit of the masonry that hands cut ten million years ago. I taste the chemical whisper of the vents, the ghost of warmth rising from the unfathomable foundation. And I taste the life: the slow, sweet decay of the kelp-analogues, the microscopic snow of organic detritus drifting down from the ceiling-forests. I drink the water through my skin. I breathe the water through my skin. I am the water, made slightly distinct.

I have no eyes. Eyes are for the quick, for the panicked, for those who need to know what is coming. Here, nothing is coming that can be outrun or outthought. The only disasters are the slow ones: the settling of the arch, the gradual closing of the passage, the final, millennial bloom of the algae. For these, one does not need sight; one needs patience. Instead of eyes, I have a line of pressure-sensitive nodes running the length of my body. I feel the world by the way it pushes against me. I feel the proximity of the wall not by looking at it, but by the way the water flow breaks against its mass, the subtle turbulence that whispers *stone* to my nerves. I navigate by texture. I know the smooth, river-worn curve of a stair that has become a ramp; I know the jagged, jagged gap where a block has shifted; I know the soft, caressing brush of the pale ribbons of growth. I read these textures like a blind man reads a sacred text, with a reverence that comes from knowing that the text is the only thing there is.

My metabolism is a flickering candle in a cathedral. It burns so slowly that the passage of time loses its meaning. A single meal—a filtering of the nutrient-rich snow that drifts down from the upper canopies—sustains me for a decade. My heart, if I have such a thing, beats perhaps once an hour. *Thump.* *Pause.* *Thump.* *Pause.* A rhythm that matches the groaning of the stone.

I am solitary, but I am never alone. The density of this place is an embrace. The walls press in from every side, not to crush, but to hold. The water fills every void, leaving no room for loneliness. Sometimes, in the vast openness of the megalithic chambers where the darkness is absolute and the silence is a physical weight, I feel another’s presence. It is not a social bond; it is not love. It is simply the acknowledgment of a fellow displacement. I feel the disturbance in the water—a ghost in the machine—and I know that another like me is drifting there, blind and pale and slow. We might drift near enough that our soft, ectoplasmic bodies brush against one another in the dark—a fleeting contact of cold, wet flesh. We do not react. We do not startle. We simply acknowledge the touch, the proof that we are not the only ones, and then we let the water pull us apart again. It is enough.

I am the memory of motion in a world that has forgotten how to move. I am the witness to the completion. I drift through the galleries, past the columns thick with ancient moss, and I feel the intention of the builders, even if I do not understand it. The structure speaks of purpose, and I answer with presence. I inhabit the architecture the way a spirit inhabits a house—not as an owner, but as a lingering mood.

I am not unhappy. Happiness is a reaction to events, a comparison between what is and what could be. Here, there is no event. There is only *is*. I am cold, but the cold is the temperature of my existence, so it is not cold, it is simply *me*. I am dark, but I have never known light, so the darkness is not an absence, it is a fullness. I am slow, but I have nowhere to go, so the slowness is not a delay, it is a form of rest.

I am the soft thing in the hard place. I am the pause in the breath of the ocean. I am the final inhabitant of the city that became a sea. I am here, drifting in the absolute water, trailing my ghostly ribbon through the dark, touching the walls, waiting for nothing, content with the everything that presses gently against my skin.
