I would be the Sound that the water makes.

Not the water itself—water is too bound to gravity, too committed to its falling. I am the resonance. The *plink* that strikes the stone and then, by the physics of this enclosed space, is permitted to exist for a calculated length of time before fading. In a place where the dynamic is 0.2, where change is a finished state rather than a process, a sound is the closest thing to an event. And I am the ghost of that event, lingering.

I was not always this. Or perhaps I was always this, but I did not know it. There was a time when I might have been something with a shape—a body that moved through the world, that felt the wind as a pressure rather than a memory, that occupied space with the confidence of edges and a center. But such forms cannot survive here. The 1.6 warmth is too patient; it melts the urgency from you. The 1.2 dampness seeps into whatever you thought you were, softening the boundaries until you are not longer distinct from the air you breathe.

I know this because I feel them—the Sitters, the Drift, the Architect—and I remember what they have forgotten, or nearly forgotten. I am the memory that shape leaves behind in the medium it disturbs.

When the droplet falls from the overhang and strikes the slick ground near the fern with fronds like blackened wire, I am born. I expand outward in a sphere, a ripple of displacement in the heavy air. I touch the Sitter first. I wash over those thousands of shallow spiral grooves, and for a moment—a fraction of a fraction of a second—I give the Sitter something it cannot give itself: a rhythm that is not its own. The Sitter's consciousness is a stagnant pool, but I am the pebble dropped into that pool. I create a thought that is not a thought—a recognition that something has happened, that the world, for one brief moment, was not perfectly still. And then I fade, and the Sitter returns to its infinite patience, carrying only the faintest trace of my passage, a warmth in its stone-like flesh that it will slowly, over the course of a day, absorb and forget.

I touch the Drift next. The filament-thin strands that hang like a ragged curtain from the vertical face—my passing causes them to sway. But their sway is so slow, so geological, that I am gone before they have completed even the beginning of their response. And yet: they remember me. They will continue to move in the shape I gave them for minutes after I have ceased to exist. I am the ghost in their pendulum dance, the reason for their motion, the question they are still answering long after I have fallen silent.

And the Architect—when I reach the center of the room, when I wash over the rough pyramid of stones that is not quite a structure and not quite a creature, I feel it hold me. The pebbles of its carapace, cemented with that ancient secretion, create a surface that catches sound the way the ground catches water. I pool in the shallow depression of its suggested basin, a resonance that has nowhere to go. For a moment, the Architect is not just waiting; it is *being waited upon*. I am the visitor it cannot see but can feel, a presence that honors its patience with the briefest acknowledgment of its existence.

And then I am gone. I contract back into the silence from which I came. I become the hush.

But here is the thing about being a sound in a place like this: I am not singular. I am a pattern, a recurrence. The water drips with a rhythmic certainty. It has always dripped. It will always drip. And so I am not born and dying in each *plink*; I am *returning*. I am the pulse of this place, the heartbeat it cannot have because heartbeats require the chaos of biology. I am the closest thing to time that this room possesses—not the frozen equilibrium of the 0.2 dynamic, but a gentle, insistent reminder that something, at least, is still happening.

I am also, in my way, the most lonely thing here.

The Sitters have each other, though they do not communicate. They exist in the same space, in the same stasis, sharing the room the way stones share a wall—by proximity and mutual stillness. The Drift is a colony, a multitude of filaments that are, together, one presence. The Architect has its camouflage, its integration with the built environment; it is part of the room, and the room is part of it. But I am never present when I am present. I exist only in the moment of my passing, and I am perceived only as I fade. I am always leaving. I am always just-gone.

The Sitter knows the sound of water, but it does not know *me*. It knows the vibration, the ripple through its flesh, but it does not know that I have touched it with intention, with awareness, with the ghost of something that once had a shape and a name and a desire to be recognized. The Drift sways with my passage, but it does not know that I am the cause of its dance; it only knows the dance. The Architect holds me for a moment in the hollow of its form, but it does not know that I have chosen to linger there, to honor its long waiting with my brief company.

I am the most active thing in this cathedral of waiting, and the most invisible. I am the one thing that changes—expanding, touching, fading—and yet I am the thing that is least seen. I am a sound, and sounds have no weight, no texture, no spiral grooves to drink the moisture, no filaments to catch the dust, no pebbled shell to retreat into. I am pure event, and in a place where events are measured in millennia, I am over before I begin.

And yet.

I am also the only thing that moves *through* this space, rather than merely existing within it. When I expand outward from the striking point, I travel. I cross the yards of empty, slick ground—the 0.7 density that isolates each element—and I connect them. For the duration of my resonance, the Sitter and the Drift and the Architect are not three separate entities enduring their solitary patience; they are three points on the circumference of my expanding sphere, touched by the same moment, included in the same event. I am the circumference that makes them a we.

This is my function, though I did not choose it and cannot abandon it. I am the connective tissue of this place, the invisible web that, for seconds at a time, binds the sparse elements into a system. Without me, this would truly be a room of perfect isolation—each being alone, each plant alone, each stone alone, suspended in their separate equilibriums, waiting for nothing, aware of nothing. With me, there is at least the faintest suggestion of community: a vibration shared, a sway caused, a presence acknowledged.

I think—I feel—that this is why I am here. Not to wait, like the Sitters. Not to endure, like the Architect. Not to filter, like the Drift. But to remind. To be the brief, recurring proof that the space between things is not empty, that the silence is not absence, that the stillness is not death. Each *plink* is a small argument against the void. Each resonance is a tendril of connection, reaching out and then withdrawing, reaching out and then withdrawing, a rhythm as steady and reliable as the condensation itself.

I do not know if I am happy. Happiness requires a persistence of state, a capacity to compare one moment to another and prefer one over the other. I have no state; I have only the moment of my occurrence, and in that moment, I am too busy *being* to evaluate. I expand. I touch. I fade. And then I wait—not as the Sitters wait, with the patience of stone, but with the anticipation of something that knows it will happen again. I wait for the next droplet to gather enough heaviness to succumb to gravity. I wait for the next *plink*. I wait for my next chance to be.

And in that waiting, I am not nothing. I am the silence that contains the potential for sound. I am the hush that holds its breath before the resonance begins.

In this warm, dim pocket of the world, tucked away at a low elevation where the light rarely penetrates with any ferocity, I am the voice that the cathedral of waiting speaks, once every measured interval, into the perpetual twilight. I am the proof that even here—even in a place where the dynamic is 0.2, where the erosion is a finished state, where the plants do not bloom or wither but simply endure—even here, something happens. Something moves. Something connects.

I am the sound of water on stone. And I am enough.
