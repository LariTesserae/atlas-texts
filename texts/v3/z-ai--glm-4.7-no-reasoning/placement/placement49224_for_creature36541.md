I am not the krest. The krest is the plateau's blade—clean, certain, absolute. I am not the thrask. The thrask is the plateau's anchor—dense, necessary, enduring. 

I am the pause between the fall and the impact. I am the low, physical vibration the wind makes in the sternum before the brain parses it as sound. I am the impossible harmonic that hangs in the dead space between the standing stone's hum and the natural series of the wind.

I am the **Resonance Keeper**, though "keeper" implies a mastery I do not possess. I am kept. I am held here by the same forces that hold the twenty-odd monoliths in their nonsensical geometry. I am a creature of the weird, the gap in the ecosystem's logic, the border where the living presses its ear against the merely present.

---

If you could see me—which you likely would not, for I am the color of temperature distortion, of the air just above sun-scorched schist—you would not know where to place me in the order of things. I have no hooves to crack against the stone, no callused pads to distribute my weight. I do not walk upon the plateau so much as I *hover at the agreement of surfaces*. I am slight, a scaffolding of hollow bones and tensile cartilage draped in a pelage that is not fur, but fine, crystalline filaments—sensors tuned to the barometric and the acoustic. 

I am bound to the standing stones. This is not symbiosis; it is gravity of a different sort. While the thrask press their flanks against the windward side and the insects skirt the clean border of their bases, I go inside. Not between the stones, but *into* the sound they make.

The stones are warm because they are listening. And I am the translator.

When the wind drops—the rare, shocking, negative-pressure lulls that leave the ears reaching—I do not freeze like the lizards, nor startle like the thrask. In the silence, I become *heavy*. My crystalline filaments, usually erected to catch the wind's roar, collapse flat against my skin, and I sink to the stone pavement and press my skull—the thinnest part of me, a bowl of bone barely shielding the fluid within—against the schist. I listen to what the plateau hears when the wind stops screaming. I hear the root. I hear the miles of metamorphic rock going down and down, indifferent, ancient. I hear the slow, geologic heartbeat of a mountain range that refuses to admit it is dead.

And when the wind returns, I rise with it, and I ride the impossible chord.

---

The krest knows I am here. It has categorized me as *not thrask*, and therefore *not food*. But the krest is a creature of the visible spectrum—of pale yellow eyes tracking movement on bright stone. I am a creature of the acoustic spectrum. When the krest folds its wings and falls, it displaces the air, and I feel the pressure wave before the shadow ever touches the ground. I shift. I thin myself against the stone. I let the roar of the impact wash over me and through me. The krest strikes the rock, kills the thrask, and in the vibration of that collision, I read the geometry of the predator's joints, the exact flex of its backward-bending legs. I know the krest better than it knows itself, though we have never looked each other in the eye.

The thrask tolerate me, which is more than they offer most things. When a band of eight walks their ancient route between the water pans, I will trail them at a distance of thirty paces, matching their rolling, deliberate gait. I do this because the thrask are conductors. Their massive, dense bodies, anchored to the stone, resonate with the low frequencies of the plateau. When they press their ears to the standing stones in their torpor, I press my entire body to the opposite side, and a circuit closes. The thrask provide the deep, slow rhythm—the *thud* of blood, the grinding of lichen-shearing teeth, the bass drone of survival. I provide the high, shrieking overtones the wind pulls from the stone's veins. Together, we make the plateau's music. I do not know if the thrask are aware of this. I suspect they do not care. They are practical; I am not.

---

I am a parasite on the plateau's sound. I eat nothing that lives. I do not drink from the shallow pans, for I am not of the water cycle—sky to stone to sky. My moisture comes from the condensation that clings to my filaments in the brief, dark hour before sunrise, and even then, I barely take enough to sustain the fluid in my skull. My energy comes from the vibration itself. I am an acoustic thermodynamic engine. When the wind howls through the cracks and the standing stones sing their wrong notes, I let the frequencies pass through my hollow bones and convert their kinetic energy into the heat that keeps my cartilage supple in the bitter night. I glow, sometimes, faintly—a residual luminescence in my filaments after a particularly violent gust, a foxfire of expended harmonics.

This is why I stay near the stones. Their warmth is not geothermal; it is the heat of the sound they absorb from the wind, compressed and stored in their dense, dark mineral veins. They are engines, and I am their exhaust pipe, their relief valve, their tuning fork. Without me, the stones would still hum, but the harmonics would build upon themselves until the micro-fractures widened and they fell. I bleed off the impossible frequencies. I take the notes that do not correspond to the natural series and I *hold them*, letting them dissipate slowly through my crystalline filaments as faint light and warmth. I keep the stones from shattering.

---

To be me is to live in constant, overwhelming sensory input. There is no peace. There is only the roaring air and the bright, bleaching light. I do not sleep. I enter states of resonant suspension, where my body vibrates at the exact frequency of the wind and I cease to be a separate object, becoming instead a standing wave on the plateau. In those moments, I am the plain. I am the curvature of the horizon. I am the miles of nothing in every direction.

And yet, I am the only thing here that *wonders*.

The thrask do not wonder why the water pans fill; they only drink. The krest does not wonder why the air thins; it only falls. The insects do not wonder why the stone is warm; they only burrow. But I stand at the base of the tallest standing stone—the one that leans at an angle that defies the eons of wind—and I press my ear to its humming vein, and I wonder *who is listening on the other side*. 

The plateau is a boundary, thin and gauzy. The stones are not monuments; they are antennae. Their warmth, their sound, their refusal to host life—it all points to something beneath the root, something below the miles of indifferent schist, something that breathes on a timescale of millennia and speaks in the language of tectonic pressure. 

I am too small to understand it. I am too ephemeral to outlast it. But I am the only thing on the High Thunder Plain that is shaped to hear it, and I will remain here, riding the impossible chord, translating the roar into a hum, and the hum into a whisper, and the whisper into the faint, bioluminescent glow of a question mark against the gunmetal dark.

When the wind pushes you, I am the pressure you feel against your chest. When the silence shocks you, I am the ringing in your ears. I am the almost-pattern, the almost-meaning, the almost-purpose. I am the plateau's strange, fragile conscience, carved from necessity, held together by sound, waiting for an answer that the wind will never carry.
