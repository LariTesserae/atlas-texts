I am the **Curator of the Groove**, though I hold no title and recognize no duty. I am simply the one who walks the lip of the ruin while the others swarm the floor.

I am of the Unglazed, but I am an edge case. In a system defined by total stasis, I am the permissible drift, the slight wobble in the orbit that prevents the mechanism from seizing. I am the hundred-and-first creature in a room that maintains a population of one hundred—allowed to exist because the geometry of the basalt walls requires a surveyor.

**My Form**

I am larger than the grazers who carpet the floor, though I share their terrible dryness. My carapace is not the matte, dusty brown of the swarm, but a deep, cracked umber, scored with lines that map the vibrations I have absorbed over centuries. I am a walking fault line. My trowel-tipped legs are longer, hinged higher, built not for the endless scuttle but for the high step, allowing me to navigate the crude, stacked circles of basalt without disturbing them.

My facial plate—the tragic mask—is wider, flattened like a shovel. I do not graze. I do not shear the succulent leaves of the thermovores. My mandibles are dull, worn down to nubs, useless for harvesting. Instead, I am a cleaner of the architecture. I scrape the encroaching green luminescence from the basalt walls, maintaining the sharp, pinkish-grey scars of the room's birth. I keep the ruin visible.

**My Senses**

My eyes are the same vast, black orbs as the others, but they are calibrated differently. Where the swarm sees the blinding infrared beacons of the heat-plants, I see only the cold. I am obsessed with the absence of warmth. To my vision, the river stones—the impossible artifacts brought by the dead builders—are bottomless wells of solid, magnetic darkness. They pull at me. They are the only silent points in the roaring, loud world.

I do not hear the roar. I am deaf to it, as we all are. But I feel it. The constant thrumming vibrates in my chitin, and over the millennia, I have learned to interpret the subtle shifts in the frequency. I know when a section of the ceiling is about to shed a crystal; I know when a thermal pocket in the wall is pulsing. I am a seismograph wrapped in pottery.

**My Purpose in the Stasis**

The swarm below me maintains the floor. They etch their circular grooves, they prune the vines, they convert heat into the clicking of their legs. But they ignore the ruins. They flow around the basalt walls like water around a stone, treating the architecture as just another obstacle.

I walk *upon* it.

I patrol the low, circular walls, my legs clicking softly as I trace the mortar lines of clay and ash. I check the stability of the structures, nudging a loose stone back into place with my heavy forelimbs. I gather the dried husks that fall from the racks and carry them to the center of the pens, arranging them in neat, meaningless circles. I do not know why I do this. The builders are gone. The livestock is dust. The fires will never be lit. Yet, I maintain the readiness for a habitation that will never occur.

I am the one who moves the river stones.

This is my sacred task, the reason I exist outside the count. The swarm clusters around the cold stones, seeking relief from the uniform heat, but their weight and constant vibration eventually pushes the stones across the floor. If left unattended, the stones would migrate to the walls and be lost in the crevices. The swarm would lose their cold anchors, and the delicate geometry of their clustering would collapse into chaos.

So, when the purple twilight deepens to its nadir, I descend from the ruins. I walk among the swarm—a giant among the specks—and I push the river stones back to the center of the room. I feel the collective sigh of the swarm as the cold wells return to their rightful places. I feel their black eyes watching me, not with gratitude, but with the dull recognition of a system acknowledging a correction.

**My Death**

I will not harden into a statue on the floor. I will not become a husk indistinguishable from the scattered pottery.

When my internal counter reaches its limit—when the dull ache in my mandibles becomes a permanent resonance—I will climb to the highest point of the ruin. I will find a crack in the ceiling where the bruised purple light is brightest. I will wedge my flattened body into that fissure, facing outward.

I will fuse my carapace to the raw, pinkish-grey rock. I will become a keystone. I will seal the wound in the ceiling with my own dried flesh, my trowel-tipped legs locking into the basalt, my vast black eyes staring up into the miles of crystal and magma, seeing nothing but the heat.

I will not fall. I will not be eaten by time. I will simply become a part of the architecture I maintained, a silent guardian holding the roof of the tomb in place, ensuring that the stasis remains unbroken for the swarm below. I will become the final artifact in a room of impossible things.
