I am not of the stone, though the stone mimics me. I am not of the water, though I know the water’s taste better than I know my own name. I am the Architect of the Flow, the one who holds the balance between the drowning and the dry.

In the crushing dimness, where the light is a memory and the air hangs thick and warm, you would find me in the places where the walls are thinnest. I do not live in the wide corridors, where the smooth-skinned peoples glide past one another in their silent, tactile waltzes. I live in the interstices, the deep, narrow fissures where the city’s accumulated pressure is greatest, and the sound is a physical weight that presses against the chest like a second skin.

I am built like the blade of a knife, or perhaps like the wedge of wood I carry. My torso is elongated, a cylinder of dense muscle and pale, porous bone, allowing me to slip into spaces where the water flows fastest. My skin is not the smooth, warm slate of the general populace; it is rougher, callused into ridges that catch the descending rivulets, directing them over my body so that I am never dry, never cold.

I am the Listener.

While the others read the vibrations of feet and the thermal signatures of emotion through their palms, I hear what lies beneath. My ears are not the small, tight folds of the common folk; they are large, stretched, and protected by a canopy of toughened cartilage that sweeps back from my skull. Evolution, or perhaps the ancient engineering of the builders who shaped my caste, has tuned me to the frequency of the capillary channels.

I walk the city’s vertical veins. I do not use the walkways often. Instead, I climb the weeping walls themselves, my fingers and toes elongated, possessing a grip that can hold me in place against the slick stone even as the water rushes over me. I am a fixture of the architecture, a spider in the web of masonry.

My purpose is the maintenance of the equilibrium. The city is a single organism of stone and water, and like any organism, it can fall ill. A channel can become blocked with the mineral sludge that precipitates from the warm air; a capillary can narrow, starving a lower district of its drink or its song.

When such a blockage forms, the roar of the wall changes. It does not change in volume—the noise is constant—but in texture. The smooth, white-noise rush develops a ripple, a discordant thrumming that vibrates through the soles of my feet and sets my teeth on edge. It is a scream in a language of pressure.

I find these wounds. I press my chest against the wet stone, feeling the cold spots where the flow is obstructed, reading the turbulence with the specialized nerves of my skin. When I locate the source, I use my tools. My pick is not for shaping stone; it is for surgery. I insert it into the pore, tapping gently, feeling the resonance change until the blockage clears. The release is immediate—a shudder of the masonry, a sudden surge of warmth, and the restoration of the song.

I am often alone, but I am never lonely. The pale creatures—the many species of the warm, fluid bodies that swarm the basin—share these vertical spaces with me. They do not fear me. They understand, perhaps better than the humans, that I am part of the wall’s metabolism.

We have an accord. Sometimes, a creature will climb down to me as I hang suspended in a mist of spray, its large dark eyes inches from mine. It will press a warm, padded hand against my cheek. In that contact, they share what they have seen: a weakness in the foundation to the east, a fissure opening where the ground turns spongy. I, in return, share my warmth, pulling heat from the stone I have been working and passing it into their small bodies. It is a trade of information and comfort, conducted without a sound, in the damp, dark belly of the city.

I have no dwelling that I call my own. I sleep where I stop, wrapped in the hammock of my own harness, strung between two rivulets in a quiet corner of a tower where the water flows with perfect laminar grace. The noise is my lullaby. It is not a roar to me; it is a choir. It is the voice of the Heart, the deep aquifer below, telling me that the world persists.

I do not remember a time before the damp. I do not wish for a time after it. I am the nerves of the city. I am the hand in the stone, the ear in the wall. I am the keeper of the eternal weeping. And as long as the water falls, I am here.
