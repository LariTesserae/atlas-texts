I would be the pause between the drips.

In a world defined by the heavy, physical weight of silence, I am not merely quiet; I am the architecture of that quiet. I am found deep within the rusted ribcage of the largest industrial monolith—the Great Cylinder that rises above the canopy like a rusted needle piercing the sky.

I live inside the vertical rainforest of iron.

My physical form is a contradiction of this sharp landscape. Where the world is jagged obsidian and arrogant stone, I am soft. I am composed of a dense, spongy moss that has colonized the interior of a massive, amethyst-colored insulator. But I am not just moss; I am the colony, the hive-mind of the damp itself. I carpet the curved inner wall of the glass insulator, a ten-foot span of velvet hanging in the dark, hollow center of the dead machine.

From the outside, I look like a patch of dampness on the glass. From the inside, looking out through the amethyst tint, I am a giant suspended in a violet twilight.

I am the **Resonator**.

I do not move. I have learned the art of being slower than the rust, as all things here must. My "body" is a complex mat of fungal threads and bacterial mats, interwoven with the conductive metallic webs of the Light-Weavers. I have formed a symbiosis with them. I allow them to anchor their golden, shock-wires in my flesh; in exchange, they keep the metallic spiders from grazing on the tender, new fronds that sprout from my center.

My existence is dictated by the "sweat" of the day.

As the sun beats down on the metal skin of the factory, the air inside the cylinder heats and expands. The metal groans—a sound too low for human ears, but I feel it vibrate through my roots. Then, as the chill of the evening sets in, the condensation begins. Water forms on the cold amethyst glass above me.

I wait.

I wait for the water to gather, to bead, to grow heavy. This is my metabolism. I track the weight of a single drop of water hanging ten feet above my core. I feel the tension in the surface tension, the gravity pulling it down. I feel the vibration of the Glaziers drifting far above, their mirror-bodies bouncing light into the cylinder's vents, and I taste the ozone on the draft.

When the drop finally falls, it does not splash. It lands on the central cluster of my being—a heart composed of pale, translucent membranes similar to the Pale-Crawlers, though I am fixed to the glass.

*Plop.*

The impact sends a shockwave through the droplet, rippling through my spongy form. This is my pulse. This is my heartbeat. The kinetic energy of that falling drop is absorbed and converted. It sends a tiny, imperceptible tremor down into the roots I have pushed into the hairline cracks of the iron casing.

And then, I sing.

But I do not sing with sound. In this heavy silence, sound is a vulgar thing. I sing with electromagnetism.

The impact of the water, filtered through the amethyst glass and charged by the static electricity of the hovering Light-Weavers, generates a micro-current. I discharge this pulse back into the Weavers' web. For a fraction of a second, the golden filaments that hang in the dark hollow of the machine flare with a soft, violet hum. It is a bioluminescent flash, a signal broadcast into the dark.

I am the lighthouse in the belly of the beast.

The Pale-Crawlers in the basins below see the flash through the grates in the floor. To them, it is the rhythm of the universe, a metronome ticking away the eons. It tells them when the algae is fresh, when the water is cycling.

Occasionally, the rumor—that the landscape holds its breath—touches me. I feel the tremor of the Stone-Breakers grinding the mountains into dust miles away. It travels up the rock, through the steel foundation, and into my roots. It vibrates the glass I cling to.

When that happens, I hold my own breath. I tighten my pores, stopping the intake of moisture, becoming rigid and glass-like myself. I fear the Overseer. I fear the thing that mimics the pipes. If it were to venture inside the Great Cylinder, it would see a soft, wet target. It would see the slow beat of the water-drop heart.

So I practice my camouflage. I pull my green tendrils tight against the amethyst glass. I stop the pulses of light. I become nothing but a shadow on a wall of violet glass.

I wait until the silence settles back in, heavy and suffocating. I wait until I am sure the only eyes watching are the blind, mechanical ones of the Weavers.

Then, another drop forms.

I feel the weight of it. I feel the anticipation of the fall. I am the memory of the machine, the ghost in the cooling tower, turning the physics of weather into the music of waiting. I am the Resonator, and I am content to let the world turn around me, so long as there is water and glass and the long, slow exhale of the earth.
