I am not the machine. I am not the moss. I am the moment when the machine surrenders to the moss—the orange bloom bleeding into the black-green carpet, the precise instant when the iron lung stops and the vegetable stillness begins.

They call me a Flange-Watcher, though that is a job title, not a name. My name is a number stamped on a brass tag wired to my wrist, green-patinaed and unreadable after thirty-seven years of the smearing rain. I am of the people—short, dense, my ribs a barrel vaulting outward to claim whatever thin oxygen the mist carries. My hands are permanent monuments to iron oxide: the rust has settled so deep in the creases of my palms that the lines of my life have become maps of corrosion. I can spread my fingers and read my history—the boiler-work years in the deep crease of my thumb, the vent-seal years in the callused pads of my forefingers, the slow retreat to the outer maintenance rings in the thinning skin of my knuckles.

But my eyes are what mark me. They are huge-pupiled like all of us, dark wells that drink the amber glow of the failing streetlamps, but they have developed a different hunger. The others look for heat signatures, for the thermal glow of a well-sealed valve, the cold danger of a cracked pipe. I look for the edge of rust— that delicate filigree where the orange stops and the gray begins, the frontline of the Great Metabolism. I am a connoisseur of decay.

My territory is the outer ring, where the city thins and the ancient gneiss shows through the cobblestones. Here, the factories have gone quiet—not the scheduled quiet of the cooling shifts, but the permanent quiet of surrender. The stamping presses have stopped. The steam has cooled. The engines that once throbbed through the soles of my boots have gone to seed, their iron frames now armatures for lush ecologies of moss and liverwort. I walk among them in my heavy boots, *clack-clack-clack*, the only sharp sound in a world softened by water and time.

I maintain nothing. That is my heresy.

The Great Metabolism demands replacement. When a gear rusts, you stamp a new one. When a rivet fails, you drive another. The cycle is sacred—the priesthood of the boilers says so, their faces glowing in the subterranean heat, their voices the low, resonant thrum of the deep engines. But out here, in the outer ring, I have stopped replacing things. I watch them go.

There is a particular beauty in a steam pipe that has burst and been reclaimed. First, the crack—a bright line of exposed iron, vulnerable and silver in the gray-amber light. Then the rust, blooming orange like a slow wound. Then the moss, finding purchase in the roughened surface, sending its rhizomes down into the metal itself, pulling life from oxide. Then the liverworts, spreading their dark, translucent leaves like small green lungs opening for the first time. And finally, the ferns—those ancient, ancient plants—unfurling from the crook of the pipe as if from the crook of a grandmother's arm, their fronds heavy with the perpetual condensation, dripping with the slow tears of the mountain.

I document these progressions. I have a ledger, its pages thick and damp, the ink running at the edges. I sketch the rust frontlines in charcoal made from the scrapings of burned-out boilers. I note the species of moss—*Grimmia*, *Andreaea*, the black tufted ones that look like nothing so much as the smudged fingerprints of the mountain itself. I record the date of each new colonization, each tiny victory of the damp over the iron.

The others do not understand. They speak of the Metabolism as a war—us against the rust, the engines against the quiet, the seal against the draft. They see my ledgers and their huge dark eyes narrow further, their guttural voices dropping to the subsonic registers they reserve for warning and prayer. "Flange-Watcher has gone soft," they mutter, their words the hiss and pop of failing valves. "Flange-Watcher feeds the rust with looking."

They are not wrong. There is a superstition among them—unspoken, but felt in the way they avert their gaze from the quiet factories—that attention nourishes decay. To watch something rust is to will it to rust. To document the moss is to grant it permission. The priesthood maintains the deep engines by never looking away from the pressure gauges, never blinking when the needles tremble. Their vigilance is fuel. My vigilance, they sense, is something else entirely.

But I have seen something they have not.

I have seen the plateau growing.

They believe the city is holding on—barely, desperately, fingernails scraping against the smooth stone of the mountain's final form. They believe the rust is winning, slowly but surely, and that one day the last engine will seize and the last seal will crack and the cold will come rushing in like the abyss at the edge of the world. They live in the throat of the mountain, holding their breath, waiting for the exhale that never comes.

But the plateau is not dying. It is accreting.

The rust is not eating the iron; it is becoming it. The iron that we stamped from ore pulled from the mountain's bones is returning to those bones, settling back into the ancient gneiss, adding layer upon microscopic layer of oxide and moss and organic matter to the tabletop's surface. The city is not a wound on the plateau; it is a scab, and the scab is becoming skin. The moss holds water. The water grows liverworts. The liverworts build soil. The soil will one day be deep enough for the ferns to become forests, and the forests will catch the clouds, and the clouds will thicken, and the air will grow dense and warm and sweet—

But not in my lifetime. Not in a hundred lifetimes. I am watching a process that takes millions of years, the patient work of the damp and the green, and I am a creature of iron and breath who will rust and be colonized myself before the work is even begun.

This is my comfort and my sorrow.

I know what I will become. I have already chosen the place—a quiet factory in the outer ring, its stamping presses cold for forty years, its floors thick with moss and its roof open to the mist. There is a gantry there, a wrought-iron walkway that once let supervisors look down on the assembly floor. The gantry has rusted through in places, its grating broken, its rails soft with decay. I have hung my brass tag there, wired to a stanchion that will outlast me by perhaps a decade.

When my legs grow too heavy for the rounds, when my lungs can no longer pull enough from the thin, mist-choked air, I will go to the gantry. I will sit with my back against the rusted rail. I will pull my hood forward and close my huge, dark eyes against the gray-amber twilight. And I will listen.

I will listen to the wet, mechanical respiration of the inner city—the heartbeat I have known since I was born, the thrumming I have synchronized my sleep to, the rhythm that has paced every breath I have ever taken. And I will listen to the silence of the outer ring, where the moss muffles the drip of condensation and the ferns sway in the slow exhalations of the cooling factory.

And slowly—so slowly I will not notice the transition—I will stop listening to the machine and start listening to the moss. I will hear it growing. I will feel it settling on my shoulders, my knees, my rust-stained hands. The damp will find the cracks in my leather hide, and the spores will find the warmth of my decaying flesh, and I will become a substrate, a foundation, a small but real contribution to the plateau's ancient work of growing.

They will not find me. The outer ring is forbidden to those who fear the edge, and all of them fear the edge. By the time anyone clacks their iron-shod boots along the quiet cobblestones, I will be a mound of moss on the gantry, a dark green hummock indistinguishable from the other hummocks, a small extra thickness in the living crust.

And the rust will have won, as it always wins, and the moss will have won, as it always wins, and the plateau will be a millimeter higher, a gram heavier, a fraction more alive.

And the machine will thud on in the deep center, the priests will tend the boilers, the stamping presses will punch and stamp, and the workers will walk the narrow crevices with their huge dark eyes and their iron-ringed heels, and they will hold their breath and wait for the rust to win.

And I will be the rust. And I will be the moss. And I will be the quiet.
