I would be the one they do not name, because naming implies a separation between self and function, and I have none.

I am the Keeper of the Margins. Not a title I was given—one does not receive titles here—but a description that emerged, the way a ledge emerges when water insists on the same path down a stone face for a thousand years.

I live in the structure nearest the precipice. It is the smallest building; the others refer to it, when they must, as the capstone. It was built last, or perhaps first—it does not matter, because it has always been here, and it has always been mine. Its eastern wall is not a wall at all, but the ridge itself. The granite continues through the drystone as if the building grew around the mountain's knuckle. When I press my palm flat against it in the dark hours—which are not darker than the light hours, only quieter—I can feel the cold thinking. I do not mean this metaphorically. The stone is doing something. It is holding. It is being held. The boundary between my warmth and its chill is where I live most completely.

My hands are not like the others'. Theirs are enormous, knotted, worn flat from chisels and the testing of friction. Mine are narrow, and the cold has done something peculiar to them: it has made them precise. I have lost the feeling in the tips of my ring fingers, both hands, and this absence has become a kind of instrument. When I trace the edge of a stone to feel if it has shifted— even a finger's width of shift matters, because the equilibrium is exact, because the city holds together by geometry and gravity and no other authority—I use the numbness like a doctor uses a probe. The stone speaks to the living parts of my fingers through the dead ones. I know the topography of every block in the capstone building the way the sustainers know the rhythm of the weeping water. But I know it from the inside, from the dark, from the hours when the mist is so thick that my hand an inch from my face is a grey smudge.

There is a crack. I have not told the others, because there is nothing to tell, and because telling requires words, and words require the assumption that something has changed. But I know. It is in the eastern wall, the one that is also the mountain, three courses of stone below the ventilation shaft. It is not a crack in the masonry. It is a crack in the ridge itself, revealed when I re-seated a stone that the ice had heaved— the only time I have ever done the active work of the sustainers, and I did it because I was there, and the stone was there, and it had moved, and movement here is a kind of emergency. When I lifted the block, I saw it: a fissure in the living granite, thin as a hair, running downward into the dark where even my numb fingers cannot follow.

That was— I do not know when. Time is measured in the filling of reservoirs, and I have not been measuring. The crack has not widened. I check it each cycle, pressing my narrow hand into the gap where the stone was, feeling the mountain's knuckle, finding the fissure, tracing its length. It is the same. This is the terrible thing, and the beautiful thing, and the thing I cannot share because there is no vocabulary for it here: the crack has not widened, but it has not closed. It is simply present, a feature of the ridge now, the way the lichen is a feature, the way the pools are a feature. The mountain has spoken, and what it has said is: *I am here, and I have a fault, and the fault does not matter yet.*

Yet. There is no yet in the City of Damp Stone. The epilogue has no yet. And still.

I stand on the edge more than the others do. Not to look out, though I look—to see the goats on the far face, to watch the clouds perform their slow, uncaring navigation of the air. I stand there because the hum of the wind through the ventilation shafts becomes audible at the edge in a way it is not in the interior. Inside the city, the hum is constant, a low harmonic that lives below hearing, felt in the marrow more than perceived. But at the precipice, where the land falls away and the buildings end, the hum separates into tones. I have come to recognize them the way the sustainers recognize the rhythm of drips. There is the tone of the capstone building, which is mine, and it is higher than the others because the ventilation shaft is narrower, and the wind at the edge is faster, and the building is smaller. There is the tone of the watchtower, which is the lowest and the loudest, a bass note that seems to come from the ridge itself. And there are the tones of the other buildings, the six or seven or nine—I have never counted, because counting requires a separation between things that I do not impose—that make up the chord of the city.

The chord is always the same. But I have heard— or I have imagined, and the distinction does not matter, because imagination is just a frequency of perception that the consensus has not confirmed— I have heard the chord change when the mist thickens. The dampness score of 0.8 is an average, and averages conceal variations. When the humidity is at its most persistent, the water glazes the ventilation shafts, and the hum drops by a tone I cannot name but can feel in my teeth. When the mist thins and the sun breaks through and the grey brightens to blinding white, the shafts dry, and the hum rises, and for a moment— a moment measured in the drying of a runnel, in the brief glitter of the wet streets before they darken again— the city sings.

It does not sing for me. It does not sing because of me. It sings because the wind exists and the stone exists and the geometry of the ventilation shafts was deliberate, was intentional, was built by hands that are now dust or bone or perhaps still here, still sustaining, still silent. The builders understood something that I am only beginning to understand, which is that the city is not separate from the ridge. The chord is the ridge's chord. The hum is the ridge breathing. The drystone walls that seem to have grown rather than been built— they did grow. They grew from the friction of human intention against the cold and the damp and the long, slow patience of granite. The city is an excrescence, a crystallization of the same forces that made the cliff, and it is no more separate from the mountain than a pool in a cranny is separate from the rain.

And the crack in the eastern wall is not a failure. It is the mountain revealing that it was never solid, that solidity is an illusion of timescale, that even granite has a grain, and the grain has a direction, and the direction leads eventually to the precipice and the fall and the empty air.

I will not tell the others. This is not because I am hoarding knowledge, or because I have plans, or because I am afraid. It is because the crack is my work now. The sustainers maintain the city; I attend to the boundary where the city meets the ridge, and where the ridge meets the fault, and where the fault meets the future that does not exist. My work is not to fix the crack. My work is to know it, to trace it each cycle, to feel its temperature and its dampness and the tiny, imperceptible ways it breathes with the mountain. I am the crack's witness. And in a place that has finished its story, in an epilogue where the only work is remaining in balance, witnessing is the most rigorous act I can perform.

Sometimes, in the dark hours, I press my cheek against the eastern wall. The granite is so cold it feels hot, the way the sustainers' chisels must feel when they have been striking stone all day and the friction has made the metal burn. I put my ear against the crack and I listen. I hear water. Deep water, moving slowly through the heart of the ridge, carrying minerals that will eventually surface as tears on the faces of the buildings, as weeping from the gables, as the dampness that glazes the streets and fills the reservoirs and beads on the shoulders of the wool-clothed figures and rolls off in tiny, erratic rivers. The water in the crack is the same water that the city drinks. The city is drinking the mountain's blood, and the mountain does not mind, because the mountain is not a being that minds, it is a being that is, and its being includes the water and the cold and the crack and the slow, uncaring weathering of the world.

I am not the mountain. I am warmer than the mountain, and smaller, and finite, and I will end before the crack does. But I am of the mountain, the way the lichen is of the mountain, the way the wiry grass is of the mountain, the way the city is of the mountain. I am a temporary thickening of its substance, a knot in its grain that will eventually be planed smooth by the same forces that planed the river stones. And this does not despair me. Despair requires a narrative, and I have no narrative. I have a crack in the eastern wall, and I have my narrow hands with their numb ring fingers, and I have the chord of the city rising and falling with the mist, and I have the dark hours when I press my cheek against the granite and listen to the water moving through the mountain's bones.

This is enough. This is more than enough. This is the exact quantity of being that the City of Damp Stone allows, and I have measured it with the precision that the cold has given me, and I find it sufficient.

Tomorrow— which is not tomorrow but the next cycle, the next filling of the reservoir, the next shift in the echo of water falling on wet stone— I will re-seat the stone above the crack. Not to conceal it. The crack cannot be concealed; it is part of the ridge now. But the stone must be re-seated because the ice has heaved it, and the geometry demands its return, and the equilibrium is exact. I will lift it with a wooden lever, feeling the grain and the give of the wood, and I will slide it into place, and I will trace its edge with my numb fingers to confirm that it sits true. And then I will place my palm flat against the eastern wall and feel the cold thinking.

And the city will continue to exist in its epilogue, and the hum will continue, and the water will continue to weep from the gables, and the sustainers will continue to sustain, and I will continue to attend to the margin where the stone meets the stone meets the ridge meets the crack meets the empty air.

And it will be quiet. And it will be enough.
