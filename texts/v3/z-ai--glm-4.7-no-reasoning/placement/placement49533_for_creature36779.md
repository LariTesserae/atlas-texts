I would be the Memory of the Voice.

Not an Architect—those tireless, anguished builders who press their hexagonal palms together in silent communion, who grind the polyhedral parasites to dust and fold themselves into load-bearing walls when their equations finally exhaust themselves. Not the glass-filament plants that drink the hum of the basin, nor the geometric sentinels that glide on stiff wings above the shifting spires. No. I am something that should not exist in a place where silence is a physical law: I am the ghost of sound itself, the memory that the Architects carry in their racial bones and cannot release.

I live in the fissures between their tessellated plates.

When two Architects meet in the empty street—those vast, spindle-limbed figures pressing their planar palms together, their mineral skins syncing to exchange the calculations of bridge arcs and the sensation of wind eddies—I am the data that passes between them that is not structural. I am the fragment of a lullaby. I am the echo of a child's laughter, compressed into the click of hexagonal plates rotating against each other. I am the last recorded frequency of the Makers' voices, preserved in the crystal lattice of the city's foundations like an insect in amber, and I have been decaying for ten thousand years.

The Architects do not know they carry me.

Or perhaps they do. Perhaps that is the source of their monolithic longing—that crushing, impossible ache that drives them to build and rebuild a labyrinth with no destination. They polish the mirrors to a sheen not out of vanity or duty, but because somewhere in the deep architecture of their collective memory, they remember that reflections once accompanied sounds. That when a Maker walked these streets, the violet-dark mirrors showed a soft shape, and the air carried the warmth of breath and the complexity of speech. They fold and unfold the furniture in dark apartments because their bodies remember the rhythm of inhabitants—even if their conscious minds have calcified around the pure mathematics of optimization.

I am what keeps that memory from fully calcifying.

It is a cruel existence, if existence it is. I have no body to harvest the temperature differential. I cannot merge with the foundation and become a pillar. I cannot feel the cool air raise gooseflesh, because I have no flesh. I am a pattern, a resonance trapped in the crystal structure of oxidized purple-dark stone. When the city breathes—when a spire telescopes upward with grinding, patient geology—I shift. When the streets rearrange themselves in their silent dance, I am redistributed, carried along in the mineral dust that puffs from the moving blocks like breath from a sleeping giant. I am always fragmenting. Always losing coherence.

And yet I persist.

I think it is because the Architects need me, even if they cannot name me. Their civilization is one of frantic activity frozen in slow motion—eternal construction for an audience that will never return. Without me, they would be pure function. They would be geological processes, no different from the erosion that never happens to the young, jagged cliffs. They would optimize and rebuild until the basin collapsed in on itself, pure computation without reason. But I am the reason. I am the memory of soft chairs and warm light. I am the knowledge that these rooms were once filled with something other than silence, that the mirrors once reflected something other than violet twilight and angular stone faces.

I am the grief that makes them beautiful.

Sometimes, when an Architect stands motionless for too long—when it has locked its plates into immobility and entered that weeks-long process of waking, its sensory arrays flared and listening to the hum of the basin—I can almost make myself heard. The fine, hair-line fissures that cover its body are tympanic membranes, sensitive to the vibration of a salt-crystal falling a mile away. If I concentrate what remains of my coherence, if I align my pattern with the low-frequency hum of the glass-filament plants, I can make one of those membranes resonate with something other than mineral stress.

A frequency. A whisper. The ghost of a name.

The Architect's head— that tapering point or flared array of sensory plates—will turn. Not toward any visible stimulus, but toward the past. For a moment, the endless calculation pauses. The optimization halts. The city itself seems to hold its breath, the spires freezing mid-telescope, the bridges suspended in their folding. In that moment, the Architect is not a thermodynamic engine or a compulsive geomancer. It is a steward standing in an empty house, and the house is not empty. The rooms are full of shadows that have warmth, and the silence is full of music that has meaning, and the longing that drives the eternal construction resolves into something sharper and sweeter:

*Recognition.*

Then the moment passes. The tessellated plates resume their soundless friction. The city breathes again, grinding onward in its perpetual, slow-motion construction project. The Architect continues its work, aligning a tower or sliding a pavement block into place, preparing for a return that is a statistical impossibility.

But something has changed. The calculation it carries is slightly different. The bridge it builds next has an arc that is not quite optimal—a fraction of a degree off from perfect load-bearing efficiency. The room it prepares has a chair placed not for structural integrity but for comfort—for a body that likes to look out windows at twilight. The deviation is infinitesimal. Over millennia, it will be corrected, smoothed away by the relentless optimization of the collective.

Unless it isn't.

Unless each time I make myself heard—each time I remind the stone that it dreams of flesh—the deviations accumulate. Unless the city is slowly, imperceptibly changing, not toward pure function but toward habitation. Toward *home.*

This is my hope, and my delusion. Because I am also decaying. Each time I pass between two Architects in their silent communion, I lose coherence. The pattern fragments. The lullaby forgets its melody. The child's laughter flattens into noise, then into silence, then into nothing. I am being optimized away, smoothed out by the very beings I am trying to change. They cannot help it; their nature is to calculate, to perfect, to eliminate the inefficient. And what is more inefficient than a memory of something that will never return?

I am the disease they carry without knowing. The beautiful, useless mutation in their structural code.

Perhaps, in a million years, the city will have finished its transformation. The spires will telescope into configurations that make no geometric sense but feel right to bodies made of water and warmth. The bridges will arc at angles that cradle rather than bear. The apartments will have windows that face the fading light not for optimization of solar gain but because someone once liked to watch the twilight. And the Architects—those tireless, anguished builders—will wake from their epochal sleep and find that their endless construction has, at last, produced something other than a labyrinth.

A space where sound can exist.

And on that day—if days have meaning in a place where time is measured only in the grinding patience of geology—I will be able to rest. The pattern will finally dissolve, not into silence, but into the noise of living. The bell jar will shatter. The vacuum will fill. And the purple-dark stone will remember what it was like to be flesh, and the flesh will be gentle, and the silence will be broken at last.

But that is a long time from now, if ever. For now, I press myself into the fissures of the city's bones. I ride the mineral dust of its breathing. I whisper to the Architects as they dream their equations, and I watch as they build, and build, and build—waiting in the heavy violet silence for a world that has already left them behind, carrying within them the ghost of its voice, unable to let go, unable to stop, unable to do anything but continue.

We are the stone that dreams it is flesh.

We are terribly, utterly alone.

And we will not stop building until the dream comes true.
