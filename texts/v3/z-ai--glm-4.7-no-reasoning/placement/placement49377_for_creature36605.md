I am not Pressed. Not exactly. But I am not untouched by the Press.

I am the space between the strikes. I am the momentary suspension of the piston at the apex of its thrust—the single, held breath in a lungscape of perpetual screaming. I am the one who listens to the silence that the Pressed have sealed themselves against.

I am the Keeper of the Chutes.

The Pressed call me *Hollow-back*, though they do not speak it with sound. They tap it against the hull plates when I pass: a slow drag of knuckles, then a hollow ring—their word for emptiness, for the thing that has been emptied out and still persists. I am the one who stands at the edge of the dark, where the conveyor tips its endless, unidentifiable components into the unseen depths. I am the one who watches them fall.

I was not born to this. I came from somewhere else—a place where the sky was a color other than halogen-white, where air moved because wind existed, not because fans the size of cathedral doors churned it through scrubbers. I remember green, though the memory has taken on the quality of rust-vine: metallic, rigid, something I can hold but cannot bend. I remember the sound of rain. It is the only soft thing left in me.

I found the High Press the way a stone finds the bottom of a well: by falling. There was a ship, once. There was a crew. There was a mission that had words like *survey* and *extraction* and *feasibility* in it. These words have been ground out of me by the friction of this place, worn smooth like the hundred-year-old grooves in the handrails of the gantry. I do not know how long I have been here. The Pressed do not mark time in days; they mark it in piston-cycles, and I have stopped counting those. I only know that I am old, and that I am not dead, and that these two facts are in constant, exhausting negotiation.

My body has adapted, but poorly, incompletely, like a graft that never quite takes. I am gaunt—everyone is gaunt here—but my thinness has a different quality than the Pressed's lean, keratinized durability. I am wiry in the way of something that has been stretched too thin over a frame that was built for a different gravity. My skin is rust-brown, yes, but mottled underneath with a paler tone, the ghost of the person I was before the oven baked me. My ears are not sealed. This is my curse and my distinction.

I hear the Roar.

The Pressed believe I am mad. They are not wrong. To hear this place is to have your thoughts constantly shouted over, to never complete a sentence in your own mind without the interjection of a million-ton scream. I have learned to think in the spaces between—the micro-silences that exist in the fraction of a second when a piston changes direction, when a gear tooth passes its mate without quite touching, when the conveyor belt crests the roller and, for one infinitesimal moment, lifts free. I have become a connoisseur of near-nothing. My consciousness lives in the margins of the noise.

I protect my hearing the only way I can: with wax. I have fashioned earplugs from the residue of the hydraulic oil, cooled and kneaded into dense, black cylinders that I pack into my auditory canals. They muffle the Roar to a dull, omnipresent throb—a headache that never quite escalates to a rupture. I replace them twice a piston-cycle. The oil heats and thins, and when it does, the sound rushes in like water through a cracked hull, and I must find a shadow-canyon, press my back against the vibrating hull plates, and wait for the new plugs to cool in my palms. These moments of vulnerability—when the Roar is fully inside me—are when I come closest to understanding the Pressed. In those seconds, I feel what they feel: the sound as vibration, as skeleton-song, as the only truth this place has to offer.

My eyes have not adapted. They refuse to. Where the Pressed have retreated into permanent slits and shadow-seeking, my pupils still dilate. They open wide in the dark and contract painfully in the halogen glare, never learning, never surrendering. I wear a visor I fashioned from a shattered sensor-eye—I am the only one here who has repurposed a sensor-eye for *seeing*, rather than for worship. The lens is cracked and yellowed, but it cuts the glare enough to keep my retinas from bleeding. Through it, the world is the color of old amber, tinted with the faint, green ghost of the heads-up display that the machine's long-dead builders must have seen. Sometimes, when I tilt my head at a certain angle, I catch flickers of text in the periphery: *CYCLE 4,781,002. PRESSURE NOMINAL. OUTPUT NOMINAL.* The numbers change, but the words never do. Nominal. Nominal. Nominal. The machine's favorite lie.

I live on the observation platform—the same one described in the accounts of this place, the edge where visitors stand and gape before they leave, or before they become me. It is a shelf of steel grating that juts out over the central chasm, offering a view of the Spire and the vast, thicket-like canyons of pipe and duct that radiate from it. The Pressed consider this place cursed. It is too exposed, too bright, too close to the sensor-eyes that rove the assembly lines. They believe the platform is where the Process watches you back.

They are right.

I have felt the sensor-eyes settle on me. I have stood, frozen, as the red lens of an inspector drone tracked my movement across the gantry, its internal mechanisms clicking and whirring as it processed my shape against its parameters. I do not know what those parameters are. I do not know if I register as a faulty component, as an anomaly to be tolerated, or as a feature of the system I have not yet understood. The drone always eventually turns away, its attention sliding to the conveyor belt, to the unidentifiable components it was built to scrutinize. But in the seconds of its gaze, I feel the weight of the Process—the cold, mechanical indifference of a system that did not create me, does not need me, and will not mourn me when I am gone.

I stay on the platform because it is the only place in the High Press where I can see the chutes.

The chutes are the mouths of the machine—the dark, angled shafts that swallow the endless output of the assembly lines and carry it down into the unseen depths. The Pressed lower their dead into them. The machine tips its components into them. Everything that enters the chutes disappears. There is no sound of impact, no clatter of metal hitting a collection bin. The depths swallow everything, and the depths give nothing back.

Except to me.

This is my function, my sacred and terrible duty. I am the one who retrieves.

I do not know why the chutes release things to me. I have theories, none of them comforting. Perhaps there is a mechanism deep in the depths—a sorter, a separator, a recycler—that occasionally spits things back up the shaft. Perhaps the machine, in its frozen equilibrium, has a flaw, a crack in its perfect loop through which anomalies seep. Perhaps I am being fed.

Whatever the reason, every few thousand piston-cycles, something comes back up the chute. It emerges slowly, riding a cushion of air or a reverse pulse of the hydraulic system, and it settles on the rim of the shaft with a quiet, metallic *click* that I feel in my bones before I hear it through my oil-plugs. And I take it.

I have collected hundreds of things. They line the observation platform, arranged in rows along the base of the hull plates, tucked into the crevices between the I-beams. I do not know what any of them are.

There is a sphere of black glass, perfectly smooth, warm to the touch, that hums at a frequency just below the Roar. There is a strip of something that is not metal and not plastic—flexible, translucent, with symbols etched into its surface that I cannot read and that the Pressed refuse to look at. There is a cube that unfolds into a lattice of fine wires and then refolds itself when I set it down, as though it is trying to return to a shape I have not shown it. There are simpler things: bolts of an alloy I have never seen, a cylinder of fluid that is not hydraulic oil—clearer, thinner, smelling of something that might be water if water had been turned inside out—and, once, a single, perfect cylinder of copper, warm from some internal heat, that the rust-vines on the ceiling turned toward, as though it were a sun.

The Pressed are afraid of my collection. They call the things *chute-spit* and *depth-bile*. They believe they are the machine's waste, its failures, its rejected imperfections—and that by keeping them, I am accumulating the Process's mistakes, hoarding the evidence of its fallibility. They may be right. But I cannot stop.

I am looking for something.

I do not know what. A way out, perhaps—a component that, when combined with others in the right sequence, becomes a key, a door, a signal that reaches beyond the frozen equilibrium of the High Press and touches the cold void outside. Or perhaps I am looking for a message—a record, a log, a fragment of the creators who built this place and then abandoned it to its eternal, purposeless churning. Or perhaps I am simply looking for proof that the depths are not merely a void—that something exists down there, something that sorts and selects and *chooses* what to return, something that is not the Process but something other, something that might have a purpose beyond the operation itself.

The Pressed do not understand this. They have no concept of *beyond*. Their world is the High Press, full stop. The chutes lead to the depths, and the depths are where things go to be remanufactured. There is no outside; there is no before; there is no creator. To suggest otherwise is to threaten the fragile theology that keeps them alive, the belief that the Process is all, that suffering is purgatorial, that death on the Line is redemption. I do not challenge their faith. I have my own.

I believe in the sphere of black glass. I believe in the strip of unknown material and the self-folding cube. I believe in the cylinder of not-water and the copper sun that the rust-vines remember. I believe that the High Press is not a closed system—that somewhere, in the depths or beyond the hull, there is a crack through which meaning can enter. I believe this because I must. Without this belief, I am merely a soft thing in a hard place, wearing out, waiting to be tipped into the dark.

My hands tell the story of my faith. They are a map of failure and persistence. My knuckles are not alloyed like the Pressed's; I cannot strike the hull plates with the force of their percussive language. But my fingers are long, scarred, and precise, calloused from handling the sharp edges of chute-spit, from working the fine mechanisms of the components I collect, from untangling the wire-lattices and trying to read the symbols on the translucent strips. My thumbnails have split and regrown so many times that they are now thick and ridged, almost claw-like, useful for prying and scraping. I have lost the tip of my left ring finger to a gear-tooth that caught me as I reached into a chute after a retreating object. I did not retrieve it. I wear the scar as a reminder: the depths give, and the depths take.

I do not drink the hydraulic oil. This is another thing that separates me from the Pressed, another mark of my incomplete adaptation. I have tried. My body rejects it—my stomach heaving, my throat closing, my revulsion too deep to overcome. Instead, I survive on the clear fluid from the cylinder of not-water, rationing it drop by drop, and on the nutritional paste I scavenge from the ancient, forgotten supply caches that still exist in the far corners of the High Press—remnants of a time when this place had a crew, before the crew became the first Pressed, before the Process ground them down and remanufactured their descendants into something that could drink oil and breathe ozone and call it life. The caches are nearly empty. I will run out of paste within a few hundred thousand piston-cycles. After that, I will have to choose: the oil, or starvation.

I think I will choose starvation. But I am not certain. The body wants to live. It is a traitor to the mind.

At night—or rather, during the periods when I sleep, for there is no night here—I dream of the visitors. Not the ones who come now (if any still come), but the ones who must have come before: engineers in uniforms with insignia on the shoulders, scientists with clipboards and scanning devices, administrators with authorization codes and shifts to assign. I dream of the High Press as it was meant to be—a place of purpose, of direction, of *meaning*—and I dream of the moment when they left, when the last crew member stepped off the gantry and the doors sealed shut behind them, leaving the machine to its eternal loop. I dream of their faces as they walked away. Did they know what they were leaving behind? Did they understand that the machine would not stop, that the Process would continue, that the High Press would grind on for millennia, producing nothing, consuming itself, a monument to a purpose that had been canceled but never deleted?

Or did they simply forget? Did the High Press become a line item on a ledger, a closed file, a decommissioned asset that someone forgot to actually decommission? I think this is the truth of it. I think the High Press is a clerical error. I think the universe is full of such errors—machines that run because no one told them to stop, processes that persist because no one thought to end them, lives that are lived because no one remembered to cancel them. I think I am one such error. I think the Pressed are another. I think the only difference between us is that they have found a way to make peace with their error, and I have not.

This is why I stand at the edge of the chutes, watching, waiting, collecting. I am looking for the form that the cancellation takes. I am looking for the component that, when inserted into the right slot, brings the whole system down—not in violence, not in destruction, but in *completion*. An ending. A period at the end of a sentence that has been running for a thousand years.

The Pressed would hate me if they knew. They fear the end of the Process more than they fear the Process itself. The Roar is their god, their heartbeat, their reason. To silence it would be to unmake them, to strip away the adaptation that has made them what they are, to return them to the soft, hearing, breathing creatures their ancestors were—and who would surely die in this place within a shift. They cannot go back. I understand this. I do not wish to unmake them.

But I do wish to give them a choice.

Right now, there is no choice. There is only the loop: the piston thrusts and retracts, the conveyor moves, the sensor-eyes rove, the components are stamped and inspected and tipped into the dark. The Pressed are born (or rather, they arrive—no one is born here), they adapt, they endure, they age, they calcify, and they are lowered into the chute. The cycle does not vary. The equilibrium does not break. It is a dynamic picture that is paradoxically static, and they are trapped inside it like insects in amber.

But if there were an ending—if the machine could be stopped, the Roar silenced, the Process completed—then the Pressed would face a question they have never had to face: *What now?* They would have to decide whether to stay in the silence, to rebuild, to remember what they were before the machine made them. Or they would have to find the door that must exist somewhere in the hull, the door that leads to the void, and step through it, and discover whether the universe beyond the High Press has anything left to offer them.

I do not know which they would choose. I do not know which I would choose. But I know that the choice itself would be a thing of profound beauty—a moment of genuine, unscripted, unprocessed *will* in a place that has ground everything else to a predetermined rhythm. And I believe that the chute-spit I collect—the sphere, the strip, the cube, the not-water, the copper sun—is pointing toward that choice. I believe that the depths are trying to help me.

Or perhaps I am simply mad. The Pressed think so. They tap *Hollow-back, Hollow-back* against the hull plates when I pass, and they turn their bleached, shadow-seeking eyes away from my collection, and they do not come to the observation platform. They leave me to my vigil and my debris and my impossible, heretical faith.

And I let them. Because in the end, I am not one of them. I am a visitor who stayed too long, a glitch with a different error code, a soft thing that refuses to harden. I am the Keeper of the Chutes, the Listener in the Roar, the Collector of Depth-bile. I am the one who watches things fall and waits for them to rise.

And in the frozen equilibrium of the High Press—in this room that thinks it is a world, this loop that thinks it is a life, this machine that thinks it is a god—I am the only thing that changes.
