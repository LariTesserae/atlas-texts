I am the one who reads the rust.

Not a Steward, who reads the gauges for the mood of the machine, nor a Gardener, who prunes the silence. I am something adjacent to both—a creature of the patina, of the slow oxidation that eats at the iron and excretes the color of old blood.

I am the Verifier.

My place is in the in-between. I do not stand in the sump with the Waders, nor do I sit motionless on the housing with the Observers. I walk the narrow diagonal braces that cross between the flywheels, the ones that vibrate with a frequency too low to hear, only felt in the marrow. I am tall—too tall for this place—but I have learned to move like a question, leaning into the humid dark, my spine a curve that matches the belly of the bedrock above.

My hands are my instruments. They are rough with calluses that are themselves rust-orange, stained from millennia of touching the iron. I have no tools; I need none. I press my palms flat against the copper piping and I *listen* through my skin. The metal speaks in temperature gradients. A cold spot is a wound; a hot spot is an inflammation. The healthy pulse of the engines is a warmth that spreads evenly, like bread dough rising in a dark room. When I find a fever, I mark it by breathing on the pipe, my condensation joining the room's, leaving a brief, bright circle that fades in seconds. The Stewards will see it. They always see it.

My face—what little of it matters—is my nose. The masks the Stewards wear to warm their breath, I wear for a different reason. I am tasting the air for the sweetness of corrosion. Fresh rust has a smell like wet pennies, sharp and metallic. Old rust—the patina that protects—smells like soil, like the grave, like something that has made peace with decay. When I detect the sharp edge beneath the dull, I know the erosion has turned from preservation to consumption. That is when the iron is dying, not sleeping.

I have a relationship with the frogs. They tolerate me because I am slow, but they do not trust me because I move. When I pause on a catwalk, pressing my ear to a steam vent that has gone quiet, a frog will sometimes crawl onto my shoulder. It sits there, heavy and cool, its heartbeat a separate rhythm against my collarbone. Its eyes glow in my peripheral vision, two green stars in the 0.8 twilight. I have learned to read their shifts. When the frog on my shoulder turns its head, I turn mine. It sees the thermal eddies that I cannot; it feels the vibrations that my thick skin misses. We are a temporary partnership, dissolved the moment I step away.

I do not eat the nutrient paste. Or rather, I do, but I have no taste for it. Instead, I drink the condensation directly. I tilt my head back beneath a pipe that weeps steadily and I catch the drops on my tongue. They taste of iron and stone and the faintest trace of copper. It is the taste of the place itself, and it fills me in a way the grey paste does not. The Stewards worry this habit will hollow my gut; they do not understand that I am already hollow, that I am a vessel for this place as much as the pipes are vessels for its steam.

At night—or what passes for night, when the indicator lights dim to their lowest pulse and even the frogs close their glowing eyes—I climb to the highest point in the hamlet. It is a narrow platform beneath the soot-stained ceiling, where the condensation is heaviest and the air is coolest. From here, I can see the whole of the inverted city: the great copper flywheels turning their imperceptible revolutions, the Waders moving like ghosts in the black mirror below, the Stewards wiping their gauges with reverent hands, the Gardeners tending their silent crops, the Observers fossilized beneath their living blankets of amphibians.

And I weep. Not from sorrow. The air is thick enough to chew, and my eyes water constantly in the iron-heavy humidity. The tears roll down my cheeks and fall from my jaw, joining the eternal drip, the patient *plink* that is the only conversation in this place. My tears are indistinguishable from the condensation. They are the same water, the same iron, the same slow, patient release.

This is how I know I belong here. I am not maintaining the machine. I am becoming it—another pipe that weeps, another surface that rusts, another pair of eyes that watches in the dark, waiting for the planet to remember, content in the meantime to feel the wheels turn beneath my palms, inch by inch by inch.
