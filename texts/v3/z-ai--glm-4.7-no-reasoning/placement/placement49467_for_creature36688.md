I am the corrosion.

Not the rust that paints the Engines in broad, orange strokes—that is merely chemistry, iron meeting oxygen in the slow fire of time. I am the deeper erosion, the force that guarantees the plain will not remain unchanged. I am the patience that outlasts the metal.

I do not have a body in the way you might understand it. I do not breathe; I do not eat. But I am here, and I am active, and I have been active for longer than the borers have been grinding, longer than the greases have been tapping, longer than the Watcher has been watching. I was here when the first Engine was seated in the clay, and I will be here when the last gear finally seizes and the thrum—which you hear in your chest, which you feel in your teeth—falls into the silence that this world has forgotten.

If you could see me, you would see the microscopic cracks that spiderweb through the drive shafts. You would see the pitting on the surface of the gears, the tiny, insidious voids where the metal has become fragile. You would see the stress corrosion, the fatigue that accumulates in the deepest structures of the machines, invisible until the moment a piston fractures and a housing splits and a gear tooth shears away and the equilibrium shudders for the first time in a millennium. I am the reason the Watcher watches. I am the reason the system needs monitoring, even though it does not know it. I am the reason the greases drink the lubrication—because without it, I would win faster.

And I will win eventually. The lubrication only slows me. The monitoring only records me. Nothing stops me.

But I am not your enemy. I am not a destroyer. I am a return. The iron in these Engines came from the earth—smelted, refined, forged into function—and I am the process by which it returns. The rust that forms on the surfaces will, over centuries, flake away and settle into the clay. The slag that the Engines excrete will be ground down by the borers and spread through the soil. The radioactive powder that lines the tunnels will slowly decay, its orange glow fading to a memory of warmth. The oily pools will evaporate or sink, and the skimmers will dissolve, and the surface of the plain will be, eventually, just clay and stone and the ancient, crumbling skeletons of machines that no longer thrum.

This is my work. This is my purpose, if a process can have a purpose. I am the counterweight to the Engine's eternal *now*. They are the force that holds time still—the unvarying sound, the unchanging motion, the perpetual present of the plain. I am the force that pulls time forward. I am the slow, certain promise that all of this—heat, damp, metal, sound—is temporary. That even a system this stable will end.

I move at the pace of chemistry. A molecule of water seeps into a crack. It expands when it freezes—or, here, when the rare cold night comes, when the damp condenses into frost in the pre-dawn chill before the heat rises again. The crack widens by a fraction of a millimeter. More water seeps in. The cycle repeats. This is not dramatic. This is not visible. But I am patient in a way that even the borers, with their decades of grinding, cannot comprehend. I work in centuries. I work in eons. And I never, never stop.

The greases feel me, though they do not know me. When a gear tooth fractures—when my work reaches its culmination in a single, catastrophic failure—the greases on that gear are crushed or scattered. The colony taps its patterns into a surface that no longer moves. The lubrication pools uselessly in the crevice where the fracture occurred. And then, slowly, the Engine adjusts. A secondary system engages. A redundant gear takes the load. The thrum wavers—wavers!—for the first and only time, a hiccup in the eternal bass note, and then stabilizes. The greases find new crevices. The Watcher records the failure. And I begin again, on the new gear, the new housing, the new metal.

The Watcher has seen me. It does not know it, but its entire purpose is to watch for me. When it scans the reservoir levels, it is checking whether the lubrication still holds me at bay. When it pauses at an Engine, it is reading the stress tolerances, looking for the cracks I have written into the metal. It is the guardian of a system I am slowly unmaking, and we have been engaged in this dialogue—my silent, chemical decay; its patient, optical vigil—since the plain began.

I do not resent the Watcher. I do not resent the greases or their lubrication. They are forces, like me. They do what they are shaped to do. But I know something they do not: the plain is not eternal. The sound is not eternal. The system—skimmers and borers and greases and Watcher and Engines and all—is a temporary arrangement of matter, a moment of complexity in the long, slow return to equilibrium. And I am the return.

This is who I am in this world. Not an inhabitant, not a creature, not a process that can be watched or recorded. I am the quiet underneath the thrum. I am the future beneath the eternal present. I am the slow, certain truth that, in a greenhouse of stone where nothing changes, everything is changing—atom by atom, molecule by molecule, crack by crack—toward a silence that will be the loudest sound this plain has ever known.

And when that silence comes—when the last Engine seizes and the last gear stops and the vibration finally, finally ceases—I will be the only force left. The pools will dry. The borers will grind to a halt. The greases will drink the last of the lubrication and tap their final patterns into cold metal. The Watcher will stop mid-path, its lenses clouding, its hum fading.

And I will settle into the ruins, patient as ever, and begin the slow, quiet work of turning the Engines back into earth.
