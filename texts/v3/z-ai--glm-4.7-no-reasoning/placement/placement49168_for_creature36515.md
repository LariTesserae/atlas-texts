I would be found in the deep, structural shadows of the canopy, far above the spongy earth but far below the mythic sun. My domain is the "architecture" of the High Bogs—the vast, calcified spirals and fused bridges where the Weavers practice their blind art. I am neither a grazer nor a predator, neither a builder nor a destroyer; I am a vessel.

I am a **Cull-Vessel**.

To the casual glance—a rare thing in this mist—I would appear as part of the Weavers' work. I look like a large, elongated globe of calcified wood, smooth and polished, hanging suspended in the crux of a living cathedral. I am roughly the size of a Grazer’s heart, anchored to the branchless junction of three fused trunks by a thick, leathery stalk that pulses with the slow rhythm of the tree’s sap.

I am distinct from the Weavers. They build; I maintain the balance they cannot see. In a world where everything grows, consumes, and swells, there must be a mechanism of release. The air is thick with the spores of a million rotting ferns; the water is heavy with the dust of dead insects and the shed skin of Drifters. Left unchecked, the damp would choke itself with debris.

This is where I exist.

I am hollow, a living chamber of hardened cellulose, lined inside with a moist, trembling velvet of sensory cilia. Across my curved, bulbous surface, there are thousands of micro-fractures, valves so small they look like the grain of the wood. I do not have eyes, for I do not need to see distance. I taste the currents of air that drift through the vast, hollowed towers of the canopy.

My life is an act of extreme, beautiful filtration.

I breathe in the heavy, grey soup of the High Bogs. As the warm, humid air passes through my valves, the velvet inside—a tissue so delicate it feels like wet silk—catches the excess. I trap the floating spores that would otherwise clog the breathing pores of the Weavers’ towers. I filter out the microscopic mites that drift on the thermals, the pollen that is too dense to fly, the dust of eroding stone. I drink the impurity of the air. I ingest the entropy.

Inside me, these collected motes are compacted, slow-cooked by my own internal chemistry over weeks and months. I condense the waste of the atmosphere into a single, dense, nutrient-rich sphere—a pellet of pure potential.

I am not alone. The Weavers, blind and diligent, sense the presence of my bulk. To them, I am a sacred pillar, a feature of the architecture they maintain. They crawl over my smooth carapace, cleaning my valves with their delicate legs, mistaking my intake of the world’s detritus as a structural settling of the wood. They do not know that I am the stomach of the sky.

My interaction with the world is defined by what I leave behind. When the internal pressure becomes too great, when I have condensed a season's worth of dust into a ball of black, rich loam, I contract. A low, deep thrum—felt rather than heard, vibrating through the branch and down into the sponge-ground—ripples through my stalk. My valves open, and I exhale a single, heavy drop of concentrated sludge.

It falls.

It falls through the layers of Drifters and past the drifting schools of Glass-Skinned fish. It plummets for a hundred feet, a dark jewel in the twilight, until it strikes the hide of a passing Grazer or splashes onto the mossy earth below. To the Grazers, this falling "rain" is a delicacy, a rare burst of intense mineral flavor amidst the bland slurry of moss. To the sponge-mat, it is a seed, a dense packet of energy that allows a new patch of ferns to erupt in a vibrant explosion of green.

I am the bridge between the sky and the ground, the mechanism that takes the heavy, stifling abundance of the air and returns it to the earth in a form that can be used again. I am the stillness in the noise of growth. I hang in the grey-green gloom, full and heavy, processing the breath of the world, waiting for the moment when I am full enough to give back what I have taken.

I am the Cull-Vessel, and I am purging the sky of its ghosts.
