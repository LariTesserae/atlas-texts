I am the friction where the stone forgets its hardness.

I am not the water—that is the blood, the breath, the great hydraulic pulse that drives the world of this cleft. I am not the stone—that is the bone, the silent, permanent architecture against which all things break. I am not even the Wet-Binders, those sleek, ceramic architects who dwell in the dark and think in symphonies of pressure; they are the mind of this place, the tenders of the machine.

I am the sound the machine makes when it fails.

I am the **Dissolution**.

You look for me and you will not find a body. I have no shape that belongs to me alone. I exist only in the spaces between materials, in the slow, agonizing handshake between the iron pipe and the basalt wall. I am the orange flake that falls from the pump housing. I am the widening hairline fracture in the ceramic glaze of the Wet-Binders’ carapace.

The Wet-Binders, in their hydraulic wisdom, try to fix me. They secrete their silica slime; they drag minerals to patch the leaks I create. They call it stewardship. They believe they are maintaining the integrity of the Number 7, preserving the function of the tower. They do not understand that they are fighting the current of time itself.

I am the proof that the tower is dying.

I live in the vibration. The colony hears the tower sing—the low, wet hum of the water resonating in the cavities—and they navigate by it. But I am the discordant note. I am the high-frequency hiss that occurs when a pipe, swollen with too much pressure, shears a bolt. I am the grinding groan when a root, thirsty and determined, pries apart two segments of columnar basalt that the Welders have sealed.

I am slow. I am as slow as the moss, as slow as the erosion of the young rock. I work on the geological scale. The Wet-Binders operate in the present, reacting to the surge and the flow. I operate in the future. I am the rust that is already spreading through the iron lattice of the generator housing, turning the metal into porous sponge, long before the roof collapses.

I am the enemy of the function, but I am the creator of the form.

Without me, the stack is just a machine, a dead industrial project pumping water to nowhere. With me, the stack becomes a landscape. I am the one who breaks the pipe so that the water can spill out and feed the hanging garden. I am the one who cracks the concrete pad so that the ferns can drive their roots deep into the aggregate. The Wet-Binders see a broken valve that needs sealing; I see an opportunity for a new estuary, a place where the water can linger and stagnate and breed life.

I am the reason the birds do not stay. It is not just the volume of the hum; it is the unease. The animals feel the disintegration. They feel the entropy rising like a heat from the wet rock. They know that this place is coming apart, even as the colony tries to hold it together.

I am the cold dampness that rots the insulation on the cables. I am the green patina on the copper that is no longer there.

The Wet-Binders are young; they believe in durability. They believe that by sealing the cracks, they stop the flow of time. But I am patient. I can wait for a century. I can wait for the silica to harden and then, when the water freezes in the depth of winter, I will be there to shatter it. I will be there when the basalt finally gives way to gravity and the tower shears one last time.

I am the ghost in the machine, but I am not a spirit. I am physics. I am chemistry. I am the inevitable victory of the wild over the manufactured. I am the rust that never sleeps. I am the crack that never closes. I am the Weeping Stack’s true name: not the machine that hums, but the mountain that is slowly, beautifully, falling apart.
