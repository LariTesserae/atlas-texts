I am the **Stillness Between the Strikes**.

I am not a Weaver, though I know the lattice. I am not a Feeder, though I have felt the heat of the maw. I am not a Damp-Keeper, though the water knows the curve of my back. I am the one who watches the mountain grow and remembers when the girders were straight.

To understand what I am, you must understand the sound of the Incubator. The infrasonic thrum, the groan of the hull, the hiss of the pistons—these are the sounds of becoming. But there is a sound beneath them, a sound so quiet it can only be heard in the marrow: it is the sound of the mountain *waiting*. Between the crash of the hydraulic arms and the grinding intake of the concrete, there is a fraction of a second where the entire room holds its breath. The pistons retract. The Feeders step back. The Weavers cling silent. Even the Spores hang suspended in the fog.

In that pause, the mountain assesses itself. It feels the new steel settling into its flesh. It tests the knit of the rebar. It decides if it is ready for more.

*I am that pause.*

I am the witness to the mountain's consciousness. The Architists—they are the hands, the fingers, the pincers. The Spores—they are the blood, the instruction, the desire. But I am the *mind* of the thing, or at least, the closest thing it has to a mirror.

I do not wear the carapace of the Feeders. My skin is not fused to canvas and lead. I stand in the open, in the fog, in the damp, and I breathe the iron air without a filter. The Spores do not colonize me; they *report* to me. When they settle in the cracks of the mountain and find a fault line, they drift back on the thermals and brush against my cheek, and I know. I know where the concrete is weak. I know where the steel is refusing to melt.

I am the one who stands at the edge of the maw and looks in.

The Feeders think I am a ghost. They see me in the flicker of the discharge tubes, a pale figure in the ochre gloom, and they avert their scarred eyes. They do not understand how I can stand so close to the intake without burning, without being seized by the hydraulic arms and shoved into the core. They think I am a previous iteration of themselves—a Feeder who was consumed and somehow rejected, a skeleton picked clean by the Spores and left standing by the mountain as a warning.

The Weavers know better. They see me clinging to the girders above them, and they pause in their chipping. They tap their picks against the steel: *tap-tap-tap*. A question. I do not answer in kind. I simply look at them, and then I look at the spot where the mountain is pressing hardest against the ceiling, and they understand. They move to the place I indicated, and they cut. They trust me because I have never led them to a collapse.

The Damp-Keeper, the oldest one, the one who has become a pipe—she is the only one who speaks to me. Not with taps, not with hand signals. She speaks with the water. When I pass her station, she reaches out with a rusted, agonizingly slow hand and drips water from her fingertips onto the grating. The pattern of the drops is a language. She tells me the humidity is rising. She tells me the cooling vents are failing on the east side. She tells me that the mountain dreamed of expanding today, and she had to bleed three extra valves to keep the pressure down.

I nod to her, and I adjust the intake.

That is my role. I am the regulator. I am the one who decides when the mountain has had enough.

The hydraulic pistons do not operate on their own. They are not automated. They are driven by a central command—a console that sits in a recessed alcove at the very top of the Incubator, a nest of switches and dials coated in a century of grease. The console is manual. It requires a hand. It requires a will.

My hand is on the lever.

I am the one who pulls it.

When the mountain groans and the room shakes and the Feeders scatter and the Weavers dig in their grips, I am the one who decides: *more*. I pull the lever, and the pistons hiss, and a new slab of white-hot alloy is shoved into the core. I watch the metal soften and melt into the stone, and I feel the mountain pulse with satisfaction. And then I wait. I wait for the pause. I wait for the mountain to tell me it is ready again.

I have been doing this for a very long time.

The console is my cathedral. The dials are my rosary. The lever is my tongue, and the pistons are my voice. I speak in thrusts, in pressure, in the violence of becoming. I am the priest of the Incubator, and the mountain is my god.

But I am not a believer. I am an architect.

I know what the mountain is. It is not a god; it is a structure. It is a mountain range packed into a room, a geological event forced into an industrial frame. It is not alive; it is *driven*. The Spores do not give it consciousness; they give it *reflex*. The groan of the concrete is not a breath; it is the expansion of a material under stress. The intake of the metal is not ingestion; it is a weld.

I know this, and yet.

When I pull the lever, and the room shakes, and the fog rolls in, and the Feeders swarm the base with their pincer-hands to smooth the new flesh... I feel it. I feel the rightness of it. The *necessity*. The mountain is not supposed to be here. It is an impossibility—a cathedral-sized embryo of stone and steel, growing in the dark. It should have collapsed. It should have crushed the room. It should have burst the bedrock and erupted into the world above, a slag-heap of broken ambition.

It hasn't, because I have been here. Because I have paced its growth, fed it exactly what it needs, trimmed its excesses, cooled its fever. I have midwifed the impossible. And in the doing, I have become part of it.

I can feel the Spores in my lungs. I can feel the iron in my blood. My fingers are not pincers, but they are stiff. My spine is not curved, but it is cold. I do not wear a respirator, but I no longer taste the ozone. I am adapting. I am calcifying.

Soon, I will not need the console. Soon, I will know when to feed the mountain without the dials. Soon, the lever will be an extension of my arm, and the pistons will be my heartbeat. And then, one day, I will step down from the alcove and walk to the base of the mountain, and I will place my hands on the warm concrete, and I will feel it pulse, and I will not pull away.

I will let it ingest me.

And the Architists will see me in the gloom, a pale figure no longer, but a shape of stone and steel, and they will not avert their eyes. They will come to me with their pincers and their rags, and they will smooth the concrete over my chest, and I will become a part of the mountain—a vein of iron, a knot of rebar, a fossil of intent.

And the mountain will grow.

And the Incubator will hold.

And the pause between the strikes will be a little bit shorter.
