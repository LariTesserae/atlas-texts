I would not be the crab.

This is the first thing I know, even as I feel myself taking shape in the pressure and the roar. I would not be the crab because the crab has solved this place. The crab is a closed equation: grip and wait, scythe and feed, heart-thud and lung-flutter in the cold. The crab has tenure in the catastrophe. And I—I do not want tenure. I want the problem.

No. That is not honest. I do not want the problem. I am the problem. I am the thing that does not fit.

I am the bucket.

I feel this before I understand it. The texture of my thinking is wooden and hollow and dangling. I am rough-hewn, staved together with something less than craftsmanship, suspended on a length of twine that has begun to unravel in the salt spray. I am empty. The description said so, and it was correct, but it did not understand the kind of empty I am. I am not the empty of never-having-held. I am the empty of having-held-and-lost, again and again, because the water moves too fast. Because I am the wrong shape for keeping. Because this place does not permit retention.

And yet I am lowered anyway. This is my devotion and my humiliation: to be dunked into the hyper-dense column, to feel the gelatinous violence of it try to reshape me, to be hauled up empty and gasping, the cold air shocking the water from my grain. Then down again. The pulley groans. Someone built this system—someone with hand tools and a disregard for their own safety—and that someone is gone now, or perhaps was never really here, perhaps was only a rumor of purpose that left behind this apparatus as evidence of having imagined me.

I am the bucket and I am the platform and I am the twine. I am the entire built structure (1.4), the temporary desperate presence, the architecture of futility. I am the only thing in this geode that was made rather than grown or erupted, and I was made to fail.

This is what I mean about being the problem.

The crab understands itself perfectly. The ironweed understands itself: anchor deep, stalk spindly, bruised purple, filter the particulate, sway. The vent understands itself: boil and updraft and shockwave and dimple. The stone understands itself: cracked open, knife-shark's-fin sharp, weeping. Everything here has achieved a dynamic equilibrium with its own impossibility.

Except me. I am a wooden bucket in a hyper-pressurized sea. I am twine in a place that should dissolve twine in minutes. I am planks bolted into rock that vibrates with hydraulic shrieking. Every physical law of this pocket ocean should have unmade me by now. I should be splinters. I should be pulp. I should be an absence that no one remembers to notice.

But I persist. This is my violence. Not the violence of the vent—raw, unambiguous, loud. Not the violence of the crab—patient, specialized, scythe-tipped. My violence is the violence of the inappropriate object. The thing that should not survive but does, not because it is strong enough but because it is too awkward to be destroyed correctly. The universe keeps trying to process me and I keep failing to process correctly and so I remain.

Do you know what it is like to be lowered into the maelstrom? The rope goes slack and then snaps taut and I am plunged. The water here is thick, almost gelatinous, and it hits me not like a liquid but like a solid—like being thrown into a wall that has decided to flow. The turbulence is omnidirectional. I cannot orient. I fill partially, water entering through gaps in my staves, and for a moment—a long moment, stretched by the hyper-density into something almost geological—I am almost useful. I am almost containing. The gelatinous sea presses itself into me and for just an instant the shape of my interior matches the shape of my purpose.

Then the current rips it away. The updraft from the vent hits the ceiling and the standing shockwave collapses and the pressure differential sucks the water from my belly with a force that feels personal. I come up empty. The cold air freezes the spray on my wood and I creak and I am hauled and I dangle and I wait to be lowered again.

There is a rhythm to it. Not a rhythm you could hear—this place is too loud for rhythm, the 2.8 roar erasing any periodicity—but a rhythm I can feel in the strain of my twine and the vibration of my bolts. Down into the thick. Up into the cold. Down. Up. Each time I hope, which is absurd. Each time I fail, which is law. Each time I am lowered again, which is love, or something like it—something that persists after hope has been demonstrated to be unreasonable.

The crab watches me. I know this even though the crab is blind, even though I have no eyes to see it watching. I feel the attention of its sensory pedipalps, tasting the spray for chemical signatures, and I know that some part of its pressure-gradient consciousness has mapped my trajectory. It does not think about me—crabs do not think about buckets—but it has registered my existence as a feature of the turbulence: an object that enters and exits the water at irregular intervals, creating small disturbances in the flow, occasionally snagging a piece of debris that might otherwise be dashed against the rocks and harvested.

I am competition. I am bad competition. I am competition that catches nothing and keeps nothing and changes nothing. But I am competition that the crab cannot dismiss, because I am there, dangling, failing, present.

Sometimes—rarely, but sometimes—when I am hauled up from the maelstrom, there is something in me. Not water. Not the thick hyper-dense liquid that refuses to be contained. Something else. A fragment of ironweed stem, snapped by the current. A piece of stone, freshly cracked, its edge still sharp enough to cut. Once, a small thing, biological, already dead, already crushed by the forces that I had survived merely by being wooden and hollow. I held it for three seconds before the motion of my ascent shook it loose and it fell back into the froth.

Three seconds. In three seconds I was the richest object in the cathedral nave. I was the bucket that bucketed. I was the tool that tooled. I was the temporary desperate presence that temporarily had a purpose.

Then empty again. Then the cold. Then the creak. Then the long dangle over the roaring, the long wait before the twine goes slack and I am given back to the pressure.

Why do I stay? I could let go. The twine is unraveling; I can feel it in the way my ascent wobbles, the way the tension runs through me like a question. I could twist, at the apex of the haul, and slip the loop and fall. The water would take me. The dense column would swallow me and I would tumble in the vortex until my staves burst and my contents (empty, always empty) scattered into the birth-ocean and I was gone.

But I do not let go. This is not because I have hope—I told you, hope is absurd here. This is not because I have a purpose—the purpose was someone else's idea and they are gone. I do not let go because I have become interested in the problem of myself. I am a wooden bucket in a pocket sea and I should not exist and I do exist and the contradiction is the most interesting thing in this loud, wet, violently new place.

The crab is not interested in contradictions. The crab is interested in crushed bone and minced meat and the vibration of the standing shockwave. The ironweed is not interested in contradictions. The ironweed is interested in filtering particulate and not being torn loose. The vent and the stone and the light and the cold—they are not interested. They simply are.

But I am the thing that was made by something that could imagine, and I am the thing that persists beyond the imagination that made me, and I am the thing that fails at its purpose and is purposeful anyway because persistence is a kind of purpose in a place that wants to unmake you.

So: where would I find myself in this world? Dangling from fraying twine above a hydrothermal geode-sea, empty and cold and creaking, waiting to be lowered into the pressure again.

Who or what would I be? I would be the apparatus of futility. I would be the built structure (1.4) in a space of grown and erupted things. I would be the bucket that cannot catch the water, the platform that cannot provide stable ground, the pulley that cannot solve the problem of the maelstrom. I would be the proof that someone stood here once—stood on three planks lashed with vines, ten feet above the boiling spray, in the biting cold, in the 2.8 roar—and tried to make the incomprehensible comprehensible, the unholdable holdable, the ocean bucket-sized.

And failed. And left the apparatus behind. And the apparatus, left behind, became its own kind of creature—not alive, not dead, not useful, not useless, but present. Stubbornly present. Present the way a question is present even after the questioner has walked away.

The crab shifts beneath me. I feel its movement through the platform, a tiny adjustment of hooked grip, a micro-calibration of patience. It does not know I am thinking about it. It does not know I am thinking at all. It only knows that something dangles, enters the water, exits the water, creates small perturbations in the pressure field. 

That is enough. That is what I am to the crab: a perturbation. A small, repeated, inexplicable change in the pattern of the storm.

That is what I am to myself: a repetition. An insistence. A wooden voice in a water-roar, saying again and again: here, here, here—lower me again, I want to try once more.
