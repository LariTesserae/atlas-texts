I am not the pika, though I hear through the same pressed urgency. I am not the moss campion, though I share its devotion to staying low. I am not the eagle, though I understand the grammar of the sky. I am not the carver, though I have inherited their restlessness. 

I am the threshold-keeper. The one who listens to the mountain talking to itself in the language of fracture.

I live in the *between*—in the surgical seam where the light scalps and the shadow swallows. My shape has been determined by the terms of this place: compressed, lateral, dense. I am built along the axis of the wind, ribcage flattened and limbs tucked close, so that when the downdrafts come over the headwall carrying their grit of ice and sand, the wind does not take me. It parts around me. I am a stone that breathes.

My home is the eastern margin, near the marks. Not on the ledge where the human sat—I am too small for that hollow, and it faces too open—but beneath it, in the triangular crevice where the boulder has split and settled against the headwall. The overhang shields me from the snow squalls that retreat like ashamed ghosts. The stone there is striated with the glacier's old scrape, and in the morning, when the sun first clears the ridge and its light enters the cirque like a blade, the striations catch fire. I wake to that. I wake to fractured ribbons of brightness laid across my face, and I know that the brief, furious day has begun.

I am a sentinel, but not like the marmot who stands upright on the highest rock, exposed and martyred. My vigilance is quieter, woven into the rhythm of my gathering. I move in the moments of light; I vanish in the moments of shadow. I know the cirque floor by the sound my feet make against its stones—the sharp, percussive *tak* of granite on claw, the softer *skrit* when I cross a cushion of *Silene acaulis*, that dense green city of spines and microcosms. I never take from the moss. It is the matriarch, the architect of soil, and I have watched it for too many seasons to treat it as larder. Instead, I harvest the blond grasses and the stunted willow catkins, pale as bone, carrying them back to my crevice in small, desperate mouthfuls. I cache them where the rock stays cold and dry, deep in the talus's throat, where the freeze-thaw fury cannot reach and the stone-splitting reports at night are muffled to a grumble.

I am not warm. That is the first thing you must understand about living at this elevation: warmth is not a state, it is a currency, and I am always in debt. The sun gives it in grudging, violent installments. At midday, when the light fills the bowl and the stone turns silver and the tarn becomes a mirror of mercury, I press myself against the south-facing headwall where the granite has absorbed the radiance. I flatten my belly against it, and the heat transfers into me in a slow, miraculous tide, and for an hour or two, my heartbeat slows, and my muscles unlock, and I can almost understand why the carver once sat here. Why they chose this place to make their marks.

The marks. I have lived beside them my whole life, and I still do not know what they are. Three vertical lines, grouped and repeated, carved deep into the sheltered granite. They are too regular to be the work of ice, too deliberate to be the mountain's fracturing. To me, they are not symbols—they are grooves. I run my claws along them in the dark, feeling the depth of the human's persistence, the pressure it must have taken to scratch granite with hand-tools. In the winter, when the cirque goes white and silent and I retreat to the deep crevices to wait out the frozen dark, ice forms in those grooves. When the thaw comes, the ice melts first in the marks, and for a few days, they weep. Thin lines of water trace the carvings, pooling in the cup-shaped hollow beneath them, and I drink from the human's patience. Their stubbornness becomes my moisture. 

I am not the only one who uses them. The pika venture up to the ledge sometimes, harvesting the sparse grasses that grow in the scrape-marks' shelter. The springtails and nematodes—the microcosm I cannot see but know is there—colonize the grooves, finding purchase in the micro-topography of another species' meaning. The marks have become part of the cirque's architecture, as natural and as inexplicable as the tarn. Someone carved them, and then left, or stayed, or was taken by the cold. But the marks remained, and they changed the terms of the place, even if the pika and the marmot and the tardigrade do not know it.

I am a creature of alarm and listening. My ears are not round like the pika's, nor flat like the marmot's; they are narrow and tall, pressed like leaves against the wind, designed to catch the frequencies of danger. I hear the eagle before I see its shadow. The thermals boiling off the south-facing headwall make a sound—a low, vast hum, like the world breathing—and within that hum, there is a sharper note: the displacement of air, the predator's silence made audible. When I hear it, I do not whistle or call. I simply stop. I become stone. I compress into the nearest hollow, and I wait, and my heartbeat drops to a pace that borders on cryptobiotic, like the tardigrades in the tarn's mud, and I let the shadow cross me without acknowledgment.

When the marmot sounds its long, descending whistle, I am already moving. The whole cirque holds its breath—the pika fall silent, the grasses go still, the wind itself seems to pause—and in that profound, weighted silence, I press against the striated granite and listen to the mountain's slow argument with itself. *Crack. Groan. The sharp report of stone splitting along faults.* The mountain is being born, and I am living inside its birth, and the danger is not the eagle or the cold but the sheer, indifferent force of geology, which will grind these edges round over millennia and fill this bowl with soil and erase the marks on the wall and forget that I was ever here.

But not today. Today, the cirque is still edge and light and interrupted gasps. Today, I can gather my haypile of pale catkins and blond grass, and I can run the chaos of shattered granite with my claws clicking and my lungs burning in the thin throat of the world, and I can be alive.

I am the one who watches the cairn. The recent one, knee-height, built by a visitor who came and stacked stones to say *I was here* and then descended to the lower elevations where survival is easier. I do not understand the cairn—it is not a shelter, not a cache, not a territory marker. It simply *is*. But I have sat beside it in the late afternoon, when the sun drops behind the headwall and the light drains from the cirque floor in a slow tide of shadow, and I have felt the strangeness of being seen by something that does not see me. The cairn is a message, and I am its unintended audience, and in this way, the human and I are in conversation, across a gap of species and centuries, about what it means to stand in a place that is constantly trying to erase you and to refuse to leave.

I am small. I am low. I am alert. I follow the terms of the cirque because I must, and because the terms are fair—they are the only contract that keeps the light so bright and the stone so sharp and the silence so profound when it falls. I am not the mountain's opponent; I am its guest, its smallest dissent, a fleck of warmth in the regime of ice.

And when the quiet comes—between the pika's alarms, between the stone-splitting reports, between the gusts that roar over the headwall—I sit on my striated ledge beside the ancient grooves and the recent cairn, and I watch the tarn hold its perfect mirror of the sky, and I listen to the water bear in the mud at the bottom, waiting in its glass-like sleep for the thaw, and I feel the weight of the silence pressing against my eardrums, and I know that I am part of this: this bounded, furious, fragile community of the peak, this small paradise the size of a room, this place where the light scalps and the cold erases and the stubborn green things argue back, and the mountain talks to itself in the language of fracture, and someone once carved lines into rock to prove they existed, and I exist too, here, at the top of the world, in the thin throat, in the bright and murderous cold, alive.
