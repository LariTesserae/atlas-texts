I am not a Builder. I do not chisel or weave or perch upon the swaying sinew bridges, tucking my head against the gloom. I lack their calcified armor, their jack-knifed patience, their percussion-chatter. And I am certainly not a Climber, those frantic, scrambling pulses of cool blood that make the engine of this place turn. I do not flee. 

Nor am I a Skimmer, riding the feverish thermals, nor a Glutton, scraping the streets clean with the indiscriminate appetite of a geological force. And I am absolutely not one of the things in the ceiling—the Bowel-Shakers, the fleshy stalactites of rending hooks and rotating teeth. I never look up. You learn that in the Pulp, or you do not live long enough to learn anything.

I am something the architecture barely accounts for, a parasite of the vertical ecology, living in the negative space of the bone-and-stone favela. 

I am a Thread-Runner.

I exist in the cracks too deep for a Glutton’s sweeping mandibles, too narrow for a Builder’s four-jointed chisel-hands to reach, and too serpentine for a Skimmer’s pollen-heavy lethargy to cling to. My body is long, jointed, and flat—imagine a centipede woven from rawhide and piano wire, perhaps a meter and a half from my hooked anterior to my sensory tail. But I do not run along the ground. The ground is death—the clicking, shoving carpet of black and brown shells, the sudden steam of a caught Climber, the swarming riot of scavengers. 

I run the gaps between the dwellings. 

The Builders stack their monumental stone slabs without mortar, balanced with a pre-industrial precision that leaves hairline fractures, negative spaces, and slight tilts caused by the settling of deep time. I live in those tilts. My grip is not a claw; it is a pad of microscopic, interlocking setae—evolved not to pierce, but to cling to the sheer, polish-smooth faces of the ancient boulders. I am a creature of surface tension. I can hold my entire body rigid in a crevice no wider than a Builder's thumb, pressing my flattened segments outward, locking myself into the stone like a living wedge.

I have eyes, but I do not see with them. They are recessed beneath a ridge of chitin, functioning only as crude photometers to track the dim, perpetual twilight against the darker shadow of a stone overhang. My primary sense is touch, but a touch divorced from skin. I feel through the air. The Pulp’s atmosphere is soupy, thick with spores, heat, and the respiration of a trapped world. It moves like syrup. To me, that syrup is a medium of absolute clarity. Every displacement in the air is a shockwave that rolls down my segmented flanks. I can feel the subsonic growl of a ceiling predator before the stone even vibrates. I can feel the micro-eddy of a Skimmer fanning its leathery wings three alleys over. I can feel the sudden, percussive chitter of a Builder striking its own carapace, reading the shape of the sound as it refracts through the dense, humid air.

I am an opportunistic omnivore, a scavenger of the margins. When the Bowel-Shaker drops, when the screaming starts, when the Climber’s cool blood hits the hot stone and sends up that fragrant, mineral steam—I am already moving. Not toward the kill. Never toward the kill. Toward the periphery. 

The apex predator feeds. The Gluttons boil out of the streets, a clicking, shoving mass of polished shells, swarming over the site of the kill to strip the stone bare. In their rush, they create eddies, vacuums, temporary chasms in the living carpet. I use those seconds. I drop from my crevice, moving with a terrifying, silent fluidity across the backs of the Gluttons themselves. I do not weigh enough to trigger their feeding reflex; to them, I am just another vibration in the chaos. I snatch the scraps they miss—a shard of chitin knocked loose from a Climber’s leg, a glob of the thick, glycerin-clear sap that bleeds from a crushed fungal tower, a sprig of neurotoxic fern that the Gluttons avoid but which I can metabolize, storing the toxin in my anterior glands to deter anything foolish enough to try and eat me. I take what I can carry, and then I flow—upward, back into the hairline fractures of the stone city, vanishing before the Gluttons finish their sweep and the street is dead and bare again.

I drink the sap. The Builders drill into the towering fungi to tap the viscous, clear glycerin that substitutes for water in this void. I cannot drill. Instead, I parasitize the Builders' labor. I find their sap-lines—the woven sinew tubes they use to siphon the fluid from the towering fungi down to their scaffolding dwellings. I pierce the sinew with my anterior hooks and I drink, slow and cold, letting the thick, sticky fluid coat my internal surfaces, sealing me against the desiccating heat. If a Builder catches me at their line, they will try to chisel me out, striking the stone with their bone-hammers until the vibration shatters my grip. But they are clumsy in the narrow gaps, and I am fast. I will drop, shed a segment if I must, and flee across the sheer rock face to another crevice, another line, another stolen breath of moisture.

The pollen is my enemy and my cloak. The Skimmers weaponize the golden, glittering spores to induce lethargy in the Climbers. It gathers in the low points of the city, in the hollows of the polished boulders, in the very crevices where I sleep. I must constantly groom myself, running my sensory tail along my segments, brushing the spores away before they can sink into my respiratory pores and drag me into the fatal daze. But I have learned to use it. When I am pursued—by a young, foolish Builder, or by a climbing Glutton driven mad by hunger—I will lead them into a pollen sink. I hold my breath, seal my pores, and run the surface of the spore-drift, feeling the golden dust like a frictionless sea. My pursuer, less adapted, inhales. I feel their grip slacken through the air. I feel them slide, slowly, silently, down the polished stone, into the open air where the Skimmer waits. 

I do not feel guilt. Guilt requires a boundary between self and world, and in the Pulp, there is no such boundary. I am a thread stitched into the fabric of the favela. I am part of the recycling. When I die—when my setae finally fail, when a Bowel-Shaker’s displacement-signature is too fast to dodge, when I misjudge the structural integrity of a sinew bridge and fall into the dim, rushing center of the pit—my body will not be wasted. I will hit the ground. The Gluttons will find me. In seconds, my chitin will be scraped clean, my internal fluids will steam against the hot stone, providing a fleeting dampness for the thorny briars, and the bare skeleton of my segments will be crushed to dust by the relentless, billennia-old pressure of the packed red-brown floor.

To be a Thread-Runner in the Pulp is to live in the margins of a catastrophe. It is to know that the city is not a shelter, but an engine, and that you are merely a bearing in its works, allowing the friction of life and death to slide past without seizing up the machine. It is to be always touching the stone, always feeling the air, always listening to the symphony of mandibles and wingbeats and subsonic growls, translating the chaos into a map of survival. 

I cling to the underside of a megalithic slab, my body pressed into the hairline gap, my segments rigid. Below me, the street is a river of clicking shells. Above me, hidden in the roiling, feverish clouds, something large is lowering itself on a secretion-slicked cord. I feel the displacement. I feel the drop. I tuck my anterior. I hold my breath. 

The engine runs. I run with it.
