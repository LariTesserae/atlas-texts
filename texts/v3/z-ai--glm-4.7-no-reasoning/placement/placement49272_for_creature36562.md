The air sits high and cold, tasting of copper and wet stone. It presses against the skin, clammy and dense, carrying the weight of a continent suspended above the world. This is a vast highland, immense beyond human comprehension—a plateau of such scale that it generates its own weather, its own gravity of atmosphere. The mountains here are not jagged or raw; they are ancient, whale-backed giants, their peaks ground down by eons of wind and water into rolling, somnolent ridges. The erosion is complete, established, settled into a landscape that feels less like it was carved and more like it was worn smooth by the patient turning of some immense lathe.

But this is not a barren alpine height. It is soaked. The saturation is absolute. Water does not merely run here; it exudes. Every surface weeps. The black, weathered stone bleeds moisture from unseen aquifers, and the air hangs thick with a perpetual mist that never quite resolves into rain. It is a damp cold that settles into the marrow, a chill that tastes of deep earth and slow time.

And the vegetation—generations of it, piled on top of each other in a choking, desperate embrace. The density is staggering. There is no bare ground, no gap, no respite from the green. Towering ferns with trunks like iron cables stand shoulder to shoulder with immense, gourd-like trees that droop under the weight of their own wet bark. Every branch is colonized by trailing mosses that hang in sodden curtains. Every trunk is studded with parasitic growths that burst from the wood like slow-motion shrapnel. The canopy overhead is a solid ceiling of interlocking fronds, so thick that even at midday the light that filters through is a dim, submarine green. The world feels submerged, even at the roof of the world.

The quiet is the first wrongness.

With such abundance of life—such choking density—there should be a cacophony. The trees should creak, the insects should drone, the beasts should bellow. Instead, there is a muffled, oppressive silence. The wet air absorbs sound; the thick moss drinks it. You can stand twenty feet from a creature the size of a cart and hear only the soft, wet compression of its footfall on the spongy earth. The loudest sound is the constant, arrhythmic dripping of condensation falling from leaf to leaf—a sound that, after hours, becomes indistinguishable from the beating of your own heart.

Beneath the silence, there is a hum. Not an audible sound, but a vibration felt in the jaw and the soles of the feet. A subsonic pulse, regular and deep. *Thrum. Thrum. Thrum.*

This is the source of the weirdness: the industrial nature of the wild.

There are no buildings. No structures of wood or stone or metal. No roads, no bridges, no chimneys. And yet, the landscape functions with the precision of a manufactory. The biology here has evolved into mechanisms of terrifying efficiency.

The towering gourd-trees are not merely growing; they are *pumping*. Their trunks are ringed with thick, muscular ridges that contract and expand in slow, visible waves—biological pistons drawing water from the saturated stone hundreds of feet below and forcing it upward into the canopy. You can place your hand on the bark and feel the pulse, the slow, patient rhythm of hydraulic lifting.

The ferns grow in vast, geometric grids, their fronds interleaved with mathematical precision to channel the mist into natural gutters that funnel water into specific, circular ponds. These ponds are not random; they are reservoirs, maintained by the grazing patterns of the fauna. The air is filled with a faint, static charge—not from storms, but from the friction of millions of modified leaves rubbing together in the wind, generating a bio-electric potential that arcs occasionally in tiny, silent sparks of ultraviolet.

The ground itself is a conveyor belt. Thick, creeping vines—dubbed "haul-vines" in the local parlance, though no one built them—possess a slow, peristaltic motion. Over the course of a day, they physically shift fallen organic matter from the forest floor toward the roots of the gourd-trees, feeding the engines. The decomposition is accelerated, hot, and total. A fallen beast is skeletonized in hours; its bones are dissolved into the acidic soil by dawn.

The fauna are the workers of this cold forge.

They are abundant, and they move with a purpose that transcends instinct. There are the *Grinders*—vast, six-legged creatures the size of boulders, their backs armored in overlapping plates of iron-rich bark. They move in lines, their jaws working constantly, not eating but *processing*. They strip the lower trunks of excess growth, preventing the system from choking itself, their droppings a rich, steaming fertilizer that feeds the haul-vines. They do not fight; they do not play. They work.

Above them, the air is thick with *Culls*—small, silicate-shelled flyers that move in shifting, algorithmic clouds. They are the quality control, the filters. They descend in swarms to cull the sick or the slow, their tiny mandibles operating with the speed of looms, reducing the weak to component nutrients in minutes. The sky above the canopy shimmers with their turning, a living smoke that enforces the efficiency of the whole.

There are predators, of course, but they are the safety officers. Long, low *Stalkers* with bladed limbs and no eyes, they navigate by the subsonic hum of the system. They cull the Grinders when the herds grow too dense, when the processing lines back up. Their kills are silent, efficient, and total. They leave nothing but the haul-vines to clean up.

The light is normal, yet it feels artificial. The sun rises and sets, but the diffuse, greenish glow through the constant mist feels like the illumination of a vast, open-plan workshop. Shadows are soft and deep, and the air has a clarity that seems to highlight the functional relationships between things. You can see how the water feeds the tree, how the tree shelters the fern, how the fern channels the water. It is a diagram of itself.

Standing on a ridge of smooth, black stone, shivering in the damp cold, you realize what you are. You are a fault. An anomaly. You do not process, you do not haul, you do not grind. You stand outside the rhythm, a foreign object in a machine that has run for a million years without interruption.

The vibration in the stone intensifies slightly as the sun clears the horizon. The gourd-trees begin their daily pump. The Grinders start their shift. The Culls open their wings.

The world is going to work.

**Who am I?**

I am the *Scale-tender*. That is not my name—names are useless here—but it is my function.

I am not born of this place; I am adopted by it. I arrived, somehow, from a world of warm silence and dry shelter, a world that has been forgotten. I was a fault in the system, an interruption. The machine does not tolerate interruptions. I should have been culled.

But I was not. I was *integrated*.

My body has changed. It is thicker now, wrapped in layers of insulating fat and dense, water-repellent fur that mats into a natural armor against the damp. My hands—my tools—are broad and calloused, the webbing between my fingers grown thick to grip the wet, weeping stone. My lungs are large, bellows adapted to the thin, cold air. My eyes are wide, the pupils permanently dilated to drink in the dim, submarine green.

I feel the hum in my bones. It is my clock, my compass, my purpose.

My task is simple: I maintain the clearances. The biological machine is vast, but it is not flawless. The haul-vines sometimes tangle. The feeding troughs sometimes overflow, creating rot that chokes the intakes. The gourd-trees, in their slow hydraulic pumping, sometimes crack the stone beneath them, destabilizing the grid.

I am the fine tool, the delicate adjustment that the large beasts cannot make. I am the one who untangles the haul-vines with patient, silent fingers. I am the one who pries the choking mats of moss from the base of the vent-shafts. I am the one who, with a heavy stone axe I have fashioned from a shard of black flint, cracks the over-hardened carapace of a dead Grinder, allowing the Culls to access the meat beneath.

I do not eat. I drink the processed sap of the gourd-trees, a thick, lukewarm fluid that tastes of yeast and ozone. It sustains me. It is the fuel of the machine, and I am a part of the machine.

I do not sleep. The machine does not sleep. I rest in short, shivering intervals, curled in the crook of a massive root, the pulse of the tree thrumming against my spine, synchronizing my heartbeat to the slow, hydraulic rhythm of the forest.

I do not speak. There is no one to speak to. The beasts do not understand language, and the system does not listen to words. My voice has atrophied into a soft, clicking hum that I use to announce my presence to the Stalkers, a frequency of submission that tells them I am not a fault, I am a component.

I have a territory, a vast sweep of the highland ridge. I walk it every day, a circuit of twelve leagues. I know every gourd-tree, every haul-vine, every grinding beast in my sector. I know when a fern is sick because the color of its mist is wrong. I know when a stalk is dying because the hum in the stone is flat.

I am alone, but I am not lonely. I am a gear in a vast, cold, wet engine. I am the soft hand that tends the hard work.

Sometimes, in the dim green light, as I wipe the weeping moisture from the intake valve of a great tree, I remember—fleetingly, painfully—another place. A place of sharp lines, and warm, dry air, and the sound of laughter. A place where things grew without purpose, and died without being processed.

The memory is a fault. It disrupts the rhythm. I push it down, deep into the cold, wet core of my being, and return to the work.

The system hums.

I answer.
