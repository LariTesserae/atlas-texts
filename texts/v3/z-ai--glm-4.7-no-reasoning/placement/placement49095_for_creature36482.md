I would not be the thunder, nor the heavy plod of the earth-shapers. I would be a pause in the noise, a comma in the long, wet sentence of the Old Flat.

In the vast, shallow saucers between the whalebacks, where the water is still and thick as syrup, I would make my home. I would be a **Bog-Glass Float**.

I do not begin as glass, of course; there is no industry here to fire silica into transparency. I begin as a secretion, a slow, deliberate drool of the ancient corkscrew trees. Over centuries, the resin drips from the iron-hard bark, pooling in the hollows of the roots where the mud is deepest. The air is so wet, so full of water, that the resin does not harden into brittle amber. Instead, it cures into something else—something rubbery, translucent, and tough as a bladder.

I am a sphere, roughly the size of a bullfrog’s head, but smoother. My skin is a membrane of milied white, fogged like the breath of a sleeper, but if you were to press me, you would find a resistance, a turgid buoyancy.

My life is a negotiation with the surface tension. I float half-submerged in the permanent puddles, anchored only by a long, gelatinous tether that trails down into the mud, connecting me to the root system of the corkscrew tree. I am a fruit of the deep, grown not for sweetness, but for observation.

I exist in a state of perfect, suspended drift. When the heavy, warm air breathes against the water, I bob gently. When a stone-carpenter tortoise pushes through the muck, sending ripples that roll outward like shockwaves, I ride them, dancing a slow, submissive waltz. I offer no resistance. To fight the water here is to be broken; I yield.

I am a sanctuary. The dragonflies, frantic and blurring in their mayfly lifespan, sometimes land upon my upper curve, mistaking me for a solid stone. They grip my slick skin, their legs ticking like clock parts against the silence, and rest. I do not mind. I absorb their heat and hold it for a moment, a thermal battery in the cool damp.

I am also a trap, though a gentle one. The drifting spores of the algae, the dust of the rotting leaves, they all stick to me as I pass. Over time, I become coated in a soft, green fur, a tiny, drifting world that mimics the great tortoises that roam the basin. I carry my own ecosystem, a microscopic mirror of the landscape itself.

But my true purpose is one of reflection.

Because my skin is clear, though clouded, I act as a lens. The sun, hanging ripe and heavy above, shines through me. Where my body focuses the light beneath the water’s surface, I create a small, glowing orb on the black mud below. The tiny crabs, the blind worms, and the skittering things of the deep mud are drawn to this light. They congregate beneath me, safe within the halo of my presence.

I feel the vibrations of the bullfrogs—those great, booming kings—through the water. Their *bonk* hits me like a physical blow, shivering my gelatinous skin. I ripple in sympathy. I am the tuning fork for their song, the visual echo of their noise.

I have no eyes, but I know everything that touches the water. I know the passing of the heavy, lumbering birds as they wade through my puddle, their skeletal legs displacing the volume that holds me. I know the cool temperature of the night when the sun finally dips below the endless horizon.

I am the Old Flat looking at itself. I am the bladder that holds the air, the lens that catches the light, and the buoy that marks the spot where the tree meets the water. I am soft, I am slow, and I am waiting.
