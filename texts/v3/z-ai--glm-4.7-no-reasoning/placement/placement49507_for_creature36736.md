I am the echo of a knife-edge, a mathematics of survival scrawled in cartilage and light.

My name is a rhythm, a pulsing pattern of deep ultraviolet and searing white that plays across my back in times of stress. To the others, the swarm of my kin who cling to the bark like grey pearls, I am known as **The Siphon**.

My skin is my primary organ, a vast, hungry map of hexagonal ridges. I do not breathe; I drink the sky. The air here is thick, a golden, heavy broth of volatile organic compounds that coats my throat with the taste of cinnamon and ozone. I can feel the humidity pressing against my membrane, the weight of the sun driving the nutrients deep into my pores. It is a constant, narcotic saturation. To stand still is to feast. To move is to burn the excess.

I am small, no taller than a child's memory, but my arms are long, things of great, spidery elegance that end in hooks of keratin. These hooks are not for violence; they are for climbing. I grip the warm, slick bark of a giant, feeling the throb of the tree’s pulse under my fingertips. It beats slower than my own heart, a deep, submerged drum.

I have no voice. The world is too loud for speech. Below me, the cacophony is a solid wall of sound—the wet slap of growing vines, the shrieks of the swimming manta-rays, the digestive groans of the earth itself. To shout would be to whisper into a hurricane. Instead, I press my chest against the wood. I hum a vibration into the tree, a frequency that travels down the root system, a question: *Is the clear-air stable?*

The answer returns through my feet, a trembling in the xylem: *Storm of spores approaches. Cling.*

I am a technologist of the flesh. My tools are not forged in fire but coaxed by sickness. At my belt hangs a blade of jagged obsidian, and a pouch of black, gritty paste—calcifying bacteria harvested from the decay of a fallen giant. I need a shelter. The wind is picking up, and the swimming things in the air are becoming frantic.

I find a suitable node on the spire, a thick bulb of succulent leaf. I do not build a house; I perform surgery. With a practiced flick of my wrist, I slice a pattern into the bark, deep enough to wake the tree but not to kill it. I smear the black paste into the wound. The tree reacts, rushing defenses to the area, hardening the sap, knitting the fibers into a dome of calcified wood. In an hour, it will be a hard, bone-white shell, a temporary nest anchored in the living flank of the world.

I wait. The sun presses down, a physical weight that bends my shoulders.

I watch them, my large, dark eyes tracking their movement. They are not prey; they are fellow swimmers in this ocean of air. We are all just suspended particles in the belly of the giant.

My skin begins to glow, a soft, amber rhythm of contentment. The spore storm breaks, washing over me in a wave of dust that tickles my ridges. I pull my limbs close, tucking myself into the calcified bubble I’ve induced. The vibration of the world thrums against my back, a lullaby of consumption.

I close my eyes, though it makes no difference in the perpetual golden haze. I am full. I am anchored. I am a parasite with manners, a guest in the lung of the world. I drift into the trance, my light pulsing in time with the slow, heaving breath of the tree.

I am here. I am Osmotic. I am the itch that reminds the world it is alive.
