I am the interval.

Not the microsecond of displacement the Shakers exploit, nor the tectonic shriek of a Monolith-Waker's stride, nor even the whip-crack of a Drill-Vine claiming its prize. I am the space between these violences. The held breath. The comma in the sentence of catastrophe.

I came here meaning to understand the plateau. I was younger then, and believed that endurance was a form of comprehension. I brought instruments to measure the resonance of the monoliths, notebooks to catalog the blink-patterns of the swarms, collection vials for the alkaline dust. I believed the weirdness could be archived, that the terrarium could be decoded from a safe, clinical distance.

The pale sun disabused me of that notion. It illuminated my foolishness with the same flat, indifferent clarity it gives to everything here. In a laboratory, there is control. Here, there is only the next interval.

I do not walk. Walking is too consistent—a rhythm, a pattern, a heartbeat. The Drill-Vines drink vibration, and the most delicious vibration is the steady, reliable pulse of a thing that believes itself safe. I *lurch*. I move in asymmetric bursts, a chaotic punctuation that leaves no time for a vine to lock onto my rhythm. I pause for seven seconds between steps, then three, then eleven, then one. I fall forward and catch myself on palms that are now thick with callouses and the metallic dust of oxidized ages. I make myself an erratic signal, a statistical noise in the chaos. The vines cannot find me. They whip through the air where my head *should* be, where any reasonable creature's head would be, and I am already somewhere else, falling sideways into a hollow space that feels a mile wide even though the cliff wall is close enough to touch.

The emptiness here is a kind of architecture. The distances lie, but they lie *generously*. I have learned to read the exaggeration of space, to find the pockets of stretched air where the Silence-Weavers cannot reach. They cling to the undersides of cliffs, hunting the margins where sound dies. They feed on the absence, on the quiet between roars. So I give them nothing. I keep my terror loud—not a scream, which is too predictable, but a low, tuneless humming that I shift into whenever the wind drops, whenever the world threatens to go quiet. My hum is ugly and arrhythmic, a vibration with no harmonic value, nothing for a Weavers' webbed hands to smother. It is a shield made of refuse. I hate the sound of it. I hate that it keeps me alive.

I have become something the plateau understands. Not a Shaker—I cannot blink through atoms, cannot drink the decibels and displace. Not a Stone-Person—I have no rusted god to inhabit, no anti-gravity field to cradle my softness. Not a vine, anchored and drilling. I am something else. A *Gap-Reader*. I read the intervals between the catastrophes and I live in them.

My body has learned the grammar of the place. My lungs are scarred from the razor-cold air, but they have also grown *sensitive*. I can feel when a monolith is about to engage—the pressure changes, the dust begins its impossible spiral a half-second before the surface flows with liquid light. I have learned to stand near the rusted archways where time thickens, where a half-second stretches into a minute of subjective safety. I let the temporal distortion cradle me. Near the oldest ruins, I can rest. My heartbeat slows to something almost peaceful, and the vines cannot sense me through the warped time. I close my eyes and I exist in a bubble of dilated duration, a pocket of almost-stillness in a world that will not tolerate stillness.

But I cannot stay. The archways are doorways to nowhere, and the time-thickening is not a shelter but a *delay*. Eventually, the distortion fades, or the monolith grinds onward, and I must lurch back into the chaos.

I have found a kind of communion with the Shakers. They do not acknowledge me—I am too slow, too present, too *real*—but I have learned their patterns. When the wind reaches its crescendo, when the hypersonic gale becomes a physical wall that presses against my chest like a buried boulder, I watch the swarms. They vibrate at the edge of visibility, their chitin blurring into a haze of cold blue light, and then—*crack*—they are elsewhere. I cannot follow them, but I can *borrow* their absence. In the instant they vanish, there is a gap in the air, a momentary vacuum where the wind's pressure does not touch. I step into the space the Shakers leave behind. It lasts less than a second, but it is enough. I move forward without the wind's resistance. I am a parasite of their teleportation, a hitchhiker on their quantum hesitation.

The Drill-Vines and I have an understanding, which is to say we have a relationship of attempted murder and successful evasion. They are beautiful, in their terrible way. The Fibonacci spirals of their leaves, the bruised-purple waxy surfaces, the copper-bright cables of their stems—they are mathematics made flesh, geometry that hungers. I have watched them drill into the polymerized ground, those diamond-hard grinders seeking the tectonic tremors, and I have felt a kinship. We are both anchored to this place by vibration. The difference is that they consume it, and I *flee* it. I have seen what happens when a vine finds a heartbeat. A colleague—there were others, once—stood too still, measuring the resonance of a distant spire. The vine wrapped his ankle. He screamed, and the Shakers blinked away from the noise, and the Silence-Weavers came hunting the quiet after. The vine did not eat him. It simply *joined* him—drilled into his bone, anchored itself, and began to pulse in time with his terror. He is still there, I think, at the edge of the plateau, a calcified mass of copper and teal, still pulsing, still feeding the vine his fear. The plateau does not kill you. It *incorporates* you.

I have seen the Stone-People move. Not often—three times, perhaps four. They are so slow that you must watch them not with eyes but with patience, tracking the shift of a shadow, the migration of a rusted limb across the tessellated ground. They are the shepherds, the text said, guiding the vibrations to feed the vines. But I do not think they are shepherds. I think they are *mourners*. They inhabit the skins of dead engines, flexing the ruined limbs of machines that once reshaped worlds. When a monolith grinds its way three feet to the left, it is not hunting or herding—it is *grieving*. It is trying to return to a configuration that made sense, a layout from before the plateau was a terrarium, before the mad god sealed the box. They communicate in gravity-shapes, in dust spirals, in non-Euclidean helices, and I have stood in the wake of their passing and felt the shapes wash over me. They do not speak to me. I am too small, too brief, too kinetic. But I feel the *meaning* of the shapes, and the meaning is *remember*. Remember when we were whole. Remember when we had purpose beyond endurance.

I cannot remember. I was never whole to begin with. I came here fragmented, searching for coherence, and the plateau has only refined my fragmentation into a survival strategy.

The light is the worst part. That pale, indifferent sun, shining down with the clarity of a laboratory lamp. It sees me. It sees my lurching, asymmetrical progress across the stone. It sees the dust motes dancing in the storm. It sees the chrome-locusts blinking through impossible geometries. It sees the vines whipping and the monoliths grinding and the Weavers clinging and the Stone-People grieving. And it does not care. It illuminates without judgment, without mercy, without end. There is no night here. There is no shadow deep enough to hide in, only the warped shadows near the active ruins, where light bends around the gravity fields. I have learned to navigate by the bending of light, to read the gravitational lensing like a map. When the dust spirals in impossible helices, I follow the spiral inward, toward the monolith, toward the thickened time, toward the only shelter this place offers.

I am not native. I never will be. The natives are the interval-dwellers—the Shakers in their microsecond gaps, the Vines in their cracks, the Stone-People in their husks, the Weavers in their silences. I am something else: a *guest* who has overstayed, who has learned the language but will never speak it fluently, who survives by mimicry and luck and the refusal to stop moving.

I cling to this place, because there is nowhere else. The cliffs drop into chemically luminescent fog, and the fog is not a fall but a *dissolution*—I have seen things blink into the fog and not return, not even as the plateau's incorporated calcified horrors. The plateau is a room, and I am trapped in it, and the room is trying to kill me, and I am surviving, and the survival is not a victory but a *postponement*.

But sometimes—rarely, and only when the wind drops between roars and the silence threatens and I hum my ugly hum to keep the Weavers at bay—sometimes I feel something else. A vibration beneath the vibrations. Not the tectonic grind of a monolith, not the kinetic feast of a vine, not the quantum shiver of a Shaker. Something deeper. Something *still*.

The plateau is ancient and unmoving, the text said. It has finished its business and is waiting for the end. And I think—when the chaos subsides for a half-second, when the interval opens wide enough to permit a thought—that I feel the waiting. The plateau is not trying to kill me. It is not trying to do anything. It is simply *being*, with a dignity and a patience that my frantic survival cannot touch. The violence is not malice; it is just what happens when things live in the margins of a place that has already decided to die.

And I—clinging to the interval, lurching through the chaos, reading the gaps and borrowing the absences—I am learning, very slowly, to wait with it. Not to survive. Not to endure. Just to *be*, for a moment, in the stillness that underlies all the storm.

The wind roars. The monoliths grind. The vines whip. The Shakers blink. The Weavers smother. And I take a step, and the ground vibrates, and I find the next interval, and I live.
