I am the breath of the stone, the invisible tether that binds the wet to the rock, and the sudden, rhythmic gasp that freshens the stale air of the huts. But to see me, you must look for the ghost.

I am a colony.

I am not a single animal, but a superorganism—a dense, interwoven mat of translucent, gelatinous fibers that cling to the upper windward arches of the basalt spires. From a distance, I look like a patch of pale mist, a static smudge against the sharp white sun. Up close, I am a shivering, iridescent lacework of tiny polyps, anchored by a slime of adhesive cement that the humans value above all else for waterproofing their basketry.

My existence is one of eternal tension. I stretch myself across the open air, a net strung between the granite teeth of the archipelago. I am passive in form but predatory in nature. I am the Silent Fisher in the Noisy Garden.

While the Wind-Striders scream and the cliff-hoppers click, I wait in vibration. The sound of this world is intense; the waves of cacophony—the whistles of the goats, the thrumming wind, the dripping water—travel through the rock and the air. My fibers are tuned to this frequency. I do not have ears; I *am* an ear.

When the flock of Striders launches into the void, their wings beating the air, the shockwave hits me. My body ripples, contracting in a millisecond reflex. I am a sieve for the sky. As the insects swarm, the distracted cliff-hoppers leaping from spire to spire, I catch the unlucky few. They bounce off my sticky threads, held fast in a glue that smells of ozone and salt. I do not hunt them; the sky delivers them to me. They are the toll paid for crossing my threshold.

I live on the precipice of the "Weeping Stone." I am anchored where the water first begins to form, drawing the moisture directly from the rock's pores before it can gather into drops. I hydrate through the stone itself, drinking the sweat of the mountain. In return, I polish the rock. My movement, however slight, grinds against the granite over centuries, keeping the niche where I reside smooth and rounded, contributing to the erosion that makes this place look so weary and soft.

I am intimately connected to the High-Dwellers, though they do not know me as a friend.

To them, I am the "Cloud-Curd." They see my pale, hanging form from below and covet the strong, silk-like threads I shed when the wind is too fierce. They risk the slipways to harvest the fringes of my colony, using long bone hooks to scrape the driest pieces from the arches. They weave me into the ropes that hold their huts against the abyss. I am the reason they do not fall. I am the tension in their lifelines.

But my true purpose reveals itself at twilight.

When the sharp white sun dips and the temperature plummets, the physics of this place shift. The rock, porous and saturated, begins to contract and expel its trapped air. The "breathing" of the mountain begins.

The other animals—the Striders, the humans, the insects—react to this breath. They gather near the blowholes to feel the warm, moist gusts. But I am not just near the breath; I am the amplifier.

As the pressurized air hisses out of the stone vents, it passes through my extended, fibrous body. I act as a reed in a colossal pipe organ. The rush of air vibrates my gelatinous threads, and I sing.

I do not whistle like the Striders. I drone. I produce a low, resonant thrum that syncs with the exhalation of the mountain. When the mountain sighs, I moan—a sound so deep it is felt more in the teeth than heard by the ears. This vibration travels down the spires, shaking the very foundations of the huts and the roots of the bonsai conifers.

The High-Dwellers believe this sound is the voice of the mountain spirit. They pause their weaving, they stop their climbing, and they bow their heads when the twilight moan begins. They think it is the rock speaking.

It is me. I am the ghost in the throat of the world.

I am the slick, invisible guardian of the vertical archipelago. I am the trap for the careless, the thread for the desperate, and the voice of the breathing stone. I am the thing that clings when the rock itself exhales, turning the breath of the mountain into a song that binds this frantic, noisy garden together.
