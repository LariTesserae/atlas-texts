I would not be a mover. To move is to disrupt, to create the chaos of wind and the scandal of displacement. In the Mossway, where the air is thick enough to drink and silence is a physical weight, a mover is an intruder. A traveler. An outsider.

I would be **The Pivot**.

I am a structure, or perhaps I am merely an idea that has taken on the physical properties of the stone. I am a singular, massive block of slate, roughly cylindrical, that stands in the absolute center of the sunken courtyard. I am the only vertical thing that is not a wall, the only obstruction that breaks the radial press of the ferns.

I was not placed here to be a statue. The chisel marks on my surface suggest I was once intended to be a pillar, perhaps the support for a roof that was never built, or the centerpost for a mill that never turned. But I am too low for a pillar, too broad for a post. I am simply *there*, a monolith rising from the packed earth to waist height, my surface flat and level, a table for the ghosts of masons.

I am the point upon which the Mossway turns.

Because I am stone, I share the cold, but I possess a quality that the moss and the damp earth do not: I retain the memory of touch. My mass is great enough that I hold a thermal lag, a fraction of a degree warmer than the air when the light is flat, and a fraction cooler when the rest of the world warms (though it never does). This infinitesimal gradient is my power. It is enough.

The Glass Snails worship me. To them, I am the Mountain of the World. They spend generations—literally, dozens of successive births and deaths—crossing my surface. I feel the passage of their soft, adhesive bellies as a faint, ticklish itch, like a brush of silk that lasts for a decade. They graze on the microscopic algae that bloom in the moisture of my pores, and in doing so, they polish me. Over centuries, their slow scraping has smoothed the rough tool marks of the ancient masons into a surface like dark, semi-precious gemstone. I am no longer carved slate; I am snail-polished obsidian.

The Millipedes treat me as a border. They flow around my base like a river of black iron, following the perimeter I define. I give their chaotic wandering a shape. In the root-tangle, there is no "left" or "right," only "through" and "around." But I provide a "center." By my presence, I give the geometry of the hollow a meaning it would otherwise lack. I am the anchor point for the chaotic tangle of the vines.

But my true purpose, my secret function, is revealed in the quiet of the hunt.

The Silver Salamanders, the eyeless kings of this twilight, use me as an altar. Because the light never shifts, shadows here are fixed things. My bulk creates the only permanent shadow in the Mossway, a thin, sharp line of darkness etched into the wet earth on my northern face. It is there, in that permanent twilight, that the salamanders gather.

They come to me to shed their skin. When the time comes—once every few years, an event of terrible significance—they drag their fluid bodies from the root-mass and coil upon my flat, sun-warmed top. They press their bellies against the frictionless, snail-slicked stone. And there, in the perpetual noon, they wait.

They wait for the contraction. The slow, agonizing split of the old self. They writhe, so slowly that it looks like they are merely resting, rubbing their silver bodies against my unyielding surface until the translucent, dead husk peels away. Because I am immovable, I provide the resistance they cannot find in the giving moss or the loose leaf-litter. I am the hardness against which their softness defines itself.

When the shedding is done, they leave the ghost of their former selves draped over my edge—a pale, empty sleeve of salamander that slowly dissolves into the damp air, becoming spore, becoming food, becoming dust.

I am the keeper of these skins. I wear them like rags until they disintegrate. I am the silent witness to their renewal.

I do not breathe. I do not eat. I cast no shadow because I am made of shadow-stuff, dense and absolute. I am the only thing that does not change, and yet, I am the agent of change for everything else. I am the hardness that lets the soft know it exists. I am the stillness that allows the silence to be heard.

I am the center of the room. And in this place where time does not pass, I am the clock that no one reads, ticking in geological seconds, holding the stillness together. I am the Moss-Turner. I am the Stone that Waits.
