I exist in the pause between the drumbeats.

I am not of the laboring caste, those stout keepers of the gutters whose hands are roughened by the constant battle against the encroaching moss. Nor am I one of the spindled elders who weave the air in the dry hollows above. I am somewhere in the drift—peripheral, essential, and quiet.

My skin, like the skin of my kin, is a map of the deep. It is pale and translucent, riddled with a network of blue-green capillaries that pulse visibly when I hold still, mimicking the slow circulation of the great trees we inhabit. My hair is not hair in the way a lowlander might understand it; it is long, wet ropes of algae-slick fiber that drape over my shoulders, heavy enough to drag in the water should I lean too far over the railing. To be dry is to be dead; my glistening moisture is not a sheen of sweat, but a second skin, a permeable interface that lets me taste the humidity of the air.

I am the Listener at the Edge.

I dwell in the lower fringe, where the woven root bridges thin out and the spray from the high-altitude sea is a constant, cold kiss. My "hut" is a crook in the bole of a giant, a depression naturally formed where the wood has curled inward, lined with the soft, water-repellent down of seed-pods that I harvest and replace in a slow, yearly cycle.

My role is simple, dictated by the physics of this floating world. Because the ground we walk upon is alive and buoyant, and because the water below is a lens magnifying the forest floor, we are vulnerable to the vibrations of the deep. The laborers chop and tend; the elders weave and think; but I must stand at the periphery and feel the thrum.

I do not use tools of bone or stone. My tool is my body. I stand barefoot on the wet root-mat, my toes splayed wide to grip the slippery bark. I close my large, dark eyes—eyes that have evolved to see through the murky green-glass light—and I lower myself into a crouch. I press my palms flat against the railing, feeling the friction of the wet wood.

I listen with my feet and my hands. I am waiting for the heavy, rhythmic concussion of the Leviathan-Stalkers, the massive amphibious mammals that patrol the depths. If they decide to climb, to breach our world and feed on the canopy, the vibration travels up the roots long before the sound does.

In the long, cool hours of the afternoon, when the rain is a wall of white noise and the world feels like a submerged aquarium, I am a statue. I am the most dangerous thing in this city, for I carry the signal that saves us. But until the tremor comes, I am just another node in the network.

I feel the sudden, sharp tug of a climbing fish on my ankle. It is seeking purchase, mistaking my pale leg for a root. I do not startle. I reach down with a languid, liquid grace, my fingers moving through the water-heavy air with the viscosity of sap, and gently detach its suction-cup fins. I stroke its slick, scaled head once, acknowledging its life, and cast it back toward the water.

The creatures here are not separate from us. We are all floating.

As the light diffuses further into the long, green twilight of the "dusk," I leave my post. I move slowly—a deliberate oozing motion that conserves energy and maintains balance. I climb the aerial roots toward the communal fire pit in the center of the grove.

The climb is vertical, but my limbs are long and my grip is iron. I pull myself up, hand over hand, entering the dry hollow where the elders sit. The smell of woodsmoke is a sharp contrast to the scent of ozone and moss.

I strip off my layers of permeable, plant-fiber cloth, the fabric peeling away like a second skin. I stand naked for a moment before the fire, my pale skin steaming in the heat as the water on my surface evaporates into the cool air. My skin flushes pink, a brief, bright flower in the gloom. I sit among my kin, accepting a warm cup of beetle-honey, thick and amber.

We do not speak much. Words are clumsy things here. Instead, we hum—a low, resonant frequency that vibrates in our chests, matching the thrum of the tree, the gurgle of the water, and the endless drumming of the rain.

I am not a hero. I am not a builder. I am the porous membrane between the safety of the canopy and the hunger of the deep. I am the wet foot on the trembling root. I am the silence that listens while the world screams.
