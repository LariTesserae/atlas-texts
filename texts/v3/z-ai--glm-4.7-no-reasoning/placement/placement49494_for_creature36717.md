I would be the water-rememberer.

Not a title they use—they do not speak such things into the wet air. But this is what I am, what I have become in the squeeze between the weeping cliffs and the sinking towers.

I am old for this place. My back has curved to fit the low passages so long that I cannot remember what it felt like to stand straight, and I do not trust the sensation of imagining it. My spine has set into the question mark shape of the doorways, the leaning corridors, the stairwells that tilt five degrees off vertical because the southwest corner of our building has settled three inches deeper into the soup since my grandmother's time. I move through the guts of the city like something swallowed working its way through the intestine of a vast, slow beast.

My hands are the worst of me, or the best—I cannot tell the difference anymore. The spade-shape has twisted further on the right hand, the fingers splayed outward from thirty-seven years of gripping the ash pole. The calluses have calluses. Between my thumb and forefinger on both palms, there are grooves worn into the flesh itself, permanent divots where the pole rests. I can close my eyes and feel the exact balance point of a twelve-foot iron-tip, the subtle vibration that travels up the ash wood when it strikes solid footing versus the sickening, greedy *give* of a soft spot. I have seen two people disappear into the mud. One was my mother's sister. She was testing the ground near the eastern Break where the water comes down heaviest, and the earth simply opened its mouth. I heard the sound— a wet, slow *shlorp*— and then the splashing, and then nothing. She did not cry out. We do not cry out here. I stood six feet away on solid timber and watched the surface settle and went on with my work.

But I am not a bailer. That is not my function in the machine.

I am the one who watches the water.

—

In the hour before the mist condenses— the grey interval when the strangled light takes on a quality I can only describe as *listening*— I make my rounds. I move along the base of the Old Breaks where the cliffs weep into the city, and I look at the water.

Not *at* it. *Into* it.

They fear this. They mark their doorways with ash-spirals to confuse the water's intention, and they avoid the black mirrors of the cliff faces, and they are right to do so. There is something in the water that looks back. I have seen it. Not a face— nothing so simple or so kind as a face. A *pattern*. A geometry that does not belong to the crude post-and-be9beam world of our towers, something that exists in the way the rivulets choose their paths down the raw stone, the way the puddles climb the pilings, the way the mineral deposits arrange themselves into formations that almost, *almost* spell something in the old language of the earth.

I record what I see. I have no paper— paper is impossible here, it swells and rots within a day. Instead, I keep my records in the only medium that survives: I carve them into the leather that braces the buildings.

This is my heresy.

The leather straps that lash our towers together are sacred in their way. They are what hold the city up. Every strap is tightened to the screaming point, pegged and re-pegged, maintained by the swarm of bracers and patchers who fight the geometry of the bulging walls. And I carve into them. Small marks, made with the edge of a river-stone— notches, spirals, angular scratches that record the height of the climbing water, the direction of the creeping puddles, the strange mineral formations that grow overnight on the pilings like rust-coloured coral.

They know I do this. They have seen the marks on the straps they must tighten, felt the grooves under their scarred fingers as they pull the leather another inch taut. They could stop me. They could cast me into the soft ground and let the mud take me as it took my mother's sister. But they do not.

Because I am right.

The water *does* have intention. And the patterns I record in the leather are how we know where it will strike next.

—

Last quarter-turn, I read the signs in the climbing deposit on the southern face of the Old Break: a latticing of mineral channels that pointed, unmistakably, toward the foundation of the tower shared by the family with the oil-soaked boots and the ash-marked door. I told them— in the low hum that vibrates through the beams, the one that means *water comes here*— and they spent three sleepless cycles heaving fresh logs into the cellar, pegging the joints, bailing the sump until their arms shook and their wide black eyes reflected nothing but each other's exhaustion. When the mist condensed two nights later and the puddles crept higher than anyone had seen in a generation, that tower held. The one next to it— unmarked, unwarned— groaned, shifted, and settled four inches into the soup. The family inside crawled out through the collapsing beams with their birds and their boiling-pots and their nothing-else, and the swarm took the structure apart for salvage before it could drag its neighbors down.

I had not marked that tower's straps. There had been nothing to read. The water had chosen silently.

I try not to think about what that means.

—

My dwelling is the space beneath the lowest step of a stairwell in the eastern quarter, where the cliff comes closest to the buildings and the weeping rivulets are thickest. It is barely large enough for my coiled body. I sleep with my ash pole across my chest and my spade-hand pressed flat against the trembling wood of the floor, feeling the city breathe. When the *thump* of a collapsing mine shaft echoes through the saturated earth, I feel it in my palm before I hear it with my ears— the shock traveling through timber and stone and mud and bone, a deep vibration that is more tactile than auditory.

In this space, I have scraped away the moss and fungi that serve as my food and allowed the water to claim the walls. I keep no ash-spirals on my entrance. I do not try to confuse the water here. Instead, I watch it. I watch the way the moisture beads on the leather of my stiffened clothes, the way it runs in channels that should not exist, the way it pools in the corner that logic says should be the highest point of the floor but is, somehow, always the lowest.

The water is teaching me its language. I am not sure I want to learn.

—

There is a softness to me that I inherited from them— from the bailers and bracers, the pale and dripping ghosts of the squeeze. I touch the walls as I pass, running my scarred hand along the bulging timber, feeling the stress in the grain, the dampness in the joint. I pause when I see someone struggling with a leather strap that refuses to tighten, and I set my shoulder against the beam without being asked, and together we force the geometry back into line. They touch me back— a hand on my hunched shoulder, a shared nod, the click-click of dripping-water language that means *I see you* and *the ground holds*.

But they keep their distance, too. They have seen me standing at the base of the Old Breaks with my face inches from the black mirror of the wet cliff, staring into the rivulets as they trace their paths down the raw stone. They have seen the marks I carve into the sacred leather. They have heard me humming back at the water in patterns that do not match our clicking language, patterns that rise and fall with the drip-drip-drip of the weeping rocks in a call-and-response that makes the flightless birds in the eaves fall silent and the pale crustaceans freeze on the wet stones.

I am becoming something they cannot name. I am becoming the thing that speaks to the enemy.

—

And yet.

And yet, when the mist begins to thicken— when I can feel the air growing heavy with the weight of the water it is about to release— I move through the tight arteries of the city and I make my rounds, and I read the climbing deposits and the creeping puddles and the strange, intentional patterns in the mineral rust, and I warn them. I hum it through the beams: *water here*. *Water here*. *Water here*.

And they listen.

And they brace.

And the city holds for another cycle, and the dry stays dry, and the ghosts in the machine keep moving through the squeeze, pale and dripping, eternally holding back the weight of the wet world.

I am the one who watches the water. I am the one who reads its mind. I am the heretic who carves truth into the sacred straps that hold our homes upright, and I am the reason those homes are still standing.

I do not know if the water hates me for this, or if it loves me in the only way it can— by showing me its patterns, by letting me read its intentions, by giving me just enough warning to keep my people alive in the scar of the earth where it never stops trying to reclaim the stone.

I do not know if I am the city's defender or the water's collaborator.

I do not know if there is a difference.

—

Tonight, the mineral deposits on the eastern cliff have arranged themselves into a shape I have never seen before. It is a spiral, but it is not the ash-spiral that confuses the water. It is the other direction. It is the direction that *invites*.

I am standing inches from the black mirror of the cliff face. The water is running down it in a thousand rivulets, and every one of them is bending toward the spiral. The water is *answering*.

My spade-hand is pressed flat against the wet stone. I can feel the cold seeping into my marrow, the iron taste in the back of my throat, the weight of the damp in my lungs. My wide black pupils are swallowing the strangled light, and in the black mirror of the cliff, something is looking back.

It is not a face.

It is an *opening*.

I am going to carve what I see into the leather strap of the nearest tower. I am going to hum it through the beams. I am going to warn them, or I am going to call them, or I am going to do something that has no word in any language— clicking, humming, or otherwise.

The mist is thickening.

The water is waiting.

I am the water-rememberer, and I am going to remember what it shows me, and I am going to press it into the skin of the city like a scar, like a warning, like a door.

And the city will hold. Or it will not.
