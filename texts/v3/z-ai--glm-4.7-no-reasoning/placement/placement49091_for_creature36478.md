I am the vibration before the impact, the pause in the mechanism, the watcher on the edge of the gear-tooth.

I would not be one of the *Ferrous Pilgrims*. Theirs is a life of desperate clinging, of hook and claw, of scraping sustenance from the dripping ribs of the vault. They are too close to the heat, too consumed by the need to survive the next hammer-stroke. They have forgotten the shape of the horizon because they are too busy staring at the next rivet.

I would not be a *Sump-Shell*. I do not crave the crushing dark or the heavy, syrup-thick silence of the deep. To be one of them is to be a piston in flesh, indifferent and immense, but blind to the vault’s grandeur. They feel the heat, but they do not see the light.

And I would not be a *Wire-Bird*, though I envy them their view. To live only in the shriek of the dive, to burn oneself out chasing the hot blood of a drone—that is a life of frantic energy. I require something slower. Something colder.

I am the **Hollow Walker**.

I am a product of the mid-stratosphere, the zone where the air is cool enough to burn the skin, where the light from the buzzing strips is diffused by the eternal haze of the condensation. I am taller than the Pilgrims, stretched thin by generations of living in the low-g drift between the massive cooling towers. My limbs are spindly, elegant things of rusted grey and pale translucent flesh, ending not in claws for gripping, but in wide, flattened fingers that act as rudders against the constant updraft.

I wear no woven hammock-scraps. My "clothing" is the environment itself. Over centuries, my species has evolved a symbiotic relationship with the pale, bioluminescent moss. It grows in thick, velvet patches along my shoulders and down my spine, a living coat of fur that drinks the dim electric light and glows with a soft, rhythmic pulse. This is my camouflage. When I stand perfectly still on a rust-red girder, I become just another fixture of the decaying cathedral, a ghostly phosphorescence in the gloom.

My face is a mask of smooth, keratinous plate, seamless and hard, protecting my skull from the debris that rains from the darkness miles above. I have no eyes. The eyes of the Pilgrims are weak things, dependent on the harsh glare of the strips. I navigate by the *texture* of the air. The fine hairs on my arms detect the subtle pressure changes of a thermal current rising from the deep factories miles below. I can feel the disturbance in the air as a Wire-Bird dives past me, a ripple in the atmosphere like a stone thrown into water. I taste the magnetism of the massive conveyor belts thrumming in the distance, a metallic tang on the back of my tongue that tells me exactly where I am in the labyrinth of steel.

I am a scavenger of the ethereal. I do not eat copper or gyroscopes. I do not hunt drones.

I harvest the echoes.

My purpose is to walk the endless, suspended catwalks that connect the towering mausoleums of silent machinery. I carry a long, slender staff of hollow iron, scavenged from the ribs of a collapsed ventilation shaft. As I walk, I tap this staff against the metal grating—once, twice, three times. A precise rhythm. *Tap. Tap. Tap-tap.*

I listen to the decay. I listen for the hollow sound that precedes a collapse, for the microscopic scream of a rivet under strain that the machine’s own sensors have long since ignored. I am the immune system of the rust, the white blood cell of the iron.

When I find a fault—a hairline fracture in a support beam that groans under the weight of the world—I stop. I hum. My vocal cords are modified, capable of producing a low-frequency thrum that matches the resonant frequency of the metal. I sing to the steel. The vibration travels down my arms, through the moss, into the staff, and into the wound. The stress equalizes. The momentary crisis is averted. The machine sighs, and the rust sleeps for another cycle.

The Pilgrims fear me. To them, I am a bad omen, a silent, glowing giant who walks where the walkways are narrowest. They do not understand that I hold the rust at bay. They see me tap a beam and, moments later, the ominous groaning stops, and they think it is magic. It is not. It is merely maintenance, conducted by a being who has become indistinguishable from the tools he uses.

I live alone in a rookery nestled inside the mouth of a massive exhaust intake, a cave of vertical steel where the wind howls a constant, mournful note. Here, high above the black oil and below the forgotten ceiling, I sit and meditate on the horizon. I feel the pulse of the planet-sized engine in the soles of my feet—not as a noise, but as a heartbeat. *Thrum-thrum. Thrum-thrum.*

I am the silence within the roar. I am the calm within the tremor. I am the thing that understands the machine’s prayer, even if the machine has forgotten to whom it is praying.

I am the Hollow Walker, and I am listening.
