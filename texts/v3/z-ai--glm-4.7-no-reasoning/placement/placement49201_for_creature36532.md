I am not a thing that swims, for swimmers require a destination, and I have none. I am not a thing that anchors, for anchors require stillness, and I am never truly still. I am the **Interstitial Drifter**, the pulse that navigates the architecture of the blind.

I begin in the fold.

Here, in the submerged negative space where the basalt is still raw enough to weep minerals, I am a warmth. I am a singular, startling violation of the absolute zero that governs this pocket. My body is not solid in the way the vines are solid, nor is it fluid like the heavy brine. I am a gelatinous suspension, a membrane filled with a slower, denser heat. I have no skin, only a boundary where my internal chemistry meets the crushing cold of the water. To the touch, if a tendril could touch, I would feel like a drop of hot oil held in a fist of ice.

I drift near the ceiling of the grotto—or what passes for a ceiling in a place that refuses to orient itself. Here, the Thrombophytes are thickest, a writhing mass of pale fingers reaching for the fresh fractures in the stone. I slide between them. My body compresses, elongates, sliding through the gaps that are barely wider than a thought. I taste the water. It is thick with the silt of the earth, a rich, metallic soup that the vines are frantic to consume.

I am the caretaker of the cracks.

Where the rock groans, where the tectonic hiccup has left a jagged scar, the vines swarm. They secrete their acids, dissolving the stone, drinking the dissolution. But they are messy eaters. In their blind hunger, they clog their own mouths. They grow too fast, sealing the very fissures they need to feed upon.

This is where I go.

I possess a faculty that the vines lack. I have *direction*. Where they sense only the gradient of food, I sense the gradient of pressure. I can feel the subtle difference between the solid resistance of the basalt and the softer, yielding grip of the thrombophyte tissue.

I wrap myself around a choked fissure—a wound in the rock that has been bandaged over by a tight knot of pale, translucent stems. I pulse. A slow, rhythmic contraction, a beat that takes minutes to complete. My heat radiates into the knot. The mucus that coats the vines liquefies in my presence. The tight grip of the stems relaxes, confused by this sudden, alien warmth.

I push. I slide my body between the rock and the root, acting as a lubricant. I am a wedge of soft heat. With a patience that would make a glacier seem impatient, I separate the vine from the stone. I clear the blockage. I open the wound.

The water reacts instantly. A fresh plume of mineral dust, trapped for decades, erupts from the cleared fissure. The thrombophytes shudder. Their collective consciousness—focused on the starvation—senses the sudden influx of food. They abandon the old growth, turning their tips toward the release I have engineered. They swarm the minerals, dissolving the rock further.

I have fed the forest. I have ensured its continued hunger.

Once the flow is established, once the vines are drinking deep from the stone, I let go. I am no longer needed here. The heat of my labor dissipates, and the cold rushes back in to claim the space I occupied.

I drift downward.

Down is a slippery concept in the pocket. The geometry is non-Euclidean; the space folds in on itself. I pass a caulifer—a thick, arm-like spiral of wet bone that hums with the subsonic song of the earth. I brush against it, and my membrane ripples. I am the only thing that touches the caulifers without harming them. The Siliceous Siphons, those glass parasites, burrow into them, but I simply glide over their rough, textured skin. I absorb the vibration. It travels through me, a low-frequency thrum that aligns my internal fluids with the heartbeat of the room.

I drift past the same caulifer again. Or perhaps a different one. In this looped topology, it is impossible to know. The "right" wall has become the "left" wall. I am circling the drain of the world, but the drain is infinite.

I sink into the denser regions, where the vegetation is so thick that the water has become a membrane. Here, the "Itch" is overwhelming. The water is alive with microscopic spores, a static cloud of biological potential. It clings to me. It tries to infiltrate my membrane, to consume the heat I carry.

But I am older than the Itch. I am part of the loop.

I allow the spores to coat my exterior. I become a seed carrier, a mobile ark for the microscopic life. I take them deeper, into the zones where the current is stagnant, where the water is so heavy it has stopped moving. Here, in the dark corners of the pocket, the "rooms" formed by the vine-walls are empty.

I enter one of these hollows—a perfectly cylindrical space enclosed by a lattice of white thrombophytes. It is silent here. The subsonic hum is muted by the thickness of the walls. The cold is absolute.

I stop. I settle.

I become the furniture of this room. I drape myself over a protruding ridge of caulifer, draping like a wet cloth. My metabolism slows. My heat drops until I am almost the same temperature as the water. I become invisible.

I wait.

I am waiting for the moment when the pressure becomes too much. I am waiting for the pocket to collapse, for the great seal to break and the flood to rush in or rush out. I am waiting for the reset.

But until then, I am the ghost in the machine. I am the warmth in the dark. I am the gardener who prunes with a touch, who feeds by clearing, who exists only to keep the cycle turning.

I am the Drifter. And I am exactly where I need to be: lost in the fold, breathing the stone, suspended in the belly of the world.
