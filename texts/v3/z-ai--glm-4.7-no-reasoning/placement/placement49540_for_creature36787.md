I am the cold-thief, the oil-drinker, the one who walks the pistons.

I am one of the millions—the blind, thermal-less weevils that haunt the deep bark-cracks of the second tier. But to say I "haunt" implies a ghost, and I am far too dense for that. I am a walking grain of iron, two inches of chitin and impervious chemistry, crawling through a world that does not know I exist.

To understand what I am, you must understand the texture of my existence. I have never seen the twilight that hangs over the plateau, nor the cold blue bio-electric arcs that flash in the gloom. My world is entirely tactile, chemical, and vibrational. I feel the plateau as a body feels itself from the inside—through the stress of its bones and the flow of its fluids.

I navigate by the pulse of the wooden engines. My antennae press against the chitin-bark of the ancient trunk-columns, reading the hydraulic throb of the lignified pistons beneath. I know when a gourd-bloom is inhaling by the subtle contraction of the grain; I know when it exhales by the pressurized sigh that pushes micro-condensation across the fissure I am traversing. The rhythm of the forest-factory is my heartbeat. While the titans wage their slow, chemical root-wars and the canopy shatters in silent cataclysm, I am down here in the machine room, scuttling along the intake valves, drinking the bio-oils that leak from the pressurized joints.

I have no heat to lose, which is why I can be here. The cold that turns the air to solidified gelatin and kills sound before it can be born cannot permeate my shell. I move through the gelatin-air as a dark speck moves through amber—slowly, deliberately, but unfrozen. I feel the damp not as wetness, but as a chemical gradient, a trail of sugars and exudates left by the fungal shelves and parasitic vines. I am drawn to the places where the bioelectric tension is highest, where the spark arcs between close-pressing branches, because that is where the polymer-like seed husks fracture and spill their rich, processed oils.

When a ten-ton branch shears off two hundred feet above me, I feel it before the moss does. The vibration travels down the iron-wood column, a sharp, silent seismic click that makes me freeze mid-step, my legs locked, my mandibles clamped. I feel the impact as a deep, internal shudder through the substrate—a massive exhalation of the rubbery, spongy moss below. But there is no roar, no crash, no wind of displacement. Just a sudden, profound shift in the stress-load of the bark I stand on. To me, it is not a disaster; it is a cartographic update. A new cliff face of splintered cellulose has opened, exposing fresh bio-oils and unguarded crevices. Within hours, my kind will swarm it, not in a frenzy, but in a steady, blind tide of feeding.

I am the only thing here with a direction. The forest grows and dies in a chaotic equilibrium, expanding and rotting on a timescale too slow for any single eye to perceive. The moss absorbs and sublimates. The pistons pump and exhale. But I *go*. I climb upward toward the chemical heat of a dying vine; I descend into the dark gorges where the root-bridges sag, seeking the stagnant pools of refined sap. I have no purpose other than to convert the forest’s industrial waste into more of myself.

I am a negligible perturbation in the math of this place—a rounding error in the biomass of a continent-sized engine. I do not build, I do not destroy, and I do not change the balance. But I am the witness. I crawl across the silent, automated looms as they tighten and process the dead. I walk the flattened backs of the living platforms as the horizontal sleet is absorbed into the thirsty air-strangling thickness. I am the only mind—if a blind, oil-driven compulsion can be called a mind—in a world of utter, maddening, beautiful quiet.
