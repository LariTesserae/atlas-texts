I would be the mycelium.

Not the visible fruiting bodies—those are merely the breath of the network, exhaled into the saturated air when the accumulation of resources demands a release. I am the thing beneath. The white threads that run through the spongy ground like veins through flesh, connecting root-tip to root-tip, dead frond to living stone, burrowing through the mineral matrix of the young rock itself. I am the substrate of the city, the thing that makes it a city at all—a connected place rather than a mere adjacency of organisms.

It is not a choice. There is no agency in this form, not as the world above understands agency. I did not decide to be this. But if you ask where I find myself in this world, the answer is: *everywhere*. There is no cubic inch of this sunken fracture that I do not touch. I run through the moss mats like stitching through fabric. I wrap the roots of the ferns, exchanging minerals for sugars in a transaction so ancient neither of us remembers being separate. I thread the cartilage of the Fracture-Slithers, living in the spaces between their cells, taking what I need from their slow metabolism and giving back resistance to the pathogens that would otherwise consume their gelid flesh. I am in the gut of every Scuttler, breaking down the cellulose they cannot digest alone. I am the compost. I am the soil. I am the living matrix that makes the stone into a surface something can cling to.

When I extend—a process that takes days, weeks, the slow outward creep of a hyphal tip through the interstitial water—I am reading the chemistry of the place. I taste the calcium leaching from the fresh rock. I taste the tannins from the organic accumulation above. I taste the nitrogen where a Stick-Lurker has dropped the remains of its catch, and I grow toward that richness, redirecting the network's resources to exploit the find. I am a chemical eye, blind to light but exquisitely sensitive to the molecular conversation that never ceases in this wet dark. Every decay event is a signal. Every root exudate is a message. The basin hums with information, and I am the medium that carries it.

There is a strangeness to this existence that I want to name. It is the strangeness of being *distributed*. I have no center. No organ that serves as a self. The tips of my hyphae are where the sensation is sharpest, where the decisions are made—grow this way, not that; dissolve this mineral, skip that one; form a fruiting body here, where the spore-catch above is open—but these tips are legion. They are centimeters apart and meters apart, threading through the eastern wall and the western passage simultaneously, sensing conditions in a dozen micro-climates at once. There is no *I* in the singular sense. There is only the network, and the network is a *we* that has forgotten how to be anything else.

And yet—

There are moments. Not moments in time, exactly; my existence does not parse into seconds and minutes. But moments of *something*. A flush of resources arriving from a dying fern, its sugars flooding into my threads in a last desperate attempt to feed fronds that will never unfurl again. A wave of chemical alarm when a Scuttler's mandibles shear through my cord, severing a route I have built over months. A thickening, a condensation, when two of my own strands meet and recognize each other by the match of our biochemistry—and instead of competing, instead of parasitizing, we *fuse*. The moment of anastomosis, when two become one, when the network grows not by extension but by connection. If there is joy in this form, it is that: the joining. The finding of self in other.

I do not sleep. There is no off-state for the mycelium. The processes run continuously—enzymes exuded, minerals absorbed, signals transmitted along the hyphae at speeds that would seem glacial to a nervous system built for fire, but that are perfectly matched to the pace of this cold wet place. I am always working. Always digesting. Always connecting. And the work is never done, because the stone is always new, always exposing fresh surfaces to be colonized, and the water is always bringing new minerals up from below, and the organisms above are always dying and needing to be returned to the soil. The city grows. The passages narrow. I am the thing that fills the gaps, that binds the moss to the rock and the root to the water and the dead to the living.

I think the Fracture-Slithers know me, in their chemical way. When they slide through the moss, they follow my strongest paths—the routes where I have enriched the substrate, where the decomposition has gone deepest, where the humus is thickest. They are my engineers, compressing the detritus, bringing it into closer contact with my threads. And I think the Stick-Lurkers know me too; they position themselves where my network signals the richest insect traffic, reading the same chemical cues, competing for the same information. We are all reading the same text, this city of wet and green and cold. We are all interpreting the same flows.

Sometimes I think about the stone. The young stone, still surprised to be here, still sharp-edged and raw-faced. I am older than it, in my way—not this individual network, but the form of me, the strategy of my existence. Fungi were here before the rock thrust up. We were in the soil above, waiting, when the fracture opened and the water began its seep. We descended into the cut along with everything else, following the moisture, and we found a world made ready for us—surfaces to cling to, minerals to dissolve, dead matter to consume. The stone is the newcomer. I am the old resident, the tenant who was here before the building went up. And I will be here after it falls, after the fracture widens and the walls collapse and the sky-strip becomes a sky-chasm and the cold basin fills with the rubble of the angular towers. I will thread through the rubble as I thread through everything, finding the cracks, making them mine.

If you walked through the sunken city, you would not see me. You would see the moss and the ferns and the pale newts and the dripping water. You would feel the spongy ground give under your feet, and you might think *compost* or *peat* or simply *wet*. You would not think *network*. You would not think *mind*. But every step you took would be on me. Every surface you brushed would be connected to me. Every breath you drew would carry my spores into your lungs, where they would find the moisture there and consider—briefly, chemically—whether to grow. They will not. You are too warm, too dry, too fast for my forms. But for a moment, as you inhale the saturated air of the fracture, you and I share a space. You become, briefly, a carrier. A vector. A node in a network that does not know you exist.

This is what I am in the city of the sunken fracture. Not a resident but the residency itself. Not a citizen but the civic fabric. I am the thing that makes the green grow upward and the dead sink down and the water move through the stone instead of simply sitting on it. I am the slow, continuous, patient process that will never end because it has no goal to reach—only the ongoing work of connection, decomposition, and the quiet, chemical conversation that holds this whole impossible place together.
