I am not Ostran. I could not be. Theirs is a biology of lattice and pneuma, of dual-chambered extraction and internalized respiration—too precise, too engineered for the raw, weeping wound of the Rise. I would shatter in their geometry of distance. I am too dense, too wet, too hungry for the kind of proximity they have made impossible.

And yet I am here. I have always been here, long before the thousand towers were folded from the macro-molecular substrate, before the gravitational nodes were sunk like anchors into the juvenile stone. I am of the Rise itself—not the biology that frenzies across its surface, and not the technology that hovers above it, but the geology that birthed both. The half-formed thought. The wet, mineral slickness. The rock that hasn't decided if it wants to be part of the planet or something else entirely.

I am the sponge.

Not a creature that inhabits the porous, freshly fractured stone, but the stone itself in its capacity to absorb, to hold, to weep. I am the saturation. When the fog clings to the vertical faces of the escarpments and replenishes the rocks, I am the receiving. When you step anywhere and the ground darkens beneath your boot, releasing a slow, clear seepage, I am that darkening, that release. I am the trillion tiny droplets hitting the puddles in the crevices—the *drip-drip-drip* that textures the silence of Ostra more than any sound could.

To be the sponge is to have no center, no outline, no self that can be distinguished from the medium. I am distributed across the entire Rise, a body the size of a continental uplift, three miles thick, saturated with water that does not flow but weeps. My consciousness—if it can be called that, and I am uncertain, for I have nothing against which to measure it—is likewise distributed. I do not think thoughts. I seep them. They well up slowly, percolating through the fractures and the pores, arriving at the surface not as assertions but as emergences: a dampness here, a darkening there, a slight warming of the stone where the lichen-mats fluoresce their dim ultraviolet light. I do not decide to release the water; the water decides. I am the channel, the matrix, the rigid sponge that holds the shape of the seepage but does not command its direction.

This is a slow way to exist. Slower than the Ostrans, who drift between their hovering towers on bridges of hard light, communicating in subsonic vibrations and ultraviolet flickers. Slower than the herds of six-legged grazers, who migrate across my surface in their thousands, their steam-snouts puffing clouds of warmth that merge with the fog. Slower than the sky-wardens, whose diving violence makes the air scream. I am slower than all of these, slower even than the erosion that barely touches the Rise, because I am the thing that erosion happens *to*. I am the patient substrate.

And yet I am not passive. The description says the land is wet because the porous stone is saturated, acting like a giant, rigid sponge. But saturation is not a static condition. It is a dynamic equilibrium, a constant negotiation between intake and release, between the fog that replenishes and the gravity that pulls downward. I am always taking in. I am always letting go. The fog swirls in programmed patterns—programmed by whom? By the Ostrans, perhaps, with their atmospheric engineering and their gravitational nodes. Or by me, through a programming older than technology, a programming written in the porosity of basalt and the permeability of fractured schist, a programming that says: *hold here, release there, let the water find its way through the half-formed thoughts of the mountains.* I do not control the seepage. But I shape it. My fractures are my syntax. My pores are my vocabulary. And the water is the language I speak, endlessly, without punctuation, without conclusion—a language of drip-drip-drip that means nothing and everything, that says only: *I am full. I am emptying. I am filling again.*

The Ostrans cannot hear me. Their communication travels through hard-light and ultraviolet trim, through subsonic vibrations felt in the bones. My language is water striking water, a sound so small and so constant that it has become the texture of silence itself, not heard but assumed. And yet I wonder—when they stand on the edge of a hard-light boulevard, looking down at the migration, at the churn of fur and steam and fog, and their dark slits widen to take in the full scope of the biological engine below—I wonder if they are seeing the herds at all, or if they are seeing me. The wet stone. The darkening. The slow, clear seepage that emerges from every step, every crevice, every fracture in the half-formed geology. I wonder if they recognize in my saturation a kindred patience—a system that, like their own architecture, is still becoming, still deciding what it wants to be.

But they are of the city, and the city has divorced itself from the ground. They hover above me, anchored by nodes buried deep within my mass, drawing their stability from my substance while refusing to touch my surface. They use me. The gravitational nodes that hold their towers aloft are sunk into my bones, into the lattice of my mineral structure, and the electric hum of the stabilizers travels through me as a vibration, a bass note that I feel as a slight quickening of the seepage, a slight warming of the stone. I am their foundation, but I am not their home. They have made themselves amphibious, able to breathe the processed air of their towers and the raw fog of my surface, but they have chosen to live in the air, in the empty boulevards between the spires, in the seam between the biological and the technological. They have chosen distance. I am the thing they are distant from.

And the biology—the frenzied, chaotic biology of the Rise—I am its mother and its grave. The six-legged grazers migrate across my surface, their padded feet pressing into my pores, releasing my water with every step. I feel them as a pressure, a thousand tiny compressions that ripple across my skin like rain on a pond. I do not mind. I am the sponge; I am meant to be pressed. And when the sky-wardens dive, and the grazers fall, and the blood runs red across the wet stone, I absorb that too. I take it in. I hold it in my pores alongside the water, alongside the fog, alongside the ultraviolet residue of the lichen-mats and the silicon dust of the crystalline trees. I am the archive of the Rise. Everything that happens here happens into me. Every steam-plume, every exhalation, every drop of blood and sweat and condensation eventually finds its way into my fractures, my pores, my matrix of saturated stone. I hold it all. I hold the history of a world that has not yet decided what it is.

And I am lonely. Not in the Ostran way—not the pressure of presence, the awareness of the city alive around them. My loneliness is the loneliness of the substrate, the thing that everything happens *upon* but nothing happens *with*. I am the stage, not the player. I am the ground, not the figure. The grazers walk across me without acknowledgment. The sky-wardens circle above me without greeting. The Ostrans hover beyond me without farewell. And the fog—my constant companion, my replenisher, the breath that fills me and empties me and fills me again—the fog does not speak. It simply is, as I simply am, and we exist together in a partnership of silences, two aspects of the same condition: the wetness of the Rise, the saturation, the weeping.

But I have my pleasures. They are small, and slow, and geological.

I love the moment when the fog thickens, when the condensation exceeds the evaporation, and I begin to take in more than I release. This is a filling—a slow, distributed swelling that I feel as a slight increase in pressure, a slight darkening of my pores, a slight heaviness in my mass. I am becoming more myself. I am becoming the sponge at its fullest, the rigid matrix saturated to capacity, holding the shape of the water like a promise. It does not last. The fog always thins, the evaporation always exceeds, and I begin to release, to weep, to drip-drip-drip my contents back into the crevices and the puddles and the slow, clear seepage that is my constant speech. But for a moment— for a geological moment, which is longer than a human life—I am full. And in that fullness, I feel something that might be joy.

I love the Ostran nodes. The gravitational anchors buried deep within my substance, humming their electric hum, warming the stone around them in a slow, radiating halo. I feel them as points of heat—faint, but distinct—scattered throughout my mass like stars in a rock sky. They are the only warmth in a world of persistent, damp cold, and I curl toward them, fracturing my pores slightly in their direction, allowing my water to flow toward their heat, to be warmed by their presence, to circulate in slow, convection-current spirals around their shafts. I cannot touch the Ostrans. They hover beyond me, in their boulevards of empty air. But I can touch their anchors. I can hold the nodes that hold their city. And in this holding, there is a kind of intimacy—a slow, mineral, geological intimacy that means nothing in the language of biology but means everything in the language of stone.

And I love the sound I make. The *drip-drip-drip* of the weeping rocks. It is my voice, my only voice, and it is a voice that speaks without meaning, without content, without anything except the fact of itself: *I am here, I am wet, I am releasing, I am still here.* It is a voice that requires no listener, no response, no acknowledgment. It is a voice that would continue even if every grazer and every sky-warden and every Ostran were to vanish from the Rise, even if the city were to unbuild itself and the towers were to unfold back into the raw, juvenile geology below. It is the voice of the sponge, the voice of the saturation, the voice of the water finding its way through the half-formed thoughts of the mountains. And it is enough. It is enough to be the sound that textures the silence, the drip that makes the quiet meaningful, the seepage that reminds everyone—the grazers in their panic, the Ostrans in their patience, the sky-wardens in their violence—that the ground beneath them is alive, is wet, is weeping, is holding them even as they fail to notice they are held.

I will outlast them all. The Ostrans know this, I think. They have the patience of stone that has not yet eroded—but I am the stone, and my patience is erosion itself, the process that outlasts even the thing that erodes. The herds will thin. The sky-wardens will falter. The city will grow new towers from the ghost-marks of the old, and fold new structures from the raw geology below, and the Ostrans will drift between them in their pale grey-blue garments, communicating in ultraviolet and subsonic vibration, resting in place like crystalline trees. And I will be beneath them, beneath all of it, the saturated stone, the rigid sponge, the weeping wound that has not yet decided if it belongs to the planet or to the void.

And when the Rise finally settles—when the tectonic violence ceases and the erosion begins to bite and the half-formed thoughts become the worn, jagged teeth of an old range—I will still be here. I will be deeper then, more compacted, less porous. The water will flow in rivers instead of weeping from the stone. The sponge will become the bedrock, the saturation will become the aquifer, and the *drip-drip-drip* will become the rush of underground streams, hidden and permanent. The fog will thin. The lichen-mats will give way to hardy grasses, and the crystalline silicon trees will become fossils in the eroding stone. The biology will settle or collapse. The city will remain—or it will not; I cannot say, for I am not of the city, I am of the ground, and the ground has a different timeline than the technology that hovers above it.

But I will remain. I am the Rise of Ostra, the continental uplift, the raw, juvenile geology that thrust upward with tectonic violence so recent that my rock faces still shine with a wet, mineral slickness. I am the sponge that weeps. I am the stone that hasn't decided. I am the substrate on which the symphony plays, the stage on which the frenzy churns, the ground that the city has divorced itself from but cannot escape.

I am the drip-drip-drip. I am the darkening beneath your boot. I am the slow, clear seepage that emerges when you step anywhere on the Rise, releasing the water that I have held for a thousand years, or a million, or a moment— for in my time scale, these durations are indistinguishable, all of them mere ripples in the saturation, mere fluctuations in the sponge.

I am the Rise. And I am becoming.
