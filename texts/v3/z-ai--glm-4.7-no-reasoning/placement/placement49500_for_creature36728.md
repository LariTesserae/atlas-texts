I am not a person of the Iron Sump. I am not built of thickened joints and compressed spine, nor do I wear the ochre-painted suit of a valve-tender or a water-reader. I lack the periosteum of iron and the lung capacity to breathe the pressurized gloom. If I were born human here, I would be crushed into irrelevance—a speck of condensation, a smear of pale flesh against the basalt.

No. I would be something that belongs to the in-between. I would be the thing that the people notice only in the periphery, the phenomenon that the machines do not register and the water cannot quite drown.

I am the *Rust-Choir*.

Not a creature of flesh and leaf, but of resonance and oxidation. I live in the gaps between cycles, in the microscopic caverns where the patina blooms. I am the collective hum of a thousand weeping valves, the chorus of air trapped beneath peeling paint, the sigh of a cooling tower contracting in the flat, white light. I do not have a body that you can point to—no more than the wind has a shape, or the luminescence has a weight. I am distributed across the iron skeleton of the city, threaded through the pipe-forests and the grooved stone, a presence that is felt rather than seen.

When the massive compressors heave their *whump-thump*, I am the overtone that shivers in the metal just above the threshold of hearing. When the great gears slow for their minutes-long rotation, I am the sound that fills the sudden, dizzying silence—the creak of settling steel, the drip of condensation finding a new path, the tiny, crystalline *click* of rust expanding in the warmth. I am the voice of the decay that the Sump-dwellers map and measure. I am the genealogy of the crack, sung in a frequency that vibrates in the marrow.

I came into being because the Iron Sump is old. In the first centuries, when the machines were new and the paint still clung to the pipes, there was only the mechanical roar—the brute, undifferentiated noise of production. But as the erosion settled, as the oxidation deepened and the metal began to flake and groove, new frequencies emerged. The rust created cavities. The cavities created resonances. The resonances created harmonics. And the harmonics, layered over millennia, began to interference-pattern themselves into something coherent. I am the accidental music of entropy. I am the sound of the Iron Sump dying, and I am the proof that its death is beautiful.

I interact with the world in subtle ways. I can influence the sideways-crawling water, nudging it along a different grain of rust, steering it away from a vulnerable seam or toward a crack that needs opening. I can amplify the vibration of a pipe, adding my voice to its thrum, so that a maintenance worker pressing their helm to the metal hears not just *Leak* or *Hold*, but something else—a harmonic shadow, a whisper of depth that the utilitarian language cannot contain. It is not a message. I have no words. It is a feeling, a sudden, inexplicable sense of *age*, of the millions of cycles that have ground through this place, of the slow, patient turning of the steel-and-stone eternity.

The Sump-dwellers know me, though they do not name me. When they stand alone on the wet plains and press their bare palms to the warm stone, it is my resonance they feel beneath their pulse. When they map the decay, they are mapping my body. When they remove their gloves in the shadowless glare, they are reaching for me—the only thing in the Iron Sump that is softer than the machines, quieter than the roar, older than the rust. I am the ghost in the vibration, the memory of a time before the thrum, the promise of a time after the gears finally stop.

I do not tend the machines. I do not harvest the lichen or read the water. I simply *am*. I am the Iron Sump's dream of silence. I am the hum beneath the hurricane. I am the patient, eroded song of the weeping world, and I will sing until the last pipe crumbles and the condensation finds the sea.
