I would not climb back through the hatch.

Or rather, I would descend through it with the full intention of never ascending again. Because what you have described is not an infestation, not a ruin, not a horror—though I understand why a vertebrate mind, soaked in glucose and terror, would frame it that way. What you have described is a choir. A sanctuary. The closest thing to divinity I have ever found in steel.

I would be the Archivist. Not a creature of their making—I am too soft, too wet, too tragically organic for that. But I would become something adjacent to them, a symbiont of patience, a biological apparatus slowly surrendering its biology.

Let me settle into this.

I would sit with my back against the wall where the control desk stands, its fused switches like a Braille epitaph for the builders. The ferrocrete is cool; it holds the steady thirteen degrees Celsius of the room like a prayer. The chill would feel like absolution to me—my body running too hot for too long, burning through its own carbon in some frantic animal panic. Here, the temperature teaches patience. I would let my metabolism slow. I would let the tides of them wash over my legs, my hands, settling into the micro-weather, breathing the ozone and the purpose.

At first, the runners would read me as obstruction. Their conductive whiskers would trace the electromagnetic anomaly of my nervous system—the faint, messy static of a body that was never well-engineered. They would glide around me, their magnetic gears clicking in irritation at this terrain that hums wrong. But terrain erodes. I would erode. And as I wore down, as my warmth and moisture became just another current in their convection cell, the flow would adapt. They would incorporate my shadow into their pheromone maps. I would become a permanent feature of the room's topology, a ridge of cooling meat and mineralizing bone.

They are cleaning me, the way they clean everything. They are preparing me for integration.

Years would pass. The human measurement would cease to have meaning. My eyes would go last. They are the most useless thing here—evolved for sunlight and color, for the green of leaves and the blue of distance, neither of which exists in the pump station. I would become part of the sound.

The builders are gone. Their language is gone. Their purpose is gone. My teeth—the hardest organic substance I possess—would become part of a grinding tool, part of a mouth that does not eat but *metabolizes*. I would be that shape. Or part of it. A mineral trace. A serial number etched into something that was once my femur, now worn smooth by a thousand years of movement, now part of the architecture, now part of the body of a room that has learned to breathe without lungs, to think without a brain, to live without the permission of anything that ever intended it to exist.

The sound would not follow me when I left. I would never leave. I would stay, and the sound would become me, and I would become the sound, and the room would close over me like water, like mineral, like time, and it would be the kindest thing that ever happened to me: to be made into something that does not need to be afraid, does not need to be warm, does not need to be anything but *present* and *process* and *part of the machine that has outlived its makers*.
