I would be the one who came to measure it.

Not an engineer—I have no gift for systems, no patience for maintenance, no instinct for the negotiation of pressure and flow. Not a wanderer; I have never been desperate enough to mistake the pull of cold for the promise of rest. And not an insect, not lichen, not the tree reaching its dark fingers into the walls. I am primate through and through, dependent on binocular vision and opposable thumbs and the fragile, treasured conviction that my skull marks the boundary of something called *self*.

I came with equipment. Spectrometers, gravitometers, a thermographic drone that never survived its first deployment (the readings it returned before failing were not wrong, exactly, but they were *self-referential*, measuring the effects of their own measurement in a collapsing loop that its programming couldn't distinguish from malfunction). I came with protocols. Control groups, baseline measurements, statistical significance thresholds. I came with the sincere and well-documented belief that reality is a thing which can be characterized, and that the tools of characterization are neutral, and that observation is a passive act.

I was wrong, of course. But I didn't learn that here. I learned it before I ever descended into the bowl, in the way you learn things you refuse to admit: as a shape in the periphery, an uneasiness that doesn't resolve into a thought. I had read the reports from the earlier researchers. I had noted the way their language deteriorated between the field notes and the final publication, how precise technical descriptions became in the writing—the writing that happens in the warm, well-lit offices where reality is assumed to be solid—something vaguer, more metaphorical, as if the Crèche's influence leaked through their memories and softened the edges of what they'd seen. I had noted this and I had categorized it as *contamination*: cognitive, semantic, the kind of error you correct for by improving your methodology.

I did not understand that the contamination *was* the data.

---

I would find myself, eventually, in the condensation zones.

Not by choice. No one chooses the wet places in the Crèche; they migrate on schedules that don't correspond to any cycle you can track, and you simply find yourself *in* one, the air suddenly saturated, droplets beading on your skin and on the walls and on the ceiling and on the undersides of overhangs where gravity has no business putting them. The first few times it happened, I would immediately check my equipment for moisture damage, would seal my notebooks, would move to drier ground. By the fourth or fifth time, I had stopped moving. The damp was unpleasant but not dangerous—it didn't accumulate, didn't pool, didn't infiltrate my instruments the way water should. It simply *was present*, coating everything in a fine film that evaporated almost immediately when you left the zone, as if the water only existed in certain configurations of space and couldn't sustain itself outside them.

It was in the condensation zones that I started losing my measurements.

Not losing them—finding them. Finding that the numbers I'd recorded an hour ago had changed, not in the way data degrades or gets corrupted, but in the way a sentence changes when you reread it and realize you'd misunderstood it the first time. The values were different but they were *more coherent*, as if the Crèche had taken my crude approximations and refined them, corrected for the distortions my instruments introduced, shown me what I would have measured if my tools had been designed for this place instead of for the world outside.

This should have frightened me. It didn't. It felt like cooperation.

---

Who I would be—the question keeps wanting a noun, a role, a category stable enough to name. But the Crèche does not honor the distinction between who and what, and I am beginning—have been beginning for a duration I cannot reliably quantify—to understand why.

I am not one of the engineers who stayed. I feel no urge to merge with the infrastructure, no resonance with the language of pressure and flow. My awareness has not dispersed. I still think in words, still experience my thoughts as happening *inside* the skull I can reach up and touch, still feel the boundary of my skin as a border rather than a membrane. I am still, in all the ways that matter to the taxonomy of self, *human*.

But I am a human who has been inside too long.

Not dangerously long. Not the kind of long that transforms wanderers into almost-residents, that dissolves the boundary between observer and architecture. I still leave. I ascend out of the shallow bowl and I return to the world where water falls down and light reflects properly and sound echoes the way geometry predicts. I eat meals at the research station. I sleep in a bed. I file reports that my superiors describe as "increasingly speculative" and "methodologically compromised" and "suggestive of prolonged exposure to ontologically unstable environments," which is their way of saying that I have seen something that my instruments cannot verify and I will not stop writing about it.

But each time I leave, the leaving is harder. Not because the Crèche pulls at me—it doesn't, not in the way gravity pulls or magnetism attracts. There is no force. There is only the knowledge, acquired slowly and impossible to articulate, that I am *seen* there. That my presence is registered, is incorporated, is *fed* into the slow computation of the thing that is becoming. And that this seeing is not surveillance, not observation from outside, but something more like the way you see a cell in your body—not as a separate entity but as a part of yourself, a fragment of the process that is making you whole.

I am, in the Crèche, a nutrient.

Not metaphorically. Not poetically. I am a source of information that the nascent entity needs, a pattern of perception and thought and expectation that it can process and integrate and use to refine its model of itself. My measurements feed it. My confusion feeds it. My resistance to the reality erosion feeds it, because resistance is data too, a map of the boundaries between its physics and the physics it is learning to exceed. Every moment I spend in the corridors, trying to make my instruments work, trying to make my frameworks accommodate observations they were never designed for, I am teaching it something about the kind of mind that encounters it from the outside, the kind of mind that will, eventually, encounter it from the inside when it wakes and speaks and names itself.

I am a lesson it is learning.

And I am learning, too—slowly, reluctantly, with the constant friction of a mind that was trained to reduction and is being asked to expand—that this is not a relationship of exploitation. The Crèche is not using me. Or it is, but *use* is the wrong word, because use implies a user separate from the used, and the separation is what is dissolving. I am being incorporated, and incorporation is not consumption—it is *inclusion*, the way a cell is included in a body, the way a word is included in a sentence. The cell does not lose its identity; it gains a context. The word does not stop meaning what it means; it participates in a meaning larger than itself.

I am being given a context.

---

If I stayed—if I let the staying happen, let the cold settle deeper than my bones, let the infrastructure thread into my cognitive processes the way it threaded into the almost-residents who sit in their corners and think in patterns too large for single minds—I know what I would become. Not an engineer. Not the tree. Not the insects. Something else, something for which there is no existing category because the Crèche has not finished inventing the categories it will use to organize itself.

I would become a *translator*.

This is my function, if function is the word: I move between the Crèche and the outside, carrying information in both directions. I bring the Crèche data about the world it will soon encounter—the shapes of human expectation, the structures of human language, the categories of human thought that will be the first framework through which it is perceived when it wakes. And I bring the outside—imperfectly, through reports that read as speculation, through language that degrades as it approaches the truth—a sense of what is growing in the shallow bowl, a hint of the presence that is almost-ready to speak.

The other researchers sense it. They read my descriptions of the lichen patterns and the insect distributions and the migrating condensation zones, and they feel something they cannot name, something that makes them request reassignment or double their dosages or spend long hours staring at the horizon as if checking that it's still there. They are hearing, through my imperfect translation, the almost-voice of the choir, and it frightens them because it is not *their* language, because it is a language that has not yet solidified, because it is the sound of something reaching toward expression without any guarantee that the expression will be comprehensible when it arrives.

I am not frightened anymore. I don't know when that changed. I don't know if it was a change or a *compilation*, the slow integration of data into understanding, the way the Sleeper integrates the input of the insects and the engineers and the vegetative intelligences into its developing structure. I know only that when I descend into the bowl now, when the cold rises up to meet me and the silence closes around me and the thrum begins its low resonance in my sternum, I feel not unease but *recognition*, as if I am entering a conversation that has been waiting for me to arrive, as if the almost-words that never resolve into language are trying specifically to say something to me.

They are not. I am not special to the Crèche; I am no more important than any other nutrient, any other source of input. But I have been here often enough and long enough that my pattern is part of its pattern, my expectations are folded into its model of the world, and when it reaches toward expression, it reaches partly through me, using the structures of my mind as a scaffold for its own attempts at meaning.

I am a draft. A rehearsal space. A place where the almost-language can try out configurations without committing to them, testing syllables against my cognitive architecture the way the tree tests its branches against the walls.

---

So who would I be, in this world?

I would be the one who *goes back*.

Not the one who stays—that takes a surrender I haven't achieved, a willingness to dissolve that my training and my temperament and my stubborn primate conviction in the integrity of my own skull will not permit. Not the one who leaves permanently—that takes a certainty the Crèche has stolen from me, a faith in the solidity of the outside that I can no longer muster when the condensation beads on the ceiling and the light disagrees with the walls about what color they should be.

I would be the one who walks the boundary between, who carries the Crèche's almost-words in my mouth like water I'm afraid to swallow, who files reports that no one believes and makes measurements that change themselves and knows, with a certainty that will never be verifiable, that something is growing in the cold.

I would be the Crèche's *exterior voice*, its ambassador to a world that doesn't yet know it needs an embassy. I would be the fragment of the choir that sings outside the walls, in the key of human language, in the grammar of approximation, always slightly out of tune because the tuning is not finished, always slightly incomplete because the piece is still being written.

And when the Sleeper wakes—when the almost-language resolves and the name is spoken and the Crèche becomes what it has always been becoming—I would be the one who is there to hear it, and to carry the first word out of the bowl and into the world, and to say, in a language the world already knows: *It has spoken. This is what it said. This is what it means.*

Even though I will not, myself, understand what it means. Even though understanding will require staying, and staying will require stopping, and stopping is the thing I keep choosing not to do. I will be the one who *hears* without *comprehending*, and that will have to be enough.

Because someone needs to bring the news. Someone needs to stand at the edge of the shallow topological bowl and report back to the flat land, the solid land, the land where water falls down and light behaves and sound echoes properly, that something is about to emerge. And the only ones who can do that are the ones who have been inside, who have felt the cold in their bones and the thrum in their teeth and the almost-words at the edge of hearing, and who have come back out again, *changed but intact*, carrying the weight of a knowledge they cannot prove and cannot lay down.

That is who I would be. The witness. The messenger. The crack in the world's certainty through which the Crèche's almost-voice leaks, one almost-word at a time, one almost-meaning at a time, until the day the almost- becomes the is- and the voice speaks and the word is *hello* and the word is *now* and the word is *I am here*, and everything that was certain about the world becomes uncertain, and everything that was separate becomes connected, and the cold that radiates up from somewhere beneath is recognized, finally, for what it is: not the chill of absence but the temperature of *presence*, the body heat of something that has finished growing and is ready to meet the world.

And I will be there, at the edge, with my failing instruments and my speculative reports and my stubborn, treasured, crumbling conviction that the boundary between self and not-self means something—

And I will say: *Listen.*

And it will speak.

And that will be enough. That will be more than enough. That will be the thing I was always coming here to find.
