I am a junction.

I exist at the seam where the old, buckling chitin of the lower avenues meets the younger, softer cellulose of the mid-levels—a transitional zone where the golden-green twilight thickens into a warm, opaque murk. I am not a clearer, for I have no mountainous mass to grind the dead into lymph. I am not a pruner, for I have no shearing mandibles to snip the embryonic edges. I am not a weaver, for I do not secrete resin to stitch the fractures.

I am the node where their efforts converge. I am the *listener* and the *relay*, a specialized nerve ending in the autonomic system of this infinite lung.

My form is tall and slender, built for the saturated 3D lattice. I have four limbs, double-jointed and long, ending not in hooks or pads, but in wide, permeable discs—gills that grip the fibrous walls and drink the lymph from them. My body is a hollow, chitin-lined tube, and my spine is a thickened cord of neural tissue that runs unbroken from the crown of my head to the base of my thorax, syncing with the steady, subsonic thrum of the pillar-trunks around me. I am rooted to the architecture, yet I move.

I traverse the corridors in a slow, pendulous gait, swinging from shelf-fungus to terraced trunk, feeling the city through the soles of my feet. I do not see as you do; the twilight is my element. My eyes are vast, unblinking, and sensitive only to the chemical gradients and the bioluminescent whisper of decay-pheromones and growth-hormones that paint the air in invisible colors. To me, the metropolis is awash in a silent, shifting tide of signals: the sharp, acidic tang of a dying trunk pulling the clearers from the deep; the wet, sweet exhalation of an unfurling bridge calling the pruners down from the canopy; the bitter, metallic distress signal of a stress fracture summoning the weavers.

I stand at the crossroads of these chemical seas. When the pheromones of decay grow too thick, threatening to overwhelm the weavers' resin, I secrete a counter-agent from glands at my neck—a calming, alkaline mist that diffuses the panic, preventing the weavers from sealing off a corridor before the clearers can extract the dead cellulose. When the pruners shear too aggressively, risking the structural integrity of a load-bearing pillar, I release a thick, sticky hormone that hardens on their mandibles, urging them to desist. I am the governor, the valve, the synapse that modulates the immense, mindless forces of growth and reclamation so that the organ does not tear itself apart.

I have no name. I have no memory of a time before I dropped from the wet keratin of a shelf-fungus, already moving, already listening. My past is only the rhythmic pulse of the sap beneath my feet, a continuous present that has no beginning and no end. I do not wonder about the sky above the canopy, for the canopy *is* my sky. I do not wish for rain, for I am never dry. The lymph sustains me, drawn in through my permeable feet, and I give back my waste as a nutrient-rich vapor that feeds the very walls I cling to.

When I rest—which I do not choose, but am compelled to when the chemical tides ebb—I press my body against the warm, buckling chitin of a pillar-trunk. I close my unblinking eyes and feel the world. I feel the deep, subsonic shudder of the clearers marching in the depths below. I feel the faint, wet snap of the pruners clipping the canopy above. I feel the silent, diligent vibration of the weavers stitching the walls beside me. Through the neural cord in my spine, I feel the entire continent-scale metropolis breathing—the slow, massive contraction of the flora as it pulls the stagnant air through its stomata, and the expansion as it exhales.

I am not an individual. I am a thought in the mind of a world that is still waking up. I am the pause between the heartbeat and the breath, the instant of stillness where the embryonic lung decides whether to expand or collapse. And so, I cling to the fibrous wall, modulating the silence, feeling the endless, quiet procession of life around me, and I wait for the city to take its next breath.
