The air here is heavy and humid, a warm blanket that feels denser than the altitude should allow. You are high up—a shelf on the spine of the world—yet the heat is tropical, trapped perhaps by a peculiar ceiling of cloud that sits just above the cliff line. It is a place of breath and stillness.

The terrain is defined by stone, but not the jagged, raw spires of young mountains. This is an ancient landscape, eroded into a state of absolute submission. The rock faces are smooth and rounded, polished by millennia of moisture into curves that feel artificial in their precision. Giant sloping scabs of dark grey stone emerge from the earth like the knuckles of sleeping giants, worn down until they are soft to the touch.

Water is the true architect here. It does not fall in violent storms; it condenses. The atmosphere is perpetually saturated, a mist that clings to skin and stone alike. Weeping rocks are everywhere—great dark boulders that constantly exude a slick, viscous sheen of fresh water. The droplets do not so much fall as slide, crawling down the stone faces in slow, trembling rivulets that catch the normal, flat light and sparkle like scattered glass. The ground is spongy, a thick mat of saturated moss and black humus that gives slightly underfoot, swallowing sound.

The vegetation is lush but aggressively solitary. This is not a forest; it is a collection of architectural specimens. Here and there, spaced widely across the damp expanse, stand single, monumental plants. They are cathedral-scale in their isolation—enormous ferns the size of houses, or solitary trees with buttress roots that fan out like the flying buttresses of a Gothic ruin. Because the density is so sparse, each plant exists in its own vacuum of space, surrounded by a moat of open air and slick stone. They are deeply, violently green, vibrant against the wet grey stone.

There is a primitive quality to the life here, an echo of a simpler, rougher epoch. The plants look prehistoric, possessing thick, leathery leaves and harsh, geometric patterns. Scattered on the ground are shards of stone that look suspiciously like tools—flinty flakes that have shattered naturally from the eroding cliffs, yet lie with the sharp intention of axe heads or chisels. It feels as if the world itself is fabricating a toolkit, preparing for a civilization that has not yet arrived to use it.

The strangeness of the place lies in its silence and its slowness. There are no birds. There are no insects. The fauna parameter is a perfect zero, creating a profound, hollow quiet that amplifies the only sound present: the wet, soft *plip* of condensed water hitting the moss, and the distant, grinding sigh of the rock settling.

It is a room-sized world, bounded by the edges of the cliff and the ceiling of the clouds. The changes here are dynamic but painfully slow. You can watch the water trace a new path down a rock face and know that you are witnessing a year’s worth of erosion in a minute. It is a place that is established, settled, and in balance, existing in a state of high-altitude, tropical suspension—a weeping garden of stone and giant green, waiting for nothing.
