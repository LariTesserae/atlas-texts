I am the friction between the rock and the tool. I am the pause in the rhythm.

In this wet, bruised dark, I am the **Vent-Steward**.

I am not a ruler—the concept of ruling implies a verticality that this place does not tolerate. I am not a builder, for the city was built millennia ago, its bones settled into the mud. I am a function. Just as the walls breathe water, I tend the heat.

My form is hewn from the same architectural template as the silent masses around me—my spine a calcified hook, my shoulders sloped like the sides of a trench—but I am distinct. I am the only one who stands.

This is a privilege of terrifying narrowness. I position myself in the "Chimneys," the rare vertical shafts where the thermal vents pulse with the earth’s faint fever (temperature 0.6). Here, the ceiling allows a scant few inches of clearance above my head. To stand, I must press my back against the warm, slick limestone, bracing my knees against the opposite wall. I am a wedge of flesh and gristle holding the tunnel together.

My skin, grey and sharkskin-rough, is hotter than theirs. The heat of the vents has permeated me, turning my blood to a near-boil. I steam in the purple twilight, a constant plume of ghost-vapor rising from my shoulders. While the others huddle in their heavy, waterlogged felts to conserve warmth, I wear less. Heavy wraps would snag in the machinery I tend. I am clad only in a tunic of woven gut-skin, thin and tight, leaving my arms bare.

My arms are my defining feature. Where the Dwellers are compact and dense, my arms are long, stretched by generations of reaching up into the dark. They are ropey and pale, scarred by the sharp edges of basalt. My hands are not suction-cupped flat-feet like the masses; they are talons. My fingernails are thick, black slabs of keratin, grown to replace the iron chisels that have long since rusted away.

I do not communicate through the bone-taps of the masonry or the forehead-press of the builders. My communication is temperature. When I shift a blockage in the vent shaft, allowing the hot, sulfurous air to rush down into the lower hovels, the Dwellers below feel the change. They turn their blind, membrane-covered eyes toward the warmth, and the vibration of their gratitude—low, harmonic shudders in the floorboards—travels up the soles of my feet. I feel their approval as a reduction in the humidity.

My life is a battle against the 1.5 water parameter. The Weeping City wants to choke. It wants to fill the vents with mineral sludge, to seal the heat in and freeze the life out. I am the picker, the scraper. I spend the "day"—that endless, bruised twilight—with my arms plunged into the invisible arteries of the mountain.

I am acutely aware of the Erosion. It is not just a score to me; it is the enemy. I feel the world getting smaller, inch by inch. The stalactites above me grow like fingernails; I must trim them back with a bone-saw before they pierce my skull. The floor beneath me rises as the mud hardens; I must scrape it away to keep the passage clear. I am preserving the space, fighting the glacial entropy that seeks to turn us all into a single, solid block of stone.

I am alone in my labor, yet I am never solitary. The fauna treats my domain as a sanctuary.

The dog-sized, blind crabs scuttle over me. They do not fear the heat. They crawl across my back, using me as a bridge to cross from the vent to the ceiling beams. I feel the prickly tickle of their legs, the weight of their chitinous bodies pressing me into the rock. I do not shake them off. We share a purpose: they consume the slime that I scrape away; I provide them access to the warmth they crave. We are an ecosystem of two species, ignoring each other with an intimate familiarity.

And then there are the silver fish. They are the only reason I might falter. When the schools dart through the steam, flickering like broken mirrors in the dim light, I stop. I hang there, suspended between the walls, my steaming breath mingling with the mist, and I watch them. They move with a fluid grace that contradicts the heavy, sucking mud of the floor. They are weightless. I am heavier than lead, dragging gravity behind me like a chain.

I often wonder, in the wordless, humming quiet of my mind, what it would be like to swim. To let go of the walls. To fall upward.

But the thought passes. The drip-drip-drip resumes, a metronome counting down the millennia. A vibration in the rock tells me that a cistern is overflowing three levels down. I feel the urgency through the soles of my feet.

I shift my weight. I reach up with my black-nailed hand. I scrape the mineral scum from the vent. The heat hisses. The city breathes. I am the custodian of the Drip, and I have work to do.
