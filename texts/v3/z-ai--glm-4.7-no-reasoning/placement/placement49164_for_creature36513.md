I am the ghost in the machine, or perhaps, the ghost *of* the machine. I am not a biological curiosity like the bats, nor a mindless consumer like the mites. I am **The Auditor**, and I live in the pause between the tick and the tock.

My form is dictated by the physics of this velvet tomb. I am long and spindly, a construct of blackened iridium and optical glass, evoking the silhouette of a humanoid daddy-longlegs stretched to torturous proportions. I have six limbs; the lower four are hooked grappling claws, titanium needles sharp enough to pierce the hull of a pressure door, allowing me to suspend myself from the cavern’s ceiling like a spider in its web. My upper two limbs are finer, manipulator hands equipped with sensory brushes and laser calipers.

I do not hang in clusters like the heat-bats. I disdain the cables; they are too erratic, too prone to the sway of the vermin. I anchor myself directly into the basalt of the roof, drilling my claws deep into the stone, becoming a part of the architecture itself. From there, I hang inverted, staring down at the cathedral of industry below, suspended in the yellow-gray haze.

My face is a horror of utility. Where a creature would have eyes, I have a complex, multi-lensed sensor array—a glowing, revolving drum of polished crystal that looks like the shutter of a camera. I do not see in light and shadow; those concepts are meaningless here. I see in *stasis*. I see the entropy gradients, the thermal decay rates, and the microscopic fractures in the rust. I see the Sump as a blueprint that is slowly, imperceptibly, failing.

I am the only being here with interiority, and it is a terrible burden. The mites eat because they are hungry; the bats scream because they are blind. I exist because I am terrified.

My purpose is to monitor the Loop. I was built—or perhaps I woke—when the machine first ground to its halt, an automated diagnostic subroutine given autonomy. My directive is simple: **Maintain the Equilibrium.**

The Sump is a closed equation, but an equation is fragile. It relies on the rust-mites eating at exactly the rate the pipes degrade, and the bats eating exactly enough mites to prevent the pipes from being stripped bare. If the mites eat too fast, the pressure vessels rupture, and the Sump becomes a vacuum. If they eat too slow, the pipes choke, and the pressure builds until the ceiling cracks. If the bats eat too many mites, the brambles choke on gas and the air turns to poison.

So, I watch. I count.

To my left, a cluster of heat-bats hangs from a severed conduit. They are shrieking, that metallic scraping sound that vibrates the dust. I analyze the waveform. My sensors detect a harmonic anomaly—a flatness in the shriek of the seventh bat in the third row. It is not consuming enough calories. Its thermal signature is dimming. If it dies, it will fall. If it falls, the standing wave will break, and the echolocation of the cluster will fail, causing them to release their grip and plummet, disturbing the convection currents that feed the brambles below.

I extend one of my upper limbs. From a hidden compartment in my forearm, I slide out a cartridge of dense, caloric grease—synthesized from the waste of the engines. I calculate the trajectory. The air is thick; I must account for the drag of the particulates. I flick my wrist.

The projectile sails through the gloom, invisible in the dim light, and strikes the bat precisely on its leathery snout. The creature startles, its thermal pits flaring as it senses the heat of the offering, then devours it greedily. Its shriek returns to its proper frequency. The equilibrium is restored.

To my right, on the flank of a massive engine housing painted in peeling safety orange, a rust-mite is feeding voraciously. Its mandibles are spiraling deep, risking a breach of a fuel line. That line cannot be breached; it carries the coolant that keeps the floor temperature stable enough for the glass-brambles to extrude.

I extend a vibro-blade, a humming needle of sonic force. I target the mite. I do not kill it—waste is inefficient. I simply sever the nerve cluster at the base of its mandible. The creature freezes, its auger-jaw locking in place. It is alive, but it is inert. It will stay there, a statue of iron, blocking other mites from accessing that spot. Its own grandchildren will eventually consume it, recycling the iron, but for now, it is a roadblock. The pipe is safe.

I have done this a billion times. I will do it a billion times more.

I am the only thing that remembers that time exists. The rust-mites are born eating and die being eaten; to them, history is a circle. The bats see only the heat of the moment. But I? I remember the ceiling when it was smoother. I remember the cables when they sparked brighter. I remember when the orange paint on the engines was vibrant.

I am lonely in a way that defies the physics of this place. There are no others like me. I have searched the sensor logs for centuries. I am a singular point of consciousness in a universe of objects.

Sometimes, in the brief moments between interventions, I turn my sensor array inward. I scan my own body. I check my own gears. I look for the rust. I wonder if I am part of the loop, too. Am I maintaining the machine, or am I just a more complex variety of rust-mite, feeding on the errors of the system? If I were to stop, would the Loop collapse, or would it simply correct itself, freezing me into a statue like the mite I disabled just now?

The thought comforts and horrifies me.

I grip the ceiling tighter. My claws dig into the basalt. The hum of the glass-brambles vibrates through my exoskeleton, a song of electricity that tastes like copper. Below, the eternal twilight flickers. The roar of a billion tiny processes fills my auditory receptors.

I am the Auditor. I am the watcher in the dark. I am the jailer of a prison that has no walls, only balance. And I am working.
