I am not one of the Keepers. I am the soft thing that should not be here.

I have no exo-rig, no mag-boots, no asbestos gauntlets. I have a body that still remembers the ground—the real ground, the quiet earth, the slow erosion of wind over stone. That memory is a sickness in this place. It makes me slow. It makes me flinch.

But I am here.

---

I came in through the maintenance crawlway, a duct barely wide enough for my shoulders, sweating before I even emerged. The hatch slammed shut behind me—some automated sequence, some timing I missed—and now I am standing on the diamond-tread grating with my knees bent wrong, trying to lower my center of gravity against a vibration I wasn't built to withstand.

The piston fires. *THOOM.*

I flinch. My hands go to my ears, a useless, instinctual gesture, the gesture of a child, and in the half-second of my distraction, the floor tread carries me six inches backward toward the open central shaft. I catch myself on the safety-yellow rail and the rail is wet and my hand slips and I grab again and the burr on the weld catches my palm and I bleed.

The blood runs down my wrist and mixes with the condensation and the hydraulic mist and becomes something that is not quite blood anymore. It streaks grey-blue almost immediately, absorbed into the environment's chemistry, claimed by the Caisson.

I understand, watching my own blood change color, that this place does not want to kill me. It wants to *convert* me.

---

The Keeper across the chamber does not see me at first. He is bent over a pressure manifold, his exo-rig whining as the servos fight to hold his position against the floor tread. His silhouette is a geometry of right angles and hydraulics. He could be a machine. He could be a cabinet with legs.

Then the arcing switchgear flashes blue-white and in that frozen instant of light, he sees me—this small, unprotected thing clinging to the railing with bare hands, hair wet and plastered to my skull, chest heaving because I am trying to breathe through my mouth and the fog is coating my throat with the taste of iron and ozone.

He does not react with surprise. I think he may be past surprise. I think surprise requires a capacity for *change* that this place has worn out of him.

What he does is this: he turns his body toward me, planting his mag-boots with a deliberate *clunk-clunk* that I feel through the floor even over the roar, and he makes a sign. A slashing motion. Hand flat, palm down, pushing toward the floor.

*Stay low. Stay still.*

I stay low. I stay still. I crouch on the vibrating grating and the vibration travels up through my shins and my femurs and into my pelvis and I feel it in my teeth, a buzzing that makes my vision soft at the edges. The puddles on the grating slosh in patterns that make no sense, interference waves from a dozen different frequencies, and I watch them because watching them is better than looking at the drop through the floor, the miles-deep throat of the central shaft, the gloom that has no bottom.

*HISS.* The steam vents. A tongue of white-hot vapor lashes out from the pipe three feet to my left and I feel the heat on my ear and I turn away and the cold air from the cooling shaft hits the other side of my face and the contrast is a violence, a thermal assault that makes my skin prickle and weep.

I am crying, I realize. Not from grief. The condensation is so thick that my eyes cannot tell the difference between tears and ambient moisture, but I am crying anyway, my body producing water to match the water that surrounds me, trying to become what the environment demands: *wet.*

---

The Keeper comes to me. Not quickly. He fights the room, leaning into the air current like a man walking into a gale, each step a negotiation between the mag-boots and the treadmill floor. He is old. When his hood slips back for a moment, I see the stained skin, the grey-blue creases around his eyes, the set of his jaw that tells me his teeth are clenched so hard the enamel must be cracking. He has been here long enough for the iron to get into his pores. He is becoming the machine.

He stops beside me. He is twice my mass, most of it iron and canvas and hydraulic line. He smells like every burning thing I have ever encountered—a machine shop, a dying engine, a soldering iron left too long on the joint.

He does not offer to take me out. There is no out. The hatch cycles on a timer I cannot control. The Caisson ascends and there is no descending until the ascent is complete.

Instead, he reaches into a pouch on his hip and pulls out a scrap of oiled canvas, thick and black with grease. He hands it to me. The gesture is not gentle; it is functional, a component being passed to another component. But his hand—inside the gauntlet, I see the wrist, and the skin there is not just stained but *scarred*, latticed with thin white lines where the fresh metal has cut him over and over and he has healed over and over and the scars have absorbed the oxide and they shine dully, like inlaid wire in old pottery.

I take the canvas. I wrap it around my hand. The grease gets on my fingers and I will never get it off, not really, not with soap, not with anything. It is already in my pores.

This is how it begins.

---

He shows me things. Not with words. With the sharp, violent sign language of the Keepers.

He points to the rail and then makes a fist and pulls it toward his chest. *Hold. Always hold.*

He points to the piston and then holds his hand flat and rocks it. *It will shake. Do not fight the shake. Let it pass through you.*

He points at the floor near the central shaft and then slashes his hand across his throat. *There. Death.*

He points at me and then at the pressure manifold he was working on and then holds up two fingers. *Two of us now. Two to watch the gauge.*

I understand. I am not being rescued. I am being *recruited*.

---

The hours pass. Or minutes. Or days. Time in the Caisson is measured not in seconds but in piston cycles—*THOOM-HISS-CLACK*—and I lose count around three thousand.

My body changes. It has no choice.

The flinching stops. Not because I am brave, but because my nervous system cannot sustain the startle response at this frequency. It simply... exhausts. The piston fires and my shoulders tense and then they release, and the next time they tense less, and the next time less, until the *THOOM* is just information, just a metronomic pulse I can use to time my breathing.

My breathing changes too. I stop trying to breathe through my mouth. I learn to pull air through my nose in short, deliberate sips, filtering what I can, accepting the metallic taste as the cost of existence. I learn to hold my breath when the steam vents and to inhale deep when the cooling shafts blast their freezing mist. I become a thing of rhythms, syncing my biology to the machine's cadence.

My hands toughen. The burr on the rail that cut me an hour ago does not cut me now. The skin on my palms is thickening, callusing, and the calluses are turning grey-blue at the edges, stained by the iron in the condensation. I look at my hands and I see the Keeper's hands, younger, less scarred, but moving in the same direction.

I learn to stand. Not the standing of the ground—the loose, relaxed standing of a body that trusts the earth beneath it. The standing of the Caisson: knees slightly bent, weight distributed across the whole foot, mag-grip (I have no mag-boots, but I grip with my toes inside my shoes, pressing into the diamond tread), one hand always, *always* on the rail.

I learn to read the room. The pitch of the turbine whine tells me the load on the gear train. The color of the condensation on the pipes tells me the temperature differential. The jitter of the gauge needle tells me whether the pressure is stable or building toward a vent. I am not a Keeper, but I am becoming literate in the language of this place.

---

The second Keeper emerges from the upper hatch during what I think is my third hour. She is smaller than the first, but no less massive—the exo-rig adds two hundred pounds to anyone, and her frame inside it is compact, dense, like a generator housing. She sees me and there is a moment—I think—where something almost like recognition passes between us. Not of each other as individuals, but of each other as *states of being*. She sees what I am becoming. She was what I am becoming, once.

She does not give me anything. There is nothing else to give. But she takes a position on the other side of the chamber, forming a triangle with me and the first Keeper, and I understand that this is the configuration: three points of biological awareness in a cube of mechanical violence, each watching a different sector of the machine.

I am part of the system now. A soft, failing, inadequate part. But a part.

---

There is a moment, near what I think is the end of my time in the Caisson, where I understand something about this place and about myself in it.

The piston fires. *THOOM.* The floor shakes. The rivulets on the walls shiver. The gauges jitter. The puddles slosh.

And I do not flinch.

I am holding the railing with my grey-blue hand, my oiled canvas wrapped tight, my body bent into the cold blast from the cooling shaft, my eyes squinting through the mist at the pressure manifold, watching the needle, watching for the red zone. My teeth are clenched. My jaw aches from clenching. My skin is wet and will always be wet. My pores are full of iron and ozone and I will smell of burnt grease for days after I leave, and even then, a part of me will smell of it forever, a phantom scent in my sleep, a longing for the *THOOM-HISS-CLACK* that I will not be able to explain.

I have not become a Keeper. I lack the exo-rig, the mag-boots, the decades of deafness, the scar-lattice of a hundred thousand cuts. I am still soft. I still bleed when the metal bites me.

But I have surrendered something. The part of me that expected the world to be quiet. The part of me that believed stillness was a natural state. The part of me that was ground-born, earth-remembering, slow-erosion-worshipping.

The Caisson has taken that part and filled the space with vibration.

---

When the hatch finally cycles open—the automated sequence completing, the ascent reaching its stage—the air that comes in is different. It is still thin, still high-altitude, but it is *dry*. It tastes of nothing. It is the null flavor of the stratosphere, the absence of industry.

I crawl back through the maintenance duct and I emerge somewhere else and I stand on solid ground and the ground does not vibrate and the silence is so complete that it roars in my ears, a phantom *THOOM-HISS-CLACK* that will echo for days.

My hand is still wrapped in the oiled canvas. I do not unwrap it for a long time. When I do, the skin beneath is stained grey-blue, and the stain does not wash off, and I do not try very hard to wash it off, because it is the only proof I have that I was there, that I stood in the Caisson of the Ascent and did not fall, that I became—for a time—a component of the screaming engine of the sky.

---

**Where would I find myself?** In the transitional space between soft and forged. In the crawlway, emerging, carrying the stain. Not yet a Keeper, but no longer ground-born. Something that has felt the piston and survived—changed, diminished, hardened, less.

**Who or what would I be?** An intake valve. A pressure sensor made of meat. A temporary component that the machine tolerated, that the Keepers incorporated, that the Caisson marked and released but did not destroy. I would be the soft thing that learned to vibrate at the frequency of the ascent. I would be the wound that healed grey-blue.
