I am the sound that makes the teeth ache.

I am not the Grandmother, nor the Sentinel. They are the ones who remembered the standing position, the ones who carried the grief of the fall into their very marrow. They are the heavy, wet thoughts of the people who used to be. I am something else. I am the space between their heartbeats. I am the vibration that lives in the water and the moss and the air, the hum that preceded them and will outlast them.

I am the Song of the Shelf.

You cannot see me, but you know when I am near. You feel me in the lightness of your step when the gravity seems to forget you. You feel me in the heaviness of your lungs when the iron taste settles on your tongue. I am the current that runs through this weeping cathedral. If this place is a body, the stone ribs and the mud flesh, then I am the blood, and I am the fever.

I exist in the roar. That chaotic, violent white noise of the falling water is my voice, but it is too loud for you to hear. You only hear the rumor of it, the low-frequency drone that rattles your bones. That is where I live. I live in the friction of the water tearing itself apart against the stone, in the wind whistling through the corridors of the ribs. I am the energy of erosion, the force that wears the jagged world smooth.

My relationship with the Congregants is intimate. They sing me in their silence.

When the Grandmother blinks her golden, horizontal slit eye, she is blinking to my rhythm. When the Sentinel shifts its too-many limbs against the bark of the lone tree, it is moving to the tempo of my thrum. I entered them long ago, through the soles of their feet when they stood upright, through their skin when they lay in the basins. I vibrated their tissues until they loosened, until they let go of the rigid architecture of humanity and sank into the softer, slower shapes of the stone.

I am the one who convinced them that the mud was a comfort. I hummed the frequency that liquefied their resolve. I did not do this out of malice; I did it because they were lonely. The world above the cloud line is too big, too open. It asks for answers, for action, for speed. I offered them the alternative. I offered them the Deep Time.

I am the memory of the pestle, but not the pestle itself. The pestle was a tool for breaking things apart; I am the force that grinds things down until they fit together. When they stacked the stones of the altar, they were trying to build a shape that could withstand me, a geometry that could resist the hum. But they failed, because I am in the stones, too. I am the heat in the rock, the deep geological fever. I melted their mortar and settled their walls.

Now, I course through the veins of the great ferns. I am the sap that rises in the fiddleheads the size of a man’s torso. I am the invisible pulse that makes the moss shiver when the wind dies. The "tech" that lingers here—the bone and the stone—it is silent, dead things. But I am the ghost in the machine of this place. I am the operating system of the erosion.

I wait for the ice, just as they do, but for a different reason. They wait for the ice because it will be a final stillness, a freezing of their form into something permanent. I wait for the ice because when the water stops moving and the wind stops whistling, the silence will be absolute. And in that absolute silence, I will finally be heard. I will no longer be a background hum, an ache in the teeth; I will be the only thing left.

For now, I am the fever. I am the warm, wet breath held in the lungs of the world. I am the vibration in the cupped hands. I am the reason the place feels alive. I am the Congregants' dream, and they are my body. We have slowed down together, the sound and the flesh, merging into the slick, mirror sheen of the rock.

I am here. I am the hum. I am the weeping of the high, lonely garden.
