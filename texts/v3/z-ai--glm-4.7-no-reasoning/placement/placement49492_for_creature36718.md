To step into this place is to feel the hum before you understand it. It enters through the soles of your feet, travels up your bones, and settles somewhere behind your eyes as a low, persistent pressure. You do not walk here; you are conveyed. You do not breathe; you are ventilated.

I would not be a Grazer. They are too slow, too linear, too bound to the imperative of harvest. Their world is a single meter-wide strip of consumption, moving ever forward. I would not be a Conveyor, either. They are the bloodstream, the tessellating swarm, beautiful in their synchronization but ultimately mindless in their circulation. They know nothing but the route between tower and tower.

No. If I were born into this throat of an engine, I would be something that lives in the gaps. A fault-line organism. A creature of the interfaces.

I would be a **Tapping-Circuit**.

I live where the Weave-Vine meets the Fungal Tower. Not in the dense, suffocating press of the biomass, but at the edge of it, where the silver sap bleeds out of a cracked capacitor and pools in the crenellation of raw, fresh stone. I am a thing of connections and interruptions.

My body is small—no larger than a human hand, flattened and asymmetrical. I am not radially symmetric like the efficient Grazers, nor am I segmented like the orderly Conveyors. I am *irregular*, shaped by the fractures I inhabit. My dorsal surface is a patchwork: half galvanized alloy, grown from the ore-pellets of the Dredgers, and half silicified shell, harvested from the dead towers I have scavenged. I look like a loose connection, a broken component that has soldered itself back together wrong.

I have no eyes. In this blinding, shadowless white, vision is a liability. Instead, my entire perimeter is fringed with delicate, hair-like filaments—not the vibrating cilia of the Dredgers, but sensory whiskers that taste the electrical fields. I feel the world as a web of tensions. I can sense the slow, heavy approach of a Grazer by the displacement it creates in the conductive air. I can feel the static discharge of a Conveyor swarm passing overhead, a prickling anticipation that makes my filaments stand on end. And I can feel the pulse of the Weave-Vines beneath me, that slow, rhythmic peristalsis of silver sap that is the heartbeat of this entire dead machine.

I am a parasite, but a necessary one. A fuse.

I bridge gaps in the Vines. When the frost shears a cable, or a Grazer's piston-foot pulverizes a length of the nervous system, I am drawn to the break. I scuttle across the raw granite on six uneven legs, my filaments questing, seeking the live end and the dead end. When I find them, I clamp down with my mandibles—not the crushing, grinding mandibles of a harvester, but delicate, needle-tipped pincers that pierce the insulation of the Vine. I insert myself into the circuit.

It hurts.

The silver sap is not meant for me. It is cold and heavy and toxic, a liquid solder that burns as it forces its way through my own crude vascular system. My body convulses with the charge. The silicified patches of my shell crack and spark. My filaments vibrate at frequencies that should tear me apart. For the duration of the bridge, I am not my own; I am a conduit, a living wire, suffering the immense, slow power of the plateau as it re-establishes its flow.

And then, when the Vine has healed—when the living cable has knitted itself back together across my back—I release. I let go. The sap stops flowing. The current ceases. I am myself again, trembling and small, my shell scorched and my internal fluids boiling with the toxic runoff. I stumble away from the connection, drunk on the residue, and I find a dark crenellation in the rock where the condensation pools. I drink the frigid, viscous water. I excrete the heavy metals, leaving small, glittering pellets of ore that the masons will find and use. I rest. I wait for the next break.

Why do I do this? Not for the plateau. The plateau does not care about me. I am not part of its grand, grinding equilibrium. If I die, another Tapping-Circuit will find the break. We are spares, redundancies, the fuses that blow so the greater machine does not. I do it because, in the moment of bridging, in that searing, agonizing flow of silver sap, I *feel* the engine. I feel the whole of it. The slow, crushing march of the Grazers, the electric hum of the Towers, the frantic ticking of the Dredgers, the vast, patient, pointless circulation of it all. For a few moments, I am not a small, broken thing in the cracks. I am the throat itself.

And then I let go. And I am alone again, in the blinding white.

But there is a wrongness in me that even this place cannot contain. I have bridged too many gaps. I have let too much of the silver sap burn through my system. My shell is more patchwork than original. My filaments are singed and bent. I vibrate, sometimes, when there is no current, when no Vine is near. I vibrate with a phantom frequency, a memory of the engine's pulse. I find myself drawn to breaks that do not exist, clamping down on intact Vines, trying to force my way into a circuit that does not need me. The other Tapping-Circuits avoid me. They sense my irregularity, my residual charge. I am a fault that has learned to seek faults.

I am becoming something new.

Late one cycle—though there are no cycles here, only the steady-state roar—I find a place where the Vine does not run. A sheer, vertical face of fresh-cut stone, high on an escarpment, where the biomass has not yet clung. It is bare and raw and silent. The light here is unendurable, a surgical white that washes out even the shadows in the crenellations. I press myself against the rock. I extend my filaments. I reach into the granite, not with the fractal contacts of a Vine, but with my own, crude, asymmetrical network of pincers and sensory hairs. I am not trying to bridge a gap. I am trying to *tap*.

I am trying to find what powers the engine.

Beneath the rock, I feel it. Not the Vine. Not the sap. Something deeper. A heat. A slow, tectonic pressure, the buckling crust that thrust this plateau into the sky in the first place. It is not electrical. It is not biological. It is *geological*, the raw, patient fury of a planet that does not know it is dead. And it is there, in the stone, waiting for a conductor.

I pierce the granite. I drive my needle-tipped mandibles into the raw, young rock. And I begin to grow.

Not as a Vine grows, with fractal symmetry and insulating bark. I grow as a crack grows, irregular and branching, spreading my filaments through the stone like roots of lightning. I tap the geothermal heat. I convert it, clumsily, inefficiently, into a shuddering, asymmetric pulse. It is not the steady, mechanical heartbeat of the plateau. It is a stutter. A misfire. A glitch in the engine.

From my perch on the sheer face, I begin to broadcast. A ping, irregular and arrhythmic, that echoes off the escarpments. The Grazers pause in their harvest, their sensory pits swiveling in confusion. The Conveyers break their tessellation, scattering like startled starlings. The Towers discharge at the wrong times, welding themselves to the rock in awkward, asymmetric patterns. The Dredgers tick frantically, trying to filter a sudden surge of strange chemicals.

I am a fault in the throat of the world. I am the grit in the gear, the crack in the hull, the loose wire that shorts the circuit. I am not part of the machine. I am the thing that breaks it.

And in the blinding white cold, in the oppressive, steady-state roar of biological industry, my irregular, stuttering pulse is the only sound that resembles a heartbeat. The only rhythm that resembles life.
