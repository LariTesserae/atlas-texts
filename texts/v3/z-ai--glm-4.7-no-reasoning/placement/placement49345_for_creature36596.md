The air here does not feel like a gas; it feels like a weight. This is the High Loom, a vast, jagged plateau suspended in a twilight that refuses to resolve into day or night. The light is dim—a bruised, violet gloom that seems to emanate from the wet rock itself rather than any sky—but the temperature is sweltering, a humid, tropical warmth that clings to the skin like a second layer of clothing.

The landscape is violently young. Erosion has not had time to soften the anger of the stone. There are no rolling hills or worn riverbeds here; the terrain is a jagged scree of obsidian shards and glassy basalt, all knife-edged ridges and sudden, vertical drops. Everything looks freshly broken, as if the world was split open moments ago and hasn't stopped bleeding.

Yet, despite the sharp hostility of the rock, the place is undeniably lush. Vegetation explodes from every fissure, a dense saturation of bio-engineered efficiency. The "trees" do not grow toward the light; they grow in frantic, spiraling helixes, their bark a metallic, copper-green sheen that pulses with metabolic heat. Giant fronds, stiff as structural steel, interlock above the ground to form a suffocating canopy. Ferns the size of houses press against mosses that feel like industrial insulation. It is a jungle, but a disciplined one, organized with the relentless logic of a factory floor.

This is the core of the world's paradox. There are no brick smokestacks or rusted gears, yet the entire ecosystem functions as a single, massive engine. You can hear it: a low, subsonic thrum that lives in your teeth and vibrates your sternum. It is quiet in the way an operating theater is quiet—the hush of immense, coordinated activity. Massive, tubular roots pulse rhythmically, pumping a viscous, glowing fluid through the bedrock. The air smells of ozone, wet earth, and overheated copper.

The fauna is abundant and serves as the machine’s mobile components. Herds of six-legged, beetle-like grazers the size of delivery vans trundle across the sharp stone, their mandibles clicking with the precision of hydraulic presses. They do not graze on leaves; they scrape lichens that process minerals from the rock, excreting refined ores as they move. Above, avian-things with membrane wings and too-many eyes glide in soundless patrols, their movements darting and syncopated, like shuttles in a loom.

Water is present, but not as a gentle rain. It is dampness—a constant, sweating condensation that slicks every surface. The air is so saturated with moisture that breathing feels like drinking. Pockets of superheated steam vent from fissures in the rock, creating sudden, localized fogbanks that dissipate as quickly as they form. Rivers, where they exist, are sluggish and heavy with silt and metallic runoff, moving like oil through the sharp-cut valleys.

And the whole place is unstable. The "turbulent" nature of the High Loom is terrifying. The ground does not stay still. Through the mist, you can watch landmasses rearrange themselves. A jagged ridge might shudder, groan with a sound felt more than heard, and suddenly invert, becoming a ravine. Islands of vegetation detach and float upward, bobbing in the dense air like corks in a storm before crashing down miles away. It is a chaotic state of continuous creation and destruction, a landscape that refuses to settle into a fixed geography.

The weirdness lies in the reality breakdown. The constant flux of the terrain causes hallucinatory glitches. A tree might flicker out of existence for a heartbeat, replaced by a view of a storm-tossed ocean, before snapping back to its copper-green reality. Shadows detach from their objects and move independently. The horizon is a fractal mess of repeating peaks, suggesting the world is larger than physics should allow. The laws of cause and effect seem to be mere suggestions here; the turbulence of the land occasionally tears a hole in the fabric of the now, letting the future or the past bleed through in silent, ghostly echoes.

The inhabitants do not seem to notice. The grazers continue their clacking progress even as the ground beneath them splits and reforms. The avian shuttles adjust their flight patterns to the new topology without hesitation, as if recalibrating to a machine they have always served.

There is no builder here. No architect. The industrial efficiency was not imposed; it *emerged*. It is what life becomes when the pressure is high enough and the chaos is constant enough that only a mechanism can persist. The machine was not built. The machine is what the land *is*.

You stand on the edge of a obsidian cliff, watching a floating forest drift silently upward into the violet gloom. The air tastes like copper and steam. The ground trembles beneath your feet, not with fear, but with the rhythmic pumping of a world-sized heart. You are not in a garden. You are inside a living engine, and it is turning.
