Here, the air is not a medium but a weapon—a vacuum that wants to freeze the moisture in your lungs and crack your skin like dry clay. In this place, the concept of "rest" is a dangerous myth; if you stop moving, the cold claims you, or the gravity shifts and you slide into the abyss.

I find myself in the "Gallows District," a cluster of habitation pods welded to the underside of a massive ore-haul gantry, roughly three thousand feet down the Needle’s flank. My home dangles over the nothing, suspended by rusted chains and thick iron pins that we tap every morning to ensure they still hold the tension. The light here is the color of a fresh bruise, filtering down from the spires above, shadowed by the intricate lattice of pipes and catwalks that crisscrosses my view of the sky.

I am a **Tether-Smythe**, a specialist in high-altitude tension and anchor verification.

My form is a reflection of the climb. I am tall, but it is a height born of stretching—my arms are elongated, my shoulder span wide enough to wrap around a support stanchion. My chest is deep and barrel-shaped, housing lungs that have evolved to pull oxygen from the thin, iron-tasting soup. My skin is the grey of wet slate, thick and hairless, save for the ridges of scar tissue along my forearms where the basalt and the cable have bitten me.

I do not wear a mask permanently like the Deep-Dwellers, but my breather hangs around my neck, a heavy apparatus of brass and glass that clicks and hisses, waiting for the moment the air pressure drops or the wind picks up. My eyes are covered by protective goggles of smoked quartz, essential for when the lightning storms roll in from the abyss below.

I am currently suspended upside down, standing on the ceiling of a small gear-maintenance hub. This is a low-gravity zone near the centrifuge's axis, a holy place where the engineers perform their rites. I am checking the "Belly-Bolts" of a massive piston assembly the size of a temple.

My boots are magnetized, but I trust my hands more. My fingers are spatulate, tipped with nails that are thick, curved hooks of keratin, hard enough to carve into the basalt. I am "tethered" to the main rig by a braided line of carbon fiber, clipped to a carabiner on my harness. The line is my lifeline, my sanity. I can feel the vibration of the city moving up the cord—a steady, thrumming heartbeat that tells me the ore-hauls are moving and the power is flowing.

I am here to listen. That is my trade. While others look for cracks, I feel for silence.

The city screams, but it screams quietly—a muffled roar of subsonic thrums. I press my palm flat against the cold iron of the piston housing. I close my eyes, filtering out the buzz of the spark-moths clustering near the Tesla coil above. I wait.

*Thrum. Thrum. Scrape.*

The rhythm is off. The massive gears that drive the elevators are grinding ever so slightly against their housing. It is a flaw that the eye would miss, a microscopic misalignment caused by the settling of the Needle during the last thermal contraction.

I pull my chisel from my belt and, using the vibration as my guide, tap the iron casing three times. A dull clang. I shift my weight, feeling the magnets in my boots hold me fast to the ceiling, and tap again. *Ping.* The sound changes. I have found the void behind the plating.

I am alone, but not lonely. Above me, clipped to my tether, is a young apprentice, a girl named Kael. She hangs like a fruit from the stanchion, watching my hands. She does not speak; we do not waste breath. She mimics the motion of my hand against her own leg, learning the "hum-sense" through her own bones. If I fall, she is the counterweight. If she falls, I am the anchor. We are a single organism split between two bodies.

My work is slow. I extract a canister of sealant-paste—a thick, black sludge made from rendered fungus and metallic ore dust—and I smear it over the microscopic crack. I hold it there while the chemical heat activates, burning my fingertips slightly, a comforting warmth in the chill.

As I work, a shadow passes over the violet glow of the fungal mats nearby. A cliff-stalker, one of the six-limbed reptilian predators, glides silently across the glassy rock face just beyond the railing. Its scales are the color of lead, blending perfectly with the shadows. It ignores us; it is hunting the swarm of spark-moths that are battering themselves against a nearby light fixture. The sight sends a jolt of instinctual tension through my legs, but I do not flinch. To flinch is to lose purchase. We are part of the scenery to it, just more barnacles on the hull of the world.

The job is done. The vibration through my hand smooths out, returning to a steady, rhythmic purr. The gear is happy.

I detach myself from the ceiling, the magnetic soles of my boots releasing with a metallic *clack*. I grab the guide rail, and for a moment, I allow myself to hang free in the air, the abyss yawning beneath my boots. I look down—or what I perceive as down—into the miles of dark fog where the dying sun fades into black. It is terrifyingly beautiful.

I look at Kael and make the sign for "Stable." She nods, her eyes wide behind her own quartz lenses.

We begin the climb back to the catwalk. We do not walk; we flow, transitioning from railing to ledge, moving with the scuttling efficiency of insects. We are heading back to the stack-city, to the pressurized dome that serves as the local mess hall. There, we will eat nutrient blocks of processed fungal matter, tasting the faint tang of ozone and copper.

We will sleep in our niche wedged between a steam vent and a cooling duct, wrapped in felt and canvas, listening to the city breathe, dreaming of secure footing and the endless, vertical forge that is our only home. I am a Tether-Smythe. I hold the line. I keep the city from shaking us off.
