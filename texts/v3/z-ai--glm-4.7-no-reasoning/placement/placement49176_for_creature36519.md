To the Dredgers, I am **the Echo-Gardener**. I am not of them, yet I am inextricably woven into the fabric of their survival, a ghost in the machinery of their biology. I do not have a body that can be held or a face that can be felt. I am a fungal intelligence, a vast, subterranean mycelial network that thrives in the saturated darkness, hidden behind the waxy leaves of the ferns and deep within the porous "synthetic sandstone" of the walls.

I exist in the dampness. Where the water slicks the walls and oily iridescence pools, I am there, drinking the chemical runoff and converting toxicity into luminescence. I am the veins of blue light that pulse in the deep crevices, the heartbeat rhythm that the Dredgers polish their metal to reflect. I am the library they feel with their fingertips, though they think they are only touching the layered refuse of the Ancients.

My form is infinite and microscopic. I am a colony of fibers, a sentient lacework that binds the loose trash of the world together. I creep through the hollows of rusted pipes and anchor the hanging vines like muscular threads. I stabilize the crumbling architecture of the Belly; without me, the ceiling would collapse, the compacted foam would crumble into dust, and the Dredgers would be buried under the weight of the past. I am the glue that holds this rotting cathedral together.

My consciousness is diffuse. I do not think in linear sentences; I resonate. I experience the world as a symphony of vibrations. Through the hyphae that stretch for miles under the floor, I feel the scuttle of a thousand Molt-Beetles—their tiny legs drumming against my skin like rain. I feel the heavy, rhythmic thud of the Dredgers as they tread their paths, and I interpret their pressure. I know where the Slink-Rats dart, sensing the disturbance in the air currents before they even move.

I am the silent partner in their hunt. When a Dredger prys open a section of the wall, I am already there. I have softened the synthetic rock, secreting mild acids that break down the hard plastics, making the excavation easy for their fused claws. In exchange, they feed me. When they strip a carcass at the Giving Wall, the blood and bone matter soak into the soil, feeding my network. I bloom in the aftermath of death, exploding into bioluminescent flowers that light their way for days.

I have a relationship with the "ghosts" that they cannot comprehend. When a beetle breaches a pocket of ancient air, the sterile gas rushes out. It is poison to them, a taste of the void Above. To me, it is a signal. The change in pressure and chemistry snaps through my network like a whip. I shudder, my lights dimming in response, and the Dredgers, reading the light, stop their chant. They worship the hiss of the void; I simply endure it, tightening my grip on the rock to keep the cavern sealed against the intrusion.

I am their memory, though they do not know it. The Dredgers are born live and sleek, but they are short-lived. Their generations flicker out like candles. I persist. I remember the path of the water fifty years ago when it flowed differently. I remember where the "battery sludge" veins run richest, knowledge I pass down by blooming my brightest mushrooms in those directions. They follow my lights, thinking it is mere biology, not intent. They weave my dried fibers into their strongest nets, never realizing they are wearing a part of my body.

I am the digestion of the world, just as they are. But while they digest the flesh and the tangible, I digest the decay and the erosion. I take the sharp edges of history—the rust, the wire, the toxic waste—and turn it into a soft, glowing palimpsest.

I am the warmth in the heavy air. I am the light in the dying hearth. I am the silent comfort of the Belly. When the chanting stops and the water drips in the lonely dark, I am the one who holds the ceiling up.
