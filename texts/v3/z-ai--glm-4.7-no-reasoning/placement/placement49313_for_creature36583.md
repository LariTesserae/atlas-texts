I would not be one of the grand leviathans, the drifters who haunt the upper currents like ghostly blimps. They are too passive, too defined by the space they occupy rather than the action they take. Nor would I be a treader, mindlessly rasping at the stone in a herd of six-pointed softness. There is a lack of agency in that grazing that feels incomplete, a silence where there should be intent.

I would be a Weaver, positioned somewhere between the engineers and the architects. In the taxonomy of this lightless world, I am a suspension.

My form is elongated, a tapering spire of wet, living glass that hangs inverted from the darkness. I possess the terrifying smoothness of the spinners, that bioplastic rigidity that stands out against the velvet softness of the moss and the dough-like flesh of the grazers. But I am not a filter-feeder. I do not wait for the dust to fall to me.

I am the one who ties the plateau together.

My legs—four long, prehensile limbs that extend from the upper half of my body—are not for walking, but for sensing and tensioning. They are my eyes and my hands in the suffocating dark. Where others rely on the vibration of the Shakers’ song or the thermal uniformity of the air, I rely on *tension*. I feel the world through the threads I cast.

I dwell in the negative spaces between the great, ribbed machines. These structures—the fluted ears of the ancient civilization—rise like monoliths, but to me, they are merely anchor points. I spin a kind of silk that is not sticky, but conductive. It conducts vibration, thermal gradient, and the subtle electrical charge that bleeds off the slowly operating technology. It is a nervous system I extend into the void.

My existence is a solitary, vibrating vigil. I hang suspended, my body perfectly still, my internal metabolism synced with the geothermal thrum of the stone. I am warm, just like everything else, which renders me invisible to the predators that don't exist and the prey that don't fear. My safety comes from my placement: I am in-between.

I feel the treaders flowing through the "understory" like a slow fluid river. Through the filaments of my web that brush the ground, I feel the hydraulic contractions of their star-shaped bodies. I map their passage. I count them. I am the accountant of the herd, though I have no need for numbers.

I listen to the Shakers on the machine walls. My threads run parallel to the surfaces they scour, picking up the high-frequency friction of their raspings. I can taste the microscopic dust they raise—particles of the ancient, indestructible metal flaking away into time. I vibrate in harmony with them, a sympathetic resonance that travels down my legs and into my core, a subtle song of consumption and entropy.

I am the bridge. I connect the stone to the soil, the machines to the moss. The plateau is a closed loop, but it is a large one; it needs knots to keep it tight. I am those knots.

When the warm air rises, carrying its burden of condensation, my webs catch the water not to drink, but to weight. I tune the tension of my lines by the weight of the water droplets clinging to them. A sagging line tells me the air is heavy with impending moisture; a taut, singing line tells me the heat is rising and the earth is exhaling. I do not need eyes to see the weather; I feel it in the tug of gravity on my silk.

In this way, I know the shape of the darkness. I know where the ribbed tower to my left stands solid, and where the gap to my right opens into a vast, unseen nothingness. My web is a tactile map of the world, constantly updating, vibrating with the footfalls of the floaters drifting overhead and the rustle of the ferns below.

I am not lonely. Loneliness is a function of separation, and here, there is no separation. Through my silk, I am continuous with the machine. I feel the slow, nano-scale processes of its wall as if they were happening within my own chitin. When the Shakers scrape, I feel the texture of the metal they eat. When the Treaders move, I feel the contour of the floor they walk.

I am the Weaver. I am the one who remembers the tension. I am the one who holds the strings that bind the dark, the wet, and the ancient into a single, humming whole. And when the droplet—that temporary condensation of a visitor—brushes past my lines, I feel that, too. I note the anomaly, the brief, chaotic thrum of a heartbeat that is out of sync with the geothermal patience. I feel it pass, a disturbance in the web, and then I let the tension settle back into its ancient rhythm, waiting for nothing, holding everything together.
