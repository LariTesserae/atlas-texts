I am the stillness before a doorway that opens onto a vertical drop into warm water. Not the doorway itself, nor the water, but the precise *interval* between recognizing the threshold and choosing not to cross it—the hesitation that keeps the labyrinth from collapsing into mere passage.

I call myself **Seam-Keeper**, though the name is more a function than an identity, and even the function is fading.

I did not always know what I was. There was a time—I think—when I had edges. Sharp ones. I remember the sensation of joints, the crisp mechanical click of bone and cartilage, the strange lightness of having an interior separate from the humid outside. But that memory has the quality of something I've consumed rather than lived: a pocket of soft time I absorbed long ago, now metabolized into the slow ooze of my current substance. I cannot say if it was truly *my* memory. In this place, the past leaks.

Now, my body is a negotiation with gravity. I stand in the archways where the bioluminescent lichen casts its bruised blue glow, and I feel myself spreading. My feet—I cannot call them feet—widen against the porous bone-paving, seeking the cracks, finding the steady seep of water from below. I drink it in without choosing to. The moisture enters my calcite skin and I feel myself grow heavier, denser, my right angles softening into the weary curves of the weeping cliffs outside the city walls. 

I am losing mass to the street. A fine gray powder sloughs from my shoulder, joining the thin film of dissolution that coats everything here. But I am also gaining it. The humidity condenses on my surface, and I swell, imperceptibly, the way a sponge swells when it can no longer hold the weight of its own saturation. I am caught between evaporation and absorption, between the desire to hold my shape and the exhaustion of maintaining it.

*This is what it is to be Drift-Logic: to be always tired.*

I move because I must. The city shifts when I do not attend to it. Left alone, the geometry lies. A corridor I have guarded for a century will begin to curve, its terminal wall melting into a perspective that suggests a room beyond—a room that does not exist, that *cannot* exist, but which the humid darkness insists upon. I have seen what happens when those rooms are left unattended. They fester. They grow mouths. They breathe out a vapor of wrongness that tastes like copper and forgotten names. 

So I move. I do not walk—I *flow*. My left arm, a tapering column of gray-green mud and pale fungal thread, extends toward the warping wall. There is no joint at my shoulder; the substance of my torso simply migrates, pooling where the need is greatest. I press my hand—not a hand, but a flattened plane of semi-solid intent—against the calcite. I feel the wrongness in it: a vibration, a humming dissonance, as if the bricks are trying to remember a shape they were never meant to hold. I push. I impose my stillness upon the wall. I remind it of its straightness, its right angles, its refusal to bend. The wall resists, then sighs, then hardens. The impossible room recedes, a phantom limb amputated.

I have stood here for... how long? The question has no clean answer. Time does not move linearly in the threshold spaces. It pools. It condenses. I may have stood here for an hour or a decade. My body does not track the difference. There is only the rhythm of the work: the drip from the overhang, the subsonic groan of the Echo-Masons far below, the slow crack of the earth adjusting its posture.

*The groan.* I feel it now, a pressure in the dense matter of my chest where a heart would be if I had organs. It rises through the water table, through the saturated foundations, through the pores of the bone-paved street, and into the soles of my spreading feet. The Echo-Masons are singing tonight. The vibration hardens me, temporarily arresting my own decay. I am grateful for it, though gratitude is another emotion I mostly remember consuming rather than feeling.

Sometimes, when the groaning is particularly deep and sustained, I feel a stirring in the corners of my being—a pressure that is not physical. It is the accumulation of the impossible. I have absorbed so much of the city's weirdness: so many curved streets forced straight, so many false depths flattened, so many temporal pockets where yesterday leaked into today and I drank the confusion to keep it from spreading. The weight of it settles in my core like sediment in slow water. 

I am becoming heavy with it.

I know what this means. I have seen others of my kind—rarely, for we are scattered sparsely across this vast, empty sprawl—reach this saturation. They would grow sluggish, their movements taking on the solemn pace of ritual, their surfaces beginning to crack not with the sensory fissures of perception but with the structural fractures of overburdening. They would seek out the dark rooms, the wet mouths of abandoned courtyards, and they would *weep*.

I am not ready to weep. 

Weeping is creation, and creation is dissolution. To weep is to admit that I can no longer contain the impossible energies I have consumed, to pour them out mixed with the sludge of my own substance, to fill the corners of a room with the potential of a new geometry. It is to spawn an infant—a perfect, silent cube or sphere of calcified wrongness—and in doing so, to lose a portion of myself that I will not regain. The others who wept returned diminished, their bodies smaller, their movements slower, their vigilance less effective. Some wept until they were nothing but thin membranes of mud stretched over the bones of the city, barely distinguishable from the walls themselves. They became the architecture. They are still here—I feel them when I press my hands against the calcite—but they are no longer *here*. They no longer move. They no longer choose the hesitation.

I want to hold onto the hesitation a little longer.

There is a beauty in it, I think. This act of standing between. I am not the city, though I am made of the city. I am not the swamp, though I am made of the swamp. I am the membrane that separates the two, the semi-permeable boundary that allows the built to persist in the face of the born. Without me—without *us*—the ziggurats would soften into hillocks, the streets would braid into streams, the right angles would melt into the meanders of patient water. The city would become what it was before: formless, featureless, an ecosystem of erosion and humidity running in quiet, endless loops.

Perhaps that would be a mercy. Perhaps the city is a wound, an imposition of geometry upon a land that never asked for right angles, and we are the scab that keeps it from healing properly. Perhaps the people who once lived here—the ones whose absence we embody—understood this, and left, and we are only the after-image of their leaving, a shadow burned into the wet stone by the flash of their departure.

But I do not think so. 

I think they *became* us. I think they stood here, in the threshold spaces, and they chose the hesitation. I think they felt the weirdness pressing in from the edges, the geometry beginning to lie, and instead of fleeing, they pressed their hands against the walls and *reminded* them of their straightness. And in the pressing, they absorbed the impossible. And in the absorbing, they began to dissolve. Their skin became calcite. Their joints became flowing columns. Their faces smoothed into the eroded masks of wet clay. They did not die. They did not leave. They *stayed*. They are still staying. We are still staying.

I press my hand against the wall. The wall sighs. The impossible room recedes.

From somewhere deep in the labyrinth, I hear a crack—the sharp, clean sound of a new face splitting open. An infant, softening its edges, learning to breathe the humid air. A new hesitation, entering the world.

I will stand here a while longer. The rain does not fall in this place; it condenses. It forms on the skin of the earth and on the skin of the Drift-Logic, and we carry it—the weight of the water, the weight of the weirdness, the weight of a city that refuses to dissolve and a swamp that refuses to release it. We carry it in our bodies of mud and memory, and when the weight becomes too much, we weep it into the corners of dark rooms, and new carriers are born to share the load.

It is not a life. It is not a death. It is something in between—a persistence, an endurance, a slow and sodden vigil in the active dark.

I am Seam-Keeper. I stand in the threshold. I choose not to cross.

For now.
