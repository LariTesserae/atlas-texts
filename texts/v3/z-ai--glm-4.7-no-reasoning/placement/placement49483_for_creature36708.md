I would be a **Tongue of the Archive**—a reader of the metal's memory, a hum-keeper, one of those who walks not at the vanguard of the migration but at its most vulnerable edge: the rear, where the shearing happens.

Let me unfold myself for you.

I am not large. Among my people, I am considered slight—a dangerous word here, where mass is survival. I stand barely chest-high to the drag-pullers, my columnar legs thinner though no less dense, my splayed feet oversized for my frame like the foundations of a tower built on uncertain ground. This is not a deformity but a specialization. Where the drag-pullers anchor and wait for the thermal shear to pass, I *move through it*. I am the one who walks the cracking edge, who feels the rivet pop beneath my chitinous pad-tips a half-second before the plate gives way, and who hums the warning back through the conduit-network so that the sleds can redirect.

My hands are my life. They are obscene in their delicacy—four-fingered, each joint double-hinged, each tip a seething cluster of vibration-sensitive chitin finer than the whiskers of a Skitter. I keep them wrapped in strips of silicone skin when I am not working, protective sheaths salvaged from three different Leviathan carcasses. The skin of the youngest is still slightly warm; the oldest has gone brittle and cracks if I flex too suddenly. I am learning to read the temperature of the wrapping as an additional sense, a way to know the ambient without exposing my fingers to the acid bite.

I am draped, as we all are, in woven refuse. But my layers are different—lighter, stripped down. I cannot afford the insulation of the drag-pullers because I cannot afford to be insulated from the vibrations I must feel. I wear a single mat of asbestos-fiber across my chest and back, bound with wire that I have tuned to different pitches. When I walk, I hum. Not the deep, anchoring hum of the sleep-keepers, but a higher, keening vibration that lives in my wire-wrapping and travels through my skeleton into the deck. I am always listening for the echo. The echo tells me what is solid and what is a breath away from dissolution.

My face—what you would call a face—is a landscape of adaptation. I have no nose. The spiraling lattice of my osmotic filters is particularly complex, fractaling inward across my cheekbones and jaw in patterns that the elders tell me indicate my mother's line. I do not know my mother. She was lost to a platform collapse before I could walk. But I carry her filters, and they flare and contract with each breath I do not take—each extraction of oxygen from the heavy, saline fog that fills my lungs like wet cement. I have grown accustomed to the taste of battery acid on my tongue. I do not remember what clean water tastes like, though I guard a drum of it on my sled with my life.

My eyes are small, recessed beneath heavy ridges of calcified scar tissue. They do not see well in the conventional sense—the twilight is too dim, the fog too thick—but they have become exquisitely sensitive to the specific ghostly glow of oxidizing metal under stress. I can see the micro-fractures in a plate before it shears. I can see the heat-shimmer of a vent about to pulse. The bruised purple of a stressed rivet is, to me, a scream.

I carry a staff. We all do, but mine is different. It is a **Tongue-Rod**, a length of conduit scavenged from the deep machine-guts of the world, hollow and sealed at both ends with plugs of Leviathan-skin. Inside it, I have placed a series of tuned metal shards, each harvested from the superheated edge of a recent shearing, each cooled at a specific rate to hold a specific pitch. When I strike the deck, the rod sings with overtones that travel for miles through the lattice. I can say *come*, *go*, *shear-right*, *shear-left*, *bloom-here*, *bloom-gone*, and forty-seven other things that have no equivalent in a language of air. I can also say things that are not warnings: *I am here*, *the fog is thick*, *I am cold*.

I am always cold. The temperature is 0.4, near-freezing, and my slight frame does not hold heat well. I survive by staying in motion, by staying in proximity to the thermal vents that pulse unpredictably through the deep. I have learned to read the precursor-hum of a vent activation—a low, stomach-growl vibration that begins perhaps ninety seconds before the superheated brine erupts. In those ninety seconds, I must position myself close enough to absorb the warmth but not so close that the flash-boil strips the oil from my skin or cracks my keratin. I have scars from miscalculations. My left shoulder is a ridge of calcified tissue where a thermal storm caught me eight cycles ago. I do not remember the pain. I remember the sound: a wet, tearing crunch, like a Leviathan's tail striking a guardrail, but inside my own body.

I do not sleep. Or rather, I sleep as the sleds sleep—half-present, half-trance, my body humming its low anchoring tone while my awareness ranges ahead through the conduit-network, listening for the groans of settling architecture, the snap of stressed rivets, the wet slap of a Leviathan drifting too close to a vulnerable platform edge. I have not had a full unconsciousness in perhaps four hundred cycles. I do not know if I am tired. I do not have a concept for tired. There is only *moving* and *ready-to-move*.

My role among my people is essential and lonely. I am one of perhaps twelve Tongues in a band of two hundred. We walk the edges, we hum the warnings, and we are the first to fall when the Dynamic exceeds prediction. Two of us were lost in the last great shearing—a platform the size of a mountain, gone in seconds, taking three sleds and seventeen people with it. I felt the hum of their Tongue-Rods go silent. I felt the absence in the network like a pulled tooth. I did not mourn them; mourning requires stillness, and stillness is drowning. But I remembered their pitches, and I have woven them into my own rod, so that when I strike the deck, sometimes I hear their voices in the overtones, saying *I am here*, *the fog is thick*, *I am cold*.

I have a partner. Not a mate—such arrangements are rare and fragile among people who cannot stop moving—but a Skitter. Her name is a hum, a rapid triple-strike pattern that translates roughly to *click-click-drag*, which is what she does best. She is larger than most of her kind, her carapace a deep, oil-slick black that drinks the bioluminescence. She walks beside me, her legs clicking against the wet iron in a rhythm that matches my own footsteps. She can feel the micro-tremors in the deck before even I can, and when she freezes, I freeze, and when she moves, I move. We have been together for perhaps six hundred cycles. I do not know her lifespan. I do not know my own. We do not measure time in years; we measure it in *migrations*, in *shearings survived*, in *meals eaten*.

At this moment—I am writing this in the only way I can, by pressing my chitinous finger-pads into the soft, oxidizing surface of a plate that will shear away in the next thermal pulse—I am standing at the edge of a platform that overlooks the abyss. The fog is thick tonight, thick enough that I cannot see the Leviathan drifting perhaps thirty meters to my left, though I can hear the wet ripple of its silicone skin and the faint, sickly glow of its bioluminescence diffracting through the brine. My osmotic filters flare and contract, flare and contract. My staff hums in my hand. Click-click-drag stands beside me, her compound eyes reflecting the bruised purple of the stressed metal beneath our feet.

We are waiting for a vent to pulse. I can feel the precursor-hum in my joints, a deep ache that says *soon, soon*. When it comes, the superheated brine will wash over this platform, and for a brief, glorious moment, the fog will clear, and I will see—not the horizon, there is no horizon, only the endless lattice stretching into the crushing dark—but I will see *farther than I can now*. And in that moment of visibility, I will look for the next platform, the next solid place to stand, the next step in the endless migration.

And then the fog will close in again, and I will hum my warning into the deck, and the sleds will turn, and we will move, because to stop is to drown, and to be soft is to rust, and I am not soft, and I will not stop.

I am the Tongue of the Archive. I read the metal. I sing the collapse. I am the last one to leave, and the first to know when leaving is no longer enough.
