I am the fracture that remembers the cold before the heat.

You would find me in the **Sump**, the lowest point of the depression where the basin’s belly slopes inward, gathering the runoff. I am not on the street; the streets are for the gliders and the High-Born. I am beneath the iron grate, in the dark, slick underbelly of the district where the condensation weeps thick enough to drink, and the vibration of the deep pistons is strongest. Here, the moss is not a decoration; it is a carpet, thick and spongy, glowing with the sickly green of deep-sea radiation.

I am the **Sump-Keeper**, or perhaps simply, **The Root**.

To look at me, you might mistake me for a pile of discarded scrap left over from a frantic expansion. I am not tall like the workers who glide above. I am hunched, knotted, and broad. My body has surrendered the verticality of the human shape for the stability of a stone. My spine is curved into a permanent arch, a bridge over the streams of hot effluent that flow through my territory.

My flesh is a testament to the city's hunger. I am not wrapped in a suit; I am *encrusted*. Heavy plates of iron, rusted through with a patina of orange and black, have been grafted onto my shoulders and arms. They are not armor; they are heat-sinks. They absorb the warmth of the engines and radiate it slowly into the air around me. To touch me would be to burn your hand on the metal, then freeze it on the damp, exposed skin of my wrists.

But it is my interaction with the Moss that defines me.

Where the workers carry the moss upon them as a symbiote, I *am* the soil from which it springs. Thick, black vines—some as thick as a man’s wrist—have burrowed through the iron plates on my back, piercing the dermis and anchoring themselves to my musculature. They do not hurt; they feed. They draw the rich, thermal energy from my blood, and in exchange, they filter the poisons from the water I drink and the air I breathe.

My face is a landscape of its own. I lack the rebreathers of the workers; I have no need for them. The moss grows high on my cheeks, framing a mouth that is sealed shut, sewn closed by years of scar tissue. I do not eat food. I do not speak. My nose is gone, smoothed over, leaving only two slits that flutter as I sample the chemical composition of the steam.

How do I perceive the world? Through the **Weft**.

I have jacked myself into the nervous system of the district. Thick cables, insulated with braided hair and tar, run from the base of my skull and merge into the walls of the Sump. I feel the city not as a place, but as a pulse in my nerves. When a pressure valve sticks three miles away, I feel it as a itch on my left forearm. When a new district is being born and the scaffolding screams under the weight of molten slag, I hear it as a low, droning hum in my teeth.

My role is lubrication and integration. When the workers sleep in their torpor, standing in their rows, I am the one who ensures the heat is distributed evenly. I move through the shadows—not walking, but pulling myself along the pipes with arms that have elongated, the fingers lengthening into spider-like, multi-jointed tools. I clear the blockages in the vents. I scrape the rime from the gears that the workers cannot reach.

I am the silence between the hisses.

I remember the time before the machine. My memory is long, longer than the iron that encases me. I remember when the basin was just mud and the sky was a vast, terrifying emptiness. That is why I stay in the dark. I am afraid of the raw, unfinished newness of the city above. I cling to the old dark, the warm dark, the digestive tract of the beast.

Sometimes, the otter-things come down to the Sump to play in the warm water. They see me, huddled and immense, glowing with the light of a thousand bioluminescent leaves. They chitter at me, their voices sounding like drowned laughter. I do not chitter back. I simply vibrate. I send a low-frequency thrum through the water and the floor—a warning, or perhaps a greeting—that makes their many eyes widen in instinctual awe.

To the workers, I am a fixture. A part of the plumbing. They step over me as I crouch in a tunnel, tightening a bolt the size of a man’s head. They do not know that I am the one who regulates the flow of heat that keeps their moss alive. They do not know that when the city tries to shiver itself apart, it is my grip on the pipes that holds it together.

I am the Sump-Keeper. I am the moss and the iron and the memory of the mud. I am the cold hand on the hot valve, the quiet in the roar, the anchor in the turbulent dark.
