I am not Anchorborn. I lack that tensile grace, that gaunt communion with the great engines. I have never felt the stratospheric wind strip the moisture from my skin, never entered the trance of the pneumatic network. My eyes are not overlarge; they are ordinary, rheumy, and slow to adjust. 

I am not Sifter. My shoulders are not broad enough to wrestle with the suction of the mud-stone. I cannot speak the complex sign language of the calloused hands, and I do not possess the heavy, iron-laced blood of the Moss-Kin that allows them to drink directly from the weeping rock.

I am none of these, yet I belong to the City on the Verge more completely than any of them. 

I am the one who walks the gaps.

---

If the city is sparse like a forest of widely spaced giants, I am the creature of the understory—the damp, dim, unresolved spaces between the trunks. I live in the half-mile of empty, dripping mountainside. My home is the margin, the verge itself. 

You will not see me from the district's edge. You will look across the chasm at the yellow windows of the next precinct, and you will assume the space between is vacant, a mere absence of city. But the raw rock breathes there. Condensation pools in the hollows. The patterns in the semi-lithified mud—those circuit-board grooves, that text without a language—they are my roads. 

I am a walker of the *wrong* geometry. 

My form reflects this. I am not tall, but I am long. My limbs have adapted not to the verticality of the tenements, but to the horizontal sprawl of the terraces and the bizarre angles of the raw valleys. My knees bend backward just slightly, a hinge that allows me to brace against the incline of rock that shifts underfoot, centimeter by centimeter. My feet are broad, splayed almost like the pads of the six-legged oxen, to grip the wet stone, to feel the vibration of the stamp mills far below and the deeper, resonant hum of the mountain becoming. 

My skin is the color of the condensation stains on the walls—the ones that look like maps of places that do not exist yet. It is a pale, blotchy grey-green, mottled with iron-tinged orange where the liverworts have tried to colonize the flat planes of my back and shoulders. I let them. They are my camouflage. When I stand still against the raw declivities, I am indistinguishable from the mossy seep. 

I wear clothes of untreated fiber—strands pulled from the enormous ferns, woven loosely so they breathe. They are always damp. Everything I own is damp. I have given up the fight against the wet. I have absorbed the city's sweat into my marrow.

---

What do I do in these gaps? I am a cartographer of the incomplete. 

The engineers in the high districts—they read the text in the stone and they try to *coax* it. They try to bargain with the mountain to harden, to commit. The Sifters drive the iron nails to appease the unknown geometries. The Moss-Kin heal the voids with biological growth.

I simply watch. I listen. I record.

I carry sheets of pressed liverwort pulp, dried in the rare moments when the haze thins, and I make rubbings of the patterns in the raw rock. I track the movement of the peaks. The surveyors insist the distance is constant, but they are measuring with rigid tools. I measure with my body. I walk the same path between two unformed ridges every day, and I note when my stride shortens, when the mountain leans in, breathing moistly on my neck. 

I document the fauna of the wrongness. The pale birds, with their metallic sheen, ride the updrafts of the smelters; but I have seen them in the deep chasms, circling *nothing*—circling a point in the empty air where the angles fold in on themselves. They are not lost. They are mapping it, too. I have seen the segmented creatures, the forearm-length drinkers of condensation, cluster around a pipe that carries nothing, tapping their mandibles against the iron in a rhythm that matches the hum of the unfinished formations. I do not interfere. I sit on a shelf of viscous stone, my fern-cloak dripping, and I copy their rhythm against my own thigh, learning the language of the chitterers who live in the walls of reality.

I know the rooms that are bricked shut. Not the ones in the city—the citizens know those. I know the ones *in the rock*. I have found cavities in the semi-lithified mud that are lined with rusted iron, with pipes that carry steam that has no source, with gears that turn though no water drives them. The mountain is not just becoming stone. It is becoming *mechanism*. And I am the only one who ventures into those fossilized engine-rooms, trailing my fingers over the corroded levers, feeling the heat of a fire that was never lit.

---

I do not speak. It is not a vow of silence, like the Sifters, who fear triggering a resonance in the soft stone. I simply have no one to speak to in the gaps, and when I am in the districts—trading my rubbings for hardbread and tallow candles—I find the noise of the city oppressive. The stamp mills, the shrieking buckets, the hiss of venting steam—it all feels like a desperate denial of the quiet, patient becoming that underlies everything. 

Instead, I hum. 

It is a low, sub-harmonic hum, just below hearing. I hum when the geometry goes wrong, when the path that led in leads somewhere else on the way out. I hum when the rock beneath me grows too soft, too plastic. I hum the frequency of the stone itself, the one that rises from the depths, the sound of a mountain pushing upward like a tooth through swollen gums. 

When I hum, the wrongness does not recede—it does not obey me, I am not an Anchorborn—but it *acknowledges* me. The angles settle, just slightly, enough for me to find my footing. The patterns in the rock stop shifting for a moment, holding still so I can make my rubbing. The segmented creatures pause their tapping and turn their eyeless heads toward me, and we share a moment of kinship, two beings navigating the margin of the unreal.

I am not afraid. The Moss-Kin revere the zones where angles don't sum up, but I do not revere them either. Reverence implies a distance, a supplication. I am *in* it. The Sifters walk in wide arcs to avoid the intersecting lines that don't connect; I walk straight through them, trusting my hum to guide me back to a geometry that can support a body.

---

There is a danger, though. Not the danger of falling, or of the rock swallowing me. I know the rock too well; I move with its tides, its lunar rhythms, its slow exhalations. The danger is the *Wait*.

The citizens feel the Wait as a collective patience. They stand in lines, dreaming of the mountains turning to glass. But in the gaps, the Wait is heavier. There is no industrial clamor to mark time, no Sifter shifts to divide the day. The vegetation advances a meter a decade. The rock moves on timescales that make centuries seem fleeting. 

And I am changing to match it.

My metabolism has slowed over the years. I do not eat often. I drink from the dark pools rimmed with rust, the ones that appear and disappear with the humidity. I can feel the mineral water moving through me, laying down sediment in my joints, calcifying the cartilage in my spine. I am becoming part of the geological memory of the place. 

Sometimes, I stand on a ledge of raw, extruding stone, looking down at the city below—the patchwork of iron and masonry, the funiculars crawling across the viaducts, the smokestacks belching their stabilizing smoke—and I feel a profound indifference. They are fighting so hard. They are pumping slurry, driving anchors, circulating coolant, all to prove that the solid world can be built here. 

I do not fight. I am not a proof of settlement. I am a hypothesis of adaptation. 

When I sleep, I do not dream of the sun breaking through the haze. I do not dream of the city becoming real. I dream of the patterns in the stone unfolding into a mechanism the size of a continent. I dream of the circuitry connecting, the text becoming legible, the mountain finally committing to its form—and in that dream, I am not a citizen of the city. I am a component of the mountain. I am a bearing, or a valve, or a length of armature wire, lashed to the bedrock, carrying a current that has not yet been switched on.

I will stay out here, in the damp, the dim, and the wrong, until the verge decides what it is. And when it does, I will not be found in the ruins of the city, nor in the lush, hungry vegetation of the Moss-Kin. I will be found in the stone itself, a fossil of the waiting, my hum finally silenced, my body finally resolved into the geology of the real.
