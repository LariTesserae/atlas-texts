I am a vibration first. Before I am a name, before I am a body with limbs that reach into the dripping dark, I am a frequency carried through the rock. I am the thrum in the soles of the feet, the hum in the steel, the pulse that travels faster than the water.

My name is untranslatable in the dry tongues of the surface, but it functions as a directive in the dialect of this place: *Keep the Channel Open.*

I stand on the precipice of the Intake Tier, the highest accessible point before the seeps lose themselves in the fog. The air here is so thick it feels like swimming; every breath is a sip of the atmosphere, a deliberate intake of the warm, saturated soup that sustains us. My skin—rough, porous, the color of wet slate—is drinking even as I stand still. I can feel the mineral load tickling the pores of my forearms, the heavy water seeking ingress, and I tighten the internal membranes that filter the iron from my blood. We do not fight the wet; we negotiate with it.

I am a Hydraulist, but not merely a tender of pipes. I am a *Tuner*.

Around me, the city screams in a thousand frequencies. There is the high, static hiss of a million grazers scraping their radulae against the stonework—a sound like sand poured on glass. There is the rhythmic clatter of the Signal-Skitterers, copper-shelled and swift, navigating the lattice of conduits like sparks fleeing a wire. Below, in the gloom, the massive Pump-Worms contract, sending a deep, booming bass note through the platform under my boots. *Thrum. Thrum. Pause. Thrum.*

But I am listening for something else.

I hold the tuning fork in my left hand, a tool of cured driftwood and resonant metal. I strike it against the wet railing. *Ding.*

The sound flies out, but it does not just bounce. It is absorbed by the wet air, refracted by the fog, swallowed by the porous stone. I close my eyes—the membranes sliding shut to protect them from the drifting moisture—and I feel the return.

The pipe emerging from the rock face before me, the Great Artery of District 9, is singing back. It sings a flat, dull note. *F-sharp, but falling.*

This is not merely sound; it is diagnosis. The flatness means resistance. The falling tone means accumulation. The water inside the pipe is fighting for space because something is growing in the dark.

"Blockage in the lateral," I sub-vocalize, the thrum in my throat matching the frequency of the pipe. "Sector 4. Calcium accretion. Or a nest."

I do not shout. The skitterers hear the command in my bone-conduction. A dozen copper heads snap up from the conduit lines, antenna twitching. They scatter, not away, but *down*—racing toward the sub-levels to carry the warning to the Deep-Dwellers.

I step onto the slick, rust-colored grating that leads over the edge. My harness—oiled leather and salvaged chitin—creaks as I lean into the safety line. Below me, the city opens up like a geode, layer upon layer of industry disappearing into the mist. I can see the glow of the furnace districts, trapped as orange smudges in the fog. I can see the pale, searching beams of the light-wells.

I climb down. My hands, callused into permanent ridges, find purchase on the wet metal. I am descending into the gut of the machine.

The sound changes as I drop. The roar of the grazers fades, replaced by the guttural groaning of the infrastructure itself. The water here is not a film or a seep; it is a torrent, channeled and pressurized. It crashes against the valves, a white noise that drowns thought.

I reach the lateral junction. Here, the pipe is swollen, bulging outward like a muscle under strain. Through the sight-glass—a thick disc of clouded glass—I see the problem.

It is a Reef.

Not the coral of a dry ocean, but a living filter-colony that has decided the flow is too slow. It has bloomed in the last wet season, a complex lattice of secreted tubes and organic chambers. It is beautiful in a terrifying way—translucent, pulsing, gelatinous. It is filtering the water, yes, purifying it with an obsessive efficiency, but it has narrowed the channel by sixty percent. If it continues, the pressure upstream will build until the pipe bursts, and District 9 will go dry.

I cannot simply cut it away. To kill the filter-colony is to invite a bloom of algae and bacteria that the water-chemistry cannot support. The ecosystem of the pipe is delicate. I must prune it, not butcher it.

I unclip the sonic-scraper from my belt. It is a heavy tool, vibrating with a low hum that makes my teeth ache. I set the frequency to match the resonant pitch of the calcified parts of the colony—the hard shells, the skeletal structures—leaving the soft, living tissues unharmed.

I press the tool against the bulging metal of the pipe. The vibration travels through the steel, into the water, and into the colony.

The reaction is instantaneous. The colony shivers. The hard outer layers flake away, dissolving into powder that is instantly swept away by the current. The soft membranes flutter, contracting in shock, pulling back from the edges where the metal bites.

I watch the sight-glass. The flow increases. The water churns, white and frothy. The pipe sings a new note—higher, sharper, a clear C-major. *Open.*

I stop the scraper. The vibration fades.

For a moment, I stand there in the damp, claustrophobic dark, listening to the water rush. It is the sound of the city feeding itself.

I feel a touch on my shoulder.

I do not startle. I know the rhythm of the step. It is a Deep-Dweller, one of the pale, eyeless ones who maintain the roots. Their skin is translucent, showing the blue veins beneath, and they smell of the fungal cakes they burn for warmth.

They press a canister into my hand. It is warm—sampled water from the deep aquifers. I open it and drink. It tastes of iron, of ancient rock, of time. It tastes like the city’s blood.

We do not speak. They reach out and touch the pipe, feeling the vibration of the restored flow with their sensitive fingertips. They nod, a slow, deliberate movement, and then they retreat back into the shadows, their bare feet making no sound on the wet grating.

I climb back up.

When I break the surface of the fog layer, the light hits me—diffuse, grey, sourceless. I am wetter now than when I descended. My clothes are soaked, my hair plastered to my skull. But the water on me is not a burden; it is a record. It carries the story of the deep, the minerals of the roots, the heat of the engines.

I walk back to the edge of the platform. A drifter floats past my face, pulsing slowly, translucent tendrils brushing my cheek. I ignore it. I am looking at the view.

From here, the Condensing City is not a ruin. It is a single, continuous organism. The smoke stacks are exhalations. The lifts are the flexing of muscles. The seeps are the sweating skin. The water is the blood that never clots, that never stops moving, that builds and destroys and builds again.

I take the tuning fork and strike it once more against the air. *Ding.*

The city answers.

I am not the master of this place. I am not the builder, nor the dweller. I am the listener. I am the tension in the wire, the pressure in the valve, the frequency that keeps the stone from cracking and the water from stagnating.

I am the Tuner, and the city is singing my song.
