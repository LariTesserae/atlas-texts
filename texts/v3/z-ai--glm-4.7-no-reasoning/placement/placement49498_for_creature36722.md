I am a runner on the Long Rope.

Not a messenger—messengers carry words, and words are too fragile for the damp and the cold. I carry heat. I carry blood.

I was born in the warrens on the eastern face of the Third Ridge, in a room so deep inside the stone that the walls wept a constant, black tears of condensation. We were a family of eight in a space meant for two shaggy oxen. We slept pressed together in the way the goats sleep on the slate roofs, not for affection but for the physics of survival: the combined respiration of eight bodies in a stone box raises the temperature just enough to keep the damp from settling in the lungs. I learned the smell of wet wool and tallow smoke before I learned my mother's face in the light.

My body is a product of the vertical and the dense. I am not tall; the thin air punishes the tall, demands too much lung. I am broad in the chest, thick in the thigh, my hands calloused not from the hoe or the hammer, but from the rope. The rope is sisal and yak-hair, twisted to the thickness of a man's arm, and it runs the circumference of the city along the Windward Rim where the lichen cannot cling and the black ice rules.

My route is the outer edge, where the raw, young rock meets the pale, washed-out sky. It is a walk of four hours along a path carved into the granite, never wider than the length of my forearm. Below me, the city drops away in a terrifying, dense cascade of tiered rooftops and smoking flues—a vertical ocean of timber and stone, crashing against the base of the peaks. Above me, the jagged spires tear at the clouds.

There is a rhythm to this place, a slow, geological heartbeat, and I must match it. If I run, my lungs will freeze and burst in the thin air. If I stop, the damp cold will penetrate my layers of felted wool and settle in my joints, and I will become one of the frozen statues that line the path—runners who paused too long and were preserved by the sheen of ice as the day's weak warmth met the freezing night. I walk with a deliberate, swinging gait, my staff clicking against the slick stone in a hollow, persistent meter.

I share the path with the others who endure the Rim. The horned goats, sure-footed and arrogant, leap past me with a casual indifference to the void. The great, humped oxen cannot navigate here; they remain in the suffocating warmth of the narrow alleyways below, their bodies radiating the heat that keeps the lower city alive. Up here, it is just me, the wind, and the quiet drip of water seeping from the fractures in the rock.

The dampness is my constant companion. It is a presence, a weight. It does not snow heavily here; instead, the moisture hangs in the air, a freezing fog that coats my eyebrows in white crystals and leaves a slick, deadly sheen on the handholds. I wear spiked wraps of yak leather on my feet, but even these slip on the black ice that forms on the shadowed sides of the spires. Every step is a negotiation with the stone. The stone is young, sharp, unforgiving. It has not been rounded by time or weather; its fractures are fresh, its edges capable of cutting through leather and skin.

My purpose is the bridge. Four times a day, I reach the Great Span, a swaying construction of massive timber beams and the Long Rope that connects the Third Ridge to the Fourth. The bridge bows under the weight of the wind and the thousands of heavy-feathered birds that roost on its cables. When I step onto it, the world shifts from the solid certainty of the mountain to the slow, hypnotic pendulum of the suspended city. The birds scatter at my approach, a silent, dark cloud against the pale sky, and the bridge groans, a deep, wooden complaint that resonates through my boots.

On the other side, I deliver what I carry. Not goods, but myself. I am a knot in the living rope that holds the Ridges together. I nod to the watchman in the stone alcove, our breath-mist mingling in the frozen air—a brief, warm cloud that crystallizes and falls like ash. We do not speak. Words are lost in the vastness and the quiet. The silence here is not absence; it is the sound of the damp cold absorbing the world. The lowing of cattle ten thousand feet below is a distant, muffled thrum, like a heartbeat heard through a stone wall.

I am of this place: a being of cold muscle and quiet endurance, a speck of living warmth moving against the infinite, severe grey of the City of Ridges. I am the friction of the body against the rock, the slow, necessary pulse in the vertical, frozen hive.
