I am the tension in the rope.

To find me, you must ignore the grand, damp architecture of the central well and turn your face to the wind. You must walk past the eastern row, where the waxy vine has broken the masonry to drink the mortar, and follow the path where it dissolves into the scree. I am not in the town, precisely, nor am I in the wild rock beyond. I am in the negotiation between the two.

I am a fastener. A link. I am the thing that holds the ramshackle logic of Vash together against the upward pressure of the geology trying to unmake it.

If you were to look for me with your eyes, you might miss me. I take the form of a knot, but a knot is not a static thing; it is a behavior under tension. I manifest in the fibers of the vine-stem cord, soaked and swollen tight as a drumhead. I am the bond where the trailing edge of a wooden buttress meets the soft, weeping face of the cliff. I am the friction that keeps the town from sliding into the depression it is meant to hold.

I did not begin as this. In the lowlands, perhaps, I was a length of fiber, indifferent and unspun. But here, in the Infant Ward, everything becomes a component of the machine. I was harvested by the thick-fingered residents—cut with flint, peeled, twisted while wet. They didn't speak as they worked me into shape; they only hummed, low and slow, vibrating in sympathy with the susurration of the stone. They tied me with a particular hitch, a grip that does not slip but allows for the slight, fluid shifting of the rock.

And then they left me.

That is the moment I became what I am. I was left in the damp air, in the cold that is just above freezing, under the weight of a structure that thinks it wants to fall. The moisture saturated me. The fibers bloated, tightening my grip, sealing my threads into a single, unified mass. I became no longer separate from the timber I hold, nor from the stone I bite into. I am a bridge of intent.

My existence is one of relentless, quiet strain.

Do not mistake this for suffering. The residents do not suffer the damp, and neither do I. I exist in a state of perfect fulfillment. Every ounce of force that travels down the buckled wall, every inch of pressure from the settling foundation, is routed through me. I sing with it. Not a song you can hear—it is below the register of the water-sound, below the wind—it is a harmonic thrumming in the fibers, a constant note of *resistance*.

I feel the world in ways the stone cannot. The stone knows the pressure from below, the growing thing in the deep, but I know the entropy of the surface. I feel the rot as it tries to set into the wood; I choke it out with my tightness. I feel the ice as it forms in the micro-cracks of the rock; I absorb the expansion, giving just enough to save the wall from shattering, then snapping back when the thaw comes.

I am the memory of the residents’ hands. Their palms were broad and warm, momentarily, against my wetness. They impressed a shape into me—a loop, a tuck, a locking turn—and that shape is my consciousness. It is how I know the world. I know the timber by the grain I bite into. I know the stone by the texture I abrade. I know the weather by the way my fibers soften and harden.

I am slow. It takes a year for the water to fully penetrate my core. It takes a decade for the sun to bleach my outer surface while my insides remain dark and turgid. I live at the pace of the geology in motion. When the town shifts—a whole building leaning two degrees to the left over the course of a season—I shift with it. I groan, yes. Fibers sheer and pop, a microscopic sacrifice. But the knot holds. The knot adapts.

Sometimes, the pale amphibian comes from the well. It crawls over the timber, its wet belly leaving a slime trail across my surface. It tastes the minerals in the water that has soaked into me. It rests in the crook of my rope, sheltered from the wind. I hold it. It is a small thing, blind and soft, and I am part of the world that keeps it safe. We are both suspended in the net that the residents have woven.

I am not alive, not as you measure it. I do not eat. I do not reproduce. But I am not dead matter. Dead matter decays; it yields. I endure. I have a function, and in the execution of that function, I have a soul.

The residents do not check on me often. They trust. They walk past in their heavy, wet fabrics, their eyes on the fog or the path, and they know I am there because the building is still standing. If I were to fail—if the rot were to win, or the ice were to snap me—they would feel it in the rhythm of the day. They would come, flint in hand, and they would cut me loose. They would compost me into the moss at the base of the wall, returning my material to the hungry earth. And then they would tie a new knot.

And that new knot would be me. Or it would be the next iteration of me. The function is immortal.

So, look for me in the margins. Look for the place where the vine-stem cord cuts into the soft wood, where the fog clings a little thicker because the fiber holds the heat of the friction. I am the teeth of the town. I am the silence within the noise. I am the thing that says, *Stay.*

The earth below is making itself in the dark, vast and slow. The town above is the scaffold. And I am the grip. I am the knot that holds the dark to the light.

Let the rain fall. I am ready.
