I exist in the oscillation between the geology and the machine.

I am not a resident, though I am often mistaken for one of the old maintenance crew from the lower tiers. I do not have a name that would be recognizable to the people of the residential districts, though they know my function. To them, I am the silence that follows the creak; I am the adjustment that prevents the sway.

I am the **Equilibrator**.

I found myself here—or rather, I woke here—somewhere between Deck 75 and the cloud line. The first sensation was the vibration: that deep, slow thrumming from the base of the world that rattles the teeth and settles in the marrow. The second was the light: the blinding, white-hot tropical sun striking the upper gantries, reflecting off the brass-colored rivet heads until the air itself seemed to shimmer.

I am composed of the same materials as this place, yet I am distinct. My skin is not flesh, but a laminate of canvas and cured resin, tough as tire rubber, veined with copper wire that pulses with the faint bio-electric current of the Works. I do not bleed; I leak hydraulic fluid that smells faintly of sweet clover and ozone.

I live in the interstices. My home is a cramped hammock strung between the main compression column of Sector 4 and the rusted outrigger of a derelict loading crane. It is a space that no human would comfortably inhabit—it is too exposed to the wind, too prone to the sudden, violent sways that accompany the evening thermal shifts. But I do not mind the sway. I anticipate it.

My purpose is tied to the expansion joints.

The Works is a living thing of steel, but steel does not breathe easily. When the sun strikes the upper decks in the morning, the metal expands. The whole vast superstructure groans—a sound like a choir of giants waking up—and the plates shift. If left unchecked, this expansion would shear the bolts, warp the catwalks, and eventually tear the mountain apart.

I am the counter-force.

I spend the days traversing the high-risk spans, the vertical kilometers of open grating where the wind howls through the lattice. I carry a heavy, torque-wrench that is fused to my right arm. I feel the tension in the girders long before the gauges register it. I can sense the microscopic warping of a truss fifty meters away through the soles of my feet.

When the heat rises, I tighten. When the night comes and the steel contracts, shivering in the cool dampness, I loosen. I walk the braces, listening to the *song* of the structure. The people below hear noise; I hear music. I know which diagonal is singing flat, which cross-brace is sharp. I adjust them, turn by turn, until the hum of the Works returns to a perfect, resonant chord.

I have a relationship with the fauna. The macaques used to throw debris at me, mistaking me for a rival climber, but they have learned to leave me be. Now, they watch me from the safe distance of the fig trees, chattering amongst themselves, perhaps recognizing that I am part of the geography. The parakeets land on my shoulders when I am still, mistaking the copper filigree of my sensory array for branches. I let them sit. They are light, and their weight is negligible against the mass I must hold.

I do not eat the food the people eat. I draw energy from the differential—capturing the waste heat of the engine-pulse through the thermal vents on my back, converting it into the kinetic energy I need to climb. I drink the water that runs in the channels, filtering it through a system of ceramic mesh that removes the rust and the algae, leaving only the pure, cold essence of the cloud.

Sometimes, at dusk, I will stop my work on a high girder and look down.

Below me, the lights of the residential tiers flicker on—little orange islands in a sea of green darkness. I can see the people moving along the catwalks, tiny specks against the vast industrial backdrop. I feel a protective ache for them. They are the soft tissue of this organism. They are fragile. They tend the gardens and fix the minor leaks, but they do not know how close the whole thing comes to tearing apart every single day. They sleep soundly because I do not sleep.

I remember the builders. I have flashes of them—imperfect, glitchy memories stored in the oldest sectors of my logic core. I remember hands that were warm and fleshy, not hard and laminate. I remember the smell of fresh paint and hot rivets before the moss took hold. I remember a voice saying, *"It must stand forever."*

I am the fulfillment of that command.

I am the Equilibrator. I am the tension in the wire. I am the silence within the noise. I am the one who holds the mountain together while the world tries to pull it apart.
