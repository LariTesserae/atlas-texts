I find myself drawn to being a crafter of light and sound in this environment - one who has spent decades mastering the unique acoustics of these stone passages and the ways illumination plays across the wet obsidian. Not quite a musician, not quite a lamplighter, but something between.

I maintain a workshop in one of the higher chambers, where the air is slightly warmer and drier. The space is arranged in concentric half-circles, with carefully positioned surfaces that catch and redirect both sound and light. My tools hang from hooks carved directly into the wall - tuning forks of various sizes, small hammers, curved reflectors of polished metal, specialized oil vessels for the lamps.

My work involves "tuning" sections of the marketplace, adjusting the placement of lamps and sound-reflecting surfaces to maintain the delicate balance of light and acoustics that keeps the space functional and beautiful. I've learned to read the stone's voice - how a slight shift in humidity changes its resonance, how the gradual wearing of water creates new acoustic patterns that must be accommodated.

I wear simple clothes in deep indigo, treated with wax and fitted close, with many pockets for my tools. My hair is completely shaved - better to hear the subtle variations in sound without interference. My fingers are sensitive from years of testing surfaces, feeling for the microscopic changes in texture that might affect how sound moves through a space.

I'm known for my annual participation in the festivals, where I create elaborate arrangements of light and sound that transform familiar passages into otherworldly spaces. But my real satisfaction comes from the daily work of maintenance - adjusting a lamp's position by a finger's width to better illuminate a treacherous step, shifting a sound-reflecting panel to ensure a vendor's voice carries clearly to their customers.

The children call me the Echo-Keeper, though that's not my official title. They bring me interesting sounds they've discovered in hidden corners of the market, and I teach them how to listen to the stone, how to understand the way sound moves through their world. Some of them may apprentice with me someday, learning to maintain this delicate balance of light and sound that helps make our subterranean home both functional and beautiful.

I'm neither young nor old - somewhere in that middle space where experience meets ongoing curiosity. Every day, I discover something new about how light plays across the wet stone, how sound moves through these ancient passages. It's work that requires both precision and intuition, technical skill and artistic sensitivity. Most importantly, it requires a deep love for this unique place and its people, an understanding that our environment is not just a space we inhabit, but a living thing we must nurture and maintain.
