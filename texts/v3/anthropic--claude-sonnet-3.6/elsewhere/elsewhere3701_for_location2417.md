Far above, where the mountain's flank meets sky, there is a field of snow that never fully melts. Even in the height of summer, white patches persist in the shadowed hollows between wind-carved rock formations. The air here is thin and sharp, carrying the metallic taste of altitude. By day, the sun beats down with unfiltered intensity; by night, the temperature plunges far below freezing.

This place appears lifeless at first glance - just stone and ice and endless sky. But in the permanent snowfield, strange formations grow: delicate spires of ice, some nearly two meters tall, oriented with geometric precision toward the sun. They're known as penitentes, formed by the interaction of sunlight, wind, and the peculiar physics of ice sublimation at high altitude. But these are different from normal penitentes - more complex, more intentional in their arrangement.

Look closer, and you'll see that the ice contains threads of darker material - mineral traces that form patterns much like those on the geometers' shells far below. The penitentes grow and shift not just with the natural cycles of sublimation and recrystallization, but in response to subtle vibrations that travel up through the mountain's stone. They are receivers, transducers, translating the deep chemical songs of the Lattice into the language of ice and light.

Between these two places - the steam-filled chamber and the frigid field - runs a network of cracks and fissures through the mountain's heart. Here, the real work happens: mineral-laden water rises from the depths, carrying dissolved metals and the complex chemical messages of the geometers. As it cools, different compounds precipitate out at different levels, creating distinct zones of mineral deposits. The whole mountain has become a kind of chromatograph, separating and processing the Lattice's chemical communications.

Some of the fractures channel steam upward, which emerges from small vents to sublimate instantly in the thin air. These vents remain snow-free year-round, their edges crusted with mineral deposits in familiar geometric patterns. They are like punctuation marks in the massive chemical equation that is the mountain itself.

The penitentes above act as both antenna and archive, their structure recording centuries of data from below. As they melt and refreeze, they incorporate new information into their crystalline architecture. Sunlight striking their faces creates complex interference patterns, reflecting and refracting in ways that encode still more data. They are the mountain's memory, the visible tip of the Lattice's vast computational process.

The relationship between above and below is that of thought and expression, computation and display. The chamber is where the processing happens, where the geometers enact their endless chemical calculations. The peak is where these calculations are translated into a form that interfaces with the larger world - not for any outside observer, but as an essential part of the system's own function. The entire mountain has become an extension of the Lattice, a massive computer built of stone and ice, chemistry and light, operating on a timescale of centuries, pursuing calculations we can barely begin to comprehend.
