Across a vast stretch of abyssal plain, nearly eight kilometers from the Archivist's pocket, there is another engineered space - but this one faces upward, toward the distant surface waters. Here, a forest of enormous transparent tubes rises from the seafloor, each one hundreds of meters tall and wide enough to swallow a submarine. They're made of something like glass, but with the resilience of diamond, their walls etched with spiraling patterns that channel currents in precise ways.

This is the Updraft Garden, where engineered organisms ride thermal convections in an eternal dance between depths and shallows. The tubes act as massive chimneys, their bases heated by geothermal vents to create powerful upward flows. The water inside them is clearer than the surrounding ocean, filtered by colonies of crystalline organisms that cling to the tube walls in fractal patterns.

The inhabitants here are different from the Archivist's precision-engineered swarms. These are more like vast jellyfish crossed with solar sails - delicate, translucent creatures with wingspans measured in dozens of meters. They ride the upward currents with carefully evolved membrane structures, then drift back down along the tubes' outer surfaces in a slow, graceful cycle. Their bodies shimmer with complex iridescent patterns, both beautiful and functional - photosynthetic organs adapted to capture the faintest hints of sunlight that reach these depths.

The space between the tubes is a forest of fiber optic-like strands that transmit this captured light downward, feeding energy into the underlying systems that keep the thermal vents active. Everything here is part of a vertical cycle - energy, nutrients, and information flowing up and down in carefully maintained patterns.

The connection between these two spaces - the Archivist's pocket and the Updraft Garden - lies in the regular pilgrimages of the tube-riders. Every few weeks, one of the great sail-creatures will break from its normal cycle and spiral down to visit the pocket. They carry with them trace elements from the upper waters that the pocket's systems need to maintain their delicate balance. In return, they receive updated genetic information from the Archivist's archives, allowing them to continue adapting to the slowly changing ocean conditions.

This exchange isn't just chemical - there's communication too, carried in patterns of bioluminescence and subtle electrical fields. The sail-creatures are simpler than the Archivist, but they're part of the same ancient system, their endless cycling between depths and heights maintaining connections that would otherwise be lost. They remember through movement what the Archivist remembers through stasis - the choreography of a civilization now visible only in the technologies it left behind.

Between these two points, messages pass back and forth through the dark water - light signals, chemical traces, patterns of pressure and temperature that carry meaning for those engineered to read them. It's a slow conversation between different approaches to preservation - the Archivist's perfect, unchanging maintenance and the sail-creatures' constant adaptation. Together, they maintain not just themselves but a legacy, each in their own way keeping alive something that was meant to last beyond its creators' time.
