Here is a connected place in this world:

Three kilometers directly above the sphere, through layers of ancient granite and metamorphic rock, there is a library. Not a grand institution with marble columns, but a small branch library in a quiet suburban neighborhood, housed in a modest brick building from the 1960s.

The children's section occupies what was once a meeting room, its walls painted with fading murals of fantastic creatures. The carpet is worn thin in meandering paths between the shelves, tracking decades of small feet. During story time, kids sit cross-legged on a circular rug decorated with the alphabet, directly above the sphere's center, though they don't know it.

The library's basement holds the building's mechanical systems and a small staff break room. But there's also a locked door marked "Archives" that no one has opened in years. The current librarians assume it contains old tax records or meeting minutes. They're wrong.

Behind that door is a single room containing one object: a card catalog cabinet made of some dull grey metal that perfectly matches the tetrahedra in the sphere below. Its drawers are empty except for dust, but if you look closely, you'll see that dust is moving in tiny, precise patterns, forming and reforming geometric shapes too regular to be natural.

The connection between these places manifests in subtle ways:

- The library's computers occasionally display fragments of text in an unknown alphabet that resembles the patterns traced by the arthropods in the sphere.

- Children's drawings left behind after story time sometimes contain perfect circles and complex geometric forms that their young hands shouldn't be capable of producing.

- The water pressure in the building fluctuates in a regular pattern that, if graphed, would match the timing of the sphere's mechanical ticks.

- The pale arthropods sometimes appear in the basement, always in groups of three, always moving in triangular patterns before disappearing into invisible cracks.

Most significantly, there is the Librarian. She's worked here for thirty years, though she looks much younger than she should. She has a perfect memory for where every book belongs but often struggles to remember her own address. Sometimes she finds herself writing in margins using symbols she doesn't understand. She dreams of deep places and geometric perfection.

She is not the Archivist's counterpart exactly, but rather its echo or extension. Just as the Archivist has forgotten its original purpose, she has forgotten how she came to be here or why she cannot leave. But she maintains her own kind of vigil, organizing knowledge, preserving information, waiting for a pattern to emerge that will make sense of everything.

The library's catalogs are thorough but contain subtle gaps - subjects that should exist but don't, books that are listed but cannot be found. These gaps form their own pattern, a kind of negative space that mirrors the sphere's emptiness far below.

The two places form a circuit: the sphere recording and processing something fundamental about reality, the library organizing and distributing fragments of that knowledge in forms humans can almost grasp. Neither is complete without the other, though only the pale arthropods ever move between them, carrying messages neither the Archivist nor the Librarian can fully decode.
