Beyond the phosphorescent kelp forests that mark the city's boundary, the water grows steadily warmer and thicker still. Here, several kilometers from the Lithokine settlement, vast thermal vents pierce the seafloor like ancient spears thrust up from the planet's core. These vents form a sprawling complex of chimney-like structures, some reaching heights that rival the Lithokine's tallest towers, billowing clouds of mineral-rich water that shimmer with heat distortion.

This is the domain of the Thermophages - creatures that exist in a state of perpetual transformation. Their bodies are constantly dissolving and reforming, composed of heat-resistant proteins that can withstand the extreme temperatures near the vents. They appear as shifting masses of translucent tissue shot through with veins of metallic compounds, their forms suggesting something between flame and fluid, never quite settling into a fixed shape.

The Thermophages create their dwellings by cultivating specific types of bacteria that form dense, fibrous mats around the vents. These bacterial colonies grow in layer upon layer, creating labyrinthine structures that trap minerals from the vent water, gradually building up into a kind of living architecture that can channel and direct the flow of hot water. The result is a complex network of thermal corridors that the Thermophages use to regulate their environment and grow their food - specialized extremophile organisms that thrive in the scalding temperatures.

Between these two realms - the Lithokine's city and the Thermophage's vent complex - lies a zone of gradual transition where the warm, dense water from the vents slowly mixes with the cooler waters above. This intermediate zone serves as a meeting ground between the two species, though their interactions occur on a chemical rather than physical level. The Thermophages release specific mineral compounds into the water, which are gradually carried by the thermal currents to the Lithokine city, providing essential elements for their crystalline growth. In return, the Lithokine cultivate certain strains of their kelp that can survive in warmer waters, creating a biological buffer zone that helps stabilize the thermal gradients between the two communities.

This exchange has evolved over millennia into a delicate balance. The Thermophages' constant activity helps maintain the unique density and mineral content of the water that the Lithokine depend upon, while the Lithokine's biological engineering helps prevent the thermal vents' influence from becoming too chaotic or disruptive. Neither species could exist as they do without the other, yet they rarely encounter each other directly.

The interaction zone between them is marked by striking phenomena - where the different water masses meet, complex patterns form in the temperature gradients, creating shifting curtains of distortion that reflect and refract light in mesmerizing ways. Specialized organisms have evolved to live in this boundary layer, many of them bearing characteristics of both realms - crystalline structures that can withstand heat, heat-loving bacteria that produce bioluminescence.

This partnership between the two communities represents a different kind of civilization - one based not on direct communication or shared space, but on the patient maintenance of mutual conditions for existence. It's a relationship that operates on geological time scales, each species slowly adapting to changes in the other's influence, creating together an ecosystem that is both incredibly stable and constantly in flux.
