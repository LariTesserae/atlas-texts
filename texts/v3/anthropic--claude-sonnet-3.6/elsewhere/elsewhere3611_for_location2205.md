Far below Ten Thousand Ladders, where the mountain's roots meet the endless salt plains, lies the Settlement of Long Shadows. Here, the ground is perfectly flat and crusted white, stretching to the horizon like a frozen sea. But this is no peaceful harbor - it's a place of extremes, where the mountain's immense presence creates powerful wind patterns that sculpt both the landscape and its inhabitants' lives.

The settlement itself appears to rise and fall with the seasons. Its structures are primarily fabric and flexible reed, designed to bend rather than break in the fierce winds. The buildings can be collapsed and re-erected as needed, following the wisdom of generations who learned that rigidity means death in the shadow of the mountain. The only permanent structures are the deep wells that tap into underground rivers - the same waters that weep from the cliffs far above.

These wells are the settlement's heart, lined with spiraling ramps that descend hundreds of feet into the earth. The water here is warm and mineral-rich, carrying traces of the mountain's depths. The wells create their own microclimate - steam rises from their mouths in the cool mornings, attracting flocks of salt-white birds that dive through the mist.

The Salt Dancers who live here have developed a profound symbiosis with their harsh environment. Their skin is tough and scaled, protecting them from both the abrasive salt winds and the intense sun. They move with a distinctive gait - a fluid, rolling motion that conserves energy and maintains balance in the unpredictable gusts. Their eyes are protected by multiple translucent lids, and their ears can close completely against driving salt storms.

The connection between these two communities - the Mistwalkers above and the Salt Dancers below - is both ancient and active. A network of caves and vertical shafts runs through the mountain's heart, carrying water, goods, and messengers between the settlements. These passages, called the Whispering Ways, are neither fully natural nor clearly artificial. They seem to have been grown rather than carved, their walls smooth and organic, marked with phosphorescent fungi that provide light.

Trade between the communities is constant but subtle. The Salt Dancers send up dried salt flowers that bloom on the plains after rains, crystals with remarkable properties, and preserved foods that thrive in their harsh environment. The Mistwalkers send down medicines made from their hanging gardens, luminous fungi, and woven tools that incorporate living materials.

But the most important exchange is knowledge. The two communities maintain their balance through careful observation of the mountain's cycles. The Salt Dancers can read the plain's patterns to predict storms that will affect the city above, while the Mistwalkers track water flows that impact life below. Messages travel through the Whispering Ways in multiple forms - carried by runners, transmitted through intricate percussion codes tapped on the rock walls, or sent via trained cloudweavers that navigate the vertical shaft network.

The relationship between the settlements is reflected in their shared mythology. They speak of the mountain as a living entity that breathes in cycles - inhaling mist at its peak, exhaling salt winds at its base. Their creation stories tell of a time when their ancestors could shift forms freely between the two environments, and their shamans still claim to make this transformation during certain rituals.

The space between the settlements - the great vertical distance marked by the Whispering Ways - is regarded as sacred by both communities. Neither purely above nor below, it represents possibility and transformation. Young members of both groups must make at least one journey through these passages as part of their coming of age, experiencing the gradual transformation of environment and learning to adapt as they travel.

This constant exchange has led to a unique form of hybrid culture. The settlements maintain their distinct adaptations while sharing a deeper philosophical understanding of balance and transformation. They see themselves as two expressions of the same fundamental relationship with the mountain - one rising, one spreading, both essential to the whole.
