Far below the hanging greenhouse, at the mountain's base, there is a small postal station - the last occupied building before the wilderness truly begins. It's a sturdy stone structure dating from the same era as the facility above, though its purpose was always more prosaic. Here, at the intersection of civilization and solitude, mail and supplies are sorted for distribution to the scattered homesteads and research stations that dot the mountainside.

The station is staffed by a single postmaster, Mrs. Chen, who has held the position for thirty-seven years. She knows every address on the mountain, including those that no longer exist except in institutional memory. Her sorting room is filled with the quiet music of paper and string - parcels being wrapped, letters being stamped, the soft thump of mailbags being filled and emptied.

The station's walls are lined with hundreds of cubbyholes, many empty now but each meticulously labeled. Some bear the names of long-abandoned projects: the geological survey, the weather station, the botanical research facility. Mrs. Chen continues to maintain these spaces, dusting them weekly, though they haven't received mail in decades.

Once a month, like clockwork, a small package arrives addressed to the greenhouse facility. There is never a return address, but the handwriting is always the same - precise, architectural, drawn with what appears to be green ink. Mrs. Chen adds these packages to a special cubbyhole marked only with a drawing of a leaf. They disappear within days, though no one ever comes to claim them.

The connection between these two places - the posthuman realm above and the carefully maintained human systems below - manifests in other ways. Sometimes Mrs. Chen finds strange plants growing in the station's window boxes, species she's never seen before that seem to pulse with tiny electrical charges. On quiet nights, she can hear the distant drone of the greenhouse's fans, carrying through the mountain air in seven-minute waves.

She keeps a logbook of these occurrences, noting them with the same methodical attention she gives to registered mail and change of address forms. Over the years, patterns have emerged. The strange plants appear most often after electrical storms. The unclaimed packages always arrive on the new moon. And sometimes, in the pre-dawn hours, she glimpses something like green lightning dancing between the postal station's radio antenna and the silhouette of the greenhouse far above.

Mrs. Chen has never visited the greenhouse, though she knows the way. She feels no need to. Her role in this ecosystem is clear - she maintains her own systems with the same dedication the Curator maintains theirs. Together, they form two poles of order in the mountain's wildness, connected by invisible lines of communication and mutual recognition.

The postal station and the greenhouse represent two approaches to time - one marking it in human increments of delivery schedules and office hours, the other in the slow pulse of growth and decay. Yet they share a common understanding of patience, of the value of maintaining systems beyond their original purpose, of finding meaning in the steady repetition of necessary tasks.

Between them, packages pass like messages between worlds, climbing the mountain by means that Mrs. Chen chooses not to question. The Curator's research continues, nourished by these monthly deliveries of specialized equipment or rare seeds or who knows what else. And Mrs. Chen's logbook grows thicker, documenting the quiet dialogue between what was and what is becoming, between the world of forms and files and the realm where such distinctions begin to blur.
