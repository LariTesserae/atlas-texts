Above, far beyond the misted caverns, there is a place where the mountain meets the sky. Here, the rock face rises sheer and bare, its surface a complex tapestry of metamorphic stone - sheets of schist and gneiss folded like ancient paper, shot through with veins of quartz that catch the high altitude sun.

The air is thin and sharp, carrying the metallic taste of snow even in summer. Winds course constantly around the peak, their patterns as complex as the water flows far below, creating invisible rivers of air that shape both weather and stone. These winds carve the rock over millennia, deposit minerals in hairline cracks, and carry microscopic life forms that cling to seemingly barren surfaces.

Here dwell the Wind Shapers - entities as diffuse as their underground cousins but composed of different stuff. They exist as patterns in the air itself, as electromagnetic fluctuations, as living variations in pressure and temperature. Their "bodies" are temporary organizations of wind and charged particles, visible sometimes as strange atmospheric phenomena - coronas of light, perfectly still zones in howling gales, clouds that move against prevailing winds.

The Wind Shapers work the mountain's surface as their subterranean kin tend the gardens below. They guide the processes of erosion and accretion, nurture colonies of extremophile bacteria that slowly transform the rock's chemistry, and maintain an intricate balance of forces that keeps the mountain stable despite its great height and the endless assault of the elements.

The connection between above and below is subtle but crucial. The Wind Shapers' work creates the network of microscopic fissures through which water seeps down to feed the misty gardens. The mineral-rich droplets that sustain the crystal-plants carry messages encoded by high-altitude bacteria. The spores and seeds that drift through the cavern sometimes rise up through hidden chimneys, drawn by temperature differentials, to be caught by the winds and carried to other peaks, other caverns.

The two species - if they can be called that - are aware of each other in their own way. The Mist Tenders feel their cousins' influence in the changing chemistry of their water supply; the Wind Shapers sense the cave system's breathing through variations in air pressure and temperature. They collaborate across the mountain's mass, maintaining a vertical ecosystem that stretches from peak to depths.

Their shared work unfolds across ages. The Wind Shapers slowly carve new channels for water to reach the caverns; the Mist Tenders gradually adjust their gardens to make use of new mineral combinations weathered from above. Together they are gradually transforming the mountain into something between geology and organism - a vast living system that operates on a timescale barely comprehensible to faster forms of life.

Between them lies the body of the mountain itself - kilometers of rock that seem inert but are actually in constant flux, threaded with water and air, alive with microscopic activity, slowly being reshaped from both above and below into something unprecedented: not quite natural, not quite artificial, but deliberately evolved.
