High in the surrounding hills, far above the glass nursery that fills the valley below, stands a weather station that appears almost as old as the hills themselves. It's a stark contrast to the organic curves of the nursery - all right angles and weather-beaten wood, with a copper dome that has long since turned the same green as the nursery's girders. The station perches on a flat outcropping like a watchful bird, its various instruments extending from it like ruffled feathers.

Here, Marcus Webb maintains the delicate atmospheric conditions that Eleanor's amphibians require. The station's interior is a maze of pipes, valves, and analogue gauges, each one monitoring a different aspect of the microclimate that exists in the valley below. Barometers tap quietly in their wooden cases, hygrographs trace endless lines on slowly rotating drums, and a complex system of bellows and tubes measures the wind's direction and speed.

Unlike the lush, humid environment below, the station is dry and creaky. The wooden floorboards announce every footstep, and the wind whistles through small gaps in the walls despite Marcus's best efforts to seal them. The space smells of old paper, machine oil, and the peculiar metallic scent of approaching rain.

Marcus himself is as angular as his domain - tall and thin, with sharp elbows and knees, and fingers long enough to adjust the finest mechanisms without tools. His clothes are practical and worn: wool sweaters, canvas pants, boots that have molded to his feet over years of use. His face is weathered like the station's exterior, lined not by age but by constant exposure to sun and wind.

The connection between these two spaces - the nursery and the weather station - is both physical and metaphysical. A network of copper pipes runs down the hillside, carrying water and data between them. The pipes are mostly buried but emerge occasionally like metallic roots, their green patina matching the surrounding vegetation. They transport not just water but information: pressure readings, temperature variations, humidity levels, all transmitted through an ingenious system of hydraulics and pneumatics.

Marcus and Eleanor rarely meet in person, but they communicate constantly through this mechanical nervous system. Each morning, Eleanor sends up readings from her instruments via pneumatic tubes. Marcus responds with adjustments to the environmental controls, his messages arriving in small brass cylinders that hiss as they open. Their correspondence is minimal but precise - numbers, measurements, occasional brief notes about unusual patterns or necessary maintenance.

The relationship between the two spaces is symbiotic. The nursery cannot maintain its delicate balance without the weather station's constant adjustments, and the weather station would have no purpose without the nursery to tend. Together they form a complete system: the nursery as the heart, the weather station as the brain, the copper pipes as arteries carrying the lifeblood of information and resources between them.

Sometimes, on clear nights, Marcus can see the glow of Eleanor's lamp moving through the nursery below. From this distance, it looks like a firefly navigating between the stars reflected in the glass roof. And sometimes, when Eleanor looks up through the patchwork of tinted panels, she catches a glimpse of lamplight in the weather station, a steady beacon guiding the passage of clouds and mist.

Between them, they maintain this tiny world within the world, each in their separate domains, connected by green copper veins and the endless cycle of water rising as vapor and returning as mist. Their work is a duet played in different octaves - Marcus conducting the symphony of weather from above, Eleanor orchestrating the chorus of frogs from below, creating between them a harmony that sustains this carefully cultivated piece of living poetry.
