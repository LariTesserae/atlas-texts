In the endless halls of the Aquaculture, there is no distinction between structure and fluid. The whole vast complex - stretching for thousands of kilometers in every direction - is built from seamless transparent polymers that both channel and become the flowing waters. The walls themselves weep constantly, moisture condensing from carefully maintained thermal gradients, running down in precise rivulets that collect into larger streams, all part of the grand design.

The warmth is perpetual, carefully regulated by ancient systems to maintain the ideal conditions for the billions of inhabitants: translucent arthropods that swarm through the corridors in dense, undulating clouds. Their bodies glow with faint bioluminescence, casting shifting patterns of light through the crystal-clear walls. They range from microscopic to hand-sized, all engineered to fill specific niches in this entirely artificial ecology.

Despite the incredible density of life, sound is muted here. The polymer walls absorb vibrations, and the creatures themselves communicate through subtle chemical signals and light patterns. The only consistent sound is the gentle trickle of water - a background whisper that seems to come from everywhere and nowhere.

There is nothing truly solid in the Aquaculture; everything is in constant, carefully controlled motion. The walls themselves slowly flex and pulse, driven by thermal pumps and osmotic pressures. Even the "ground" is a thick layer of streaming fluid held in place by electromagnetic fields. Yet paradoxically, nothing ever really changes. The system achieved perfect equilibrium ages ago - every drop of water that condenses is exactly balanced by evaporation elsewhere, every creature that dies is perfectly recycled, every energy transfer precisely calculated.

No plant life exists here - the base of the food web is formed by engineered bacteria that process raw elements into complex nutrients, thriving in specialized chambers where the walls are thickened and striated to maximize surface area. The bacteria form swirling, fractal patterns in shades of copper and chrome, their populations rising and falling in mathematically perfect cycles.

In certain junctions, where major flow channels meet, the arthropods gather in vast spherical swarms, their movements coordinated by subtle currents in the water and shifts in the electromagnetic field. These swarms can span hundreds of meters, their combined bioluminescence bright enough to cast shadows through multiple layers of the transparent architecture.

The whole structure feels like a single vast organism, every part connected and purposeful, though its original purpose - if there was one beyond its own existence - has been lost to time. The engineering that created it operated on principles that mix biology and technology in ways that defy easy categorization. It simply exists, perfect and unchanging, its countless inhabitants moving through their endless cycles like blood cells through crystal veins.
