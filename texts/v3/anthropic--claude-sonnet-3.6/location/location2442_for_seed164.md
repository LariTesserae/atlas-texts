A warm dark rises from beneath, seeping through hairline fractures in the maintenance corridors of the geothermal plant. The plant itself is more theoretical than visible - a sparse network of pipes and pressure vessels distributed through natural caverns, most of them accessible only by knowing which particular crack in the rock leads to which particular ladder.

The caverns themselves are new - or becoming. The heat and periodic steam releases are slowly wedging apart layers of rock that had been sealed for millions of years. Engineers mapped the emerging spaces as "Sector 7 Expansion Zone C," but the local workers call it the Incubator. The rock here is dark and glossy, still bearing the sheen of recent fracture, occasionally weeping mineral-laden condensation that leaves pristine white traces down the walls.

The main corridor is barely wider than two people side by side, and even that space is largely claimed by the main steam line, its insulation peeling in the perpetual dampness. The air is thick and hard to breathe - not just from the heat, but from the constant sublimation of minerals from the newly-exposed rock faces. Tiny crystals nucleate in the air itself, creating halos around the few permanent lights mounted at critical junctions.

The deeper you go, the more the industrial architecture gives way to raw geology. The newest spaces are still being born - hairline cracks that widen by millimeters each month as the heat differential works its way through the rock. In these deepest reaches, the only light comes from head lamps and the occasional bioluminescent colony of extremophile microorganisms that somehow survive in the mineral soup of the condensation flows.

Every few hours, the deep rumble of the main turbines reverberates through the rock, setting up standing waves in pools of accumulated condensation. The sound seems to come from everywhere and nowhere at once, as if the mountain itself is purring. During these periods, workers check their handheld seismometers carefully - not out of any real danger, but because the vibrations occasionally shake loose new openings in the rock, revealing pristine cavities that might house new heat exchanger installations.

The few creatures that make their home here are mostly invisible - colonies of thermophilic bacteria creating slick biofilms on the cooler pipes, and occasionally small blind arthropods that have found their way in from connected cave systems, adapted to the dark and heat over countless generations. They skitter away from light beams, disappearing into cracks barely wider than a credit card.

Time moves differently here. The rock's expansion is measured in decades, the crystal growth in months, the bacterial colonies in weeks. Only the human presence moves quickly - maintenance crews making their rounds, engineers taking measurements, geologists sampling the newest formations. They pass through like ghosts, their headlamps creating momentary constellations in the crystal-laden air, then vanish back into the maze of corridors, leaving the cave system to its slow process of becoming.
