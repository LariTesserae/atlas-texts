The insects are building mountains.

From a distance, the peaks look like normal geology - soaring geometric forms of raw stone thrust skyward, their faces too steep and fresh to have gathered the marks of time. But as you draw closer, the constant near-silence resolves into a vast, barely-audible hum, felt more through bone and metal than through air.

The mountains are alive with termites. Not Earth termites - these are larger, more purposeful, their segmented bodies gleaming with a dull metallic sheen. Working in perfect coordination, they harvest raw stone from deep quarries and process it through their bodies, adding layer upon microscopic layer to the towering forms above. The mountains grow slowly but inexorably, like crystals precipitating from solution.

The air is warm and extraordinarily dry, stripped of moisture by the insects' metabolic processes. What little water exists is bound up in their bodies and in the strange mineral matrix they excrete - a complex crystalline lattice that gives the mountains their characteristic dark purple-grey color and their incredible structural strength.

The termites work in dense, three-dimensional highways that permeate the entire structure. Each mountain is less a solid mass than a carefully engineered matrix of tunnels, chambers, and support columns, optimized over millions of years of evolution to balance strength against material use. Looking closely at any surface reveals countless tiny openings, each perfectly hexagonal, through which workers constantly stream.

Scattered throughout the region are the artifacts of previous attempts at research or exploitation - abandoned monitoring stations, failed mining equipment, the occasional crashed drone. The termites simply build around these intrusions, incorporating them into their ever-growing architecture. Some of the older equipment has been completely enveloped, visible only as subtle distortions in the mountains' otherwise precise geometry.

The insects seem to operate on geological time scales - their movements are purposeful but unhurried, each individual following chemical trails and social signals in perfect accordance with some vast, slow plan. They don't react to disturbances except to route around them. They don't compete or fight. They simply build, endlessly, turning raw stone into impossible architecture with perfect patience.

The mountains themselves seem almost artificial in their precision - faces meeting at exact angles, ridges running laser-straight for kilometers, peaks rising to identical heights as if measured. But there's an organic quality to them as well, a subtle irregularity that emerges at larger scales, like the difference between a perfect crystal and a living thing.

As the sun sets, bioluminescent patterns begin to emerge across the mountains' faces - not for light, but for communication and coordination across the vast colony. In the growing darkness, the peaks become constellations of tiny blue-green dots, shifting in slow, complex patterns that might hold meaning for those who know how to read them.

The air remains warm even at night, heat bleeding from the endless metabolic processes within. The hum never changes. The building never stops. The mountains grow, molecule by molecule, according to plans laid down before humans existed, reaching toward heights that only the termites understand.
