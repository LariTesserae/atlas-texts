A pocket beneath the city, where the old stones weep. Here, in chambers no wider than outstretched arms, water beads endlessly on curved ceilings and runs down walls worn glassy-smooth by centuries of touch. The drops fall in complex rhythms, striking copper bowls and ceramic vessels placed with careful precision by forgotten hands. The resonant tones blend and echo, creating an ever-present song that fills these narrow spaces - sometimes a thunderous chorus, sometimes a whispered percussion, but never silent.

The walls themselves are a dense maze of niches, shelves, and alcoves carved from the living rock, each holding vessels of various sizes. Some are crude clay, others beaten copper gone green with age. They catch the water that seeps from above, each contributing its own note to the underground symphony. Between them, the walls glisten with biofilms - not quite alive in any way we'd recognize, but somehow more than mineral. These slick, iridescent patches slowly process the mineral-rich water, leaving behind delicate traceries of metallic deposits that grow into strange geometries over decades.

Light comes from luminescent patches of these pseudo-organisms, casting a dim blue-white glow that reflects off the wet surfaces. The light seems to pulse faintly, though whether in response to the water-music or some deeper rhythm is unclear. The illumination reveals glimpses of crude tools scattered about - copper scrapers, clay-working implements, vessels in various stages of completion - all covered in a thin film of moisture.

The air is cool and thick with humidity, every breath tasting of copper and stone. Small movements send ripples through the dense atmosphere, momentarily disturbing the precise fall of drops. Occasionally, something that might be a blind cave fish or might be a living shadow flicks through the larger collecting pools, gone too quick to properly see.

The space feels ancient yet maintained, as if some unseen custodians still tend to this water-music machine. The arrangement of vessels seems to shift subtly between visits, though no one is ever seen moving them. The whole chamber complex exists in a state of dynamic stasis - always changing in its details but never in its essential nature, an eternal instrument played by the patient hand of seeping groundwater.

This is a place of preservation rather than decay, where the boundary between built and natural has worn away until only purpose remains. The city above may forget, but here the songs of stone and water play on, keeping time by their own measure.
