The Glass Plains stretch to every horizon, a vast expanse of smoothed obsidian and volcanic glass that catches and multiplies the light of three small suns. The surface isn't flat - it undulates in long, slow waves formed eons ago when the planet's crust cooled and settled into its final form. These waves create valleys miles wide and shallow bowls where moisture gathers, forming lakes of perfectly clear water that seem to float on the black glass beneath.

In these water-catches, the true inhabitants thrive: vast colonies of crystalline creatures that neither plant nor animal fully describes. They grow in fractal patterns, sometimes transparent, sometimes shot through with color, their structures catching and refracting light in ever-shifting displays. Some float on the water's surface like geometric lace; others extend downward in stalactite-like formations that pulse with slow rhythms.

The air is warm and carries a faint mineral tang. Moisture regularly condenses on the cooling glass at night, creating sheets of fog that roll across the plains in the morning before burning away. This daily cycle has, over millennia, etched subtle patterns into the glass - whorls and striations that only become visible when the light hits at certain angles.

Most remarkable are the aerofauna - creatures that spend their lives entirely aloft, riding the thermal currents that rise from the sun-warmed glass. Their bodies are partly translucent, like living prisms, ranging in size from tiny motes to vast beings whose wings span hundreds of meters. They seem to feed on light itself, or perhaps on the energy differentials in the air. Their movements create subtle harmonics that can be felt more than heard - a deep, almost subsonic thrumming that ebbs and flows with the day.

Scattered across this landscape are the remnants of some ancient civilization - structures that might be buildings, might be machines, might be both or neither. They're constructed of the same glass as the plains, but worked in impossible ways - twisted into spirals that shouldn't be able to support their own weight, formed into lenses and prisms that focus not just light but seemingly more exotic energies. Most are dormant now, but some still hum with power, their purposes long forgotten.

The air is rarely still. Convection currents create persistent wind patterns that have carved channels in the glass over millions of years. These winds carry moisture from the water-catches, creating localized weather systems - brief, warm rains that fall in perfectly straight lines, or clouds that form geometric patterns as they interact with the energies emanating from the ancient structures.

The crystal-creatures in the water-catches occasionally release swarms of smaller forms - something between seeds and spores - that drift on the winds, catching the light like diamond dust. Most will not survive, but some will find their way to other water-catches, establishing new colonies. Their release is accompanied by high, bell-like tones that echo across the plains.

The landscape operates on multiple timescales simultaneously - the quick darting of the aerial fauna, the daily cycle of fog and sun, the slow growth of the crystal-creatures, and the even slower erosion of the glass plains themselves. It's a place of profound activity that nonetheless feels eerily quiet, where even the most dramatic events unfold with a strange grace.

At the horizons, the glass plains seem to curve upward slightly - a reminder of the truly planetary scale of this terrain. Somewhere, presumably, there must be something else - different terrains, other biomes - but from here, the glass and its inhabitants extend seemingly forever under the bright, triple-sunned sky.
