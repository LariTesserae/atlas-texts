A perpetual late afternoon hangs over the power station district, where brick cooling towers sweat steadily in the warm air. The neighborhood is dense but not tall - a collection of industrial buildings from various decades of the last century, pressed together like books on a shelf, their heights rarely exceeding four or five stories. Steam rises constantly from grates and vents, joining the general atmosphere of moisture that seems to pervade everything.

The ground here is almost entirely paved, but water finds its way through every crack. The pavement has a constant sheen from the combination of condensation and the fine mist that drifts down from the cooling towers. In the narrow alleys between buildings, moss grows in the perpetually damp mortar between bricks. Small ferns have established themselves in rain gutters and along window ledges, though they remain sparse - there's too much steam and smoke for most plants to thrive.

The air is thick with sound: the low thrum of turbines, the hiss of steam releases, the clatter of conveyor systems, and the occasional deep boom from within the largest buildings. These mechanical sounds mix with an unexpected abundance of bird calls - pigeons and starlings have made their homes in every nook and cranny of the buildings, seemingly unfazed by the industrial atmosphere. They've adapted to perch on warm pipes and nest in the shelter of vents, creating their own ecosystem within the mechanical one.

Water drips everywhere - from pipes, from eaves, from the metal walkways that connect buildings at various levels. The constant moisture has left beautiful patterns of rust and mineral deposits on many surfaces, creating unintended compositions in orange and white against the dark brick and metal. Some walls weep continuously, fed by hidden condensation systems within the buildings.

The layout feels almost organic despite its industrial nature - buildings have been added onto and modified over decades, creating a warren-like complexity of passages and courtyards. Steam pipes and power lines create another layer of "vegetation," twisting between buildings like vines. The overall effect is of a small, self-contained district that operates as a single machine, every part connected to every other part through a complex network of pipes, cables, and walkways.

Workers move through the space with practiced familiarity, their paths worn smooth through years of identical journeys. They know which valves hiss at what times, which puddles are permanent features, which bird colonies get territorial during nesting season. The district maintains a steady state - neither declining nor growing, simply continuing its eternal process of power generation, creating the same sounds, the same steam, the same patterns of light and shadow, day after day after day.

At night, the steam catches the orange glow of sodium lights, creating an eternal twilight atmosphere. The birds settle into their roosts, but the mechanical sounds continue unchanged, maintaining their steady rhythm through all hours, all seasons, marking time in cycles of pressure and release, heating and cooling, the eternal circulation of water through the district's mechanical veins.
