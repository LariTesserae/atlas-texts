In the liquid-glass halls of New Benthopolis, the ancient proteins still remember how to fold. The city spreads across the abyssal plain in a fractal lattice of engineered coral, each branching tower holding thousands of climate-refugee families in its translucent chambers. The structure grows continuously but imperceptibly, like a time-lapse played too slow to see movement.

The water here is thick with life - not the sparse, occasional creatures of Earth's deep oceans, but a constant storm of bioluminescent organisms engineered to thrive in these depths. They form living constellations, pulsing information networks that carry messages between distant sections of the city. Schools of augmented fish move in mathematical patterns, their synchronized movements maintaining the delicate chemical balance that keeps the coral-city alive.

The darkness is never complete. Besides the blue-green flutter of the messenger-plankton, the city's structure itself has a faint pearl-white glow - the result of quantum-scale reactions in the coral's crystalline skeleton. This dim radiance reveals the endless forests of filter-feeders that carpet every surface, their fronds moving in currents that feel more like atmosphere than ocean.

The sound is constant and overwhelming: the deep bass rumble of water moving through the city's arteries, the higher harmonic frequencies of the coral's internal communications, the clicking and humming of countless creatures that have evolved or been designed to use sound as both sight and speech in these lightless depths. The acoustics are strange here - sound travels differently through the engineered materials of the city, creating zones where conversations from kilometers away can be heard clearly while nearby noises become muffled and distant.

Temperature remains steady, cool but not cold, maintained by the city's metabolic processes and the careful balance of currents. The water itself isn't quite water anymore - it's been modified over generations, its molecular structure subtly altered to better sustain this dense ecosystem of natural and engineered life. It feels thicker than normal seawater, almost syrupy, rich with suspended nutrients and information-carrying molecules.

In the spaces between the coral-towers, vast gardens of modified kelp and other aquatic plants grow in carefully maintained spirals. They provide food, oxygen, and raw materials for the city's biological manufacturing processes. The plants seem to move with purpose, their movements too regular to be natural current patterns.

The city's residents - human and otherwise - have adapted to this perpetual submersion. Many have opted for subtle biological modifications: enhanced night vision, pressure-resistant tissues, improved oxygen absorption. They move through their water-world with the ease of native creatures, their homes and workplaces designed around three-dimensional movement through liquid space.

Most striking are the megafauna - enormous, engineered creatures that serve as living infrastructure. Transport-whales move between the towers, their bodies modified to carry passengers in comfortable, dry internal chambers. Maintenance-leviathans slowly patrol the city's exterior, their tentacles delicately repairing damage and managing the growth of new sections.

The whole system exists in a carefully maintained equilibrium, each component - from the smallest modified plankton to the largest living transport - playing its role in maintaining this underwater civilization. It's an architecture of life itself, where the line between building and organism, between city and ecosystem, has long since dissolved into the dense, living water.
