The first thing you notice is the clicking. An endless, arhythmic symphony of tiny impacts, like rain on tin but sharper, more purposeful. The clicking comes from the shells - billions of them, coating every surface of this flooded settlement. The gastropods are everywhere, their spiral houses ranging from pinhead-sized to fist-sized, crawling over each other in endless procession.

This was once a fishing village, you think, though now it's hard to tell where the intentional construction ends and the accidental begins. The snails have been building too, their calcified trails gradually accumulating into new walls and archways, filling gaps between the old stone structures with organic architecture that follows its own peculiar logic. The original buildings, made of local limestone and rough timber, are now more suggestion than substance - their forms still visible but transformed by decades of mollusk modification.

The ground is uncertain here. What looks like solid stone may be a crust of shells floating on mud or water. The whole place feels like it's slowly sinking, or perhaps being gradually consumed from below. Every few steps, your foot breaks through a shell-crust into cold, black water. The air itself seems heavy with moisture, and thin rivulets constantly trickle down the walls, feeding the thick patches of grey-green algae that provide food for the endless snails.

In the center of the settlement stands what might once have been a marketplace or gathering space. Now it's a cathedral of shells, its dome formed by millions of tiny spiral houses aggregated over decades. When the wind blows through its many openings, it produces an eerie whistling that mingles with the endless clicking of shells against shells.

The few plants that manage to survive here are tough, primitive things - mostly ferns and horsetails pushing up through gaps in the shell-crust, their stems often serving as highways for the ascending gastropods. The light that filters through the perpetually moist air takes on a greenish cast, as if you're viewing everything through shallow water.

While the snails are the dominant presence, they're not alone. The air is thick with moths - huge, pale things with wingspans as wide as your hand, their wings patterned with spirals that echo the shells below. They seem drawn to the moisture, landing on wet walls to drink, their proboscises unreeling like tiny hosepipes. Sometimes you glimpse larger movements in the deeper waters beneath the shell-crust - sinuous shapes that might be eels, or might be something else entirely.

Time moves strangely here. The snails' pace becomes hypnotic - their endless, patient construction project continuing without haste or pause. The village is becoming something else, something that follows molluscan rather than human architecture. In another decade, will anything recognizable remain? Or will it all be spiral and whorl, a monument to gastropod geometry?

The whole place feels like an experiment in alternative civilization - not human abandonment giving way to nature, but human structure being actively reinterpreted by other minds, other builders, following other laws of design and purpose. The clicking never stops. The shells keep moving. The transformation continues.
