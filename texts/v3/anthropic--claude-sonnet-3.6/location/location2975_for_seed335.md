A hidden grotto curves inward beneath a high mountain meadow, where the limestone has been slowly dissolved by centuries of trickling water. The entrance is barely visible - just a dark crescent between tumbled moss-covered boulders that you might mistake for shadow if you didn't know what to look for.

Inside, the space opens to about the size of a small room, its walls rippling with smooth erosion patterns. Water seeps constantly from the ceiling, not in drops but in thin sheets that catch the indirect light filtering through the entrance. These sheets have encouraged the growth of delicate ferns that seem to defy gravity, growing sideways and even upside down wherever water touches stone. Their tiny leaves shiver occasionally in imperceptible air currents.

The floor is uneven but mostly level, scattered with soft moss in the slightly higher spots and shallow pools in the lower areas. The pools are crystal-clear and surprisingly deep, disappearing into dark crevices below. They teem with pale, translucent cave amphipods and tiny blind salamanders that seem to materialize from nowhere and vanish just as quickly.

The grotto's real secret reveals itself gradually as your eyes adjust: it serves as a nursery for an enormous colony of cave swallows. Their nests, crafted from mud and strands of moss, cling to the higher reaches of the walls like organic architecture. The adult birds slip in and out through a hidden fissure near the ceiling, their movements so practiced and precise they barely disturb the air. The constant but quiet sound of cheeping fills the space - hundreds of hungry chicks waiting for their next meal.

Here and there among the nests are curious artifacts: small clay bowls, crude but carefully made, positioned to catch the pure seeping water. They're old, their rims soft-edged from years of water wear, but still serviceable. Someone once knew this place well enough to leave offerings, or perhaps to gather the sacred water that filters through hundreds of feet of living stone.

The air is cool but not cold, humid enough that you can feel it on your skin but not unpleasant. There's a stillness here that feels ancient, yet the space is far from dead - it pulses with the slow rhythm of water, the patient growth of ferns, and the endless cycle of swallow generations. Time moves differently in this pocket of the mountain, measured in drips and wingbeats rather than days.
