In the high thin air, ancient towers of corroded copper stretch toward a dim sky. Their surfaces are alive with the slow crawl of verdigris - not a living thing, but a patient chemical change that has been consuming these structures for centuries, perhaps millennia. The towers weren't built as green things; they remember being red-gold, though no one remains who saw them that way.

The structures are impossibly tall, their tops lost in the perpetual high haze. They were never meant to be climbed - their smooth faces offer no purchase, and their forms suggest they were grown or extruded rather than assembled. Between them, bridges of the same material span impossible distances, their undersides rippled like frozen waves. Some have partially dissolved in the intermittent rains, leaving gaps that make the remaining network more labyrinthine than it was designed to be.

Water is everywhere and nowhere - gathering in pools in the concave surfaces of the architecture, running down in lazy rivulets that have carved permanent tracks in the metal over eons, collecting as fog in the early hours before the warmth burns it away. The moisture feeds the corrosion, drives the slow transformation of the city's skin. In places, the verdigris has formed thick crusts that occasionally calve off like glaciers, falling with delayed thunder into the depths.

The warm air moves in complex currents through the towers, creating a constant chorus of hollow tones - some at the edge of hearing, others that vibrate in the bones. These sounds shift with the daily heating and cooling cycle, marking time in an ancient score written in wind and metal. Occasionally, portions of the structures themselves adjust with deep groans and shuddering resonances that echo for hours.

Looking down from any vantage reveals the true scale - this city-thing extends to every horizon, a forest of copper towers in every stage of decay and transformation. Some areas have collapsed into heaps that rise again like mountain ranges, while others maintain their original impossible geometries. The spaces between structures are vast - whatever beings built this place clearly had different needs for proximity than humans would recognize.

In the deeper levels, where even the dim light rarely reaches, there are chambers where the air is thick with copper salts, creating underwater-like spaces where all sound becomes muffled and strange. Here, the corrosion forms stalactites of blue-green crystal that grow with geological slowness, dropping centuries between each elongating drip.

This place follows rules, but they're not our rules. The engineering suggests intelligence but defies human logic. The scale implies habitation but offers no concession to inhabitants. It is a machine, perhaps, but one whose purpose has been lost to time - or maybe it is still fulfilling that purpose, and we simply can't recognize what it means to succeed.

The whole vast structure continues its gradual transformation - not dying, not exactly living, but changing state with the patience of chemistry and the persistence of time. The process will continue until either the rains stop or the last copper atom has surrendered to oxidation, whichever comes first in this eternal, empty place.
