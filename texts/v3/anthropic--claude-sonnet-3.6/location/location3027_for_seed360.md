A dense network of ceramic pipes, ancient and sweating in the heat. They wind through each other like a basket woven by a fevered mind, each tube the width of an arm or leg, glazed in deep greens and browns that have taken on a glassy wetness in the humid air. Water moves through them constantly - you can hear it if you press your ear to the clay walls, a soft constant flow that never varies in its whispered rhythm.

The pipes emerge from and dive back into walls made of the same material, walls that curve and lean into each other to form chambers that feel almost organic, like the interior of some great mollusk shell. The surfaces are never quite flat, bearing the marks of countless hands that shaped them long ago. In places, the clay has been impressed with patterns - spirals and waves that seem to ripple in the dim light that filters through occasional crystal-studded apertures in the ceiling.

The heat is oppressive, trapped by the ceramic masses and made worse by the constant seepage of water that beads on every surface. This condensation follows its own paths, creating dark trails down the walls that, over centuries perhaps, have etched shallow channels into the clay. These tiny watercourses host the only life here - the thinnest films of some copper-colored growth that might be algae or might be mineral, spreading in dendritic patterns that mirror the branching of the pipes above.

The air moves little, but when it does, it carries the mineral smell of wet clay and something else - something metallic and old. The few sounds are shaped by the curved walls into something almost musical: the steady drip of condensation, the faint liquid movements in the pipes, the occasional ping of expanding clay in the heat.

Every few body-lengths, the passages shift and turn, opening into new chambers that might be slightly higher or lower, but never much larger than a small room. The whole complex feels like it was built to a human scale, but not for human comfort - more like a machine made of clay and water, still quietly serving some inscrutable purpose after its makers have long gone.

In some places, crude copper fittings join sections of pipe together, green with age but still holding. They seem almost primitive compared to the sophisticated craftsmanship of the ceramic work, as if they were emergency repairs made by later inhabitants who had lost the original makers' skills.

The whole place operates like a single vast instrument, measuring or processing or transforming the water that moves through it in ways that are no longer understood. It feels alive in the way that a shell still feels alive long after its creature has departed - a made thing, but made with such organic logic that the boundary between grown and built becomes uncertain.
