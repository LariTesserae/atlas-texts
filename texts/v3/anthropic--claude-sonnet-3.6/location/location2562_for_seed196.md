In the heart of an ancient mountain's collapse lies a cathedral-sized hollow, worn so smooth and deep over eons that it seems almost artificial - though no hand shaped it. The space feels like the inside of a bell, or perhaps a massive geode split and emptied of its crystals. What was once a mighty peak has been ground down to a gentle rise, its core exposed and polished by time beyond counting.

The walls weep constantly. Not with water exactly, but with a thick, clear substance that moves more slowly than syrup but never quite solidifies. It creates ever-shifting patterns that catch and split what little light filters down from above through a canopy of impossible ferns - their fronds are translucent and overlap in complex geometries, creating a natural geodesic dome over the hollow.

The air is cool and heavy with moisture, but the real presence here is sound. The hollow amplifies everything to overwhelming levels - the drip of the wall-seep becomes a resonant boom, the rustle of ferns transforms into oceanic roars. Most remarkable are the creatures that have adapted to this acoustic amplification: thumbnail-sized amphibians whose whispered croaks become thunderous declarations, insects that communicate through the precise positioning of their wings to create standing waves of sound that make the very air visible as patterns of vibration.

These sound-shapers have evolved complex social structures around their acoustic abilities. They gather in precise formations, their combined sounds creating interference patterns that seem to bend space itself. The smallest movement of a single creature can shift the entire acoustic landscape, transforming the hollow from a chamber of thunderous chaos to one of harmonious, though still deafening, order.

The vegetation here has adapted too. Besides the geometric ferns above, strange tubular plants grow in spiral patterns along the walls. They appear to feed not on light but on vibration itself, their hollow stems swaying and pulsing with the sound-tides. Some produce spores that hang suspended in the air, held aloft by the constant acoustic pressure.

In the center of the hollow stands a single column of the original mountain heart - not placed there, but rather left behind as everything around it wore away. It's darker than the surrounding stone, and seems to drink in both light and sound. The column is covered in fine striations that, if one could somehow quiet the space enough to study them, might read like a record of the mountain's dissolution.

The whole space operates on cycles tied to the intensity of sound - periods of near-painful cacophony followed by intervals of merely loud harmony. These cycles seem random at first, but over many observations might reveal patterns operating on timeframes too long for a single viewing to capture.

The floor is scattered with the delicate shells of extinct sound-shapers, their forms suggesting different acoustic strategies tried and abandoned by evolution. They're so fragile that the constant vibration gradually turns them to powder, adding another layer to the soft, silver-grey sediment that carpets the hollow.

It's a place that challenges normal sensory hierarchies - sound becomes visible, vibration becomes food, and time itself seems to operate in acoustic rather than solar cycles. The hollow feels like a pocket universe that has reached its own unique equilibrium, where the normal rules of nature have been worn away along with the mountain that once stood here, leaving something stranger but no less ordered in their place.
