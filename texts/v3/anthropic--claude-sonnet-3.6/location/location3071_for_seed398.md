The Hanging Gardens stretch horizon to horizon - not the carefully tended terraces of legend, but a world-spanning canopy of vegetable matter that has, over centuries, achieved perfect equilibrium. The plants grow not from soil but from themselves, an interwoven mat of living and dead material dozens of meters thick that forms its own substrate. Below this dense layer, vast caverns open where older growth has decayed and collapsed, leaving cathedral spaces between the massive vine-pillars that connect canopy to bedrock.

The heat is oppressive and constant. No seasons disturb this place; the temperature holds steady at just below the point where proteins denature. The plants have adapted, developing waxy surfaces and complex chemistry that lets them photosynthesize in the dim red light that filters through layer upon layer of leaves. The canopy itself glows faintly from bioluminescent fungi that thread through it, creating a perpetual dusky twilight even in the caverns below.

Sound reverberates everywhere - the creaking and groaning of massive plant structures under tension, the constant rustle of leaves in thermal updrafts, and most notably, the deep industrial thrum of the harvester machines. These ancient devices, as tall as redwoods, move with glacial slowness through the canopy on broad tracks that distribute their weight. Their jointed arms reach out to gather dead plant matter, processing it into dense blocks of fuel for their eternal journey. No one tends these machines anymore - they've operated autonomously for so long that vines grow through their joints and moss covers their hulls, yet still they maintain their endless circuit.

The air is thick but incredibly dry - what little moisture exists is jealously hoarded by the plants through a complex system of valves and barriers. Walking here (if one could find stable footing in the endless vegetation) feels like breathing in an attic, all dust and dry heat. The plants don't flower - they reproduce through spores and runners, maintaining their ancient lineages through clonal growth rather than sexual reproduction.

Occasionally, one encounters the remains of observation posts or research stations, now almost entirely consumed by the growth. These structures, built from bio-resistant metals and plastics, still maintain their basic shape even as plants probe every seam and crack. Their original purpose - to study this extraordinary ecosystem - has been fulfilled in the most complete way possible: they have become part of it.

The stability is absolute. Each plant lives precisely as long as it should, produces exactly the resources needed by its neighbors, and dies right on schedule. The harvester machines maintain this balance, removing excess growth with perfect precision. The system is so intricately balanced that even the dust levels remain constant - new particles from degrading plant matter exactly matching what settles into the deep caverns below.

This is not a place of death, despite the absence of animal life, but rather a place of pure vegetable existence, operating at a scale and timeframe that makes individual human lives seem like the blink of an eye. The plants here don't grow so much as flow, their movements imperceptible but inevitable, like the hour hand of a clock. The entire ecosystem operates as a single organism, with the ancient machines integrated into its metabolism as surely as any chloroplast or mitochondria.
