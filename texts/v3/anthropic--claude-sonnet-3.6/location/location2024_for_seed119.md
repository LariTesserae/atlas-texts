In the sealed bronze sphere, the water is always hot and always moving. The ancient mechanisms still work - crude pumps and channels carved into the metal walls keep the currents flowing in their ordained patterns, maintaining the perfect temperature for the vast schools of blind fish that have lived here for uncounted generations.

The sphere is perhaps forty meters across, though in the total darkness it's impossible to be certain. The fish know its extent by feel, by the familiar rhythms of the currents that carry them in their endless circuits. Their scales occasionally scrape against the intricate relief patterns that cover every surface - pictographic instructions for maintaining the machinery, now illegible under centuries of mineral deposits.

The water tastes of metal and salt. In places, copper has oxidized to form fuzzy green patches that wave in the artificial currents like seaweed. These copper-gardens are sparse but vital - home to colonies of tiny crustaceans that provide food for the fish.

The sound is constant: the deep resonant hum of water moving through bronze pipes, the clank and wheeze of the ancient pumps, the countless tiny impacts of fish bodies against metal walls. All these sounds are carried and amplified by the water until they blend into a continuous roar that seems to come from everywhere at once.

At random intervals, massive gears grind behind the walls - adjusting something, though what exactly is unclear. The fish don't react to these sounds anymore. They've evolved to this environment over thousands of years, their bodies becoming more streamlined, their eyes vestigial, their lateral lines exquisitely sensitive to the sphere's eternal rhythms.

The only light comes from bioluminescent bacteria that grow in some of the deeper channels - not enough to see by, just occasional ghostly blue-green glimmers that fade as quickly as they appear. The fish don't need light to navigate the perfectly circular space with its carefully maintained currents and precisely spaced obstacles.

Sometimes new fish appear or old ones vanish, but the overall population remains stable. The water stays at exactly the same temperature. The pumps continue their work. The sphere neither grows nor decays. It simply persists, a perfectly engineered aquatic habitat whose original purpose, if it had one beyond its own existence, has been long forgotten.

The whole system feels like a machine that has become an ecosystem, or perhaps an ecosystem that has become a machine. It's unclear which came first - the engineering or the life that now depends on it. Both seem to have adapted to each other so completely that they've become a single integrated system, eternal and self-sustaining in its warm, dark, watery isolation.
