In the perpetual gloaming, the factory-flowers bloom. They rise from the cold marsh in thickets of copper and brass, their metallic petals unfurling and folding in endless mechanical cycles. Steam hisses softly from their joints, adding to the low-hanging mist that makes the boundaries of things uncertain. The ground never quite freezes despite the chill - too much heat leaks from the deep root-engines that pulse beneath the surface, turning unknown substances into the materials that feed the flowers' endless growth and transformation.

The landscape stretches for miles, a dense forest of industrial flora. What seems at first like vegetation reveals itself, on closer inspection, to be an intricate symphony of self-replicating machines, though "machine" feels too crude a word for these elegant entities. Their forms echo organic life but follow their own logic - pistons mimicking stamens, gears spinning like seed-pods, delicate filaments of gold wire branching like veins through translucent sheets of some glass-like material that might be leaves.

Water is everywhere but never still. It condenses on the cool metal surfaces in complex patterns, running down in rivulets that seem to carry information between the factory-flowers. The marsh beneath squelches with every step, each footfall releasing bubbles of gas that smell faintly of ozone and copper. The water itself has a slight iridescent sheen, and sometimes tiny lights can be seen moving beneath its surface, though whether they are living things or some mechanical process is impossible to tell.

In the dim light, the factory-flowers cast shifting shadows that never quite match their forms. Their movements create a constant soft background of clicks, whirs, and gentle chimes - a mechanical music that stays just at the edge of hearing. Occasionally there are louder sounds: a sudden release of pressure, the grinding of larger gears deep below, or the musical toning of metal cooling in the cold air.

The few organic creatures that make their home here have adapted to this hybrid ecosystem. Moth-like insects with wings that seem partially metallic flit between the factory-flowers, perhaps pollinating them in some way that bridges the gap between organic and mechanical reproduction. Long-legged birds with unusually geometric patterns in their plumage stalk through the marsh, picking out small shining things with precise movements.

The factory-flowers are constantly building and rebuilding themselves, cannibilizing their own outdated parts and incorporating new materials that emerge from the marsh-depths. Sometimes entire sections of the forest will shudder and reconfigure themselves over the course of hours or days, the individual flowers linking together into larger structures before dissolving again into their component parts. The process seems random at first, but longer observation suggests patterns - like weather systems made of brass and steam, or thoughts slowly forming in some vast distributed mind.

The cold air carries tiny particles that catch what little light there is, creating halos around the factory-flowers' moving parts. These motes might be spores, or microscopic machines, or something in between. They accumulate in drifts like snow, but disappear when touched, leaving only a slight metallic residue on the skin that tingles for hours afterward.

There's an overwhelming sense that this place is both ancient and in constant flux - as though it has been here since long before humans walked the earth, but has never been the same thing twice. The factory-flowers feel less like machines that were built than like a natural process that discovered an alternative path to evolution, one that works in gears and circuits rather than cells and DNA.
