A vast field of earthenware tiles stretches to the horizon, each one hand-pressed and sun-baked generations ago. The tiles interlock in subtle patterns - hexagons within hexagons, spreading outward like frozen ripples. Their surfaces hold countless minute variations in texture and hue, ranging from deep terra-cotta to pale sand, each one unique in its imperfections. Some bear the ghostly impressions of long-gone craftspeople's palms and fingers.

The air hangs thick and warm, carrying the mineral scent of clay. In the pre-dawn hours, dew beads on the tiles' slightly porous surfaces, gathering in the shallow channels between them before slowly seeping down through hairline cracks. This brief morning moisture sustains the sparse life here - hardy mosses that grow in the seams, appearing dead and crusty most hours but unfurling tiny green fronds in the dampness.

At intervals across the expanse, broad shallow bowls are formed by subtly sunken tiles. These catch what little rain falls, holding it just long enough for the surrounding area to drink deeply. The bowls have their own weather - in the heat of afternoon, they generate gentle updrafts that gather what moisture remains in the air, creating fleeting mists that dance briefly before dissipating.

The tile-field is not flat but undulates in long, low waves - the legacy of whatever ancient ground lies beneath, now completely covered by this constructed skin. These swells create acoustic effects, carrying sounds in unexpected ways. A lizard's skittering steps might travel for hundreds of meters along a curved path, or a whisper might pool in one of the bowl-depressions, cycling there until it fades.

The few creatures that make their home here have adapted to move across the tiles without disturbing them - thread-thin snakes that follow the seam-lines, translucent geckos whose foot-pads grip the smooth surfaces, colonies of tiny iridescent insects that harvest the morning dew. They leave no marks; the tile-field remains as it has for centuries, its patterns unbroken.

At the lowest points, where multiple swells converge, there are "wells" - deep cylindrical structures lined with spiraling tiles that descend into darkness. The wells rarely contain water, but they breathe - drawing air down or pushing it up depending on the temperature differential between surface and depths. This breathing can be heard as a low, barely-there hum that changes pitch with the cycles of day and night.

Time here is marked by the slow, inexorable shifting of the tiles themselves - settling, pressing against each other, minute adjustments over decades that produce new patterns in the greater whole. These movements can sometimes be heard as faint clicks or gentle grinding sounds, especially in the hours around dawn when temperature changes are greatest.

The tile-field seems to extend forever, but locals speak of its edges - places where the tiles finally give way to something else. However, these edges are different for each telling, as if the field's true extent cannot be fixed or measured. What's certain is that it has existed longer than memory, and will likely outlast those who cross it today, step by careful step across its sun-warmed surface.
