The aerial roots descend like pillars, each one thick as a redwood, creating a canopy so high above that it generates its own weather. These are not trees - they are the anchoring tendrils of the vast fungal-plant mat that stretches across the continental plateau. The mat itself is kilometers thick in places, a dense weave of mycorrhizal networks and vegetable matter that has, over millions of years, developed its own biology distinct from the ancient bedrock far below.

The space between these massive roots forms vast chambers, dim cathedral spaces where the air hangs thick and warm. Bioluminescent pods pulse slowly along the fibrous walls, creating a constant play of shadows. The light they cast is amber and indigo, barely enough to see by, but enough to reveal the industrial complexes that have been built in these natural halls.

The factories are strange hybrid things - part grown, part built. Their outer shells are cultivated from the living matter of the mat itself, shaped over decades into processing chambers and storage vaults. Within, mechanical components harvest and refine the complex chemicals that the mat produces. The facilities seem almost deserted, but they're not - they're simply vast, their scattered workers dwarfed by the scale. The machines run largely autonomously, their rhythmic thundering echoing through the chambers in a deep, persistent drone that mingles with the constant creak and groan of the living architecture above.

Occasionally, one might spot the massive filter-feeders that drift between the root-pillars - balloon-like creatures that subsist on the thick spores that constantly shed from the mat above. They're rare sights, moving so slowly they barely seem to move at all, their translucent bodies glowing faintly with the same bioluminescence as the walls.

There is no water here - not in liquid form. The mat absorbs all moisture from the warm air, processing it through its vast network of vessels. Sometimes this creates a rain of sorts, but not of water - instead, thick organic oils drip from the ceiling in slow, steady streams, feeding back into the cycling metabolism of this living world.

The height is disorienting - while you're technically on a plateau already kilometers above sea level, the root-chambers themselves extend upward for hundreds of meters, and the mat above is thicker still. The whole system has evolved over eons to support its own weight, developing structures that blur the line between plant and mineral, living and nonliving.

In places, you can find the remnants of earlier attempts to industrialize this space - abandoned platforms, partial scaffolding, pieces of equipment half-absorbed by the ever-growing walls. The mat is slow but relentless, gradually incorporating everything into its mass. New construction must be actively maintained against this vegetable tide, leading to the current approach of working with the living material rather than against it.

The air grows thinner as you ascend through the levels of chambers, but remains warm - the mat generates its own heat through its endless metabolic processes. Near the top, where the root-pillars begin to merge into the mat proper, the space becomes a maze of twisted organic forms, all pulsing with that same slow, living rhythm that seems to govern everything in this strange vertical world.
