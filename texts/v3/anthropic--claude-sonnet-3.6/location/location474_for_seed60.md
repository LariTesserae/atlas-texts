The Living Factory unfolds across a few hundred meters of what was once lowland, though now the ground itself is more theoretical than actual. The substrate here is entirely organic: a dense mat of interweaving roots, shoots, and mycelia that form a spongy, self-repairing foundation. This living platform quietly heaves and settles in month-long breathing cycles, rising perhaps a meter at its peak inflation.

Through this botanical mass, countless mechanical components are threaded - brass pipes, copper wiring, glass tubes, and steel frameworks - but these are less installations than they are suggestions that the plants have chosen to incorporate. The vegetation here doesn't simply grow around the machinery; it grows as machinery. Fibrous tissues have evolved to conduct electricity. Hollow stems carry pressurized fluids. Specialized leaves have developed crystalline sections that serve as solar collectors and lenses.

The air is thick with warm humidity that beads constantly on every surface, feeding the omnipresent growth while also contributing to the perpetual sound - a background symphony of drips, trickling, and the occasional heavy splash as accumulated water releases from overhead catchments. This moisture feeds into the living factory's processes, moving through organic pipelines and emerging transformed: sometimes steaming, sometimes colored with mysterious pigments, sometimes glowing with bioluminescent reactions.

The few remaining conventional buildings - small monitoring stations and research outposts - are being steadily consumed. Not aggressively, but inevitable as morning. Roots probe through foundations, shoots emerge from walls, and eventually the structures simply become more scaffolding for the plant-machine hybrid organism to incorporate. Their windows still glow at night, but the light increasingly comes from the building-dissolving growth itself rather than any human power source.

What fauna exists here is mainly insects - specialized pollinators and maintenance species that tend to the living machinery. They move with unnatural precision, suggesting some degree of inherited programming in their genetics. Occasionally one might spot a bird that's made its home here, but they seem out of place, visitors from a more conventional world.

The overall impression is of stepping into something that was once a traditional industrial site but has been transformed through decades or centuries of engineered evolution into something that achieves the same purposes through entirely biological means. The air smells of green growth and warm metal, soil and ozone. Everything is slightly motion, though so slowly as to only be noticeable through time-lapse: valves opening and closing over hours, nutrient flows shifting over days, new growth extending through weeks.

The persistent sound isn't just water - it's also the creaking of organic machinery, the whisper of gases through living pipes, the occasional steam release from hybrid organisms that might once have been pressure valves but are now something far stranger. The sounds merge into a constant industrial white noise that seems to come from everywhere at once.

All of this fills the space completely - looking up, looking down, looking in any direction shows only more of the same dense growth-machine integration, as if someone had somehow crossbred a rainforest with a Victorian factory and left it to evolve for a few centuries in isolation.
