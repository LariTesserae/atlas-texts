A sunken factory garden, claimed by moss and sound. The space feels contained, almost cupped - maybe it was a quarry once, or some industrial basin that settled into the wet earth over decades. The walls curve up gently, striated with old concrete and newer organic growth, everything wrapped in a perpetual pearl-grey mist that seems to amplify rather than muffle the overwhelming noise.

The sound hits you first - a cathedral roar of water and machinery locked in endless conversation. Ancient pumps thunder somewhere in the depths, their rhythms mixing with cascade-sound from dozens of small waterfalls that thread down the walls. The water moves in established patterns, having carved its own channels through the infrastructure over years. In places it pools in terraced basins filled with writhing vegetation; in others it vanishes into mysterious pipes only to emerge elsewhere transformed into steam or fine spray.

The air is thick with moisture and motion. Countless small creatures dart through the fog - iridescent insects, tiny amphibians, darting shapes that might be mechanical or might be living. Their movements create complex eddies in the mist, momentarily revealing glimpses of the dense ecological tapestry that fills every surface. Moss carpets everything in layers of green-grey velvet, hosting colonies of luminescent fungi that pulse dimly in the gloom. Vines as thick as your arm snake through old machinery, using gears and pulleys as natural trellises.

What might once have been a factory floor is now a series of interconnected pools and islands, some still bearing the geometric ghosts of industrial planning but most evolved into organic shapes. The water is never still - besides the constant flow from above, there's regular movement from below as ancient mechanisms continue their blind work, sending up bubbles and currents and occasional bursts of warmer water that feed the ecosystem.

The remaining machinery has been thoroughly incorporated into the living architecture. Rusted conveyor belts serve as highways for small mammals that nest in the upper levels. Crumbling control panels sprout delicate ferns from their crevices. A partially collapsed crane has become the backbone of a complex structure of vines and epiphytes that forms a living arch over one of the larger pools.

The scale is intimate despite the overwhelming sensory experience - you can see most of the space from any point, though the mist plays tricks with distance and perspective. The boundaries feel organic now, but there's still something artificial in the containment, in the way the space holds itself apart from whatever lies beyond its rim.

The energy is constant but not chaotic - more like an established dance between the mechanical and organic components that have learned each other's rhythms over time. Even the loudness feels purposeful, as if the space is performing its own industrial-natural symphony, the beats of pistons and pumps providing percussion for the liquid melody of the falls and the constant counterpoint of creature-sound.

In the dimness, small lights move and pulse: the bioluminescent fungi, yes, but also indicators still glowing on half-buried control panels, the eyes of nocturnal creatures, and occasional electric sparks from exposed wiring that has been colonized by some kind of conducting moss. These points of light reflect in the omnipresent water, creating a constantly shifting constellation below to match the one hidden by mist above.
