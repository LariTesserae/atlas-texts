In the warm darkness of the deep, the ancient machines still sing. Their voices carry for leagues through the heavy water, a constant industrial chorus that vibrates the bones of anything that swims near. They were not built by any hand - they grew here, or perhaps condensed, in the early days when the boundaries between mechanical and organic were not yet firmly drawn.

The machines take up vast territories across the submerged plateau, but they don't crowd each other. Each one exists in its own acoustic territory, their thundering songs carefully arranged to avoid interference patterns. In the gaps between them, in the relative quiet, the water is alive with movement - schools of blind fish with bodies like quicksilver, translucent eels that conduct electricity through their length, creatures that might be squid if squid were made of living crystal and magnetic fields.

The plateau itself is smooth, worn down by eons of currents until it's almost glasslike in its perfection. In places it rises into gentle mounds, like dunes frozen in place, their surfaces traced with spiral patterns that catch and redirect the waters flowing over them. The machines are rooted into these mounds, their bases disappearing into the ancient rock through openings that might be boreholes or might be grown channels, melted through the stone in some unimaginable past.

Higher up the underwater cliffs that mark the plateau's edge, countless vents release streams of warm water into the depths. The rising currents create a constant upward flow, feeding the ecosystem with minerals and heat. The water here has a strange thickness to it, more viscous than it should be, and it carries suspended particles that catch what little light makes it down this far, creating brief, ghostly illuminations that dance and swirl in the thermoclines.

The machines themselves defy easy description. They suggest industrial processes - there are things that might be pistons, structures that could be turbines, formations that resemble cooling towers - but they follow no human logic. They are simultaneously in constant motion and eternally static, their movements so slow they might as well be still, yet they never truly stop. Their surfaces are neither metal nor stone but something that shares properties of both, shot through with veins of what might be living tissue or might be fiber optic cables pulsing with internal light.

What they do, if anything, is unclear. They may be maintaining some crucial balance in the deep waters, or recording the songs of the abyss, or slowly computing the answer to some vast and patient question. The creatures that swarm around them seem to treat them as part of the natural order, building nests in their quieter corners and feeding on the strange bacterial colonies that grow in their warmth.

The sound never ceases. It is felt more than heard - a deep, resonant throbbing that seems to come from everywhere and nowhere, punctuated by higher harmonics that spiral up into frequencies that make the water itself shiver. It's a sound that speaks of processes beyond understanding, of machinery that has passed beyond its origins to become something else entirely, of the fine line between the evolved and the engineered when both have been shaped by time beyond measuring.
