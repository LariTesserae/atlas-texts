In the darkness between mountain peaks float the Garden Cities - vast spherical structures of woven plant matter, their surfaces a continuous mesh of living bamboo and rattan that grows and regrows itself according to ancient patterns. Each city is the size of a small town, tethered to its neighbors by thick braided bridges of the same living material that flex and creak in the warm winds that move between the peaks.

The surfaces breathe. Every few minutes, with a sound like distant ocean waves, millions of stomata open and close in coordination, drawing moisture from the night air. The collected condensation runs in rivulets through specialized channels - not built but grown - that carry water to every part of the sphere. The whole structure exists in a state of constant, deliberate growth, with older sections gradually composting themselves while new growth emerges, maintaining the city's form through continuous renewal rather than static construction.

Inside, the darkness is nearly complete. The inhabitants - if they can be called that - are the plants themselves, forming a dense three-dimensional maze of chambers, corridors, and vertical shafts. What appears to be furniture or architecture is simply more specialized growth: sleeping platforms of springy mycelia, storage chambers lined with modified pitcher plants, spiral staircases of interlocked branches that adjust their angle in response to pressure.

The only light comes from patches of bioluminescent moss that pulse slowly, their rhythm matching the breathing of the stomata. Their dim blue-green glow reveals glimpses of the endless vegetation - walls that are really curtains of roots, ceilings that writhe imperceptibly as new shoots extend themselves, floors that yield slightly with each step as countless tiny adjustments distribute weight through the living network.

The sound is constant: the creaking of the main structure as it sways, the whisper of water moving through the channels, the deep bass note of the stomatal breathing, and occasionally a sharp crack as a new growth chamber suddenly expands to its full size or an old one begins its controlled collapse into compost. These sounds echo strangely in the organic chambers, seeming to come from everywhere and nowhere.

There are tools here, primitive but effective: pruning hooks grown from hardened thorns, containers that are modified gourds, nets of living fiber that can be commanded to contract or expand. But these are rare - most needs are met by the direct growth and adaptation of the city itself.

This is not a place that was built, but rather one that was grown, guided by techniques passed down through generations of what must once have been human gardeners. Now the cities maintain themselves, following their embedded patterns, extending new bridges to create new spheres when they reach a certain size, letting old ones decompose when their time comes. They are simultaneously ancient and new, their substance constantly replacing itself while their form persists across centuries.

The weirdest thing about them is how they seem to hover between being places and being organisms - or perhaps they've found a way to be both at once, or neither. They are architecture that lives, or life that has learned to become architecture, perpetually emerging into new forms while holding to their essential pattern, like a thought that dreams itself endlessly into being.
