The plateau stretches out like an ancient sea floor lifted to the sky, its surface a testament to deep time. What was once jagged and mountainous has been ground down over eons into something more honest - a vast expanse of exposed bedrock polished by centuries of wind into undulating waves of stone. The rock itself holds a pattern like marbled fat, bands of darker and lighter material twisted together by forces too slow and massive to comprehend.

The wind never truly stops here. It moves in cycles - hours of low moaning punctuated by sudden rushes that send loose grit skating across the stone. The sound fills the space, a constant presence that seems to emerge from the rock itself.

Moisture comes in the form of a regular fog that rolls in during the early hours, condensing on the cool stone and gathering in countless shallow depressions. These temporary pools, some no larger than a palm and others stretching meters wide, host brief explosions of life - specialized algae that can complete their entire lifecycle in the span between fog and evaporation. Their emergence and dissolution happens daily, leaving faint stains that add another layer to the rock's complex patterning.

The only permanent water exists in the "sound wells" - deep vertical shafts worn into the plateau by millennia of dripping water. They descend far below the surface, and their peculiar acoustics transform the wind into something like music - deep, resonant tones that vary with the weather and season. Local legend says you can hear the planet breathing through them.

Vegetation is minimal but persistent. Lichen forms vast, subtle mandalas across the stone, visible only when you step back far enough to see the pattern. Tough grasses grow in clusters wherever soil has managed to accumulate, their stalks wrapped in a water-preserving waxy coating that gives them an artificial sheen.

The plateau's industrial heritage is written in rust and steel. Ancient railway tracks, their ties long rotted away, trace straight lines across the curved stone. They lead to and from the processing plants - massive structures of corroded metal and pitted concrete that rise from the landscape like fossilized creatures. Most are abandoned, but a few still operate, their internal machinery thundering day and night as they extract mineral deposits from deep below the surface. The operating plants are sheeted in modern solar panels that glitter incongruously against their weathered bodies.

The facilities are largely automated now, monitored remotely and maintained by occasional visits from engineers. But they've attracted their own ecosystem. Modified ravens nest in the higher structures, their feathers glinting with metallic additions that help them harvest static electricity from the atmosphere. Small, quick things that might once have been rats scurry through the plants' internal spaces, their bodies adapted to metabolize industrial lubricants and coolants.

At certain times of day, when the light strikes the stone at just the right angle, you can see the shadows of what came before - hints of structures and civilizations worn almost completely away. They show up as subtle variations in the rock's surface, geometric shapes too regular to be natural but too eroded to be clearly artificial. Whatever built them is long gone, leaving only these ghosts pressed into stone.

The overall impression is of a place that has already witnessed its own end and continued on regardless, where the boundaries between natural and artificial, between geology and technology, have worn away just like everything else. It feels both deeply ancient and oddly ahead of its time, as if you're seeing both the past and future of all built things.
