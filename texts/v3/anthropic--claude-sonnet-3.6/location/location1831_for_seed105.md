A vast plain of black glass stretches toward distant horizons, its surface worn to a dull sheen by eons of windblown crystalline dust. The glass is not smooth but fractured into irregular polygons, their edges softened by time until each segment nestles against its neighbors like pieces of an ancient puzzle. In places, the segments have settled at slightly different heights, creating a subtle topography of steps and tilts that catches the dim light of the violet sun.

The air is thin and cold, carrying particles that chime faintly when they collide – tiny shards of the same glassy material that makes up the plain. These particles collect in the deeper cracks, forming lines of glittering dust that trace geometric patterns across the landscape. The sound they make is barely audible: a whispered tinkling that comes and goes with the sparse, intermittent winds.

At irregular intervals across the plain stand structures that might be mistaken for natural crystalline formations, except for their precise geometries. They are constructed from the same glass as the ground, but darker, almost opaque, rising in spiraling forms that reach heights of twenty or thirty meters. Their surfaces show the same weathering as the plain – the same softened edges, the same accumulation of glassy dust in their grooves and hollows. Whether they were built or grown is impossible to tell.

The only movement comes from what the locals call "wind-walkers" – collections of the crystalline dust that sometimes cohere into roughly bipedal forms when caught in thermal updrafts. They drift across the plain like hesitant ghosts, maintaining their shape for minutes or hours before dissolving back into scattered particles. The locals themselves are rarely seen, living in deep fissures that crack through the glass plain to unknown depths.

Near the horizon, where the plain begins to rise into foothills, stand groves of what appear to be trees but are actually formations of metallic filaments. These "wire forests" vibrate in the wind, creating long, low harmonics that can be felt more than heard. They mark the boundary between the glass plain and the higher elevations, where the geometry of the land becomes increasingly complex and unintuitive.

The entire landscape feels like the end of a long, slow process – as if some ancient civilization or geological event transformed everything into this glassy state, and the land has been settling into its final form ever since. The air carries the taste of metal and stone, and occasionally, during the planet's lengthy dusk, the whole plain seems to hum with accumulated memories of whatever came before.

In the deepest cracks, where the glassy surface has split completely, one can sometimes glimpse lights moving in the depths – perhaps the locals, perhaps something else entirely. These lights seem to follow patterns too complex to discern, and they're accompanied by vibrations that suggest vast mechanisms still turning somewhere far below the surface.
