A plain of rust extends to the horizon, densely packed hexagonal plates that fit together like ancient pottery shards reassembled with mathematical precision. The plates are enormous - each one perhaps thirty meters across - forming a vast tessellation that undulates gently over low hills. Their surfaces hold complex patterns: whorls and striations in deeper reds and browns, like wood grain frozen in metal.

The air is warm and still, holding a faint metallic taste. What little sound exists comes as occasional low groans - the plates shifting minutely against each other as they expand and contract with temperature changes. These movements are imperceptible to the eye but accumulate over months and years, grinding the edges of the plates into fine red dust that collects in the seams between them.

At irregular intervals, slim towers rise from the junctions where three plates meet. They appear to be some kind of monitoring stations or processing plants - their dark angular forms suggesting industrial purpose rather than habitation. Most are clearly abandoned, their surfaces pitted and worn to match the plain below. A few still pulse with dim lights or emit barely audible hums, maintaining whatever function they were built for long after their builders departed.

The sparse life here has adapted to extract sustenance directly from the metal. Patches of filamentous growth - somewhere between fungus and wire - spread in fractal patterns across the plates. They gleam with a dull silver-purple iridescence in the wan light that filters through the perpetually hazy sky. Occasionally, something like a cross between a millipede and a magnetic tape winds its way along the seams between plates, leaving faint scratches in the rust as it scrapes sustenance from the metal.

The most striking features are the "bloom fields" - areas where the rust has grown upward in delicate crystalline structures, like gardens of metallic flowers. These formations take decades or centuries to develop, requiring specific conditions of temperature and mineral composition. Their growth is so slow as to be imperceptible, but over time they create intricate forests of copper-red fractals that can rise several meters high.

Near one of the more active towers, a small structure hunkers close to the ground. It might be a research station or maintenance hub, its purpose unclear but its design clearly adapted to this strange environment. Its walls are the same red-brown as the plain, as if it's slowly being consumed by the rust that dominates this world.

The whole landscape feels like a vast circuit board that has been abandoned to corrode, or perhaps a living machine that operates on a timescale too slow for human perception. There's an odd beauty to its geometric regularity and saturated colors, but also a sense of loss - of processes continuing long after their original purpose has been forgotten.
