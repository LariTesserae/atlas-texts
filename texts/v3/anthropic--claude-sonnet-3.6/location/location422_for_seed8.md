The ancient salt flats stretch to the horizon, a vast tableland of hexagonal tiles that catches and holds the sky in countless shallow pools. What was once an inland sea has been baking here for millions of years, leaving behind these geometrically-cracked plains where water still remembers its old home. The surface is almost perfectly flat - any elevation change across dozens of miles could be measured in inches.

During the wet season, warm rains sweep in daily, turning the entirety into a mirror barely ankle-deep. The water holds so still you can see individual clouds drift overhead in its reflection. As the water slowly evaporates, it leaves behind fresh crystals that grow in delicate formations - white fractal blooms that crunch underfoot like delicate snow, despite the constant warmth.

Life here has adapted to the rhythm of flood and drought. Hardy grasses grow in scattered clumps along the slight ridges between salt pans, their roots protected by symbiotic fungi that process the brine. During wet periods, brine shrimp emerge in countless numbers from cryptobiotic eggs that can survive years of dormancy. Their movements create a constant soft stirring in the shallow waters, a background sussurance beneath the calls of the wading birds that arrive to feast.

The birds are everywhere - stilts, flamingos, spoonbills, ibises - their calls echoing across the flat expanse. They move in large, loose colonies that shift and reform like slow clouds, following the optimal feeding depths as water levels change. Their waste feeds algal mats that spread in shifting watercolor patterns just beneath the water's surface, providing food for the next generation of brine shrimp.

At dawn and dusk, warm air rising from the salt flats hits cooler air above, creating sheets of ground-hugging mist that flow like slow rivers across the plain. The mist catches and magnifies the low sunlight, turning the landscape into something that seems more like a dream of a place than a place itself - all soft gold light and indistinct boundaries, punctuated by the dark vertical lines of birds standing motionless in the shallows.

The whole landscape feels impossibly ancient and patient. The salt that forms its bones has been dissolving and recrystallizing in endless cycles since before the continents reached their current arrangements. The processes here operate on geological time - even the daily floods and evaporations feel less like events and more like the breathing of some vast, slow organism that measures its lifecycle in eons.

The air smells of salt and sun-warmed mud and the slightly sweet decay of algae. Sound carries strangely across the flats - bird calls seem to come from everywhere at once, and distant thunder arrives as a felt vibration as much as a sound. There's a constant background of small noises: the crystalline tinkling of salt formations growing and dissolving, the subtle stirring of brine shrimp, the soft splash of birds fishing, all combining into a kind of white noise that feels as ancient as the salt itself.
