A glass-roofed industrial nursery stretches through what was once a natural hollow between hills, now carefully terraced and reinforced. The structure is long but narrow, following the natural contours of the land, its skeleton made of weathered copper-green girders that have taken on the hue of the abundant ferns and mosses that grow in every crevice.

The air inside is cool and thick with moisture that beads on the glass panels above. These panels are not uniform but a patchwork - some clear, some frosted, some tinted in subtle greens and blues, creating a dappled light that shifts as clouds pass overhead. Every few minutes, a fine mist hisses from brass nozzles set along the walls, maintaining the delicate moisture balance that the facility's inhabitants require.

The space is divided into irregular sections by copper mesh screens that have oxidized to a soft verdigris. These screens are permeable but mark distinct environmental zones, each one a carefully maintained ecosystem. The floors are an intricate network of shallow channels and raised platforms, made of textured ceramic in muted earth tones. Water moves slowly through these channels, never more than a finger's depth, but constant in its circulation.

Various species of amphibians make their homes here - not in great crowds, but in carefully spaced territories. Glass frogs with translucent skin rest on broad leaves, their organs visible as shadows. Spotted salamanders emerge from hidden crevices to patrol their territories. The animals move with deliberate slowness, but their calls create a constant chorus that echoes off the glass ceiling - some high and bell-like, others deeper and thrumming, combining into a surprisingly loud ambient symphony.

Mechanical elements are everywhere but unobtrusive: small copper pipes carrying water and nutrients, clockwork timers controlling the mist cycles, brass-housed thermometers and hygrometers monitored by white-coated attendants who move quietly through the space recording measurements in leather-bound logs. The technology feels archaic yet precise, more like a nineteenth-century naturalist's dream than a modern facility.

The plants are the true masters of this space - not wild exactly, but cultivated to achieve a controlled lushness. Ferns unfurl their fronds in slow motion, mosses create thick carpets that soften every hard edge, and delicate orchids bloom in unexpected corners. The flora is arranged sparsely enough that each specimen has room to be appreciated individually, yet densely enough to create a coherent environment.

At regular intervals, glass-walled observation pods protrude from the sides of the structure, each one a small study space where researchers can sit and observe without disturbing the carefully maintained atmosphere within. Their breath frosts the glass on cool mornings, adding another layer to the constant play of condensation that makes the boundaries between inside and outside seem permeable and alive.

The whole place feels like a piece of living machinery, but one designed to nurture rather than produce - a botanical chronometer measuring time in unfurling leaves and the slow pulse of amphibian hearts.
