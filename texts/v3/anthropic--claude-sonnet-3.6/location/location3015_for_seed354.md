Deep beneath the thermal seas lies the Manufactory - not a single facility but a vast interconnected complex stretching across the abyssal plain like a submerged city. Its architecture follows the ancient contours of the seabed, buildings grown from reinforced coral-concrete rising in terraced layers that echo extinct underwater mountains ground down by eons of currents.

The waters here maintain a constant, heavy heat, fed by geothermal vents that were tamed and engineered centuries ago. Their output now flows through a labyrinth of pipes and exchanges, powering the endless processes of the deep. Steam rises in thick columns before condensing again in the cooler waters above, creating slow-moving rivers of warmth that drift through the facility's upper levels.

Dense gardens of engineered kelp forests wave in the artificial currents, their translucent fronds glowing with bioluminescent markers - navigation aids in the perpetual twilight. These are not natural plants but living machines, each strand processing the mineral-rich waters through microscopic factories in their cellular structure. The kelp forests grow in strict geometric patterns, their careful spacing maintained by automated trimming drones that move almost invisibly through the gloom.

Everything here is precisely managed, every surface colonized by purpose. The facility's walls are alive with filter-feeding mechanisms that draw nutrients from the water. Transmission cables are sheathed in synthetic anemones that prevent build-up of deep-sea minerals. Even the sediment on the facility floor is host to networks of mycological processors, breaking down waste into usable materials.

The sound is oddly muted - the heavy pressure of the deep ocean absorbs most frequencies, leaving only the low throb of the geothermal processors and the occasional ping of sonar guidance systems. The water itself seems thick here, dense with suspended particles that catch what little light makes it down from the surface, creating patches of drifting luminescence.

There are no fish, no natural sea life. The complex is too hot, too artificial, too saturated with industrial processes to support them. Instead, maintenance drones move through the waters like mechanical plankton, their lights creating brief constellations in the murk as they tend to the endless tasks of upkeep.

The facility's true purpose is obscured by its scale and complexity - perhaps even forgotten by its builders. Its processes continue with the inexorability of geological time, neither hurried nor delayed. The distinction between what was built and what has grown has blurred over the centuries of operation. The Manufactory simply exists, as much a part of the deep ocean as the pressure and the darkness, continuing its slow work in the hot, heavy waters of the abyss.
