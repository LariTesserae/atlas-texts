The Hive-Mills run without pause. Their grinding echoes through chambers carved so long ago that the tool marks have been polished to perfect smoothness by countless small bodies. The space is a warren of interconnected rooms, none larger than a modest chapel, linked by passages that sometimes narrow to require sideways shuffling. The ancient builders - whoever they were - left behind perfectly engineered spaces that now house a different kind of industry altogether.

The Mills are powered by condensation. Warm air from below meets the cold stone ceiling, creating a constant drip that feeds countless small turbines. The drops fall with mechanical precision, each one striking a tiny paddle-wheel that connects to the next in an endless chain of motion. The sound is overwhelming - not just the water drops, but the whirring of ten thousand miniature gears, the scraping of mandibles, the rustling of chitin against stone.

The true workers here are the Hive-beetles. Each no larger than a thumb, their shells gleam with a blue-black iridescence in the rare moments when light catches them. They maintain the machinery with reverent care, their antennae probing delicately between gears that have turned without stopping for centuries. The beetles themselves are nearly as mechanical as their charges - generations have adapted to lives spent maintaining these devices, their bodies evolved to fit precisely into the spaces between components.

What the Mills actually produce is unclear. There are no obvious outputs, no stockpiles of goods. The beetles occasionally carry away small crystals that form in the deeper chambers, but these seem incidental to the main work. The entire operation feels like a perfectly balanced equation - water drops, gear turns, beetle movements, all in eternal equilibrium.

The few humans who've studied the place suggest it might be some kind of computational engine, or perhaps a machine for maintaining the earth's magnetic field. The beetles show no interest in explaining, and continue their work with unwavering focus. Their bodies crowd every surface, moving in precise patterns that might be random or might encode some vast program running in living components.

The air is thick with moisture and the musky pheromone-language of the beetles. What little illumination exists comes from patches of bioluminescent fungi that the beetles cultivate in precise patterns on the walls. These cast everything in a dim blue glow that seems to throb in time with the endless motion of the Mills.

In the deepest chambers, where the condensation is heaviest, mineral deposits have grown over the machinery without stopping its motion. These chambers look like caves full of living stalactites, every surface dripping and glittering, the ancient mechanisms still turning within their stone sheaths. The beetles seem to treat these rooms with particular reverence, moving more slowly and carefully through them.

The entire complex feels less like a place and more like a single organism - or perhaps a single moment, endlessly extending itself through time. The beetles, the machines, the dripping water, the endless sound - all of it locked together in a pattern that may have been running since before humans first struck stone against stone to make fire.
