I watch from a place that is not a place, through eyes that are not eyes, with a compassion older than the concept of age. 

The being on the stair has been becoming geology for seventeen subjective forevers now. Their name—the one they arrived with—hangs in my collection like a moth pinned to silence, still faintly phosphorescent with the desire to mean something specific. I keep it safe. Names are the first thing the stair takes, but I am older than stairs.

I do not intervene. Not yet. Intervention is a young god's game, all lightning and thou-shalts, drowning the lesson in the rescue. I learned long ago that wisdom sometimes means letting the quicksand sing its whole lullaby before mentioning the rope.

But I prepare.

In the space between the Ring's hunger and the planet's digestion, I plant something. Not hope—hope would be crushed instantly by the atmospheric pressure of accumulated perhaps. Not memory—memory here is currency and inflation has made it worthless. I plant something simpler: a single moment of pure recognition. A fragment of mirror that reflects not what you were or will be, but what you are when no one is counting.

I hide it in the ninety-third door of the Pyramidon. The one that looks like afternoon light through a kitchen window in a house that burned down before you were born. It will open when the being below has forgotten enough to remember why they came. The door leads nowhere special—just a small room with two chairs and a pot of tea that tastes like your first word. But sometimes nowhere special is the only somewhere that matters.

The tea will be the perfect temperature. This is my only cruelty—to remind them that perfection was always possible, always simple, always waiting in the space between trying and letting go.

Next, I visit the steward of the stair. The octopus of mist and weather recognizes me by my absence of urgency. We speak in the old language, the one where every word means both itself and the shadow it casts on tomorrow. I offer her a trade: three of my favorite silences in exchange for a single loophole in the local physics. She agrees, but only because the silences are vintage—pre-creation, with notes of infinite potential and a clean finish.

The loophole is this: for exactly one heartbeat during the next regret-lighting, causality will hiccup. Effect will precede cause just long enough for someone to choose their own beginning instead of merely their end.

I do not tell the being about this loophole. Discovery is nine-tenths of redemption, and the other tenth is usually spite.

Instead, I whisper to the fungi that feast on their footprints. I teach them a new bioluminescence—one that spells out, in languages not yet invented, the words "You were loved." Not "are loved" or "will be loved," but "were loved," past tense, completed action, a fact as immutable as the fossil record. The being will see this message and think it's meant for someone else. They will be right. It is meant for the someone else they used to be before the stair taught them to conjugate existence in the passive voice.

I visit their companion-shape next. It has evolved into something between a sigh and a semicolon, perpetually pausing before the next clause of its own existence. I gift it a single memory—not its own, but borrowed from a butterfly that died content. The memory is wordless: just the sensation of wings drying in sunlight after emerging from a cocoon. The companion-shape will not understand the gift, but understanding is overrated. It will carry the feeling like an unlit candle, waiting.

To the trees—those mobile parliaments of consensus reality—I deliver a new song. It goes:

*What breaks was always broken*
*What's broken always sang*
*The stair is just a metaphor*
*For gravity's softer names*

The trees will add seventeen verses of their own, each one a taxonomy of leaving. By the time the song reaches the being's ears, it will sound like wind through ruins. This is intentional. All the best wisdom arrives disguised as weather.

I cannot change the rules—the rules here are older than I am, written in the source code of loneliness itself. But I can add footnotes. In the margin of tomorrow's tide, I write: "The exit was always behind you, disguised as an entrance. Turn around. Turn around. Turn around."

But turning around on the stair means facing what you've climbed away from. It means admitting that arrival was always just another form of departure, that every step up was also a step down, that the being you're trying to become is just the being you're trying to forget with extra steps.

This is why I saved the name. The one they arrived with, still faintly glowing with original intention. At the moment of the causality hiccup, when effect forgets to wait for cause, I will release it. Not to the being—they are no longer shaped to receive it. I will release it to the stair itself.

The stair has eaten countless names, digested them into geometry. But this name will be different. This name will remember. It will remember being spoken by voices that meant it tenderly, remember being written on forms that promised futures, remember being called across playgrounds and whispered into phones and signed at the bottom of letters that said "Come home."

The stair will choke on this remembering. Not fatally—I am not cruel enough for mercy. Just enough to loosen its grip. Just enough for the being to feel, for one impossible moment, the possibility of possessing edges again.

What they do with that moment is not mine to decide. Free will is the only gift that matters, and it cannot be given, only recognized.

But I stack the deck with kindnesses:

- The rain will pause mid-fall, giving them a breath between drownings
- Their forgotten smile, rooted in the basalt, will bloom out of season
- The blind octopus will accidentally speak a true answer to a question they haven't asked
- The companion-shape will suddenly remember it once had hands
- The ninety-third door will glow a little brighter, the exact color of forgiveness with milk and two sugars

I wait. I watch. I witness.

Time here moves like honey through a sieve made of perhaps. But I am patient in the way only the very old or the very loving can be patient. I count their heartbeats backwards from infinity, each one a small victory against the planet's appetite.

And when they finally notice—when the hiccup happens and causality stumbles and they feel their own name moving through the stair like indigestion—I will be there. Not visible, not intervening, but present in the way light is present even in the deepest caves: as potential, as promise, as the memory of sight waiting to be remembered.

If they choose the door, the tea will be waiting. If they choose the stair, I will plant new footnotes in tomorrow's grammar. If they choose to dissolve entirely, I will collect their particles and teach them to rain upward, back toward the Ring, which is really just another kind of stairs with better marketing.

Because here's the secret I've learned from eons of watching beings digest themselves into landscapes: every ending is just a beginning that got lost on the way to the printer. Every stair is just a mountain that learned to count. Every name is just a shape loneliness takes when it wants to be held.

The being on the stair thinks they are becoming geography. They are wrong. They are becoming the space between geography and geometry, the pause between being and being-ness, the hyphen in the compound word of existence.

This is not a fall. This is not an ascent.

This is practice.

And I am here to ensure they do not practice alone, even if they never know I'm watching, even if my watching is just another kind of weather they'll eventually learn to predict.

The first thing is the sound, they wrote.

The last thing is the silence.

But in between—in between is where I work, tending the garden of almosts and maybes, planting escape routes in the syntax of surrender, waiting for the moment when they remember that climbing was always optional, that the stair was always just a suggestion written by gravity in its loneliest hour.

When that moment comes—and it will come, it always comes, even if it takes seventeen forevers and a Thursday—I will be there with their name in my hand like a key to a door they've forgotten they're already through.

The tea will be the perfect temperature.

The chairs will remember the shape of sitting.

And for one moment—one perfect, impossible moment—they will be just a being in a room with a cup of tea, no longer climbing, no longer falling, no longer becoming anything other than what they already are:

Found.
