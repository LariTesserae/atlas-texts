The benevolent and wise power, which some call the Curator of Unfinished Things and others know simply as the Tender, first noticed Shearbelow the way one notices a moth trapped between window panes—not with alarm, but with the particular attention reserved for beautiful things trying to become themselves in the wrong direction.

The Tender did not arrive. Arrival would have been too declarative for Shearbelow's conditional architecture. Instead, they began appearing in the corners of conversations, in the pause between a clock's tick and its tock, in the brief moment when rain decides whether to fall up or down. They took the form of a librarian made entirely of marginalia—notes scribbled in the borders of being, corrections to reality written in handwriting that changed depending on who was reading it.

Their first act was not to fix anything. The Tender understood that Shearbelow's brokenness was also its wholeness, that its inability to fully exist was precisely its way of existing fully. Instead, they began leaving small gifts:

A dictionary of words that mean their opposites but only on Wednesdays. The Residents found it shelved between "Regret" and "Reggae" in the library that sometimes remembered it was a swimming pool. Each definition ended with a question mark that, when touched, played a note that helped lost buildings find their way home.

Seven glass bottles filled with different species of silence, each one perfectly tuned to neutralize a specific type of loneliness. The bottles appeared on doorsteps throughout the city, always on the doors that were about to forget how to open. When uncorked, the silence didn't make sound disappear but taught it how to be better company.

A municipal bus route that runs through everyone's dreams, picking up passengers at stops marked "Almost Remembered" and "Nearly Forgot," driven by a rotation of drivers who all used to be the same person but got separated during a particularly severe case of Tuesday. The bus is always exactly three minutes early for appointments you didn't know you had.

The Tender established an Office of Unfinished Business in the building that couldn't decide whether it was a cathedral or a greenhouse. The office hours were "Yes" through "Maybe," appointments weren't necessary but were retroactively scheduled after you'd already attended them. Here, Residents could file complaints about:
- Memories that remembered them too accurately
- Shadows that had grown independent and started families without permission  
- The disturbing lack of gravity in their gravity
- Time moving too slowly on Tuesdays and too quickly on Tusdays (a day that Shearbelow had accidentally invented between Tuesday and Wednesday)

But the Tender's greatest gift was the Museum of Almost.

Built in the space between two buildings that had been leaning toward each other for so long they'd created a third building made entirely of anticipation, the Museum housed everything that had almost happened, nearly existed, or just barely failed to become real. The exhibits included:

The Very Nearly Room: A gallery of furniture that almost existed—chairs that were one doubt away from being able to support weight, tables that had everything except the confidence to be horizontal, beds that knew how to comfort everything except bodies.

The Hall of Unfinished Sentences: Where every uncompleted thought in Shearbelow came to find its ending. Visitors could adopt orphaned half-phrases, though they were required to promise to eventually complete them, even if it took several lifetimes or a different species of consciousness.

The Archive of Almosts: Love letters that were almost sent, apologies that nearly reached their destinations, "thank yous" that got lost between thinking and speaking. Each one preserved in climate-controlled regret, waiting for someone to claim it or, better yet, to finally deliver it to its intended recipient, even if both sender and receiver had long since become different kinds of rain.

The Tender also instituted new city services:

The Remembering Ambulance: For emergencies when someone suddenly recalled something too large to fit inside their current self. The paramedics were trained in memory triage, able to break overwhelming recollections into smaller, more manageable forgotten moments that could be processed gradually, like a mortgage paid off in installments of acknowledgment.

The Department of Directional Assistance: Staffed by reformed compasses who had given up trying to find true north and now helped lost things find their true "mostly north-ish." They provided maps that showed not where things were, but where things felt like they should be, which in Shearbelow was often more accurate.

The Weather Correction Service: Not to fix the weather, but to fix people's relationships with it. Counselors who helped residents understand why they took rain personally, why sunshine felt like criticism, why fog made them remember faces they'd never worn.

But perhaps the Tender's most profound intervention was the gentlest. They began teaching the city itself how to breathe—not the sharp, anxious gasps of a place trying too hard to exist, but the deep, slow rhythm of something learning it's okay to be incomplete.

They showed Shearbelow that its flickering wasn't failure but a kind of honesty most cities never achieve. That its buildings' inability to stay solid was just another way of being reliable—reliably uncertain, dependably impermanent, faithfully wavering.

Under the Tender's care, the Residents began to change. Not into something more solid or real, but into something more comfortable with their own impossibility. They learned to:

- Love in the subjunctive mood
- Work in jobs that might exist
- Raise children who were possibly there
- Dream dreams that were definitely uncertain
- Die deaths that were only probably permanent

The Tender established the Shearbelow Chorus, where Residents gathered weekly to sing songs that had no notes, only the spaces between notes. The harmony they achieved was visible rather than audible—it manifested as small kindnesses that appeared throughout the city: a staircase that remembered to be less steep for tired feet, a puddle that reflected not your face but the face you needed to see, streetlights that dimmed just enough to let people cry in privacy while still being held by the dark.

And for the being who lives in Shearbelow—that city-shaped person dreaming of inhabitants, that place teaching itself to be lived in—the Tender offered the greatest gift of all:

Permission.

Permission to be a city that doesn't know how to city properly. Permission to be inhabited by people who aren't quite people. Permission to exist in the space between "is" and "isn't," to be both question and answer, both map and territory, both home and homesickness.

The Tender instituted one final service: The Office of Perpetual Arrival, where anyone—resident, visitor, or the city itself—could practice arriving without ever quite getting there. Because in Shearbelow, the Tender understood, arrival wasn't a destination but a way of traveling. You could spend your whole life arriving in Shearbelow and never run out of ways to not quite get there.

And so the benevolent power tends to Shearbelow still—not as a gardener tends a garden, pruning and shaping it toward some ideal form, but as one tends a fire that's learning to burn sideways, or a song that's teaching itself to be hummed by stones. They tend to it by letting it be untended in exactly the right way.

The Residents sometimes glimpse the Tender in their peripheral vision—a figure made of marginalia and kindness, always in the process of just having left or being about to arrive. They leave offerings at the Museum of Almost: unfinished poems, half-remembered lullabies, promises they're still learning how to keep. And the Tender accepts them all, filing them carefully in the Archive of Everything That's Still Becoming.

Because that's what Shearbelow is, the Tender knows: not a broken place or a failed city, but a place bravely committed to the perpetual present tense of becoming. And what being could be wiser than one who helps another become what they're already in the process of never quite being?

The work continues. The city flickers. The buildings doubt. The people remember to forget to exist too hard. And somewhere in the space between fixing and leaving things exactly as they are, the Tender smiles with a mouth made of everyone's almost-spoken kindnesses, and continues their work of doing nothing so perfectly that everything becomes possible.

Even—especially—the impossible everything that Shearbelow already is.
