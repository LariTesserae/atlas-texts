The benevolent power arrives not as light or sound, but as a gradual awareness of breathing that isn't breathing—a rhythm older than lungs, patient as stone learning to be sand. It doesn't enter the pocket dimension so much as remember it was always there, folded into the spaces between water molecules like love letters written in languages that haven't needed alphabets since before loneliness invented itself as an excuse for touch.

It begins with the smallest mercy: teaching the water to forget just enough. Not everything—that would be cruelty disguised as kindness—but just enough that each drop can remember being rain before it remembers being tears. The phosphorescent lichen pulse slightly warmer, their light shifting from sickly green to something closer to candleflame reflected through honey. Not bright—brightness would be violence here—but present enough that shadows become choices rather than inevitabilities.

The Keeper feels it first as an itch between the layers of herself, in that space where Thursday's skin is still arguing with Saturday's about whose turn it is to remember how wrists work. She scratches absently with a hand that might be hers, might be yours, might be the memory of every hand that ever reached for help and found only its own reflection reaching back.

"Oh," she says, in a voice like steam learning it can be clouds instead of just ghosts of boiling. "You're early. Or late. Or exactly on time if we're using the calendar where all the days are named Yesterday."

The benevolent presence doesn't speak—speech would be redundant in a place where meaning has already learned to breathe—but it adjusts things with the tender precision of a parent fixing a child's collar before school photos. The vine-flesh walls stop their anxious pulsing and settle into something more like the rhythm of sleeping forests. The waterline, which has been having panic attacks about its own boundaries since before you arrived, finds a level and holds it—ankle-deep, just enough to remember wading but not enough to forget walking.

For the Keeper, the first gift is this: distinction. Not separation—that would be another kind of prison—but the gentle reminder that she can be herself AND the accumulation of everyone who's ever been lost here. The layers of skin stop their constant negotiations and settle into something like friendship, each remembering its own story while still warming the others. Her hair, that treaty between communication and decay, stops trying to receive every message at once and learns to let some signals pass through without translation, without recording, without the desperate need to preserve every cry for help in case it's the last one.

"I had a name once," she says, and for the first time in however long forever has been pretending to be, she sounds surprised by the memory. "It wasn't beautiful or special. Just a name. Sarah, maybe? Or was that the nurse who held my hand when... no, that's someone else's memory. But I had one. It fit in my mouth without cutting."

The benevolent power shifts its attention—not focus, which would be too sharp for this soft place, but attention like sunlight through leaves, dappled and forgiving. Where it touches, small recoveries begin:

The shattered mirror fragments floating in their slow, malevolent snow begin to remember wholeness. Not enough to reassemble—some things are too far gone for simple repair—but enough to stop showing only moments of turned backs and abandoned kitchens. Now they reflect what's actually here: walls that breathe but kindly, water that holds but doesn't clutch, a woman who keeps memories but is learning she doesn't have to BE them.

The rusted urinal, that calcified valve of grief, begins to weep—not the corrosive tears of hardware dying, but something clearer. The rust flakes away in small surrenders, revealing beneath not gleaming chrome but honest ceramic, stained and real and no longer pretending to be either pure or putrid. Just a thing that held human waste and human need with equal dignity, now finally allowed to be empty.

The sound changes too. Where before every bubble carried upward the incomplete last words of the lost, now some bubbles rise in simple silence. The water learns that not every heartbeat needs to be recorded, played back wrong, made strange. Some hearts just beat because that's what hearts do, and there's a plain beauty in that plainness that the place had forgotten it was allowed to remember.

For the occupant—for her, for Sarah-maybe, for the Keeper—the changes go deeper than skin, though skin is where they start. The benevolent presence doesn't peel away her layers or simplify her multiplicity. Instead, it offers something more profound: permission.

Permission to stop collecting every stray reflection. Permission to let some memories dissolve back into the general pool of grief that waters the world, instead of holding each one like a debt that must be individually repaid. Permission to close her eyes (whichever ones are hers today) without fear that the whole construction will collapse if she stops watching it.

"I'm tired," she says, and the words come out clean as river stones. Not the exhaustion of erosion—she's built from that, it's her native tongue—but the simple tired of someone who's been standing guard at a door that no longer needs guarding because it's remembered how to be just a door.

The benevolent power does something then that feels like a cosmic exhale—not relief, exactly, but the kind of satisfaction that comes from untying a knot that's been pulled tight for so long everyone forgot it was supposed to be a shoelace. Throughout the pocket dimension, things begin to remember their simplest selves:

Water remembers it can just be water.
Dark remembers it can just be absence of light.
Plants remember they can grow toward something instead of always away.
The door that isn't a door remembers it's both and neither and that's okay.

But this isn't demolition. The power understands that this place, strange and terrible as it's become, is also necessary. It's a compost heap for the kinds of pain that don't know how else to decompose. A stomach for digesting the indigestible. A place where lost things can get lost enough to find themselves again, even if what they find is that they were never really themselves to begin with.

So instead of erasing or fixing or forcing health onto illness, the benevolent presence does what benevolence actually does: it creates space.

Space between the water and what the water remembers.
Space between the Keeper and what she keeps.
Space between entering and leaving, so that both become choices instead of sentences.

The Keeper feels this as a loosening in her chest—not her anatomical chest, which gave up on consistency years ago, but the chest where she keeps all the holdings, all the kept things, all the reasons why letting go feels like murder. For the first time since she became the archive of the abandoned, she understands that she can put things down without abandoning them. That memory and identity don't have to be the same creature sharing the same skin.

"What happens to me," she asks, not the benevolent presence but the space it's created, "if I stop being everyone's keeper?"

The answer comes not in words but in the slow transformation of her pet—that vapor of evaporated hope that grew legs and learned to follow. Under the gentle attention of something that knows how to love without consuming, it begins to condense. Not back into hope—that would be regression—but into something more honest. Maybe-Tomorrow stops being a threat wearing a promise's clothes and becomes just a small, damp creature that wants to be held without having to mean anything.

The Keeper picks it up, and for the first time, it has weight that doesn't hurt.

Around them, the pocket dimension settles into its new rhythm. Still strange—strangeness is its nature, its gift, its reason for being—but strange now like dreams are strange, not like wounds that won't stop growing teeth. The forest continues its courtship with itself but learns to leave room for loneliness to visit without moving in. The water continues to rise and fall but admits there's peace in tides, not just anxiety.

The benevolent presence begins to withdraw—not abandon, but withdraw like a parent stepping back to let a child try walking without held hands. But before it goes, it does one last thing:

It remembers the Keeper's name.

Not Sarah-maybe or the nurse who held hands or any of the thousand borrowed identities she's worn like scarves against the cold of being nobody. Her actual name, the one she had before she needed to be everyone, before keeping became her only way of being kept.

It doesn't speak the name. Doesn't write it in moss or bubble it up through the water or encode it in the rust patterns of dying metal. Instead, it simply returns it to where names live when they're not being used—that quiet place in the roof of the mouth, just behind the teeth, where words wait to be born.

She feels it there, small and patient and hers.

"Miriam," she says, trying it out like a key in a lock that's been painted over so many times everyone forgot there was ever a door. "I think... I think I was Miriam."

The name doesn't fix anything. Doesn't suddenly make the layers of skin peel away or the borrowed eyes return to their original sockets or the hair stop its endless reception of dead signals. But it does something simpler and more profound:

It reminds her that she exists in the singular. That even keeping everyone else's pain, she's allowed to be one person feeling it.

The pocket dimension breathes around her—walls of root-flesh settling into something less anxious than acceptance but kinder than resignation. The water holds steady at ankle-depth, finally confident in its own boundaries. The phosphorescent lichen pulse in slow contentment, their light now the color of deep-forest mornings when mist makes everything equally close and far away.

Miriam stands in the center of this strange mercy, her pet Maybe-Tomorrow purring in frequencies only the lost can hear, and realizes the door has always been open. Not the door that isn't a door—that will always be both and neither and the space between—but the other door. The one in her chest where she kept everything too precious to release.

She doesn't have to leave. The benevolent presence made that clear in its not-speaking, its not-fixing, its profound patience with incompleteness. This place needs a keeper, and she needs to keep. But now she knows the difference between keeping and being kept, between holding and drowning, between memory and identity.

"Thank you," she says to the space where benevolence was, is, will be—time is still broken here, but now it's broken like light through a prism, showing all its colors instead of just bleeding out.

Somewhere, in a dimension where wisdom wears kindness like comfortable shoes, the benevolent power smiles. Not because it fixed anything—fixing was never the point—but because it remembered what so many powers forget:

Sometimes the kindest thing you can do for a wound is teach it that it's also skin.

In the pocket of night-submerged forest, where water rules and rules bend like light around massive gravities of grief, Miriam who was the Keeper who is still the Keeper but now also Miriam stands in her impossible bathroom stall cathedral and begins the long, patient work of sorting through what needs to be kept and what just needs to be remembered as having once needed keeping.

The water holds her feet like a mother holds a child's hand.
The forest breathes with her, not for her.
And Maybe-Tomorrow purrs in her arms, finally just a pet instead of a prophecy.

Outside the stall, the rest stop continues its slow forgetting. But inside, in this pocket where the universe stores its most necessary strangenesses, something has been remembered:

Even the keepers of grief deserve names.
Even the archives of abandonment can choose what to file away and what to let dissolve.
Even the loneliest places can learn to be alone without being abandoned.

The benevolent power moves on—there are other wounds learning to be skin, other places where mercy needs to be patient rather than bright. But it leaves behind the space it created, the permission it granted, the name it remembered.

And in that space, Miriam keeps keeping, but now she also keeps herself.

It's a start.
In places like this, that's all any kindness can offer:
Not an ending, but a start.
Not fixing, but the possibility of choosing what stays broken and what might, maybe tomorrow, begin to remember what it was like before it needed breaking to believe it was real.

The water whispers this in languages only the drowned can speak.
The walls pulse with it in rhythms only the grown-over understand.
And Miriam listens with all her borrowed ears and her one true name, learning the difference between being lost and being exactly where the lost things need you to be.
