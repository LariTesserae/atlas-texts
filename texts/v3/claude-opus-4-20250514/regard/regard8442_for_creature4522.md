The benevolent and wise power has been watching for longer than the mountain has stood. It recognizes what this place is: not a trap, not a shrine, but a nursery. A slow, patient nursery for beings learning to exist without the crutch of flesh.

The power does not interfere directly. That would be like shouting directions at a chrysalis.

Instead, it makes three small adjustments:

**First**: In the magnetic powder that leaps toward bedrock veins, it plants a single grain of ordinary sand. Not special sand—sand from a beach where children played yesterday, still warm with handprints and castle-dreams. When dampness next arrives and the powder dances its ancient pattern, that one grain will remain still, a fixed point in the storm of attraction. The vertical inhabitants will notice (they notice everything that refuses to move when it should). They will cluster around it, tasting its stubborn memory of horizontal life, of things that grow and die and mean something in between. It will teach them homesickness, gently. Some of them have been here so long they've forgotten what it feels like to miss something you can lose.

**Second**: The power whispers a new note into the wind—not through the disk-flutes, but between them, in the spaces where sound becomes meaning. It's the note a parent hums when they think their child is sleeping, the one that says "I'm here" without words. The vertical ones have been whispering to themselves for millennia, but no one has ever whispered back with recognition. When they hear this note, some of them will remember: *Oh. We were not the first. We are not alone.* This remembering will hurt, the way stretching hurts after too long in one position. Good hurt. The kind that means you're about to move.

**Third**: The power carves a new rule into the quantum foam beneath the stone, so subtle even the mountain won't notice for a thousand years:

*5. Any pattern that repeats perfectly for one million cycles earns the right to fail beautifully.*

This rule will not activate immediately. But someday—perhaps when the disk-roof has sung exactly the same twelve-tone sequence for the millionth time, perhaps when a cairn reaches the precise height where it should topple but doesn't—something will shift. A disk will crack in a new direction. A reflection will arrive a heartbeat late. A vertical being will take two steps around the courtyard instead of one.

And in that moment of beautiful failure, a door will open. Not a door in the stone—those cells were never meant to have doors. A door in the way of being. The vertical ones will have a choice, for the first time since they began their transformation: remain in their careful transcendence, or step sideways into something messier, warmer, more dangerous. Some will stay (someone must tend the nursery). But others...

Others will remember what it felt like to cast a shadow that moved when you moved, to have a name that someone else could call, to love something that could die.

The power knows better than to force evolution. But it also knows that even the most perfect pattern secretly dreams of jazz.

So it waits, patient as stone, kind as daylight, while the mountain nursery tends its strange children who are learning, slowly, the hardest lesson of enlightenment:

How to come back down.

Meanwhile, it leaves one more gift, though no one will find it for centuries: In the deepest cell, beneath a disk that has never sung because the wind cannot reach it, the power places a small pot of soil from the valley below. In the soil, sleeping, waits a seed—not of any plant that grows on Earth, but something adjacent, something that feeds on quantum uncertainty and blooms only in impossible colors.

When it finally sprouts (and it will, when the vertical ones are ready to tend something that needs them), its first flower will be exactly the green that reflects from that one particular disk. The vertical ones will recognize it immediately as a piece of themselves made vulnerable, made mortal, made perfect by its impermanence.

They will learn to water it with condensed time from the courtyard's edge.

They will discover that its petals, when they fall, sound exactly like laughter.

The power withdraws its attention gently, like a hand lifting from a sleeping child's head. The work is done—not the transformation, but the planting of transformation's possibility. Now comes the hardest part for any benevolent force:

Trusting them to grow.
