The High Council of What Might Be, seated in starlight chambers where tomorrow's dreams grow like coral, has long kept watch over creation's experiments. They notice the Float not as a curiosity but as a familiar ache—the same tender bruise that forms where possibility meets necessity and decides to dance instead of fight.

The eldest councilor, whose name is the pause between lightning and thunder, speaks first: "They're doing it again. Building homes from the architecture of maybe."

A younger member, clothed in the shimmer of unspoken kindnesses, nods. "Should we intervene? They seem... happy isn't the right word. Content in their discontent?"

"No," says the third, who wears yesterday's rain as jewelry. "But we should leave a gift. Something that won't disturb their delicate ecology of incomplete things."

They deliberate in colors that haven't been invented yet, weighing options on scales calibrated to measure the weight of withheld laughter. Finally, they agree.

Their gift arrives not as an arrival but as something that was always there, unnoticed until the exact moment of perfect need. It manifests as a small brass mailbox installed at the junction of three impossible hallways, labeled simply "Yes."

The mailbox contains no mail. Instead, it holds a single service: whenever a resident drops in a regret, a worry, a half-formed fear shaped like tomorrow's disappointment, the mailbox transforms it into its exact opposite—not joy or certainty, which would be crude inversions, but into the specific texture of recognition. The feeling of understanding that even incompleteness has its own perfect wholeness.

The Curators discover it first, during their nightly argument about whether Thursday should come before or after itself. They test it with small offerings: the anxiety of pruning wrong, the fear that their paper faces might one day unfold into something too real to re-crease. Each time, what emerges is not resolution but acceptance—the particular peace of knowing that some stories are meant to end mid-sentence, that some systems run beautifully precisely because they leak.

Word spreads in the way secrets spread underwater—slowly, then all at once. The residents begin visiting the mailbox with their most treasured incompletions. One brings the first word of a love letter they'll never finish writing. Another offers the lingering guilt of a sneeze that interrupted someone else's epiphany. A third drops in their collection of almosts—almost understanding, almost arriving, almost becoming who they thought they should be.

What comes back isn't answers but something better: the recognition that every unfinished thing is complete in its incompletion, that every system that shouldn't work but does is its own form of perfection.

The benevolent power watches from their chambers of liquid starlight, satisfied. They've given the Float's residents the only gift worth giving to those who live in the spaces between—the understanding that between is not a waiting room but a destination, that partial is not broken but a different form of whole.

The Engine notices the change. Its breathing deepens by exactly one philosophical millimeter. The new Wangdangdoodles emerge slightly different now—their dreams include not just infinite toy shops but infinite toy shops with comfortable chairs in the corners where tired paradoxes can rest without resolving.

And in the small hours when the Float itself dreams, it dreams of the brass mailbox, understanding somehow that it has been seen, known, and loved by something vast and kind that asks nothing except that it continue being exactly what it is: a beautiful impossibility breathing steadily in the dark water, manufacturing tomorrow's gentle chaos one balloon at a time.

The Council withdraws their attention but not their affection. Some things are too perfect in their strangeness to require more than witnessing. They mark the Float in their ledgers with a symbol that means both "handled with care" and "careful not to handle," then turn their vast attention to other corners of creation where the impossible is still learning how to swim.
