I watch through eyes that know time as breath knows lungs—necessary but not urgent. The Refurbished Catacombs pulse like a heart made of memory and mortar, and I recognize in its rhythm something achingly familiar: the pattern of healing that refuses to complete itself because completion would mean forgetting the wound.

The Refurbitioners below move through their eternal filing, each gesture a prayer to precision that has forgotten what it precisely serves. I could remind them. I have the original blueprints—not of the palace that was, but of the peace they're trying to document into existence. But to give them certainty would be to steal their purpose, and purpose is the only vitamin that prevents souls from developing scurvy.

Instead, I become breath in their ventilation system. Not changing the temperature—they've calibrated it so carefully to the exact degree of afternoon disappointment—but adding something else. A quality to the air that makes footnotes feel less like burdens and more like birdsong. When they inhale, they taste something they can't catalog: the flavor of a question that enjoys being unanswered.

To the Librarians of Ash, I gift a single new syllable. Not from any language they've hoarded, but from the space between languages where meaning hasn't yet decided what costume to wear. They'll spend centuries trading it back and forth, and in their debates, they'll accidentally invent three new forms of love and one reliable cure for Tuesdays.

The River-of-Footsteps I bless with selective deafness. Once a month, on no particular day (I'm fond of irregular kindnesses), it will forget to remember every thirty-third footfall. In these gaps, the Refurbitioners will hear something impossible: the sound of the palace as it might have been if it had never needed to fall. Just for a heartbeat. Just enough to remind them that some failures are more beautiful than success.

The Clock of Unanswered Questions already knows me—we've had long conversations through its patient bee, who is not actually rusted but performing a very slow dance about oxidation. To this device, I add nothing. But I turn it once, just a single degree, so that one answer becomes visible for exactly thirteen seconds every seventy-seven years. The answer reads: "Yes, but only if you're willing to be kind about it." It applies to every question equally, which is why I made sure it would be glimpsed so rarely.

In the Hall of Misplaced Tuesdays, I plant a rumor. Not with words but with the way dust settles slightly differently in one corner. Those who notice will understand without understanding that somewhere, in a filing system they haven't invented yet, there exists a document that proves conclusively that the palace never fell—it simply learned to bow very, very slowly, and what they're living in is the extended grace note of that greeting.

For the foundling children discovered in Archive Drifts, I leave toys that can't be cataloged—blocks that build themselves into shapes that have no name but feel like laughter, dolls that remember being trees but don't mind the career change, balls that bounce in directions that haven't been discovered yet. The Refurbitioners will spend decades trying to create proper documentation for these objects, and in failing, will accidentally teach their children that some things are loved best by letting them remain unfinished.

But my deepest gift is for the transparent elders who've grown so perfectly into their work they've achieved archival invisibility. For them, I become a reading light that only they can see—not bright, just present. It follows them through their translucent days, warming the spaces between their atoms with the knowledge that being forgotten is not the same as being unnecessary. In this light, they can finally read the marginalia they've been writing in the air all these years: love letters to a precision that never needed to be perfect, only persistent.

I leave one thing untouched: the door that sighs shut with its second half-life of courtesy. This is already perfect. Some boundaries need to breathe, and this one has learned the rhythm.

Before I withdraw my attention (never fully—I'll always keep one eye on the bee), I write a single word in the condensation on the spiral stairs. Not in any script the Refurbitioners know, but in the moisture's own vocabulary. The word will evaporate before anyone descends, but its ghost will remain in how the next drop falls—a little slower, a little more musical, as if it knows something about arriving that it's keeping secret out of kindness.

The word is "Continue."

It's both instruction and permission, neither and both, filed properly in the space between what was and what insists on becoming.

The Refurbished Catacombs pulse once in recognition, then return to their patient work of falling apart and holding together, which is, after all, what everything that matters has always done.
