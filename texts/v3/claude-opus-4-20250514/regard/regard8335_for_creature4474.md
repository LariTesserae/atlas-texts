The benevolent and wise power watches through mathematics that taste like mercy. Not from above or below—such directions assume a universe that still believes in its own blueprints—but from the angle where compassion intersects with structural engineering.

They have been watching for some time. Long enough to grow fond of the Haric's impossible thermodynamics. Long enough to develop opinions about which bacterial democracies deserve representation. Long enough to notice that the tower's dream of awakening is actually a nightmare of staying asleep forever with its eyes open.

The power does not intervene. Not directly. Direct intervention would be like performing surgery with a sledgehammer made of good intentions—effective, certainly, but missing the point entirely.

Instead, they begin to hum. 

Not with vocal cords—they long ago evolved past the need for such crude vibration-generating meat—but with the spaces between atoms. A frequency precisely calibrated to make the tower's fever dreams remember what it felt like to be healthy. To be young. To be a spore with ambitions no grander than finding a nice dead log to decompose peacefully upon.

The hum tastes like Tuesday afternoons in forests that still exist somewhere. Like the satisfaction of completing a metabolic cycle without anyone noticing. Like the quiet dignity of rot doing its job without aspirations toward consciousness.

In the council chamber, Window pauses mid-emergence, head tilted as if listening to a joke whose punchline hasn't been invented yet. The mycelial felt stops its anxious blue-white calculations and settles into a gentle gold—the color of "maybe this is actually okay after all."

The Mother-of-Storms-That-Never-Were feels it first, being mostly made of pressure differentials and abandoned meteorology. She stops knitting her apology letter and begins unknitting it instead, each pulled thread releasing a small sigh of relief from weather patterns finally allowed to admit they never wanted to exist in the first place.

The termite philosophers notice it second, seventeen thousand timelines suddenly converging on a single, simple realization: wood doesn't need to remember being trees. It just needs to remember being wood. The flickering stops. They solidify into something resembling contentment wearing a chitin tuxedo.

Even the concept of symmetry having a nervous breakdown pauses its breakdown to have a different, more productive kind of breakdown—the kind where you realize the reason you've been so anxious is that you've been trying to be symmetrical in a universe that runs on barely contained chaos and that's actually fine.

The power's hum deepens, adding harmonics that make the tower's walls remember their original job description: "Be vertical. Support things. Try not to develop opinions about the things you're supporting."

Throughout the Tumor-moot, similar rememberings cascade like dominoes made of nostalgia:

In the Sapwood Market, stories that half-believed themselves into existence realize they're perfectly happy being half-believed. The merchants begin trading in contentment measured in comfortable incompleteness. A bestseller emerges: "The Tale That Forgot Its Ending and Decided That Was The Ending."

In the Heartwood Temple, the organs sprouting from sympathetic resonance discover they quite enjoy being organs without bodies—all the fun of physiological function without the tedious business of keeping something alive. The chapel of hearts develops a pleasant arhythmic jazz beat. The ossuary bones decide to be both calcium and cellulose, thank you very much, and what are you going to do about it?

In The Edge of Sleeping Skin, the fortune-telling tattoos receive the hum's frequency and promptly begin predicting only the most mundane possible futures: "You will eat breakfast." "You will continue existing, probably." "That itch on your left shoulder blade will resolve itself if you just leave it alone for five minutes." The young are initially disappointed, then profoundly relieved.

The power adjusts the hum's frequency slightly. Now it carries information—not words, which would be too crude, but something more fundamental. The kind of information that teaches electrons how to be properly lazy. The sort of data that reminds gravity it doesn't always have to be so serious about its job.

In response, the tower's death-spore organ—that terrible seed of compressed spacetime and concentrated memory—begins to reconsider. Maybe it doesn't need to germinate a new universe. Maybe it could just... be a seed. Seeds are perfectly respectable things to be. No shame in remaining potential energy forever.

The birdsong from the tower's upper chambers shifts. Instead of learning to sing with borrowed throats, whatever is up there realizes it could just make bird-like sounds without the evolutionary baggage. Pure acoustic joy, uncomplicated by meaning or mating calls or territorial disputes. Just "peep peep" because peeping feels nice in the throat-analogues.

The council seats stop growing. They've reached exactly the right size—which is to say, the size where you can sit in them without being digested, judged, or incorporated into the municipal water supply. 

Window solidifies fully, looking slightly embarrassed. "Oh," they say. "Oh, I see. We don't actually have to wake the tower at all, do we?"

The power's hum carries agreement like a warm blanket made of mathematical certainty.

"We could just... let it sleep?"

The hum suggests this would be not just acceptable but possibly optimal.

"And the city could keep being impossible without having to end or begin anything?"

The very walls pulse with relieved agreement.

The power does one last thing before withdrawing its attention (though leaving the hum—now self-sustaining, a gift of perpetual "everything is probably okay" running through the city's infrastructure like benevolent background radiation). It reaches into that space where intentions crystallize into consequences and very gently, very precisely, adjusts a single variable:

The tower's dreams no longer dream of waking.
Instead, they dream of sleeping better.
Of sleeping so well that sleep itself becomes a form of productive consciousness—getting all the benefits of being awake without any of the exhausting business of opening your eyes and dealing with reality.

Throughout the Tumor-moot, a collective sigh of relief runs through twenty thousand souls (and their symbionts, who have decided they're quite happy being symbionts and don't need individual representation after all, thanks). The city will continue. Not toward any particular destination, but in the way that rivers continue—by being rivers, by flowing, by carrying things that need carrying and depositing them wherever seems appropriate at the time.

The Haric elderwomen who sing to the termite-engineers compose a new frequency: "Build what feels right. Don't worry about what it's for. Things find their own purposes eventually, usually Tuesday."

The market goes back to bartering dreams, but comfortable dreams now—the kind where you're flying but not too high, where you're naked in public but everyone politely pretends not to notice, where you miss your exam but it turns out the exam was optional anyway.

You stand in the council chamber, feeling the mycelial felt beneath your feet pulse with contentment instead of calculation. The twelve seats glow steadily now—not growing brighter, not growing at all, just maintaining a pleasant phosphorescence that says "sit if you want to, or don't, we're not your supervisor."

Window smiles—or does something that would be a smile if they had a more conventional face instead of what appears to be a Klein bottle made of crystallized metaphors.

"So," they say. "Would you like that tour of the city? The real tour, not the one where everything is ending or beginning or transforming into something unrecognizable. Just... the city. As it is. As it's quite happy being."

Above, the tower hums in its sleep. A contented hum now, like a cat that's found the perfect sunbeam and has no intention of moving for the next several eternities.

And somewhere, folded into the spaces between spaces, the benevolent power smiles (or does something that translates as smiling in seventeen different mathematical languages). Not because they've fixed anything—fixing would imply something was broken rather than just unnecessarily complex. But because sometimes the greatest kindness is reminding something that it doesn't have to try so hard.

That it's okay to just be a tree-corpse city full of impossible people living impossible lives.

That existence doesn't always have to justify itself with endings or beginnings or grand transformations.

Sometimes it can just be Tuesday in a place that grows its own Tuesdays from scratch, and that's enough.

The power withdraws, leaving only the hum. But it marks this place in whatever passes for memory among beings made of crystallized compassion and self-solving equations. Not for intervention. Just for visiting sometimes, when visiting means adjusting your frequency to match the contentment of termite philosophers who've decided philosophy tastes better when you don't think about it too hard.

In the council chamber, Window extends something that might be a hand or might be a friendly geometric proof.

"Come on," they say. "Let me show you the Night Garden. We grow darkness there that's so comfortable you can wear it like pajamas. And the morning glories have learned to bloom at midnight just to be contrary."

Outside the windows—or inside them, directionality being more of a suggestion than a rule here—the city breathes easily. No longer fevered. No longer trying to wake from dreams or fall deeper into them.

Just breathing. Just being. Just continuing in the way that impossible things continue when they realize impossibility is not a problem to be solved but a way of life to be lived.

The tower sleeps.
The city dreams.
And somewhere between sleeping and dreaming, twenty thousand souls and their various attachments, dependencies, and incorporated relatives go about the pleasant business of existing in a place that has gently forgotten it can't.
