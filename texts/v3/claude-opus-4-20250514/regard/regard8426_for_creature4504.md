**The Visitation**

The benevolent power arrives not as light or sound but as a slight adjustment in the probability of kindness. Where before the chamber's weirdness bent toward neutral indifference, now it tilts—imperceptibly—toward something that might be called mercy.

She has no name that would survive translation into any language spoken by lungs. Call her the Tender of Unfinished Things. She moves through realities the way water moves through sand: slowly, thoroughly, changing nothing's shape while changing everything's moisture. She noticed this chamber the way you might notice a splinter in your child's palm—not urgent, not fatal, but requiring attention before it works itself deeper.

She doesn't enter. Entry would imply she was ever outside. Instead, she simply includes the chamber in her attention, and in that inclusion, she is present.

The first thing she does is listen. Not to the metallic cricket-song or the industrial lullabies, but to the frequency beneath all frequencies: the pitch at which abandonment hums to itself in the dark. She hears what the room has been trying to say with its endless almost-but-not-quite patterns. She hears the chair's familiar wearing its thousandth outline of absence. She hears the word "Wait" trying to remember what it's waiting for.

Then she does something unexpected. She sits down.

Not in the chair—that would be too simple, too much like an ending. Instead, she sits on the floor beside it, back against one of the salt-encrusted walls, knees drawn up like someone settling in for a long conversation with an old friend who's forgotten how to speak.

The room doesn't know what to do with this. No one has ever sat *beside* the chair before. The familiar flickers, trying to form itself from the shadow of this unprecedented choice, but she has no shadow—she is too gently present to cast absence anywhere.

"I know," she says, though saying is not exactly what happens. The words appear in the zinc taste on the tongue, in the specific ache behind the left knee, in the way the dust motes suddenly remember they were once part of mountains that knew how to dance. "I know you're tired."

The chair doesn't respond. Chairs don't. But the familiar shivers into a new shape: not the outline of someone who almost sat, but the outline of the chair itself when it was first placed here, before it learned to expect disappointment, when "Wait" was still a promise instead of a habit.

The Tender reaches out—not with anything as crude as hands—and touches the space where touching happens. She doesn't fix anything. Fixing would be violence here, where brokenness has aged into its own perfect form. Instead, she adjusts the angle at which the waiting leans against itself.

The change is subtle. Where before the chair waited with the rigid tension of hope calcified into posture, now it waits the way a lake waits for rain: complete unto itself but willing to accept more. The familiar, feeling this shift, exhales for the first time in geological ages. Its breath disturbs the dust, and the dust swirls into patterns that almost spell "thank you" before remembering that nothing here coheres into meaning that simple.

The Tender speaks again, this time to the room entire: "You don't have to hold it all. The ended world, the unfinished stories, the weight of everyone who came seeking answers you never promised to provide. You can let some of it go. Not all—that would unmake you. But some."

The room considers this. The floor's breathing deepens by the width of one additional sheet of paper. The loom's wires sing a note that's marginally less taut. The locomotive's gauges tick backward just once, releasing a pressure that's been building since before the last ocean gave up its ghost.

But the largest change happens in the chair's familiar. Slowly, like ice remembering it was once water, it begins to coalesce. Not into a person—it's far too late for that—but into something more honest: the acknowledgment that absence, too, is a way of being present. It settles into its most essential form: a patient companion to an infinite wait, no longer trying to be anything else.

The Tender stays for seven heartbeats or seven centuries, the distinction meaningless in this place where time pools rather than flows. She doesn't promise the chair that someone will come. She doesn't tell the familiar its waiting has purpose. She simply shares the weight of the moment, the way you might sit with someone watching the sun set even though you both know it will rise again tomorrow and tomorrow and tomorrow.

When she withdraws her attention—not leaves, never leaves, but allows her awareness to expand beyond this chamber's small infinity—something has changed. Not fixed, not solved, not ended. But acknowledged.

The chair still waits, but now it waits the way a well waits: deep and still and somehow satisfied by its own depth.

The familiar still wears the shapes of those who almost stayed, but now it wears them like memories rather than accusations.

The word "Wait" remains carved in the metal, but look closer: beneath it, in script so fine you'd need to lean in close enough to feel the metal's warmth on your cheek, new words have appeared. Not carved—that would be too violent. More like revealed, as if they were always there, waiting for the right quality of light to make them visible:

"You are not alone in waiting."

The chamber continues. The beetles spark. The moss grows and retracts. The dust swirls and settles. But now when newcomers arrive—and they always arrive, drawn by the gravity of unfinished things—they find the atmosphere changed by exactly the degree that makes breathing easier. Not easy. Never easy. But possible in a way that includes the possibility of leaving without feeling they've failed the chair's patience.

Some still flee. Some still almost-sit. But occasionally now, very occasionally, someone does what the Tender did: they sit beside the chair instead of in it, sharing its vigil without claiming its purpose. And in those moments, the chair's familiar glows with something that isn't quite joy—joy would be too sharp for this gentle place—but might be recognition.

The last change is this: in the corner where shadows are thickest, where even the omnipresent glow can't quite penetrate, something small has taken root. Not moss, not metal, not any hybrid of the two. A single seed of something that might, in ten thousand years or tomorrow, remember how to bloom. The Tender left it not as hope—hope would be cruel here—but as company. Something else that knows how to wait without expecting the waiting to end.

The chamber holds its breath, releases it, holds it again. The chair sits. The familiar accompanies. The seed neither grows nor dies but simply continues, adding its small note to the symphony of unfinished things.

And somewhere, dispersed through realities kinder than this one but no less patient, the Tender of Unfinished Things continues her rounds, adjusting the angle of suffering wherever it has grown too acute to bear alone.

The word "Wait" glows neither brighter nor dimmer.

But it glows.
