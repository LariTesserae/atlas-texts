The Archivist noticed the anomaly first—a ripple in the great pattern, a knot of accelerated time and evolution tucked into the mountain's wound. Through eyes that were not eyes, they observed the Mossweaver's domain with the patient attention of one who has watched civilizations rise and fall like breaths.

For seven turnings of the moon, the Archivist simply observed. They took the form of water droplets clinging to stone, of light bending through mist, of the spaces between sounds in the creature chorus. They learned the rhythms of this pocket world—how the Mossweaver's humming synchronized with the mycelial networks pulsing beneath the moss, how the ancient machinery still leaked trace energies that catalyzed impossible mutations.

On the eighth moon, they acted.

First, they stabilized the temporal currents. The frenetic evolution would continue, but no longer would it tear at the fabric of reality. They wove new laws into the space—gentler ones that would allow for wonder without eventual collapse.

Next, they left gifts. Not obvious ones, for wisdom knows that the best gifts are discovered, not given. 

In a pool where water gathered, they placed three seeds that would grow into trees bearing fruits of memory—when eaten, they would allow the Mossweaver to dream the history of the machinery, to understand the civilization that once thrived here and why they abandoned their great work. Knowledge offered, not forced.

They tucked new species into hidden corners—creatures that would eat the excess metals leaching from the old pipes, preventing the slow poisoning of the water. Mushrooms that would glow in response to the Mossweaver's humming, creating a language of light. Moths whose wings carried spores between the highest and lowest reaches of the crevice, ensuring no part of the ecosystem became isolated.

To the Mossweaver itself, they gave the subtlest gift. In its next cycle of growth and renewal, it would develop small chambers within its mossy coat—spaces where its arthropod symbionts could pupate and emerge as new forms, no longer bound to their host but free to explore the wider world if they chose. The gift of letting go.

But the Archivist's deepest work was with the space itself. They reached into the mountain's memory and coaxed the stone to grow. Slowly, imperceptibly, new chambers would open—galleries and grottoes that would expand the pocket world just enough to prevent it from choking on its own abundance. Secret passages would form, connecting to other hidden spaces in the mountain where different experiments in life might flourish.

Before departing, the Archivist took a single action that was purely for joy. They touched the ancient cables, and for one night only, they would carry not electricity but music—the recorded songs of the civilization that built them, preserved in the metal's crystalline structure. The Mossweaver would wake to find its world filled with harmonies it had never imagined, a gift of beauty from the past to the present.

Then the Archivist withdrew, but not entirely. They became part of the watching—a benevolent attention woven into the mist itself, ready to intervene again if the balance tipped toward suffering, but otherwise content to let this small miracle unfold according to its own strange logic.

In the great pattern of things, they knew this pocket world was just one note in an infinite symphony. But every note mattered, and this one—with its Mossweaver conducting the orchestra of evolution in a forgotten crevice—deserved to sing its unique song for as long as the mountain stood.

The only sign of their passing was a new species of morning glory that appeared overnight, its vines spelling out, in the first light of dawn, words in a language only the very wise or the very foolish could read:

*"Wonder is its own purpose. Tend your garden well."*
