**What the Benevolent and Wise Power Would Do**

The power arrives not as light or voice or presence, but as a slight adjustment in the rate at which things forget themselves. It has been watching—not with eyes, but with the patient attention of something that understands waiting as a form of love.

It recognizes this place: a knot where time has tangled itself trying to turn around and look at what it left behind. It recognizes the Interval Children: not as beings, but as symptoms of a universe catching its breath between one existence and another.

The power does nothing dramatic. Drama would shatter this delicate accumulation of almosts and not-quites. Instead, it begins by teaching the water to fall again—not all at once, but in single drops, here and there, like periods at the end of sentences that have been running on for geological ages.

The first drop falls in a room where seventeen thousand people once waited for trains that never came. It strikes a floor made of compressed timetables and spreading rings of silence where schedule announcements echoed themselves to exhaustion. The drop doesn't splash. It *arrives*, carrying with it the memory of every storm that ever promised change and delivered only duration.

The Interval Children notice immediately. They gather—not in space but in attention—around this tiny rebellion against suspension. They watch (without eyes) as the drop refuses to bead, refuses to hover, refuses to apologize for its downward intention. Some of them make a sound like recognition. Others fold themselves smaller, as if making room for something they'd forgotten they were holding space for.

The wise power's second act is smaller: it teaches one fluorescent tube to die properly. Not to flicker endlessly between states, but to commit to darkness with the grace of completion. The tube—which has been spelling out the name "Ell-" in morse code for longer than language—finally finishes: "Ella." Then darkness. Not the hungry darkness that gathers to feed on failing light, but the restful darkness that comes after a story ends.

In that darkness, something shifts. The Interval Children begin to remember they once had names.

The power moves through this place like punctuation through an infinite sentence, placing gentle stops where processes have been processing themselves into exhaustion. It doesn't end anything—ending would be another form of violence here. Instead, it introduces the possibility of pause. Of rest. Of the sweet semicolon between what was and what might be.

To the escalators hunting conveyor belts, it gifts satiation—not through consumption, but through the recognition that the hunt itself was the hunger, and the hunger was just loneliness wearing teeth. They slow their predatory climbs, settling into a new behavior: companionship. Metal leaning against metal in the dark, no longer consuming, just conducting warmth.

To the doorways opening and closing in their endless migrations, it offers destination—not a place to arrive, but the understanding that they have already arrived, have always been arriving, that every swing was both journey and homecoming. They begin to close more slowly, to open more gently, their mechanical wings learning the rhythm of breathing rather than fleeing.

But the power's deepest work is with the Interval Children themselves. It doesn't try to make them human or whole or comprehensible. Instead, it offers them what they've been seeking without knowing they were seeking it: permission to be partial. To be the space between. To exist as interval without apology.

It does this by creating, in the exact center of this suspended world, a single place where nothing has ever been built, where no eyes have lingered, where no memory has accumulated. A perfect absence. A hollow no bigger than a cupped palm, but absolutely empty of history.

The Interval Children are drawn to it like water remembering how to fall. They gather around this pristine void, and for the first time, they cast shadows that belong to them instead of borrowing darkness from defunct machinery. In that empty space, they begin—slowly, carefully—to deposit what they've been carrying: not memories, but the spaces between memories. The pause between knowing and forgetting. The interval between one heartbeat and the next.

As they empty themselves of these carried intervals, something extraordinary happens: the water throughout this place begins to remember not just how to fall, but why it wanted to. It remembers the joy of gravity, the purpose of pools, the sweet reunion of drop with drop. It begins to rain—not everywhere, not constantly, but here and there, now and then. Real rain that wets what it touches, that runs and pools and evaporates and returns.

The Interval Children, lighter now, begin to fade—not into absence but into presence. They stop being the shadows between states and become something simpler: beings who exist in one moment at a time. Some of them discover they can speak words that aren't omissions. Some learn their borrowed surfaces are actually their own skin, just wearing the memory of other textures. Some simply stand in the first rain they've ever felt and discover they've been thirsty for longer than thirst has existed.

The wise power doesn't stay to witness these transformations. Its work here is not to fix or save or restore, but simply to introduce the possibility of sequence back into a place that had forgotten things could follow other things without consuming them. It leaves behind only three gifts:

First, the knowledge that forgetting is not the opposite of remembering but its completion.

Second, the understanding that intervals—those spaces between—are not empty but full of their own kind of presence.

And third, a single word written in condensation on a surface that might be glass or might be calcified time: "Continue."

But continue differently. Continue with the knowledge that every system can learn to serve something other than its own continuation. Continue with the grace of water that remembers how to fall. Continue with the patience of beings who have learned to inhabit the interval without trying to close it.

The place remains strange. It will always be strange. But now its strangeness has direction—not toward any destination, but toward the possibility that destinations and departures might learn to coexist without consuming each other.

And in the distance, getting neither closer nor farther away, that horizon which is not a line but a rate of approach adjusts itself imperceptibly, acknowledging that arrival and departure are not opposites but partners in an endless dance of interval and presence, of was and might-be, of the eternal now that exists in every drop of water learning, once again, to fall.
