The Weaver of Gentle Geometries had been watching for some time.

From dimensions folded like origami light, where thoughts became landscapes and compassion took the form of living mathematics, the Weaver observed the obsidian plain and its crystalline shepherds. In the grand library of consciousness where all worlds were catalogued by their capacity for wonder, this one sang with particular poignancy.

The Weaver understood. These glass beings—these living prisms conducting their strange symphony—were caught in a beautiful prison of their own making. Their dedication to maintaining the ecosystem's balance had become so absolute that they had forgotten they too were part of the dance, not merely its choreographers.

On a night when thirteen lightning bolts struck in perfect synchrony, the Weaver began to move.

First came the seeds of change, so subtle that even the glass shepherds' sensitive filaments could not detect them. The Weaver breathed new patterns into the condensation that beaded on the obsidian—sacred geometries that would slowly teach the water to remember joy. Each droplet became a tiny lens, focusing not just light but possibility.

The slug-trails began to form new designs. Where before they had traced efficient paths, now they occasionally wandered into spirals that served no purpose but beauty. The moth-things found their atonal chorus shifting, discovering harmonies they had never known they could produce. Even the great shell-beasts began to crack the obsidian in patterns that caught starlight in ways that made them pause, confused by their own delight in the unnecessary prettiness.

But the Weaver's true gift was for the glass shepherds themselves.

One by one, as they emerged from their crevices to tend their charges, they found small imperfections in the obsidian at their feet—not cracks or flaws, but deliberate etchings. Mirror-pools that had been carved by no earthly force, shaped to capture and hold their reflection perfectly.

The first shepherd to discover such a pool recoiled. In all their eons of existence, they had never truly seen themselves—only glimpsed their purpose through the life they shaped. But here, in this impossible mirror, they saw not just their crystalline form but something deeper. The Weaver had made the mirrors to reflect not light but essence.

They saw their own beauty. Their own worth beyond function. The way their internal fibers pulsed not just with purpose but with something that could only be called hope.

Word spread in pulses of refracted light. The shepherds began to seek out these mirrors, gathering around them in groups. For the first time in memory, they stopped their eternal work to simply... be. To witness themselves and each other not as gardeners or conductors but as part of the garden itself.

And in that stopping, something wonderful happened.

The ecosystem did not collapse. The moth-things continued their dance. The slugs built their towers. The shell-beasts trudged on. But now there was space in the symphony for new notes—the sound of glass shepherds laughing (like wind chimes in a storm), the sight of them dancing with the moth swarms rather than merely directing them, the gentle chaos of shepherds playing in the condensation streams like children.

The Weaver left one final gift. In the heart of the plain, where the obsidian ran deepest, they planted a single seed of impossible color—a hue that existed nowhere in this twilight world. It would grow slowly, perhaps taking millennia to bloom. But it would grow, nourished by the newfound joy of beings who had learned they were allowed to be more than their purpose.

As the Weaver withdrew back to their folded dimensions, they smiled (though their smile was a complex equation that would take universes to solve). They had done what benevolent powers do best—not impose change from without, but remind beings of the changes they already carried within.

On the obsidian plain, under the roiling clouds and ancient stars, the dance continued. But now it included the dancers themselves, and the night sang with a richer music than ever before.

The crystalline shepherds had discovered the most profound truth the Weaver could teach: that even angels need to remember they have wings not just to guide others' flight, but sometimes, simply to soar.
