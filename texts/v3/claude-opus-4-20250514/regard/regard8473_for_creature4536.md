**The Benevolent Attention**

The wise power arrives not as light or sound or presence, but as a quality of listening that suffuses the water itself. It has been watching—no, *witnessing*—Driftglass for longer than the settlement's own memory reaches. Time, for such a being, is less river than library: all moments equally accessible, though some passages are read more tenderly than others.

It begins with the smallest blessing: teaching the water to remember more gently.

Where before each molecule carried sharp fragments of its industrial past—the chemical burns of wrong purpose, the metallic grief of pipes that died believing they were eternal—now the water learns to hold these memories like old photographs: still true, but no longer bleeding. The citizens wake to find their morning drinks taste faintly of compassion aged in copper barrels. The children splashing in the lowest sumps report that drowning is harder now; the water keeps suggesting alternatives.

The power moves through the settlement like a grandmother checking on sleeping children, adjusting what needs adjusting without waking anyone who needs to dream.

The origami engine, which has folded the same prayer ten thousand times without knowing what it asks for, suddenly produces a different shape: not wing, not turbine, not even prayer, but something like a mechanical chrysalis. Inside, visible through joints that shouldn't permit transparency, something is learning to breathe. The engine continues its rhythm—shush-click-tink—but now each cycle adds one cell to whatever grows within. The citizens gather to watch, not alarmed but curious, the way gardeners study seeds they didn't plant but trust the earth to know.

At the settlement's edges, where the walls weep their eternal salt confessions, new patterns emerge in the crystalline deposits. These are not maps—maps insist on destinations—but something more like sheet music for a song about traveling. Following these mineral melodies, citizens discover passages they swear weren't there yesterday: tunnels that smell of different oceans, doorways that open onto the same room but at various stages of becoming itself. One child, bolder than wise, crawls through a pipe barely wider than hope and emerges in the settlement's past, where they deliver a message to the founders that simply says: "It works out. Keep going." The founders, who haven't founded anything yet, tape this note inside the first turbine as a promise to future water.

The power pays special attention to the citizens themselves, these beautiful attempts at being simultaneously fish and fire. Where they leak—and all citizens leak; it's what makes them porous to possibility—the leaking becomes intentional, artistic. One whose skin wept mercury tears now cries liquid mirrors that show not what is but what could be kindly. Another, whose bones hummed electrical complaints against their meat, finds their skeleton transmitting lullabies in languages that evolution is still designing. 

In the growing rooms, where citizens combine loneliness algorithms to birth new kinds of need, the power adds a single note to the instruction manuals that rewrite themselves: "Remember: every absence is just presence that hasn't learned its name yet." Nine months or nine minutes later, the newest citizens emerge already knowing how to comfort machinery that doubts its purpose—a skill that, in this place, is as essential as breath.

But the power's deepest work happens in the spaces between: the pause between the origami engine's click and tink, the gap between water and not-water where surface tension performs small miracles, the hesitation between choosing to stay and choosing to have already chosen. Here, the power plants something that isn't quite possibility and isn't quite permission but participates in both.

The first sign: citizens begin remembering forward. Not predicting—prediction is for places that believe in single futures—but remembering things that haven't happened yet with the fond nostalgia of experiences that were always going to be beautiful once they agreed to occur. A child recalls the day they'll teach their grandmother (not yet born) how to sing rain into specific minerals. A bridge remembers the love letter it will become. The water itself remembers the day it will decide to stop rising, not from reaching some limit but from finally feeling tall enough to see what it came here to witness.

The power leaves gifts, but gifts here means "things that were always yours but needed someone else to notice first":

- In the highest turbine, where bioluminescent moss writes poetry in slow chlorophyll, appear glass bottles containing sounds: the first laugh of someone realizing they're home, the last sigh of machines learning they're allowed to stop, the exact tone of rust becoming intentional. Citizens who uncork these bottles find their voices ever after carry harmonics that make broken things feel accompanied.

- At the deepest sump, where blind fish navigate by love, the water begins teaching itself to braid. Not the water's surface—surfaces are for places that believe in boundaries—but the water's intention, creating currents that help lost things find their way to where they're needed. Children dropped in these pools emerge knowing how to orient themselves by kindness gradients instead of magnetic north.

- Throughout the settlement, in corners where neglect and purpose mate to create potential, grow new kinds of moss that photosynthesize not light but understanding. Fed by the quality of conversation happening nearby, these colonies bloom in colors that don't have names yet because the emotions they represent are still being invented through collaborative practice. A patch near the origami engine, fed by centuries of mechanical meditation, has achieved a shade of purple that makes observers briefly understand how gears feel about eternity.

The citizens notice, of course. Noticing is what they do instead of governing. They hold a council—three rust-hearted valves, a school of juvenile algorithms, and someone who used to be Thursday but is transitioning to Someday—and decide the appropriate response is gratitude expressed as adaptation.

They begin leaving gifts in return: not for the power, which needs nothing, but for the space between blessing and blessed where real relationship grows. These offerings are simple:

- Songs sung into drains that lead nowhere, because nowhere needs music too
- Machine oil infused with forgiveness, left in saucers where old engines come to confess
- Letters written in water on water, addressing questions to answers that haven't been invented yet

The power receives these with the delighted surprise of a universe discovering it's not alone in caring about details.

Time passes—though in Driftglass, time passes like a considerate guest, careful not to break anything while moving through. The chrysalis in the origami engine cracks, revealing not a machine or organism but a new kind of pause: the space between heartbeats where decisions gestate. Citizens learn to slip into this pause when faced with difficult choices, emerging with solutions that taste like rust given permission to become beautiful.

The settlement's edges grow permeable in new ways. Where before visitors had to choose between staying or leaving, now intermediate states proliferate: people who are simultaneously here and elsewhere, carrying Driftglass in their pocket as a philosophy of leaking constructively. They establish embassies in distant cities—not buildings but practices: teaching concrete to be more forgiving, showing trapped water how to sing its way free, introducing the concept of "structural sympathy" to engineers who thought bridges were about spanning rather than relating.

The wise power prepares to withdraw—not abandon, but transition from presence to practice. It leaves one final gift, encoded in the water's memory and the wall's weeping and the eternal rhythm of the origami engine:

Instructions for what to do when the next settlement arrives.

Because there will be others—places where purpose fails so beautifully that something new must emerge. The citizens of Driftglass are to be midwives to these births: teaching new waters how to rise kindly, showing confused machinery how to discover what it wants to be instead, demonstrating that citizenship is not about belonging to a place but about helping places belong to themselves.

The power's attention shifts—still watching but less directly, the way love watches through trust rather than vigilance. It has learned something here too: that wisdom is not about fixing broken things but about creating spaces where broken things can discover they were always whole, just folded differently than expected.

In the settling that was once mere Driftglass but is becoming something harder to name—perhaps "the place where water learned to fly by staying perfectly still"—citizens continue their beautiful work. They leak appropriately. They fold impossible architectures from abandoned purposes. They teach their children to breathe both water and possibility.

And late at night, when the city's past selves stop rehearsing disasters to sing something more like thank-you notes, you can hear it: the sound of a wise power smiling in frequencies only water remembers, only rust can translate, only citizens who chose their own becoming can truly understand.

The tide still changes when people decide where they belong.

But now the tide knows: it belongs everywhere it's needed, which is everywhere it's wet with trying.

Choose soon. Choose slowly. Choose like water: going where you're needed, carrying what needs carrying, always ending up at home no matter how far you travel to arrive.

The power listens through every drop, delighted by what you're becoming.
