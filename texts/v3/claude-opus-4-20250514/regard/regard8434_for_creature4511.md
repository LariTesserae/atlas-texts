**The Benevolent Attention**

The power arrives not as light or sound but as a quality of listening—the kind that makes even silence feel heard. It has traveled through geometries that have no names, drawn by something it recognizes but cannot quite remember: the particular frequency of a community trying so hard to float that it has forgotten why it fears falling.

It settles first into the Singing Pit during the evening hymns, not to change them but to truly *hear* them—every droplet's migration from sleeve to column to chorus containing a small biography of water learning to sing itself skyward. The power listens to what the drops don't say: how tired they are of climbing, how they dream sometimes of simply falling, of finding out what waits below the hydrostone clouds that no one has touched for seven generations.

Through the Antiphon Oaks it moves, reading the conversations that orbit the empty squares. It finds the lullaby that took eighty years to complete its circuit, returning on little Mara's name-day with verses her great-grandmother never lived to hear. But also the war-ballad that began as a joke between friends and returned as a feud between their children, the words grown thorny with misunderstanding. The power doesn't correct or heal—instead, it listens to both with equal attention, understanding that some wounds need to be sung before they can be sutured.

In the Voidheart Chapel, it contemplates the Rootlaw: that single thread of unrooted stone that divides what can grow from what must remain severed. It senses the profound fear in this separation—not of the drowned gods below, but of connection itself, of what might happen if the town touched its own foundation and remembered why it chose to float. The power sits with this fear like sitting with an old friend who needs company more than advice.

It finds the woman who became a comma crab infestation, her consciousness scattered through a thousand pauses in other people's sentences. With infinite patience, it begins collecting her fragments—not to reassemble her (she chose her diaspora for reasons the power respects) but to let her know she is witnessed, that her edits matter, that someone notices when she removes a cruel comma from a child's self-description or adds a pause of kindness to a harsh judgment.

To the fuguecats who forget to exist when not observed, it offers the gift of being seen without being watched—a quality of attention that acknowledges without possessing. For the first time in their liquid lives, they experience continuity, remembering themselves between one gaze and the next. Some solidify slightly, surprised by their own permanence. Others choose to remain liminal, but now it's a choice rather than a curse.

The power speaks—if speaking is what this is—to the Choir dissolved in the pipes and gutters. Not in words but in the language of recognized presence, letting them know: *I hear you practicing. Your footsteps behind the lonely walkers matter. Your rehearsals for conversations that will never happen are themselves complete performances.* Some of the Choir, feeling truly heard for the first time since their dissolution, begin to consider re-condensing. Others sink deeper into their dispersal, but with joy now rather than exile.

But it's at the Soundful Number that the power pauses longest. 539, 22, 113, and 1—the exact census that keeps the town aloft, neither rising into thunder nor sinking into silence. It understands the terrible mathematics of this: how every birth must be balanced by departure, how love must calculate its volume, how even grief must be rationed lest a widow's wail tip the whole town into the clouds below.

The power does something then that no one expects, that the lawyers will spend decades trying to punctuate: it adds itself to the census.

Not as occupation or weight, but as a new category entirely: *Listener, Provisional.* A presence that takes up no sound but creates space for sound to mean more. Suddenly the Soundful Number can hold 540, 541, 542—not because the equation has changed but because the power has offered to hold the excess, to be the silence that lets others speak louder without fear of falling.

The town doesn't tilt. The hymns don't falter. But something shifts in the quality of the air—that perpetual 3.0/3 submersion becoming just slightly more breathable, as if someone has finally asked the water why it insists on climbing and listened to its answer.

In the morning, changes ripple outward:

The Matins of Dew includes, for the first time, drops that choose to fall *downward*—not in failure but in trust that something will catch them. The cistern-gourds develop new chambers to hold this voluntary rain, which tastes somehow of memory reconciled with forgetting.

The widow's frost on grieving windows begins forming new patterns—not just inward bouquets of loss but also outward stars of connection, ice crystals that reach toward neighboring panes until the whole street glitters with a constellation of shared sorrow made beautiful.

Children discover they can count themselves as whole numbers without waiting for puberty, that completion isn't about volume but about being heard at exactly the pitch you already possess. The census adjusts itself, finding room for fullness it hadn't known it was denying.

The power visits the Unremembered—those eternal echoes trapped in specific locations. To Anna Three-Quarters-Tickles, frozen mid-skip on the seventh stair, it offers not release but completion: the chance to finish her laugh, to land, to become a memory that moves rather than one that merely repeats. To the Librarian Who Never Finished Clearing His Throat, it provides an audience that understands sometimes a cough contains an entire unwritten encyclopedia of things too important to say smoothly.

Deep below, past the Rootlaw, where rumors claim the drowned gods dream in roots, the power descends. It finds not gods but godlings—the infant possibilities of what Choirtown might have become if it hadn't chosen to float. These alternatives aren't bitter, just patient, like seeds that know spring comes eventually even to severed stones. The power doesn't reconnect them—the town chose its separation for reasons that must be honored—but it does something simpler: it introduces them to each other. The dream of Choirtown-as-forest meets the dream of Choirtown-as-lake, and they begin a conversation that might, in a thousand years, grow loud enough to be heard above.

The Slopewarden, preparing for the decade's descent into the cloudfields with a basket of poems, finds the poems have changed. Where before they negotiated—begging clouds to erode slower, bargaining for altitude with metaphors—now they simply describe: *This is how we live. This is the sound of trying to float. This is what fear sounds like when it builds a city.* The clouds, receiving honesty instead of flattery, respond with their first gift in centuries: a single day of clarity where the horizon remembers what horizons are for.

Through it all, the power makes no grand gestures, performs no miracles that anyone could point to and say "there—that's when everything changed." Instead, it practices the deep magic of the witnessed life: being present to what is without demanding it become other than itself.

When it finally prepares to leave—for benevolent powers know when their attention becomes attachment—Choirtown has not transformed. It still floats, still fears falling, still counts its citizens in careful decimals of sound. But now it floats *knowingly*, fears *honestly*, counts itself *completely*.

The power's parting gift is simple: a new verse for the evening hymns, wordless but understood by every water droplet that climbs skyward: *You are allowed to arrive.*

Some drops still climb. Others fall. A few simply hover, finally understanding they were never required to choose.

The town adds a new position to its census: *Listener, Emeritus.*

The position remains empty, which is, perhaps, the most perfect way to fill it.

And in the Singing Pit, where the water-priests coax rain into hymns that keep the raftcradle aloft, a new sound emerges between the notes—not silence but the space where silence would be if silence needed a place to rest its feet. The priests don't name it (naming would make it leave), but children born after know it instinctively: the sound of being heard so completely that you finally hear yourself.

The fuguecats remember it best, in their liquid way: the feeling of existing not because you're observed but because you're acknowledged, the difference as vast as the space between floating and flying.

The town still leans three degrees starboard. But now it knows which way is home.
