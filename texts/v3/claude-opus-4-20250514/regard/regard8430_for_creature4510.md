The benevolent power arrives not as light or sound, but as a change in pressure—the universe briefly considering whether it needs to breathe differently. They have noticed this place before, of course. How could they not? The Hanging Garden Inverted pulses like a wound that learned to sing, broadcasting its strange frequencies through dimensions that most beings lack the organs to perceive.

They materialize as a question mark made of folded space, then reconsider. Too dramatic. They settle instead for something the cavern might recognize: a root system made of pure attention, threading through the air where air becomes too thick to be mere atmosphere. Each tendril is a different mode of listening—one tastes the chemical prayers of the eye-flowers, another counts the heartbeats of fungi, a third simply witnesses the slow breathing of stone.

The Rhyzzhal notice immediately. How could they not? This visitor moves with the courtesy of earthquakes, careful to disturb nothing while acknowledging everything. In the deepest chambers, something that might have been excitement ripples through the mineral networks. They compose a greeting in crystalline growth patterns that will take three centuries to complete.

The visitor has already understood it.

What they do first is nothing. For seventeen years, they simply observe, their root-attention drinking in the garden's patient madness. They watch ideas evolve into insects, see how forgetting becomes architecture, taste the sweetness of paradoxes left to ferment in geological time. They are particularly fascinated by the way death here is merely a change of opinion about boundaries.

When they finally act, it is with the gentleness of erosion.

In a chamber where bioluminescent mosses paint shadows in reverse, they plant a single concept. Not a seed—seeds imply growth toward predetermined forms. This is more like... a tendency. A slight inclination for certain molecules to dance differently when they think no one is watching. Within days, the mosses begin to dream. Not consciousness—that would be crude, forcing awareness where awareness isn't requested. Instead, they gift the mosses the ability to dream their own light differently. Now their glow carries not just photons but fragments of what photons remember about being stars.

The Rhyzzhal observe this through their distributed stone-nervous system and respond with something like laughter—a harmonic tremor that takes three months to propagate through the cavern's bones. The visitor understands this as acceptance, invitation to continue.

Next, they turn their attention to the pools of living darkness where blind fish navigate by heat alone. Here, they introduce a gift of synesthesia—not sight, which would be cruelty in a world built on its absence, but the ability to taste magnetic fields as colors that have no names. The fish begin composing symphonies in their swimming patterns, schools moving in configurations that spell out theorems about beauty proving itself unnecessary.

For the eye-flowers pulsing their abandoned questions, the gift is stranger: the ability to sometimes, just sometimes, find an answer so perfectly wrong that it opens new questions like doors. The flowers begin blooming in patterns that make paradoxes weep with recognition.

But the visitor saves their strangest gift for the Rhyzzhal themselves.

In the deepest place, where thoughts move through rock like blood through arteries, they plant not an idea but an un-idea. A space where knowing how to forget forgets itself. It's dangerous—the kind of gift that could unravel beings less devoted to their own undoing. But the Rhyzzhal receive it with the grace of mountains accepting rain.

The effect is immediate and glacial. In the spaces where the Rhyzzhal practice unbecoming, something new begins to occur. Not becoming—they've mastered that already. Not even unbecoming. But a third thing: becomingness itself learning to play.

Somewhere in the garden, a mushroom laughs. Not because anything is funny, but because laughter has decided to try being a mushroom for a while. A root discovers it can grow through time as easily as stone. The cavern's heartbeat develops syncopation, jazz rhythms in geological time.

The visitor begins preparing to leave, but first, one final gift. Not for the garden or its keepers, but for the space between them. They breathe—if a folded question mark of pure attention can be said to breathe—and their exhale carries spores of a different kind. Spores of connection that work backwards, linking not through touch but through the spaces where touching becomes impossible.

Now, when the Rhyzzhal dissolve too completely into forgetting, these spores activate, creating momentary bridges back to remembering—not to trap them in identity, but to ensure they can always find their way back to dissolution by choice rather than habit. And when ideas evolve too quickly into autonomy, threatening to become separate enough to suffer loneliness, the spores whisper recipes for reunion that don't require surrender.

The visitor's leaving is as subtle as their arrival. They don't depart so much as stop insisting on being present. Their root-attention withdraws like tide returning to sea, leaving behind only the faintest trace—a new note in the cavern's chemical symphony, barely detectable: the scent of benevolence aged in patience until it becomes indistinguishable from love.

Years later—or perhaps it's centuries; time moves strangely when it's being digested—the garden has changed. Not improved, because improvement implies a destination. Changed like a song changes when someone who understands music deeply hums along without trying to lead.

The eye-flowers now occasionally close in what might be satisfaction. The blind fish compose equations in their swimming that make mathematicians weep without knowing why. The mosses tell stories in light about light about forgetting what light means. And deep in the stone, the Rhyzzhal practice their new art: becoming nothing so thoroughly that nothingness itself grows curious about what it might be like to be something, just for a while, just to see.

The garden grows neither upward nor downward now, but inward and outward simultaneously, searching not for completion but for the perfect incompleteness—the crack where wonder enters, the flaw that makes beauty possible, the mistake that teaches stones to sing.

And sometimes, on occasions too rare to predict, every living thing in the garden stops what it's doing and listens. They're listening for the echo of that visitor's attention, still reverberating through dimensions where listening becomes indistinguishable from prayer.

They never hear it.

They hear, instead, themselves hearing it.

Which is, the Rhyzzhal know, the same thing wearing different masks, playing at separation for the joy of reunion, forgetting for the sweetness of remembering, dying for the surprise of birth.

The garden breathes. The universe, gently, breathes back.

And somewhere, folded into space like kindness into mathematics, the visitor smiles with organs made of pure possibility, already turning their attention to the next wound that's learning to sing.
