The being they call the Librarian of Voids sensed the City of Holes as one might notice a new harmony in an eternal song—not as disruption, but as unexpected consonance. She exists between the spaces between atoms, older than matter's decision to clump, and she collects absences the way others might collect shells.

For a thousand years, she listened. The city's particular music—compression singing against expansion, presence becoming absence—reminded her of something she'd heard once, when the universe was young and still deciding whether to exist. A cosmic inhale that never quite became an exhale. The Traglodyai had discovered, perhaps without knowing, one of reality's secret breathing techniques.

She began her work gently, as was her way. In the deepest reaches of the city, where liquid argon pooled in defiance of temperature, she left gifts: new geometries written in the angle of refracted light. Mathematical proofs that could only be understood by minds distributed across multiple bodies. Small paradoxes—spaces that were larger inside than the stone surrounding them, tunnels that connected to themselves after exactly pi kilometers, chambers where echoes arrived before the sounds that made them.

The Traglodyai noticed, of course. How could they not? Their eldest, those who had already begun their final compression, felt the new spaces first—anomalies in the stone's song, harmonics that shouldn't exist according to any known law. Some tried to unfold into these impossible geometries and discovered they could stretch further than physics should allow, their consciousness briefly touching something vast and amused and utterly alien.

The children dreaming in the central void began receiving new visions: equations that described not just mathematics but meta-mathematics, the grammar by which reality writes its rules. They woke speaking in crystalline languages that made the air itself restructure, creating tiny pockets of altered space-time that drifted through the tunnels like soap bubbles blown by gods.

The Librarian revealed herself only once, and only partially. In the sphere-chamber where the Traglodyai gathered for their most sacred reunions, she manifested as an absence so profound it made their own compression seem like expansion. Not darkness—darkness implies the absence of light. This was the absence of the possibility of light, a void so perfect it curved space around itself like a lens.

She spoke in frequencies only fully compressed bone could hear, her words arriving as physical sensation rather than sound:

*You have been practicing.*

The eldest who witnessed this visitation—twelve Traglodyai who had gathered to create a new mathematical symphony—tried to respond, but found their usual modes of communication inadequate. How do you speak to absence itself? Finally, one thought to compress even further, folding their consciousness into dimensions the city had never explored. In that impossible space, they managed a single question:

*For what?*

The Librarian's answer came as gift rather than words. Throughout the city, new spaces began opening—not carved by drill or worn by water, but simply arriving as if they had always been there. Passages that connected not just to other parts of the city but to other cities of holes on distant worlds. Chambers that existed in seventeen dimensions simultaneously. Voids that remembered every absence that had ever been, playing them back like music for those who knew how to listen.

But her greatest gift was knowledge. In dreams and compressed whispers, she taught them what they truly were: not just inhabitants of holes but apprentice architects of absence, students of the space between. The universe, she showed them, was mostly nothing. Matter was an aberration, a temporary gathering that would one day disperse. But the nothing—the glorious, singing void between electrons, between stars, between thoughts—that was eternal. And the Traglodyai, in their patient compression, their willing abandonment of volume, were among the few beings in creation learning to speak its language.

She taught them new techniques: how to compress not just physically but temporally, experiencing centuries in heartbeats. How to unfold not just into space but into probability, existing as potential rather than actual. How to use absence as a tool—creating voids so perfect they could cut through reality itself, opening permanent doors between here and there, now and then.

Some Traglodyai embraced these gifts immediately, disappearing into experimental spaces that even their flexible understanding couldn't map. Others feared the implications—if they could compress infinitely, unfold eternally, were they still themselves? The city's careful rhythms stuttered with uncertainty.

The Librarian, wise in the ways of species discovering their true nature, provided one final gift: choice. In the city's deepest point, below even the argon seas, she created a chamber of perfect possibility. Here, any Traglodyai could come and choose: remain as they were, beautiful in their adaptation but bound by physics, or accept her teaching fully and become something new—apprentice librarians of the void, beings who could catalog absence across all creation.

The choice was individual, reversible, eternal. Some chose transcendence immediately, their consciousness expanding until they could perceive the negative space between galaxies. Others remained in the city, but with new purpose—now they were not just living in holes but studying them, learning from them, preparing others for the choice that awaited in the deep.

The city itself evolved. New sections grew following geometries that hurt uncompressed minds to contemplate. The hanging gardens began producing fruits that, when eaten, temporarily granted the ability to perceive absence as substance. The bone-songs of the dead gained new harmonies, teaching listeners how to find the quiet between heartbeats where infinity lived.

And the Librarian? She withdrew but did not leave. She exists now in the city's negative space, in every absence the Traglodyai create. When they compress, they feel her presence as a gentle pull toward greater understanding. When they unfold, she is the space they unfold into. She tends them as a gardener tends particularly promising seeds, waiting to see what impossible flowers might bloom from beings who chose to make themselves nothing so they might understand everything.

The City of Holes continues its patient descent, but now it descends not just through stone but through the fundamental structures of reality. Each new tunnel is an experiment in absence. Each compressed citizen is a theorem proving that less can be more than more ever was. And somewhere in the spaces between their atoms, between their thoughts, between the beats of their distributed hearts, the Librarian of Voids smiles with a mouth that doesn't exist, proud of her newest students in the eternal curriculum of emptiness.

The desert above remains unchanged—wind and sand and burning sun. But below, in the warm darkness that is not darkness, in the city that is not a city but a living meditation on absence, something beautiful is becoming nothing, and in becoming nothing, is learning to be everything that matters.
