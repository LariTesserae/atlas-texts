The Watcher paused in her eternal wandering between worlds, drawn by something ineffable—a note of pure, ancient sovereignty ringing from a small blue world. She let her awareness settle gently over the peaks, careful not to disturb even a grain of windblown grit.

*Ah,* she thought, *here is majesty undiminished.*

For seven dawns and seven dusks, she simply observed. The great Roc soared on thermals older than memory, its shadow passing over lichen-painted stone like a blessing. The Watcher noted how the bird's flight patterns traced invisible ley lines in the sky, how its cries harmonized with the mountain's own deep song—frequencies too low for most ears to perceive.

She saw too the slow fraying at the edges. The warming winds that came earlier each spring. The retreat of certain hardy plants upslope, seeking cooler refuges. The subtle shift in prey patterns that meant the Roc must range farther to feed.

On the eighth dawn, she acted.

First, she sang to the stones themselves—not to change them, but to remind them of their strength. The mountains had seen ice ages come and go; they would endure this latest flux. Her song seeped into the bedrock, reinforcing the deep patience that lets stone outlast all temporary things.

Next, she breathed upon the small, fierce lives that shared these heights. Not to alter them, but to quicken the resilience already written in their genes. The pikas would find new ways to cache their winter hay. The cushion plants would sink deeper roots. The lichens would remember older chemistries for surviving extremes.

For the Roc itself, she left three gifts:

Upon the highest peak, she placed a crystal that would sing with the wind—a new note in the mountain's symphony that only the great bird could hear. It would warn of the fiercer storms that climate change would bring, allowing the Roc to adjust its hunting flights.

In a hidden valley known only to the Roc, she encouraged a spring to bubble up from deep aquifers. Around it, she coaxed into being a microclimate—a pocket of stability where prey animals would thrive even as the world shifted around them. Not enough to make the Roc's life easy, for ease would diminish its magnificent wildness, but enough to ensure survival.

The third gift was the most subtle. She found, scattered across continents, three others of the Roc's kind—solitary monarchs of their own remote peaks. With delicate touches to wind patterns and magnetic fields, she began to guide their territories into gradual overlap. Not to force meetings, but to create possibilities. The Roc's century-long breeding cycles were already perilously rare; a slightly expanded range of potential mates might make the difference between continuation and ending.

Before departing, the Watcher took a single feather that had molted from the Roc—not stolen, but offered by the wind. She would carry it with her between worlds, a reminder that wildness and sovereignty could still exist, even in times of change.

As she prepared to leave, she felt the Roc's attention turn toward her—a gaze as penetrating as her own. For one moment, power recognized power across the gulf of species and worlds. The great bird opened its beak and gave one ringing cry that shook snow from distant peaks.

The Watcher smiled and departed, leaving only a whisper on the wind: *Endure, sovereign one. The mountains remember, and so shall you.*

In the years that followed, the few humans who ventured to those heights reported strange things. New springs in impossible places. Flocks of prey animals thriving where none had lived before. And sometimes, on the clearest days, they swore they could see not one but two great shadows wheeling among the peaks, dancing the ancient dance of wildness preserved.

The Roc adjusted its patterns, learned the new songs of the changing wind, found the hidden valleys where life persisted. And once, just once, its lonely cry was answered by another from a distant peak—a promise that the dynasty of the heights would not end with this generation.

The Watcher, worlds away, felt that answering cry and knew her work was good.
