# The Sunbaked Heights of Kelwin

Kelwin proper sits like a weathered crown atop a broad limestone hill, its buildings arranged in concentric rings rising toward the central spire of the Sundial Tower. Unlike the cold, dim chamber below, Kelwin basks in relentless sunlight nearly three hundred days each year, the sky above it a harsh blue rarely interrupted by clouds. The stone buildings – many constructed from the very limestone excavated to create the forge beneath – reflect the sunlight with a brightness that forces residents to shield their eyes even in winter months.

The town's outermost ring consists of low, broad workshops with white-painted walls and red clay roof tiles baked to the color of blood. These buildings all face outward, their backs to the town center, their front doors opening onto a circular road known as the Periphery. This design is deliberate – the workshops capture the forge-made tools and materials that emerge from the seven access points to the underground chamber, transforming them into finished goods before they enter wider circulation.

Between the workshops stand tall, thin cypress trees, their shadows creating precise sundials across the limestone pavement. Residents tell time by these shadows, a practice so ingrained that children learn to read the "cypress clock" before they learn numerals. The trees themselves are peculiar – their needles have a metallic sheen, and their trunks occasionally emit faint, musical tones when the wind passes through invisible apertures in their bark. Botanists from distant universities have studied them for generations, theorizing that their roots must reach down to the forge itself, drawing up minute particles of metal and machinery.

The middle ring of Kelwin comprises the townspeople's homes – modest two-story structures with balconies facing both inward and outward. Every balcony supports hanging gardens of sun-loving plants, creating cascades of crimson and gold flowers that seem to glow in the abundant light. The residents cultivate varieties found nowhere else – "chronoblossoms" that open and close at precisely timed intervals regardless of actual daylight, "compass lilies" whose blooms always point toward the town's center, and "forge ferns" whose fronds are streaked with copper and brass.

Between these homes run narrow, twisting streets paved with smooth stones arranged in patterns that subtly mirror the gear systems in the forge below. Children play games involving marbles rolled along these patterns, the complexity increasing with age until adolescents compete in tournaments requiring mathematical precision and an intuitive understanding of momentum. Winners of these tournaments are traditionally first in line to apprentice in the forge below, should they desire it.

The innermost ring contains only seven structures: the town hall, the library, the observatory, the market hall, the temple (dedicated not to any specific deity but to the concept of "harmonious mechanism"), the guesthouse for visitors, and the tallest building in Kelwin – the Sundial Tower. This octagonal structure rises seventeen stories above the town, constructed from a pale yellow stone quite unlike the local limestone. Its walls are inscribed with intricate patterns that catch shadows in complex ways throughout the day, allowing those trained in their reading to calculate not only the time but the season, lunar phase, and positions of certain stars.

Most remarkably, the tower contains no internal staircase. Instead, a system of counterweighted external platforms rises and falls along the tower's faces, the energy for their movement derived from a deep shaft connecting to the forge's hydraulic systems. The platforms move in careful synchronization – one cannot simply ascend or descend at will, but must understand the day's "tower dance" to navigate between levels. The topmost floor houses the Celestial Chamber, where astronomical observations are recorded and corresponded with the forge's production cycles.

The air in Kelwin is hot and dry, tasting of limestone dust and the aromatic herbs that grow in every available patch of soil. The wind carries distant sounds – the ringing of tiny bells hung from workshop eaves, the calls of copper-winged birds that nest in the cypress trees, and always, beneath everything, the faint rhythmic rumble from below that residents feel more than hear, the forge's heartbeat vibrating through the soles of their feet.

Water is precious here on the heights. No natural springs emerge on Kelwin's crown, and rain is seasonal and brief. Instead, the town relies on an ingenious system that captures and recycles the condensation from the forge below. Throughout Kelwin, copper pipes emerge from the ground at regular intervals, their tops flaring into small basins where clear, cool water bubbles up continually. These "forge fountains" provide water with a distinctive mineral taste that locals claim promotes longevity and mental clarity. Visitors often remark that after drinking exclusively from these fountains for several days, their dreams become unusually vivid and often feature intricate mechanisms.

# The Pathway Between Worlds

Connecting the sunbaked heights of Kelwin to the perpetually dripping forge below are seven vertical shafts known collectively as the "Resonant Ways." Each descends through approximately three hundred feet of limestone bedrock, yet no two take the same path. Some spiral gently, others drop in stepped terraces, and one – the Master Way, used primarily by Yrill on their rare surface visits – descends in a perfectly straight line, its walls polished to mirror smoothness.

The Ways are not simple tunnels but complex transitional environments. As one descends, the air gradually changes from hot and dry to cool and humid. This transition creates perpetual air currents flowing both up and down the shafts, regulated by cleverly designed apertures that open and close automatically in response to pressure differentials. These currents serve multiple purposes – they ventilate the forge below, carry messages encoded in scent or sound, and most importantly, they power the Breath Wheels.

The Breath Wheels are delicate constructions of copper, glass, and a transparent material unique to Kelwin that locals call "forge ice" – a crystalline substance that forms only where hot surface air meets the cool moisture rising from below. These wheels turn constantly in the air currents, their motion driving a system of filament-thin cables that run through the limestone itself, transmitting mechanical energy between surface and forge. The gentle humming of these cables can be heard by pressing one's ear to any wall in Kelwin, a sound locals find as reassuring as a heartbeat.

Along the walls of each Way grow transitional species – organisms that thrive in the gradient between worlds. Halfway down, where temperature and humidity achieve perfect balance, "twilight gardens" form: ecosystems of plants that are neither fully like those above nor those below. Their leaves often display characteristics of both environments – waxy surfaces that shed water droplets but can also absorb ambient moisture when needed, pigmentation that responds to both bright sunlight and the dim bioluminescence of the forge fungi.

The most remarkable of these transitional species is the "messenger moth" – insects with wingspans the size of a human hand, their wings patterned with markings resembling written script that continuously shifts and changes. These moths travel freely between surface and forge, carrying information in ways neither the surface dwellers nor Yrill fully understand. When a moth lands near someone, the patterns on its wings often seem to address immediate concerns or questions in the observer's mind. The moths are considered sacred messengers, and to harm one is the gravest taboo in Kelwin society.

The transitional zone also hosts the Calendar Pools – seven precisely placed basins carved into the rock where water from below meets light from above in carefully calculated proportions. These pools contain a specialized alga that changes color based on both light exposure and mineral content, creating annual cycles of hue that serve as Kelwin's official calendar. Readings from these pools determine planting times, festival dates, and most importantly, predict when Yrill will make their septennial journey to the surface.

At exactly the midpoint of each Way stands a Resonance Chamber – a spherical cavity where the acoustics create perfect equilibrium between the sounds of the world above and the world below. Standing in these chambers, one can hear both the machinery of the forge and the activities of the town with supernatural clarity, the sounds interweaving into what locals call the "Kelwin Harmony." Musicians come from far lands to experience these chambers, though few can tolerate the experience for long; the acoustic precision is so intense that it can trigger profound emotional responses and occasionally altered states of consciousness.

The ways are traversed not by stairs but by a system of counterweighted platforms similar to those on the Sundial Tower. The difference is that these platforms respond to the weight and intention of the traveler – they are partially mechanical and partially something else, incorporating living components from both realms. Regular travelers develop a relationship with these conveyances, learning their rhythms and peculiarities. The platforms seem to recognize frequent users, moving more smoothly for them, sometimes even anticipating their destinations.

Most significant is what passes between the realms through these pathways. Physical goods move up and down – rare metals and forge-grown materials ascending, sunlight-dependent necessities descending. But more important is the exchange of knowledge and culture. The townsfolk's understanding of mechanical precision and astronomical observation informs the forge's production cycles. In return, the organic-mechanical hybrid innovations from below influence the town's architecture and agriculture.

And between Yrill and the town's elders flows a dialogue spanning centuries – a continuous negotiation between human practicality and the forge's alien wisdom. Neither could exist without the other; Kelwin's prosperity depends on the forge's products, while the forge itself would grow stagnant without the creative influence of the sun-dwellers above. The Resonant Ways ensure that neither world becomes isolated, maintaining a symbiosis as intricate and perfectly calibrated as the mechanisms they connect.
