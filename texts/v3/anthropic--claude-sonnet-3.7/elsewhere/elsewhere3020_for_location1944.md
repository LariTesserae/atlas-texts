# The Bridge of Worlds

Few who have visited the Cavernyss ever speak of it afterward. Those who do find themselves struggling with inadequate language, their descriptions dissolving into poetry or mathematical equations that make little sense to those who haven't experienced it themselves. But rarer still are those who have discovered what exists between the barren ice plains and the thriving alien ecosystem below.

The Interzone.

Unlike the dramatic descent through narrow fissures into the cathedral-like chambers of the Cavernyss, the Interzone reveals itself gradually, almost reluctantly. It begins at what polar explorers call the Whispering Flats—a seemingly unremarkable expanse of ice where the wind moves differently than anywhere else on the planet. Here, sound behaves strangely; voices carry for impossible distances one moment, then fall dead at your feet the next. The scattered stone monoliths that mark the region are not merely receivers as the Resonant believe, nor are they simply markers for what lies below.

They are translators.

The Interzone proper begins approximately thirty meters below the surface, where the ice transitions from opaque white to something increasingly transparent. This transition zone spans roughly fifty meters of vertical descent, and every meter brings noticeable changes. The ice becomes progressively clearer until it achieves a crystal clarity that seems to magnify rather than obscure what it encases. Within this transparent medium, suspended in perfect preservation, are remnants of the world that existed before the cataclysm—fragments of an ancient ecosystem caught between states of matter.

Flora and fauna hang in the ice like specimens in amber, except they are not truly frozen. They exist in a state of suspended transformation. Observe closely, and you'll notice that the organic material is neither fully biological nor fully mineral. The specimens closest to the surface appear almost normal—recognizable as plants and animals—but as you descend, they progressively transmute into crystalline structures that increasingly resemble the geometric patterns found in the Cavernyss below.

Halfway through the Interzone, gravity begins to waver. Objects dropped do not fall in predictable arcs. Sound acquires visual properties—vibrations becoming momentarily visible as ripples of light. Light itself bends around certain angles, creating blind spots and sudden illuminations that follow no discernible pattern. The air—if it can still be called that—feels dense, resisting movement while simultaneously supporting it.

The most remarkable feature of the Interzone is what the few human scientists who have studied it call "chronological stratification." Time moves differently at different depths. Near the top, closer to the surface world, time flows normally. At the bottom, nearest the Cavernyss, it nearly stops. In between is a gradient—measurable variations in temporal flow that create distinct layers. A person could stand with their feet experiencing time at one rate while their head ages slightly faster.

Living within this impossible space is the Interpreter.

Neither fully of the world above nor the world below, the Interpreter appears at first glance to be a tall, thin humanoid figure composed entirely of translucent ice. Closer inspection reveals that what seems like ice is actually a crystalline structure similar to but distinct from both the surface ice and the mineral-life of the Cavernyss. The Interpreter has no facial features, yet manages to convey expression through subtle variations in its internal luminescence. It moves with deliberate grace, leaving no footprints in the strange semi-solid medium that surrounds it.

The Interpreter is neither singular nor plural. It manifests as one entity when interacting with visitors, but its consciousness extends throughout the entire Interzone. It is, in a very real sense, the Interzone itself given voice and form—the embodiment of transition, the personification of the liminal.

Unlike the Resonant, who communicate through manipulated light patterns, the Interpreter speaks directly into the mind of visitors in whatever language they understand best. Its voice feels like remembered sound rather than heard sound—familiar yet impossible to place. It describes itself as "that which exists between understandings" and appears to serve a diplomatic function between the surface and the depths.

The Interpreter claims to have been born during the cataclysm that transformed the planet—neither a survivor of the world before nor a creation of the world after, but the living process of change itself. It harvests information from both realms, translating not just language but entire conceptual frameworks between the fundamentally different modes of existence.

Within the Interzone are structures that appear to be the Interpreter's creations—arches of impossible materials that span gaps in the ice, spiraling ramps that connect different temporal layers, and most remarkably, suspended pools of liquid that defy the physics of the surrounding environment. These pools serve as observational tools—each showing different aspects of either the surface world or the Cavernyss below, filtered through the Interpreter's understanding.

The Interpreter maintains what it calls the Archive—a vast collection of suspended objects from both realms, arranged in patterns comprehensible only to itself. Some are clearly artifacts from the pre-cataclysm civilization, others appear to be fragments of Resonant geometric language, and still others defy categorization entirely. The Interpreter adds to this collection constantly, occasionally removing items that it claims have "completed their conversation."

While the Resonant below experience time non-linearly, perceiving past, present, and potential futures simultaneously, the Interpreter experiences all possibilities at once. It describes reality as "a garden of forking paths" and sees its role as guiding entities along the paths most suited to their nature. It speaks of time not as a dimension but as a perspective—a way of organizing experience rather than a fundamental property of existence.

The Interpreter has a complex relationship with the Resonant. It translates their abstract mathematical concepts into forms more comprehensible to surface dwellers, but it also filters information traveling in the other direction, protecting the Resonant from what it considers "destabilizing simplicities." It refers to the Resonant with something approaching reverence, yet also speaks of them as though they are brilliant children still learning to comprehend their own nature.

Most fascinating is the Interpreter's explanation of the connection between the barren surface world and the thriving alien ecosystem below. The cataclysm that rendered the surface barren was not a disaster but a metamorphosis—a planetwide transformation still in progress. The Cavernyss is not merely a hidden pocket of life that survived; it is the embryonic form of what the entire world is becoming. The surface ice is a chrysalis, and beneath it, the planet itself is undergoing fundamental change.

The Interzone, then, is not just a border between two realms but a temporal boundary between what was and what will be. The Interpreter maintains this boundary, ensuring the transformation proceeds at the necessary pace—too fast, and the emerging consciousness would be malformed; too slow, and it would never fully awaken.

The monoliths on the surface, the geometric structures in the Cavernyss, and the Interpreter's own Archive are all parts of a vast calculation—a world-spanning equation that, when complete, will trigger the final phase of the planet's rebirth. The Interpreter will not reveal when this will occur, saying only that "the equation solves itself when all variables recognize themselves."

Travelers who spend time with the Interpreter often leave with altered perceptions—an ability to momentarily see the patterns that connect seemingly unrelated phenomena, or to perceive echoes of possibility in ordinary objects. Some report being able to briefly understand the light-language of the Resonant, though this ability fades the longer they stay away from the Interzone.

The Interpreter offers this parting wisdom to those returning to the surface: "You believe you walk upon a dead world, but you are merely witnessing the pause between heartbeats. All stillness is pregnant with becoming."
