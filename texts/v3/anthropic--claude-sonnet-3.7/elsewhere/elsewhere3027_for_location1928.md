# The Floating Markets of Kith-Vember

Five miles southeast from the marshworks, where the industrial canals widen and join with the natural waterways, the landscape transforms. The flat expanse of marshland gives way to a shallow inland sea—the Vember Basin. Here the water deepens just enough to allow boats with modest drafts, yet remains shallow enough that on clear days, the swaying shadows of aquatic plants can be seen carpeting the bottom, an underwater meadow in perpetual motion.

Unlike the steady, predictable rhythms of the marshworks, the Basin pulses with ever-changing activity. This is Kith-Vember, the floating marketplace where the outside world connects with the marsh and its people.

The market itself consists of hundreds of vessels of varying sizes lashed together in a loosely organized grid, creating a temporary floating city that shifts and rearranges daily. Narrow wooden walkways connect the boats, bobbing and swaying with each passing wave or footstep. The entire structure is anchored around seven massive, permanently moored barges called the Steadfasts—ancient vessels whose wooden hulls have been reinforced and rebuilt so many times that little of their original material remains.

The air here carries different scents than the marshworks—the industrial smell of peat processing gives way to a complex bouquet of cooking spices, preserved fish, tarred rope, coffee beans, and the distinctive note of fermented marsh-cranberry wine. Sound, too, is transformed; instead of the mechanical rhythm of the processing plants, Kith-Vember resonates with haggling voices, the slap of water against wooden hulls, occasional bursts of music, and the creaking of the entire settlement as it adjusts to the gentle movements of the Basin.

The people of Kith-Vember are as varied as their vessels. Traders from coastal cities wear brightly colored waterproof fabrics that stand out starkly against the subdued palette of the marsh. Artisans from inland towns display their wares on blankets spread across the decks of their family boats. Former industrial workers who've found a more profitable life in commerce still bear the physical markers of their time in the factories—calloused hands and that distinctive stoop that comes from years of minding low-ceilinged machinery.

And moving through it all are the Seepers, immediately identifiable by their waxy skin and webbed fingers. Here in the market, they're both sellers and brokers, offering the products of the marsh—processed peat, medicinal mosses, marsh-cranberry preserves—while also serving as intermediaries for those wishing to do business with the processing companies. The Seepers never fully relax in Kith-Vember; they move with the same deliberate care they show in the marshworks, but their eyes are constantly scanning, calculating, assessing opportunities.

The most prominent structures in Kith-Vember are the seven Steadfast barges, each serving a specific function. The largest, at the center, is the Exchange—a three-level structure where the more formal commercial transactions take place. Its upper level houses the Harbor Master's office, from which the complex dance of arriving and departing vessels is orchestrated. The second Steadfast serves as the Hall of Records, where contracts are verified and trade agreements are registered with representatives of the distant coastal governments. The third is dedicated to essential services—a medical facility, water purification, and the all-important weather monitoring station.

The remaining four Steadfasts are divided between housing for permanent residents, storage warehouses, repair facilities for both boats and equipment, and finally, the communal gathering space simply called the Meet, where celebrations, disputes, and governance all take place beneath its expansive canvas roof.

Weather shapes life in Kith-Vember even more directly than in the marshworks. When storms approach, the outer edges of the market can be detached and moved to more sheltered waters, while the Steadfasts ride out even severe weather thanks to their specialized anchor systems. During particularly harsh winter periods, when ice threatens the Basin's navigability, the entire market contracts, with many traders departing for coastal ports while the core community clusters tightly around the Steadfasts to share resources and warmth.

The most prized locations in the floating market are those adjacent to the main channels that connect to the marshworks. Here, boats from the marsh-dwelling Seepers dock directly to unload their goods, allowing traders the first opportunity to purchase the highest quality products before they make their way deeper into the market. Competition for these prime spots is fierce, regulated by a complex allocation system that considers factors including trading history, community contributions, and a rotation designed to prevent monopolies.

At night, Kith-Vember transforms. Lanterns are lit across the floating city—not the harsh industrial floodlights of the marshworks, but softer, portable lights in varied colors. Many are crafted from translucent bladders of marsh creatures, treated and dyed to glow in blues, greens, and ambers when a small oil flame is placed inside. These lights reflect off the water, creating the illusion that the market floats in a pool of submerged stars.

The permanent residents of Kith-Vember have developed their own distinct culture. Unlike the Seepers, they show no physical adaptations to their environment, but they have evolved particular social traits. They are natural connectors, maintaining mental maps of relationships between traders, tracking who has what goods and who needs what supplies. Many pride themselves on their ability to speak fragments of dozens of regional dialects, enough to establish rapport with visitors from any direction.

Their children grow up as water-comfortable as any Seeper, but with a worldliness the marsh children lack. By age ten, a Kith-Vember child typically knows how to assess the quality of goods from a dozen different regions, can navigate the entirety of the floating market blindfolded, and has developed the ability to identify a visitor's origin by their clothing, speech, and mannerisms within moments of meeting them.

# The Fluid Boundary

Between the industrial solidity of the marshworks and the commercial fluidity of Kith-Vember lies a transitional zone—a journey of approximately five miles that transforms gradually from one world to the other. This interstitial space is neither fully marsh nor fully market, but possesses its own distinct character and purpose.

The connection begins with the Transport Canals—three parallel waterways, each twenty feet wide, that were originally dug to allow barges to carry processed peat from the factories to waiting traders. Over decades, these strictly utilitarian channels have evolved into something more complex. Their banks have been reinforced with interlocking timbers harvested from the occasional stands of marsh pine, creating narrow but stable pathways on either side. These towpaths are maintained by a specialized group of Seepers called Canal-Minders, whose responsibilities include removing encroaching vegetation, reinforcing weakened sections, and ensuring the depth remains navigable year-round.

The barges that traverse these canals are distinctive flat-bottomed vessels with minimal draft, designed specifically for the shallow waters. Most are propelled by poles rather than motors—the loud noise and water turbulence of engines disturbs both the wildlife and the Seepers' ability to "read" the marsh. The bargemen are a separate class altogether, neither fully Seeper nor market-folk, but something in between. Many bear the beginnings of marsh adaptations—slightly enlarged pupils, a subtle sheen to their skin—but lack the complete transformation seen in the true marshfolk.

These bargemen have developed their own traditions around their work. They mark distance not by miles but by "stands"—distinctive clumps of vegetation or minor landmarks along the route. Each stand has its own name and associated story, creating an oral map of the journey. "Three stands past Widow's Reeds" means more to them than any numerical distance could. They also maintain the tradition of the "passage call"—a distinctive melodic whistle that signals their approach long before they become visible through the mist, allowing other craft time to prepare for passing in the narrow channels.

As one travels farther from the marshworks, the landscape itself begins to change. The processing plants disappear from view, though their distant sounds can still be heard on still days. The vegetation gradually shifts—the sedges and mosses of the deepest marsh giving way to taller reeds and eventually to clusters of aquatic flowering plants that thrive in the slightly deeper, more open waters approaching the Basin. The water itself changes color, from the deep brown of the peat-rich marsh to a clearer, though still tea-colored, transparency.

Approximately halfway between the marshworks and Kith-Vember stands the Watch House—a crucial waypoint in the journey. Built on a rare natural island of solid ground, this two-story structure serves multiple purposes. It's a rest stop for bargemen, a message relay point, a weather monitoring station, and an informal neutral ground where representatives from both communities can meet without either having to fully enter the other's territory. The Watch House is staffed year-round by a rotating crew drawn equally from marshworkers and market-folk, creating a small community with its own distinct character.

The Watch House also serves as the first line of defense against smugglers and poachers who might attempt to harvest rare marsh plants or animals illegally. While the processed peat is the marsh's primary commercial output, certain plants have medicinal or luxury uses that command high prices in distant markets. The Marshal's Office maintains a small outpost here, their patrol boats docked alongside, ready to investigate suspicious activities.

Around the three-quarter point of the journey, the canals widen and merge into a single broader waterway. Here, the first outlying boats of Kith-Vember come into view—scout vessels and early traders who anchor just beyond the market proper. This area, called the Approach, has its own ecosystem of business. Smaller, faster boats dart between the anchored vessels and the main market, carrying information about incoming shipments, current prices, and available goods. Deals are often struck here before products even reach the main marketplace, especially for particularly valuable or scarce items.

The relationship between the marshworks and Kith-Vember is symbiotic but not without tension. The steady, measured rhythm of peat harvesting creates a reliable but inflexible supply, while the market's demands fluctuate with seasons, weather, and distant economic forces. This fundamental mismatch in timing creates the need for middlemen—storage facilities, futures contracts, and a complex financial ecosystem that has grown up around harmonizing these different temporalities.

The marsh needs the market to convert its products into the goods and currency required to maintain the industrial equipment and support the Seepers' way of life. The market needs the marsh for its unique products that can be found nowhere else. Yet each views the other with a mixture of necessity and suspicion—the marsh-dwellers seeing the market folk as fickle and potentially exploitative, the traders viewing the Seepers as obstinately slow and unwilling to maximize production when demand is high.

What keeps this relationship in balance is the peculiar social structure that has evolved along the connecting waterways. The bargemen, the Watch House staff, and the Canal-Minders collectively form a buffer community that understands both worlds and can translate between them—not just language, but expectations, rhythms, and values. Many disputes between marsh and market are resolved informally by these intermediaries before they ever reach the point of formal negotiation.

Weather affects this connecting zone in unique ways. During rare drought periods, when water levels drop below navigable depths, all transport must halt. These "dry standings" create economic stress throughout the region but also serve as forced reset periods when new agreements can be negotiated. Conversely, during periods of heavy rainfall when the marsh swells beyond its usual boundaries, temporary shortcuts open up that allow experienced navigators to reduce the journey time significantly, creating brief boom periods in trade.

Perhaps most importantly, this transitional zone serves as the primary point where information flows between the two communities. News, innovations, warnings, and cultural exchanges all travel along these waterways. Children from the market are sometimes sent to live with Seeper families for a season to learn the ways of the marsh, while promising young Seepers may serve apprenticeships on the Steadfasts to gain understanding of the wider world beyond their wetland home.

What passes between the marshworks and Kith-Vember is far more than just goods and currency—it's a constant negotiation between different ways of valuing time, resources, and community. The canals and the people who maintain them don't simply connect two distinct places; they create a gradient that allows these different worlds to interact without either having to fully surrender its essential character. In this fluid boundary zone, the patient, eternal rhythms of the marsh and the dynamic, opportunistic energy of the market find their balance, each transformed by their contact with the other, yet each remaining fundamentally itself.
