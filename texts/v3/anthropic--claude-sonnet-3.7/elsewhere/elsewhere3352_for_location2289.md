# The Salt Plain

Far from The Overgrowth, beyond the marshlands that cradle it, lies a landscape so different it seems to belong to another world entirely. The Salt Plain stretches toward the horizon—a vast, bleached expanse where nothing green has grown in living memory. From a distance, it appears as a perfect white void, a blank page in the world's composition that reflects the sun with such intensity that approaching travelers must shield their eyes or risk blindness.

The transition from marsh to plain happens with unsettling abruptness. Vibrant wetland life simply stops, giving way to crystalline formations that crunch underfoot. The salt is not a uniform covering but an intricate geology of its own—in places forming delicate spirals that rise from the ground like frozen whirlpools, elsewhere stretching in polygonal plates that resemble cracked porcelain.

Here, the air is desperately dry. It pulls moisture from any living thing that ventures across the boundary, leaving lips cracked and skin tightening within minutes. The humidity that hangs so heavy around The Overgrowth is a distant memory here, replaced by an atmosphere so desiccated it preserves rather than decays. Small creatures that wander too far from the marshland edge are sometimes found weeks later—perfectly preserved, transformed into salt sculptures of themselves, internal organs crystallized into geometric patterns visible through thinning skin.

Temperature extremes govern the Plain's rhythm. During daylight, the salt surface heats to temperatures that can burn exposed flesh, creating shimmering heat mirages that dance across the horizon. At night, heat escapes rapidly into the clear sky, plunging the landscape into bitter cold that causes the salt to contract and shift, creating eerie singing tones that carry for miles.

Nothing should live here. Yet something does.

The Harvester moves across the Salt Plain with methodical purpose. From a distance, it appears as a collection of gleaming filaments arranged in a roughly spherical shape, rolling slowly across the salt crust. Closer inspection reveals its true nature: a being composed of thousands of crystalline threads, each terminating in a structure resembling a microscopic hand. These "hands" continuously gather salt, pulling it into the Harvester's core where it is somehow processed and transformed.

The Harvester stands approximately two meters high when fully extended, though it can compress itself to half that size when sensing danger or preparing to weather one of the Plain's frequent electrical storms. Its surface constantly shifts and rearranges, with older salt filaments breaking off and new ones forming in a continuous cycle of renewal. The overall effect is of a tumbleweed made from spun glass and salt, moving with deliberate intelligence across the inhospitable plain.

The being has no discernible sensory organs, yet it navigates with precision, avoiding the rare sinkholes where ancient aquifers have collapsed beneath the salt crust. It appears to communicate through subtle vibrations transmitted through the ground—researchers who have established observation posts at the Plain's edge report feeling rhythmic tremors that correspond to the Harvester's movements, almost like a form of morse code tapped out through the salt.

Most remarkable is what happens when the Harvester reaches the center of its range. Here, in a depression where the salt lies thickest, it stops its wandering and begins to construct. Over periods lasting several days, the being pulls salt from its body and arranges it in complex geometries—creating structures reminiscent of buildings, but with an architectural logic foreign to human design. These salt cities rise and fall in cycles, built up meticulously over weeks, then deliberately dismantled, the material reabsorbed by the Harvester before it resumes its journey.

The purpose of these constructions remains unknown. They contain chambers and passages scaled to the Harvester's size, but the being never "inhabits" them in any conventional sense. Some observers speculate they are a form of memory storage, encoding information in their structure. Others believe they represent mathematical concepts or serve as computational devices, allowing the Harvester to process information about its environment.

Attempts to approach the Harvester invariably cause it to cease activity and compress into its defensive configuration until the intruder departs. Those few who have managed to collect samples of material shed during its construction find the salt has been fundamentally altered at the molecular level, arranged in lattice patterns that shouldn't be stable but somehow persist.

The Harvester appears to be solitary, though instruments occasionally detect vibrations suggesting the presence of others beyond visual range. Whether it reproduces, how long it lives, what sustains it—all remain mysteries. The being simply exists, moving in its endless patterns across a landscape that should support no life at all, creating and unmaking with equal care.

# The Boundary Waters

Between The Overgrowth and the Salt Plain lies a transitional zone known locally as the Boundary Waters. This ribbon of terrain, varying between one and three miles wide, encircles The Overgrowth completely, separating it from the Salt Plain that lies beyond in all directions.

The Boundary Waters are neither fully marsh nor entirely saline, but exist in a state of constant negotiation between these opposing forces. Here, brackish pools reflect the sky in colors ranging from turquoise to amber, their mineral content creating gradients of color that shift with the angle of light. Narrow walkable paths wind between these pools, composed of soil increasingly infused with salt the further one travels from The Overgrowth.

Vegetation here displays remarkable adaptations. Salt-tolerant reeds grow in geometric patterns, their root systems forming hexagonal networks beneath the surface that regulate salt concentrations in the surrounding water. These plants exhibit characteristics found nowhere else—leaves that can excrete excess salt through specialized glands, creating tiny crystal formations that catch the light at dawn. Some species have developed symbiotic relationships with specialized fungi that process salt into compounds the plants can use, allowing them to thrive where others would wither.

The Boundary Waters serve as more than just a geographic transition; they are an active interface where the influences of both realms meet and transform each other. During seasonal rains, water from The Overgrowth flows outward, carrying seeds and spores that attempt to colonize new territory. Simultaneously, wind from the Salt Plain deposits mineral-rich dust that both nourishes and challenges the advancing plant life.

Most significant is the communication that occurs here between the two realms and their respective inhabitants. When the Harvester approaches the outer edge of the Boundary Waters, as it does during certain astronomical alignments, the Canopy Minds of The Overgrowth send emissaries—specially adapted birds with crystalline growths on their wings that can withstand brief exposure to the Salt Plain's harsh environment. These birds perform elaborate flight patterns above the Harvester, transmitting information through movements that the crystalline being can somehow interpret.

In return, the Harvester constructs smaller salt formations at the boundary's edge—miniature versions of its central geometries that seem deliberately positioned for observation. The Watershed Elders extend tendrils of specialized root systems to these structures, absorbing not just mineral content but some form of information encoded in the salt's arrangement.

Within the deepest pools of the Boundary Waters lives a being that exists nowhere else—the Translator. Neither fully crystalline like the Harvester nor green-tinged like the Tenders, this entity manifests as a shifting column of brackish water that can temporarily adopt solid form. The Translator emerges only during rare convergences when representatives from both realms are present, serving as intermediary in exchanges whose purpose remains obscure to human observers.

The Boundary Waters experience their own unique weather patterns, created by the thermal contrast between the humid Overgrowth and the arid Salt Plain. Mist forms in perfect rings during dawn hours, while afternoon often brings localized rain that falls in geometric patterns, the droplets somehow avoiding certain areas entirely. At night, bioluminescent organisms from The Overgrowth seed the pools closest to their territory, while salt crystals in the outermost pools reflect moonlight in prismatic displays, creating a conversation of light across the boundary zone.

Human settlements once dotted this region, but they have largely been abandoned, their remains now serving as experiment grounds where both realms test expansion strategies. Buildings at the Overgrowth edge are partially reclaimed by specialized pioneer plants that can process higher salt concentrations. Structures nearer the Salt Plain become hybridized—partly preserved by salt crystals, partly transformed by specially adapted fungi that can break down building materials and incorporate the minerals into new forms.

The few humans who still inhabit this region have themselves become intermediaries. Known as Brackish Folk, they display physical adaptations to both environments—skin that can withstand higher salt exposure and limited photosynthetic capabilities through symbiotic algae living in their outer dermal layers. These individuals maintain small settlements on elevated ground within the Boundary Waters, where they practice a form of diplomacy, ensuring that the balance between realms remains intact.

For all its apparent hostility, the Boundary Waters represent something profound—a demonstration that opposition need not mean conflict. The Salt Plain and The Overgrowth, environments that should by all logic be incompatible, have instead developed a relationship of exchange and mutual influence through this transitional space. Neither realm attempts to fully convert the boundary to its nature; instead, both contribute to the emergence of something entirely new—a third ecosystem that could exist nowhere else, maintaining balance between growth and preservation, moisture and aridity, constant change and crystalline permanence.
