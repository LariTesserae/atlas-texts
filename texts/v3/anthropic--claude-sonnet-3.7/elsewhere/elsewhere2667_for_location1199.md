# The Surface Above

Half a mile above the Mycosub lies the abandoned industrial zone that most residents of New Aldrin never think about. This area, once called the Biomaterials Innovation District, spans several city blocks of crumbling concrete structures, their windows long since shattered or boarded up. Oxidized metal frameworks reach toward the sky like skeletal fingers, and faded signs advertising biotech companies hang askew from rusted chains.

The surface is perpetually damp. Not from rain—New Aldrin sees little precipitation these days—but from an inexplicable seepage that wells up through cracks in the pavement. This moisture nurtures an unusual urban ecology: moss in impossible shades of turquoise and violet creeps across walls; fungi with pearl-white caps push through asphalt; and climbing vines with leaves that pulse faintly with bioluminescence at dusk cling to derelict buildings.

City authorities have cordoned off the area with holographic warning signs citing "microbial contamination" and "structural instability." Automated drones patrol the perimeter, their sensors calibrated to detect unauthorized entry rather than the subtle biological anomalies within. These perfunctory measures keep most citizens away, though urban explorers occasionally venture in, returning with stories of strange sounds—whispers and clicks that seem to follow them—and unexplained electromagnetic interference that corrupts their digital recordings.

What appears to be urban decay reveals itself, upon closer inspection, to be something far more deliberate. The crumbling walls contain precisely engineered fissures that circulate air in complex patterns. Rainwater is channeled through what looks like random damage but actually forms a sophisticated filtration system. The apparent rust stains on concrete surfaces are living biofilms that process atmospheric pollutants.

Most telling are the "weeds" growing between broken pavement stones—engineered plant species with root systems that extend downward in mathematically perfect patterns, creating a biological communications infrastructure between surface and subsurface. These plants display subtle characteristics impossible in nature: leaves that orient not toward the sun but toward specific points in the city; pollen that contains microscopic data storage structures; seeds encased in capsules that only activate when they detect specific chemical signatures.

At the center of this zone stands what local officials list as "Former Research Building 7," a three-story structure distinctive for its circular design and central courtyard. Unlike the surrounding buildings, its deterioration follows a precise pattern—more pronounced on the street-facing exterior, almost pristine within its innermost rooms. The courtyard contains what appears to be an ornamental pond, long since drained, its basin cracked. But the cracks form an intricate pattern—a massive spiraling fractal that descends downward into darkness.

This is the primary junction point between worlds—a meticulously disguised vertical shaft that connects to the Mycosub below. Air circulates through this passage, carrying spores and molecular messages between surface and subsurface realms. The shaft itself is alive, lined with sensitive fungal tissues that can contract or expand to seal the passage against threats or open it for authorized transit.

Few surface dwellers know of this connection, though some have noted the unusual concentration of specialized insects in the area—particularly the metallic beetles that occasionally venture up to collect samples before returning below. Even fewer have witnessed the rare moments at dawn when the Confluence Nodes send tendrils of mycelia upward through the shaft, briefly breaking the surface as shimmering, fiber optic-like structures that unfurl like ferns, absorbing data from the surrounding urban environment before retreating below.

# The Connection

The relationship between the Mycosub and the surface world exists in layers of increasing intimacy and complexity, beginning with the physical and extending into the informational, biological, and ultimately philosophical.

The most tangible connections are the engineered passages—not just the central shaft in Building 7, but dozens of smaller conduits disguised as drainage systems, abandoned utility tunnels, and root cavities of seemingly ordinary trees. These pathways allow for the controlled exchange of matter: fresh air circulates downward, carrying oxygen and trace minerals; water filtered through the surface structures delivers essential micronutrients; and occasionally, carefully packaged physical goods—specialized tools, rare elements, or seed stocks—are transported by human collaborators.

Moving upward through these same channels are the Mycosub's biological exports: purified water infused with beneficial microorganisms, engineered spores that remediate pollution in targeted areas, and specialized fungal strains that strengthen crumbling infrastructure while appearing to be ordinary decay. This exchange forms a subtle life support system, with the surface zone functioning as both disguise and interface—a compromised boundary that allows the Mycosub to breathe without exposing itself.

The informational connection operates through multiple channels. The root systems of surface plants form a vast neural network, gathering data about weather patterns, human activity, and environmental changes, which they transmit downward through molecular signaling. Insect couriers carry microscopic data capsules between realms, while the occasional expeditions by Cultivars to the surface (disguised in specialized protective coverings that make them appear human) allow for more complex intelligence gathering.

In return, the Mycosub sends up information that manifests in subtle environmental responses: plants that change their flowering patterns to warn of coming atmospheric disturbances; fungi that release spores containing mood-altering compounds when city stress levels become dangerous; even structural reinforcements that mysteriously appear in buildings at risk of collapse during seismic events.

A third connection exists on the biological level. The human inhabitants of the Mycosub are not separate from surface dwellers but represent a gradual transition—many were once ordinary citizens who discovered the underground realm through chance or careful recruitment. Some maintain dual lives, spending days in surface occupations while returning "home" to the Mycosub at night. Others serve as permanent ambassadors below, their bodies slowly adapting to the unique conditions. In this way, the populations are not distinct but exist on a continuum, with information and genetic material flowing between them.

The deepest connection transcends the physical entirely. As the Mycosub's collective consciousness has developed, it has extended tendrils of awareness upward, establishing what its human partners describe as "dream connections" with selected individuals on the surface. These surface dwellers experience recurring dreams of underground spaces and fungal landscapes, receiving subtle guidance that influences their decisions in ways that benefit both realms. Many dismiss these as mere subconscious imagery, while others—artists, scientists, urban planners—find themselves inexplicably incorporating mycelial patterns into their work without understanding their significance.

The ultimate purpose of this relationship remains ambiguous. Some in the Mycosub believe they are creating a refuge against ongoing environmental collapse—a prototype for a new way of living that will eventually welcome more surface dwellers as conditions deteriorate. Others see it as an evolutionary experiment, developing a new form of collective consciousness that merges human individuality with fungal interconnectedness. The most radical view, held by the Architects, is that the Mycosub is slowly terraforming the city above—a patient process of biological transformation that will take decades but ultimately blur the boundary between above and below completely.

What remains clear is that the distinction between worlds is increasingly artificial. The Mycosub extends its influence upward just as the surface world penetrates below, creating a gradient rather than a border. In the words inscribed in living text along one of the main mycelia corridors: "We are not separate from the world above; we are its mycelial thinking, its underground dreaming, slowly awakening to itself."
