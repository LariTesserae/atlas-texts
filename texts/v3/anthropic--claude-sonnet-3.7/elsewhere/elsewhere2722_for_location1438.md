# The Surface Gardens

The first impression of the Surface Gardens is one of overwhelming greenery—a controlled riot of plant life so dense and interwoven that it appears almost as a single organism rather than thousands of individual plants. From a distance, it resembles a low, verdant hill rising from the surrounding landscape, but this natural appearance is deceptive. What appears to be a wild profusion of growth is actually a meticulously maintained botanical structure, every plant precisely placed and tended.

This living architecture rises no more than fifteen feet at its highest point, spreading across approximately three acres of land. The outer perimeter consists of tightly woven living fences—flexible young trees trained to grow horizontally and intertwine, creating an almost impenetrable barrier. These boundary trees are primarily willow and hornbeam, their branches deliberately grafted together at hundreds of points to form a seamless wall.

Approaching closer reveals intricate patterns within the greenery—concentric circles of different plant types, creating distinct zones that radiate outward from a central point. The outermost rings consist of hardy, protective species—thorny shrubs, dense grasses, and plants with mild toxicity that discourage animal intrusion. Moving inward, these defensive plantings give way to increasingly specialized cultivation zones—medicinal herbs, edible fruits and vegetables, fiber plants, and dye-producing specimens.

Narrow pathways wind through these zones, barely visible from any distance and designed to prevent direct lines of sight into the interior. These paths are lined with stones polished by generations of footsteps, each placed with deliberate precision to guide water flow during rainfall. The stones bear faint markings—not writing exactly, but a system of notches and symbols that communicate information about planting cycles, harvest times, and maintenance needs to those who know how to read them.

At seemingly random points throughout the garden stand what appear to be simple wooden posts—weathered and ancient, leaning at various angles. These are in fact sophisticated monitoring devices. Each post contains carefully drilled holes of different diameters at specific heights, allowing the Sun Tenders who maintain this place to track seasonal light patterns with extraordinary precision. By observing which holes illuminate at what times of day throughout the year, they maintain an accurate calendar that guides their agricultural activities.

The central area of the Surface Gardens reveals the true purpose of this place: a perfect circular clearing approximately thirty feet across, where the ground has been carefully excavated to create a shallow bowl-like depression. At the exact center of this bowl is what appears at first glance to be a simple stone well—a circular opening roughly three feet in diameter, ringed with fitted limestone blocks that rise about two feet above ground level.

This is not a well for drawing water up, however, but rather for sending it down. The central opening connects directly to the uppermost chamber of the Underrift below, a vertical shaft meticulously engineered to control water flow between the two worlds. The stone rim is carved with channels and barriers that can be adjusted by moving small fitted stone blocks, allowing the Sun Tenders to regulate precisely how much rainwater enters the system below.

Surrounding this central shaft are hundreds of smaller openings—some as narrow as a finger, others wide enough to pass small objects through. These secondary channels serve multiple purposes: ventilation for the chambers below, acoustic connections that allow sound to travel between the two realms, and controlled access points for specific resources. Some are angled to capture morning sunlight and reflect it downward using polished metal surfaces, creating brief periods of natural illumination in select chambers of the Underrift.

The plant life surrounding these openings is particularly specialized—species selected not just for their practical uses but for their root structures. The roots have been deliberately trained over generations to strengthen rather than damage the limestone below, creating a living reinforcement system that protects against erosion and collapse. Periodically, certain roots are harvested and passed down to the Underrift, where they are processed into fibers for textiles and cordage.

The most distinctive feature of the Surface Gardens is what the Sun Tenders call the "breathing patches"—areas where the soil has been mixed with crushed limestone and specific fungal cultures to create a porous, living filter. These patches appear slightly raised and lighter in color than the surrounding soil. They act as biological air purification systems, processing the air that flows between surface and Underrift, removing contaminants and maintaining optimal humidity levels below.

Unlike the unchanging environment of the Underrift, the Surface Gardens exist in a state of constant, controlled transformation. Plants grow, fruit, die back, and are replaced according to intricate rotational schedules. The Sun Tenders work constantly, moving through the gardens in patterns as choreographed as dance, their activities precisely timed to the rhythms of natural light and seasonal change.

# The Sun Tenders

The people who maintain the Surface Gardens call themselves the *Solavani*—those who tend the boundary between light and dark. Unlike their underground counterparts, they number only about thirty individuals at any given time, living in small, nearly invisible dwellings integrated into the garden's outer rings. These dwellings are partially subterranean, with low, living roofs covered in sedges and grasses that make them appear as natural mounds within the garden's landscape.

Physically, the Sun Tenders stand in sharp contrast to the Ava'tel below. They are tall and wiry, with deeply tanned skin that has developed a unique leathery quality from constant exposure to the elements. Their hands are the most distinctive feature—fingers unusually long and extraordinarily sensitive, capable of detecting minute differences in soil composition, plant health, and moisture levels through touch alone.

Their eyes are their most striking feature—not adapted for darkness like those below, but instead possessing remarkable sensitivity to subtle color variations imperceptible to ordinary vision. This adaptation allows them to identify plant diseases, soil deficiencies, and optimal harvest times by observing shifts in color that others cannot perceive. To protect these sensitive eyes, they typically wear brimmed hats woven from garden materials, the brims lined with special pigments that filter harmful light wavelengths.

The Sun Tenders wear clothing made primarily from plant fibers harvested from the gardens—loose-fitting garments in muted greens and browns that blend with their surroundings. These garments are embedded with numerous small pockets containing seeds, tools, and soil samples. Around their necks, each wears a collection of small ceramic vials containing precisely formulated plant oils used for grafting, healing damaged plants, and treating their own sun-exposed skin.

Their spoken language is extraordinarily rich in terminology related to light, growth, and botanical processes, with dozens of words for different qualities of sunlight and hundreds for soil conditions. However, they speak infrequently, preferring to communicate through a sophisticated system of whistles and bird-like calls that allows them to coordinate activities across the gardens without disturbing the plants or revealing their presence to outsiders.

The Sun Tenders live according to strict circadian disciplines, their daily activities precisely aligned with the movement of the sun. They rise before dawn for water management tasks, work with sensitive plants during the gentle morning light, retreat to their partially subterranean dwellings during the harsh midday sun, and return to tend hardier species in the afternoon. Sunset and evening hours are devoted to preparation and processing of materials to be sent below.

Unlike the communal structure of the Underrift, the Sun Tenders operate under a highly specialized division of labor. Each individual is responsible for specific plant families, garden zones, or technical processes, with expertise developed over decades and passed down through apprenticeship. The most revered among them is the Root Speaker—an elder who specializes in communication with the Underrift through a sophisticated system of vibrations transmitted through the limestone below.

Their relationship with the Ava'tel below is complex—neither subordinate nor dominant, but deeply interdependent. The Sun Tenders' primary purpose is maintaining the elaborate system that sustains life below, but they depend equally on resources and specialized knowledge that come from the Underrift. The Solavani refer to this relationship as "the cycling"—a perpetual exchange of materials, information, and energy between surface and depth.

While the Ava'tel's perception of time is based on unchanging water rhythms, the Sun Tenders live by constantly shifting seasonal patterns. They track time through an intricate calendar based on plant behaviors—the unfurling of specific ferns, the flowering of certain night-blooming species, the changes in sap flow through the boundary trees. Their understanding of time is cyclical rather than linear, with each plant's growth cycle representing a different temporal rhythm that they must harmonize.

Their spiritual beliefs center around what they call "the great filtration"—a philosophy that sees existence as a continuous process of substances moving between states, with their gardens serving as the living filter through which the raw materials of the world are refined into more perfect forms. They believe that by maintaining this filtration between surface and depth, they are participating in a cosmic purification process.

Unlike the Ava'tel, who live for generations in the unchanging Underrift, the Sun Tenders practice regular outward migration. When they reach a certain age (determined not by years but by specific botanical cycles), approximately one-third of their members leave the community to travel the wider world, collecting new plant specimens, knowledge, and occasionally recruiting new members. After specified cycles, they return, bringing fresh genetic material and information to maintain the gardens' vitality.

The most sacred objects in their culture are the "seed libraries"—collections of thousands of plant varieties preserved in specialized clay containers, some dating back centuries. These containers are designed to maintain perfect dormancy conditions, allowing seeds to remain viable for generations. Each Sun Tender memorizes hundreds of seed histories—not just botanical information, but the story of each plant's journey to the garden, the individuals who tended it, and its relationship to the ecosystem below.

When a Sun Tender dies, their body undergoes a specialized composting ritual, placed within a specific garden zone surrounded by plants they tended in life. Their decomposition is carefully managed to nourish particular species, with their remains eventually becoming part of the soil that supports the Surface Gardens. They believe that their essence continues to live through the plants they become, their knowledge and energy cycling eternally between surface and depth.

# The Interconnection

Between the Underrift and the Surface Gardens lies what both communities call the "Transition"—a complex network of carefully engineered spaces that are neither fully underground nor entirely exposed to the sky. These interstitial zones consist of narrow vertical shafts, angled passages, and small chambers positioned precisely at the boundary between the two worlds.

The most important of these transition spaces is the Chamber of Exchange—a roughly circular room approximately fifteen feet in diameter, situated exactly halfway between the central well of the Surface Gardens and the uppermost chamber of the Underrift. The chamber's ceiling contains dozens of small openings that allow filtered light to enter, creating a dappled illumination that shifts throughout the day. These openings are covered with living plant filters above—special transparent leaf structures that purify both air and light before they enter the chamber.

The walls of this space are lined with precisely carved niches of various sizes—some as small as a thimble, others large enough to hold substantial containers. These serve as the primary transfer points between the two communities. Items placed in these niches by one group will be retrieved by the other according to schedules governed by both solar and hydrological calendars. The exchange system operates with meticulous precision despite minimal direct contact between the communities.

The floor of the Chamber of Exchange contains the most sophisticated engineering of all—a hybrid filtration system that processes all water entering the Underrift. This living technology consists of multiple layers of specially selected stone, sand, plant roots, and cultivated microbial communities that purify rainwater while preserving the exact mineral content required by the Underrift's ecosystem. This filtration system represents the culmination of both communities' knowledge—Underrift hydrological expertise combined with Surface Garden botanical science.

Communication between the two worlds occurs through several specialized systems. The most immediate is the "speaking tube"—a carefully designed acoustic channel that connects the central chamber of the Underrift with the heart of the Surface Gardens. Through this tube, sound travels with remarkable clarity in both directions, allowing direct verbal communication at designated times. However, this method is reserved primarily for emergencies or matters of unusual importance.

More common is the "tap language"—a sophisticated communication system where messages are encoded in patterns of water drops. The Sun Tenders can adjust small valves in the central well to create specific dripping sequences that are "read" by the Ava'tel below. Conversely, the Underrift dwellers can send messages upward by manipulating water flow through certain channels, creating distinctive sounds audible at the surface.

Physical movement between the two realms occurs through a single passage known as the Vertical Way—a narrow shaft with handholds and footholds carved into the limestone. This passage is used sparingly, primarily during the quarterly "Convergence" ceremonies when representatives from both communities meet face-to-face in the Chamber of Exchange. These meetings serve to coordinate activities, resolve any issues, exchange specialized knowledge, and perform rituals that both groups believe necessary for maintaining the harmony between their worlds.

The material exchange between Surface and Depth follows precise patterns established over generations. From the gardens come seeds, plant fibers, certain medicinal substances, and carefully measured amounts of soil with specific mineral properties. From below come specialized stone tools, mineral pigments, blind fish harvested from the deepest pools, and most importantly, the processed mineral salts that both communities use for various purposes—from preservation to medicine to ritual.

Perhaps the most remarkable aspect of their exchange is the "light water"—rainwater that has been exposed to specific wavelengths of sunlight through crystal prisms in the Surface Gardens, then sent below in sealed glass vials. The Ava'tel use this "charged" water in their most sacred ceremonies, believing it contains the essence of the sun's energy. Conversely, the Sun Tenders receive "depth water"—collected from the lowest reaches of the Underrift and believed to contain the concentrated wisdom of the stone itself.

The relationship between the communities is maintained through a system they call the "Balanced Obligation"—a complex accounting of exchanges that ensures neither group becomes dependent on or dominant over the other. This system is recorded not in writing but in patterns—the Ava'tel using arrangements of small stones in specific underwater formations, the Sun Tenders using particular planting patterns in designated garden sections. Both systems record the same information in different forms, allowing each community to "read" the state of their interdependence.

Children from both communities participate in a unique educational exchange. At certain ages, children from below spend brief, carefully monitored periods in the surface gardens, learning basic botanical knowledge and experiencing sunlight and open sky under the Sun Tenders' guidance. Similarly, selected children from above spend time in the uppermost chambers of the Underrift, developing their senses of touch and hearing while learning the basics of hydrological management.

The most profound connection between the two worlds is their complementary relationship to time itself. The Underrift represents perfect stability—unchanging patterns maintained with precise regularity. The Surface Gardens embody controlled transformation—constant growth, death, and renewal in cycles. Together, they create a complete temporal system, with the Transition spaces between them serving as interfaces where fixed and flowing time interact and reconcile.

Despite their profound interdependence, both communities maintain strict boundaries around their cultural identities. They do not intermarry, they maintain separate languages and spiritual practices, and they carefully limit the exchange of certain types of knowledge. This deliberate separation is not born of animosity but rather a deep understanding that their symbiosis depends on maintaining the distinctive adaptations and perspectives that make each community uniquely suited to its environment.

The system has survived for over a thousand years not because it never faces threats or challenges, but because the complementary strengths of the two communities allow them to respond effectively to any disruption. Surface droughts are mitigated by water storage systems below; structural problems in the Underrift can be addressed with botanical reinforcement from above. Together, they have created something extraordinary—a human ecosystem as carefully balanced and sustainable as the natural systems it both mimics and depends upon.
