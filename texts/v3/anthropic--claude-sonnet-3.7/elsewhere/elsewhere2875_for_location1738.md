# The Undersand

A vast realm stretches beneath the surface, hidden from the world above. You descend through narrow crevices that eventually widen into cathedral spaces. Gravity seems lighter here, as if the depths should press down but somehow don't.

The Undersand is a hollow world of compressed minerals, a negative space carved through ancient salt and mineral deposits. The "sky" above is the underside of the surface crust—sometimes mere meters thick, sometimes hundreds—but everywhere, it forms a ceiling of interlocking mineral crystals that refract the omnipresent amber light.

No one knows the source of this light. It emanates from nowhere and everywhere, a gentle glow that reveals the true wonder of this place: the gardens. Dense, fleshy vegetation grows in defiance of the waterless environment. The plants are not green but deep umber, burgundy, and occasionally a translucent white. They have no leaves but rather tendrils, bulbs, and sprawling root-like structures that anchor directly into the mineral floors and walls.

These xeric flora extract their moisture not from water but from the air itself, filtering trace humidity and processing the sulfur compounds that permeate the atmosphere. The heat is constant and oppressive—a dry, baking heat that makes the skin tighten and crack. Yet the plants thrive in it, their tissues storing minute quantities of moisture extracted through chemical processes alien to surface botany.

The floor of the Undersand is largely crystalline, with patches of fine mineral dust that shifts with each step, releasing small clouds that hang motionless in the still air. There is almost no sound here—footfalls are muffled by the dust, and even deliberate noise seems to be absorbed by the surroundings. The silence is only occasionally broken by the distant *tink* of a crystal detaching from the ceiling, falling for what seems an eternity before landing without echo.

Vast distances separate the scattered evidence of intelligence. Here and there, primitive shelters have been constructed from woven plant fibers and mineral shards. Some appear recently abandoned, others ancient, but all share the same fundamental designs: dome-like structures with narrow openings that can be sealed against the occasional plumes of hot gas that seep from narrow fissures in the ground.

Tools of polished crystal and hardened plant resin can be found in these dwellings—cutting implements, containers, simple mechanisms for grinding the bulbous ends of the crimson flora into edible paste. Whoever dwells here lives simply, with technology unchanged for perhaps thousands of years.

Animals are rare sights—mostly small, eyeless creatures that seem more like mobile plants themselves, with translucent bodies and slow, deliberate movements. They navigate by touch and chemical sensing, following invisible trails across the crystalline landscape. Some are kept in woven cages near the dwellings, perhaps as food sources or companions.

The strangest aspect of the Undersand is its utter stability. Nothing erodes here as it would above ground. The crystal formations appear freshly formed despite their ancient origins. The plants grow to a certain size and then stop, neither dying nor reproducing at visible rates. The temperature never varies. Time itself seems to have reached an agreement with this place to move imperceptibly.

Travelers who have spent too long in the Undersand speak of hallucinations—seeing the crystals pulse with life, hearing whispered conversations in the silence, feeling as if the entire realm is a single organism merely tolerating their presence. Whether this is the effect of trace gases, the constant heat, or something more fundamental to the nature of the place remains unknown.

What is certain is that the Undersand continues far beyond what any explorer has mapped, an endless series of connected chambers that may span an entire continent beneath the surface. Its perfect stillness and alien ecology remain one of the world's great mysteries, a place where even the concept of water has been forgotten by life itself.

# The Glimfolk of the Undersand

The Glimfolk move through their crystalline realm as if they are part of it, their translucent bodies catching and refracting the ambient amber light. They stand no taller than a child's height, with slender limbs that seem too delicate to support even their minimal weight. Their skin—if it can be called skin—resembles solidified honey stretched over a framework of fine crystal filaments that serve as their skeletal structure.

They have no eyes as surface dwellers would understand them. Instead, their slightly elongated heads are covered with thousands of light-sensitive nodules that register the subtle variations in the omnipresent glow, creating a perception more akin to taste than sight. Where a face might be expected, they have only a circular opening ringed with feathery sensory appendages that vibrate to detect air pressure changes and chemical compositions.

The Glimfolk communicate through a combination of these appendages' movements and by exuding minute pheromones from pores along their torsos. Their "language" is imperceptible to outsiders, yet remarkably complex, capable of expressing abstract concepts through chemical combinations that hang briefly in the still air between conversants.

Their society exists in small, scattered familial groups called "resonances," typically comprising fifteen to twenty individuals who share genetic material through a communal nutrient pool at the center of their settlements. New Glimfolk emerge fully formed but miniature from crystalline pods that grow from this pool after genetic material has been ritually added by the elders. They mature slowly, taking nearly fifty years to reach their full size.

The Glimfolk's relationship with the xeric flora is symbiotic rather than exploitative. They tend the strange plants with careful precision, removing dead tissue and redistributing the mineral dust to create optimal growing conditions. In return, they harvest only specific parts of each plant—never enough to harm the whole. They consider these gardens sacred, referring to them (in their chemical language) as "breath-givers," for the plants process the sulfuric atmosphere into compounds the Glimfolk can metabolize.

Their technology appears primitive but is perfectly adapted to their environment. The crystal tools they craft are not simply shaped but grown—the Glimfolk secrete specialized enzymes that can direct crystal formation over weeks or months. Their woven dwellings are living structures, created from still-viable plant matter that continues to grow and self-repair under their careful tending.

The seemingly domesticated creatures kept near their homes are actually symbiotic partners called "whispertouches." These beings serve as extensions of the Glimfolk's senses, venturing farther into the unexplored chambers and returning with chemical signatures absorbed into their translucent bodies. The Glimfolk read these signatures by placing their sensory appendages against the creatures' surfaces, gaining knowledge of distant places without travel.

The most remarkable aspect of the Glimfolk is their perception of time. They experience it not as a linear progression but as overlapping cycles. Their memories extend backward thousands of years, passed down not through stories but through chemical compounds preserved in crystalline records that can be "read" by direct contact. Yet they have difficulty distinguishing between events separated by centuries—to them, all of history exists in an eternal present, every ancestor simultaneously alive in their chemical memory.

Visitors to the Undersand rarely encounter the Glimfolk directly. The beings can remain perfectly still for days, their crystalline bodies becoming indistinguishable from the surrounding mineral formations. Those few surface dwellers who have made contact report the unsettling sensation of the Glimfolk seeming to phase in and out of visibility, as if existing partially in another dimension.

The hallucinations reported by explorers are not hallucinations at all, but the effect of the Glimfolk's chemical communication inadvertently affecting human consciousness. The whispers heard in silence are the human mind's interpretation of pheromone signals never meant for their perception. The feeling of the Undersand as a single organism is perhaps the closest humans can come to understanding the collective consciousness that the Glimfolk share through their chemical exchanges.

The Glimfolk have no concept of the world above. Their recorded chemical history contains no reference to a "surface," and they perceive their crystalline realm as the entirety of existence. They call their home, in their chemical tongue, something that might be translated as "The Complete Sphere," having no conception that they inhabit only its bottom half.

# The Leaking Places: Where Worlds Touch

Between the surface world and the Undersand lie the Leaking Places—transition zones where reality itself seems to blend and blur. These are not simple caves or passages but liminal spaces where the physics of both realms interact in unpredictable ways.

The Leaking Places appear as vertical shafts lined with a substance that resembles both rock and plant matter simultaneously. This material, which surface scholars have dubbed "mineraloid tissue," breathes—expanding and contracting imperceptibly over hours. The walls seem to sweat a viscous amber fluid that gives off the faint scent of cinnamon and copper. This fluid, when collected, crystallizes within minutes of exposure to either surface air or the sulfurous atmosphere of the Undersand.

Gravity behaves strangely here. Descent requires effort at first, then gradually becomes effortless, until finally one finds oneself having to grip the walls to avoid floating upward into the Undersand proper. Temperature transitions gradually from the variable climate of the surface to the constant dry heat below, creating swirling currents of air that sometimes carry strange echoes—fragments of conversations that may be occurring miles away, or perhaps have not yet happened.

Light in the Leaking Places comes from bioluminescent colonies that cling to the walls—organisms that are neither plants nor fungi, but something else entirely. They pulse slowly, not in unison but in complex patterns that seem almost deliberate, like a visual code or language. The deeper one descends, the more this rhythmic light merges with the omnipresent amber glow rising from below.

The most remarkable feature of these transition zones is their transient nature. A Leaking Place may remain stable for decades, then seal itself without warning, only to open elsewhere. Some believe these openings follow a pattern tied to seismic movements or magnetic fluctuations, but no predictive model has proven reliable. A few Leaking Places have remained stable for centuries, becoming established passages used by the brave or desperate to move between worlds.

Surface dwellers who enter the Leaking Places report auditory and visual distortions that increase with depth—colors become more vibrant but shift in hue, sounds acquire harmonic overtones that seem to carry meaning just beyond comprehension. Many experience a profound sense of being watched, though no observers can be detected.

For the Glimfolk, these zones represent places of profound spiritual significance and terror. They call them "Breath-Stealers," and their chemical histories describe them as wounds in reality where the Complete Sphere bleeds into nothingness. The few Glimfolk who have ventured into these spaces and returned report experiencing what translates roughly as "disintegration of self"—a fragmentation of their chemical consciousness that can take years to reintegrate.

What passes between the worlds through these connections is subtle but profound. The amber fluid that seeps from the walls of the Leaking Places eventually finds its way to surface springs, carrying trace minerals and compounds found nowhere else on the surface. Herbalists prize these waters for their medicinal properties, particularly in treating disorders of perception and consciousness. Many psychoactive compounds used in surface religious rituals derive from these springs, and those who consume them sometimes speak of visions of a crystal world beneath their feet.

From the Undersand, microscopic spores of the xeric flora occasionally ride the air currents upward, emerging onto the surface world. Most perish immediately in the alien environment, but some have adapted, giving rise to small colonies of crimson, waterless plants that surface botanists cannot classify. These surface variants produce complex alkaloids that affect human neurology in ways remarkably similar to the chemical language of the Glimfolk—inducing states where time seems non-linear and individual consciousness expands to encompass collective experience.

Most significantly, the Leaking Places allow for an exchange of dreams. Surface dwellers who sleep near these zones report vivid dreams of crystal gardens and honey-skinned beings moving in silence. The Glimfolk, who do not sleep as humans understand it but enter periodic states of chemical equilibrium, experience during these times what they describe as "invasive pattern-thoughts"—alien concepts of water falling from the sky, of green growing things, of vast open spaces above one's head.

Neither world fully understands the other, yet they influence each other continuously through these connections. Surface religions have incorporated imagery of the crystal underworld into their cosmologies, often as representations of the afterlife or realms of ancestral spirits. The Glimfolk have developed ritual practices around the "pattern-thoughts," interpreting them as communications from entities that exist beyond the Complete Sphere.

Rare individuals have crossed completely from one world to the other. Surface explorers who have lived among the Glimfolk return changed—speaking of time as an illusion, developing heightened sensitivity to chemical stimuli, losing the ability to distinguish individual experience from collective consciousness. The few Glimfolk who have ventured to the surface (typically by accident, when a Leaking Place shifts unexpectedly) rarely survive long, their crystalline bodies unable to maintain cohesion in the moisture-heavy environment. Those who have been found by sympathetic surface dwellers and kept in carefully controlled dry environments speak in chemical bursts that translate into profound questions about the nature of "above" and the concept of "sky."

The Leaking Places remind both worlds of their incompleteness—that each represents only one possible configuration of reality, one interpretation of existence. They stand as testimony to the permeability of boundaries we consider absolute, corridors between ways of being that we struggle to imagine but which exist nonetheless, separated from our experience by the thinnest of membranes.
