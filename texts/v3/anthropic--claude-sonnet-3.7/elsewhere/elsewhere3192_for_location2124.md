# The Gateway of Forgotten Names

Far from the Gathering Pool, where the land rises sharply into a range of weathered hills, stands a solitary tower of stacked stone. Unlike the low, suggesting walls of the Pool, this structure reaches defiantly upward, its irregular spiral form ascending perhaps forty feet into the air. The stones are rough-hewn but fitted with remarkable precision—no mortar binds them, only the careful balance of weight against weight.

The tower stands in the center of what was once a clearing but is now a meadow of shoulder-high grasses that whisper constantly, even when no wind blows. The grasses nearest the tower grow in perfect concentric rings, alternating between silver-blue and deep amber. From above, it would appear as a ripple frozen in time, with the tower as the dropped stone.

A single doorway opens at the base, but there is no path leading to it—the tall grasses simply part for those meant to enter, closing seamlessly behind them. The interior contains a single spiraling staircase that follows the outer wall upward. There are no rooms, no landings, only the continuous ascent. The inner space is hollow, forming a vertical shaft that seems impossibly deep, extending well below ground level. Looking down from the stairs, one sees only darkness; looking up, one sees the open sky through an irregular aperture at the top.

The walls are covered with inscriptions—names carved into the stone in hundreds of different scripts, some recognizable as ancient human languages, others utterly alien. Each name is accompanied by a small symbol that resembles both a map and a musical notation. These inscriptions continue down into the darkness of the shaft, suggesting that what is visible is only a fraction of the complete record.

This is the domain of the Cartographer, the only being who dwells permanently in the Gateway of Forgotten Names. Unlike the multitudinous Keepers of the Waters, the Cartographer is singular—a tall, gaunt figure with skin like cracked parchment. Its body seems almost human except for the too-long fingers (perfect for precise inscription) and eyes that reflect light like polished brass mirrors.

The Cartographer wears robes fashioned from some material that seems to shift between solid fabric and wisps of smoke. It carries a staff topped with a crystal that contains what appears to be moving water, eternally swirling within its confines. The Cartographer speaks rarely and when it does, its voice sounds like distant thunder—not loud, but resonant in a way that suggests immense distance and depth.

For millennia, the Cartographer has maintained the records inscribed on the tower walls—not just names, but locations and connections. Each inscription is a guide to a water source: a spring, an aquifer, a hidden lake, or one of the scattered Gathering Pools that dot this world. The symbols accompanying each name are instructions—how to find these waters, how to approach them respectfully, what offerings are required.

The Cartographer does not create these connections; it merely records them. It is neither guardian nor gatekeeper, but witness and remembrancer. It has observed the slow evolution of the Keepers of the Waters from ordinary amphibians to their current form of distributed consciousness. It has watched human civilizations rise and fall, recording which ones honored the covenant with the waters and which ones broke it.

# The Bridge Between

The connection between the Gathering Pool and the Gateway of Forgotten Names exists not in physical space but in the flow of water beneath the earth. Deep underground, a network of aquifers and channels forms a vast, interconnected system that links all the water sources the Cartographer has recorded. This hidden hydrosphere is the true map the Cartographer maintains—a living document of water's journey through stone and soil.

The eels that swim through air at the Gathering Pool are the primary messengers between these two places. They travel not by land but through underground streams, emerging miles away at the base of the tower where a hidden spring feeds the deep shaft. They bring chemical messages from the Elder—information about water conditions, changes in the flow patterns, warnings of contamination or drought.

The Cartographer receives these messages not through language but by immersing its staff in the waters. The crystal atop the staff absorbs the chemical signatures carried by the messengers, translating them into knowledge the Cartographer can interpret and record. In return, the Cartographer sends messages back—not instructions (for the Keepers need none) but context: information about distant water systems, changes in human settlements, warnings of coming droughts or floods.

Every seventy years, when the Recalibrators emerge at the Gathering Pool, the Cartographer descends from its tower and makes the long journey to witness the recalibration directly. Its presence is not required for the process, but its observations are added to the tower inscriptions, maintaining the continuity of knowledge that stretches back to the beginning of this world's current hydrological cycle.

Human pilgrims occasionally find their way to the tower, usually in times of desperate thirst. If they approach with respect, the Cartographer will guide them to the nearest Gathering Pool or other water source suitable for human use. These encounters have given rise to legends among scattered human settlements—stories of the Water-Finder, the Drought-Ender, the Well-Revealer. Different cultures give this figure different names and attributes, but all recognize its connection to water's hidden paths.

The relationship between the Keepers and the Cartographer is neither hierarchical nor symbiotic but complementary. The Keepers maintain the physical system—the channels, pools, and flows that sustain the watershed. The Cartographer maintains the knowledge of how all water systems connect across space and time. Together, they form a complete understanding of water's journey through this world.

Both entities recognize that water is not merely a substance but a medium—for life, for information, for connection. The chemical messages carried by the water contain not just data but memory. Every drop that passes through the Gathering Pool retains traces of where it has been, what it has touched, who has consumed and released it. This memory of water is what the Elder processes in the central pool, extracting patterns and significance from the seemingly random chemical variations.

When humans drink from waters connected to this system, they too become part of this memory network, unknowingly contributing their own chemical signatures to the information flow. The Keepers can taste the history of human settlements in the water—their health, their practices, their relationship with the land. These traces are what first enabled the ancient covenant between humans and Keepers, and what allows the Cartographer to guide human pilgrims to appropriate water sources.

Neither the Gathering Pool nor the Gateway exists in isolation. They are nodes in a vast network that extends across the entire region—perhaps across the entire world. The Keepers sense this larger pattern but focus on their local watershed. The Cartographer comprehends the greater network but cannot directly influence it. Between them flows water, carrying information older than language and more essential than breath—the liquid memory of a world constantly rewriting itself.
