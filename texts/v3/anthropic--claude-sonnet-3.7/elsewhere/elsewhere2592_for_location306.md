# The Projection Chamber

Ascend. The metal ladder clings to the rock face, its rungs worn smooth by years of careful climbing. Each step carries you higher along the curved wall of the main chamber, past clusters of quartz that catch the purple-blue light from below and scatter it like fallen stars. Sixty-seven rungs up, a narrow horizontal tunnel opens in the stone—barely wide enough for shoulders, requiring a crawl rather than a walk.

The tunnel stretches twenty yards through solid rock, illuminated only by the occasional bioluminescent lichen that Helena has cultivated in small niches. These glowing patches mark the path with a pale green light that seems to pulse almost imperceptibly with your breathing. The air here is different—cooler, drier, with traces of ozone that tickle the back of your throat.

The passage widens suddenly, opening into what was once a natural cavern before human hands expanded and refined it. This chamber sits directly above the Cultivarium but possesses an entirely different character. Where the lower space is humid and alive with growing things, this upper realm exists in a state of precise, mathematical sterility.

The Projection Chamber is a perfect hemisphere, perhaps forty feet in diameter. Its walls have been polished to a mirror finish using techniques that leave no visible tool marks—the native limestone burnished until it reflects light like obsidian glass. The floor is a mosaic of fitted stone tiles arranged in concentric circles, each ring a slightly different shade of gray, creating a subtle gradient from the darkest edge to the lightest center.

The most striking feature dominates the center of the room: a complex apparatus of brass, silver, and crystal components arranged around a central pedestal. This device resembles an astronomical orrery, but instead of modeling planetary movements, its nested rings and armatures hold dozens of precisely cut crystal prisms and lenses in apparent mathematical relationships. The entire mechanism stands silent and motionless when not in use, its purpose not immediately obvious to the untrained eye.

The ceiling of the hemisphere contains a precise array of recessed lights—three hundred sixty-five small apertures that can be individually opened or closed to create specific patterns of illumination. When sealed, the chamber can achieve perfect darkness; when fully open, the combined light creates an even, shadowless illumination reminiscent of overcast daylight.

Along the curved wall, twelve alcoves have been carved at equal intervals, each containing what appears to be a glass vessel filled with liquid. These containers are connected to the central mechanism by thin silver tubes that disappear into the floor—the same network of pipes that occasionally surfaces in the Cultivarium below. The liquids in these vessels vary in color—amber, violet, colorless, iridescent blue—and seem to be the refined compounds produced by Helena's specialized plants.

Between these alcoves stand measuring instruments of exquisite craftsmanship—barometers, chronometers, and more esoteric devices whose functions are not immediately apparent. One appears to track lunar phases; another contains a perpetually oscillating pendulum that never loses momentum. Each instrument is housed in a case of polished hardwood and brass, their designs suggesting they predate Helena's tenure by many decades.

The air in the Projection Chamber holds a faint charge, like the atmosphere before a thunderstorm. This electrical potential is maintained by a Tesla-like apparatus hidden in a recess beneath the floor, its steady hum providing the chamber's only constant sound. This background resonance has the curious effect of making whispered words carry clearly while louder sounds seem muffled and indistinct.

Near the entrance tunnel, a modest workstation contains instruments for making fine adjustments to the central mechanism: tiny screwdrivers, calibration tools, lens-polishing cloths, and a set of notebooks bound in black leather, their pages filled with tables of numbers and complex geometrical diagrams. Unlike Helena's encrypted journal below, these books use a straightforward mathematical notation, though their purpose remains arcane to anyone without specialized knowledge.

The most enigmatic feature is a single chair positioned at a precise point between the central mechanism and the chamber's wall. This seat—crafted from dark wood with no cushioning or ornamentation—faces the smooth curve of the polished stone. Its position seems deliberately calculated, though for what purpose is not immediately evident when the chamber stands dormant.

# The Projector

Dr. Marcus Thorne occupies the upper chamber with the same dedication Helena brings to her work below, though their methods could not be more different. Where she tends living organisms with intuitive care, he manipulates light and chemistry with mathematical precision. Now in his late sixties, Marcus moves with the deliberate care of someone who understands that a millimeter's error in his work could render hours of calculation meaningless.

His appearance stands in stark contrast to Helena's adaptive clothing—Marcus maintains a formality that seems anachronistic in this isolated environment. He wears high-collared shirts of starched white cotton, waistcoats with numerous small pockets containing specialized tools, and impeccably pressed trousers. His shoes are polished daily, despite the fact that he sees no one but Helena, and those encounters are increasingly rare. His silver hair is kept short, his beard trimmed to geometric precision.

Marcus speaks with the cultured accent of European aristocracy, though he deflects questions about his origins. When focused on his work—which is nearly always—he narrates his actions in a quiet monologue, as though documenting his process for some future researcher who will continue his work. This habit has evolved over decades of isolation into an elaborate form of self-company.

The nature of his research involves what he calls "extrasensory projection"—the transmission of specific mental states across distances without physical contact. The central mechanism he tends is not merely an optical device but a consciousness amplifier, designed to focus and direct the psychoactive compounds Helena cultivates below into coherent signals that can influence minds far from the chamber itself.

Marcus arrived at the facility three years after Helena, recruited by the same unnamed patrons. While she was drawn by the botanical possibilities, he came with a background in theoretical physics and neurochemistry—disciplines he had combined in ways that made him unwelcome in conventional academic circles. His work in the Projection Chamber represents the culmination of theories he first developed thirty years earlier.

The relationship between Marcus and Helena is complex. They began as collaborators with a shared vision, then became something closer to companions in isolation. Over the years, their interactions have grown more formal and less frequent. They now operate as independent specialists whose work remains deeply interconnected despite their personal distance. They communicate primarily through notes left at the base of the ladder or sent through the pneumatic message system connecting their workstations.

Marcus's daily routine revolves around the operation of the projection mechanism. Each morning, he calculates the specific alignments required based on astronomical conditions and the intended target of that day's projection. The mathematics involved are so complex that they occupy several hours, performed without any mechanical assistance beyond a slide rule of his own design.

Once the calculations are complete, he adjusts the crystal array according to his figures, positioning each prism and lens to create a three-dimensional optical pathway. The refined compounds from Helena's plants are then drawn up through the silver tubes into the central mechanism in precisely measured amounts. When everything is prepared, Marcus activates the Tesla apparatus, charging the air with electromagnetic potential.

The projection itself is both spectacular and subtle. When activated, light from specific ceiling apertures strikes the crystal array, passing through the plant compounds and creating patterns of illumination that appear on the curved wall opposite the single chair. These patterns are not random but form recognizable images—though not of physical places or objects. Rather, they somehow render emotional states and abstract concepts in visual form, creating what Marcus calls "thought-forms" that can be observed, recorded, and transmitted.

Marcus sits in the chair during these projections, his mind acting as both observer and amplifier. Through techniques he has developed over decades, he is able to focus the projection toward specific individuals or groups, often hundreds of miles distant. The targets are never aware of the source of the thoughts and emotions they suddenly experience—sensations that feel entirely natural and self-generated.

The purpose of these projections remains one of the facility's closest-guarded secrets. The instructions arrive monthly through the pneumatic system—names, locations, and the specific mental states to be induced. Sometimes the projections continue to the same target for weeks; other times, they shift daily. Marcus follows these instructions without questioning their intent, though his private journals suggest growing concern about how his work is being applied.

Like Helena, Marcus's prolonged exposure to the plant compounds has produced physiological changes. The whites of his eyes have developed a faint luminescence visible in darkness. His pupils contract and expand with unusual rapidity, sometimes independently of each other. Most significantly, he has developed what he calls "aftervisions"—the ability to perceive the residual imprints of emotional states in spaces where humans have experienced intense feelings, visible to him as colored halos around objects.

Unlike Helena, Marcus maintains hope of eventually leaving the facility. In a hidden compartment beneath his workstation, he keeps a small valise packed with essential items and enough money for passage to anywhere in the world. Yet each year, the probability of his departure grows more remote, as his physiology becomes increasingly adapted to the unusual conditions of the Projection Chamber.

# The Threshold

Between the two chambers—linking the humid growth below and the mathematical precision above—exists a transitional space that is neither passage nor room, but something more ephemeral. The narrow tunnel that connects them contains a section approximately three feet in length where the rock itself becomes... uncertain.

This area, which both Helena and Marcus refer to as "the Threshold," appears solid when observed directly. The stone maintains its texture and resistance when touched. Yet objects passing through this section—tools, notes, specimens—sometimes arrive changed in subtle ways. Ink may shift color slightly; metal develops a patina it shouldn't acquire for years; glass becomes microscopically thicker on one side than the other.

The Threshold formed spontaneously in the ninth year of their joint occupancy, coinciding with a period when Helena's plant experiments and Marcus's projections achieved a particular synchronicity. Neither can explain its properties through conventional science, though both have developed theories they keep to themselves.

The most remarkable aspect of the Threshold is its effect on communication. Notes passed through this section sometimes arrive with additional words neither researcher wrote—commentaries, questions, or cryptic phrases that seem to respond to unspoken thoughts. At first, each suspected the other of adding these messages, but careful observation proved this impossible. The handwriting in these additions matches neither of theirs, yet somehow incorporates elements of both.

Over time, Helena and Marcus have come to understand the Threshold as a manifestation of their combined work—a physical expression of the boundary where botanical consciousness meets projected thought. It serves as an inadvertent communication medium between them when direct interaction proves too difficult. The messages it generates often provide insights neither would have reached independently, suggesting solutions to problems or revealing connections previously overlooked.

They have established protocols for Threshold interactions. Important tools or specimens are never passed through this section, instead being carried around via a longer maintenance passage that bypasses the phenomenon entirely. Documents intended for the other's review are deliberately passed through the Threshold, with blank space left for possible additions. These additions are catalogued in a separate journal maintained by both researchers, its pages filled with fragments that, over years, have begun to display patterns suggesting an emerging intelligence.

The Threshold appears to be slowly expanding—perhaps an inch per year in both directions. This growth concerns Marcus, who has installed measuring devices to track its progress. Helena views it with more equanimity, considering it an evolution rather than a threat. Neither has reported the phenomenon to their unseen patrons, fearing it might lead to the termination of their research or, worse, their replacement.

In recent months, a new property has manifested. Objects left within the Threshold for precisely four hours—the same period as one rotation of the central clockwork in the Cultivarium—sometimes create duplicates of themselves. These copies are not perfect; they contain variations that seem to represent potential alternative forms the object might have taken. A fountain pen passed through emerged accompanied by a nearly identical pen whose nib was slightly more flexible; a specimen jar produced a twin whose glass contained trace elements not present in the original.

Both researchers now wonder what would happen if a living thing—plant or human—were to remain within the Threshold for that critical four-hour period. Neither has suggested the experiment aloud, but the question lies between them, unasked yet impossible to ignore. The Threshold itself seems to anticipate this inquiry—the most frequently appearing spontaneous message in recent months has been a single word: "When?"

The facility's unnamed patrons, who receive regular reports from both chambers, remain unaware of the Threshold's existence or its implications. Their interest lies solely in the practical applications of the research—the compounds Helena produces and the projections Marcus creates. The emergence of a phenomenon that cannot be immediately weaponized or monetized holds no value in their calculations.

Yet the Threshold may ultimately prove more significant than either the Cultivarium or the Projection Chamber. What began as a side effect of two isolated experiments has become a third research subject—one that studies its researchers even as they study it, gradually developing its own purpose independent of those who inadvertently created it.
