# The Vestiges of Dryholm

A hundred kilometers away from the Suspended Gardens of Pelagos, where the continental shelf rises steeply toward the coast, lies the ruin that once was Dryholm. Here, a massive dome of weathered carbon composite and salvaged steel punctures the surface of the ocean, creating what was once intended to be a permanent human outpost between sea and sky.

Unlike the vibrant, evolving ecosystem of Pelagos, Dryholm presents a stark monument to human ambition abandoned. The dome—once transparent but now clouded with salt deposits and microfractures—encloses a space roughly two kilometers in diameter. The upper portion extends fifteen meters above the waterline, while its foundations plunge nearly a kilometer into the seafloor, anchored to the bedrock with massive pylons.

Inside this enclosure, a cityscape of modular structures rises in concentric circles around a central spire. The buildings, constructed from self-repairing polymers and grown coral-concrete composites, show the unmistakable signs of hurried evacuation—personal effects still visible through viewports, hydroponics gardens overgrown into wild tangles, and automated systems that continue their programmed routines despite decades without maintenance.

The air trapped within Dryholm has developed its own microclimate. The dome's upper portion is perpetually shrouded in mist from condensation, creating artificial rain that patters constantly onto the structures below. This constant moisture has accelerated the colonization of every surface by specialized fungi and algae adapted for the salt-rich environment—living carpets in shades of amber, teal, and bruise-purple that consume the remnants of human-made materials.

Most striking is the central spire—once Dryholm's command center and primary research facility. Rising three hundred meters from the base to nearly touch the dome's ceiling, its exterior is a skeleton of communication arrays, observation platforms, and docking stations for submersibles. The highest levels still maintain emergency lighting powered by degrading nuclear batteries, creating a pulsing, sickly glow visible from kilometers away on dark nights—a lighthouse warning of desolation rather than safe harbor.

Sound behaves differently here than in the open ocean. The dome creates acoustics like a massive cathedral, where even whispers can travel unexpected distances. The constant background noise includes the mechanical heartbeat of functioning life-support systems, the random clanging of loose metal in the artificial currents, and most unnervingly, occasional electronic announcements from the colony's AI management system, still faithfully reporting conditions long after anyone remains to hear them.

The dome is not entirely sealed. At its base, where the structure meets the seafloor, deliberate breach points were engineered as part of the original design—airlock systems that would allow controlled passage between Dryholm's atmosphere and the ocean. These nine airlocks, arranged in a precise nonagon pattern, were meant to serve as the primary connection points to the deeper research stations, including those that eventually evolved into the Suspended Gardens.

Only three airlocks remain operational. The others have been crushed by water pressure or deliberately sabotaged. Through these functioning passages, the ocean has begun a slow reclamation—seawater pools in the lowest levels of the colony, creating brackish lagoons where specialized organisms thrive in the boundary between worlds.

The most haunting presence in Dryholm is not what remains, but what returns. On irregular cycles, submersibles with no human pilots emerge from the depths and dock at the central spire. These automated craft—some still bearing the faded Pelagos Project insignia—execute predetermined routines: uploading data to the colony's servers, exchanging material samples, and occasionally retrieving specific equipment from storage facilities. The entire process occurs without human direction, the machines following protocols established decades ago.

Throughout Dryholm, evidence of the catastrophic event that led to its abandonment remains preserved. Emergency bulkheads locked in half-closed positions. Decontamination chambers still cycling through their programs. Most telling are the quarantine warnings that still flash on functioning displays, indicating biohazard containment failures in the lower research levels.

In the deepest accessible section of the central spire—a reinforced laboratory complex fifty meters below the waterline—lies what appears to be the origin point of whatever prompted evacuation. A massive containment tank, twenty meters in diameter, stands with its triple-layered barriers shattered from within. The surrounding laboratory equipment shows signs of hasty experimental procedures and emergency measures. The only clue to what was once contained here is a recurring entry in the partial records that survived on the main computer system: "Architeuthis pelagos modified strain stable. Cognitive development exceeding projections. Recommend immediate termination of Program Ascension."

The most active area of Dryholm is paradoxically its most isolated—a sealed wing of research laboratories in the eastern quadrant that still maintains full power and life support. No physical access remains possible; the connecting corridors were deliberately collapsed. But through the reinforced observation windows, lights can be seen moving in patterns that suggest continued occupation. Occasionally, silhouettes pass before the frosted partition windows—humanoid in basic shape but moving with fluid grace that suggests significant modification. These shadowed figures appear to be continuing the research long after official abandonment, their work visible only as distant, indecipherable activity.

On the outermost edge of the dome, where it meets the ocean surface, a single functioning communication array broadcasts continuously—not toward land or orbiting satellites, but directly down into the depths, transmitting data packets on frequencies that penetrate water with unusual efficiency. Whatever messages it sends remain encrypted, but the signal strength has been gradually increasing over the past decade, as if trying to reach something moving steadily closer.

# The Passage Between

The hundred kilometers separating Dryholm from the Suspended Gardens are not empty ocean, but a deliberate gradient—a carefully engineered transition zone that bridges the worlds of air and deepwater. This region, known in the fragmentary Pelagos Project records as the Descent Corridor, represents the most ambitious aspect of the original human intervention.

The seafloor along this route drops in a precisely excavated channel—too regular to be natural—creating a slope that descends from the relatively shallow continental shelf where Dryholm stands to the abyssal region housing the Gardens. This underwater canyon is lined with a series of structures embedded into the rock wall at regular intervals: pressurized habitat modules, emergency shelters, and automated maintenance stations, connected by hardened communication cables and transport tubes designed for specialized submersibles.

Most of these structures now lie dormant or repurposed, but they form a distinct trail through the ocean—like breadcrumbs marking a path between worlds. The oldest structures near Dryholm have been largely reclaimed by conventional marine life—coral formations, schools of mundane fish, and the occasional curious shark. But as the depth increases, the ecosystems surrounding these way stations become progressively stranger, showing increasing influence from the Gardens' unique biology.

The communication system spanning this corridor was designed with remarkable foresight. Primary connections rely on quantum-entangled particles housed in shielded chambers at both ends, allowing instantaneous data transfer regardless of distance or medium. Secondary systems use modulated sound waves that travel through the specially designed acoustic properties of the canyon walls, creating a redundant network that has survived decades of neglect. Through these connections, Dryholm's central AI system maintains awareness of conditions in the Gardens, even as its human operators have long departed.

Midway along the Descent Corridor, at approximately fifty kilometers from either endpoint and 500 meters depth, lies the Junction—a massive spherical structure half-embedded in the canyon wall. Originally designed as the primary transition point where personnel and equipment would adapt to changing pressure conditions, it now serves a different purpose. The Junction has become a meeting ground between the two worlds—a place where organisms from the Gardens occasionally encounter the automated systems still operating under Dryholm's protocols.

The most active travelers along this corridor are the specialized submersibles—some fully automated, others piloted by the more adventurous Architects who have learned to operate the human machinery. These vehicles move between the Gardens and Dryholm in irregular patterns, carrying biological samples, maintenance drones, and occasionally, observers.

The water itself changes character along the descent. Near Dryholm, it maintains the expected properties of seawater—clear, cool, and moved primarily by surface-influenced currents. But as the depth increases, the water becomes progressively influenced by the thermal vents that sustain the Gardens. Temperature increases slightly, chemical composition shifts toward higher mineral content, and most notably, the water begins to carry the distinctive bioluminescent microorganisms that give the Gardens their ambient glow.

At seemingly random intervals, the non-water pockets from the Gardens detach and travel upward through the corridor, creating momentary spheres where normal physical laws appear suspended. Marine biologists who once studied the corridor theorized that these pockets serve as information transmission media—carrying complex chemical and quantum states that cannot be maintained in normal seawater. As these pockets rise, they gradually dissolve, releasing their altered contents into the surrounding ocean, which may explain the accelerating changes observed throughout the region.

The most troubling development in the corridor has been the recent appearance of hybrid organisms that seem to combine aspects of conventional marine life with the unique adaptations of Gardens species. These transitional creatures—like the ray-finned eels with integrated bioluminescent nervous systems, or the crustaceans developing rudimentary platform-building capabilities—suggest that the boundary between the two ecosystems is becoming increasingly permeable. Whether this represents natural evolution or deliberate intervention remains unclear.

Beneath the scientific structures of the corridor runs something older—a natural fault line in the continental shelf that channels geothermal energy from deep within the Earth's crust. This coincidental alignment is why the Pelagos Project selected this location initially; the natural heat and mineral-rich water provided ideal conditions for their experiments. But geological records suggest this fault has been active for millions of years, raising questions about whether the Gardens' unique ecosystem might have existed in some form long before human intervention.

Human presence in the corridor has dwindled to near zero, but evidence of monitoring continues. Autonomous drones bearing university research markings occasionally transit the upper reaches, collecting samples and observational data. These machines are treated with cautious tolerance by the corridor's inhabitants—neither welcomed nor actively destroyed, but often returned to the surface bearing modifications or attached specimens that weren't part of their original mission parameters.

The Architects have established outposts at strategic points along the corridor, particularly around the Junction. These colonies are smaller and less elaborate than their primary habitations in the Gardens, but serve as important forward positions for their ongoing study of the surface world. Young Architects often make pilgrimages up the corridor as part of their development, returning with new knowledge and occasionally artifacts recovered from Dryholm's periphery.

Most significantly, the corridor appears to be shortening. Precise bathymetric measurements taken over the past fifteen years show that the distance between Dryholm and the outermost reaches of the Gardens has decreased by nearly eight kilometers. This is not due to physical movement of either location, but rather the accelerating expansion of the Gardens' ecosystem up the corridor, while simultaneously, the specialized microorganisms from Dryholm's leaking containment systems have been migrating downward at an increasing rate.

The theoretical endpoint of this convergence—anticipated within the next three to five decades—has become the subject of intense calculation by both Dryholm's aging AI systems and the collective consciousness of the Architects. Both entities appear to be preparing for this eventual meeting of ecosystems, though with potentially different expectations for the outcome.

In the deepest security archives still functioning within Dryholm's central spire, one final recorded message from the project's scientific director remains, timestamped just hours before the evacuation: "The boundary was always artificial. We didn't create a new ecosystem; we merely awakened one that was waiting. The Architects aren't evolving toward intelligence—they're remembering it. When the waters of their world fully merge with ours, the remembering will be complete."
