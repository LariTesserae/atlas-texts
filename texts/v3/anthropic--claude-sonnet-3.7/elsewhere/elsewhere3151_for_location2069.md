# The Reservoir City

The city lies submerged beneath a vast, perfectly clear reservoir. Not drowned—built to be underwater. The water itself is cool but not cold, clean but not sterile, a perpetual medium through which life moves and light cascades in brilliant shafts from above.

The settlement occupies what was once a natural basin, now transformed into a deliberate underwater metropolis. Buildings rise from a gently curved depression in the earth, ancient stone foundations merged with industrial-era architecture that has been meticulously adapted for submersion. The structures cluster together with purpose—dense enough that one could traverse much of the city without touching the silty bottom, moving instead from balcony to bridge to tunnel.

Bright light penetrates the water with surprising intensity. The reservoir's surface, far above, has been engineered with enormous floating lenses—circular glass platforms that gather and direct sunlight downward in concentrated beams. These columns of illumination move slowly across the cityscape as the sun traverses the sky, creating a rhythmic dance of brightness and shadow that marks time in this waterlogged world.

The urban infrastructure follows a particular logic. Pneumatic tubes crisscross between buildings, carrying messages and small goods in bubbling bursts. Massive pumping stations regulate water flow, their mechanical arms and turbines in constant, deliberate motion. The hum of these machines resonates through the water—a deep, persistent thrum felt more than heard, punctuated by the higher-pitched whine of pressure valves releasing excess.

What little vegetation exists has been cultivated with purpose. Hardy water plants with silvery leaves cling to designated growing frames along the sides of buildings. These sparse gardens catch the directed light during their designated hours, photosynthesizing with remarkable efficiency. Their slow, undulating movement in the gentle currents creates the impression of breathing.

The city appears ancient in its erosion patterns. Every edge has been smoothed by water and time. Nothing here is sharp or new; even the most recently repaired sections quickly take on the patina of age, as though the water itself accelerates time's passage. Mineral deposits create abstract patterns across every surface—whorls and layers of sediment telling the story of centuries of submersion.

Most striking is the absence of fauna. No fish dart between buildings; no crustaceans scuttle along the pathways. This absence feels deliberate rather than tragic—as though the architects of this place designed it to be free of animal interference, focusing instead on the mechanical and human elements that maintain it.

The scale is contained, almost intimate. From the highest point—a central tower with a copper-domed observation chamber—one can see the entirety of the settlement and the boundaries where it gives way to the uniform blue of open water. This is not a sprawling metropolis but a concentrated node of habitation, every square meter utilized with purpose.

The water itself seems to be in perpetual circulation. Currents move through the city streets in predictable patterns, channeled by architectural features designed to direct flow. These underwater rivers carry small particles that catch the light—not dirt or contamination, but some kind of deliberate medium that sparkles and seems to communicate information through patterns visible only to trained observers.

Sound travels differently here. The mechanical operations of the city's maintenance systems create a constant background of noise—pumps, turbines, pressure equalizers all contributing to a symphony of industrial purpose. These sounds carry through the water with peculiar clarity, creating zones of different acoustic textures throughout the settlement.

The inhabitants, whoever they might be, have clearly adapted to this submerged existence over generations. Their absence from view suggests they might be inside the sealed buildings, observing through thick glass windows, or perhaps they visit only periodically to maintain the mechanisms that keep this underwater city functioning in their absence.

This is a place of deliberate submersion, not catastrophic flooding—a settlement that has embraced its watery medium and evolved to thrive within it, ancient in its foundations but perpetually maintained through industrial ingenuity, small in its footprint but dense in its complexity.

# The Sky Desert

Beyond the reservoir's edge, where the water meets its concrete boundaries and the enormous pumping stations draw and filter the surrounding watershed, lies a landscape that exists in stark opposition to the saturated world below. The Sky Desert stretches outward in all directions—a vast expanse of parched earth and withered vegetation that seems to absorb light rather than reflect it.

The transition is jarring. The reservoir's edge forms a perfect circle, its waters held back by a combination of ancient stone aqueducts and more modern concrete embankments rising thirty meters above the surrounding terrain. From this elevated boundary, one looks out upon a panorama of desolation, the horizon shimmering with heat distortion even in the cooler seasons.

This is not a natural desert but a manufactured one—the consequence of centuries of engineered water diversion. The soil is not sand but desiccated earth, cracked into geometric patterns like shattered pottery. Vegetation exists in sparse, deliberate arrangements: clusters of genetically modified succulents and thornbushes that grow in perfectly straight lines radiating outward from the reservoir like spokes from a hub. These plant formations follow the buried pipeline network that extends the reservoir's influence into the wasteland.

The sky above this desert is rarely blue. Instead, it maintains a perpetual amber hue, tinted by fine particulate matter that remains suspended in the atmosphere. This dust—composed partly of mineral deposits and partly of engineered reflective materials—creates a filtering effect on the sunlight. When observed from certain angles, this particulate layer displays subtle iridescence, shimmering with blues and greens that briefly mimic the colors of the water it surrounds.

Architectural remnants dot the landscape—the deteriorating husks of what were once outlying settlements. Unlike the meticulous preservation evident in the submerged city, these structures have been allowed to decay. Their partial walls and exposed foundations create shadow patterns across the cracked earth, marking time like enormous sundials as the amber light passes overhead. Some still bear faded inscriptions and symbolism matching motifs found in the underwater metropolis, suggesting they once belonged to the same civilization before the waters were divided.

The most prominent features of this landscape are the massive extraction towers—twelve enormous structures arranged in a perfect circle around the reservoir at a distance of exactly three kilometers. Each tower reaches over a hundred meters into the amber sky, its surface covered in complex arrangements of solar panels and atmospheric moisture collectors. These towers pulse with visible energy, particularly at dawn and dusk, when they emit synchronized flashes of blue-white light visible from both the desert and the depths of the reservoir.

Between these towers runs a single elevated rail line, suspended ten meters above the ground on slender pylons. A sleek, silver transport pod makes one complete circuit every twenty-eight hours, matching the internal time cycle of the submerged city. The pod never stops; it has no visible doors or windows, yet subtle variations in its speed suggest it may be delivering something—or someone—at designated points along its endless circuit.

The desert is not without its inhabitants. The Sky People make their homes in semi-nomadic encampments clustered around the extraction towers. Their settlements are distinct from both the submerged architecture and the abandoned ruins—lightweight structures of tensioned fabric and recycled materials designed to be dismantled and relocated with changing conditions. These dwellings catch and filter the amber light, creating habitable microclimates within.

The Sky People themselves are as adapted to their environment as the Immersed are to theirs. Tall and lean, with skin that has developed a protective patina resembling the iridescence of the atmospheric dust. Their eyes are narrow and deeply hooded to shield from the constant glare, and many have developed a specialized third eyelid—not for water protection like their submerged counterparts, but as a dust filter that gives their gaze a distinctive golden tint.

While the Immersed cultivate their silver-leaved aquatic plants, the Sky People maintain complex aerial gardens—vegetation that requires no soil, growing instead on mesh frameworks that capture atmospheric moisture. These plants have evolved to photosynthesize using the peculiar amber light, producing fruits and seeds with dehydration-resistant properties studied by surface scientists from more distant regions.

The most curious aspect of the Sky Desert is what locals call the "Rain Columns"—highly localized phenomena where water from the reservoir appears to defy gravity, rising in narrow, controlled streams from hidden outlets at the reservoir's edge. These columns of water, each about two meters in diameter, rise up to fifty meters before dispersing into a fine mist that creates temporary zones of humidity in the otherwise arid landscape. The columns appear at irregular intervals that have, thus far, defied prediction by outside observers, though the Sky People seem to anticipate them with remarkable accuracy.

# The Confluence

Between these two worlds—one defined by total immersion and the other by its absence—exists a third space where water and aridity meet in perpetual negotiation. The Confluence occupies the thirty-meter-high boundary wall of the reservoir, a circular interface that extends both above and below the waterline.

This boundary is not merely a wall but a complex, inhabited structure—a ring-shaped settlement with chambers and passages running throughout its considerable thickness. The wall itself is constructed from a material not found elsewhere in either realm: a porous, living stone that appears to have been cultivated rather than quarried. This biocrete, as the inhabitants call it, regulates water passage at a molecular level, allowing controlled seepage that maintains precise humidity gradients throughout the structure.

The Confluence is divided into vertical zones. The deepest sections, fully submerged in the reservoir, house the massive filtration systems that keep the city's waters pristine. These chambers are inhabited primarily by a specialized subset of the Immersed called the Boundary Keepers—individuals who have undergone additional adaptations allowing them to withstand greater pressure variations. Their bodies bear more pronounced webbing and their skin contains higher concentrations of the silver particles found in their cultivated plants.

At the waterline itself lies the Exchange—a series of partially submerged chambers where the Immersed and Sky People meet for carefully regulated interactions. These spaces feature graduated pools and humidity-controlled air pockets where representatives from both societies can exist in relative comfort. The architecture here is distinctive—neither fully aquatic nor fully terrestrial, but a hybrid form with sliding panels and adjustable environments that can accommodate different physiological needs.

Above the waterline, extending to the top of the wall, are the Atmospheric Works—the control centers for the Rain Columns and the regulatory systems that maintain the amber particulate layer above the desert. These chambers are primarily inhabited by Sky People technicians, though they regularly coordinate with Immersed Flow Engineers through specialized communication systems at the Exchange level.

The most remarkable feature of the Confluence is the Remembering Pool—a perfectly circular chamber at the exact center of the wall, half-filled with reservoir water. The pool's surface is exactly level with the reservoir itself, creating the illusion that they are continuous, though specialized membranes prevent actual mixing. Above the water, the chamber extends upward in a perfect cylinder open to the amber sky. The water in this pool contains the highest concentration of the light-catching particles found throughout the reservoir, making it appear almost solid with luminescence when the sun reaches its zenith.

It is here, at the Remembering Pool, that the true nature of the relationship between these two worlds becomes clear. Once every twenty-eight days, when the circulating transport pod completes a precise number of circuits around the desert perimeter, the eldest members of both societies gather at this chamber. The Still Waters are brought from the central tower in specially designed submersibles, while the Sky Elders arrive via the rail system. Together, they participate in a ceremony that appears to involve the exchange of genetic material—the Sky People contributing skin cells and hair that are ritually immersed in the pool, while the Immersed offer carefully cultivated coral-algae hybrids that are exposed to the open air.

This exchange reveals the secret that binds these seemingly opposed worlds: they are not separate civilizations but a single, bifurcated society—one that deliberately divided itself centuries ago to pursue parallel evolutionary paths. The Immersed and Sky People are relatives engaged in a multi-generational adaptation experiment, maintaining genetic diversity between their populations while developing specialized traits suited to their respective environments.

The extraction towers surrounding the desert are not primarily taking from the land but giving to it—gradually rehydrating the terrain through the Rain Columns according to a timetable measured in centuries rather than years. The amber particulate layer is not natural atmospheric dust but an engineered sunscreen, protecting the developing landscape during its transition. Meanwhile, the reservoir itself is not merely a habitat but a controlled evolutionary environment, its waters specially formulated to encourage specific adaptations in its inhabitants.

Communication between these realms is constant but subtle. The particle patterns in the reservoir water that seem to transmit messages are, in fact, connected to fluctuations in the desert's amber atmosphere—the two systems encoding information that passes continuously between water and air. The pneumatic tube system of the submerged city finds its counterpart in the rail line circling the desert, both forming a continuous loop of material exchange.

The ultimate goal of this divided society remains known only to the elders who meet at the Remembering Pool, but ancient inscriptions found in both realms hint at a shared vision: the development of human adaptations capable of thriving in multiple environments, preparing for some future necessity only partly understood by current generations. The regular genetic exchange ensures that neither population diverges too far from the other—that someday, when their respective evolutionary journeys reach their conclusion, reunification will remain possible.

For now, the Immersed continue their water-bound existence, their bodies growing more efficient at processing dissolved oxygen with each generation. The Sky People cultivate increasing tolerance to aridity and radiation, their aerial gardens yielding increasingly drought-resistant nutrition. And between them, the Confluence maintains the delicate balance of their separated but interconnected worlds, preserving the memory of their shared origin and the possibility of their eventual reunion.
