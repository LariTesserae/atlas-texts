# The Surface Above

Above the Undercity of Grath-Mol, where the Grathkin believe nothing exists but myth, sprawls the Canopy Kingdom of Vash-Tair. Where Grath-Mol is stone and water, Vash-Tair is wood and wind, a city built entirely in the branches of colossal trees that have long since fused into a single, vast organism.

These ancient trees—called Grandfather Pines by the locals—reach heights of over a thousand feet, their trunks wider than village squares. Their bark has deepened to the color of dried blood, furrowed with canyons that could hide a person. Unlike normal conifers, they've evolved a lateral growth pattern, sending massive branches horizontally for hundreds of feet before these limbs themselves sprout secondary trunks downward, creating a self-reinforcing latticework across the entire mountain range.

The people of Vash-Tair, the Vashkin, have built their civilization on and within this living architecture. Their dwellings are partly constructed, partly grown—chambers hollowed from the heartwood of the largest branches, outer shells encouraged through decades of guided growth. Bridges of woven vines and living wood connect these aerial neighborhoods, swaying gently in the constant winds that rush through the canopy. The buildings spiral upward along the trunks, linked by stairs carved directly into the living wood, polished by generations of bare feet.

Unlike the mineral blue-green glow of Grath-Mol, Vash-Tair bathes in dappled emerald light filtered through translucent needles the size of human fingers. This light shifts constantly with the movement of branches and clouds, creating a rippling effect across all surfaces that makes the entire city seem to breathe. When wind surges through the canopy, the sound is like an immense choir drawing breath.

The Vashkin are tall and lean, with skin that ranges from amber to deep green. Many bear birthmarks that resemble wood grain, which they enhance through tattooing rituals in adolescence. Their eyes are remarkable—flecked with photosynthetic cells that grant peripheral vision sensitive to the slightest movement, an adaptation for life among swaying branches. They dress in loose garments woven from processed pine fiber, dyed in greens and golds that help them blend with their environment.

Where the Grathkin society centers around water management, Vashkin culture revolves around wind cultivation. They've developed complex systems of fabric sails and wooden funnels to direct airflow through their city, driving their machinery and carrying messages through pneumatic tube systems. Specialized musicians play wind organs built into the city's structure, their melodies carrying for miles when atmospheric conditions are right. During major storms, "wind dancers" perform on platforms at the canopy's edge, their choreography both art form and meteorological record.

Religion in Vash-Tair venerates the Breath Cycle—the belief that all living things exchange essence through the medium of air. Their most sacred ritual is the Exhalation Festival, where the entire population gathers to breathe in unison, alternating deep inhalations and forceful exhalations that supposedly regenerate the community's collective spirit. They collect morning dew in ceremonial bowls, believing it contains concentrated dreams that can be inhaled for prophecy.

Unlike the structured castes of Grath-Mol, Vash-Tair society operates through a system of overlapping guilds. The Sap-Speakers communicate with the trees through a process of tapping and tasting that borders on communion. Needle-Weavers create the intricate textiles that serve as both art and engineering. Wind-Catchers maintain the city's elaborate ventilation systems. Guild membership is fluid, with citizens often apprenticing in multiple disciplines throughout their lives.

The higher one lives in Vash-Tair, the greater their social standing. The oldest and most respected families reside in dwellings near the crown of the tallest trees, where they maintain observatories of polished resin that magnify the stars. These Elder Houses trace their lineages back thousands of years, claiming descent from the first humans who learned to communicate with the Grandfather Pines.

Water, so central to Grath-Mol, is relatively scarce in Vash-Tair. The Vashkin collect it from morning condensation, rare rainfall, and carefully managed catchment systems. They treat water as precious medicine rather than infrastructure, measuring it in drops and vials rather than channels and cisterns. They know nothing of the vast hydraulic network beneath their feet, just as they've forgotten what lies below the trees altogether.

# The Connection

Between these two realms—one of perpetual darkness and flowing water, the other of filtered light and rushing wind—lies the Forgotten Stratum. Once a thriving middle kingdom where the two civilizations met and traded, now it exists as a haunted, misremembered space separating worlds that have forgotten each other.

The Forgotten Stratum consists of the mountain's central region—vast caverns where the tree roots reach downward and the ancient water pipes lead upward. The massive roots of the Grandfather Pines have grown around and through the uppermost chambers of Grath-Mol's infrastructure, sometimes cracking pipes, other times incorporating them into their woody mass. In places, tree roots have absorbed sections of copper pipe into their cellular structure, creating hybrid conduits that carry both water and sap.

This middle realm is illuminated by a mix of light sources: faint blue-green glow rising from Grathkin crystals below, weak emerald sunlight filtering down through rare gaps in the canopy above, and the bioluminescent fungi that thrive in this interstitial zone. The fungi here grow larger than in either realm above or below, forming towering structures like melted cathedral pillars, pulsing with gentle light in colors that have no name.

The air in the Forgotten Stratum tastes of both worlds—the mineral dampness of Grath-Mol mixing with the resinous oxygen of Vash-Tair. Mist forms where these atmospheres meet, creating a perpetual fog that hangs in distinct layers, sometimes flowing like slow rivers through the air. Sound behaves strangely here—voices echo too long or fall dead without resonance, and occasionally sounds from either realm above or below will suddenly amplify through natural acoustic phenomena, carrying fragments of Vashkin wind songs or Grathkin pipe-rhythms to ears that no longer recognize their origin.

The ruins of an ancient civilization lie scattered throughout the Forgotten Stratum—stone buildings with both below-ground foundations and above-ground timber frames, suggesting a people who moved comfortably between worlds. Fragments of text in a script neither modern civilization can read hint at a time when the Vashkin and Grathkin were one people, before some cataclysm or deliberate schism divided them.

Most striking are the meeting halls—circular chambers where stone seats face wooden ones across central platforms, clearly designed for dialogue between different peoples. The largest such hall contains a mechanism of incredible complexity—part water wheel, part wind turbine—that once converted the power of flowing water to that of moving air and back again, a technology now lost to both realms.

Over centuries, both civilizations have mythologized what lies beyond their domains. To the Grathkin, the ceiling of their world occasionally leaks strange debris—fragments of green matter they cannot identify as pine needles, having never seen a plant. They incorporate these into protective amulets, believing they fall from the Overworld, a spiritual realm where ancestors go after fully petrifying. Some Grathkin religious texts speak of a time when water flowed upward as well as around, but these passages are now interpreted metaphorically.

The Vashkin similarly mythologize the depths. They tell children stories of the Root Realm, where spirits tend the unseen foundations of their world. When they occasionally discover ancient pipes exposed by erosion or root growth, they repurpose them as wind instruments or speaking tubes, never suspecting their original hydraulic purpose. A few Vashkin scholars collect folklore about "the people below the roots" but consider these tales metaphors for the unconscious mind rather than literal truth.

Yet connection persists in subtle ways. The central water system of Grath-Mol still sends water upward through ancient pipes, though these now feed directly into the root systems of the Grandfather Pines rather than human settlements. The trees have grown dependent on this consistent water source, their evolution shaped by its mineral content. Meanwhile, the complex ventilation system of Vash-Tair inadvertently creates pressure changes that affect water flow in the pipes below, sometimes causing the mysterious fluctuations that Grathkin Conduit-Speakers interpret as omens.

Most poignantly, both civilizations share one tradition they no longer recognize as common. The Grathkin's sacred ceremony of dropping a stone into perfectly still water parallels the Vashkin ritual of releasing a pine cone to fall through still air. Both practices create ripples in their respective mediums—concentric circles in water below, spiraling currents in air above—which practitioners "read" for guidance. Neither people remembers that these were once two halves of a single divination method, performed together by travelers moving between realms.

In recent decades, small changes have begun to accelerate. In Grath-Mol, some pipes have started delivering water with traces of an unfamiliar, resinous taste—sap leaking from roots that have penetrated the system. In Vash-Tair, certain wind currents carry mineral scents and occasionally deposit fine silt that has been carried upward through forgotten ventilation shafts. A handful of curious souls in each realm have begun to investigate these anomalies, following pipes or roots toward their source.

The Forgotten Stratum stirs with their approach. Fungi pulse brighter when footsteps approach from either direction. Ancient mechanisms, dormant for centuries, collect enough energy from water and wind to turn a few degrees, shedding dust as gears engage for the first time in memory.

The grand calculation of Grath-Mol's hydraulic computer nears completion just as Vash-Tair's wind-readers detect patterns suggesting imminent change. Neither civilization yet understands that their systems were designed to function together—that the water-driven computation below and the wind-carried information above are two halves of a single process, meant to reach simultaneous conclusions that only make sense when combined.

When representatives of these long-separated peoples finally meet in the misty chambers of the Forgotten Stratum, they will find more than each other. They will discover their shared past, and the reason their ancestors built these mirrored civilizations of water and wind, stone and wood—a purpose encoded in the very infrastructure of their worlds, waiting for the day when the divided knowledge would finally flow together once more.
