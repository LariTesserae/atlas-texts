# Between the Mountain and the Valley: The Mistwash

Where the steep slopes of the Serpent's Spine begin to gentle into foothills, there exists a transitional zone neither fully of the mountain nor truly of the lowlands. The locals call it the Mistwash—a perpetually sodden belt of land that circles the mountain like a loose collar, catching the constant runoff from Glimmerwick above.

The landscape here is dominated by a chaotic network of braided streams, some merely finger-width trickles while others swell seasonally to rushing torrents. These waters carve ever-changing channels through peaty soil, creating a maze-like terrain of hummocks and hollow depressions. The ground itself seems alive—breathing and shifting as waterlogged earth collapses and reforms with each new rain or meltwater surge from above.

Vegetation here is riotous but specialized. Massive cushion plants form islands of relative stability, their densely packed stems creating living platforms that can support weight even as the ground beneath them turns to slurry. Carnivorous plants thrive in this environment—not the delicate, insect-catching varieties of drier regions, but robust species with pitcher-like traps large enough to digest small mammals that mistake them for shelter during storms. Most distinctive are the "memory reeds"—tall, flexible grasses that bend without breaking in the frequent downpours, only to return precisely to their original position once the deluge passes, creating stable pathways that locals can identify even when submerged beneath floodwaters.

The air here is thick with moisture, but unlike the fine mist of Glimmerwick above, it manifests as fat, lazy droplets that hang suspended for minutes before finally surrendering to gravity. These droplets refract light in peculiar ways, creating phantom rainbows that appear and vanish without warning. Sound behaves strangely too—sometimes carrying for impossible distances, other times being absorbed completely by the dense atmosphere just feet from its source.

Few permanent structures exist in the Mistwash. Instead, the primary inhabitants—known as the Rivermen—construct temporary shelters from waterproof hides stretched over frameworks of flexible memory reeds. These dwellings move with the landscape rather than resisting it, designed to float should their foundations become suddenly submerged.

The Rivermen themselves are as adaptable as their homes. Tall and lean, their skin has a faintly blue undertone, waterproof and slightly oily to the touch. Their feet are broad and splayed, distributing weight effectively on unstable terrain. Most distinctive are their eyes—large and round with horizontal pupils like those of goats, providing excellent peripheral vision to navigate the constantly changing landscape. They move with a peculiar gait—part stride, part bound—that allows them to traverse the unpredictable terrain without sinking into saturated soil or disturbing the delicate equilibrium of floating vegetation mats.

These people are consummate middlemen, facilitators of the rare exchanges between the mountain-dwelling Myrsha and the distant valley settlements. They speak a unique pidgin language incorporating elements from both cultures, supplemented with whistles that mimic the calls of local birds and carry effectively through the thick air. Their entire society is organized around water flow—not controlling it as the Myrsha do, but reading and responding to it. Status is determined by one's ability to predict which channels will flood next, which paths will remain passable, which areas will offer the best fishing or foraging as the waters rise and recede.

The Rivermen cultivate no crops in the conventional sense but are expert tenders of the semi-aquatic resources native to their realm. They harvest the protein-rich seeds of floating grass mats, collect eggs from the amphibious birds that nest in the tallest reeds, and maintain carefully managed pools where they farm luminous salamanders similar to but distinct from those found in Glimmerwick—these specimens larger and capable of limited terrestrial movement during the wettest periods.

Their most valuable trade resource is "mistwater"—collected from specific locations where mountain runoff mixes with mineral-rich springs. This liquid is prized by valley dwellers for its purported medicinal properties, particularly its ability to treat respiratory ailments and extend youthful appearance when consumed regularly. The Rivermen transport it in containers made from the bladders of marsh beasts, preserved using techniques known only to their shamans.

# The Connection: The Living Waters

The relationship between Glimmerwick and the Mistwash is neither simple nor static—it flows and changes like the waters that physically connect them. These waters themselves form the primary link between mountain and foothills, carrying more than mere moisture as they descend.

The most visible connectors are the Seven Sisters—major waterfalls that cascade from Glimmerwick's lowest tier into deep, misty pools at the upper edge of the Mistwash. These falls roar with such intensity during peak flow that they generate their own weather systems, creating localized rainstorms that can last for days. The pools they feed—called the Cauldrons—are sites of tremendous significance for both cultures.

For the Myrsha, these falls represent their city's contribution to the wider world. Carefully engineered channels direct specific water flows to each of the Seven Sisters—one carrying mineral-rich water from the eastern hot springs; another filtered through beds of medicinal fungi; a third passing through chambers where elders sing to the water, imbuing it with what they consider beneficial vibrations. The waters that leave Glimmerwick are not simply runoff but deliberate offerings, each with specific properties the Myrsha believe benefit those who live below.

At regular intervals determined by their fungal-growth calendar, small groups of Myrsha make the perilous journey down to the Cauldrons, transporting sealed containers of specially cultivated fungi and crystalline deposits left by the Mist Leviathans. These expeditions are led by Water-Speakers—specialists who have developed heightened resistance to the drier conditions of the descent. At the Cauldrons, they perform elaborate ceremonies, introducing these materials into the pools in precisely measured amounts, believing they are maintaining the health of the entire watershed.

The Rivermen observe these ceremonies from a respectful distance, having learned through generations of experience that the Myrsha's water rituals genuinely affect the quality and behavior of the flows that sustain the Mistwash. In the days following these ceremonies, certain pools develop enhanced properties—waters that heal wounds without scarring, waters that temporarily enhance night vision when splashed on the face, waters that preserve food without changing its flavor.

Physical exchanges between the cultures occur primarily at designated trading outposts built on relatively stable ground near the Cauldrons. These outposts feature distinctive architecture that accommodates both peoples—partially enclosed spaces maintaining the high humidity the Myrsha require, alongside more open areas where the Rivermen can move freely. Trade items flow in both directions: the Myrsha primarily seeking salts, pigments, and certain aquatic proteins unavailable on their mountain, while the Rivermen covet the specialized fungi, tools made from leviathan tears, and the intricate water-filtering technologies developed in Glimmerwick.

But perhaps the most important connection between these realms comes in the form of the children. Occasionally, Myrsha infants are born without the ability to form proper symbiotic relationships with their illuminating fungi—a condition they call "shadow-soul." In earlier times, such children rarely survived in the light-dependent culture of Glimmerwick. Now, through ancient arrangement, these children are brought down to the Cauldrons and given to the Rivermen, who raise them as their own.

Similarly, some Rivermen children are born with unusually permeable skin that cannot develop the waterproof qualities needed to thrive in the perpetually sodden Mistwash. These "dry-skins" are carried up to Glimmerwick in specialized mist-palanquins, where they are adopted into Myrsha families, their unusual dermal structure proving ideal for hosting the most elaborate and beautiful fungal patterns.

These exchanged children grow up between worlds—never fully of either culture but vital to maintaining relations between them. As adults, many become intermediaries who can physically tolerate both environments for limited periods, facilitating trade and diplomacy. Some develop into visionaries and innovators, bringing fresh perspectives to traditional practices. The oldest and wisest eventually take on the role of "Watershed Guardians"—respected elders who arbitrate disputes over water usage and ensure that the delicate hydrological balance between mountain and foothills remains intact.

The mythology surrounding this arrangement is rich with symbolism. Both cultures share a foundational story of the Great Separation—a time when mountain and valley were one environment before an ancient cataclysm (described variously as a battle between primordial entities or a cosmic readjustment) created the dramatic vertical division of the landscape. In these stories, the Mistwash represents the memory of unity—a place where elements of both worlds mingle and transform—while the exchanged children symbolize the potential for eventual reunification.

This relationship is not without tension. During rare drought years, when the flows diminish, the Rivermen sometimes accuse the Myrsha of hoarding water or failing to perform their maintenance rituals correctly. Conversely, during periods of exceptional rainfall, the Myrsha may blame the Rivermen for improper stewardship of the Cauldrons, believing that mismanagement below affects the delicate hydrological systems above. These disputes rarely escalate to outright conflict but manifest in temporarily restricted trade or modified ceremonial practices.

Most fascinating is how the two cultures interpret the Mist Leviathans differently while acknowledging the same phenomenon. To the Myrsha, these entities are ancient architects of the mountain; to the Rivermen, they are water-spirits that migrate seasonally between mountain and distant ocean. When the massive shadow forms occasionally drift down from Glimmerwick into the Mistwash, the Rivermen perform their own ceremonies—not of reverence but of navigation, using the movements of these beings to predict major shifts in water patterns months in advance.

The waters that connect these worlds carry more than dissolved minerals and organic matter—they transport ideas, beliefs, genetic material through traded foods, microbial communities that affect both environments, and subtle chemical signals from the unique biota of each region. Neither culture would survive unaltered without this exchange. The Myrsha's water management systems depend on the regular drainage created by the Mistwash's absorbent landscape, while the Rivermen's entire way of life is determined by the carefully maintained flows from above.

Together, they form not two separate societies but a single adaptive system spread across a dramatic ecological gradient—mountain and foothills, mist and marsh, fungi-folk and river-people—connected by the living waters that gather in the heights of Glimmerwick and disperse through the braided channels of the Mistwash, eventually reaching distant valleys and seas beyond, carrying with them the mingled influence of both worlds.
