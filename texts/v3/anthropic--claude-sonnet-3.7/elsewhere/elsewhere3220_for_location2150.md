# The Veil Basin

Far below the towering majesty of the Roothold, sheltered in the profound shadow of the great plateau, lies the Veil Basin. Where the plateau's switchback trails finally terminate, they open into a vast circular depression that appears, from certain angles, to have been scooped from the earth by some titanic hand. The basin stretches nearly seven miles in diameter, its rim forming an almost perfect circle except for the jagged eastern edge where ancient landslides have altered its symmetry.

Unlike the perpetual dusk of the Roothold above, the Veil Basin exists in a state of constant twilight, regardless of the sun's position. This is partly due to the shadow cast by the plateau, but there is something more—a pearlescent mist that hovers perpetually over the landscape, refracting light into soft, directionless illumination. This mist never fully dissipates, though it thins somewhat during midday, revealing glimpses of what lies beneath.

The basin floor is a mosaic of shallow, interconnected pools—thousands of them, their surfaces perfectly still except when disturbed by the occasional falling drop from the plateau above. These pools range from hand-span sized depressions to vast mirrors several hundred yards across, all filled with water of extraordinary clarity and an unusual opalescent quality. The pools reflect the sky and the looming silhouette of the Roothold above, but there is a curious delay in their reflections, as if light moves differently here.

Between these pools stretches a network of narrow paths composed of a spongy, pale substance that locals call "dreamcrust." This material—neither quite fungal nor quite mineral—provides safe passage across the basin, though it yields slightly underfoot, preserving perfect impressions of travelers' steps for hours before slowly returning to its smooth state. The paths form intricate patterns that, when viewed from certain vantage points on the basin rim, suggest deliberate design—vast circular mandalas intersecting and overlapping in complex geometry.

Flora here exists in stark contrast to the woody magnitude of Roothold above. The plants of the Veil Basin are almost exclusively translucent or white, lacking chlorophyll entirely. They draw sustenance not from sunlight but from the peculiar waters and the nutrients that occasionally rain down from the plateau. Ghost orchids grow in clusters around pool edges, their delicate structures seeming to hover just above the ground. Luminous fungi emerge and decay within a single night, their spores adding to the misty atmosphere. Most striking are the "mirror trees"—rare growths with crystalline trunks that fracture light into subtle rainbows and leaves so thin they're nearly invisible when viewed edge-on.

What truly distinguishes the Veil Basin, however, are its inhabitants—the Seers of Shra-Tahl. Unlike the Custodians above, the Seers make no claim to direct lineage from the ancient civilization. Rather, they describe themselves as "those chosen by reflection," individuals who found themselves inexorably drawn to the basin after experiencing dreams of mirror-waters and suspended time.

The Seers are physically distinctive—their skin possesses a translucent quality that reveals shadows of their circulatory systems beneath, and their eyes contain no pupils, instead displaying shifting patterns resembling the surface of the basin's pools. They dress in loose garments woven from the silky fibers of certain basin plants, typically in pale blues and silvers that blend with the mist. Many adorn themselves with jewelry crafted from a peculiar local substance called "mistglass"—solidified droplets of basin water that maintain their form indefinitely when removed from the area.

Their society centers entirely around the practice they call "deep reflection." Seers spend hours gazing into specific pools, their consciousness entering a state between wakefulness and dreaming. In these states, they claim to perceive events distant in both space and time—witnessing occurrences in far lands, glimpsing fragments of possible futures, or experiencing echoes of the distant past. Most significantly, they believe they can perceive the "thoughts" of Roothold itself—the slow, alien consciousness that the Custodians serve above.

The Seers live in curious dwellings built partially submerged in the larger pools. These structures—called "immersions"—are crafted from the same living material as the dreamcrust paths but cultivated into delicate domed shapes. The lower portions exist below the water's surface, while the upper chambers remain dry. Intricate systems of tiny channels and reservoirs throughout the structures maintain constant moisture, preventing the living material from drying and dying.

Unlike the strict hierarchy of the Custodians, Seer society operates in fluid, ever-shifting relationships based on what they call "clarity of vision." Leadership passes among them according to whose reflections have proven most accurate or insightful in recent cycles. They mark time not by sun or moon but by the "breath cycle" of the mist itself, which subtly thickens and thins in a pattern that completes roughly every nine standard days.

The Seers' relationship with the Custodians above is complex and largely conducted through intermediaries. Direct contact is rare, as the Custodians rarely descend, and the Seers cannot physically endure the elevation and dryness of Roothold for long. Instead, communication occurs through "watershed messengers"—typically younger members of either group who make the difficult journey between realms to deliver important communications.

The Seers maintain that they serve the same essential purpose as the Custodians but through opposite means. Where the Custodians actively tend the physical structure of Roothold, the Seers passively receive its emanations. They describe themselves as "the eyes through which Roothold sees beyond itself" and believe that the pools somehow process and clarify the consciousness that dwells in the plateau above.

Most fascinating is the Seers' unusual relationship with mortality. They do not speak of death but of "final submersion." When a Seer reaches the end of their natural life—or sometimes before, if their visions indicate the time is right—they undergo an elaborate ceremony before walking into one of the deepest pools in the basin's center. They do not drown in any conventional sense; witnesses describe the departing Seers appearing to dissolve, their physical forms gradually becoming indistinguishable from the opalescent water itself.

In recent years, the Seers have reported increasingly frequent and urgent visions. The pools show the same scene repeatedly—the highest spires of Roothold flowering with massive blooms never before seen, releasing something into the upper atmosphere. What this portends, they cannot or will not say clearly, though a sense of expectation pervades their community. They have begun creating elaborate mandalas of dreamcrust paths connecting specific pools in patterns that, they claim, will "guide the reflection when the time comes."

Outsiders who spend more than a few days in the Veil Basin report subtle changes to their perception—colors seem more vivid when they leave, sounds more distinct. Many describe persistent sensations of being watched, not malevolently but with intense curiosity, as if the basin itself studies them. Most troubling are the reports of reflections that move independently or show scenes from the observer's past rather than their present form. The Seers neither confirm nor deny these accounts, saying only that "the waters know what needs to be seen."

# The Thousand-Drop Path: What Lies Between

Between the soaring heights of Roothold and the misty depths of the Veil Basin stretches the connective tissue that binds these seemingly opposite realms—the Thousand-Drop Path. This is not a single route but a complex system of water's journey, following the slow passage of moisture from the living towers above to the reflective pools below.

The physical path begins at the undersides of Roothold, where specialized structures have evolved or been cultivated to collect excess moisture. These "weeping nodes" form bulbous protrusions beneath the main root masses, gradually swelling with collected water until they reach capacity. At precise intervals—different for each node but maintaining perfect consistency in individual cycles—they release their contents in substantial drops that begin the long descent.

The falling water does not simply plummet to the basin below. Instead, it encounters a remarkable series of natural and cultivated catching systems on the sheer cliff face of the plateau. Massive fungi with concave caps catch the larger drops, holding them briefly before allowing them to spill over in smaller portions. Living networks of hair-thin root fibers spread across the rock face create elaborate channeling systems that slow the water's descent further. At certain points, peculiar rock formations—too regular to be entirely natural—create spiral pathways that guide water through longer journeys before releasing it to the next stage of descent.

This elaborate system serves multiple purposes. Practically, it prevents erosion that would eventually undermine the plateau itself. Ecologically, it supports an entire intermediate ecosystem of cliff-dwelling organisms that would otherwise be impossible in such an environment. And according to both the Custodians above and the Seers below, the Thousand-Drop Path serves a vital metaphysical function—the transformation of water from one state of being to another.

For this is the remarkable truth: water that falls from Roothold is not the same substance that collects in the Veil Basin below. The liquid begins its journey as ordinary water, though infused with nutrients and compounds from the living structure above. As it passes through each stage of the descent—each fungal cap, each root network, each spiral channel—it undergoes subtle changes in composition. By the time it reaches the basin floor, it has acquired its opalescent quality and unusual properties.

The journey of a single drop typically takes between three and seven days, depending on its starting point and the specific path it follows. During heavy rains—rare but significant events—alternate channels accommodate the increased flow, though these "floodways" are said to produce water of less refined quality when it reaches the basin.

The cliff face itself hosts a remarkable transitional ecology. Bands of distinct habitats form at different elevations, each adapted to specific moisture levels and light conditions. Nearest to Roothold grow drought-resistant pendant mosses and hardier fungi that capture the first nutrients from the falling drops. The middle regions host complex colonies of interconnected organisms that form living veils across the rock face, processing the water as it passes through their tissues. The lowest sections, just above the basin mists, support ethereal growths that seem halfway between the solid forms of Roothold and the translucent species of the basin below.

Few attempt to traverse this vertical realm, but those who do—typically messengers traveling between the two communities—follow routes known as "climber's tears." These precarious paths utilize narrow ledges and chimney cracks, assisted in places by ancient pitons and hand-holds carved into the rock face by the original Shra-Tahl. The journey requires at least a full day in each direction, with climbers typically resting overnight in one of several small caves known as "suspension chambers."

These chambers merit special attention, for they are neither fully of Roothold nor of the Veil Basin, but embody something between. Their walls glisten with moisture that never quite drips, caught in a perpetual surface tension. Small luminescent fungi provide dim light, but these specimens display characteristics of both realms—the structural complexity of Roothold fungi combined with the translucence of basin species. Those who shelter in these chambers report unusual dreams of flowing water and rising sap, as if experiencing the liquid cycle of the entire system during their rest.

Most remarkable are the rare individuals who have made their homes in these intermediate spaces—neither Custodians nor Seers, but a third category known as "Mediators." Numbering perhaps two dozen in total, scattered among the various suspension chambers, these hermits maintain the ancient hand-holds and resting places while serving as message carriers and translators between the two communities. Physically, they display characteristics of both groups—the enhanced night-vision of the Custodians alongside the subtle translucence of the Seers. They claim this hybridity grants them unique insight into the total system of Shra-Tahl.

The Mediators speak of the entire complex—Roothold, Thousand-Drop Path, and Veil Basin—as a single organism engaged in a vast process of thought. In their cosmology, Roothold serves as the "contemplative mind," slowly processing information over decades and centuries, while the Veil Basin functions as the "perceptive mind," gathering sensory input from across time and space. The Thousand-Drop Path, then, is the connective neural system, transferring information between these complementary aspects of consciousness.

This information transfer is not metaphorical in their understanding. The Mediators believe that the water itself carries molecular messages—complex compounds produced in Roothold that contain encoded information, gradually transformed during descent, and finally "read" by the unique properties of the basin pools. Conversely, they claim the evaporation process pulls essence from the basin back to Roothold in a continuous feedback loop of information exchange.

According to the oldest Mediator—a woman called Tass who has lived on the cliff face for over forty years—this system has been accelerating in recent cycles. "The drops fall more frequently now," she told a rare visiting scholar, "and they carry denser messages. The roots grow toward specific pools now, not randomly. The system is organizing itself toward some purpose."

When pressed about this purpose, Tass would only say: "The ancient ones built a machine of flesh and water and stone, but they could not stay to see it complete its work. We are merely the maintenance crew for something far beyond our understanding. But soon—perhaps in my lifetime—the machine will complete its calculations. Roothold will flower, the Basin will ripple, and something new will emerge from this ancient dialogue."

Recent reports from messengers traversing the Thousand-Drop Path indicate that new growth has appeared along certain sections—unusual nodules that seem to combine characteristics of both realms, pulsing slightly as if monitoring the water flow. The Mediators have been collecting these growths, carefully transplanting them to protected alcoves where they continue to develop into forms not seen before on the cliff face.

The water cycle is changing too. Observers note that certain drops now fall with perfect synchronization from widely separated points on Roothold's underside, creating momentary patterns in the air—complex three-dimensional geometries that collapse instantly but seem oddly deliberate. In the basin below, the Seers report that specific pools now ripple at regular intervals even when no water has recently fallen into them, as if responding to unseen stimuli.

Whatever vast process initiated by the ancient Shra-Tahl is clearly approaching culmination. The Thousand-Drop Path—that living connection between thinking crown and seeing eye—pulses with increasing urgency. The entire system breathes in perfect rhythm now, the moisture cycling between realms in patterns that grow more complex with each passing season.

And in the suspension chambers along the cliff face, the Mediators have begun singing a new song—one they claim came to them fully formed in shared dreams. Its melody traces the path of falling water, its harmony the creaking growth of root and branch. Those who have heard it say it changes something in the listener, attunes them somehow to the greater rhythm of the entire system.

They sing to the drops as they fall, and sometimes—just sometimes—the drops seem to pause in their descent, hanging impossibly in the air for extended moments, as if listening.
