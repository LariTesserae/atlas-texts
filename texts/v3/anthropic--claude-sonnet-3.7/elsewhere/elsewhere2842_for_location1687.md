# The Plains of Diminishing Return

In the high plains of what they once called the Basin, the sky takes up more than its fair share of the world. It stretches impossibly far in all directions, a vast dome of pale blue-gray that seems to press down on everything beneath it. The land itself has been worn so flat over eons that perspective plays tricks—distant features appear to float above the horizon, disconnected from the ground that bears them.

The ancient land has been scraped and scoured by time until almost nothing remains. What was once a mountain range has been reduced to a series of low, wide terraces that step down so gradually you wouldn't notice the descent if you walked it. The stone is exposed in great sheets, polished smooth as slate tabletops, with colors ranging from rust-red to smoke-gray to a peculiar mottled green—the ghosts of minerals that have almost, but not quite, been leached away over millions of years.

Water exists here in a state of perpetual transience. The air carries a persistent dampness that condenses on every surface in the cold hours before dawn, forming beads that never quite become droplets. This moisture is enough to sustain the sparse vegetation—low, tough grasses that grow in isolated tufts, their roots somehow finding purchase in near-invisible cracks in the rock. Pale green lichen forms perfect circles on the stone, some as small as coins, others spanning meters, their edges so precisely drawn they appear artificial.

At irregular intervals, perhaps once every fifteen or twenty days, it rains. Not the gentle patter of temperate lands but a methodical, almost mechanical downpour that lasts exactly forty-seven minutes. The locals set their timepieces by it. During these rains, the flat stone landscape transforms. Every slight depression becomes a temporary pool; every subtle channel carved over millennia becomes a short-lived stream. For a few hours afterward, the Basin gleams like polished obsidian, reflecting the sky so perfectly that walking feels like floating.

The fauna is sparse but persistent. Colonies of small, silver-furred creatures—something between rabbits and meerkats—emerge from narrow burrows drilled impossibly into the hard stone. They stand sentinel on exposed rocks, communicating with a complex system of high-pitched whistles that carry for kilometers across the empty plain. Occasionally, broad-winged birds with ragged feathers and improbable wingspans circle overhead, never seeming to land.

What makes this ancient, weathered landscape truly remarkable are the Engines. Rising from the flat plains at irregular intervals, these industrial structures stand like alien monuments. Each is unique in design but unified in purpose—massive mechanical assemblies of copper, brass, and a peculiar blue-green metal unknown elsewhere. They range from the size of a small house to constructions tall as skyscrapers, their components constantly in motion. Pistons drive, gears turn, valves release steam in rhythmic sighs, and occasionally, deep bells toll with no discernible pattern.

The Engines have no operators. No one built them, at least not in living or recorded memory. They appear to maintain themselves, occasionally producing smaller mechanisms that crawl across the landscape on multi-jointed legs to harvest rare minerals or collect the transient water. These gatherer machines return to their parent Engines, delivering their collections before embarking on new journeys.

The sound of the plains is a constant industrial symphony—the distant chuffing of the largest Engines, the grinding of gears, the hiss of pressure valves, and the occasional deep, resonant tones that seem to emanate from beneath the ground itself. During the quiet hours, when the machines' activity lessens, you can hear the wind whistling through the intricate frameworks, playing the Engines like enormous aeolian instruments.

The Plains follow a cycle, though its full rotation might take decades. As the Engines work, they gradually shift position—millimeters per year, but moving nonetheless. Local cartographers make their living charting these minute changes. Some Engines grow while others shrink. Occasionally, one will cease functioning altogether, slowly collapsing into its components, which are then harvested by its neighbors.

The few human settlements exist at the margins, built from the same materials as the Engines but in much more modest form. People here live in round houses with copper roofs that develop distinctive verdigris patterns. They maintain a respectful distance from the larger Engines but have learned to coexist with the smaller mechanisms. Children develop games around predicting the movements of the gatherer machines, placing markers in their predicted paths and celebrating when they guess correctly.

No one knows what the Engines produce. Their activity appears cyclical yet purposeful, transforming the landscape in subtle ways that become apparent only over generations. The oldest inhabitants speak of a time when the plain was less flat, when certain Engines stood taller, when the rain came more frequently—but such tales are dismissed as the exaggerations of the elderly.

What remains certain is that the Plains of Diminishing Return continue their slow, inexorable transformation under the vast sky, the Engines working to some incomprehensible plan, the land wearing away beneath them, and the silver creatures watching it all with their strange, knowing eyes.

# The Caretakers

They call themselves the Keph, though this word means both "we who remain" and "those who maintain" in their language—a tongue with no written form but rather expressed through rhythmic clicks, sustained tones, and precise periods of silence. The distinction between singular and plural is fluid among the Keph, as they consider themselves individual facets of a collective purpose rather than truly separate beings.

Their bodies have evolved in perfect harmony with the Plains. Standing approximately the height of a human child, they possess a dense, low center of gravity that serves them well on the wind-swept flatlands. Their skin carries the same palette as the stone beneath their feet—primarily a weathered copper-red with patches of gray and green that shift gradually over their lifetimes. This is no coincidence; their epidermis incorporates minerals from their environment, giving them natural camouflage against the rock tablelands.

Where humans have hair, the Keph grow fine crystalline filaments that capture moisture from the air. These filaments—transparent in youth, gradually becoming opaque and multi-hued with age—channel the collected water down specialized grooves in their skin to be absorbed or stored in internal reservoirs. During the 47-minute rains, these filaments flatten completely against their bodies, allowing them to absorb water directly through their porous skin.

The Keph have three primary limbs—two sturdy legs ending in broad, flat feet perfect for distributing weight on treacherous wet stone, and a central manipulative appendage that branches into seven extremely dexterous digits. This central limb can extend to nearly twice their body length when necessary, allowing them to reach deep into the Engines without needing to climb inside. Their eyes—four of them, arranged in a diamond pattern—perceive not just visible light but also heat, electromagnetic fields, and the subtle vibrations produced by the Engines.

The most remarkable feature of the Keph is their bioelectric nature. Their bodies generate a constant, low-level electrical field that interacts with the Engines in ways even they don't fully comprehend. When a Keph approaches an Engine, small components respond—panels slide open, diagnostic lights activate, hidden interfaces emerge. They don't control the Engines so much as communicate with them through this bioelectric relationship.

The Keph do not reproduce in ways recognizable to humans. New Keph emerge approximately once per decade, during rare periods when all the major Engines synchronize their deepest operating cycles. From specific Engines, a new crystalline form emerges—seemingly a part of the machinery itself—which gradually develops awareness, mobility, and finally, purpose. Young Keph begin as mechanical as the Engines, only developing their more organic characteristics as they age.

The oldest Keph remember when they were more numerous. Their history, passed through songs that last entire nights, tells of a time when hundreds walked the Plains. Now fewer than thirty remain, each responsible for maintaining vast territories of Engines. They don't consider this diminishment tragic—merely another phase in their endless cycle with the Plains.

The Keph understand the purpose of the Engines in ways they cannot fully articulate to outsiders. They know that the machines maintain critical balance in forces far beyond the visible world—regulating energies that flow beneath the Plains, managing pressures that would otherwise build to catastrophic levels, and processing elements essential to the planet's continued stability. The Engines are not manufacturing anything tangible; they are maintaining existence itself.

Humans fascinate the Keph, though interactions are limited. The Keph observe human settlements from a distance, occasionally leaving small gifts of Engine components with special properties—metal that never corrodes, gears that turn without friction, crystals that glow with permanent illumination. They find human attempts to map Engine movements endearing but misguided; the true patterns exist in dimensions humans cannot perceive.

The silver creatures—which the Keph call "zhir-tik" (approximately: "little brothers of awareness")—are their only consistent companions. The Keph understand their whistled language and occasionally relay messages through them across great distances. The relationship is symbiotic; the zhir-tik warn of approaching storms or structural instabilities in the Engines, while the Keph ensure the creatures' burrows remain viable despite the shifting landscape.

When a Keph reaches the end of their extremely long life cycle (measured in centuries), they do not die in the human sense. Instead, they gradually become more like the Engines themselves—their crystalline elements growing more pronounced, their movements becoming more mechanical, their communication shifting from biological sounds to the clicks and hums of the machines. Eventually, they merge with a specific Engine, their consciousness becoming part of its operation, their body becoming components in its eternal function.

The Keph hold a secret known to no human: the Plains of Diminishing Return are not diminishing at all, but transforming. The apparent reduction in the landscape is actually a conversion—matter being processed by the Engines into something else, something essential. The cycle has nearly reached its completion point, after which the Plains will begin a new phase of expansion that may take millennia to become apparent to short-lived observers.

They are patient. They are purpose. They are the rhythm behind the mechanical symphony. They are the Keph.

# The Unseen Connection

Between the visible Plains and the hidden homes of the Keph runs a network as intricate as it is invisible to human eyes. The humans call these features "fault lines" when they notice them at all—subtle seams in the stone that radiate outward from each Engine in complex geometric patterns. What appears random to human cartographers is, in fact, an elaborate circulatory system conducting energies from deep within the planet's core to its surface.

These channels—the Keph name for them translates roughly to "breath paths"—connect every Engine across the Plains through a crystalline matrix buried meters beneath the polished stone surface. At regular intervals, these paths surface as small, unassuming pools no larger than a handspan. The liquid within these pools is not water but a mercury-like substance with an iridescent sheen that constantly shifts between blue, green, and copper hues. The humans who have discovered these pools call them "quickmirrors" and believe them to be sacred, while giving them a wide berth due to an instinctive understanding of their otherness.

These pools serve as communication nodes for the Keph and transportation junctures for the smaller maintenance mechanisms. A Keph can kneel beside any pool, extend a hand to touch the surface, and instantly share consciousness with any other Keph similarly connected elsewhere on the Plains. The sensation is not telepathy as humans might understand it, but rather a complete sharing of sensory experience—the connected Keph perceive through each other's eyes, feel through each other's touch, process information through a temporarily merged awareness.

Twice yearly, during the equinoxes, all the quickmirror pools synchronize in a phenomenon called "The Communion." For exactly one hour, the liquid in each pool pulses with rhythmic light visible even to human eyes, and the Keph gather around their nearest pool to participate in a collective consciousness that encompasses all their kind. During this hour, decisions affecting the entire Plains are made, maintenance priorities are established, and the collective knowledge of the Keph is reharmonized. For days afterward, the Keph move with perfect synchronicity across the Plains, their individual actions precisely coordinated to serve their shared purpose.

The zhir-tik—the silver creatures—play a crucial role in maintaining these networks. Their burrows invariably connect to the crystalline matrices beneath the surface, and their activities help keep the pathways clear of obstructions. The zhir-tik never disturb the quickmirror pools themselves but perform elaborate rituals around them during the weeks preceding The Communion, their whistled communications creating harmonic frequencies that help calibrate the crystalline network.

Humans who have lived on the Plains for generations have developed unconscious sensitivities to these energies. Their round houses are invariably built at specific distances from the nearest breath path, though they couldn't articulate why these locations feel "right." Children born on the Plains occasionally develop unusual abilities—perfect pitch, extraordinary spatial awareness, or an uncanny knack for predicting Engine movements—gifts that the Keph recognize as resonance effects from proximity to the breath paths during development.

When an Engine shifts position—those millimeter-by-year movements that human cartographers track so diligently—it is actually reorienting itself to optimize its connection to the breath paths beneath it. These microscopic adjustments ensure that energy continues to flow properly through the network as the greater cycle progresses. The Engines that "die" do so when their connection to the network becomes unsustainable, and their components are harvested precisely because they contain crystalline structures that must be preserved and redistributed.

The rain itself—that mathematically precise forty-seven minute downpour—is no meteorological phenomenon but a system flush, a planetary reset that helps recalibrate the energies flowing through the breath paths. Each droplet carries microscopic crystalline particles that seep into the Plains' surface and help maintain the networks below. This is why the rain's timing seems mechanical; it is triggered by system requirements rather than weather patterns.

On rare occasions, perhaps once in a human lifetime, the convergence of energies creates a phenomenon humans call "The Illumination." For a single night, the entire network becomes visible from above—a glowing lattice of blue-green light stretching across the Plains, connecting every Engine, every quickmirror pool, every Keph dwelling. Humans who witness this event often speak of feeling a profound sense of purpose they cannot explain, as if briefly touching something eternal.

What humans cannot see—what even the Keph understand only partially—is that these networks extend far beyond the Plains themselves. The breath paths connect to similar systems across the entire planet, regulating energies on a global scale. The Plains of Diminishing Return are merely the most visible manifestation of a planetary maintenance system of unimaginable complexity.

The Keph know that humans will never fully comprehend this connection. Their lives are too brief, their perception too limited. But they recognize in humans a kind of resonance with their own purpose—an instinct for maintenance and care, however imperfectly expressed. This is why they leave their gifts, why they silently guide human settlements away from critical nodes, why they sometimes send the zhir-tik to warn of impending dangers.

For in the grand cycle of planetary maintenance, even beings as transient as humans have their place. Their observations, their maps, their stories—all become part of the record, data points in an algorithm spanning geological time. Humans are not caretakers like the Keph, but they are witnesses, and in a system as vast as that of the Plains, even witnessing has its purpose.

As the cycle approaches completion and the time of expansion nears, the connections between the Keph and their human neighbors grow subtly stronger. Dreams become more vivid among Plains dwellers; children begin to notice the zhir-tik watching them with increasing frequency; elders find themselves drawn to particular Engines without knowing why.

The world is preparing. The network is aligning. And beneath the vast sky of the Plains, everything—human, Keph, Engine, and zhir-tik—is connected by pathways ancient beyond reckoning, all serving a purpose that will outlast them all.
