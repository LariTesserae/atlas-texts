# The Hanging Gardens

Far above the Hive Below, atop a windswept plateau where the air is thin and crisp, sprawls a settlement that appears, from a distance, to be merely a collection of oddly shaped boulders. Only upon closer inspection does the visitor realize that these are structures—domes and arches of living stone that have been coaxed rather than carved into their forms over generations.

This is the City of Cultivators, though its inhabitants rarely use this name, preferring to simply call it "the Surface." The city is built around a series of terraced gardens that cascade down the eastern face of the plateau, capturing the morning sun. These are the famous Hanging Gardens, the antithesis of the Hive Below in almost every respect, yet bound to it by threads both visible and invisible.

Where the Hive Below is dark, the Gardens are bathed in sunlight that reflects off polished stone surfaces, creating a brilliance that would blind unaccustomed eyes. Where the cavern air below is thick with moisture and the scent of earth, the air here carries the sharp tang of alpine herbs and the sweetness of countless flowers. And where the Hive drowns in its perpetual insectoid symphony, the Gardens are defined by their stillness—a silence punctuated only by the whisper of wind through specialized plants and the occasional chiming of crystal bells that hang from curved stone arches.

The Cultivators themselves are as adapted to their environment as the Attendants are to theirs, but in radically different ways. They stand taller than average humans, their bodies lean and angular. Their skin has a translucent quality that reveals chlorophyll networks just beneath the surface—green latticeworks that pulse slightly with the sun's position. Their hair grows in thick, fibrous strands that photosynthesize, changing color with the seasons—from vibrant greens in summer to deep russets and golds in autumn.

Most striking are their eyes—large, with vertical pupils like those of a cat, and irises that shift in color throughout the day, tracking the sun's position with biological precision. The Cultivators' fingers are extraordinarily long, ending in hardened tips that can delicately manipulate the most fragile seedling or split stone along natural fault lines.

The Gardens themselves are marvels of botanical engineering. Plants grow here that exist nowhere else—species developed through centuries of careful breeding and mysterious rituals performed under specific stellar alignments. Flowering vines with blooms larger than a human head cascade down stone walls. Trees grow in perfect spirals, their branches creating living staircases. Fruits hang heavy on specially trained bushes, each variety serving a specific medicinal or nutritional purpose.

Water is precious here, managed with fanatical efficiency. The plateau captures morning mist in specialized collection basins, and every drop is accounted for in the Cultivators' meticulous records. Nothing is wasted—water cycles through the Gardens in precisely calculated routes, eventually collecting in a series of pools carved into the living rock, where it is purified by specialized aquatic plants before beginning its journey again.

The Cultivators' dwellings are marvels of organic architecture—buildings that are neither fully constructed nor fully grown, but something in between. The outer shells are stone, seemingly solid but actually porous at a microscopic level, allowing for carefully controlled air exchange. Inside, spaces are defined by living partitions—plants that have been trained over decades to create walls, ceilings, and passageways. These living structures are both beautiful and functional, purifying air and responding to seasonal changes by altering their configuration to optimize light and temperature.

The society is ordered around specialties, though not the rigid castes of the Hive Below. There are Seed-Keepers, who maintain the vast library of plant genetic material; Stone-Shapers, who communicate with the living rock through methods involving song and touch; Sky-Readers, who track celestial movements and weather patterns; and Root-Tenders, who manage the unseen but vital root networks that stabilize the entire plateau.

Most important are the Grafters—individuals who display the rare ability to directly communicate with plant consciousness. Their bodies show the most extreme adaptations, with bark-like patches covering vital organs and visible veins that pulse with chlorophyll-rich fluids. Grafters can spend days in meditative communion with the oldest plants in the Gardens, emerging with knowledge that shapes community decisions.

The Cultivators measure time differently than other societies—not by days or years, but by blooming cycles, growth patterns, and stellar alignments. Their history is recorded in the growth rings of ancient trees and in the patterns of specially bred flowers that bloom only once per century.

# The Passage Between

Connecting these two seemingly incompatible worlds is the Spiral Path—a narrow tunnel that winds through the mountain, neither entirely natural nor clearly constructed. Its walls bear curious markings that might be writing, might be decorative patterns, or might be something else entirely—maps, perhaps, or mathematical equations describing the relationship between the worlds above and below.

The journey between the Hive Below and the Hanging Gardens takes precisely three days, regardless of the traveler's pace. This temporal consistency defies explanation but has been verified countless times. Those who attempt to hurry find themselves slowed by mysterious fatigue; those who dawdle discover they arrive exactly when they should, no matter how many stops they make.

The Spiral Path is maintained by a third group—neither Attendants nor Cultivators, but individuals who call themselves the Intermediaries. They are fewer in number than either of the communities they connect, and display physical adaptations suited to both environments. Their eyes can adjust from the blinding sunlight of the Surface to the near-total darkness of the Hive. Their lungs can process both the thin mountain air and the dense humidity of the cavern. Their skin changes texture gradually as they move between realms, hardening for protection below and becoming more translucent above.

Intermediaries wear distinctive garments woven from both insect silk and plant fibers, symbolizing their dual allegiance. They are bound by ancient oath to maintain absolute neutrality in any dispute between the worlds they connect, serving only as messengers and traders, never taking sides.

The trade between the two societies is as precisely balanced as everything else in their existence. The Hive Below produces silks of extraordinary quality, medicinal compounds derived from insect secretions, and specialized tools crafted from chitin and the unique metals found only in the depths. The Hanging Gardens offer rare herbs with powerful properties, fruits that can cure specific ailments, and textiles dyed with colors impossible to create underground.

Every exchange is carefully calculated to maintain perfect equilibrium. No community gains advantage over the other; no resource becomes depleted; no population grows beyond sustainable limits. This balance has been maintained for so long that it has taken on religious significance for both societies—a sacred equilibrium that must never be disturbed.

But the most crucial exchange—the one that truly binds these worlds together—involves neither goods nor information, but people.

Children born in the Hive Below who display certain rare genetic markers—an unusual response to light, a particular pattern in their vocal development—are identified early and, upon reaching their seventh year, are sent up the Spiral Path to join the Cultivators. Similarly, children born in the Hanging Gardens who demonstrate specific traits—an affinity for darkness, an unusual resistance to insect toxins—make the journey downward to become Attendants.

This exchange ensures genetic diversity in both populations and maintains the adaptations necessary for survival in each extreme environment. The children, prepared from birth for their journey, show no resistance to this transfer. They understand their purpose in the greater equilibrium.

Neither community speaks openly about the origin of this arrangement or the catastrophe that necessitated such extreme adaptations and separation. But in both societies, there are whispered stories of a time long past when humans lived across the entire mountain, neither above nor below, but everywhere between. They tell of a calamity that poisoned the middle realms, forcing survivors to adapt to the extremes of mountain peak and deepest cavern.

The Spiral Path, these stories suggest, once led not just between two isolated communities, but through a vibrant middle kingdom now lost to memory. And the strict protocols governing exchanges between the Hive and the Gardens are not merely tradition or spiritual practice, but vital safeguards against whatever ancient threat still lingers in the abandoned middle realm.

What truly lies at the heart of the Spiral Path—in that central section where Intermediaries grow quiet and hurry travelers along—remains unknown to all but the eldest members of both societies. There, in the middle distance between absolute darkness and blinding light, something waits. Something that necessitates the perfect balance maintained for countless generations. Something that must never be awakened.

And so the delicate dance continues—insects buzzing in perfect harmony with flowering plants blooming precisely on schedule, two communities linked by necessity and separated by design, maintaining an equilibrium that is both their greatest achievement and their heaviest burden.
