# The Glassblower's Refuge

Among the warren of crooked brick passages, one building emits light unlike any other. Not the earthen orange glow of the omnipresent kilns, but something clearer, more piercing—a white-blue radiance that escapes through windows of actual glass, their surfaces rippled but transparent.

Here, in a rounded structure twice the height of its neighbors, I have made my home and workshop. The Clayborn tolerate my presence because I provide what they cannot create from their beloved earth alone: vessels that hold water without seeping, windows that admit light without crumbling, beads and trinkets that catch and transform the eternal kiln-glow.

My furnace burns hotter than their kilns, fueled by a jealously-guarded supply of coal rather than the local clay-mixed dung. The heat is focused, concentrated, a single relentless point rather than their slow, ambient baking. When I work, sweat pours freely—a sacrilege in this water-scarce place, but necessary for my craft.

I arrived a generation ago, an outsider with strange tools and stranger ideas. They distrusted me at first—as they distrust all who aren't born of their russet earth—but the first time I blew a water vessel that didn't require constant refilling, their elders granted me this space at the slum's northeastern edge, where occasional breezes sometimes reach.

The workshop's lower level houses my furnace, glory hole, and workbench. A crude spiral staircase leads to my living quarters above, sparse but comfortable by local standards. My windows face outward toward the basin's rim—partly to catch what little fresh air circulates, partly because the Clayborn find my transparent barriers unsettling. They prefer walls solid and certain, not this material that is neither here nor there.

I am not like them. My skin remains pale despite the ever-present heat, marked with burns rather than the fine crazing they develop. My lungs labor in the thick air they breathe without complaint. I require more water than they do—another reason they might resent me if my craft didn't prove so valuable.

Each morning I work alone, creating the practical pieces they trade for—the vessels, the window panes, the tools that need transparency or impermeability. But each evening, as the perpetual twilight deepens toward true night, I create solely for myself.

These pieces never leave my workshop: delicate sculptures of fantastical creatures unknown in the Foundry Slum. Birds with wings so thin light passes through them. Fish that seem to swim in the solid medium. Trees with leaves that chime softly against each other. I fashion the world beyond the basin, the world I sometimes fear I've begun to forget.

The Clayborn children sometimes gather outside my workshop at dusk, pressing their faces to the lower windows to watch the glass transform in my hands from glowing blob to creature of impossible delicacy. I let them watch. Some of the parents disapprove—they fear I plant dangerous notions about impermanence and fragility—but others recognize the value of seeing different ways of making.

Once each year, when the Clayborn hold their secret ritual, I lock my doors and windows tight. I blow seven perfect spheres of the clearest glass, each no larger than a child's fist. Inside each, I seal a single drop of water from my private store—water carried from the distant sea before I came to this place. When the ground trembles that night, my spheres chime against each other, the water inside them catching the dim light, refracting it across my walls in patterns like the memory of rain.

I am aging faster than the Clayborn. The heat and silica are taking their toll on my foreign body. My hands shake slightly now when I'm not working, though they steady the moment I take up my blowpipe. My breath is shorter, my eyes clouding at the edges.

The Clayborn have begun sending me an apprentice—a girl whose skin hasn't fully hardened, whose hands show unusual dexterity. She watches silently for hours, absorbing every movement. I caught her once pressing her palm against a cooling piece, not from curiosity about the temperature, but seemingly to feel the difference between her substance and mine.

I don't know if she can learn this art, if lungs adapted for dust can master the controlled breath needed for blowing glass. But I teach her anyway. The thought of my furnace going cold after I'm gone, of the slum returning to its uniform earthen glow, is somehow more frightening than my own ending.

Sometimes at night, I climb to my roof—another architectural oddity that raises local eyebrows—and look out over the endless labor of the Foundry Slum. From here, I can almost imagine the ceaseless building has purpose beyond itself, that all this effort shapes something grander than mere walls and passages. From here, with my failing eyes, the glow of countless kilns transforms into something almost beautiful—a constellation fallen to earth, still burning.

I am the glassblower, the shaper of transparency in a world devoted to opacity. I preserve what little I can of clarity and light in a place perpetually becoming but never quite being. When I die, the Clayborn will not know what to do with my body—I am not meant for their kilns, will not vitrify into an ancestral figurine. Perhaps they will simply wall up my workshop with me inside, another curiosity buried beneath the ever-growing, ever-crumbling slum.

But until then, I blow glass. I create windows in walls that never knew them. I transform sand and fire into vessels that hold without losing, into forms that can be both seen and seen through. In this way, I too am building, adding something necessary to the eternal labor of the Foundry Slum—the gift of glimpsing what lies beyond.
