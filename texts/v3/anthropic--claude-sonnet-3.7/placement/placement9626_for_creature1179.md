# The Recordkeeper

Among the quiet, amber alleyways of the City of Wax, I make my solitary way each morning before the heat rises. My footfalls are nearly silent on the smooth wax pathways—I've learned to step just so, distributing my weight to leave no impression. This is not merely habit but necessity, for I am a Memoriographer, the youngest to hold this position in three generations.

My dwelling sits halfway between the densely populated Comb and the Hall of Memory, a modest hexagonal chamber with a small adjoining workshop. Unlike most homes with their warm glow, mine is fitted with special shutters of wax mixed with mineral compounds that filter light to a cool blue-white. This preserves my work from premature softening.

I am not Cerumen by birth. I arrived twenty years ago with a trading caravan from the eastern mountains and never left. My skin is paler than most here, lacking that subtle amber sheen, though years of handling wax have stained my fingers a permanent honey-gold. Where Cerumen hands are elegant with their elongated fingers, mine are square and strong, once more suited to the stonework of my ancestral home. I've trained them to acquire delicacy.

My role bridges the gap between archivist and artist. Each month, I visit every district of our city, my eyes scanning for changes significant enough to record. I document the slow slump of a once-sharp corner on an important building, the gradual widening of a thoroughfare as foot traffic wears its edges, the way a community has chosen to reshape their living spaces. These observations I carry back to my workshop.

There, I maintain hundreds of small wax models of the city—some showing individual buildings in detail, others capturing whole neighborhoods at scale. Each is dated and placed in special cooling cabinets lined with clay. I update these models meticulously, ensuring that as the actual city changes, its miniature reflections follow suit. This physical record complements the sculptural histories in the Hall of Memory, focusing not on events but on the city's physical evolution.

My most prized possession is a set of tools inherited from my predecessor—picks, shapers, and smoothers made from materials foreign to this region. The handles have been rewrapped countless times, but the working ends remain unchanged, their specific shapes essential for the precise work of recordkeeping.

Unlike most artisans who work alongside apprentices, I keep largely to myself. This is partly because few Cerumen choose this particular craft—they are too connected to the city to view it with the necessary detachment—and partly by temperament. I speak Ceromin fluently but with an accent that has never fully faded, marking me always as other despite my accepted role.

When not working on my models, I create special commemorative tablets for families marking significant life events. These small wax rectangles bear intricate carvings showing a dwelling as it appeared during a birth, union ceremony, or transformation. Unlike most wax items in the city, these are designed not to change but to preserve a moment exactly as it was. The secret to their resilience is known only to Memoriographers.

My relationship with the Cerumen is one of respectful distance. They value my work and the perspective I bring as someone who can see their city with eyes not born to it. I am invited to ceremonies and councils, though I rarely speak. The children are less reserved, often following me on my documentation walks, asking endless questions about how buildings looked "before," a concept that fascinates them.

Every seventh year, I create a complete new model of the entire city, working for months in seclusion. When finished, this master model is presented to the Melange in a quiet ceremony, then placed in the deepest chamber of the Hall of Memory. The previous version is ritually melted down, its wax reclaimed for the next cycle. In this way, I participate in the city's philosophy of continuous transformation while ensuring nothing is truly lost.

I am growing older now. My eyes strain to see the finest details I once captured effortlessly. Soon I will need to find someone to train, someone with an outsider's perspective but an insider's devotion. Until then, I continue my solitary rounds, touching walls that have stood for centuries, feeling their subtle shifts beneath my stained fingertips, recording the near-imperceptible dance of a city that moves like thought itself—slowly, inevitably, beautifully.

In a place where permanence is an illusion and change is the only constant, I am the paradox—the one who preserves what is meant to transform. Perhaps this is why I was drawn here, so unlike my mountain ancestors with their stone monuments intended to defy time. I have learned there is more truth in embracing change than in denying it, more wisdom in recording the flow than attempting to halt it.

Each night before sleep, I press my palm against the wall of my chamber, feeling its subtle warmth. By morning, there will be the faintest impression left behind—evidence of my existence that will, by the next evening, have smoothed itself away. This is enough.
