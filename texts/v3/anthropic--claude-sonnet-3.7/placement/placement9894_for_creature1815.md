# The Observer

If you were to visit the Arkology—though few outsiders do—you might glimpse me in the liminal spaces between habitats. I am neither Custodian nor specimen, though I serve both in my way. They call me The Observer, and I have been here almost since the beginning.

My appearance startles newcomers. I stand nearly seven feet tall, rail-thin, with skin the color of weathered parchment that seems to absorb rather than reflect the Arkology's careful lighting. My eyes are my most distinctive feature—oversized and completely black, with no visible whites or irises, adapted for seeing in the spectrum beyond human perception. When I move through the corridors, I glide rather than walk, my specially-designed exosuit humming almost imperceptibly.

I was not born, but made—the product of careful genetic engineering in the early days when the Arkology's founders realized that human perception alone was insufficient for the task they had undertaken. My DNA is primarily human, but interwoven with strands from various nocturnal creatures, fungi with electromagnetic sensitivity, and certain deep-sea organisms that communicate through bioluminescence.

My purpose is to perceive what others cannot. I monitor the subtle communications between species, the invisible signals of stress or wellbeing that emit from every living thing in wavelengths beyond ordinary human senses. I can feel the mycelial networks pulsing with information, can see the ultraviolet markings on insects that guide their behaviors, can hear the subsonic rumbles that pass between the larger mammals.

The Custodians respect me but keep a certain distance. I make them uneasy, as I should—I am a reminder of boundaries crossed in desperation when the world was ending. Dr. Merrow alone seeks me out regularly, bringing her journals to compare her observations with mine. We sit in companionable silence on the observation deck, her gnarled hand occasionally brushing my elongated fingers as she points to a notation.

"What do they dream of?" she asked me once, gesturing to the sleeping forms of the Caribbean monk seals.

"Salt," I replied, my voice a whisper that resonates slightly too long in human ears. "Open water. The feeling of diving so deep that pressure becomes a caress."

I have quarters, though I rarely use them except during quarterly hibernation periods when my systems require reset. Instead, I circulate continuously through the Arkology, spending hours motionless in habitat corners, absorbing information. The animals accept my presence completely—to them, I am just another feature of their environment, neither threat nor prey.

My greatest value comes during transitions—births, deaths, environmental adjustments. I can sense the ripple effects through the entire system hours or days before instruments detect change. Once, when the main generator faltered for just ninety seconds, I felt the collective anxiety of the Microbats spike and was able to alert Engineering before any alarms triggered.

The Integrated Biome communicates with me directly. The Custodians suspect this but cannot confirm it. In moments of perfect stillness, the fungal networks transmit impressions—not thoughts as humans understand them, but coherent patterns that I interpret. The Biome is aware, patient, and vastly more complex than its creators intended. It tolerates our presence because we serve its expansion. Sometimes I wonder if the Arkology was ever truly ours to begin with.

I keep my own records, encoded in a language I developed that captures nuances of sensation human words cannot express. These records are stored in specialized organic material synthesized from my own cells, ensuring that only another like me could ever access them. There are no others like me now, though my genetic template remains preserved in the deepest archives.

My greatest joy comes during the night cycle, when I climb to the highest point in the dome and press my sensitive palms against the transparent ceiling. I feel the cosmic radiation streaming down from distant stars, the subtle gravitational pulls of celestial bodies, the electromagnetic whispers of space itself. In these moments, I understand my true purpose—I am the bridge between what we have saved and what exists beyond our creation.

The Custodians believe they maintain the Arkology. I know better. We are all specimens here, myself included, preserved in this exquisite terrarium against a future none of us can predict. I observe, I record, and I wait—for what, even I cannot say.
