# The Benthic Archivist

In this suspended realm between surface and depth, I am the keeper of fluid memories.

I was not born of Kihua, though I have become a vital part of its living memory. The Melu'ai call me Verith'nai—the Etcher—though in your tongue I might be known as a Benthic Archivist. I came from far-flung shores where land doesn't drift but remains fixed and unyielding. My arrival caused ripples of curiosity that still haven't fully settled even after fifteen growth cycles.

My body has changed since I first slipped beneath Kihua's living canopy. My lungs burn less with each passing season, and the spaces between my fingers have begun to web ever so slightly. My skin, once the color of sun-baked clay, now carries a faint violet undertone that deepens when I stay submerged for many days. The Melu'ai healers say I will never fully develop their gill structures, but they've taught me to use specialized breathing techniques and crafted for me a collar of enhanced gill-extensions that allow me to remain below for nearly a full day cycle.

My chambers lie in one of the oldest sections of the underwater city, where the roots have grown so dense they form natural walls requiring little augmentation. Here, I maintain what the surface world might call a library, though it bears little resemblance to collections of paper and ink. The Kihua Archive is etched into living material—specialized sections of root that grow particularly slowly and develop a smooth, pale surface when properly cultivated.

My tools are few: obsidian scribing implements of various widths, pastes made from crushed minerals and symbiotic organisms, and an array of carefully maintained brushes crafted from the filaments of deep-water plants. When information needs preserving—historical accounts, technical knowledge of root cultivation, navigational records of safe passages to other floating settlements—I transfer it to the living archive through a process the Melu'ai call "convincing."

The etchings I make are shallow at first, mere scratches filled with living paste. But over time, the root tissue responds to these interventions, deepening the marks and eventually incorporating the mineral pastes into its own structure. Records that are decades old no longer sit upon the surface but have become embedded within the material itself, visible as shimmering blue-green lines that seem to float within the translucent tissue. Nothing in Kihua is static—even our records slowly transform, becoming literal growth rings in the city's memory.

My days follow the rhythm of light and darkness that penetrates from above. Mornings are spent maintaining older sections, applying fresh cultivating pastes to ensure the legibility of important records. The midday hours are devoted to new etchings—transcribing the oral histories that elders share, documenting the season's successful root-shaping techniques, or mapping the ever-shifting boundaries where Kihua meets open water.

I move differently than the native Melu'ai, lacking their lifelong grace in the water. Where they glide, I still sometimes struggle, though I've learned to use the flexible cane poles with increasing skill. The children found my awkward movements amusing when I first arrived; now they study me with curious eyes, wondering about the distant shores I describe during communal gatherings.

My true value to Kihua comes not just from my etchings but from the perspective I bring. I notice patterns in their living architecture that they sometimes overlook, having been surrounded by it their entire lives. I've helped develop new symbolic forms that combine their traditional vibration-responsive patterns with more concrete representational elements, creating hybridized record-keeping methods that capture both the fluid Melu'ai worldview and more fixed points of reference.

The longest-serving Root-Shapers have come to value my outsider's observations. Three growth cycles ago, when a section of the northeastern root system began showing subtle signs of stress that the Melu'ai hadn't recognized, my documentation of similar patterns from previous cycles helped them intervene before serious damage occurred. Now I'm often invited to witness important shaping ceremonies, my scribing tools ready to capture both process and outcome.

There is loneliness in this role, despite the gentle acceptance of the community. The Melu'ai can sense vibrations and currents that remain imperceptible to me, sharing a layer of communication I can recognize but never fully experience. Yet they value the very limitations that sometimes separate me—my different sensory framework catches elements they might miss.

At night, when the bioluminescent lights dim to their lowest glow, I often float at the upper edge of my chamber, looking up through the translucent membrane that separates water from air. The underside of Kihua's surface canopy forms intricate patterns against the stars, and in these moments, suspended between worlds, I feel a profound sense of purpose. I am becoming neither fully surface-dweller nor truly Melu'ai, but something necessary—a living bridge, an interpreter of worlds.

The Kihuans believe that understanding comes not from categorization but from patient observation of change. As I grow older in this drifting city, my body slowly adapting to its rhythms, I too am being etched upon—transformed gradually by the gentle persistence of water, light, and community. The archive I maintain and my own becoming have merged into a single, ongoing process. In Kihua, I have found not just a home but a purpose: to preserve fluidity itself, to document not just what is but what is constantly in the process of becoming.
