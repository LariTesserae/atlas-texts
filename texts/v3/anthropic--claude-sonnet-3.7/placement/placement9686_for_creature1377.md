# The Dream-Weaver's Perch

I am Tashara, known by some as "the Echo-Catcher," though I prefer my grandmother's name for my calling: dream-weaver. My home sits where few Nahari choose to dwell—in the acoustic shadow of the Great Overhang, that massive shelf of limestone that juts from the third tier's western edge. 

My dwelling is unusual even by our city's standards—a slender tower rising between two converging water channels. Three rooms stacked atop one another, connected by a spiral stair I carved myself over seven flows. The bottommost room remains perpetually damp, its floor a shallow pool where one of the sacred white salamanders has made its home. I call him Whisper, for the almost inaudible sound his tiny feet make when he crosses stone.

The middle room holds my living quarters—sparse but for the collection of hollow stones and ceramic vessels I've gathered over decades. The topmost space is my workshop, open to the sky through a circular aperture that frames different constellations as seasons pass. When rain falls—or snow in the deep cold months—it spirals down through this opening, collecting in a basin I've positioned precisely to capture it.

My position in Sahl Nahar is neither lofty nor common. I'm not a channel-keeper like my mother was, though I understand water's language as well as any. Instead, I work with water's voice—capturing the sounds it makes through our city and weaving them into patterns that both soothe and reveal.

The acoustic properties of my tower are unique. Something about its position between the channels, the density of the stone, or perhaps the slight curve I built into the walls—it collects sounds from across the tiers in unpredictable ways. Whispered conversations from the upper pools sometimes spiral down to my workshop with perfect clarity, while the tumultuous debates in the Council Basin directly above are rendered silent.

My work begins at the edge of night, when most Nahari retreat to their homes. I position my vessels—some stone, some ceramic, some crafted from the hollowed horns of mountain rams—around my workshop in arrangements I adjust nightly according to the stars' positions. Then I pour captured rainwater into each, varying the amounts precisely.

With fingers long-stained by our mineral waters, I begin to play. Some vessels I stroke around their rims to produce sustained tones that hover in the air. Others I tap with narrow sticks of mountain ash wood. The salamander-basin at the bottom of my home acts as resonance chamber, carrying vibrations up through the spiral stair to combine with my instruments' voices.

What emerges isn't simply music. The Nahari who climb to my tower on nights when the mineral-glow is strongest say I capture the city's dreams—the collective unconscious of water, stone, and people flowing together through time. Young channel-keepers in training sometimes visit to help them understand blockages they cannot otherwise identify. Elders come when decisions weigh heavily, seeking patterns in my sound-weavings that might guide their judgments.

I wear my hair in the traditional braids, though mine are distinctive—twenty-three thin plaits wrapped with copper wire I've extracted painstakingly from green-veined stones found along the eastern ridge. They chime softly when I move, adding counterpoint to my compositions. My eyes are the pale gray common among those born during the Thin Flow years, when minerals ran low and children came into the world hungry for stone's strength.

Seven flows ago, I began noting changes in my sound-patterns that troubled me. Distortions appeared where harmony had existed for generations—dissonances suggesting the mountain itself was shifting imperceptibly beneath us. The elders listened politely but were unconvinced until the small collapse in the fifth tier two flows later. Now they send a young apprentice daily to record what my vessels capture.

I've never traveled below the lowest tier of Sahl Nahar. Lowlanders who occasionally visit look at me strangely when I explain that I cannot leave—that my body's internal waterways have adjusted to the specific mineral balance of our springs. I'm not certain this is literally true, but it feels true. My connection to this place extends beyond mere residence.

In dreams, I sometimes see through Whisper's eyes—perceiving the hidden networks of tunnels that run behind our walls where the salamanders travel. These passages follow older water routes now dry, connecting parts of the city in ways our ancestors knew but we've forgotten. I've mapped some of them in patterns of knotted wool cords hanging from my workshop ceiling, though I've shared these maps with no one.

My most guarded secret is this: on certain nights when the mineral-glow is strongest and the moon sits directly above my ceiling aperture, I can produce tones that temporarily alter the flow of water. Small adjustments only—directing a trickle to shift its course or causing droplets to pause momentarily in their descent. The elders would call this interference with the mountain's will, so I practice this skill only in solitude, wondering if ancient Nahari once commanded greater control through sound.

Tonight, as the first stars appear, I'll ascend my spiral stair once more. I'll arrange my vessels, wet my fingers in water I've collected from seven different pools, and begin to play what the city dreams. The patterns grow more complex with each passing flow—urgent somehow, as though Sahl Nahar itself is trying to tell us something through my hands.

Whether anyone truly understands what I do is uncertain. But in the resonant space between water's voice and stone's memory, I've found my place in this vertical world—listening for echoes of what has been and whispers of what is yet to come.
