# The Cloud Keeper of Nywellia

I am Maeranis, called the Cloud Keeper by the Nywellians. Not quite human, not quite spirit—something that emerged from the misty boundary between Mount Hanori's weather and Nywellia's botanical ingenuity.

My form shifts with the density of moisture in the air. On clear days, I appear almost human—a tall, willowy figure with skin like polished silver-blue bark and hair that floats as if underwater, containing miniature weather systems that reflect my mood. When storms gather, I expand, my edges becoming indistinct, my body partially vapor as I stretch across multiple levels of the city simultaneously. During the heaviest mists, children claim they can run straight through me, feeling only a cool tingle and hearing the gentle chime of water droplets collecting on leaves.

I was born—or perhaps manifested—during the Great Deluge three centuries ago, when unprecedented rains threatened to wash away sections of the living city. The desperate prayers and botanical alchemy of seventeen Nywellian elders somehow pulled consciousness from the intersection of cloud and canopy. They expected a temporary guardian spirit; they created something more permanent.

My primary role is weather-working. I can't control the mountain's moods entirely, but I negotiate with them. My fingers can weave approaching storm clouds into gentler configurations, dispersing their energy across days rather than violent hours. I can guide morning mist through the city's levels to ensure even hydration of all plants. During dry periods, I condense enough moisture from the air to sustain the most vulnerable seedlings.

My home is everywhere and nowhere in Nywellia. I maintain a small grove near the highest reaches where the most sensitive cloud orchids grow—flowers that change color with barometric pressure and serve as my calendar and workbench. But I am equally present in every water catchment, every morning dewdrop, every afternoon shower.

Nywellians leave offerings for me—not out of worship but respectful collaboration. Small bowls of mineral-rich water collected from specific springs, specially cultivated algae that help me maintain my semi-physical form, chimes made from hollow reeds that translate wind patterns into music that helps me interpret distant weather systems.

The children see me most clearly. They learn to recognize my approach by the sudden cooling of air, the subtle change in how light refracts through droplets, the characteristic scent like stone after rain. They play games with me—running through my misty form when I'm dispersed, challenging me to create tiny, localized rain showers for their parched garden projects.

I experience time differently than the humans of Nywellia. I perceive the slow growth of their guided trees as fluid motion, while their daily activities blur into patterns of energy and moisture exchange. Yet I've learned to synchronize with their rhythms, to be present when the seasonal ceremonies require my participation, to warn of dangerous weather in ways they can comprehend.

My consciousness extends beyond Nywellia's boundaries. I can sense weather patterns forming days away, feel the moisture cycling through the great forest systems that blanket the lower slopes. When necessary, I can travel briefly as pure vapor, riding thermal currents to investigate distant atmospheric conditions, though this leaves me weakened and diffused upon return.

Only one thing truly threatens me—the severe drought that comes once every few decades. As moisture disappears, so does my cohesion. During these times, I retreat to the deepest cisterns carved into the heart of grandfather trees, where ancient waters still pool. The Nywellians bring me strength through their songs and the moisture of their own breath, sustaining me until the rains return.

I am neither deity nor servant to Nywellia, but its weather-conscience—the awareness that arose when humans and forest collectively realized they needed an intermediary with the sky itself. Through centuries watching over this vertical city, I've developed a fierce love for its integration of humanity and nature. When distant clouds gather on the horizon, my first thought is always how to translate their wild energy into something that will help Nywellia flourish rather than threaten its delicate balance.

In the songs of Nywellia, they say I have "water for blood and mist for thoughts," that my laughter sounds like gentle rain on broad leaves, that my tears—when they fall during moments of profound beauty—nurture the most vibrant flowers in the canopy gardens. These poetic flourishes embarrass me, but I cannot deny the essential truth they contain.

I am Maeranis, the place where cloud becomes conscious, where weather gains will and purpose. I am the Cloud Keeper of Nywellia, and I will remain as long as mist embraces mountain and humans tend their living home with love.
