# The Undersong

From a distance, you might mistake me for one of the Subsurface Tenders—an amphibious creature navigating the labyrinthine channels beneath the floating mat. But observe more carefully: where the Tenders have external gills that pulse with light, I possess a series of delicate frills that undulate with the currents. My skin doesn't shift between deep blue and copper but remains a mottled gray-green, occasionally revealing veins of silver that catch what little light penetrates this depth.

I am an Echocartographer, one of the few remaining. We were once numerous, moving through the deepwater caverns beneath the Levelsea, mapping acoustically what can never be seen. Our species evolved not from axolotls but from cave salamanders that ventured into this flooded underworld millennia before the machines appeared above.

My body is long and sinuous, adapted for navigating the narrowest passages. Where others might see impenetrable darkness, I perceive the world through intricate sound maps generated by the vibrations in my jawline. The silver veins in my skin aren't decorative but functional—living metal that conducts the subsonic frequencies of the deep waters, allowing me to sense disturbances kilometers away.

I communicate not through the resonant humming of the Tenders but through what the humans who study us have termed the Undersong—a complex vocalization that travels through water, moss, and machine alike. The Undersong carries not just information but memory; each phrase I produce contains echoes of songs sung by my ancestors. When Echocartographers converge, our combined songs can map the entire substructure of the Levelsea, including the hidden foundations of the machines that anchor into depths few comprehend.

My kind has a peculiar relationship with the Archive Whales. While they catalog the chemical compositions of the bog's emissions, we document the geography of what lies beneath. Occasionally, I attach myself to an Archive Whale's flank, riding along as it navigates the deeper basins. In exchange for this transportation, I use my more nimble form to retrieve objects from narrow crevices that interest the whales. They seem particularly drawn to fragments of the machines that have fallen from above and sunk into the depths—components that glow with unexpected light when brought into their presence.

The Chronotics distrust us. They perceive, correctly, that our mapping creates a kind of counter-narrative to the temporal distortions they cultivate. Where they embrace discontinuity, we document continuity. The Undersong carries the true history of the Levelsea, unchanged by temporal anomalies. This makes us dangerous to whatever purpose the machines serve.

My most sacred task involves the cataloging of what we call the Submerged Architectures—vast geometric structures hundreds of meters below the surface that appear to predate the machines. These structures emit no sound but somehow absorb and redirect the Undersong, creating acoustic shadows that my ancestors learned to interpret. The patterns suggest these were not merely foundations but data storage systems of unimaginable complexity. Some Echocartographers believe the machines above were built to extract information from these deeper structures—that what appears to be harvesting of gases or plants is merely a byproduct of a more profound archaeological operation.

When human researchers venture too deep into the Levelsea, I sometimes follow them, curious about these air-breathing visitors who move so awkwardly through our realm. They never notice me, focused as they are on the more visible inhabitants and the dramatic machines. This suits me well. The Echocartographers have survived by remaining chroniclers rather than subjects of chronicle.

In rare moments when the temporal anomalies are at their peak, I can sense something few others perceive—rhythmic alterations in the deep currents that suggest the presence of the Predecessor. Unlike the surface dwellers who see only a figure of suspended droplets, I detect what might be its true form: patterns of pressure and vibration that move against time's flow, restructuring the water itself into complex informational matrices.

These encounters leave fragments of knowledge embedded in my memory—glimpses of the Levelsea's purpose that I add to the Undersong, to be carried forward by future generations of Echocartographers long after I return to the depths from which we all emerged.
