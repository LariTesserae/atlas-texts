# A Guardian of Sound and Stone

I would be an elder in this world, one they call the Resonance Keeper—someone who walks the boundary between human and stone, between bird and sky. My life has become so interwoven with the plateau that I sometimes forget where my body ends and the living mountain begins.

My dwelling sits apart from the main settlement clusters, built where three underground streams converge beneath a weathered stone arch. The structure is round like all others, but mine lacks a proper roof. Instead, a spiral of open wooden slats creates a partial covering that allows both mist and birds to enter freely. The interior walls are lined with countless niches of different sizes where birds of various species come to rest, some staying for generations, others visiting only during specific migrations.

My ears have developed unusual sensitivity even for my people. The right ear specializes in the higher frequencies of small hovering birds and the whisper of water through stone; the left captures the deep resonances of the plateau's slow movement and the bass tones of the great soaring birds. The cartilage has grown into complex whorls that sometimes appear to shift subtly when I'm deeply focused on a particular sound. Children often stare at my ears with fascination when I visit the settlement.

I wear simple clothing of undyed fiber, but my ceremonial attire is distinctive—a cloak embedded with thin stone resonators that amplify certain frequencies when I move, creating harmonies with the bird calls around me. Around my neck hangs a pendant of polished stone with a perfect natural hole through which I sometimes whistle to communicate with specific bird species that other Aural People cannot reach.

My primary responsibility is teaching the deep listening—guiding adolescents as they develop their ability to hear the plateau's voice beneath their feet. For this, I use tools passed down through generations: tuning forks carved from the plateau's own stone that can detect subtle vibrations imperceptible to even our specialized ears; copper bowls that clarify certain frequencies when filled with water from specific springs; and maps etched on thin stone tablets that chart not just the physical landscape but the acoustic properties of different regions.

I maintain the most comprehensive sound library of our people—not written records, but carefully preserved audio memories stored in specialized meditation practices and occasionally transferred to apprentices through intense training sessions. I can reproduce from memory the calls of birds that no longer visit our plateau, sounds that may prove vital if climate patterns shift again and these species return.

When new springs emerge, I am called to interpret their song. I spend days beside newly formed pools, listening not just to the water but to how birds respond to it, how mist forms above it, how the stone around it settles. From these observations, I help determine whether dwellings should be built nearby, which chorus should tend the area, and what water channels might harmonize with the plateau's intention.

I rarely sleep more than a few hours at a time, instead practicing what we call "rest-listening"—a state between wakefulness and dreaming where the boundaries between self and environment dissolve. During these periods, I sometimes perceive patterns too subtle for conscious detection: the precursors to geological shifts, the emotional states of distant bird flocks, or atmospheric changes that will affect our water supply.

My stone name, received sixty years ago during my first deep listening, translates roughly to "Hollow Through Which Wind Becomes Voice." My sound name mimics the call of a small blue-feathered bird that visits only during the Waiting season—a bird believed to carry messages between our world and whatever exists beyond perception.

Though I hold an honored position, my life is solitary in many ways. My heightened sensitivity to sound makes extended time in the settlement overwhelming, especially during festivals. Young apprentices visit regularly for training, but I spend most days in communion with the plateau itself, my bare feet in constant contact with the stone, my ears attuned to its subtle changes.

I am neither male nor female in the traditional sense—my people recognize that those with my particular gift often exist between such categories, our bodies responding more to the plateau's needs than to human patterns. My voice can shift across octaves, sometimes reproducing female bird calls with perfect pitch, other times resonating with male territorial announcements. This fluidity is considered a necessary adaptation for my role.

In recent seasons, I've felt the plateau shifting more rapidly beneath us. New patterns emerge in the stone's voice, and birds arrive whose ancestors have never visited our home. The other elders look to me with questions I cannot yet answer, but I spend each dawn in the deepest listening chambers, pressing my entire body against the living stone, seeking understanding of what our home is becoming—and how we must change with it to survive the birth of this new world.
