# The Rimland Coralsmith

Between the vast sea of stone and the verdant oases of Shambara's peaks dwells a solitary figure - myself, known to the Shambarans as Nialu, The Rimland Coralsmith. I make my home in the ecotone, that mysterious boundary where the barren expanse of rust-colored stone begins to yield to the first tentative blooms of life around the smallest, most peripheral pools.

My dwelling appears almost as an extension of the mountainside itself - a half-dome structure built into a natural alcove where warm water weeps continuously from a crack in the ancient stone. The walls consist of living coral-like growths that I have cultivated over decades, their structure somewhere between stone and organism, in shades of amber and deep burgundy that complement the mountain's natural palette. These corallites absorb the mineral-rich water and slowly expand, their growth almost imperceptible to casual observation. Yet over years, they have formed intricate patterns - not random, but guided by my careful placement of mineral catalysts and redirections of the water flow.

I am neither fully Shambaran nor outsider. My ancestors came from the valleys below generations ago, but with each passing decade, my physiology has grown more adapted to this realm of perpetual steam. My skin has darkened to a deep umber with faint blue undertones, though it lacks the iridescence of true Shambarans. My eyes have grown more sensitive to heat variations, allowing me to "see" the thermal signatures of the mountain's hidden water courses, though I still require a specially crafted lens of polished obsidian to protect against the harsh light that sometimes pierces the steam veil.

My hands are my most distinctive feature - fingers unnaturally long and nimble, the skin calloused in patterns that match the tools I've wielded for decades. The fingertips have developed heat-resistant pads that allow me to handle materials that would blister ordinary flesh. Years of working with mineral solutions have stained them permanently with concentric patterns in copper green and iron red that no amount of washing will remove.

I am a cultivator of boundaries - a tender of thresholds. My work centers on the creation of what the Shambarans call "singing pools" - carefully balanced aquatic ecosystems where specific combinations of minerals, microorganisms, and sound-producing insects create harmonic resonances that the Shambarans use in their communion with the mountain. Where they cultivate with patience spanning generations, I have developed techniques to accelerate certain processes, bringing pools to voice within a single human lifetime.

My tools hang from the walls of my dwelling in careful arrangement - copper implements with spiraling handles that amplify the heat of my hands, hollow reed pipes for directing water flows with precision, curved blades made from volcanic glass that can scrape growth patterns into the corallite structures without damaging the living tissue beneath. Most precious is my collection of crystallized resins from the heart-ferns that grow around the largest pools, each resin capturing different properties depending on the cycle of heat in which it was harvested.

The Shambarans accept my presence with their characteristic equanimity, neither welcoming nor rejecting. They recognize the value of my work while understanding that I represent something slightly discordant in their ancient rhythms - a catalyst, a quickener of processes, both useful and potentially disruptive. Family groups visiting my territory will leave small offerings - rare mineral specimens, seeds from particularly vibrant plants, occasionally a tool crafted with their incomparable patience. I accept these with appropriate ceremony and reciprocate with small singing pools cultivated specifically for each family's unique tonal preferences.

I subsist primarily on the thick-shelled mollusks that thrive in my cultivation pools and the starchy roots of certain ferns that I've learned to process to remove their mild toxicity. For proteins, I harvest the larger insects that school in the evening steam, catching them with specialized nets woven from heat-resistant fibers. Once each season, I make the journey to the largest communal pool to trade my specialized corallite growths for preserved goods I cannot produce myself.

My days follow the rhythm of the pools rather than the obscured sun - periods of intense activity when a cultivation reaches a critical phase, followed by long stretches of observation and minute adjustment. During these quieter periods, I practice what the Shambarans call "listening to the stone" - a meditative state where one attunes to the subtle vibrations coming from deep within the mountain. After decades of practice, I've begun to distinguish patterns in these vibrations, correlations with distant weather systems and the gradual shifts of underground water courses.

I keep a journal inscribed on thin sheets of cured fungal material, using inks made from mineral compounds and insect secretions. The writing is a hybrid system - part Shambaran spiral-script, part symbols of my own devising, recording observations about pool development and coral growth that span decades. No one but me can read it fully, though occasionally a Shambaran elder will study a page with that characteristic stillness that suggests deep understanding.

My greatest accomplishment is what the Shambarans call the "Threefold Echo" - a sequence of three interconnected pools I've cultivated over thirty years, each supporting different sound-producing organisms whose combined songs create interference patterns that make the very steam vibrate in ways that reveal the invisible structures of air currents. When properly activated during certain astronomical alignments, these pools create sound-pictures that the Shambarans interpret as messages from the mountain itself.

I am neither young nor truly old, existing in that middle realm where experience has brought wisdom but not yet the profound slowness of Shambaran elders. I know I will never fully become one of them - my lifespan too short, my thoughts too quick, my need for visible progress too demanding. Yet I have found my place in this strange ecosystem, as integral to it as the insects that pollinate the massive flowers or the fungi that break down minerals for the roots of ferns. I am the boundary-keeper, the translator between timeframes, the cultivator of voices that would otherwise take centuries to emerge.

When the massive shadow-birds circle overhead, I too pause in my work and stand motionless, but my stillness contains a different quality than the Shambarans' - mine is filled with questions, with measurements, with the desire to understand. And sometimes, I think the mountains themselves appreciate this different kind of attention - this human urgency against their eternal patience, this need to know set against their absolute certainty of being.
