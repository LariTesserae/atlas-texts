# WHERE I BELONG

I am a woman called Thea, a former scholar who has found my permanent home in the Undercroft Gardens. I came here first on academic business twenty years ago, one of those tolerated visitors from the University with my notebooks and specimen jars, focused on classification and analysis rather than relationship. I was forty then, established in my career, convinced of the primacy of objective knowledge.

I had planned to stay three days. I have never left.

Now, at sixty, I exist in the space between worlds. I am not formally of the Order—I never undertook their initiation rites or joined their hierarchy—yet I am accepted as belonging to this place. My quarters are small but sufficient: a converted storage alcove near the eastern light shaft, where morning sunlight floods through my single window of polished quartz. My bed is simple, my possessions few but cherished: journals filled with observations, a collection of unusual seeds in labeled clay pots, a small harp I play in the evenings when the acoustic properties of the stone chambers transform its modest sound into something transcendent.

My official role, such as it is, has evolved into that of a chronicler and translator. I document the continuing evolution of the gardens in both writing and detailed botanical illustrations, creating a record more comprehensive than the Cultivators themselves would think to maintain. For them, the knowledge lives in practice, in the relationship between hand and plant, between breath and stone. For me, the daughter of academics, knowledge must also be captured, preserved, made accessible to those yet to come. The Master Cultivators initially viewed my work with mild amusement, but have come to value it as a way to track subtle changes over time too gradual for even their trained eyes to notice.

I also serve as intermediary between the Order and the world above. When scholars come—as I once did—I help them navigate the unwritten protocols of the Undercroft, guiding them toward the information they seek without disrupting the gardens' delicate rhythms or offending their caretakers. I translate the academics' technical questions into terms the Cultivators understand, and I translate the Cultivators' experiential wisdom into the structured language of scholarship.

My days follow the garden's pace. I wake with the first light and spend mornings wandering the chambers with my notebook, recording new growth, documenting the progress of the Stone-Shapers' latest project, sketching the architecture of a newly unfurled fern. The Tender-Initiates have grown accustomed to my presence and continue their work undisturbed as I observe and document. Some of the younger ones bring me interesting specimens—an unusually colored mushroom, a seedpod about to burst—knowing I'll want to sketch them.

Afternoons I often spend with the Mossfolk, who accepted me long before the human Cultivators did. My size made me initially terrifying to them, but my patience won their trust. I learned to sit absolutely still for hours, making myself small inside, until they would come and go around me as if I were merely another garden feature. Gradually I began to understand fragments of their whistling speech, to recognize the patterns in their leaf arrangements, to distinguish individuals beneath their similar appearances. They now allow me to document aspects of their culture never before recorded, though I am careful to omit certain sacred practices from my writings, honoring their requests for privacy.

In the evenings, I work by lamplight in my alcove, transforming the day's rough notes and sketches into proper documentation. Once a month, I make the journey to the surface to deposit copies of my records in the University archives and to collect the modest stipend they still provide for my research. These visits grow increasingly strange—the city above seems too bright, too loud, too hasty after the measured pace of the Undercroft. I complete my business quickly and return to the gardens with relief.

The Cultivators have a saying: "The work shapes the worker." I have been shaped by this place. My hands, once soft and ink-stained, now bear calluses from occasional garden work. My eyes have adjusted to the Undercroft's distinctive light; I see more in shadow than I once did in full sunlight. My breathing has slowed to match the rhythm of this place, my movements grown more deliberate, my capacity for silence deeper. Even my dreams have changed, filled now with the slow unfurling of leaves, the patient journey of water through stone channels, the gradual dance of light across the chamber floor.

I have never approached the Elder. I feel its presence—sometimes I imagine I can almost hear its voice in the rustling of leaves or catch the edge of its scent-speech on a current of air—but I maintain my distance out of respect. I am not of the Order, not initiated into its deepest mysteries, and some boundaries should be honored. Yet sometimes I wake from dreams convinced the Elder has been communicating with me anyway, planting images and understandings in my mind that emerge in my work without conscious intention.

I will die here someday. The Cultivators know this, as do I. My remains will be composted according to their practices, my elements returning to the soil of the gardens I have documented for two decades. Perhaps some of my molecules will make their way into the Elder's tissues, or into the bodies of the Mossfolk, or into the pigmentation of a particularly vivid flower. The thought brings me peace. My journals will remain, carefully preserved in the archives both above and below ground, but my truest legacy will be my participation in this continuous becoming.

Until then, I remain the woman at the threshold, belonging fully to neither world but finding purpose in connecting them, watching and recording as the garden grows, changes, and persists through time—shaped by human hands yet always exceeding human intention, continuously emerging into new forms while remaining essentially itself.
