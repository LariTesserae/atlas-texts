# A Voice Among the Vines

I am Lisha, a young tender in the House of Spiral Growth, responsible for the training of climbing vines across the eastern terraces of Eshalar. My name means "one who listens to tendrils" in the old tongue. I was born during the Season of Copper Waters, when the springs run rich with minerals that turn the channels a gentle rusted hue.

My hands bear the early markings of my discipline—calluses along my left palm from guiding young shoots, and small permanent stains on my fingertips from the sticky resins of the moonthread vines I specialize in. The scarification patterns on my forearms are still simple, showing only my lineage and apprenticeship completion, though I hope to earn the intricate spiral motif of a master tender before the turning of five more seasons.

I rise before dawn each morning, while mist still clings to the stone channels. This is when the vines are most receptive, having spent the night extending their reaching fingers toward new support. I move silently through my assigned terraces, a section of gardens where three varieties of climbing plants interweave to form living walls that protect more delicate growths from the occasional harsh winds that sweep down from the higher peaks.

My tools are few but precious—a thin rod of flexible wood for guiding new growth, copper wire for creating temporary supports, and a small knife carved from volcanic glass for precise pruning. Most important is the small pottery vessel I wear on a cord around my neck, containing a mixture of spring water collected during the last full moon and essence from mature vines, which I apply with my fingertips to encourage new growth at specific junction points.

What outsiders don't understand about our work is that it isn't about forcing the plants to our will. It's a conversation. When I approach a reluctant climbing spiral, I don't immediately reach to redirect it. I sit beside it, my fingers lightly touching the soil around its base, and I listen. Sometimes for an hour or more, I feel its resistance, understand its preference for another direction. Only then do I begin the gentle negotiation—offering alternatives through slight adjustments to water flow, tiny additions of specific minerals, or the placement of new support structures that might better serve both our purposes.

The elders say I have unusual sensitivity to the flowering moonthread vines, whose pale blossoms open only during the three nights of the full moon. These plants produce a nectar that, when properly prepared, enhances the deep tending state, allowing gardeners to perceive more subtle communications from their charges. Last season, my section produced a record yield of these blossoms, earning me my first recognition mark—a small circular scar at the base of my thumb that still fills me with quiet pride.

I wear my hair in the traditional style of vine tenders—long and braided with copper wire that catches the light, creating patterns that mimic the trellising systems we design. The older tenders say this helps the plants recognize us, though the younger ones sometimes joke that we're trying to look like the vines we tend. Perhaps both are true.

My greatest challenge remains patience. While I've mastered the technical aspects of my work, I still struggle with what the elders call "growth-time consciousness"—truly perceiving the world at the pace of the plants rather than human urgency. During my meditative sessions, Master Evren often places a hand on my shoulder and whispers, "Slower, Lisha. Think in seasons, not days."

I've noticed changes in myself over the years of tending. My fingers have grown slightly longer, the webbing between them more pronounced than in my childhood. When I stand still among my vines for long periods, small tendrils sometimes reach toward me, gently wrapping around my wrists or ankles—not to constrain but in recognition. The other tenders say this is rare, that the plants have accepted me as part of their colony rather than an outside influence.

At night, I dream of spiral patterns and upward growth. Sometimes in these dreams, I am not tending the vines but am one of them, feeling the exquisite slow unfurling of new leaves, the satisfying stretch toward sunlight, the patient exploration of my tendrils seeking new support. I've shared these dreams with Elder Nima, who tends the ancient thornless bramble on the highest terrace. She smiled and told me, "The boundary between tender and tended becomes more permeable with true understanding. This is the beginning of deep knowledge."

Today I begin a new project—creating a living bridge of intertwined moonthread and strongstem vines to connect two terraces that have long been separated by a gap too wide to cross. The Watershed Council believes this connection will improve water flow throughout the eastern gardens. It will take three growing seasons if all goes well, but the vines and I have already begun our conversation about how we might grow together toward this new possibility.

As the afternoon light filters through the mist rising from the channels, I sit with my back against the warm stone wall, watching the subtle movements of my vines reaching incrementally toward the support structures I've prepared. My fingertips rest lightly on the soil, feeling the gentle vibrations of growth beneath the surface. In this moment, I am exactly where I belong—neither fully human nor fully plant, but something in between. A translator. A bridge. A tender of connections in the eternal gardens of Eshalar.
