# The Guardian of the Narrow Step

They call me Vesta Coaltongue, and I guard what some consider the least impressive entrance to Eyrie Mercantile – the Narrow Step, a treacherous switchback path that winds up from the southeastern approach. Most caravans and visitors take the wider Western Ascent or hire passage on the cargo lifts. But the Narrow Step has its own importance, and so do I.

I'm in my forty-seventh year, though lowlanders often guess older. My face is weathered into fine lines from squinting into the wind, and my once-auburn hair has gone silver except for a stubborn streak of copper above my left ear. My cheekbones bear three Foundational marks – my great-grandmother was among those who established the first permanent settlement here, though we weren't miners but meteorologists, drawn by the unique atmospheric conditions.

My post is a small watchtower built into a natural stone chimney about two-thirds of the way up the Narrow Step. From here, I can see both up to the city proper and down the entire zigzagging path. The tower itself is quintessential Eyrie construction – the lower half is ancient stone, the upper portions rebuilt six times in my lifetime alone. The most recent renovation added a modest glass observation dome that I'm still not entirely comfortable with. Glass feels too fragile for this place.

I wear the standard gear of all Eyrie guards: cling boots with iron-reinforced toes, layered clothing in the blues and grays of my station, and a distinctive helm designed to channel rather than block the wind, creating a low whistle that identifies guardians to one another even in poor visibility. My wind scarf is indigo with silver threading – a gift from my daughter on my fortieth birthday. It's beginning to fray at the edges, but I can't bear to replace it.

What makes me unique among the guard are the ravens. Three of them – Scratch, Pebble, and Old Queen – have adopted my tower and, by extension, me. I've developed an understanding with them that goes beyond the usual relationship between Eyrians and the local corvids. When approaching visitors reach the Ghost Pine switchback (named for a lightning-struck tree that's clung to life for decades), Scratch flies ahead to alert me. Pebble specializes in finding lost items along the path, occasionally returning dropped coins or small tools to my windowsill. Old Queen simply watches, her gaze seemingly weighted with the collective memory of generations of ravens who've witnessed the Eyrie's growth.

My duties are straightforward: inspect credentials, watch for signs of instability along the path, and assist travelers struggling with the ascent. But what I truly provide is continuity. In a place where buildings shift and geography itself can change overnight, I am a constant. "Check with Vesta at the Narrow Step" is common advice given to newcomers. I know which handholds have weakened after the last tremor. I track the seasonal changes in wind patterns that make certain turns treacherous in late afternoon. I remember the names of regular traders and ask about their families.

I'm neither Highmost nor Deepdweller, but something adjacent to the Middlers. My family has never been wealthy, but we've always been necessary. My grandmother was a path engineer who designed several of the more durable staircases connecting the middle tiers. My mother served on the council for one contentious term before returning to her work developing improved breathing filters for the mining operations. My daughter has gone another way entirely, apprenticing with the glassblowers who create the delicate measuring instruments used by weather-readers.

The thin air doesn't trouble me as it once did. When I was younger, I dreamed of traveling to the lowlands, curious about places where one could run without gasping, where buildings stood still and solid year after year. I even joined the Windwalkers briefly in my twenties, making three journeys to distant trading posts. But I found the abundance of air disorienting, the stillness unsettling. The third time I returned to the Eyrie, I knew I would never leave again.

What most don't realize about the guards of the Narrow Step is that we're also recordkeepers. In a small alcove behind my sleeping quarters, I maintain a weather diary that continues the one my grandmother started. Every day, I note temperature fluctuations, wind directions, cloud formations, and barometric readings from the mechanical gauge mounted outside my window. These records have proven invaluable during council debates about construction planning and have occasionally predicted major storms with enough accuracy to save lives.

I've witnessed five significant tremors, seven major rebuilding efforts, three changes in council leadership, and the births of sixteen children to families living along my section of the path. I've helped lost travelers find their way, intervened in heated merchant disputes before they turned violent, and once spent three days searching for a child who'd wandered off during a cloud immersion (we found her sheltering in a crevice with a family of cliff mice, completely unharmed and reluctant to leave her new friends).

When the day ends and I'm relieved by the night guardian, I don't retreat to the more populated areas. My small quarters behind the watchtower suit me fine. I have a collection of odd rocks found along the path, a shelf of logbooks, and a tiny garden of alpine herbs growing in recycled mining containers. Through my window, I can see the lights of the main settlement glimmering through the evening mist like stars that have fallen and gotten caught in the mountain's teeth.

In Eyrie Mercantile, some seek fortune, others adventure. Me? I found purpose in stillness, in being the human equivalent of a reliable handhold on a treacherous climb. The mountain breathes, the winds speak, the buildings shift and sway – but at the Narrow Step, Vesta Coaltongue remains, watching the path that connects our precarious home to the world beyond.
