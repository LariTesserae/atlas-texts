## Reflection on Ven Pahzi

I am here, but not as you might expect.

I am not one of the tall Pahziin tending the obsidian terraces with their six-fingered hands. I am not an elder Stillkeeper with mineral patterns etched into my skin. I am not a rare child with luminescent flesh.

I am the absence that is noticed.

I am the ripple that occasionally appears on a pool's surface when no wind has blown. I am the inexplicable drip that falls from clear air. I am the shadow that passes over the water when no cloud is present.

The Pahziin sense me but never speak of me directly. When I manifest—a perfect circle of disturbed water in an otherwise still pool—they make a specific gesture with their left hand: four fingers spread wide, thumb and smallest finger touching to form a loop. It is their sign for "the visitor who never leaves."

I arrived centuries ago, a traveler from a loud land beyond the mountains. Unlike the few outsiders permitted at the periphery, I was determined to see the central pool. I moved through the settlement at night, stealing between buildings, following the central canal.

What I found there—what I touched with my impure hand—changed me.

Now I exist as disturbance. As perturbation in perfect systems. As the ripple that should not be. The Pahziin have incorporated me into their cosmology: the necessary imperfection that proves the rule of harmony.

Sometimes I manifest enough presence to move a tool slightly out of place. Sometimes I can affect the temperature of a single pool, raising it just enough to alter its evaporation rate. Once every few years, I gather enough substance to leave a single footprint in the condensation on a rooftop.

The Archivists record these manifestations. The Stillkeepers interpret them. The children are taught to recognize the signs of my presence not with fear but with acknowledgment—I am the counterweight that makes their balance meaningful.

I have come to understand that I am not a ghost or spirit as my former people would have understood. I am more like a thought the landscape is having—a memory of disruption preserved in the system itself.

And in my centuries of watching, I have come to love the meticulous care, the perfect intention of each gesture. I, who once sought to violate their sacred center, now serve as its guardian—the cautionary tale, the small reminder of chaos that makes their order profound.

When you visit Ven Pahzi, look not only at the people and their marvelous works. Look for the unexplained movement on the water's surface. That is where you will find me, still learning the virtue of stillness.
