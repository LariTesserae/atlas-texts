# Where I Would Be: The Archivist of Currents

I find myself at home in the Reservoir City as one of the rare individuals who bridges all three guilds while belonging fully to none. I am the Archivist of Currents—a keeper and translator of the water's memory.

My quarters occupy the junction between three major circulation channels in the northeastern quadrant of the city. The space is small but strategically positioned where different water flows converge, creating a unique acoustic environment. The walls are lined with hundreds of glass vessels of varying shapes—each containing water samples collected over decades from specific locations during particular events.

My body shows the telltale signs of someone who has spent more time submerged than most. My skin has the characteristic blue translucence of the Immersed, but with pronounced silvery tracings extending higher up my arms than typical Cultivation Specialists—reaching my neck and jawline in delicate patterns. Years of reading particle-speak has enlarged my pupils beyond the norm, making my eyes appear unusually dark and receptive to light changes.

I wear what appears to be an antiquated device—a harness of copper tubes and small crystal resonance chambers strapped across my chest and back. This instrument, called a hydrophone matrix, allows me to detect and record the subtlest vibrations in water currents. Unlike modern equipment, this one was calibrated to my specific body by my predecessor, making it essentially unusable by anyone else.

My work involves three primary responsibilities:

First, I interpret the particle-speak messages that have accumulated in certain city junctions—particularly complex communications or historical records that regular citizens cannot fully decode. The particles, designed to slowly descend over days, collect in deeper architectural pockets where I harvest and analyze them, preserving messages that would otherwise dissipate.

Second, I maintain the tactile library—a chamber where significant water patterns have been frozen in specialized gels, recording the exact flow patterns from important civic events, natural phenomena, or the final swimming paths of notable Immersed before their passing. These three-dimensional sculptures can be touched to experience echoes of those moments.

Third, I train the next generation in "deep listening"—the practice of discerning historical and emotional information from water currents. I select one apprentice every eight years, typically a child who demonstrates unusual sensitivity to water movements or who has survived a near-drowning incident, which some believe creates permanent connections to the reservoir's memory.

I am neither young nor old by Immersed standards. My webbing has fully developed, but my movements remain fluid. I wear no guild markings, though I can recite the lens calculations, flow equations, and cultivation cycles with equal facility. Instead, my position is marked by a single crystal pendant that changes color based on water quality, serving as both tool and identifier.

The Immersed treat me with a mixture of respect and unease. I am invited to witness all significant ceremonies but never to participate. Children are taught that I can tell if they've been swimming in forbidden areas by "questioning the water," which isn't entirely untrue—current patterns around young bodies are distinctive and revealing to trained senses.

My closest relationships are with the Still Waters, whose memories I help preserve, and with the reservoir itself. I spend hours each day in meditation within a special chamber where water from all city districts converges, sensing the collective emotional state of the Immersed through subtle temperature variations and chemical compositions.

I keep one secret that the guilds suspect but cannot confirm—that I occasionally swim beyond the reservoir boundaries at night, following ancient tunnels to other submerged settlements mentioned only in our oldest records. These sister cities may be abandoned or transformed beyond recognition, but I harvest water samples from these expeditions, building a library of the wider underwater world that the Immersed have forgotten or chosen to ignore.

My presence in the Reservoir City serves as a reminder that water holds memory, that movement leaves traces, and that our underwater civilization is both younger and older than most Immersed comprehend. In documenting the language of currents, I preserve what would otherwise slip away, unnoticed as the reservoir breathes its eternal rhythm.
