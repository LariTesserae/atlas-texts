# The Fog Singer

I am one of the Fog Singers of Brynwater, though outsiders might call me a mere street musician. We are neither as revered as the Mist Readers nor as essential as the Water Keepers, but our role in the city's life is no less vital.

My instrument is a peculiar one, crafted from the hollowed branches of the gnarled trees that survive on our plateau. It resembles a flute but with curved chambers that hold small amounts of collected water. As I play, the notes interact with the liquid, creating sounds that seem to both part the mist and become one with it. The tones shift with the density of the fog, making each performance unique to that specific moment in Brynwater's endless communion with the clouds.

My fingers bear calluses not from the instrument itself but from the constant work of adjusting its water levels. The markings on my fingertips—pale spirals that form naturally from constant contact with moisture and wood—identify me to other Brynwater citizens as clearly as the Water Keepers' blue tattoos.

I make my home in the Whisper District, where the buildings curve inward to create natural amphitheaters. My dwelling is modest—a single room with walls of local granite worn smooth by generations of my family before me. The ceiling features a small aperture that collects specific forms of mist, which I store in ceramic vessels of varying shapes. Each vessel produces a different resonance when the collected water is later used in my instrument.

My days begin before most citizens stir. I climb to particular corners of Brynwater where the morning fog gathers most densely, playing melodies that help define the city's waking rhythms. Children learn to tell time by which song echoes through their neighborhood; merchants time the opening of their stalls to specific refrains.

The elders say we Fog Singers began as practical communicators—our ancestors developed musical patterns to convey messages across the plateau when visibility was poor. Over centuries, these signals evolved into something more essential: we became the keepers of Brynwater's emotional weather. Our songs express what the typically reserved citizens often cannot—joy, grief, anticipation, warning.

My most important duty comes during the Thinning—those rare days when the fog briefly retreats and exposes the dizzying drop from our plateau's edge. The sudden clarity disorients many citizens, creating a specific anxiety known as "edge sickness." On these days, I play constantly, moving through affected neighborhoods with melodies specifically composed to recreate the comforting acoustics of the fog. The music helps re-establish the auditory boundaries the mist usually provides.

I have heard many stories of what lies below our plateau, though I've never ventured to the edge to see for myself. Some Fog Singers claim our music sometimes receives answers—faint, strange harmonies rising from the depths that match no instrument known in Brynwater. I neither confirm nor deny these tales when asked, but I do leave small offerings—perfectly tuned water droplets in tiny stone cups—at certain city boundaries on moonless nights.

Unlike the other specialized roles in our society, Fog Singers are not born to our position but chosen by it. My own calling came when I was nine years old, during a period of unusually thin mist. I developed a persistent cough that produced no discomfort but created perfect, sustained tones when I exhaled. A senior Fog Singer heard me and recognized the sign. I was apprenticed that same day.

There are only seventeen of us in the entire city. We know each other by sound alone—I could identify my fellows by their playing even if blind and standing at the opposite end of Brynwater. We rarely gather together, as our collective music might disrupt the city's carefully balanced acoustics. The exception comes during the annual Water Ceremony, when our combined playing creates the harmonics necessary to vibrate the ceremonial liquid at exactly the right frequency.

My relationship with Brynwater's other citizens is complex. Children follow me through the streets, attempting to mimic my playing with makeshift instruments. Adults nod respectfully but maintain a careful distance, understanding that too much conversation might alter the tonal quality of my voice. The Mist Readers occasionally request specific ancient melodies, claiming they make certain patterns more legible in the fog. I comply without questioning why.

The Stonewrights value my services particularly—they believe certain stones will only reveal their true qualities when exposed to the right musical vibrations. I spend every third afternoon playing for newly quarried rock, watching as the master crafters place their ears against the stone, nodding when my notes help them determine where to cut or how to shape.

I own little beyond my instruments and water vessels, yet I want for nothing. Citizens ensure I have food and shelter, leaving offerings outside my door before dawn. In return, I give Brynwater its voice—translating the whispers of stone and water and mist into something human ears can comprehend and human hearts can feel.

When I die, my primary instrument will be disassembled. Its water will be added to the central cistern during a quiet ceremony at dawn, and its wooden components will be buried beneath one of the city's rare trees. My secondary instruments will pass to those who show the particular sensitivity required of a Fog Singer, regardless of their age or prior occupation.

Until that day comes, I will continue my daily circuits of Brynwater, playing the city into being with each note, making audible the conversation between plateau and cloud that has continued since time immemorial. In a city defined by water and stone, I provide the third essential element—breath made visible through sound, connecting what is solid with what flows, just as our city itself stands between earth and sky.
