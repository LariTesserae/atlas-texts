# The Tide Scribe

I live in a small dwelling built where three massive mangroves have grown together, their trunks twisting into a natural archway. My home sits just high enough to stay dry during normal tides, but during the highest lunar floods, water laps at my doorstep. The locals know me as the quiet figure who comes and goes with the changing waters.

My physical form is mostly human—slender and weathered like driftwood—but my skin bears natural markings that resemble the patterns of light filtering through canopy leaves. These patterns shift subtly with the phases of the moon, growing more pronounced during full tides. My eyes reflect light like a nocturnal creature's, appearing almost silvery in darkness.

I am a keeper of transition—moving between the village and the deepest parts of the mangrove where few dare to venture. The villagers respect my role but maintain a cautious distance. They come to me when they need messages carried to remote settlements, or when they seek knowledge about the forest's changing moods.

My true purpose, unknown to most, is to document the relationship between the mangrove and its invisible guardian. I possess a rare ability to perceive the Breath Taker more clearly than others—not just sensing its presence, but actually seeing the intricate patterns of collected breath as they flow through the hollow. Using pigments made from crushed shells, mangrove bark, and the bioluminescent lichens that grow in the hollows, I transcribe these patterns onto specially prepared leaves that preserve the records.

I serve as witness and translator between worlds. During the highest tides when the Breath Taker disperses throughout the forest, I sit in my doorway with water at my feet, writing rapidly as impressions flow through me. These trance-like sessions produce texts that become part of the village's oral history—warnings about coming storms, guidance for sustainable harvesting, or sometimes simply the ancient memories of the mangrove itself.

The Breath Taker tolerates my presence because I take only what is freely given. I have learned to offer my own breath consciously, exhaling stories of what I've witnessed in the human realm. In return, I'm occasionally granted insights that help the village adapt to changes in the delicate ecosystem we inhabit.

My lifespan exceeds human norms—I have watched children grow into elders—yet I age, just slowly. The villagers believe I was once fully human, transformed gradually by spending too many nights in the breathing rooms. Perhaps they're right. I no longer remember a time before the mangroves.

What I know is this: as long as there are breathing rooms and as long as the Old One continues its ancient vigil, I will continue my work—recording the silent conversation between forest and water, between breath and memory, between what is seen and what merely exists at the corner of vision, felt more than perceived.
