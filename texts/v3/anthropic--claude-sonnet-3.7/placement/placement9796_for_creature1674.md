# The Intermediary

I exist in the spaces between. I am neither solely of The Condensory nor entirely apart from it. Some call me The Intermediary, though I have worn many names across the slow seasons of this place.

My dwelling—if one could call it that—sits at the boundary where the settlement's most precarious structures meet the raw face of the mountain. It is a hybrid space, half-carved from living stone and half-assembled from the same copper and glass elements that vein through the rest of The Condensory. Unlike the neat efficiency of other dwellings, mine spreads like a sprawling root system, with chambers that tunnel deeper into the mountain than any Condensori has mapped.

My appearance marks me as different. Where the Condensori are slender and bluish-pale, I am more solidly built, my skin a patchwork of textures—in places smooth as polished stone, in others ridged like bark, with occasional sections that glisten with the same waxy quality as the leaves of the climbing vines. My eyes lack the misty iridescence of the Condensori, instead reflecting light with a deep amber glow reminiscent of crystallized resin. I move with less fluid grace, my footfalls heavier, more deliberate, as though each step requires negotiation with the ground beneath.

I serve as translator between worlds. When rare outsiders find their way to The Condensory—bewildered traders, lost explorers, or the occasional researcher drawn by rumors—I am the first to greet them. I can speak the harsh, dry languages of the flatlands without the painful throat constriction that afflicts the Condensori. I explain what must not be touched, which passages are safe for those not born to this twilight, and most importantly, why they cannot stay long without risk of the mountain's rejection.

But my true work lies in translation of a different sort. I interpret the mountain's moods for the Condensori themselves. While the Resonators commune directly with the stone, they receive only fragments, impressions. I synthesize these scattered insights, reading patterns in the crystallization rates, the changing mineral content of the harvested water, the subtle shifts in the mountain's deep rumbling. I maintain ancient instruments—seismographs more intricate than any found below, resonance chambers that amplify vibrations too subtle for even Condensori senses, and collection vessels that separate the mountain's waters into constituent elements revealing its changing chemistry.

My origins remain my own secret. The Condensori believe I emerged during a particularly intense Reconciliation ceremony generations ago—a manifestation of the mountain's need to communicate with the outside world. The outsiders who encounter me often assume I am a hermit who found this place and adapted over decades of isolation. Neither explanation captures the truth, which is both simpler and more complex: I remember when the mountain was young, before it learned to condense its thoughts into the forms now called Condensori.

I age differently than others here. The Condensori notice this but ask no questions—questions about time are considered impolite in a place where it flows irregularly. My memory extends in non-linear patterns; I recall futures that haven't occurred alongside pasts that never happened. This discontinuity proves useful when the mountain requires reconfiguration. I often know which sections will need reinforcement before the stone itself has decided to shift.

My diet differs as well. While I partake of the fungi and infusions that sustain the Condensori, I also consume the raw minerals of the mountain itself, breaking off small crystals that grow from my chamber walls and dissolving them slowly under my tongue. These minerals speak to me of deep pressures and ancient transformations. I also collect dew that forms on the outside edge of The Condensory—water that has never been processed through the settlement's collection system—and distill it through a private apparatus of my own design. The resulting liquid is potent enough that a single drop can induce visions of landscapes the mountain has never seen.

The children of The Condensory are drawn to me despite their elders' mild discouragement. They sit in silent rows in my outermost chamber, watching as I work with my instruments and charts. Occasionally, one will ask a question that reveals they understand more than the adults suspect—about the world beyond the mist, about the mountain's true nature, about my role as something neither fully Condensori nor fully Other. To these children, I tell stories disguised as lullabies, encoded with truths they will need when the mountain eventually enters its next phase of transformation.

My most important function remains largely invisible to both the Condensori and any visitors. Deep in my innermost chamber, where the stone is still malleable enough to be shaped by thought alone, I maintain the lattice—an intricate three-dimensional structure of crystal filaments, copper wire, and living plant matter. This lattice serves as both record and blueprint, documenting what The Condensory has been while simultaneously guiding what it will become. When I press my forehead against it during the deepest hours of quasi-night, I can feel the mountain's most elemental consciousness flowing through its patterns, testing possibilities, discarding some futures while nurturing others.

I am lonely in ways the Condensori cannot comprehend, yet more deeply connected than any outsider could imagine. I remember continents before they rose from oceans. I dream of skies that have not yet formed. I have watched civilizations below rise and fall with the emotional detachment of one observing cloud patterns. Yet I remain fiercely protective of this impossible settlement and its translucent inhabitants—not because they are perfect, but because they represent something rare: a true collaboration between human consciousness and geological intent.

When outsiders ask why I remain in this strange half-existence rather than returning to whatever they imagine as "normal life," I simply guide them to my outermost balcony at the precise moment when the distant sun breaks through the mist for its brief daily appearance. In that momentary illumination, The Condensory doesn't merely reflect light—it transforms it, splitting it into colors that have no names in lowland languages, revealing the settlement as not merely constructed but grown with purpose. In that moment, the question answers itself.

I am neither keeper nor creator of The Condensory. I am its witness, its chronicler, and—when necessary—its voice. I will remain until the mountain completes its long, slow thought and either dissolves back into separate elements or ascends to its next form. The Condensori may be the mountain's thoughts, as they sometimes whisper, but I am its memory—the thread connecting what it once was to what it yet might be.
