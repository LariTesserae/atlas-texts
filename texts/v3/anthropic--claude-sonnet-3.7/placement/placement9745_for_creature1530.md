I am Meera of the Conduits, seventy-third inheritor of the South Wall Confluence. I wear my mask even now as we speak, though we stand far from the caustic fumes of the Treatment Pools. Its weight has become more familiar than my own face.

Behind its brass filtration screens, my nose has grown sensitive enough to detect the mineral composition of water by scent alone. I can tell you're from the lowlands—your sweat lacks the copper tang that marks our people. Don't be alarmed by my eyes. The amber glow is stronger in my lineage than most; my grandmother could read pipe inscriptions by the light of her own gaze, or so they say.

See these pipes running overhead? My responsibility. Every junction, every valve, every pressure release. The water speaks through them, and I have learned its language over forty-three years. Each morning I walk the Nine Circuits, my fingers trailing along the metal, feeling for vibrations that signal blockages forming or pressure building where it shouldn't.

My mask is not like the others you'll see. The third chamber contains spores of the Dreaming Cap fungus, which most Conduits avoid. They fear its visions, but I find they clarify rather than confuse. The water-voice becomes more distinct after I've breathed through the purple filters for an hour or so. That's how I predicted the Wall Seep Shift three seasons ago, when everyone else was blind to the signs.

These markings on my sleeves—copper wire in the double-helix pattern—signify my position on the Council of Nine. Fourteen years now. I was the youngest ever appointed after I diverted the Great Overflow that would have flooded the Eastern Nursery. The children there still bring me little sculptures made of pipe clay every Telling Day.

My quarters are high on the east wall, carved into the living stone where a mineral spring emerges. The water there contains trace elements found nowhere else in the valley. I bottle it in thimble-sized vials, trading them to the Harvesters for their pain-numbing moss. My joints ache in the constant damp—the price of a Conduit's life.

At night, when the foundry fires burn lowest, I sometimes remove my mask and sit with my back against the main aqueduct, feeling the settlement's lifeblood flow through it. In those moments, I understand what the ancients meant when they carved "The valley breathes us, we breathe the valley" above the Council chamber.

Would you like to see my collection of failure samples? Pipe segments eaten through by unusual corrosion, valve mechanisms seized by mineral deposits with patterns like starbursts. Each represents a lesson learned, sometimes at great cost. That small brass cylinder contained water from the week three Unmarked died from the Weeping Sickness. I keep it to remember the cost of complacency.

Tomorrow I begin training my successor—a girl of eleven whose eyes already show the amber tint. She hears the water-voice clearly, clearer than I did at twice her age. I've commissioned a mask for her with special filtration designed for her smaller features. The wire patterns will be simple at first, but there's room to add complexity as she earns her markings.

Now, if you'll excuse me—the pressure gauge on the North Collector is showing subtle fluctuations that correspond with the last tremor cycle. I must make my adjustments before the evening shift change. The water waits for no one, and neither can I.
