# The Archivist

In the semi-abandoned record room above the hexagonal Mother Furnace, I make my home among towers of ledgers, production journals, and glass sample cases that date back two hundred years. The constant rising heat from below keeps the room dry despite the district's pervasive moisture, making it the only place where paper can survive without growing mold or warping beyond use.

I was born Eliza Clearwater, daughter of a middle-tier Lumina family glassblower, but for twenty-seven years I have simply been "The Archivist" to most. Not quite Prism Born, I came into the world during a rare winter solstice when light struck the district at such a low angle that the buildings themselves seemed to burn from within. My eyes developed differently than most—one deep cobalt blue, the other a translucent gray that catches light strangely, seeming to fracture it internally like a prism.

While my hands proved nimble enough for the family craft, my true gift emerged early—an eidetic memory for formulas, techniques, and the oral histories passed down through generations. By twelve, I could recite the exact composition needed for any specialty glass produced in the district. By sixteen, I knew the complete lineage of every furnace in operation. The elders noticed, and when old Archivist Thomson's hands grew too arthritic to maintain the records, I was apprenticed to him instead of continuing in my family's workshop.

My quarters consist of a small cot wedged between tall shelving units, a writing desk positioned beneath the room's single large window, and a kitchenette where I prepare simple meals. The window itself is my own creation—a complex installation of thirty-seven different glass types that creates a calendar of light patterns across the floor as the seasons change. I read these patterns like others read clocks, knowing the district's rhythms without ever needing to step outside.

I dress practically in layers that can be adjusted as the day progresses and the heat from below intensifies. My clothes are chosen for their lack of loose threads or fibers that might contaminate glass samples. My fingers are perpetually stained—not with the blue of clay workers but with the varying inks I use to transcribe oral histories and technical specifications. A magnifying lens hangs around my neck on a leather cord, a tool I use to examine glass fragments brought for identification.

My days follow a careful routine. Mornings are spent updating production records as workshop representatives deliver their previous day's figures. Afternoons see me preserving and cataloging sample pieces from new techniques or particularly notable works. In the evenings, when the district transforms into its luminous twilight state, I often host elder glassmakers who dictate their knowledge while I transcribe, creating the living memory of techniques too subtle to capture in mere formula.

What few realize is that I am more than just a keeper of records. Over the years, I've become a repository of secrets. Hidden beneath the standard ledgers are my private journals containing formulas developed and lost, techniques abandoned for being too difficult or dangerous, and maps to clay deposits outside the district that only a handful know exist. I've recorded feuds between families that shaped production lines, love affairs that created unexpected collaborations, and accidents that resulted in miraculous discoveries.

I've developed certain peculiarities from my years amid the records. I can identify the workshop origin of a glass piece by its scent alone. My hearing has become attuned to the subtle differences in the ring of various glass compositions when struck. I sleep lightly, waking at the slightest change in the Mother Furnace's rhythm below, often before even the Furnace Keepers notice something amiss.

The district's inhabitants treat me with a mixture of respect and mild unease. I am neither fully part of their daily commerce nor separate from it. They understand that I see patterns they cannot—connections between techniques separated by decades, correlations between atmospheric conditions and glass clarity that emerge only when studying centuries of data. Children bring me interesting glass fragments found in the canals; masters consult me when attempting to revive ancient methods; even the Clay Diggers occasionally seek my counsel on historical mining locations.

My greatest work remains largely unknown. For fifteen years, I've been compiling what I call "The Complete Taxonomy of Light and Glass"—an attempt to catalog every possible interaction between the district's unique environment and the materials produced here. It's a massive undertaking, with thousands of observations of how light behaves in different conditions, weather patterns, and glass compositions. I believe that hidden in these patterns is something profound—perhaps the reason our district formed here in the first place, or why the adaptations in plants and animals occur as they do.

Sometimes, late at night when the furnaces burn lowest, I stand at my window and watch the glass snails traverse the wet cobblestones below, their translucent shells capturing moonlight. In those moments, I feel as though I too am slowly being transformed—not into glass exactly, but into something that exists between states: part human, part living record, as transparent and enduring as the materials we shape.

Few visit my quarters uninvited, but those who do often remark on a curious phenomenon—the air around me seems to hold tiny motes of light, suspended as if in glass. The older I grow, the more pronounced this becomes. Ellis Glasshart once told me, in a whisper, that the oldest archivists eventually fade entirely into this state—becoming living prisms, visible only when light strikes at certain angles. I don't know if I believe this, but sometimes when the light falls just so across my hands as I write, I see a transparency beginning at my fingertips that was not there in my youth.

Perhaps one day I will indeed become pure memory, preserved like an inclusion in the district's collective consciousness—a fitting fate for one who has devoted her life to ensuring nothing of value is ever truly lost in our glistening, ever-condensing world.
