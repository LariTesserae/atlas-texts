# The Song Catchers of Bhazaal

In the most breathtaking heights of the aerial cities, where the suspended pathways narrow and the winds speak in ancient tongues, lives a class of Whisperwings known to outsiders as the Song Catchers. While visiting scholars have bestowed this name upon them, within the silent language of air currents and wing patterns, they identify themselves with a particular undulation—a motion that suggests the gathering of something precious from nothingness.

Unlike most Whisperwings, whose crystalline growths form symmetrical patterns across their translucent bodies, Song Catchers develop distinctive spiral formations that begin at their throat and curve around their elongated necks. These living crystals are uniquely structured—hollow and intricate, containing microscopic chambers and passages. Where ordinary Whisperwings absorb moisture through specialized skin patches, Song Catchers have evolved something extraordinary: their throat crystals can extract sound itself from the mountain air.

Their dwellings hang from the highest fibers of the city, shaped like inverted bells with walls thinner than anywhere else in Bhazaal. These structures are not merely homes but instruments—each one calibrated over decades to resonate with specific frequencies carried by the mountain winds. When winds pass through the chasms between peaks, they create tones too subtle for most creatures to detect—echoes of the mountains slowly wearing away, the whispers of ice crystals forming and melting, the vibration of stone expanding and contracting with temperature.

Song Catchers spend their lives in patient collection. During specific alignments of the sun and moons, when wind patterns shift in predictable cycles, they enter a meditative suspension within their bell-homes. Their bodies remain motionless for days, their specialized crystals gradually absorbing the subtle harmonics. This process leaves visible traces—their normally translucent bodies temporarily take on faint colorations that correspond to the tones they've gathered, swirls of pale blues and violets flowing beneath their skin.

What they collect is not merely sound but something the Whisperwing culture considers far more valuable—the voice of the mountains themselves, a record of geological time compressed into vibration. Each Song Catcher specializes in a particular "voice" of Bhazaal: some collect the deep resonance of stone shifting, others the crystalline tinkle of ice formations, others still the hollow moan of wind through ancient erosion channels.

Every seventy-three cycles of the larger moon, the Song Catchers descend to the central cistern chambers for the Harmonic Confluence. Here, in a ceremony that lasts from one dawn until the next, they release their collected sounds back into the water through a precise beating of their four wings. The ripples carry the stored vibrations, combining them into what outsiders might call music but what the Whisperwings consider historical testimony—the mountains recounting their own autobiography.

Young Whisperwings absorb these harmonics through their skin during immersion, incorporating the mountain's memory into their own beings. It is believed that this is why the cities move in such perfect synchronicity with the mountains' erosion—the inhabitants carry within them an intuitive understanding of geological time and change.

Song Catchers rarely reproduce. When they do, their crystalline egg cases develop distinctive resonant chambers that hum softly for years before hatching. The young emerge already attuned to particular frequencies, their destiny determined before birth.

The eldest Song Catchers, those who have absorbed harmonics for centuries, develop an additional adaptation—their wing membranes become so thin they vibrate sympathetically with the mountains' voice without conscious effort. These elders eventually enter the Final Resonance, where they fly to remote crevices in the highest peaks, anchor themselves to the stone, and slowly crystallize completely, becoming living instruments that will continue to play the mountains' song for millennia after their consciousness has faded.

Human observers who have been permitted to witness the Harmonic Confluence report experiencing visions of the mountains as they existed millions of years ago—jagged, volcanic, and new. Others describe sensing the slow future collapse of the range into rolling hills over eons yet to come. Most leave with a profound shift in their perception of time itself, as if they've briefly experienced existence at the pace of stone rather than flesh.

The Song Catchers serve as memory for a civilization that exists beyond conventional time—archivists of erosion, librarians of wind, and translators for mountains that speak too slowly for most to hear.
