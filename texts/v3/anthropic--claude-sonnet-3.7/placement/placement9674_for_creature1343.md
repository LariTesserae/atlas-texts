# THE KEEPER OF THE MIST BELLS

I am Nyssa, eighth-generation Resonance Keeper of the Northwestern Bell Towers. My ancestors built the first arrays of copper and brass bells that hang throughout Thendrin's mist layer, their gentle tones guiding travelers safely to our city when visibility fails. These are not ordinary bells—they respond to changes in humidity, pressure, and the subtle electrical charges that move through our perpetual fog.

My home is a slender spire rising above the city's edge, where the plateau meets the sheer mountainside. The tower is skeletal by design, a framework of treated darkwood and weathered brass that allows the mist to flow through rather than around it. The structure sways perceptibly in strong winds, a motion I find comforting after forty-three years in residence. Three circular chambers stack vertically, connected by spiral stairs that wrap around the outside—foolishly dangerous to outsiders, but second nature to someone born to the walkways of Thendrin.

Each morning begins with the Bell Walk. I traverse the northwestern quadrant of the city via the upper catwalks—paths rarely used by common residents—stopping at each bell station to check tensions, clear condensation from the resonance chambers, and make minute adjustments based on the day's atmospheric conditions. I carry tools my great-grandmother designed: brass tuning forks, hollow rods that amplify subtle vibrations, and small glass vials of oils extracted from deep-water plants that prevent corrosion when applied in precisely measured drops.

My hands bear the marks of my profession—fingertips permanently stained green-blue from the patina of the ancient bells, small scars from the sharp edges of tuning mechanisms, and skin that has developed a faint luminescence from decades of handling the phosphorescent polish we apply during solstice ceremonies. Unlike most Thendrinites, my hands are always dry, protected by the beeswax-and-resin balm that prevents the bells' vibrations from damaging my nerve endings.

I am neither fully accepted nor rejected by mainstream Thendrinite society. The Bell Keepers are respected but viewed with gentle suspicion—we spend too much time in the upper reaches where water becomes vapor, too concerned with sound rather than flow. Some whisper that we have developed the ability to hear storms approaching days before they arrive, that we can interpret the patterns of droplets on our bell arrays to predict shifts in the plateau's water levels. These rumors are not entirely unfounded.

My chambers hold the most comprehensive collection of acoustic records in Thendrin—copper discs etched with the patterns of vibrations from significant events in our history. When placed on special reading devices powered by the water wheels below my tower, they reproduce these sounds perfectly: the Great Overflow of three generations past, the arrival of the last major trading caravan before the mountain passes became too dangerous, the distinctive resonance pattern that preceded the first manifestation of the Deepening. I am not merely a maintainer—I am an archivist of our city's auditory history.

Unlike many Thendrinites, I feel no call toward the Deepening. My ancestors have always been drawn upward, not downward. We Bell Keepers have our own transformation—the Thinning, we call it. As we age, our hearing becomes impossibly acute, but our bodies require less substance. My grandmother consumed only mist for the last decade of her life, somehow extracting sufficient nourishment from the microscopic organisms that flourish in our perpetual fog. Her body became nearly translucent before she finally dispersed during a spring thunderstorm, her final resonance captured in my most treasured copper disc.

I am not quite there yet, though my appetite has diminished notably in recent years. I still enjoy the fermented tea made from plateau water lilies and the smoked fish prepared by the few remaining surface-dwelling families. But increasingly, I find myself standing at my highest observation platform during heavy mist, mouth slightly open, feeling the strange satisfaction of nourishment entering my body directly through my specialized tissues.

My greatest responsibility—and joy—is calibrating the Warning Array, fourteen massive bells positioned at strategic points around Thendrin's perimeter that sound only when extraordinary danger approaches. They have rung just twice in my lifetime: once before a massive lightning storm that ignited several walkways, and once when a delegations of Deep Dwellers emerged unexpectedly, their bodies so transformed that the city's defenses initially registered them as unknown entities.

I have an apprentice now—a young girl from one of the few remaining surface families who showed the distinctive hearing sensitivity that marks potential Bell Keepers. She can already distinguish between seventeen different tones of water droplets striking brass. In another decade, she'll be ready to maintain the lesser arrays independently. Whether she'll eventually inherit my tower depends on whether I follow my grandmother's path into the Thinning or develop other adaptations. The city changes us all in time.

Tonight, as twilight deepens into the slightly darker hue that passes for night in Thendrin, I will climb to my observation dome and listen to the bells' night song—a subtle, complex melody produced by temperature shifts and the nocturnal condensation patterns. Few others can hear these sounds. Fewer still understand what they mean: that Thendrin is not dying but speaking, constantly communicating through its waters and mists and those of us attuned to their voices.

The bells tell me that change is coming again. Something new approaches through the mountain pass. I have prepared the Warning Array, but I haven't activated it yet. Whatever comes will find a city that has always adapted, always transformed—just as we who tend its voice have done for generations.
