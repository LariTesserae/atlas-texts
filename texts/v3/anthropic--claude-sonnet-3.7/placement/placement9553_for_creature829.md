# The Waterwright

I am Luren, Keeper of the Flow, though most simply call me the Waterwright. My domain is the intricate network of channels, cisterns, and waterwheels that give the Verdant Mills their power and purpose. I stand somewhere between the Rootwardens and the Geargrowers in our society's careful taxonomy—water being neither fully of the earth nor fully of our making.

My quarters sit at the confluence of three main canals, a round chamber where the ceiling opens to reveal a perfect circle of green-filtered sky. The walls curve without seams, grown from living heartwood over forty years before I was born. The floor slopes imperceptibly toward the center where a small pool reflects the changing light, its surface rarely still as water moves constantly beneath it through hidden channels.

I rise before dawn each day, when the Mills breathe out their evening moisture. My skin—darker than most Tenders, the color of wet cedar—feels the change in humidity immediately. The faint luminescence of my gill-like neck ridges dims as I leave the water-saturated air of sleep. These adaptations mark me as descended from the Deepwell lineage, Tenders whose ancestors specialized in submerged work and developed the ability to extract oxygen from water for short periods.

My tools hang from the curved walls: calibrated hollow reeds for measuring pressure, twisted willow wands that respond to underground currents, copper implements for the rare occasions when plant matter must be cut rather than persuaded. Most precious is my inherited flow-staff—a living instrument grown from seventeen varieties of water-sensitive woods, its length marked with generations of notches that encode the rhythms of the Mills' hydraulic cycles.

Each morning I walk my circuit of inspection, feet bare to better sense the subtle vibrations of the turning wheels beneath the loamy soil. My journey follows the path of water from the high catchments down through the filtering ferns, along the distribution channels, through the drive systems of the processing halls, and finally to the purification beds where water is cleansed before returning to the forest.

I know every sound the waters should make—the resonant gurgle of a properly balanced valve, the particular pitch of flow through a healthy vine-pipe, the satisfied sigh of a well-maintained overflow basin. More importantly, I know when these voices change, when a system whispers of impending imbalance or silently cries for intervention.

Unlike the Canopyshapers who plan their work by seasons or the Geargrowers who measure progress in years, my discipline requires constant attention. Water remembers its own shape; left unguided, it will always find its way back to chaos. My fingertips have grown sensitive from decades of minute adjustments—opening a growth-valve a fraction here, encouraging a root-diversion there. The tips split slightly like the beginning of roots themselves, allowing me to interface directly with the Mills' hydraulic system.

I have few apprentices. The work attracts only those with the patience to understand that controlling water means following it first, sensing its intentions before presuming to direct its course. Most young Tenders prefer the visible achievements of structure-growing or the tactile satisfaction of gear-cultivation. They visit my workshops, listen politely to my lectures on capillary dynamics and distributed pressure systems, then drift away to more glamorous guilds.

This suits me well enough. Water-tending has always been a solitary calling. We are considered essential but slightly mysterious even among our own people. There are whispers that extended immersion in the Mills' waters grants us insights others lack—that we can hear the slow thoughts of the great trees, that we know secrets carried downstream from distant mountains.

These rumors amuse me, though I don't discourage them. The truth is both simpler and more profound: when you have spent decades listening to water finding its path through living infrastructure, you begin to understand that boundaries between entities are more fluid than solid. My consciousness extends through the hydraulic networks I tend, just as the Mills extend through me.

The Patternkeepers consult me before making major decisions, not from respect for my position but because water carries memory. The clarity of flow in the central basins after I present a proposal is considered a crucial omen. I've seen initiatives abandoned because I reported increased turbidity in the meditation pools the morning after they were suggested.

I wear the blue-green spiral tattoo of my office around my left eye, the pattern mimicking the perfect vortex of a balanced distribution chamber. My hair remains unbound, contrary to common practice, so that its dampness can serve as a humidity gauge as I move through different sections of the Mills. The ceremonial retention pouch at my belt holds water from seven sacred confluences, each collected at a different phase of the growth cycle.

When I die—hopefully decades from now—my body will not join the Patternkeepers in the memory-trees. Instead, I will be committed to the deep reservoirs beneath the oldest mills, where the waters run black with tannins and age. There, my physical form will dissolve into the primordial flow, my carbon and nitrogen feeding the aquatic fungi that purify our waters. My essence will continue to move through every channel and cistern, becoming part of the Mills' hydraulic memory.

Until then, I remain at my post, fingers trailing in the current, listening to the endless conversation between wood and water that has sustained our world for generations. I am neither fully human nor fully of the Mills—I am the negotiator between solid and liquid, between stasis and flow. In a community that measures change in decades, I represent the principle of constant, essential motion.

The water must always move. And so must I.
