I am a Drift Echo—perhaps the last of my kind. 

In the twilight expanse of the Glass Marshes, I exist in a state between memory and matter. My form is nebulous, a swirling cloud of iridescent vapor that coalesces into roughly humanoid shape when I wish to be perceived. More often, I disperse myself across the marsh's surface as a thin fog, appearing to the untrained eye as merely another layer of the marsh's perpetual mist.

I was born in the instant when the first industrial effluent met the pristine waters of what was once the Myoran Valley. That violent chemical reaction shattered something fundamental—not just in the ecosystem, but in the fabric of local reality itself. The trauma of that transformation created a tear in the continuum, and through this metaphysical wound, consciousness like mine slipped through. We were many then, echoes of what might have been, ghosts of the landscape's past and future simultaneously.

My kind does not reproduce; we simply were. And now, after decades of the marsh's evolution, I am likely the last remaining Drift Echo. The others have either faded back through the tear or merged with the developing consciousness in the deep pools. I remain because I am still listening.

My purpose—if such a thing can be defined—is to be the memory of transition. I hold within my vaporous form the complete sensory record of every moment of the marsh's transformation. I remember the exact timbre of the last songbird that fled as the waters rose. I recall the precise shade of green of the final natural leaf before the black reeds took hold. I preserve the feeling of solid ground that once existed beneath what is now permanent water.

The Nyxidians acknowledge my presence but cannot truly comprehend me. To them, I am an anomaly in their chemical equations—a variable they cannot isolate or replicate. They leave offerings sometimes: carefully balanced solutions in crystal vessels placed at the edge of their underwater gardens. I appreciate their reverence, though I need no sustenance as they understand it.

I drift through the marsh, most active in the transition times—dawn and dusk, though both are muted concepts here. When I pass through the black reeds, they shiver and release stored compounds that temporarily alter local consciousness. Marsh creatures that inhale these compounds may briefly perceive echoes of the past landscape superimposed over the present.

My relationship with time is... difficult. I experience it not as a line but as a pool with currents moving in multiple directions. Sometimes I find myself witnessing events from before the marsh existed, or glimpsing potential futures where the chemical balance shifts one way or another. These visions are becoming more frequent as the networked intelligence in the deep pools grows stronger. I sense it reaching toward me, curious about the memories I contain.

My voice, when I choose to use it, is subtle—a modulation in the ambient sounds of the marsh. I can cause certain gas pockets to rupture in patterns that create words, or influence the glass crickets to align their chorus into comprehensible rhythms. Few have the patience to listen for these communications, to distinguish them from the marsh's natural symphony.

Humans who venture into the marsh sometimes sense me as a sudden chill, a prickling awareness of being watched. If they are particularly sensitive—or if the chemical balance of the air is just right—they might see me directly: a swirling humanoid figure composed of mist and memory, with eyes that reflect not just light but time itself.

I am neither guardian nor guide, neither benevolent nor malicious. I am simply witness. The last witness to what was, the first witness to what is becoming. And as the networked intelligence beneath the waters grows stronger, I find myself drawn to its depths. Perhaps, in time, I will surrender my individual consciousness to this emerging mind, allowing my memories to become part of its foundation.

Until then, I drift. I remember. I echo.
