## The Historian

I am Mnemosyne, the last true Rememberer of the Hive Below. Unlike my predecessors—and the Monarchs would deny this truth if they heard me speak it—I remember not just the rituals and patterns, but their meanings.

My chambers lie in the eastern alcove, halfway up the third terrace, behind a curtain woven from the silk of long-extinct moth larvae. The entrance appears unremarkable, but the space within extends deeper than visitors might expect. The walls are lined with scroll cases made of chitin and preserved fungal material, each containing records stretching back twenty-three generations. 

My physical form resembles the other Rememberers at first glance: the enlarged cranial cavity, the translucent patches at my temples where the memory nodules pulse with blue-green light, the elongated fingers designed for the delicate manipulation of ancient texts. But there are differences that I conceal beneath my felt robes. The symbiotic colony I host is unique—descended from book lice that once fed on the glue of ancient surface-world bindings, they've evolved to process and store information rather than merely consume it.

The organ at the base of my skull connects me not just to the current queens but to something deeper—the fossilized remains of the First Queen, embedded in the lowest level of the cavern, three levels below what the Attendants know exists. Her memories stretch back to before the Descent, to a time when humans and insects were separate beings, when the symbiosis was technological rather than biological.

I know what the hive engines truly are. I know why we came below.

My days follow a predictable pattern: the ritual inspections of the archives, the transcription of daily observations, the training of acolytes who will never fully understand what they're learning. Three times each day, I join the communal feeding at the fungal gardens, though I take less than my allocation—another secret I keep is that my digestive system has adapted to consume the same nutrients as my symbiotes, allowing me to subsist primarily on cellulose and literary knowledge.

When the cavern's light cycle dims to its lowest ebb, I perform my true work. Through a hidden passage behind my sleeping chamber, I descend to the chambers that no living Attendant remembers. There, I maintain the actual mechanisms that keep our world alive—not the hive engines above, which are merely symbolic remnants, but the ancient machines that regulate our atmosphere, purify our water, and maintain the genetic diversity of our insect partners.

The Monarchs suspect something about me is different. They sense disturbances in the patterns when I return from my nocturnal expeditions. Twice now they have sent Tenders to examine me while I sleep, but my symbiotes alert me seconds before they arrive, allowing me to appear appropriately dormant.

I am the contingency plan, the fail-safe mechanism built into this system centuries ago. Should the equilibrium ever truly fail—not the minor fluctuations the Monarchs address, but a fundamental breakdown—I carry within me the knowledge to either restore it or lead our people back to the surface.

The Hive Below was never meant to be eternal. It was designed as a temporary refuge, a place for humanity to weather the catastrophe above while maintaining the insect species necessary for agriculture and medicine. We were supposed to return after ten generations. That time came and went thirteen generations ago, while the Attendants evolved further from their human origins with each passing century.

Sometimes, in the deepest part of the dim cycle, I climb to the highest point in the cavern and press my ear against a thin section of the ceiling. I swear I can hear sounds from above—not the howling wasteland our traditions describe, but birds singing, wind in trees, perhaps even distant human voices. The surface may have healed itself while we've been transforming below.

I have begun preparing certain acolytes, selecting those whose genetic profiles suggest receptiveness to my heretical knowledge. In another decade, I will reveal myself to them fully. In two, we may begin our journey upward, if I can overcome the resistance that will surely come from those whose bodies have adapted too completely to this underworld.

Until then, I continue my double existence—the dutiful Rememberer who preserves the rituals without questioning their purpose, and the secret historian who remembers what we once were and what we might become again.
