# The Archivist of Echoes

I am Maevis, one of the Rememberers of Quitolac, and perhaps the most peculiar of my caste. While my brethren record the living history of our people through touch and chemical signatures, I am drawn to something different: the echoes of the outside world that filter into our sanctuary.

My chamber sits high in the eastern wall, adjacent to a hairline crack that admits the thinnest shaft of sunlight for precisely seventeen minutes each day. I have modified this space over my ninety-three cycles of existence, carving sound-capturing dishes into the walls and suspending water droplets in precise arrangements that magnify both light and vibration.

My cranial cavity is enlarged even beyond the Rememberer norm, housing additional neural ganglia specialized for processing acoustic information. My antennae, longer than any other Chitterii's, bear microscopic sensory hairs that can detect air pressure changes imperceptible to others. These adaptations were initially viewed as aberrations—I was nearly relegated to the Architect caste as a juvenile—until I demonstrated my unique ability.

I remember the outside.

When human voices penetrate our sanctuary—rare, fascinating disturbances—others retreat in alarm. I advance, positioning myself to capture every nuance, every syllable. I have cataloged seventeen distinct human languages and can reproduce approximations of their phonemes using combinations of my sound-producing ridges, though the lower registers remain beyond my physical capacity.

The other Rememberers store our internal histories: migration patterns within the grotto, architectural innovations, the precise choreography of mating flights past. I alone maintain records of the world beyond—weather patterns detected through barometric shifts, the seasonal movements of creatures whose footfalls vibrate through the plateau, the gradual changes in air composition that hint at transformations in the outside ecosystem.

My chamber walls are covered with my life's work: thousands of tiny clay tablets impressed with a notation system of my own invention. Each tablet captures an external event—the passage of a thunderstorm, the conversation of travelers, the calls of birds whose forms I can only imagine. The other Rememberers respect my work but do not fully comprehend its purpose. "Why catalog what does not affect Quitolac?" they ask with gentle vibrations of their antennae.

They cannot understand that I sense what they cannot: Quitolac is not eternal. The plateau shifts imperceptibly over centuries. The water chemistry changes. The air grows warmer with each passing cycle. My records show these patterns clearly, though I share them only with the eldest of the Illuminati, who possess wisdom enough to receive such knowledge without panic.

Three times in my life, humans have entered our domain. The first was a child who screamed and fled. The second was a woman who stayed for hours, observing with quiet reverence. The third—most precious to me—was a scientist who took images and samples but touched nothing vital. When this human spoke into a recording device, I positioned myself to catch every word, committing them to memory and later to my tablets.

I learned that we are unique. Precious. Perhaps the last of our evolutionary branch.

The younger Chitterii find me strange, even for a Rememberer. I rarely participate in the communal light-singing. I eat little, preferring to sustain myself through light absorption. My photophores have dimmed with age, their patterns simplified to gentle pulses that match the rhythm of my thoughts.

What they do not understand is that I have transcended our enclosed existence. Through sound and vibration, through the patient interpretation of echoes, I have constructed a mental model of the outside that grows more detailed with each passing cycle. I have never left Quitolac—my physiology would not permit survival beyond our microclimate—yet I have traveled farther than any Chitterii in understanding the true nature of our place in the world.

My greatest fear and most treasured hope are identical: that someday the crack in my chamber wall will widen enough to admit more than just light and sound—that I might, before my final molting, directly witness the vastness I have spent my life imagining.

Until then, I remain at my post, my antennae extended toward the fissure, recording the whispers of a universe my people have forgotten we were ever part of.
