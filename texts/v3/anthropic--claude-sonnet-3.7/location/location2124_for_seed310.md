# The Gathering Pool

You have come upon a depression in the land, a shallow bowl perhaps fifty paces across, ringed by low stone walls that are not quite walls. They are more like suggestions of enclosure, piled rocks that never rise above your hip. The air is cool and slick with moisture, carrying the scents of wet stone and something faintly mineral-sweet that you can't identify.

Water permeates everything here. It beads on the mossy rocks, trickles in thin rivulets down carefully carved channels, and pools in dozens of shallow basins that pock the ground. These are not natural formations but clearly hand-hewn: crude, uneven depressions chiseled into the bedrock with patient, primitive tools. Each basin connects to others through a network of finger-width canals that form a complex, seemingly purposeful pattern across the floor of the depression.

The vegetation is sparse but deliberate. Tufts of pale reeds grow from cracks between stones. Low-lying mosses form dense carpets in patches, their surfaces glistening with moisture. A few stunted trees with pale, almost translucent bark stand at irregular intervals around the perimeter, their branches reaching inward over the pools rather than up toward the sky.

But what dominates this space—overwhelmingly, impossibly—are the creatures.

They come in uncountable numbers, flowing into the gathering space from crevices and tunnels that honeycomb the surrounding earth. Most are no larger than your thumb: glistening amphibians in improbable colors, their skin patterns shifting subtly as you watch. Pale blue salamanders with too many legs. Frogs with translucent bodies that reveal not organs but tiny points of light pulsing within. Sinuous eel-like creatures that seem to swim through air as easily as through the shallow pools.

The sound is a constant, discordant chorus: chirps, croaks, trills and pops that seem to follow some rhythm you can almost but never quite grasp. It's as though they're speaking to each other in a language just beyond human comprehension.

The creatures move with purpose. They congregate in specific pools at specific times, flowing from one basin to another along the carved channels. Sometimes dozens of a single type gather in one pool, pulsing and writhing in synchrony before dispersing again. It's a slow dance, taking place over hours rather than minutes, but undeniably intentional.

The built elements reveal themselves gradually to your eye. What first seemed like random arrangements of stone resolve into a planned space. The basins form concentric rings, their connecting channels creating a spiral pattern that draws inward toward a central pool—deeper than the others and lined with a material that shimmers faintly even in the diffuse light. The surrounding walls contain niches at regular intervals, each holding crude clay figures shaped like the various creatures that inhabit this space.

This place is neither fully natural nor wholly artificial. It exists in some in-between state—a collaboration between primitive human craft and the overwhelming biological abundance of these strange amphibious beings. It feels ancient yet unfinished, as though it's been under continuous construction for centuries and will remain so for centuries more.

As you watch, a new type of creature emerges from a crevice in the northwest wall—something like a crab but with a flexible, segmented shell that ripples as it moves. Dozens follow the first, and they begin to carefully rearrange small stones along one of the channels, subtly altering its course. The construction continues, the place remaking itself according to some incomprehensible plan.

Whether this is shrine, habitat, meeting place, or something entirely other, you cannot tell. But you sense that you've stumbled upon something simultaneously sacred and mundane—a ritual space for creatures with needs and purposes utterly foreign to human understanding.
