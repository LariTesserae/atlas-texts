# The Hangings of Mavalith

The dome of Mavalith stretches no more than sixty paces across at its widest point, yet seems to contain multitudes within its cramped confines. From the curved ceiling, hundreds—perhaps thousands—of terracotta pots dangle on ropes of varying lengths, suspended at different heights to create a forest of clay that moves with subtle currents of air. Water drips steadily from the ceiling's center where a natural spring has been channeled through bamboo pipes, feeding a complex irrigation system that distributes moisture through hemp wicks to the hanging vessels.

The pots themselves are a craftwork wonder: each one hand-formed, fired in kilns whose smoke-stains mark the lower eastern wall. Some contain small gardens—hardy ferns and moss colonies; others house mushroom clusters that glow with faint bioluminescence; still others serve as homes for the cave's remarkable fauna. The clay has been worn smooth by generations of hands, many pots bearing the thumbprints of their long-dead makers.

The floor is never still. It writhes with life—colonies of pale, eyeless crabs scuttle between collection basins where excess water pools before being reclaimed by ceramic channels. Snails with luminous shells leave glistening trails across the packed-earth floor, while hand-sized geckos with translucent skin dart between shadow and light, hunting insects that buzz in clouds around the ripest of the hanging fruits.

The warm air hangs heavy with moisture and the rich scent of fermentation. Along the perimeter walls, shelves carved directly into the stone hold clay jars of varying sizes, their contents bubbling and transforming—wines, vinegars, and medicines in perpetual states of becoming. Elders of the Mavalith tend to these concoctions with tools of bone and wood, measuring doses with shells worn smooth by centuries of use.

Sound reverberates powerfully in this chamber. The cacophony is constant: water dripping in arrhythmic percussion, the hollow knock of clay pots colliding in the artificial breeze generated by leather bellows worked by children at the chamber's edge, the calls of small birds that nest in the highest pots, and the ever-present hum of conversation. The people of Mavalith speak in a unique dialect that accommodates the dome's acoustic peculiarities—short, clipped phrases that don't muddy in the echoes.

Three times daily, the hanging pots are rearranged in a choreographed dance using an intricate system of pulleys and counterweights carved from dense wood. This ritual serves multiple purposes: ensuring equal distribution of moisture and light (which enters through five small apertures in the dome's upper reaches), preventing any single plant from dominating resources, and maintaining the precise microclimate that has sustained this underground society for countless generations.

The inhabitants themselves are as dense as their environment—perhaps sixty people occupy this space, living in small alcoves carved into the walls. Their skin bears the pallor of underground dwelling, though not to the extreme of true cave-dwellers. They wear simple garments woven from the fibers of fungi cultivated specifically for this purpose, dyed with pigments extracted from the minerals they mine from narrow tunnels branching off the main chamber.

The entire ecosystem operates with precision born of necessity and refined through time. Nothing is wasted. Water cycles through plants, animals, and people before being filtered through successive layers of charcoal, sand, and moss to begin again. Even the dead are returned to the system—bodies dried and pulverized to enrich the soil in the hanging gardens.

At the chamber's center stands the only metal object in Mavalith: a bronze bell of unknown origin, green with verdigris, struck to mark the changing of shifts and significant moments in their underground calendar. Its tone—somehow both mellow and penetrating—is the heartbeat of this peculiar world, a world entirely crafted by human hands yet seeming as natural and inevitable as any forest or reef.
