# The Suspended Cetacean City of Muan'va

Here in the upper waters, where the sea meets a diffuse sky, lies Muan'va, the drifting habitation of the Deep Singers. From a distance, it appears as a clustered archipelago of massive, weathered shells—once belonging to creatures beyond imagination—now hollowed and connected by sinew bridges that undulate with the gentle currents.

The city floats at this high elevation within the world-spanning ocean, suspended in a thermocline where warm waters create a perpetual, hazy twilight. Light filters through the liquid atmosphere in long, attenuated beams that barely illuminate the shell-structures. The water here has a peculiar viscosity—thicker than expected, causing movements to appear dreamlike and sounds to travel as if through honey.

Muan'va was not built so much as grown and guided over centuries. The primitive tools of the Deep Singers—bone harpoons, shell scrapers, and woven net-looms—have shaped but never dominated their environment. Instead, they have cultivated a symbiosis with the massive chambered nautiloids whose discarded shells form the city's foundations. These shells, some spanning fifty meters across, have been polished by time and current to a pearlescent sheen, their spiraling chambers subdivided into living spaces by carefully cultivated coral partitions.

The sparse vegetation consists primarily of phosphorescent kelp gardens tended on the outer surfaces, glowing faintly blue-green and providing food and materials. These gardens attract the abundant fauna that sustains the city—schools of translucent fish with impossible anatomies, mollusks that secrete valuable building resins, and the guardian thresher-eels that patrol the city's perimeter.

Most unsettling to outside observers is the fundamental nature of reality here. The water itself seems aware—sometimes flowing against gravity, forming momentary sculptures of faces or spirals before dissolving. Occasionally, a Deep Singer will swim through what appears to be solid matter, or speak words that materialize as tiny bubbles containing moving images of distant places.

Time behaves inconsistently in Muan'va. In some chambers, moss grows to maturity in moments; in others, a dropped object might take days to reach the floor. The Deep Singers themselves—humanoid only in the broadest sense, with translucent skin revealing organs that pulse with luminescence—navigate these anomalies with practiced ease, their movements slow and deliberate.

The soundscape is haunting in its restraint. The Deep Singers communicate through vibrations too low to be properly heard—felt instead as resonances in the bones. The only audible sounds are the occasional crystalline tones from the "singing shells," ceremonial instruments played during the alignment of the three pale moons visible through the water's surface.

What appears as chaotic to visitors follows patterns established over millennia. Muan'va doesn't change quickly, but rather shifts position with seasonal currents, following ancient migratory routes mapped in constellations of bioluminescent creatures that live far below.

The most sacred mystery lies in the central spiral of the largest shell—an apparent void where water itself ceases to exist, revealing something the Deep Singers call "the dry world" beyond. None who enter have returned to describe what they found there.
