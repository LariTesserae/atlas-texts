# The Frostblooms

At the crest of the mountain, where thin air burns cold in the lungs, there exists a place not quite like any other. The locals call it the Singing Hollow—a depression in the high slopes that seems, from a distance, like a mere wrinkle in the terrain. Only when you stand at its rim do you realize it's a cathedral-sized amphitheater carved into the mountainside, a relatively young formation that still bears the sharp edges of its recent emergence.

The brilliant light that floods the Hollow is almost painful—not sunlight, but the combined bioluminescence of millions of frost-resistant plants that have colonized every available surface. These are the Frostblooms, a dense tapestry of vegetation unlike anything in the valleys below. They grow in tight, overlapping clusters that form the very architecture of this space: walls of intertwined stems, cathedral arches of elongated fronds, chambers formed by the hollows of massive bulbs. The plants pulse with light in synchronized waves, creating the illusion that the entire hollow is breathing.

Water is omnipresent but never still. It seeps from the rock faces, creating glistening sheets that freeze into delicate ice sculptures at night and thaw with the morning light. Small springs bubble up through the plant matter, forming shallow pools that never quite freeze despite the piercing cold. The water carries nutrients through the plant colony and creates a perpetual mist that clings to everything, refracting the bioluminescence into prismatic halos.

But it's the sound that overwhelms all other senses. The Hollow SINGS.

Every drop of water, every movement of plant matter, every breath of wind produces tones that resonate and amplify within the natural amphitheater. The plants themselves contribute—hollow stems that whistle, membranous leaves that vibrate, bulbs that pop and crackle as they release spores. The combination creates a deafening symphony that ebbs and flows but never ceases, a wall of sound so physical you can feel it pressing against your skin.

And within this cacophony moves the fauna—uncountable and bizarre. Insects the size of hummingbirds with translucent wings that catch and scatter the bioluminescent light. Rodent-like creatures with elongated limbs that scamper through the plant matrix, their fur adapted to hold water droplets that nourish the plants they live among. Small flying creatures—not quite birds, not quite bats—dart through the mist, their calls adding piercing notes to the Hollow's song.

Most strange are the Dancers—knee-high, many-limbed entities that seem part plant, part animal. They move with deliberate grace through the vegetation, tending to the plants, redirecting water flows, maintaining a balance that feels both chaotic and perfectly ordered. They carry primitive tools fashioned from plant matter—cutting implements, hollow tubes for redirecting water, small hammers for breaking ice that forms in crucial pathways.

Scattered throughout the Hollow are simple structures—small shelters woven from living plants, observation platforms built into the higher reaches, and curious altars where objects are arranged in patterns that change daily. These constructions feel temporary despite some showing signs of age—as though whoever built them acknowledges they are merely borrowing space from the overwhelming plant life.

The entire ecosystem pulsates with activity. Water flows change course throughout the day. Plants open and close, grow and retract. The Dancers work continuously. Predator-prey relationships play out in flashes of movement and sound. Nothing remains static for long; the Hollow is in constant flux, a dynamic system operating according to rhythms a visitor might sense but never fully comprehend.

At the very center of the Hollow stands a structure more deliberate than the rest—a spiral tower of living plants reinforced with a framework of carved bone and ice. It rises perhaps twenty feet, topped with what appears to be a primitive sound-gathering device: a broad disc of tightly woven fibers that vibrates visibly with the Hollow's song. The Dancers attend to this structure with particular care, adjusting its components in response to subtle changes in the surrounding symphony.

To visit the Singing Hollow is to be overwhelmed—by sound, by light, by the sheer density of life compressed into this relatively small space. It defies easy categorization, existing in a state of perpetual becoming, a young ecosystem still finding its balance yet already complex beyond ready understanding.

And always, always singing.
