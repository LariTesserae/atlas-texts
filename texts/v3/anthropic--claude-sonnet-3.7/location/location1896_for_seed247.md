# The Ascending Gardens of Tarnish

In a mountain valley where the air runs thin and clear, the Ascending Gardens catch the morning light. The settlement unfurls in tiers of curved stone structures that follow the natural contours of the mountain, like a series of cupped hands gathering dew. Each tier connects to the next by slender bridges of woven vine and wooden planks that flex slightly underfoot.

The Gardens have only recently begun to be built. The raw, unweathered stone of the newer structures gleams with a peculiar bluish tint, their edges still sharp and definite against the sky. The older sections—no more than a decade in age—have already begun to take on the character of the place: stone lightly etched by weather, corners gentled, surfaces hosting pale lichen in geometric patterns.

Water is the architect here. From underground springs, it wells up through channels carved deliberately into the stone foundations, following paths laid out by the Garden's designers. The water circuits through every level, murmuring quietly, keeping time for the settlement. It pools in small basins where children gather to play, splashing softly, their voices kept low by cultural habit rather than rule.

Above the Gardens, a system of translucent canopies captures morning mist, funneling condensation into the water system. The canopies, made of some stretchy, fibrous material harvested from local plants, glow golden-green in the bright sunlight, casting dappled patterns across the walkways and common spaces below.

The vegetation thrives in this carefully managed microclimate. Plants grow in terraced beds, climbing up walls, spilling over edges—not wild exactly, but abundant and meticulously tended. Many have unusual adaptations: leaves that fold closed at midday, flowers that emit gentle phosphorescent glows at dusk, root systems that interlace with the stonework to strengthen rather than erode it.

The air hums with insect life—not the whine of mosquitoes but the gentle vibration of pollinator wings, iridescent creatures halfway between butterfly and hummingbird that move from bloom to bloom in choreographed patterns. Small lizards with extra eyelids and scales that shift color with temperature bask on sun-warmed stones, occasionally flicking their forked tongues to catch passing insects.

The inhabitants move with deliberate purpose. Their clothing is simple but intricately embroidered with patterns that echo the movement of water through the Gardens. They use tools of wood, stone, and hammered copper—simple in form but elegant in function. Communication happens primarily through gesture and expression, with speech reserved for complex matters or storytelling.

At the center of each tier sits a communal workshop where residents craft the tools and implements needed for daily life. The crafting itself seems ceremonial, with multiple people working together in coordinated movements that almost suggest dance. The sound of stone on stone, wood on wood creates a gentle percussion that blends with the background murmur of flowing water.

Most unusual are the "growth chambers" positioned at strategic points throughout the settlement. Within these domed spaces, new sections of the Gardens take shape—not through conventional construction but through some combination of guided crystallization and accelerated plant growth. Workers tend to these chambers with reverent attention, adjusting the flow of mineral-rich water, positioning seedlings, singing in low tones that seem to resonate with the forming structures.

The Gardens exist in a state of perpetual becoming, never finished, always emerging into some new configuration. The inhabitants appear to view this not as incompleteness but as the natural state of their home—a living entity that must grow and change to remain vital.

As evening approaches, lights appear—not flames, but glass vessels containing bioluminescent cultures that cast a cool blue-green glow across the Gardens. In this light, the boundaries between built and natural, between organism and architecture, seem to dissolve entirely.
