# The Evermost

The canopy stretches beyond sight, a verdant ceiling so vast it generates its own weather patterns. From this elevation—some two thousand meters above what might be called sea level if one could find the sea—the plant-metropolis extends in all directions to the curved horizon. This is Evermost, the living city-forest that has existed in stable equilibrium for countless millennia.

The cold is not harsh but persistent, hovering just above freezing. It preserves rather than destroys. The dense foliage exhales a constant mist that condenses on every surface, creating the impression that everything weeps—leaves, branches, the bioengineered structures that serve as dwellings and thoroughfares. This perpetual moisture never pools deeply but is omnipresent, glistening on every surface, trickling down pathways, collecting in the cupped leaves of the million varieties of plants that form the foundation of Evermost.

What appears at first glance to be architecture reveals itself as something else entirely: hyper-advanced botanical engineering. The inhabitants of ages past—whoever they were—didn't build this place so much as they grew it, guiding plants with genetic and quantum manipulations beyond current scientific understanding. The "buildings" are living organisms, their interiors hollowed not by tools but by specialized growth patterns, their support structures not columns but massive vascular systems that transport water and nutrients throughout the city-organism.

The sound is a constant symphony: the creaking of enormous boughs under their own weight; the steady drip-drip-drip of condensation; the rhythmic pulse of sap through vessels as wide as tunnels; the whisper of leaves that never cease their subtle movements despite the absence of wind. In certain districts, the resonant chambers of hollow trunks amplify these sounds to thunderous levels, creating acoustic zones where conversation becomes impossible.

Fauna is nearly absent. Occasionally a small creature—something like a cross between a squirrel and a gecko, with specialized adaptations for traversing wet surfaces—might skitter across a branch, but such sightings are rare enough to merit mention. The ecosystem has long since reached a balance where animals are largely unnecessary. Plants pollinate plants through engineered mechanisms; decomposition happens through specialized fungal networks that break down dead material with perfect efficiency.

The plant density defies comprehension. Every cubic meter contains hundreds of distinct species, all coexisting in a complex three-dimensional arrangement that maximizes sunlight capture and resource utilization. Vines thread through branches, epiphytes cling to trunks, moss carpets every horizontal surface, and specialized air-plants hang in curtains, filtering the moisture-laden atmosphere. Nothing is bare. Nothing is wasted. The cityscape is saturated with life to a degree that makes it difficult to distinguish individual plants from the whole.

The technology that maintains Evermost operates at a scale and sophistication that renders it nearly invisible. Nanoscale machines—or perhaps entities in the blurred boundary between machine and organism—regulate growth patterns, repair damaged systems, and maintain the delicate homeostasis that has kept this ecosystem functioning without change for eons. These systems are integrated so completely into the plant matter that they are indistinguishable from natural processes, yet their presence is evident in the mathematical precision with which resources are allocated throughout the vast structure.

The light is unremarkable only in its intensity—neither too bright nor too dim. It filters through the highest layers of canopy in a way that suggests deliberate design, distributing illumination evenly throughout the vertical spaces of the city. This engineered light distribution allows photosynthesis to occur at all levels, from the highest branches to the deepest interior chambers.

Pathways wind through this living metropolis—not streets but something more organic: channels formed where plant growth has been programmed to create gaps just wide enough for passage. These corridors twist and branch fractally, forming a transportation network that spans the entire structure. Some are broad enough for dozens to walk abreast; others narrow to tight passages where shoulders brush the living walls.

The erosion is not of soil or stone but of purpose. Whatever civilization engineered Evermost has long since departed or evolved beyond recognition. The city functions perfectly, maintains itself flawlessly, but there is a sense of ancientness that goes beyond physical age—a spiritual erosion, where function has outlived intent. The botanical machinery continues its programmed tasks with perfect fidelity to instructions whose meaning has been lost to time.

Despite its age and complexity, Evermost does not change. The self-regulating systems maintain a perfect equilibrium, countering any deviation with subtle adjustments propagated throughout the organism-city. Seasons do not affect it; climate shifts are compensated for; even the slow drift of continental plates is accommodated by gradual root adjustments that maintain the city's elevation and stability.

Water cycles endlessly through the system—absorbed by roots that extend kilometers downward, transported through massive vascular structures, transpired into the atmosphere, condensed on engineered surfaces, and channeled back down to be used again. Nothing is lost. Nothing is gained. The water that moistens your face as you walk these living corridors may have followed the same path a million times before.

In the distance, where the curvature of the planet becomes visible from this elevation, similar structures can be seen—other city-forests rising from the landscape, connected to Evermost by threads of green so vast they can be seen from space. The entirety of this world may be covered in variations of this same engineered ecology, a planetary civilization of plants maintaining the perfect, unchanging equilibrium established by creators long gone.
