# The Eternal Steam Works

The ceaseless sound of falling water fills this enclosed world—a great drum-like chamber suspended high in a mountain range. The interior of this vast dome is lit by the warm, honey-amber glow of iron lanterns that hang like stars from the vaulted ceiling, their light never quite pushing back all the shadows.

The Stone Cathedra, locals call it, though it's more a gigantic industrial workshop than a place of worship. The ceiling soars eighty feet above, yet the space feels contained, knowable—perhaps a quarter mile across at its widest. The dome itself is crafted from interlocking plates of hammered copper and brass, now patinated to rich teals and verdigris from decades of exposure to the perpetual humidity.

Water is the constant presence here. It cascades from hundreds of precisely engineered fissures in the walls, flowing down carved channels into collection basins, where it's heated by underground thermal vents. The entire chamber seems to weep—condensation forms on every metal surface, dripping in musical patterns that create a background symphony. Steam rises constantly, catching the dim light, creating a perpetual haze that softens all edges.

There is no plant life here—not even lichen or moss has taken hold. The metallic and stone surfaces remain pristine, as though time itself cannot gain purchase. The minerals in the water have formed striking crystalline deposits that might be mistaken for vegetation from a distance—glassy formations in amber, cobalt, and alabaster that catch and fragment the lantern light.

The floor is a testament to engineering—concentric rings of wrought iron walkways suspended over a lake of warm water that serves as both reservoir and temperature regulator. The water bubbles gently, heated from below, filling the air with humidity. The metal never rusts—something in the water's chemistry prevents oxidation while encouraging the beautiful patination.

Buildings rise like islands throughout this circular domain—copper-roofed workshops, brass-fitted living quarters, and iron-framed factories. They stand on pillar-like structures that elevate them above the water. Networks of bridges connect these structures, creating a three-dimensional latticework of pathways. Each building releases its own contribution to the symphony of sounds—the hiss of pressure valves, the rhythmic clanking of machinery, the low hum of generators.

The inhabitants are as much a part of this landscape as the structures. Small, nimble creatures with fur adapted to shed water move effortlessly along railings and up walls—domesticated mammals somewhere between otters and monkeys that carry tools and messages between workshops. Larger creatures with thick hides pull carts along the wider causeways, their breathing adding a bass note to the chamber's soundscape.

Humans and similar humanoids bustle about, their clothing practical and waterproofed—waxed canvas, treated leather, and rubberized fabrics. They wear goggles pushed up on foreheads, ready to be deployed when working near the steamier sections. Many sport mechanical augmentations—brass-fitted hearing aids to filter the constant industrial clamor, articulated gauntlets for manipulating hot materials, respirators for areas where the steam is thickest.

This place exists in perfect equilibrium. The water that falls is the same water that rises as steam, condenses on the dome, and falls again. The heat from below is precisely balanced by the cooling effect of the higher elevation's surrounding mountain air. The machines run at a constant rate, maintained by generations of engineers who have perfected the art of preservation rather than innovation.

Time seems suspended here. The lanterns never dim, the water never stops its cycle, the machines never alter their rhythm. It is an ecosystem of metal and water and animal and human, interdependent and unchanging. The Stone Cathedra breathes its steam and sings its mechanical song exactly as it did a century ago, exactly as it will a century hence.

At the chamber's center stands the Engine Eternal—a massive brass and copper construction that pulses with the heartbeat of the entire complex. No one quite remembers who built it or when, but every child born here learns to maintain it, to honor it, to ensure that its measured tempo never falters.

This is a place of warm water and hard surfaces, of eternal industry and perfect balance, of dim light and ceaseless sound—a self-contained world suspended in the mountains, defying both time and the elements with its perfect, unending cycle.
