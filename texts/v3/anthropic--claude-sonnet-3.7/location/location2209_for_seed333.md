# The Constant Echo

Water seeps through everything here. The ground is a tapestry of shallow puddles, reflecting the vast sky above—a sky so impossibly bright it hurts to look directly upward. Ancient plateaus stretch to the horizon, their edges worn to graceful curves by countless millennia of wind and water. This is a high place, where the air thins and cools, where clouds sometimes form below rather than above you.

The sound is overwhelming. It comes not from any single source but from the land itself—a perpetual, thunderous resonance that vibrates through bone and soil alike. The ancient rock formations act as enormous resonance chambers, amplifying and reflecting sound waves in complex harmonies. No one speaks here; they only listen, or cover their ears in futile defense.

Green life flourishes in unexpected abundance, given the elevation. Mosses carpet the weathered stone in thick, vibrant cushions. Ferns with delicate, unfurling fronds emerge from every crevice that collects moisture. The vegetation has adapted to the sonic bombardment—some plants have evolved specialized tissues that actually harness vibrations for growth. Their leaves tremble constantly, drinking in both sunlight and sound.

Water doesn't flow so much as it migrates across the landscape. It rises as mist in the morning, condenses on the cool stone by midday, trickles into collections by afternoon, and seeps into the porous ancient rock by nightfall. This daily cycle has carved an intricate network of channels across the plateaus, each one smoothed to glassy perfection over countless ages.

The few animal species here have adapted remarkable specializations. Rare birds with modified eardrums soar through the brightness, using the acoustic landscape for navigation rather than sight. Small amphibians vibrate their entire bodies in synchrony with certain frequencies of the omnipresent sound, becoming nearly invisible to predators. These creatures move with deliberate purpose—feeding, mating, sheltering—all choreographed to the rhythm of the land's endless song.

Human artifacts appear occasionally, weathered almost beyond recognition. Stone circles with peculiar acoustic properties. Ruins of what might have been observatories or temples, positioned to channel both light and sound in specific patterns during celestial events. These structures are impossibly old, built by people who understood something profound about this place that has since been forgotten.

Most mysterious are the "echo flowers"—plants with broad, hollow stems that selectively amplify certain frequencies while dampening others. They grow in patterns that seem random until viewed from specific vantage points, where they reveal perfect geometric arrangements. The flowers' positions correspond to star patterns that haven't appeared in the night sky for thousands of years.

When rain comes—and it comes often—the sound reaches truly painful levels as billions of droplets strike stone surfaces simultaneously. During these deluges, everything living retreats into shelter, emerging only when the acoustic storm subsides. The rain transforms the landscape into a network of temporary streams and expanding pools, all of which will be absorbed back into the porous plateau before the next dawn.

The most unsettling feature is what locals call "the resonance"—moments when all the disparate sounds suddenly synchronize into a single, pure tone that seems to freeze time itself. During resonance events, water stops in mid-drip, plants cease their trembling, and even the omnipresent winds fall still. These moments might last seconds or hours; no one has found a way to predict them.

This is an ancient place that has settled into its final form—a form that continues to speak, endlessly, to anyone who climbs to listen. What it's saying, no one knows.
