# The Exhalation Chamber

At the summit of a dizzying obsidian spire, where the air thins to a whisper, there exists a hollow unlike any other. The Chamber reveals itself only after a treacherous climb up glassy, knife-edged ridges that seem to have been thrust from the mountain's core mere moments ago—raw, unweathered formations with edges still molecularly sharp.

The Chamber itself is roughly spherical, perhaps forty paces across, suspended impossibly near the mountain's peak. The walls are black volcanic glass, but unlike the exterior's chaotic juts, these surfaces are eerily smooth, as if polished by something other than time. In places, the obsidian becomes so thin it turns translucent, casting weak, distorted shadows of the dim world beyond.

Moisture clings to everything here. Not as water exactly, but as a viscous, mercurial substance that beads along the curved walls, collecting in shallow depressions before slowly migrating downward in rivulets that move with deliberate purpose. This liquid—neither fully water nor fully mineral—catches what little light filters through the glass walls and holds it, giving the Chamber a faint, pulsing luminescence like a heartbeat slowed to geologic time.

The air is warm, unexpectedly so for such elevation. It comes in waves that seem timed to something unseen—a warmth that feels respired rather than ambient. Occasionally, the Chamber itself seems to inhale, drawing the mercury-like moisture into microscopic fissures, before exhaling it again as a fine mist that temporarily obscures vision and leaves a metallic taste on anything it touches.

Set into the curved floor at uneven intervals are seven shallow bowls, each perhaps the size of two cupped hands. These are clearly fashioned rather than formed—primitive tools must have worked this impossible glass, for there are faint concentric marks around their rims. Each bowl holds a different volume of the quicksilver-like moisture, and each emits a single, sustained tone when the Chamber exhales its mist—a different pitch from each, creating a slow, discordant harmony that hovers just at the threshold of hearing.

Between these bowls lie scattered remnants of some primitive craft: splinters of obsidian knapped to razor-sharpness, simple stone hammers with wrapped handles of some material that resembles neither plant fiber nor animal hide, and small heaps of a reddish powder that smells of copper and salt.

The Chamber has a distinct emptiness that goes beyond mere absence of life. It feels hollowed out intentionally, as if it were an organ removed from some greater body. When sound occurs—the occasional drip of liquid, the rare settling of stone against stone—it doesn't echo so much as it seems absorbed, studied, and then carefully released back in slightly altered form.

Nothing grows here. Nothing lives. Yet there is an unmistakable sense of emergence—not that the Chamber is new, but that it is only now beginning to serve its purpose. The entire space feels like an instrument being tuned, a vessel being prepared, a lung drawing its first breath.

And from time to time, almost imperceptibly, the entire structure shifts a fraction of a degree toward a distant light source that never quite reveals itself through the translucent walls.
