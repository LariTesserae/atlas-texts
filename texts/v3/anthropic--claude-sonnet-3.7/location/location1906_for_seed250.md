# The Ice Grotto of Countless Wings

You stand at the mouth of a cave that punctures the side of a windswept mountain. The entrance is small—you must duck to enter—but beyond is a cathedral-like chamber hollowed from ancient stone. This high place, this frozen sanctum, was not carved by human hands but by the patient labor of mineral-rich water seeping through limestone over millennia.

The air inside strikes cold against your face, hovering just above freezing. Despite the chill, the grotto isn't entirely dry. The walls glisten with a thin sheen of condensation that catches what little light filters through small fissures in the ceiling. In places, this moisture gathers into slow, methodical drips that fall with mathematical precision, each droplet taking exactly as long as the last to form and release.

These droplets feed shallow pools on the stone floor, their surfaces perfectly still, reflecting the chamber above like black mirrors. The water never flows away or rises further; whatever enters must leave through evaporation or some unseen subterranean passage, maintaining the chamber in perfect hydrological balance.

Vegetation is scarce but determined. Pale lichen clings to the walls in geometric patterns—white, mint, and occasional splashes of sulfur yellow. Near the moisture-rich areas, emerald-green moss forms thick, cushioned islands no larger than dinner plates. Nothing taller grows here; there is neither soil nor sunlight enough to sustain it.

But what the grotto lacks in plant life, it compensates for with its fauna.

The air is thick with movement—thousands of bats have made this chamber their permanent home. They hang from every available surface of the high ceiling, clustered so densely that in places you cannot see the rock behind them. Their tiny bodies generate just enough heat to maintain the chamber's constant temperature, slightly warmer than the mountain exterior.

The sound is overwhelming.

The beating of countless tiny wings creates a continuous roar that seems to come from everywhere at once. Layered beneath this are the high-pitched echolocation calls—mostly beyond human hearing but occasionally dipping into perceptible range as sharp, metallic clicks. These sounds reverberate against the stone walls, multiplying and intensifying until the entire chamber seems to vibrate with a living pulse.

The floor below is carpeted with guano—years of accumulated droppings that have formed a dense, nitrogen-rich crust. This is the only part of the chamber that changes at all, building up incrementally over decades, though even this accumulation has reached a kind of equilibrium as the oldest layers slowly mineralize into the cave floor.

In the dimmest recesses of the chamber, where the moisture is most reliable, colonies of pale, eyeless insects thrive, feeding on bat droppings and occasionally serving as food themselves when a bat swoops low enough.

Time seems meaningless here. The bats have established a society without day or night, without seasons. They hunt in shifts, some always remaining while others venture out, maintaining the constant density and buzz of activity. The chamber exists in a state of perpetual now, the same scene playing out with microscopic variations that add up to no change at all.

This place was here long before humans climbed high enough to discover it, and it continues unchanged by their occasional visits. The bats tolerate brief intrusions but never alter their patterns. The water drips. The sound resonates. The darkness cradles it all in timeless suspension.
