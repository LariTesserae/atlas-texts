# The Roar of Eternal Echoes

High above the world where the air is thin and the breeze carries a persistent chill, the massive plateau of Vorren stretches beyond immediate sight. This elevated expanse has been shaped by countless millennia of wind erosion, sculpting what was once a jagged mountain range into something more deliberate—a vast network of stone corridors and amphitheaters of impossible scale.

The darkness is profound here. The plateau sits perpetually beneath a ceiling of dense, low clouds that neither part nor dissipate. What little light penetrates casts everything in twilight hues of indigo and charcoal. This darkness has remained unbroken for as long as any record suggests, an eternal dusk that has shaped all life here into something uniquely adapted to seeing without seeing.

But what defines Vorren—what has always defined it—is the sound. 

The plateau roars with a deafening, unceasing chorus. Sound is channeled through the natural stone corridors, amplified by the architecture of erosion until it reaches painful levels. The source remains contested—some claim it's the wind through precisely formed apertures in the rock, others believe underground gas vents create the pressure differentials, while the more mystically inclined insist the mountain itself is alive and breathing. Regardless of origin, the noise never stops, never changes, never diminishes. It simply is, and has always been.

Scattered across the landscape are sturdy industrial structures—sound foundries, they're called—squat buildings of thick stone and metal that harness the acoustic energy of Vorren. Inside these facilities, massive tuning forks and resonance chambers convert the plateau's roar into mechanical energy that powers a network of industries that have operated unchanged for generations. The technology is neither primitive nor advanced but specialized, evolved to extract power from this specific phenomenon. Workers in these foundries wear elaborate helmets that protect their hearing while allowing them to perceive subtle shifts in vibration that might indicate maintenance needs.

Between these industrial outposts lie vast stretches of emptiness—kilometers of exposed stone swept smooth by wind. But this apparent barrenness is deceptive. Within the sculpted channels and depressions, moisture collects and sustains pockets of extraordinary life. Emerald moss carpets the crevices, growing in thick, plush mats that absorb both sound and moisture. Twisted, resilient trees have evolved to grow horizontally rather than vertically, their trunks and branches spreading along the ground to resist the plateau's constant winds while their roots delve into the collected rainwater beneath the stone.

The fauna of Vorren is remarkably diverse despite the harsh conditions. Most creatures are small but abundant—colonies of vibration-sensitive rodents that communicate through touch rather than sound, birds with overdeveloped chest cavities that allow them to sing above the constant roar, and insects that have evolved specialized hearing organs that filter out the plateau's frequency. The larger animals move in coordinated herds, their migrations following the seasonal shifts in moisture as dew-collection points dry up and new ones form elsewhere.

After rainfall—which comes in brief, intense bursts—the plateau transforms temporarily. Water fills the stone channels, creating ephemeral rivers that flow into depression basins where the moisture may linger for weeks. These temporary oases burst into frantic life, with plants flowering and seeding within days, and animals gathering to drink and mate before the water recedes.

The human settlements here are minimal—mostly the dormitories and support structures for the sound foundry workers. These buildings are partially embedded in the stone, their walls nearly a meter thick to provide some acoustic shelter. Communities are small and close-knit, with generations of families specializing in the various tasks required to maintain the sound-energy industry. They communicate through a complex sign language augmented by light patterns from handheld lanterns, having adapted to a world where spoken words are rarely heard.

What's most remarkable about Vorren is its unchanging nature. The plateau exists in a state of perfect equilibrium. The erosion that formed it now maintains it, the sound that defines it never varies, and the cycles of life follow predictable patterns established since time immemorial. Records in the foundries' archives—their pages yellowed with age—describe the exact same conditions centuries ago. The workers perform the same tasks their great-grandparents did, using the same tools, producing the same results.

In Vorren, time doesn't pass so much as persist.
