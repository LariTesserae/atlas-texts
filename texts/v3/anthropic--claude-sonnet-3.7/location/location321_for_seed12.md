# Veils of Kihua

Here, you are suspended in warmth. The salt-dense waters of Kihua hold you like an embrace, so buoyant that rising and falling requires only the slightest intention. The surrounding sea is a deep, translucent teal that absorbs light gradually, creating layers of visibility—sharp details nearby that soften with distance into spectral suggestions of form.

Above you, perhaps a hundred meters up, the surface ripples as a living ceiling. It isn't the open air beyond, but rather the underside of the floating city-forest of Kihua. The city has no land beneath it; it grows and spreads across the water's surface like a vast living raft.

The foundations of Kihua are ancient rootworks—interlaced, deliberate tangles of mangrove-like structures that have been cultivated over generations. These pale, bone-white roots hang down into the depths, creating columns and lattices that plunge thirty meters below the surface. Between them, cloth-like sheets of woven fiber have been stretched to create underwater chambers where the Kihuans live and work.

The water here carries sound differently—voices become muffled ghosts, but the subtle creaks and groans of the living city above transmit with surprising clarity. Sometimes, you can hear the slow, deep exhalation of the city itself as pockets of fermentation gases escape from the rootworks into the air above.

Moving through this underwater realm requires patience. The Kihuans don't swim so much as glide, using long poles of flexible cane to push against the root structures. They wear special garments with gill-like extensions that process oxygen from the water, allowing them to remain submerged for days. Their skin has taken on a bluish tint through generations of adaptation.

Everything here exists in a state of gradual becoming. New root structures are constantly being coaxed into existence, guided by frames of bent cane and weighted stones. Young roots hang like pale tentacles, not yet thickened into their mature form. What might become a new dwelling chamber in another decade is now just a suggestion—a geometric arrangement of guiding lines and newly sprouting tendrils.

The underwater structures are lit by bioluminescent globes—cultivated fungi and bacteria housed in translucent bladders that provide a gentle blue-green glow. These lights sway with the water's subtle currents, casting moving shadows that make the space feel alive and breathing.

Most of the city's food comes from above—the dense canopy of fruiting plants and cultivated algae mats that cover the surface. But here below, delicate strands of edible seaweeds grow in careful arrangements, tended with wooden tools and harvested with ceramic blades. The Kihuans use no metal; salt water would claim it too quickly.

The surrounding waters are remarkably empty of large creatures. Occasionally small, transparent fish dart between the roots, and tiny crustaceans cling to the fibrous walls, but the larger sea life seems to keep its distance from the vast, spreading shadow of Kihua above. Some say it's the city's slow exhalations that keep predators away.

In the distance, where the roots thin out and eventually end, the open sea darkens to an indigo void. The boundary between Kihua and the true deep is marked by a series of hanging chimes—hollow tubes of fired clay that produce low, resonant tones when the water's movements become too forceful. These warning sounds can be heard throughout the underwater city, a signal for inhabitants to retreat to the central chambers when the open sea grows restless.

Time here feels measured differently. The Kihuans mark it by the growth of the roots, by the slow expansion of chambers, by the gradual accumulation of mineral deposits on the older structures. Nothing happens quickly in Kihua; everything is in process. The city has no final form—it grows, shifts, and remakes itself with each generation, always in a state of becoming.
