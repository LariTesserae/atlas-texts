# The Anhydrobe Enclosure

The industrial dome rises from the frigid salt plain like a luminous pearl, its translucent panels catching and amplifying the harsh external light. From a distance, it appears to be a singular structure, but as you approach, the complexity reveals itself—a dense network of interconnected chambers, tubes, catwalks, and machinery compressed into a space no larger than a small village. This is Anhydrobe Enclosure #37, one of the mineral extraction colonies established after the Great Desiccation.

Inside, the air is dry but warmer than the biting cold beyond the walls. The dome's interior is a three-dimensional maze of activity, with hardly a cubic meter wasted. Machinery crowds every available space—hydrophobic filters, salt processors, mineral extractors, and pneumatic transport systems. Pipes snake between levels, carrying powders and granulates rather than liquids. There is no water here—not a drop. Not in the traditional sense.

The sparse vegetation exists in sealed terraria scattered throughout the enclosure. Strange, crystalline growths with geometric branches extend from nutrient beds of powdered minerals. These are not plants as once known, but silicon-organic hybrids developed to photosynthesize in anhydrous conditions, extracting oxygen from mineral compounds rather than water. They glitter under the intense artificial lights that bathe the entire enclosure in a brightness that makes shadows sharp and distinct.

Workers move through narrow corridors with practiced precision, their bodies enclosed in moisture-retention suits—not to keep water out, but to keep it in. Human sweat and breath are considered resources too precious to waste in the Anhydrobe economy. The suits collect and recycle the body's moisture, allowing humans to survive in an environment that would otherwise desiccate them within hours.

The soundscape is industrial and relentless. Crushers pulverize crystalline formations harvested from the salt plains. Pneumatic tubes whoosh with the transport of powdered goods. Extraction fans whir continuously. Electric motors hum. Occasional sharp cracks echo when mineral deposits are fractured by thermal shock—a harvesting technique that uses the cold outside air as a resource rather than an obstacle.

Rarely, one might glimpse the enclosure's few fauna—engineered microarthropods that process waste materials, or the occasional anhydrobe mouse, a small mammal modified to metabolize moisture from solid compounds. These creatures move quickly, darting between machinery, their metabolisms accelerated by the need to generate heat in this cold environment.

The enclosure has existed here for generations, its form settled into a functional equilibrium. The exterior panels show the etching of countless salt storms, giving them a frosted appearance except where recently replaced. Inside, pathways have been worn into the metal flooring, machines have been repaired so many times that few original parts remain, and the entire structure has a weathered permanence despite the constant activity within.

The bright light serves both functional and psychological purposes. The intensity mimics a sun that is seldom seen through the frequent dust storms outside, and alleviates the claustrophobia that might otherwise take hold in this dense industrial warren.

The air carries an unusual tang—not the metallic scent of machinery alone, but something sharper: the smell of chemical reactions that have replaced traditional water-based processes. The inhabitants no longer notice it, but visitors find it distinctly alien.

At regular intervals, harvester crews in sealed mobility suits exit through airlocks to gather new mineral deposits from the salt plain. They return dusty and cold, bringing with them the raw materials that keep the enclosure functioning. The relationship between the colony and its harsh surroundings has achieved a strange symbiosis—the humans extract what they need, and the endless salt plains regenerate through the complex mineral upwellings that occur beneath the surface.

This is a place that has redefined what it means to live, adapting human needs to conditions that would once have been considered unsurvivable. The Anhydrobe Enclosure stands as testament to human ingenuity and the lengths to which we will go to establish ourselves in environments never meant for our kind.
