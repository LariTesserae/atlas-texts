# The Humming Hollow

You first notice the noise. Before your eyes adjust to the dim light, your ears fill with a cacophony of sounds—a deafening orchestra of whirrs, clicks, bubbles, hisses, and whistles that seem to pulse with strange rhythms. It's not random noise but something structured, almost like a language speaking at volumes that press against your eardrums.

The ground beneath your feet is damp, cushioned with thick moss that yields with each step. As your vision adapts, you find yourself in a bowl-shaped depression no larger than a cathedral's interior. This hollow has clearly existed for centuries, its sides smoothed by water and time into perfect curved walls of dark stone veined with luminescent blue minerals that provide the only reliable illumination.

Water is everywhere but never deep. It weeps from the hollow's walls, collecting in hundreds of small pools connected by rivulets that form an intricate vascular system across the floor. These pools reflect what little light exists, multiplying it into constellations of shimmer. The air itself seems partially liquid—not quite fog but a perpetual mist that condenses on every surface, giving everything a glistening quality.

The vegetation is astonishingly dense. Ferns with fronds as long as your arm unfurl in spirals, their undersides dotted with spores that occasionally puff into the air in golden clouds. Mosses in impossible shades of green, blue, and purple carpet every surface not claimed by water. Fungal structures rise in tiered shelves along the hollow's walls—some bioluminescent, some translucent, some pulsing with what appears to be circulating fluids. Tiny white flowers with multiple centers bloom in clusters, opening and closing in rapid sequence as if breathing.

The cool air carries a complex perfume—earthy decomposition, floral sweetness, and an underlying mineral tang like copper pennies held under the tongue.

What at first appeared to be geological formations reveal themselves, upon closer inspection, as the remnants of industrial installations. Ancient iron pipes, their surfaces almost entirely reclaimed by rust and lichen, emerge from the hollow's walls only to disappear again into the stone. Scattered throughout are the remnants of mechanical devices—gears half-buried in moss, the skeleton of what might have been a small generator now serving as a trellis for climbing vines, pressure gauges with cracked glass faces that somehow still hold residual readings.

Most curiously, small brass boxes with tarnished copper wiring appear at regular intervals around the hollow's circumference. They emit a constant low humming that forms the bass note of the hollow's overwhelming soundscape.

The fauna is as abundant as it is strange. Amphibians no larger than your thumbnail, their skin patterned in geometric shapes that seem too precise to be natural, leap between pools. Insects with too many legs and bodies that appear partially mechanical scuttle across surfaces or hover in swarms that move with unsettling coordination. Bird-like creatures with membranous wings and long proboscises dart through the mist, drinking from the pools without ever quite landing.

Most striking are the hollow's main inhabitants—things that resemble salamanders but are the size of house cats. Their skin shifts color constantly, not as camouflage but seemingly in response to the sounds around them. They appear to be the primary source of the hollow's deafening noise, their throats vibrating as they call to one another in what sounds like Morse code crossed with whale song.

The entire hollow exists in a state of constant motion and transformation. The pools rise and fall in alternating patterns. Vegetation grows visibly, unfurling new growth only to wither and be replaced within hours rather than seasons. The salamander-things construct and dismantle small structures of pebbles and plant matter, working with frantic energy before abandoning their creations to start anew.

Occasionally, with no discernible pattern, one of the ancient brass devices will activate with a shower of sparks and a momentary intensification of light. When this happens, all activity in the hollow synchronizes briefly—water flows in perfect circles, creatures freeze mid-motion, plants bend toward the activated device—before erupting into even more chaotic activity than before.

The hollow gives the unmistakable impression of a system abandoned by its creators but continuing to function according to its own evolved logic—an industrial process reclaimed by nature but still operating, its purpose long forgotten but its energy undiminished.
