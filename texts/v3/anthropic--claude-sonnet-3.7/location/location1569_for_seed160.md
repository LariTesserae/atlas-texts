# The Glass Dome

In the highlands of a strange realm exists a perfect sphere of transparent material, perhaps thirty meters in diameter. The dome sits in a cradle of weathered stone, an ancient caldera whose sharp edges have long since been rounded by time. The smooth glass-like surface seems manufactured, yet there are no signs of tools or construction—it emerged from this place, or perhaps was placed by entities who left no trace of their passage.

The thin highland air is hot and still around the dome. Heat shimmers rise from the rocky soil, which is cracked and patched with lichens in dull orange and gray. Sparse, reedy plants thrust up in the gullies where occasional rainwater carves temporary channels down the hillside. These plants are adapted to thirst—woody stems and small, waxy leaves that collect dew in the mornings.

Inside the dome is another matter entirely. It is a microclimate preserved in perfect stasis—neither growing nor decaying, neither gaining nor losing moisture. The dome's interior is dense with life, its atmosphere thick and tropical. The condensation that forms on the interior creates tiny rivulets that run down precisely the same paths they have followed for perhaps centuries or millennia, feeding the same rootlets, pooling in the same depressions.

The space within is crowded with forms of vegetation that seem almost mechanical in nature. Fern-like structures with coppery fronds unfurl in perfect spirals. Bulbous pods hang suspended by fibrous threads, their translucent shells revealing intricate clockwork components meshed with organic tissue. What might be flowers open and close in timed sequences, though no pollinator ever visits them. They need none—nothing here reproduces or dies.

Small creatures inhabit this preserved ecosystem. They move with purpose but no urgency. Crystalline beetles with geared legs traverse the same paths endlessly. What appear to be hummingbirds hover in fixed positions, wings a blur, drawing nectar from tubular blooms—but their eyes are faceted gems and their bodies contain visible pistons and miniature flywheels. A salamander with a segmented metallic spine basks eternally on a stone, neither hungry nor sated.

The sounds inside are minimal and repetitive—the gentle click of insectoid limbs, the soft whirr of wings, the occasional ping of a droplet falling onto a particular leaf. These sounds have maintained their pattern without variation for as long as the dome has existed.

The dome's glass is warm to the touch, but neither hot nor cool enough to collect external condensation. Occasionally, during the brief rainstorms that sweep the highlands, water sheets over its surface, briefly distorting the view inside before running off into the rocky soil.

No entrance exists. No creature has ever entered or departed. The dome sits as a perfect, preserved moment—an industrial ecosystem in miniature, functioning without growth, without change, without purpose beyond its own existence.

Scientists who have studied it cannot determine its age. The caldera that cradles it is ancient, but the dome itself exists outside normal measures of time. Some theorize it is a sample collection from elsewhere; others believe it to be an experiment abandoned by unknown intelligences; still others suggest it is simply the dream of the mountain made manifest.

Whatever its origin, the glass dome remains—hot, dense, strange, and utterly unchanging.
