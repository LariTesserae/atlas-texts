# The Glass Garden

A single mountain dome rises from the austere highland plain, its crystalline walls glinting in the relentless light. This is not a structure built so much as encouraged into being—a perfect hemisphere perhaps sixty paces across, enclosing a miraculous impossibility.

The dome's surface is seamless silica, clear as finest glass yet strong as stone, formed through some ancient technique of sand-fusion. When struck, it resonates with a pure, lingering tone that hangs in the cool, still air.

Inside blooms the impossible: a dense tapestry of life thriving in absolute drought.

The flora within has evolved beyond the need for liquid water. Instead, plants extract moisture directly from the cool air, their translucent tissues glistening with tiny pockets of captured humidity. Broad leaves stretch toward the ceiling, their surfaces studded with microscopic crystalline structures that split light into rainbow fragments. Thick stalks rise in spiraling patterns, each supporting dozens of smaller offshoots creating an architectural density that feels both chaotic and perfectly arranged.

The vegetation displays a palette unexpected in a waterless realm—deep emeralds and teals alongside vibrant magentas and golds. The plants grow not in soil but into a substrate of fine mineral dust, their root systems forming intricate lacework patterns just visible beneath the surface.

Small creatures dart through this crystalline forest—six-legged lizards with scales that refract light like living prisms; diaphanous moth-like beings with wings that make no sound; tiny translucent snails leaving trails of crystallized mucus that glitter in the brightness. These animals have evolved similarly extraordinary adaptations, extracting moisture from the air and the plants themselves, existing in perfect equilibrium with their environment.

Around the base of the dome, simple stone seats have been arranged in a circle, fashioned from the same highland rock that stretches to the horizon. Primitive tools—chisels of copper and hardened wood, polishing stones, and crude brushes—lie neatly arranged on a flat boulder. Their well-worn handles suggest generations of use.

The dome itself appears to be still forming at its edges, with thin new growth extending outward in perfect geometric patterns. This expansion happens imperceptibly slowly, adding perhaps a finger's width every decade. The entire structure seems to exist in a state of perpetual becoming, neither complete nor truly changing.

The light within is amplified by the dome's curvature, creating an interior brightness that almost hurts the eyes, yet casts no harsh shadows. This illumination remains constant regardless of the time of day outside—a perfect, suspended moment of clarity.

The quiet here is profound but not absolute. The occasional crystalline ping of plant growth echoes softly. The tiny fauna produce gentle clicks and whispers as they move. Sometimes, a single clear note will sound from an unknown source within the tangle of life, hanging in the air before fading to silence.

This place feels both ancient and newborn, a perfect microcosm preserved in glass—a garden without water that has found a different way to flourish.
