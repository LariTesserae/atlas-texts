# The Hollow Upthrust

The storm-wash has just passed, leaving behind a network of thin, silvery streams that meander across the impossibly steep slopes of the crystal columns. These are not mountains in any traditional sense, but rather enormous hollow spires of translucent mineral that thrust upward from the plateau's broken floor, reaching heights that make the thin air whistle through their natural apertures. The sound is barely perceptible—a soft, distant keening that comes and goes with the warm winds.

Each spire is a study in geometric precision, as though extruded from the earth rather than built upon it. Their surfaces are fresh and glassy, unweathered by time, with edges that could slice flesh with casual indifference. The entire field of spires extends beyond sight, a forest of crystalline towers rising from a landscape that seems to have been birthed only moments ago in geological time.

The water that remains from the storm doesn't pool but rather seems alive with purpose, flowing in rivulets that defy gravity, sometimes moving upward along the crystal facets before disappearing into microscopic fissures. The moisture has an iridescent quality, catching the merciless sunlight that bears down from a cloudless sky, casting rainbow spectrums that dance across the internal hollows of the towers.

There is no plant life here—not a blade of grass nor a speck of lichen—just the bare, glistening mineral world reflecting the brutal light. The absence of vegetation reveals the raw architecture of this place: a canvas of silica, quartz, and unknown compounds arranged in patterns too regular to be natural, too wild to be constructed.

But the absence of plant life does not mean absence of life.

They emerge as the water recedes—countless tiny creatures that swarm from within the hollow spires. Some skitter across the crystal surfaces on hair-thin legs, moving in coordinated waves that from a distance resemble flowing liquid. Others take to the air on translucent wings that catch the light in blinding flashes. Still others leap impossible distances between spires, their bodies elongating and contracting like living elastics.

These fauna are not insects as we know them, nor birds, nor mammals—they exist in a taxonomy all their own. Their bodies appear partly crystalline themselves, as though evolved from the very substance of this place. They communicate through subtle vibrations that travel through the mineral structures, creating a complex language that stays just below the threshold of human hearing.

Occasionally, a larger entity will emerge from one of the hollow spires—something that moves with frightening intelligence, collecting the smaller creatures not as prey but as components. These gatherers assemble the smaller beings into temporary, composite forms that serve unknown functions before disaggregating again, returning their components to the general swarm.

In certain places, primitive structures can be found—not buildings exactly, but arrangements of crystal fragments that have been deliberately positioned. Some form spiral patterns that channel the strange upward-flowing water; others create acoustic chambers that amplify the barely-audible sounds of the creatures' communications. These constructions show evidence of tool use—simple hammering and chipping—though who or what created them remains uncertain.

The entire ecosystem exists in a state of perpetual flux. The spires themselves are not static but growing, contracting, occasionally shattering and reforming in violent, soundless explosions that send crystal shards flying outward before they're caught in some invisible force and drawn back into new configurations. These dramatic restructurings happen seemingly at random, forcing the swarming creatures to adapt their migrations and nesting patterns continuously.

Despite the constant motion, there is an overwhelming emptiness to this place—vast spaces exist between the spires, and even the teeming creatures occupy only a fraction of the available volume. Sound travels strangely here, sometimes carrying for impossible distances, sometimes being swallowed entirely by the crystal structures.

The air is warm but dry, and though water is present everywhere in thin sheets and flowing lines, there is a sense of underlying thirst to the landscape, as though it consumes moisture as quickly as it appears, fueling some unseen process deep within the hollow cores of the mineral forest.

This is a world in its infancy, or perhaps one constantly reborn—a place of endless becoming with rules that seem to rewrite themselves moment by moment under the unrelenting brightness of the sun.
