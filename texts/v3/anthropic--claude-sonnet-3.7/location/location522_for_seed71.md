# The Primordial Heights

The vast plateaus stretch beyond all horizons, flat as if pressed by some cosmic iron, yet reaching so high into the atmosphere that the air remains perpetually cold and thin. This ancient tableland—a continental expanse that seems to have no edge—has been worn almost perfectly smooth by incalculable eons of erosion. The stone is polished in places to a glassy finish that glimmers wetly under the normal light of a distant sun, though that light somehow feels stretched and attenuated across the impossible breadth of this place.

Across this high plain grow forests of crystalline vegetation—structures that resemble plants only in the most abstract sense. They push upward in geometric fractals of translucent green and blue, capturing the light and refracting it in patterns that sometimes appear to move independently of their source. These "plants" grow in dense but widely separated groves, leaving vast expanses of bare, polished stone between them. When the wind moves through them—and the wind is constant here—they emit a soft, bell-like hum that seems to travel farther than sound should.

The moisture here doesn't fall as rain but rather manifests as a pervasive dampness that rises from within the stone itself. The plateau "breathes" on some incomprehensible cycle—exhaling a fine mist that condenses on the crystal growths and collects in shallow, perfectly circular pools that dot the landscape at mathematically precise intervals. These pools never overflow, regardless of how much moisture accumulates; something in the bedrock slowly drinks it away again.

At irregular intervals across the plateau rise cities—or perhaps a single city distributed across space in a way that confounds normal geography. The structures are built of the same polished stone as the plateau itself, as if they weren't built at all but extruded from the ground. Their architecture defies conventional understanding: buildings that seem to fold into themselves, streets that spiral upward only to return to their starting points while having climbed thousands of feet, arches that appear to support nothing yet somehow hold up everything.

The inhabitants of this place, if they can be called that, aren't creatures of flesh but rather densities of purpose—concentrations of intention that manipulate crude tools carved from the crystalline vegetation. They move about their incomprehensible business with slow deliberation, weaving materials into forms that seem to flicker between dimensions. Their "voices" are the occasional shifts in the background hum of the crystal forests—subtle modulations that might be language or might be something else entirely.

The most disturbing aspect of this realm is how reality itself seems worn thin, like the plateau's surface. In certain areas, particularly near the centers of the distributed city, space folds inappropriately. A step that should cover a meter might traverse a kilometer, or vice versa. Time behaves similarly—the sun occasionally jumps in the sky, or hangs motionless for what feels like days. Sometimes the crystal forests appear to age centuries in moments, growing wildly before settling back into their previous forms.

The pools of water occasionally mirror not the sky above but impossible landscapes that might exist elsewhere—or elsewhen. When the mist is thickest, it sometimes parts to reveal glimpses of other plateaus, identical to this one but somehow fundamentally different, as if the universe here has been thinned to the point where parallels bleed through.

Despite all this activity—the breathing of the plateau, the growth of the crystals, the intentions-made-manifest going about their business—a profound quiet blankets everything. Sounds don't echo here; they terminate with unnatural abruptness, absorbed perhaps by the worn fabric of reality itself. The winds that cause the crystal forests to sing move without the rushing noise they should make, as if sound itself is a scarce resource in this ancient, elevated realm.

And beneath it all lies the sense that this place has reached its final form—that the processes of erosion have not just shaped the physical stone but have somehow eroded possibility itself, wearing down the very concept of change until only these few, strange constants remain: the stone, the crystals, the mist, the city, the quiet, and the thinness between here and elsewhere.
