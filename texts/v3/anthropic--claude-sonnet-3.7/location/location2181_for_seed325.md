# The Undercavern Gardens of Ithme

Below the surface, the Undercavern Gardens of Ithme spread in vast, interconnected chambers—not deep enough to reach the hot stone of the earth's belly, but far enough that the upper world is a distant memory. The cave system expands for miles in every direction, a sprawling network of inhabited grottos and passageways.

The foundation of Ithme is still being born. In the outer reaches, raw caverns continue to form as water slowly carves through limestone, creating nascent spaces that will one day join the greater network. Here, stalactites are mere nubs, stalagmites barely risen bumps, the stone still surrendering to the patient persistence of water. Geological adolescence.

The inhabited central chambers are bathed in a warm, diffuse light that seems to have no single source. The air here maintains a comfortable warmth, as if the stone itself exhales heat. This warmth encourages a peculiar humidity that clings to everything—not wet enough to drip, but present enough to dampen cloth left too long in one place. The moisture supports life in abundance.

Plants thrive impossibly in this subterranean realm. Vines with translucent leaves spiral up carved stone columns. Phosphorescent fungi grow in thick carpets, casting subtle blue-green illumination across the cavern floors. In corners, collections of strange tubers push up through the thin soil, their skin almost glasslike, revealing pulpy interiors that glow with stored light. The vegetation doesn't reach for the sun; it reaches for each other, creating a dense network of intertwined growth that forms natural curtains and partitions throughout the space.

The people of Ithme build with and around this growth. Their dwellings are semi-permanent structures made from cured fungal slabs and carefully bent living wood, supplemented with clay harvested from underground rivers that thread through the lower chambers. Each home connects to others through a network of short tunnels and shared spaces, creating neighborhoods that grow organically like the plants themselves.

The settlement is dense—structures packed close, utilizing every stable surface of the cavern. Yet despite this density, there is order. Water channels are carefully maintained with wooden sluices and clay pipes, directing moisture from the natural seepage points to collection basins and growing areas. Storage niches are carved into walls, their edges smoothed by generations of hands.

Animals have found their place in this strange ecology. Blind fish swim in the slow-moving streams. Small, furry creatures with oversized ears scuttle between dwellings, kept as pets and pest-catchers. Colonies of bats roost in the highest reaches of the caverns, emerging through vertical shafts to the surface world at dusk and returning before dawn. There are insects too—not the biting, stinging kind, but luminescent beetles that crawl in spiraling patterns across the walls and ceiling, adding their light to the gentle glow of the fungi.

The people work primarily with bone, wood, and stone tools. Metal is rare and precious, usually small pieces hammered from nuggets found in the streams. They carve intricate patterns into everything—tools, homes, even the passable walls of the caverns themselves—recording stories and knowledge in a pictographic script that spirals and branches like their ubiquitous vines.

Sound behaves strangely here. Voices don't echo as one might expect in a cavern; instead, they seem muffled by the dense vegetation and the particular acoustics of the space. Conversations remain private even in the communal areas. The most prevalent sounds are the gentle trickle of water through channels, the soft rustling of leaves as air circulates through natural vents, and occasionally, the distant chittering of bats.

What's most remarkable about Ithme is its stability. While the outermost caverns continue their slow formation, the central network has reached a perfect equilibrium. The temperature never varies. The water flows at a constant rate. The plants grow to a certain size and then maintain themselves. Even the population seems fixed—not growing beyond what the space can support. It's as if time moves differently here, or perhaps doesn't move at all in the conventional sense. The Undercavern Gardens simply exist, neither advancing nor retreating, a pocket universe of perpetual now.
