# The Cavernyss

Embedded beneath the barren ice plains of this world exists a world-within-a-world, a place of improbable life where physics seems to fold in on itself. From the surface, only small stone monoliths suggest anything unusual—irregular formations arranged in patterns that seem too deliberate for natural occurrence, yet bear no discernible meaning.

The descent through narrow fissures reveals the Cavernyss—a system of massive underground chambers that defy comprehension. The ceiling soars hundreds of meters overhead, forming cathedral-like spaces encased in bare obsidian and basalt. No water exists here, not as liquid, not as ice, not even as vapor. This realm exists in perfect aridity, a dryness so complete it feels like a physical presence.

What makes the Cavernyss remarkable is not the stone but what grows upon it. Every surface—walls, ceiling, floor—teems with a dense carpet of bioluminescent plant-life that is not quite vegetation as we understand it. This is growth without moisture, life thriving on something other than water. The plants—if that term applies—form a dense tapestry of interweaving structures: translucent tendrils, fibrous mats, bulbous protrusions, and thin filaments that hang like ghostly curtains.

They glow. They all glow. Blues and purples dominate, casting just enough light to render the darkness navigable but never dispelling it completely. The darkness here feels primordial, as though it predates the light itself. The glow pulses subtly, not in unison but in complex patterns that ripple across chambers, creating the unsettling impression of communication.

The temperature remains cool—not the killing cold of the surface above, but a persistent chill that preserves rather than destroys. Breath would fog here, if there were breath to be seen.

The plant-mass seems newly formed, still expanding. At growth edges, you can observe new structures crystallizing directly from the stone, neither seeds nor spores involved. The rock itself appears to be transforming into this living material through some inscrutable geological-biological process. In places, half-formed tendrils emerge from stone like sculptures being revealed by an invisible chisel.

And then there is the noise. The unrelenting, bewildering noise.

The Cavernyss roars with sounds that have no obvious source. Clicks and buzzes form a constant backdrop, punctuated by occasional resonant booms that shake loose dust from the heights. Somewhere between these layers exist sounds like distant voices engaged in urgent conversation—but no mouths form the words. The plant-mass itself seems to vibrate, contributing harmonics to this living symphony.

Moving among this vegetative abundance are the fauna—though "fauna" seems an inadequate descriptor. They are chitinous things, translucent creatures with too many legs, beings that appear to phase in and out of solidity. They skitter across walls and ceiling with no regard for gravity. Some are small as insects, others large as dogs. None have eyes, yet all seem aware. None have mouths, yet all consume, absorbing nutrients directly from the plant-mass through specialized limbs.

Occasionally, one encounters small stone structures amid the growth—perfect cubes, spheres with precise geometric patterns, spirals that couldn't possibly have formed naturally. These bear no tool marks, no signs of construction, yet could not be natural. They appear neither ancient nor new but somehow outside of time altogether, as though they've always been forming but never complete.

The Cavernyss exists in perfect stasis. What grows does not decay. What moves does not age. The temperature never fluctuates. The light never brightens. The sound never ceases. Time feels irrelevant here, a concept belonging to another world entirely.

To visit the Cavernyss is to encounter a pocket universe with its own rules—a place that should not exist but stubbornly does, hidden beneath an unremarkable surface, waiting to be found by those who know where to look.
