# The Monolith Fields of Aadkur

Obelisks of ancient manufactured stone thrust upward from the continental expanse, each stretching kilometers into the thin air. They stand like dark sentinels against the perpetual amber twilight—their tops lost in the low-hanging mists that gather around their heights.

The air is warm and carries the taste of copper. It moves slowly between the monuments, generating low, mournful tones as it finds resonant chambers within the ancient structures. These deep harmonic drones occasionally swell into thunderous bass notes when the winds align, sending vibrations through the porous stone beneath your feet.

The ground itself is not natural rock but a manufactured composite—a smooth, slightly yielding surface of interlocking geometric patterns that extend to every horizon. This material, neither metal nor stone, bears the patina of incalculable age. Its once-perfect tessellations have been weathered by eons of slow erosion into softer, rounded forms. Small pools of condensation gather in the deeper depressions, collecting the moisture that weeps from the monoliths during thermal shifts.

Nothing grows here. No plant has ever taken root in this artificial landscape. No animal has ever called it home. The entire region—perhaps the entire world—appears to be a single vast construction, built for purposes no living mind could fathom.

The monoliths aren't uniform. Some are angular and faceted, others spiral upward in impossible geometries. Many bear surfaces etched with patterns too regular to be natural, too complex to be decorative. These might be writing, or circuitry, or mathematical notations from some civilization so ancient that time itself has nearly forgotten them. Occasionally, during certain thermal alignments, droplets of moisture trace these patterns, momentarily highlighting them with glistening trails before evaporating in the ambient warmth.

The dim light never varies in intensity, though its source remains unclear. No sun is visible through the perpetual amber haze. The light seems to emanate from the material itself—a residual energy from whatever processes once powered this place.

Sound carries strangely here. The drone of the wind through the monoliths forms a constant background, but occasionally sharper sounds emerge: the crack of an ancient support finally giving way after millions of years of stress; the thunderous collapse of a distant structure; the ringing echo of water droplets falling into perfect acoustic chambers.

Movement here happens on geological timescales. The moisture that condenses at the heights of the monoliths might take decades to trickle down to their bases. The erosion that has softened the once-sharp edges of the ground patterns has worked over spans of time that defy comprehension.

What built this place? What was its purpose? There are no answers in the Monolith Fields of Aadkur, only the silent testimony of structures so old they might outlast the concept of age itself. They stand as they have always stood, neither alive nor truly dead, waiting for something no one remembers.
