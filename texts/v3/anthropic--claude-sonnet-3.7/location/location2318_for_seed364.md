# The Canopy Garden

The humid air weighs on your skin as you enter the ancient terrarium. The glass dome rises perhaps thirty meters at its apex, a cathedral to chlorophyll whose panes have gone milky with mineral deposits and the slow breath of uncounted generations of plants. Narrow iron ribs arch upward, holding the structure in place—Victorian-era metalwork that has mellowed into a deep rust-green patina that harmonizes with the living walls.

This place sits nestled in the mountains, one of a string of botanical research stations established by an empire long since faded from the world. The thin air makes your breathing shallow, though the dome's interior is several degrees warmer than the rocky landscape outside.

The floor has long since disappeared. What was once tile and concrete has been claimed by the dense carpet of moss, ferns, and flowering ephemerals that consume every horizontal surface. Your feet sink slightly with each step, water seeping around your ankles—not a true marsh, but a perpetually damp ecosystem where condensation drips constantly from the overhead glass.

The original planting beds have overflowed their boundaries, creating a landscape where distinctions between path and garden have dissolved over centuries. Mechanical remnants of the original irrigation system poke through the undergrowth—copper pipes, brass valves, and the occasional gauge whose needle trembles slightly as water still somehow flows through ancient channels.

Most striking is the noise. The dome reverberates with a cacophony of life. Birds of impossible variety—descendants of escaped specimens, evolving in isolation—dart between hanging vines, their calls echoing against the curved glass ceiling. Iridescent insects hum in dense clouds around flowering vines. Small primates, no larger than squirrels but with almost human faces, chatter as they leap between branches, their families having adapted to this enclosed world over countless generations.

In the center stands what must have been a control station—a two-story iron platform with spiral stairs, now completely overtaken by climbing plants. Their thick, woody stems have incorporated the metal into their own structures, creating a hybrid of machine and life. Brass dials and levers protrude from flowered vines, still connected to unseen mechanisms that regulate temperature and humidity.

The air vibrates with the steady drone of unseen pumps somewhere beneath the verdant floor. Occasionally, jets of mist spray from nozzles hidden among the foliage, triggered by ancient timers still faithfully maintaining their cycles. The technology doesn't appear to have been maintained by human hands for decades, perhaps centuries, yet some symbiosis between the machines and the life they sustain keeps the system functioning.

Deeper in, past curtains of trailing vines, you glimpse small structures—stone grottos and wooden pavilions now subsumed by plant life, their original purpose obscured by time. In one, copper wires trail from an ancient recording device, its brass horn pointed outward as if still collecting the sounds of this evolved ecosystem.

The whole garden feels like it's breathing—expanding and contracting with the natural rhythms of the day. Light shifts through the clouded glass panels, creating pockets of golden illumination that move slowly across the dense vegetation. At the edges of your vision, the movement of countless small creatures creates a constant animation, a perpetual dance of consumption and growth.

Water is the lifeblood here—condensing on the glass, trickling down iron columns, pooling in broad leaves, and eventually returning to the unseen reservoir below. The entire space exists in a state of perfectly balanced decay and renewal, an ancient system that has found its equilibrium after centuries of evolution.
