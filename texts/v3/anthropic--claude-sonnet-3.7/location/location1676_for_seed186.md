# The Hollow Spire

The spire juts from the earth like a tooth from parched gums. Its surface, scoured by millennia of scorching winds, reveals whorled patterns of ancient sedimentary stone—maroon, ochre, and bone-white strata compressed into a tapering needle that climbs nearly vertical toward a sky that seems too close, too heavy.

Inside is hollowness. A narrow opening—more crack than entrance—admits you to the darkness within. The temperature drops only marginally from the blistering exterior; this is not a shelter from heat but rather a different expression of it: contained, dense, pressing against your skin from all directions.

The interior space spirals upward in a natural chimney perhaps forty meters high. What little light filters through cracks in the stone reveals a chamber that could hold a modest chapel, though no human hands shaped this place. The stone walls bear the patient signatures of water's occasional passage—smooth channels and tiny basins where moisture briefly pooled before evaporating into the perpetual dry heat.

In the deepest corner, nearly invisible until your eyes adjust, a thin rivulet of water emerges from the rock face. It trickles down with painful slowness, each droplet seeming to deliberate its path before committing to gravity. The water collects in a shallow depression in the stone floor, a basin no larger than a serving platter, before disappearing through an imperceptible crack.

This miniature spring shouldn't exist. The surrounding landscape is a high desert plateau, cracked and baked under a relentless sun. Yet here water emerges, defiant of the aridity, maintaining its cycle with stubborn persistence.

Near the water, improbably, three pale plants grow—spindle-stemmed with tiny leaves that never fully unfurl, adapted to require almost nothing. Their roots spread visible across stone rather than digging into it, white tendrils seeking sustenance in the thin film of moisture that occasionally slicks the rock face.

But the water isn't quite water. Observe it closely and you'll notice how it sometimes moves against gravity, how it refracts light at impossible angles, how it occasionally forms perfect geometric patterns before dissolving back into ordinary fluid behavior. This isn't just an anomalous spring—it's a place where physical laws thin.

In the absolute quiet of the chamber, you might hear the water's impossible voice—not a babbling or rushing, but a sound like very distant machinery. A rhythmic clicking, a hum at the threshold of perception, suggesting processes occurring somewhere unreachable.

Time behaves strangely here. The shadows cast by the sparse light don't move at the expected rate. Sometimes they appear to pulse slightly, like the chamber itself is breathing. Occasionally, if you watch the water long enough, you'll see it freeze in mid-drip for several seconds before resuming its course, as though reality briefly buffered.

The only fauna are tiny translucent arthropods, barely visible, that occasionally emerge from crevices to harvest moisture. Their bodies seem to flicker in and out of existence, sometimes solid, sometimes just suggestions of form. They leave no tracks.

High above, at the spire's peak where the chamber narrows to a throat barely wide enough for a bird to pass through, the stone has worn thin enough to admit a shaft of light for perhaps twenty minutes each day. When this light strikes the pool, the water sometimes separates into colors that don't belong in any rainbow, and the air fills with a scent like ozone and copper.

This place has stood unchanged since before humans walked the earth, yet feels transient, as though at any moment it might fold into itself and disappear. It exists in defiance of its surroundings: a hollow monument to improbability, a tiny pocket where reality's fabric has worn just thin enough to glimpse something else working behind it.
