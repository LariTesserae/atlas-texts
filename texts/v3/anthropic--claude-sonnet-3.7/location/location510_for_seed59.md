# The Living City of Nywellia

In the crook of Mount Hanori's eastern slope lies Nywellia, a place where the boundary between city and forest has long since dissolved into irrelevance. From a distance, it appears as merely another verdant outcropping on the mountainside, distinguished only by occasional wisps of cook-smoke curling above the canopy and the distant chorus of its inhabitants.

Nywellia exists at that peculiar elevation where clouds frequently settle—not high enough to challenge breathing, but sufficient to sit amid the mists that roll through daily. The air hangs cool and moist against the skin, carrying the earthy perfume of perpetual growth and decay.

The city itself is a triumph of botanical engineering passed down through generations. Ancient hardwood trees form the skeletal structure of what outsiders might call buildings, though Nywellians consider them living homes. These trees were carefully guided over centuries, their growth directed to create chambers, walkways, and public spaces. The spaces between are filled with a meticulously maintained cascade of lesser flora—vines that serve as stairs, broad-leaved plants that collect rainwater, thick moss carpets underfoot that absorb sound and provide insulation.

Water is the lifeblood of Nywellia, coursing through the settlement in countless ways. Small streams have been coaxed into channels that wind through the living architecture. After rain—which comes frequently in the afternoon—thousands of natural gutters formed from broad leaves and hollow stems direct water into collection basins and cisterns made from the hollowed trunks of ancients. The sound of running water is a constant companion, sometimes a gentle background trickle, other times a rushing torrent during the heavy mountain rains.

Movement in Nywellia occurs on multiple levels simultaneously. Bridges of woven vines connect tree-chambers above, while below, narrow pathways wind between the massive trunks. In the busiest sections, three or four layers of activity might occur vertically in the same footprint of land. The density creates an intimacy—voices call across gaps, conversations flow between levels, and neighbors live in such proximity that privacy is maintained more through social custom than physical separation.

The fauna of Nywellia is as much a part of the city as its plant structure. Birds nest in the upper reaches, their songs forming a constant chorus that swells and diminishes throughout the day. Small primates—distant cousins of monkeys but unique to Mount Hanori—have adapted to city life, serving roles between pets, assistants, and neighbors. They help collect fruit from high branches and carry messages between distant parts of the settlement. Brilliantly colored frogs and salamanders inhabit the damp corners, kept for both their beauty and their ability to control insects. Countless insects buzz, crawl, and flutter throughout the layers, most in careful balance with the ecosystem, though occasional swarms require collective action to redirect away from crucial food stores.

The technology of Nywellia is entirely derived from its environment. Metal is rare and primarily reserved for cooking implements and specialized tools. Instead, artisans work with materials the forest provides—hardened resins, cured vines strong as rope, polished wood, shaped gourds, and woven fibers. Water power drives simple machines—grinding mills, pulley systems, irrigation networks—all constructed primarily of wood, stone, and plant materials.

The most striking aspect of Nywellia for visitors is its constant motion and sound. Nothing is ever truly still. The living infrastructure sways subtly with the mountain breezes. Residents move up and down through the layers with practiced ease. The primate assistants chatter and leap between perches. Water splashes and gurgles through the city's veins. Singing is common practice, voices joining the birds in what outsiders might first perceive as chaos but soon recognize as an informal communication system—different tunes signaling different activities or needs.

During storms, which come frequently in the wet season, Nywellia transforms. The outer layers of vegetation fold inward according to their nature, providing protection to the inner chambers. Waterways that normally trickle become rushing torrents, carefully channeled to avoid damage while storing what can be kept. The sound rises to a magnificent crescendo—rain on thousands of leaves, wind through the swaying upper reaches, the excited calls of children celebrating the drama. Then, as quickly as it arrived, the storm passes, and Nywellia unfolds again, refreshed and glistening.

The people of Nywellia do not see themselves as separate from this living architecture but as its caretakers and component parts. Their hands are perpetually stained green from working with the plants, their clothing woven from local fibers in patterns that mimic the surrounding foliage. They move through their vertical world with the same ease that flatland dwellers cross a town square, thinking in three dimensions as naturally as breathing.

Here, in this cool, mist-wrapped enclave on the mountain's flank, humanity has not conquered nature but rather joined it in intimate partnership, creating something neither could achieve alone.
