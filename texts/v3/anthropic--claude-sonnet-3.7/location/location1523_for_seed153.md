# The Hollow

A sphere of warm air nestles in the hollow trunk of an ancient mangrove, one of thousands in this flooded forest. The tree is massive, its gnarled body carved out over centuries by patient termites and opportunistic fungi, leaving a chamber almost large enough for a person to stand in. Almost, but not quite—you must crouch to enter, ducking beneath the curtain of aerial roots that veil the opening.

Inside, the wood's inner walls glisten with moisture. Water seeps through the living tissue of the tree, not enough to drip constantly but sufficient to maintain a perpetual slickness. Pale blue-green lichens trace delicate patterns across the curves of the hollow, their luminescence so faint it seems more memory than light. The air is heavy with humidity, warm and thick in your lungs.

This chamber sits barely a foot above the water table. Through a gap in the floor's tangled roots, you can glimpse the tea-colored water below, moving with languid purpose as the tide gradually rises. By nightfall, the lower third of this space will be submerged.

The hollow feels impossibly empty for a tropical environment. Where you might expect insect colonies and nesting creatures, there is a peculiar absence. The locals call these rare empty chambers "breathing rooms" and believe they are maintained by the forest itself—spaces where the mangroves exhale.

Outside, the dense canopy of the mangrove forest stretches for miles, broken only by the meandering channels that serve as roads for the small settlement built throughout the trees. Simple wooden platforms and walkways connect the community's structures—homes and workshops built from local timber and palm thatch, elevated safely above the twice-daily floods.

The near-silence inside the hollow is profound but not absolute. The muffled sounds of the mangrove forest seep through: the distant call of a solitary kingfisher, the gentle lapping of water against roots, the occasional soft voices of villagers passing along a nearby walkway. Sometimes, when the breeze shifts just so, you can catch fragments of conversation or the rhythmic sounds of someone weaving palm fronds into baskets.

Despite the relative stillness, the hollow is alive with subtle movement. Beads of condensation form and roll down the inner walls, following paths worn by countless predecessors. The lichens slowly expand their territory, molecule by molecule. The level of water visible through the root gap rises incrementally. The light changes quality as clouds pass overhead, filtered through layers of leaves before reaching this hidden pocket.

Time moves differently here. The hollow has existed for decades, maybe centuries, and will likely persist for decades more—until the inevitable day when a storm topples this ancient tree, returning its material to the cycle of the mangrove forest. But for now, it remains—a pocket of emptiness in a world of teeming life, a breathing room both separate from and utterly part of the water-rich landscape that contains it.
