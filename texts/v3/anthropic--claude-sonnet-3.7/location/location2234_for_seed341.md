# The Nascent Hollows

Below the surface, an underground realm is taking form. Not yet a cave system, not yet hollowed enough to be called caverns, not old enough for stalactites, stalagmites, or calcite formations. This is a younger kind of dark.

The noise is overwhelming—a cacophony of ceaseless, thunderous cracks and groans as stone shifts against stone. The very bones of the earth are being rearranged. Every few minutes, a sound like distant cannon fire signals another chamber being born as rock faces collapse into new openings. Water plays percussion: dripping, running, pouring, splashing through newly formed channels.

This is a vast expanse of becoming. Miles of interconnected passages and chambers in various stages of formation extend throughout the region. Some areas are merely hairline fractures in bedrock; others are rooms large enough to house several villages. The scope is difficult to grasp, especially in near-total darkness.

Water is the architect here. It seeps through the porous limestone from above, emerges from hidden springs, pools in depressions, and carves paths of least resistance. Underground streams have begun to form, not yet rivers, but ambitious in their erosion. The air hangs heavy with moisture; every surface is slick with condensation. Your breath fogs visibly in the cool atmosphere.

Vegetation is sparse but persistent. Pale fungi colonize the dampest corners, their mycelium networks spreading unseen. Some sprout bioluminescent fruiting bodies that provide rare points of faint greenish-blue light. Slimy algae carpets some of the more established water channels, giving them an uncanny glow when light strikes them.

The few animals that have found their way here are pioneers. Blind crayfish feel their way through shallow pools. Small colonies of bats have discovered some of the higher chambers, using them as daytime sanctuaries. Occasionally, a translucent spider or albino insect scuttles across a wet stone surface. They make little noise compared to the geological symphony surrounding them.

What's unusual is the evidence of early human presence. Not permanent settlements, but signs of exploration and primitive industry. Crude ladders made of bound saplings lead between levels in some areas. Simple wooden dams redirect water in others. Torch marks blacken sections of wall near wider chambers. Most curious are the clay vessels placed to catch mineral-rich dripping water—these are arranged in patterns that suggest purpose beyond mere collection.

The tools left behind are elementary: flint cutting edges bound to wooden handles, bone needles, clay pots, woven reed baskets sealed with pitch. Some chambers contain simple shrines—arrangements of stones circling small carved figures, offerings of feathers and seeds.

The entire system feels dangerously unstable. Rock falls are common. New water channels appear daily, sometimes as sudden floods that rearrange the geography within hours. The cool air carries a constant mist that sometimes thickens into actual fog banks in larger chambers. Navigation would be nearly impossible without leaving markers, and even those might be washed away or buried under fresh debris.

This is a place between places—not yet what it will become, no longer what it was. The darkness is both womb and workshop. And through it all, the ceaseless roar of creation drowns out any possibility of silence, as the earth continues its patient, violent birth of a new world beneath the old.
