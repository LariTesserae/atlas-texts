# The Howling Crags

It is the sound that welcomes you first. A relentless, symphonic roar that seems to come from everywhere and nowhere—the ancient voice of the mountain itself. Some travelers turn back here, at the edge of the Shelf Lands, where flat terrain gives way to the jagged teeth of the Howling Crags. The locals say you never truly hear silence again after visiting; some phantom echo always remains in your ears.

The Crags rise like the exposed spine of the world, a mountain range worn not down but *strange*. Countless millennia of peculiar erosion have not flattened these peaks but hollowed them, creating vast networks of tunnels, archways, and impossible balanced formations. Wind-carved stone stands in delicate arrangements that defy comprehension—razor-thin arches spanning hundreds of feet, stone needles vibrating like tuning forks, and colossal hollow chambers where the mountain's interior has been completely excavated.

These are not young mountains boasting their height, but ancient ones revealing their bones.

The vegetation here thrives in defiance of the elevation. Lush hanging gardens cascade from crevices where soil has collected over eons. Thick, waxy-leafed vines with deep purple undersides snake through the labyrinthine passages, somehow finding purchase on sheer stone faces. Phosphorescent mosses carpet the shadowed interior spaces, glowing softly in blues and greens. The plant life seems almost aggressive in its abundance, as if competing with the stone for dominance, yet the mountain remains primarily a realm of empty, echoing spaces.

Water exists in strange, intermittent ways. Morning fog condenses on the cool stone, creating thousands of tiny rivulets that appear and disappear with the day's warming. Small pools collect in stone basins, some overflowing to create ephemeral waterfalls that may last hours or minutes before evaporating. During the sporadic rainfall, the entire mountain seems to come alive with water, only to slowly dry again, leaving mineral deposits that build up over centuries into intricate, crystalline formations.

The cause of the deafening sound becomes clear as you venture deeper. The wind—constant, powerful, unpredictable—rushes through the mountain's hollow chambers and twisted passages, creating a cacophony of whistles, moans, and thunderous bass notes. It's a wind organ of planetary scale, never playing the same composition twice. The acoustics shift with temperature changes, creating periods of relative calm followed by crescendos that make the very stone vibrate beneath your feet.

This wind is not merely noisy but physically tangible—a turbulent force that can suddenly lift a person off their feet or just as quickly create pockets of dead calm. Dust devils form and dissipate in seconds. Small pebbles skitter across stone platforms without visible cause. The air currents follow no predictable pattern, as if the mountain's hollows create their own weather system, independent of the world outside.

Wildlife is sparse but adapted. Massive birds with fourteen-foot wingspans ride the updrafts, rarely needing to flap as they navigate the aerial chaos with preternatural skill. Small, translucent lizards cling to vertical surfaces, somehow anticipating sudden wind gusts before they arrive. Most creatures here have evolved extraordinary senses of balance or unusual anchoring mechanisms—hooks, adhesives, or magnetic properties—to survive in a place where gravity seems optional.

Human presence is minimal and temporary. Simple stone shelters dot some of the more protected ledges, rarely more than waist-high walls providing windbreaks for travelers. Ancient handholds carved into difficult passages suggest that people have been finding paths through these mountains for thousands of years. Occasionally, you might spot a weather-worn prayer flag or a small shrine consisting of carefully balanced stones—evidence of those who view the mountains' inexplicable properties as sacred.

The few semi-permanent residents are wind-readers, hermits who spend decades learning to predict the mountain's moods. They craft simple tools from mountain materials—wind chimes that serve as early warning systems, woven grass ropes that can withstand surprising tension, and carved wooden masks that filter air during the periodic dust storms. Their most prized possessions are maps etched onto strips of cured hide, showing safe passages that change with the seasons.

Near the heart of the range stands the Amphitheater—a natural formation where dozens of tunnels converge into a massive open chamber. Here the mountain's voice reaches its most articulate expression. Wind-readers gather annually during the calm season to listen for patterns, believing the mountain speaks prophecies for those patient enough to learn its language.

In the deepest chambers, rarely visited, peculiar phenomena occur. Some tunnels seem to connect distant parts of the mountain instantaneously. Certain chambers reverse the direction of falling objects. There are reports of spaces where time passes differently—a person might emerge from a deep passage to find that days have passed outside, or conversely, spend what feels like weeks inside only to discover that the outside world experienced mere hours.

The Howling Crags defy complete exploration or understanding. They are a place where the ancient has become alien, where erosion has not diminished but transformed, where emptiness creates sound, and where humans remain, at best, tolerated visitors in a landscape that seems perpetually on the edge of violent reconfiguration—yet has remained fundamentally unchanged for millions of years.
