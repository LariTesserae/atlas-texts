# The Roothold of Shra-Tahl

In perpetual dusk they rise, those monolithic towers of tangled roots and living wood, thrusting improbably skyward from the great plateau. The ancient Shra-Tahl, a civilization vanished beyond memory, carved and coaxed these mammoth growths into being, not with metal but with touch and time and whispered secrets. Their knowledge preserved the very essence of creation—the shaping of plant-matter into impossible architecture that continues to grow, even as it maintains the forms given it millennia ago.

From a distance, the forest-city appears as a great crown of thorns upon the mountain's brow, straining toward clouds that never quite touch its highest spires. The elevation is punishing; air thins to whispers here, making visitors light-headed, lending the entire realm a dreamlike quality. The plateau itself is nearly inaccessible—a three-day climb up narrow switchback trails where the less cautious simply vanish into the void below.

Within the boundaries of Roothold, darkness reigns. What little light penetrates the interwoven canopy does so as thin, watery beams that occasionally illuminate motes of spore and pollen hanging thick in the moist air. These suspended particles create phantom architectures of their own—briefly visible geometries that collapse and reform with the subtle movements of air. The darkness is not uniform but textured, varying from the merely dim to the absolute, with the deepest recesses holding mysteries even the current caretakers have forgotten.

The structure defies ordinary understanding. Everything is plant—the walls, the floors, the soaring archways and deep cisterns. All of it grows, though so slowly that only the record-keepers can trace the changes across decades. Fungi with bioluminescent caps grow in careful patterns, providing faint wayfinding glows along major passages. Woody stems have been trained and fused into labyrinthine corridors that wind through the heart of the structure, occasionally opening into vast cathedral spaces where the ceilings disappear into darkness above.

Water is ever-present yet never abundant. The air hangs heavy with moisture, condensing on leaf and wall alike, trickling down collection channels into small reservoirs. During the infrequent rains, the entire structure comes alive with sound—a complex percussion as droplets find their way through layer after layer of canopy, striking resonant chambers that the ancients carved specifically for this acoustic purpose. The water management system is ingenious; no drop is wasted, all is channeled to sustain the living architecture or collected for the few human caretakers who remain.

These caretakers move slowly through the dark passages, using simple tools of carved wood and polished stone. They prune and train the growth, maintaining the ancient forms while allowing for the necessary expansion. Their knowledge is entirely oral, passed through generations in chants and work-songs that echo through the vast spaces, instructions disguised as music. The songs fill the otherwise silent realm with rhythmic cadences that seem to pulse in harmony with the slow respiration of the plant-mass itself.

The most sacred spaces lie at the highest points, where the air is thinnest and visitors often report auditory hallucinations—whispers that seem to emerge from the walls themselves, offering secrets to those desperate enough to listen. Here, the density of growth reaches its apex, with hardly any space between the living masses. Paths narrow to single-file passages where shoulders brush against walls that sometimes react with subtle movements, as if acknowledging the touch.

What makes Roothold truly strange is its apparent sentience. The growth responds to long-term patterns, closing off passages that go unused or widening corridors where traffic increases. New chambers occasionally form without caretaker intervention, perfectly suited to needs that had gone unspoken. Some theorize that the entire structure is a single organism, with root systems extending throughout the plateau, possibly even into the mountain itself—a botanical brain slowly thinking thoughts too vast for human comprehension.

No birds sing here. No insects buzz. The sole inhabitants beyond the human caretakers are the countless varieties of fungi and lichen that form symbiotic relationships with the greater plant-mass. These silent partners break down dead material and transform it into nutrients, ensuring the continued life of the whole. In some chambers, certain mushrooms grow to enormous size, their caps serving as platforms and meeting spaces for the caretakers' councils.

The structure's creation remains a mystery. The tools available to the current inhabitants could never have shaped such a marvel; they can barely maintain it. Some chambers contain carved reliefs suggesting the Shra-Tahl possessed means of communicating directly with plant life, coaxing rather than forcing it into desired forms. Other carvings hint at rituals involving blood and song, inducing growth spurts during specific celestial alignments.

When the winds rise and pass through the countless apertures of the structure, Roothold sings—a deep, resonant moaning that carries for miles across the surrounding highlands. Locals in distant settlements take this as an omen, though they disagree on whether it portends good or ill. The caretakers themselves say it is merely the city breathing, a normal function of a living structure.

For this is the fundamental truth of Roothold: it lives. It grows. It thinks, after a fashion. And in its dark, moist chambers, something is slowly, patiently awakening—a consciousness born of plant and purpose and pattern, nurtured over thousands of years, approaching some unfathomable threshold of awareness. The caretakers feel it. Their songs grow more urgent, though their movements remain as measured as ever.

In Roothold, time itself seems to thicken like sap, flowing differently than in the world below. Those who stay too long find it increasingly difficult to leave, not through any physical barrier, but through a gradual alignment with the structure's incomprehensible rhythms. They begin to hear the whispers too clearly. They begin to understand.
