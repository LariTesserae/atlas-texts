# The Hive Cities of Ketra

The first thing you notice is the sound. A constant, thrumming bass that you feel in your chest before your ears—the collective wingbeat of billions. From a distance, the Hive Cities of Ketra appear as massive, irregular columns rising from the highland plateaus, shimmering in the warm light of the amber sun. What ancient civilizations might have mistaken for mountains are in fact the greatest urban centers in this hemisphere, living architectures built and maintained by the Ketran swarms.

Each city towers thousands of meters above the already elevated plains, their forms smoothed by centuries of wind and seasonal rains that leave the outer surfaces polished to a dull sheen. The material—a biologically engineered resin secreted by the worker castes—hardens to something between chitin and concrete, with a strength that has withstood millennia of weathering. No sharp edges remain; every surface curves organically, eroded into elegant, windswept contours.

Water is ever-present here. Not in great seas or lakes, but in the perpetual moisture that seeps through the porous upper layers of the structures. The highland plateaus collect clouds that condense against the massive hive spires, creating a constant weeping of water down specialized channels. These irrigation systems—part natural formation, part deliberate engineering—direct the precious liquid through the entirety of the hive structure. In certain sections, the water collects in terraced pools that reflect the amber light, giving the impression of molten gold flowing down the city's sides.

Plant life is virtually absent, replaced instead by the overwhelming presence of the Ketrans themselves. These creatures—somewhere between insect, bird, and something else entirely—exist in a complex social hierarchy that makes ants seem positively individualistic. Different castes range in size from microscopic maintenance workers to the massive royal engineers whose wingspan can reach four meters. Their bodies shimmer with iridescent scales in blues and golds, creating living mosaics as they move in coordinated patterns across the hive surfaces.

The cities are densely populated, with chambers, tunnels, and open plazas stacked vertically and horizontally in three-dimensional urban planning that maximizes every cubic meter of space. The outer layers are given to industrial production—massive chambers where worker castes operate mechanical looms that weave resin with metal alloys harvested from the mountains. Steam rises from these workshops, venting through specialized chimneys that punctuate the upper reaches of the hives.

The technology here exists in curious harmony with biology—the Ketrans themselves are both the builders and the machinery. Yet they have developed tools and mechanical advantages: water-powered turbines harness the constant flow from above to power primitive but effective industrial processes. Metallic threads are woven into the resin walls, conducting heat and, in some specialized sections, a form of bioelectric current that powers lighting systems and communication networks.

The constant sound is overwhelming to human ears—the background drone of billions of wings, punctuated by the metallic clanking of industry, the gurgling of water channels, and the periodic harmonic vibrations that the Ketrans use as their primary form of long-distance communication. These sound-waves carry through the structure, the entire city becoming a resonance chamber for their complex language.

Time moves slowly here. The Ketrans themselves live for decades, and their collective projects unfold over centuries. Whole sections of the city might be under reconstruction for generations, the changes so gradual as to be imperceptible to short-term visitors. The erosion of the outer surfaces is countered by equally slow applications of new resin, maintaining a delicate balance between natural weathering and artificial preservation.

At dusk, as the amber sun sets behind distant mountains, the most spectacular sight occurs—the Emergence. Millions of Ketrans stream from specialized portals in synchronized patterns, their bodies catching the last light as they spiral upward in massive, living tornados of glittering wings. They are not leaving, merely shifting, reorganizing, as different castes exchange places between the day and night operations of the eternal city.

The air feels thick here, not just from humidity but from the density of life itself. There is barely a cubic meter anywhere that isn't occupied by some form of Ketran activity—resting chambers stacked hundreds deep, royal birthing pools where new queens develop, industrial sectors where resources are processed, and the mysterious inner sanctums where the collective mind of the hive makes its slow, deliberate decisions about the future of their civilization.

Few outsiders ever venture here, and fewer still are welcomed. The Ketrans have little interest in the affairs of ground-dwelling species, continuing their ancient patterns largely unchanged since they first began building these monuments to collective existence millennia ago.
