# Levelsea

From the pass at Heldermoss you first see the Levelsea as a thin green band beneath distant fog. The air grows heavy and cold as you descend. Not the cutting cold of winter, but the deep-in-your-lungs cold of ancient places where warmth has slowly leaked away over eons.

The ground transforms gradually—first the scattered lichenous pads underfoot, then carpets of spongy moss that hold your footprints for minutes after you've passed. Soon you're walking on a living substrate that gives slightly with each step. The moss stretches in all directions, the surface broken only by clustered islands of taller growth.

The plateau finally reveals itself: an impossibly vast highland bog stretching to the horizon, its surface undulating in slow waves. What appeared solid from a distance is a kilometers-wide floating mat of vegetation in constant, imperceptible motion. Below it lies water—not a lake with a bottom, but something deeper and more ancient.

The mat is primarily composed of sphagnum moss in colors from emerald to copper, but also hosts countless other adaptations: tentacled sundews whose glistening droplets catch what little light filters through the mist; colonies of pitcher plants forming boggy archipelagos; knots of twisted heather that have evolved to float rather than root.

Look closely and the surface is alive with movement. Thumb-sized frogs with translucent skin pulse with bioluminescent organs as they leap between moss islands. Clouds of midges rise and fall in shifting columns. Long-legged beetles skitter across the surface tension of exposed water pools. Occasionally, something larger moves beneath the mat, causing ripples that travel dozens of meters before dissipating.

The ambient sound is a layered composition: the deep bass thrumming of gas released from decomposition below, the constant soprano chorus of insects and amphibians, and periodically, a resonant groaning as sections of the mat shift against one another. Every few minutes a geyser-like eruption sends water and plant matter fountaining upward before collapsing back onto the surface with a thunderous splash.

What makes the Levelsea truly unsettling are the machines. Ancient devices of tarnished copper and verdigris-covered brass rise from the surface at irregular intervals—clearly manufactured but of indeterminate purpose. Their moving parts continue to function without apparent power source, pistons pumping slowly, gears turning, occasionally releasing clouds of steam into the cold air. Some tower twenty meters high, their upper workings lost in fog.

The machines seem to harvest something—perhaps the gases from below, maybe the plants themselves, possibly something less tangible. They were built by someone or something, but no sentient beings remain to tend them. They operate according to parameters set so long ago that their original purpose may no longer exist.

Most disturbing are the temporal anomalies. Near the larger machines, time flows irregularly. You might watch a frog leap and freeze mid-air for several minutes before suddenly completing its jump. Or witness rain begin to fall upward, collecting on the underside of leaves. Sometimes you can see your own afterimages trailing behind you, catching up minutes later.

The deeper one ventures into the Levelsea, the more pronounced these effects become. At its center, observers have reported seeing ancient versions of the machines rising whole from the bog, operating briefly, then sinking again—as if the present is being continuously overwritten by fragments of the past.

The Levelsea existed before anything remembered it. It will continue long after memory itself has passed. The machines might be maintaining it, or they might be hastening its transformation into something else entirely. No one stays long enough to find out.
