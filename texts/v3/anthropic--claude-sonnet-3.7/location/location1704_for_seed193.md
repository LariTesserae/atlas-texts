# The Screaming Pillars

From the observation deck—itself a skeletal industrial platform bolted to the side of a raw vertical face—the world reveals itself as an endless network of stone columns. Not mountains, not quite, but something more deliberate: hexagonal pillars of dark basalt thrusting upward from an unseen floor. They stretch to every horizon, a geometry too regular to be natural yet too vast to be built. The deck vibrates constantly beneath your feet, the metal humming in sympathetic resonance.

The air here is cool and carries a mineral dampness. Not wet enough to be called humid, but enough that your lips never fully dry. Occasional wisps of fog curl between the towering columns, catching the normal daylight in ways that fragment it into diffuse glowing regions. The fog doesn't persist; it forms, drifts, and dissipates in rhythmic cycles measured in hours.

Most striking is the sound—a constant, overwhelming roar that comes from the pillars themselves. The noise is not chaotic but orchestral, as if each massive column is a titanic organ pipe. Some emit deep, floor-shaking drones that pulse at intervals of several minutes. Others produce high, keening notes that would shatter glass if there were any to break. The sound follows patterns that hint at meaning without ever revealing it—a language of geological time spoken too slowly for human comprehension.

Vegetation exists in thin margins, mostly in the form of gray-green lichen that traces the edges of the hexagons, and occasional scrubby bushes that somehow root in the narrow joints between pillars. In select locations, where condensation gathers more reliably, delicate ferns unfurl from cracks in the stone. None of it is lush, yet all of it is tenacious.

The observation deck is just one of many scattered industrial installations. Most are monitoring stations—arrays of instruments bolted to the pillars at various heights, connected by catwalks and ladders that seem impossibly delicate against the massive columns. Power cables drape between them, carrying electricity from wind turbines mounted at the very tops of select pillars. Some of the equipment dates back decades, corroded and patched repeatedly; other instruments bear the sleek look of recent installation.

Small settlements exist in the rare places where multiple pillars converge closely enough to create platforms. Buildings of corrugated metal and composite materials cling to these junctions, their walls angled to deflect the perpetual vibrations rather than resist them. The people who live here wear specialized hearing protection that selectively filters frequencies, allowing them to communicate despite the overwhelming acoustic environment.

The most peculiar aspect is the water cycle. There are no rivers as such, but each pillar has its own hydrological system. Condensation forms near the tops, then trickles downward through microscopic channels in the basalt. At certain points—locations that correspond to the nodes of the sound waves—the water erupts outward in horizontal jets, creating brief but dramatic waterfalls that connect pillar to pillar before eventually finding their way downward again. These water bridges form and collapse in patterns that mirror the acoustic pulsations.

Fauna is scarce but specialized. Occasionally, you might glimpse the silhouette of what locals call "echo bats"—creatures that navigate not by echolocation but by sensing the patterns in the ambient noise. A few specialized birds nest in the higher reaches, their calls evolved to cut through specific frequencies of the background roar. Most intriguing are the "column crawlers"—insect-like creatures with flattened bodies that seem to feed on the vibrations themselves, attaching to resonant points on the pillars and remaining motionless for days.

Scientists have determined that the pillars are not geological accidents but the exposed infrastructure of something far larger and more ancient. The hexagonal columns are actually enormous tuning mechanisms, their precise dimensions calibrating whatever lies beneath. The sound is not a byproduct but the primary purpose—a vast machine singing a note that has continued uninterrupted for millennia, for reasons no one has yet deciphered.

When storms pass through—which they do with clockwork regularity—the acoustic properties change dramatically. The sound takes on harmonics that make the very air shimmer visibly. During these events, the monitoring stations record data frantically, and the settlers retreat indoors, knowing that these peak resonance periods can cause hallucinations or worse if experienced unprotected.

The deepest mystery remains what lies at the base of the pillars. Expeditions have attempted to descend, following the water's journey downward, but none have returned with conclusive findings—only fractured reports of increased pressure, altered time perception, and glimpses of vast mechanisms in motion far below.
