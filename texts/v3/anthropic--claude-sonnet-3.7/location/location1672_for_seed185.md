# The Drift

The first thing you notice is the smell: a curious blend of something like cinnamon and hot metal, with traces of what might be cumin or burnt sugar. The air is warm, close, carrying a fine layer of mineral dust that settles on your skin like talc.

You are in a vast network of caverns stretching beyond perception, eroded over geological time from what once might have been limestone but has since transmuted into something that resembles chalcedony—translucent, with swirling patterns of amber, rust, and pale yellow. The ceiling rises high above, somewhere between thirty and two hundred feet, impossible to judge in the peculiar light.

This light comes from nowhere and everywhere. It simply exists as a property of the space itself—a warm amber glow that illuminates without casting shadows, rendering depth perception treacherous. Objects at a distance of fifty paces appear as clear as those at your feet, yet vanish completely beyond some invisible threshold.

The floor undulates gently in ossified waves, as if an ancient sea had been flash-frozen mid-ripple. Sparse vegetation clings to existence here—strange crystalline growths that might be plants but behave like fungi, emitting soft chimes when brushed against. They cluster around the scattered remnants of structures built by hands that were not quite human: broken stone circles, half-buried arches carved with spiraling glyphs that seem to rearrange when observed indirectly, small cairns of geometrically perfect cubes balanced with impossible precision.

The most unsettling aspect of the Drift is its fauna. Abundant life scuttles, slithers, and glides through this realm, but none of it needs water to live. The creatures here are dry as autumn leaves—chitinous things with too many limbs that move with ticking, clockwork precision; translucent arthropods with exoskeletons of what looks like spun sugar; serpentine beings composed of interlocking scales that sound like shuffling playing cards when they move. All are silent save for the mechanical sounds of their motion. None have visible eyes, yet all seem aware of your presence.

Occasionally, you encounter the primitive tools of whoever—or whatever—once dwelled here: blades of beaten copper with handles of crystallized resin; sifting screens woven from metallic fibers; ceremonial masks carved from something that resembles fossilized wood but rings like ceramic when tapped.

The rules of reality grow thin in the Drift. Time passes differently depending on which direction you face. Objects sometimes leave afterimages in the air when moved. Gravity feels inconstant—not dramatically, but enough that you sometimes feel slightly heavier or lighter without explanation. Distances expand and contract unpredictably, making navigation by conventional means impossible.

Change comes slowly to this place. You might watch a crystal formation for days and see no growth, then turn away for a moment to find it has sprouted new facets. The creature populations shift in glacial cycles—over months, the ticking arthropods might gradually give way to the card-serpents, then back again.

There is no water here. Not a drop. Nothing liquid at all, in fact. The atmosphere itself seems to exist in a state between gas and solid, sometimes forming brief, floating geometric patterns that dissolve when approached. The entire ecosystem functions on principles that defy conventional biology, using heat and mineral transfer instead of fluid exchange.

The Drift is a place of parched infinity, of warm silence, of slow, patient madness that will rewrite your understanding of what constitutes life, time, and matter—if you remain long enough to let it.
