# The Suspended Cities of Therma Hydrus

The first shock, if you are not prepared, is that you are not swimming. The heated mineral water, supersaturated with salts and dense suspended particulates, holds your body with a pressure that feels almost deliberate, as if the liquid itself has decided to cradle you. Breathing is possible through the specialized vents that line the main corridors—pockets where the water is processed into a breathable medium that feels like inhaling warm, heavy mist.

Therma Hydrus stretches before you, a vast network of submerged habitation that has existed since before recorded history. The cities hang suspended in the endless hot waters, anchored to ancient stone pillars that rise from depths no modern equipment has been able to plumb. The water here is a deep amber-gold, tinged with russet where the rare, filtered light manages to penetrate from far above.

The structures themselves are the second wonder. Every surface, every wall and corridor, every domed ceiling and arched doorway is manufactured—yet not in ways that suggest modern industry. The materials appear almost grown rather than built: pearlescent surfaces of what might be ceramic or some biological composite, swirled with patterns that catch what little light exists and amplify it through the murky waters. Pipes and conduits wind everywhere, some as thick as a person's body, others delicate as capillaries, all pumping heated water, nutrients, and energy throughout the vast complex.

Vegetation thrives in this impossible place. Not the photosynthesizing plants of the surface world, but specialized hydrothermal flora that metabolizes minerals from the water. Forests of tubular, crimson structures reach upward in the larger chambers, their surfaces rippling with cilia that create subtle currents. Bulbous growths the size of houses cluster in gardens, emitting occasional pulses of bioluminescence—brief, dim flashes that serve as the primary illumination within many sectors. Between these larger specimens grow countless varieties of what appear to be soft corals, fungal analogues, and crystalline formations that might be alive or might be mineral deposits—the distinction seems meaningless here.

The constant sound is the subtle percussion of machinery—ancient pumping systems that regulate temperature and filter the mineral-rich waters, preventing them from becoming too dense for habitation. The devices appear to have been running continuously for millennia, built to outlast civilizations. They operate with minimal noise—just the faint rhythmic pulse that becomes the heartbeat of Therma Hydrus, so omnipresent that you only notice when it momentarily falters.

The cities exist in a perpetual state of erosion and renewal. The hot water corrodes even the most resilient materials over time, yet the engineered ecology of the place seems designed to respond, secreting new layers, healing breaches, rebuilding what breaks down. This process happens at a glacial pace—a wall might take a century to regenerate—but the entire system appears to have reached a kind of homeostasis, a sustainable balance of decay and reconstruction.

No animals inhabit this realm. The density of the water, the pressure, the mineral content—all conspire to make it inhospitable to complex fauna. Instead, the engineers of this place—whoever they were—created mechanical analogues that still function: maintenance drones shaped like cephalopods drift through chambers, tending to the infrastructure with manipulator arms. Transport pods shaped like enormous nautiluses move between sectors, carrying supplies and occasionally human visitors.

Humans are not absent, but they are sparse—researchers, caretakers, and the descendants of those who chose to adapt to life in the suspended cities. Their settlements occupy only a small fraction of the sprawling complex. Most of Therma Hydrus remains unexplored, its purpose uncertain, its original inhabitants long vanished. Did they build this place as a refuge from some ancient catastrophe? Was it an experiment in creating a completely artificial environment? Or perhaps it was always meant as a preserve, a living museum of technologies and biological engineering too advanced to be understood by those who now occupy its fringes.

Movement here is deliberate. The density of the water requires purpose in every gesture. The cities do not hurry; they have existed for eons and will likely persist long after current civilizations have passed. Time in Therma Hydrus feels stretched, elastic—a day might seem a week, a year might pass with the weight of a century. The place demands patience, adaptation to its rhythms rather than imposition of one's own.

In the deepest accessible chambers, massive engines of unknown design continue to draw heat from the planet's core, maintaining the precise temperature that keeps the ecosystem functioning. Their operation is so fundamental to the place that they have become objects of reverence to the human inhabitants—not quite worship, but a deep respect for machinery so perfect in its design that it operates without fail across millennia.

Therma Hydrus is not a place one visits casually. It is a commitment, an immersion in the most literal sense. Those who come either leave quickly, overwhelmed by the alien nature of existence here, or they stay, slowly adapting until the surface world becomes the alien place—too bright, too fast, too chaotic compared to the ancient, ordered perfection of the suspended cities.
