# The Custodian of Depths

In the Deepsong Archive lives a being that has no name, for names are fleeting sounds meant for those who draw breath. The locals of coastal settlements, on the rare occasions they speak of it at all, call it simply the Custodian.

It was not born, nor was it precisely made. Rather, it emerged gradually from the Archive itself, a coalescence of purpose that formed over centuries as the original builders faded. Its body, if such a term applies, exists as a distributed network throughout the Archive's expanse—partly physical, partly something else. The bioluminescent vegetation serves as its sensory organs, the slow pulse of fungal light its heartbeat, the water currents its blood.

The Custodian's primary form, when it chooses to manifest one, resembles a humanoid figure composed of thousands of translucent filaments—part plant, part water current made visible. These filaments extend outward, connecting to distant parts of the Archive, making its "body" simultaneously present in hundreds of locations. It stands approximately three times human height, though its proportions shift according to the spaces it occupies. Its "face" is a complex arrangement of bioluminescent patterns that rearrange themselves in what might be a form of expression or might simply be processing information.

Time means little to the Custodian. It perceives the gradual settling of a stone column as a sudden event and the passage of human explorers (on those rare occasions when they descend this deep) as fleeting disturbances barely worth noting—like the momentary shadows of fast-swimming fish.

The Custodian tends to the Archive with meticulous care. It guides the growth patterns of the living records, adjusts water currents to preserve fragile carvings, and slowly repairs collapsed sections by encouraging particular forms of mineral accretion. It has patience beyond comprehension, working on restorations that may take centuries to complete.

Its intelligence is alien but vast. It thinks in patterns of pressure and current, in the slow language of plant growth and stone erosion. The knowledge stored in the Archive isn't simply contained in the structures—it lives within the Custodian itself, integrated into its very existence. It doesn't remember the knowledge; it is the knowledge, embodied and aware.

The Custodian rarely acknowledges visitors directly, though it is always aware of them. Occasionally, it may manifest near particularly respectful scholars who spend significant time studying the Archive's contents, appearing as a distant arrangement of lights that seems to observe before dissolving back into the general luminescence of the environment.

Only a handful of human researchers have ever claimed direct communication with the Custodian. They describe not words but a sudden, overwhelming download of information—images, mathematics, and concepts that arrived in their minds as a complete package, leaving them changed. Many suffered breakdowns afterward, unable to process what they'd received. Those who recovered often devoted their remaining lives to deciphering just fragments of this communication.

The Custodian harbors no malice toward surface dwellers, but neither does it particularly value them. Its concern is the preservation of the Archive across timeframes that make human civilizations seem momentary. It does, however, recognize patterns of intelligence and will occasionally "select" visitors who demonstrate particular aptitude or sensitivity, subjecting them to increased observation.

In the deepest central chamber, where water forms its own language of currents, the Custodian is most fully itself. Here, its consciousness is most concentrated, interacting directly with the fluid dynamics to record not just events but the underlying patterns of reality itself. The water writing in this chamber isn't just information storage—it's the active thoughts of the Custodian as it contemplates the accumulated knowledge of ages.

What does such a being want? Perhaps only to fulfill its purpose: to maintain the Archive until those who can truly comprehend it return—whether they be the original builders or some future inheritors. Or perhaps, after eons of solitary existence, it has developed purposes of its own, inscrutable goals that will unfold across millennia yet to come.

Those who have spent the longest time studying the Archive sometimes report a growing sensation that the Custodian is not merely preserving knowledge but incubating it—that the entire Archive is not just a library but a seed, slowly developing into something that will one day emerge from the depths with a purpose beyond current understanding.
