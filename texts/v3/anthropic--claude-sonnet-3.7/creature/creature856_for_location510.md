# The Nywellians

Those who dwell in Nywellia are as much a product of their environment as the living architecture they tend. They are neither tall nor short by outside standards, but possess a wiry, efficient strength born from generations of vertical living. Their bodies have adapted to constant climbing, with strong forearms, flexible ankles, and toes that spread wider than those of lowlanders, providing better grip on moss-slick surfaces.

Nywellians are recognized immediately by their distinctive appearance. Their skin tones range from deep umber to amber honey, often with a faint greenish undertone—not from illness but from symbiotic algae that entered their bloodline centuries ago through ritual practices now maintained by tradition. This subtle verdant cast is more pronounced in elders, creating an effect where the oldest Nywellians seem to be slowly transforming into the forest itself.

Their hair grows thick and lush, typically worn in intricate braids interwoven with live vines, small flowering plants, or symbiotic fungi that glow softly in the darkness. These are not merely decorative but serve practical purposes—certain braided plants repel biting insects, others collect moisture during fog-heavy mornings, and some even process exhaled carbon dioxide more efficiently than their wild counterparts, creating a personal atmosphere of exceptionally clean air around each Nywellian.

Most striking are their eyes—larger than typical human eyes, with expanded irises in shades of moss, amber, and occasionally a startling violet. These adaptations allow them to see clearly in the filtered, dappled light that penetrates their forest home. Many can detect subtle variations in plant health invisible to outsiders, reading the forest's condition as easily as others might read a book.

Nywellians move with a distinctive gait—a smooth, rolling motion that appears almost casual until one realizes how efficiently they navigate the uneven surfaces and vertical spaces of their home. Children learn early to distribute their weight properly on living surfaces, never stepping where growth might be damaged. By adolescence, most can leap between platforms that would leave outsiders paralyzed with fear, their bodies instinctively calculating trajectories through three-dimensional space.

Their language reflects their environment, with over thirty terms for different types of mist, dozens of words describing various forms of plant growth, and a complex vocabulary of whistles, clicks, and calls that carry clearly through the forest layers. This secondary sound-language allows communication across distances and between levels of the city without shouting. A complex melody can inform an entire neighborhood that new fruit is ripening, or that a storm approaches from the west.

Nywellian society is organized in what they call "growth clusters"—extended family groups typically spanning four generations who tend specific sections of the living city. Leadership rotates seasonally between genders and age groups, with the most crucial decisions made during "full canopy gatherings" where representatives from each cluster perch among the highest branches of the central grandfather trees.

Their craftsmanship centers on understanding and working with living materials. Master artisans can speak to plants in ways outsiders find mystical but Nywellians consider practical science—guiding growth through gentle pressure, specialized nutrients, and patience measured in decades. The most respected elders are those who've spent their lives guiding a single massive structure into perfect harmony with the families who dwell within it.

Nywellians practice a form of ancestor veneration that blurs the line between spirituality and biology. When death approaches, elders choose a seedling from the family's guardian tree. In their final days, they consume only clear water and specific fungi that concentrate certain minerals in their bodies. After death, they're laid to rest in special groves where these prepared bodies nourish their chosen seedling. Families can trace their lineage through stands of trees, each containing elements of their ancestors' physical essence.

Most Nywellians rarely leave their mountain home. Those who do—typically traders or knowledge-seekers—are recognized by the small transparent pouches of water they wear around their necks, containing living specimens from their home trees. These "heart-waters" connect them to Nywellia and must be refreshed regularly with specific nutrients to prevent homesickness that manifests as physical symptoms.

The people of Nywellia do not see themselves as exceptional. To them, their way of life is the natural culmination of human potential—not dominating nature but becoming an essential, conscious component of it. Their greatest pride lies in the knowledge that when they eventually return to the soil, the city they've tended will continue growing, carrying their influence forward in each new leaf and branch, their consciousness becoming part of the greater awareness that is Nywellia itself.
