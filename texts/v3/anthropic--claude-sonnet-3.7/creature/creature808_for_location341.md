# The Gardeners of Eshalar

The people of Eshalar are as carefully cultivated as their gardens. Tall and lithe, with limbs that seem slightly elongated, they move with a deliberate economy of motion that borders on ritualistic. Their skin carries a distinctive blue-green undertone, particularly visible around the fingernails, earlobes, and the delicate webbing between their long fingers—an adaptation some believe comes from centuries of contact with the mineral-rich waters that flow through their city.

Eshalari features are striking—high cheekbones, wide-set eyes that often display unusual amber or violet hues, and hair that grows in thick, straight locks ranging from deep black to an unusual silver-white, even among the young. They wear this hair either very long and intricately braided with thin copper wire or cut precisely at the jawline, depending on their gardening specialty. Most distinctive are the fine patterns of scarification that adorn their forearms and hands—geometric designs that record their lineage, gardening discipline, and personal achievements in cultivation.

They speak in measured tones, their native language rich with specialized terminology for water conditions, plant states, and cultivation techniques. Many Eshalari can communicate entirely through subtle hand gestures when working in the gardens, a silent language evolved to prevent disturbing what they call the "listening plants." Their voices carry a slight sibilance, words flowing together like water over stones.

The society follows a structure they call the "Watershed Harmony"—neither hierarchical nor entirely egalitarian, but organized around specialized knowledge of particular plant families and water management techniques. Leadership rotates seasonally among the seven principal Cultivation Houses, each responsible for different aspects of Eshalar's integrated garden ecosystem. Decisions are made through a process they call "root consensus," which can take considerable time but results in remarkably stable outcomes.

Eshalari children begin specialized training by age five, apprenticed to master gardeners who teach them not just techniques but an entire philosophy of relationship with plants. By adolescence, their hands have developed calluses in patterns specific to their gardening discipline, which they display with quiet pride. Coming of age involves a year-long cultivation project where each young person develops a new plant variant or garden structure technique.

Their clothing is practical yet distinctive—layered garments of plant-derived textiles in subdued greens, blues, and earth tones that can be adjusted for the temperature variations between morning mist and midday sun. Pockets and specialized loops accommodate the tools each carries. Most wear wide-brimmed hats woven from a reed-like plant unique to Eshalar, which they can quickly adjust to serve as a rain shield or sun protection.

What visitors find most unsettling about the Eshalari is their relationship with their plants. They don't merely cultivate them—they communicate with them through a system they call "deep tending." Each gardener spends hours in meditative states beside their assigned plots, hands lightly touching the soil, seemingly listening. They claim the plants speak not in words but in "growth intentions" that can be perceived through dedicated practice.

Indeed, the plants of Eshalar seem unusually responsive to their caretakers. Vines will often change direction overnight to follow the trellising patterns a gardener has only begun to construct. Certain tubers develop growth patterns that make them easier to harvest. The Eshalari describe this not as domestication but as "collaborative evolution"—a mutual shaping between human and plant consciousness.

Most remarkable is the Conclave of Elder Gardeners, individuals who have devoted over fifty years to the cultivation of a single plant species. These elders gradually take on characteristics reminiscent of their plant charges—those who tend the jointed reed plants develop unusually flexible limbs, while those cultivating protein-rich seed pods often develop a subtle rattle in their speech. They rarely leave their garden terraces, and in their final years, they request to be "planted"—a ceremony where they are seated in specially prepared soil beds where they remain, barely moving, sometimes for months, communing constantly with their plants until death.

The Eshalari practice no religion as outsiders would recognize it, yet their entire culture revolves around what might be called reverence. They believe water carries memory, plants possess intention, and that human consciousness serves as a bridge between the two. Their calendar tracks not just seasons but subtle changes in water mineral content, naming each twenty-day period after the dominant taste profile in the springs.

They have no word for "wilderness"—to them, all growth is part of a continuum of relationship between tenders and tended. Their myths speak of a time when humans were themselves tended by greater gardeners who have temporarily withdrawn but left humans as caretakers in their absence. Many Eshalari believe their careful cultivation is preparing for these greater gardeners' return, and that the patterns of their city, visible only from high above, form messages legible to these ancient beings.

Visitors to Eshalar are welcomed but closely observed. The Eshalari share their abundant harvests generously but remain protective of their cultivation techniques. They treat outsiders with polite curiosity, often offering gifts of preserved foods and medicinal preparations, but they rarely form close bonds with them. As one Elder Gardener explained to a persistent visitor: "You move too quickly to hear what the plants are becoming. When you learn to listen with your fingertips, perhaps then we can truly speak."
