# The Tenders of the Dome

They call themselves Keepers, though no official title has ever been bestowed. Five individuals, each uniquely adapted to life within the shuttered terrarium, maintain a delicate existence that oscillates between stewardship and symbiosis.

The eldest is Verna, a woman in her seventies whose skin has taken on the texture of weathered parchment. She was born in the town but moved into the dome permanently forty years ago after her husband's death. Her fingers are perpetually stained green, nails trimmed short except for her right index finger, which she's allowed to grow long and has carefully shaped into a grafting tool. Her eyes, once brown, have developed a curious chlorophyll-tinted ring around the iris—a medical anomaly documented by visiting researchers but never explained.

Verna sleeps in a small alcove built into the eastern curve of the dome, where the morning light first enters through a deliberately left-open panel. She rises with the sun and begins each day by tasting the condensation that forms on the brass gauges—her tongue having developed an extraordinary sensitivity to pH and mineral content. Without instruments, she can detect imbalances in the ecosystem that would take machines hours to identify.

Paolo is the youngest at twenty-four, the first generation born within the dome itself. His mother was a botanist who came to study the unusual plant adaptations and never left. His physiology shows the most dramatic adaptations to dome life—his lungs have developed unusual efficiency in the humid, spore-rich air, and his hearing is attuned to frequencies most humans cannot perceive. He can detect the ultrasonic emissions of plant cell walls stretching during growth spurts. Paolo rarely speaks above a whisper, communicating instead through an elaborate sign language he shares with the other Keepers.

The twins, Eliza and Emmett, arrived fifteen years ago as university students researching sustainable closed ecosystems. They intended to stay for a semester but found themselves unable to leave. Now thirty-six, they finish each other's sentences and move with synchronized precision through the narrow paths. Their specialty is maintaining the mechanical systems, having developed a sixth sense for which valves need adjustment before any gauge shows change. Their bodies have grown unusually thin and flexible, able to contort into the narrow spaces between overgrown pipes and control panels. Both have developed a peculiar circadian rhythm that aligns not with the sun but with the dome's internal moisture cycles.

The fifth Keeper is known only as Moss. Their age and origin are unclear—town records suggest they arrived around thirty years ago, though they appear neither young nor old. Moss speaks rarely and possesses extraordinary patience, sometimes remaining in one position for days, observing a particular plant's growth or the behavior of the dome's insect colonies. Their skin bears patches of what appears to be lichen—not a disease but a symbiotic relationship that medical doctors from the outside world have been unable to classify. These patches photosynthesize, providing Moss with supplemental nutrition during periods when they choose not to eat conventional food.

The Keepers consume primarily what grows within the dome—specialized fungi with unusually high protein content, select leaves prepared according to traditions Verna maintains, and occasional supplements brought by respectful townsfolk. Their diet has altered their biochemistry; their blood carries elevated levels of plant hormones and their sweat contains compounds that certain dome plants react to positively.

Each Keeper maintains their own area of specialization. Verna cultivates the medicinal plants that townspeople depend on. Paolo communicates with the semi-mobile plant species through a combination of gentle touch and the vibrations he creates by humming specific tones. The twins maintain the water systems and study the merger of plant and machine. Moss tends to the insects and smaller fauna, ensuring pollination continues and the food web remains intact.

They sleep in shifts, ensuring the dome is never without conscious attention. Their dreams are communal in a way science cannot explain—each often waking with knowledge of what the others have discovered during their rest periods. They have developed a calendar not based on months but on the blooming cycles of key indicator plants within the dome.

The Keepers are neither cult nor scientists, though they incorporate elements of both spiritual reverence and empirical observation. They maintain meticulous journals, recording every minute change in the ecosystem, creating a living archive that spans generations. These records are kept in waterproof cases made from a resin derived from dome plants, the pages themselves made from pressed fibers grown specifically for this purpose.

Visitors describe an uncanny sensation upon meeting the Keepers—not fear, but a recognition that they are encountering humans who have become something not entirely human, yet not alien either. They seem to process information differently, often responding to questions before they're fully articulated. Their movements among the plants are so fluid that they appear to pass through dense growth without disturbing a single leaf.

Their relationship with the town is complex. They are respected, consulted on matters of health and agriculture, but also regarded with a gentle wariness. Children are both fascinated and slightly frightened by them. Teenagers occasionally attempt to spend a night in the dome as a rite of passage, but few last until morning, unsettled by the breathing quality of the darkness and the sensation of being observed by intelligence that resides not in bodies but in the collective awareness of the ecosystem.

The Keepers themselves believe they are merely temporary components in the dome's longer existence. They speak of the structure not as a human creation but as an entity that called itself into being, using human hands merely as tools. In their understanding, the terrarium is engaged in a slow experiment, testing the boundaries between different forms of life and intelligence.

Most telling is how they refer to themselves in their private conversations—not as caretakers or even inhabitants, but as necessary synapses in a thinking system far larger and more patient than human comprehension can grasp.
