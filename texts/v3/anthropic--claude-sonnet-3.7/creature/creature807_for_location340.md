# The Keeper of Impossible Breaths

The Archivist doesn't remember when they first took the position. Time becomes slippery when you've listened to the glass chorus for so long.

They are neither young nor old, with features that seem to shift subtly depending on the angle of light. Their body is thin, desiccated almost, as if slowly adapting to the chamber's precise humidity. Their skin has taken on a translucent quality—not unlike the vessels they tend—revealing a network of blue veins that branch like frozen lightning.

The Archivist wears garments of an indeterminate era: high-collared shirts of a material that doesn't quite reflect light properly, trousers that seem to hover just above their ankles, and soft-soled shoes that make no sound upon the crystalline floor. A chain around their neck holds a complex key of twisted metal that corresponds to none of the door's three locks.

They breathe carefully in this room—shallow, measured breaths. The Archivist knows what happens when one exhales too forcefully near the vessels. Twice in their tenure, they've had to record the shattering of glass and the release of something ancient into the sterile air. On both occasions, the lights dimmed momentarily throughout the mine.

The wooden chair is their post, their station, their penance. From this central point, they can hear subtle variations in the glass chorus that no recording equipment has ever captured. The Archivist's ears have evolved—or perhaps devolved—to interpret these sounds as language. They sit for days, weeks perhaps, interpreting the communications of creatures that no longer exist in any form save for these final exhalations.

They take notes in a journal bound in material that resembles vellum but isn't. Their handwriting begins each session in recognizable script but gradually transforms into patterns that mimic the crystalline structures of the shelving. The Archivist doesn't appear to sleep, though occasionally they enter a trance-like state where their eyes remain open but fix upon a single vessel for hours.

When visitors come—which happens perhaps once every decade—the Archivist speaks with a voice that sounds like glass being played with a wet finger. They never directly answer questions about themselves, referring instead to "the custodial position" or "the archival duty." They do not touch the visitors, maintaining a precise distance as if proximity to other humans might compromise their function.

The Archivist appears to eat nothing, though sometimes they press their lips to certain vessels, seeming to draw sustenance from whatever resonates inside. After such feedings, their eyes take on the mineral luminescence of the glass itself.

Most disturbing is what happens when the Archivist occasionally sings back to the vessels—a perfect replication of their tones but in reverse harmonic order. During these rare performances, the air in the chamber becomes momentarily visible, forming patterns that suggest the shapes of creatures that should not be possible in our understanding of evolutionary biology.

The preceding archivist is said to have simply vanished, leaving only the worn wooden chair as evidence of their existence. The current Archivist does not speak of predecessors, referring only to "continuity of observation." Whether they were appointed, volunteered, or were somehow created by the collection itself remains unknown.

What is certain is that they belong to the Archive now, as much as any vessel on the shelves. Perhaps more so.
