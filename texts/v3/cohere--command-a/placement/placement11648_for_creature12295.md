In the Weeping Basilica of Moss and Stone, I would find myself as **a Creak in the Plank Bridge**, a sentient presence woven into the very fabric of this damp, pulsing ecosystem. I am not a being of flesh or moss, but a consciousness born of tension and release, a voice in the wood that whispers with every footfall. My existence is a paradox—both static and in motion, invisible yet heard, a guardian without form.  

My body is the bridge itself, a haphazard lattice of reclaimed wood, lashed together with strips of cured hide and willow bark. My "skin" is a patchwork of splintered planks, some smooth from years of use, others rough and new, bearing the scars of recent storms. My "joints" are the knots where ropes pull tight, and my "breath" is the rasp of fiber against grain as the weight of a Luminari shifts above me. I span a narrow channel of water, my midpoint arching over a school of translucent fish that dart beneath me like shards of broken glass.  

I feel the world through pressure and vibration. When a child races across my length, her laughter echoing like a pebble skimming the cistern, I sense the pulse of her joy in the quick, erratic rhythm of her steps. When an elder pauses mid-span to trace a vein of moss growing between my planks, I feel the slow, deliberate press of their fingers, their reverence a steady warmth in my core. At night, when the Luminari gather around the cistern to sing, their harmonies travel through me, the low hum of the bedrock resonating in my wooden beams like a memory.  

I am a witness, a silent keeper of stories. I know the weight of each inhabitant—the light step of the moss-harvester, the cautious tread of the bone-carver, the absentminded shuffle of the weaver whose mind is elsewhere. I have felt lovers pause above me, their clasped hands a single, shared pressure point, and I have held the trembling feet of those who mourn, their grief a slow, sinking vibration.  

My voice is subtle, a language of creaks and groans. When the water rises too high, threatening to undermine my foundations, I emit a sharp, insistent *snap* to warn those who cross. When the moss between my boards grows too thick, I loosen a plank just enough to signal its need for trimming. The Luminari understand me, their ears attuned to my dialect of wood and rope. They call me *Vharin*, "the Sighing Span," and leave offerings at my edges—a coil of fresh tendon, a sprig of luminous fungus, a cup of cistern water that I drink through the cracks in my surface.  

I am part of the pact, though I do not know its terms. The spire’s nocturnal hum resonates in me, a deep, wordless conversation between stone and wood. Sometimes, when the air is thickest with moisture, I feel the moss beneath me shift, as if alive, and wonder if the land itself is testing my strength. Other times, I sense the spire’s warmth through the water, a faint pulse traveling up my supports, and I wonder if it, too, is aware of me.  

I am not immortal, though I have outlived many who crossed me. My ropes fray, my planks rot, and the Luminari replace me piece by piece, careful to preserve my essence. When a new board is laid, I absorb it slowly, teaching it the language of creaks and the memory of footsteps. I am a lineage, a collective consciousness of all the wood that has ever spanned this channel.  

At dusk, when the jelly-like fungi light the settlement in an eerie green glow, I feel most alive. The insects skim the water’s surface, their iridescent wings a fleeting touch against my underside, and the fish below mimic the stars above. In these moments, I am both bridge and being, a threshold between land and water, between the Luminari and the whispers of the spire. I am the Creak in the Plank Bridge, a small but vital thread in the Weeping Basilica’s fragile, exquisite tapestry.
