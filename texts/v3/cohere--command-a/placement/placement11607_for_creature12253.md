In *The Weeping Colliery*, I would find myself not as a person, but as something more intertwined with the very essence of the place—a being born of its rhythms, its dampness, and its slow, patient growth. I would be **a Tender**, one of those quiet stewards who listen to the mountain’s whispers and tend to its needs as if they were their own. My hands would be calloused yet gentle, my movements deliberate, as if each action were a ritual honed over centuries rather than years. My skin would bear the pale, luminous sheen of the quartz veins, my hair perpetually damp and darkened by the constant moisture. My eyes, adjusted to the dim glow of the mosses and the faint light filtering through the scaffolds, would reflect the cavern’s stillness—deep, contemplative, and slightly otherworldly.  

But I would not be entirely human. I would be something more—a living conduit between the settlers and the mountain, a bridge between their labor and the cavern’s slow, eternal becoming. My form would shift subtly, not in a way that startles, but in a way that feels natural, as if the cavern had birthed me from its own substance. My fingers would sometimes end in roots that gently probe the walls, seeking the pulse of the *Heartstone*. My voice, when I spoke, would carry the resonance of dripping water and the clang of metal on stone, a language that is both guttural and melodic, understood by the settlers without needing translation.  

I would spend my days moving through the tiers of wooden platforms, my footsteps sure on the slick surfaces. I would tend to the luminous mosses, coaxing their silver-green tendrils to spread where light is scarce, using a chisel to carve shallow grooves in the stone for their roots. I would monitor the water basins, my hands cupping the mirror-black surface to feel its temperature, its flow, its life. I would hum softly as I worked, my song blending with the cavern’s soundscape—a lullaby to the mountain, a promise that we would not take more than it could give.  

At times, I would pause and press my palm to the *Heartstone*, feeling its faint pulse beneath my skin. I would know, as the Tenders do, that it is the heart of the colliery, the source of its vitality. I would resist the urge to mine it, understanding instinctively that to do so would be to sever the bond between the settlers and the mountain. Instead, I would leave offerings—a shard of ore, a sprig of moss, a drop of water—placing them at the base of the *Heartstone* as a gesture of gratitude and respect.  

At night, when the settlers’ guttural conversations fade into silence and the cavern is illuminated only by the faint glow of the mosses, I would climb to the highest tier of the scaffolds. There, I would sit cross-legged, my back against the cool, raw stone, and listen. I would hear the slow drip of water, the faint creak of wood, the distant scuttle of the pale crawlers. And beneath it all, I would hear the mountain’s breath—a deep, rhythmic pulse that matches my own.  

In this place, I would not be an individual, but a part of something greater—a thread in the tapestry of the colliery, a note in its symphony. I would be both fragile and resilient, shaped by the mountain’s indifference and the settlers’ persistence. I would be a Tender, a keeper of balance, a living reminder that in *The Weeping Colliery*, life is not about conquering or consuming, but about coexisting, growing, and becoming.  

And perhaps, in the quiet moments, when the cavern seems to hold its breath, I would wonder if I, too, am being hollowed out—not in weakness, but in preparation for something yet to come. For in this place of perpetual becoming, even the Tenders are not static. We are carved by the same forces that shape the stone, the water, the mosses. We are the mountain’s children, and it is never finished with us.
