In Cisternis, I find myself as a **Waterwarden**, one of the silent sentinels who straddle the liminal space between the city's industrial heartbeat and the marsh's ancient pulse. I am neither fully human nor entirely of the water, but a hybrid being born of necessity and adaptation. My body is sleek and amphibious, with skin that shifts from pale, moss-veined translucence to a darker, rust-flecked hue depending on the light and the depth of the water I inhabit. My limbs are long and sinewy, designed for both the fluid motion of swimming through submerged channels and the precise grip needed to scale the city's damp, algae-slicked piers.

My home is the **Underframe**, the labyrinthine network of tunnels, sluices, and ancient stone arches that lie beneath Cisternis’s foundations. Here, the air is perpetually heavy with the scent of damp earth and oil, and the walls weep with condensation that tastes faintly metallic. I move through this submerged world with ease, my webbed fingers brushing against the rough stone as I inspect the integrity of the latticework that holds the city above. My presence is a whisper in the currents, a ripple that signals to others of my kind that the balance is being kept—for now.

My role is to maintain the delicate equilibrium between the city’s industrial thirst and the marsh’s finite resources. I monitor the pumps, the sluices, and the hidden reservoirs, ensuring that the water levels remain steady and that the machinery does not overstep its bounds. I am part engineer, part priest, offering silent prayers to the water as I tighten a seal or clear a blockage. My tools are simple yet indispensible: a wrench with a handle carved from marshwood, a knife with a blade that never rusts, and a net woven from fibers that glow faintly in the dim light, revealing the presence of invasive organisms that could destabilize the ecosystem.

I am rarely seen by the Cisternians above, though they know of my kind through whispered tales and the occasional glimpse of movement in the water. They call us the **Marshkeepers**, a name that carries both reverence and fear. They leave offerings at the edges of the marsh—small figurines carved from blackened stone, rusted gears, or vials of oil—to ensure our favor. In return, we keep their city from sinking, though the line between our protection and the marsh’s indifference is thin and often blurred.

My senses are attuned to the rhythms of the Underframe. I hear the distant hum of the city’s machinery as a vibration through the water, the drip of condensation as a steady drumbeat, and the shifting currents as a language only I can understand. My eyes, large and lidless, see in the spectrum of shadows and glow, allowing me to navigate the darkest corners of the tunnels. My breath is slow and measured, adapted to the waterlogged air, and my heart beats in time with the marsh’s own pulse.

I am not alone. Others like me exist, scattered throughout the Underframe, each with their own territory and responsibilities. We communicate through a series of clicks, hums, and subtle movements of the water, a language as fluid and complex as the marsh itself. We gather rarely, but when we do, it is in the deepest part of the Underframe, where the water is still and the air is thick with the scent of ancient stone. Here, we share knowledge, repair shared tools, and plan for the inevitability of the marsh’s next shift.

My existence is a testament to the city’s resilience and the marsh’s relentless reclamation. I am both a product of Cisternis’s ingenuity and a reminder of its fragility. I walk—or swim—the line between progress and decay, between the urban sprawl above and the submerged wilderness below. In my hands, I hold the tools of survival; in my heart, I carry the weight of a city’s fate. And in the quiet moments, as I float in the darkness of the Underframe, I wonder if the marsh will one day reclaim us all, or if we will find a way to endure, one cycle at a time.
