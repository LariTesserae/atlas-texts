The air hangs heavy with moisture, condensing into droplets that cling to every surface before sliding back into the perpetual dampness. You stand at the edge of *The Weeping Colliery*, a cathedral-like cavern carved into the flank of a high, cool mountain. Its walls are raw, almost embryonic—veins of quartz and unshaped stone gleam faintly under the normal light filtering through a latticework of wooden beams and moss-crusted scaffolds. The cavern is vast yet confined, its scale echoing the hollowness of a grove stripped of its fullness, save for the rhythmic clang of primitive tools against stone.

This is a place under construction, not by nature’s hand alone but by the labor of a sparse, urban settlement clustered near the cavern’s mouth. Their tech is simple—iron-tipped hammers, chisels, and rope woven from mountain flax—yet their ambition is layered, like the half-built tiers of wooden platforms ascending the cavern walls. Water seeps constantly, weeping from cracks in the ceiling and pooling in shallow, mirror-black basins at the base. The atmosphere is cool, sharpened by the scent of wet stone and the faint tang of rust.

Vegetation clings where it can: luminous mosses in shades of silver and green, their tendrils dripping with condensation; ferns unfurling from crevices, their fronds trembling under the draft of slow-moving air. The flora is lush but contained, as if the mountain itself resists being overrun. Fauna is rare—only the occasional scuttle of a pale, eyeless crawler or the distant echo of a bird’s cry from the unseen surface.

The soundscape is loud yet paradoxically static, dominated by the steady ring of metal on stone, the slosh of water, and the creak of wooden supports groaning under their own weight. Conversations in a guttural, rhythmic tongue rise and fall like the ebb of a distant tide, their words lost to the cavern’s immensity. Here, change is gradual, patient—the slow accumulation of mined ore, the incremental widening of the cavern, the imperceptible deepening of water channels carved into the floor.

The rules of this world are unspoken but felt: gravity seems lighter here, as if the mountain’s density has been hollowed out, leaving only its skeleton. Time moves in long cycles, marked by the filling and emptying of the water basins, the completion of a scaffold tier, the emergence of a new vein of ore. The weirdness lies in the tension between creation and emptiness, between the raw geology and the fragile human order imposed upon it. This is not a place of endings or beginnings, but of perpetual becoming—a settlement not built but *grown*, like a crystal forming in the dark.
