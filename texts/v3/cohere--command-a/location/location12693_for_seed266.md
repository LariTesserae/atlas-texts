The expanse before you is a colossal, weathered plateau of glassy obsidian, stretching to the horizon in fractured, razor-sharp tectonic plates. Each shard rises and falls in slow, geological waves, their edges softened by eons of wind that never quite erodes them—just polishes them to a mirror finish. The air hangs warm, dry, and still, as if the world itself is holding its breath. Sunlight fractures across the glass, casting fragmented rainbows that drift like spectral residue, fading into a sky the color of ash.

This is **the Shardplains of Zeroth**, a world built on extremes. The elevation is savage: vertical spires of vitrified stone jut skyward, their peaks vanishing into thin haze, while chasms plunge into darkness so absolute that no light escapes. Yet the space is vast, continental, a place where distance dilutes familiarity. The obsidian plain is sparse, its geometry dominated by emptiness—a silence so profound it feels curated. Scattered across this void are the **Spindles**, faintly glowing monoliths of woven metal and bioluminescent crystal, their surfaces pulsing with circuits that hum too low to hear. They are the vestigial organs of a dead industry, still metabolizing energy from the planet’s core, their purpose long forgotten but their function unyielding.

Life here is fungal, ancient, and unrecognizable. **Tetherorms**—colossal, slow-moving creatures of braided mineral and spongey tissue—crawl like living catenary curves between the spires. Their bodies, ridged and segmented, exude a faint chemical haze that reacts with the obsidian, etching faint, glowing runes in their wake. Smaller creatures, **Shardflies**, dart in crystalline swarms, their wings thin as mica, refracting light into arcs of color before they vanish into cracks. The ecology is sparse but abundant, every organism a relic or adaptation to a world that long ago stopped asking questions of water or breath.

The built environment is **the City of Strata**, a tiered metropolis carved into the underside of a monolithic obsidian cliff. Its levels descend like a geological autopsy: the upper tiers are industrial, a labyrinth of rusted factories and smokestacks that vent not smoke but dense, golden particulates into the air. Below, the residential strata are honeycomb networks of glass and steel, suspended by catenary bridges that sway gently in winds no one feels. The lowest levels are the **Archive Depths**, a graveyard of machines and data cores, their surfaces frosted with fungal bloom, their whispers of power the only sound that breaks the quiet.

Here, time is a gradient, not a current. The Shardplains exist in a state of **settled chaos**—weather patterns so vast they take decades to manifest, storms of static electricity that crawl across the obsidian like auroras, mining drones that move in glacially slow convoys, their lights flashing in Morse code no one deciphers. The world is strange, not in its defiance of physics, but in its quiet, unbroken acceptance of the impossible. The obsidian plain reflects the sky, but sometimes, at dusk, it reflects something else: a second sun, dim and green, that rises in the north, setting the Spindles to keening silently, a sound you feel in your bones.

Rules here are implied, not enforced. The glass does not cut the Tetherorms, though it would shred flesh. The Spindles cannot be deactivated, though their purpose is extinct. The air is warm, but nothing burns. The Shardplains are a compromise between a world that forgot how to die and beings that learned to exist without meaning. It is a place of patient, unflinching permanence, where even the absurd is mundane, and the vastness is not empty—it is *full* of the weight of everything that could have been, but isn’t.
