In the quiet cradle of a high valley, nestled between jagged peaks that pierce a sky perpetually heavy with moisture, lies **the Weirden**, a place where water and stone have carved an uneasy truce. The air is warm but damp, thick with the scent of wet earth and the faintest metallic tang of mineral-rich mist. The ground here is a patchwork of lush mosses and ferns, their greens vivid against the dark, freshly cleaved rock that rises in sharp angles and jagged spines. This is a young landscape, its features unsoftened by time, as if the mountains themselves have only recently exhaled the valley into existence.

Water is everywhere, but it moves with a strange restraint. Streams carve paths through the rock, their edges glinting with veins of quartz and mica, while weeping stones drip steadily from heights, their surfaces slick and black. The atmosphere condenses into beads of moisture that cling to leaves and hair, and the very air feels saturated, as though it could be wrung out like a cloth. Yet despite the abundance of water, there are no great rivers here, no vast lakes—only a network of shallow pools and marshy edges, where the earth seems to breathe in and out with the weight of the water pressing from below.

The Weirden is an open place, its scale expansive yet compartmentalized. To the east, the valley broadens into a series of terraced fields, crude but functional, where hardy root vegetables and moss-like grains are cultivated by the sparse population. To the west, the ground rises into a labyrinth of stone spires and natural arches, their surfaces etched with intricate patterns of lichen and mold. Between these extremes lies the **Sprawl**, a settlement of interlocking stone huts and wooden platforms built above the ever-shifting marshland. The architecture is primitive but ingenious, adapted to the wetness—roofs slope sharply to shed water, and elevated walkways connect clusters of dwellings, their surfaces grooved to prevent slipping.

The people of the Weirden are few, their lives dictated by the rhythms of the water. They are craftsmen of necessity, their tools simple but honed to precision. Stone chisels, bone knives, and wooden mallets are their trade, and their clothing is woven from fibrous plants, treated to repel moisture. They move quietly, their voices low and infrequent, as though afraid to disturb the stillness. Their only music is the occasional hollow knock of a mallet against stone or the soft hum of a spindle turning. Time here feels suspended, the landscape and its inhabitants locked in a state of frozen equilibrium. Nothing changes; the water drips, the moss creeps, and the people build and rebuild, their existence as spare and essential as the land itself.

There is no animal life in the Weirden, no movement save for the slow creep of vegetation and the relentless drip of water. The silence is profound, broken only by the occasional splash of a stone dropped into a pool or the distant rumble of a minor landslide. It is a place of quiet industry, where the absence of life beyond the human underscores the solitude of the landscape. The Weirden is a world unto itself, a place where the elements have conspired to create a fragile balance—wet yet warm, lush yet sparse, urban yet untouched. It is a place that feels both eternal and newly born, a landscape that has always been and has only just begun.
