**The Workshop Expanse of Ironmire**

The landscape is a vast, low-lying plain, its surface a mosaic of interlocking iron plates and hydrophobic ceramic tiles, each etched with the faint gloss of millennia. Water is omnipresent but tamed—thin rivulets of dark fluid (too dense to be purely liquid, too fluid to be oil) snake through seams between the plates, their paths dictated by an unseen logic. Every few kilometers, towering condensers loom like skeletal trees, their surfaces weeping a constant drizzle that gathers in basins below, where it vanishes into drains leading to depths unknown. The air is cool and heavy, carrying the tang of rust and the faint acridity of ozone, as if the atmosphere itself is a byproduct of labor.

Vegetation clings where it can, though it is sparse and twisted. Dark, waxy fronds sprout from cracks between plates, their leaves veined with silvery filaments that hum faintly when touched. Some growths resemble fungi more than plants, clustered around the bases of condensers, their stalks pulsing rhythmically in time with the machinery’s groan. These are the only living things here—fauna is absent, save for the occasional metallic scuttle of a maintenance drone, its glow-eyes scanning the gloom.

The terrain is ancient, not in the sense of decay, but of completion. It is a landscape that has settled into its final form, every edge blunted, every surface worn smooth by eons of function. Yet it is not stagnant—it is *active*. Gears grind beneath the plates, their motion felt as a low, constant vibration. Pistons thump in unseen chambers, and chimneys belch steam that condenses into rain before it reaches the ground. The processes are cyclical, relentless, though no clear purpose is visible beyond the motions themselves.

This is a made world, utterly and entirely. No natural substrate remains; even the water is engineered, its composition tuned to prevent corrosion of the infrastructure. The scale is immense—the horizon is a jagged line of silos and derricks, their shapes silhouetted against the perpetual twilight. Light is scarce, most of it artificial: the glow of furnaces, the flicker of arc lamps, the bioluminescent patches on the faux-plants. The darkness is not oppressive but *intentional*, as though the landscape is meant to be experienced through sound and vibration rather than sight.

And the sound. It is deafening, a symphony of industry without melody. The hum of generators, the rattle of conveyors, the hiss of escaping steam, the occasional shriek of strained metal. The air carries the din in layers, each frequency competing for dominance. Yet within the chaos, there is rhythm—a pulse that matches the flicker of the lights, the drip of the condensers, the beat of the machinery. It is a world that *feels* alive, even as it remains entirely inhuman.

The weirdness lies in the details. Why is this place here? Who built it, and for what purpose? The answers are not obvious, though hints exist: a series of monumental inscriptions, worn almost to illegibility, etched into the central spire. Their language is geometric, composed of interlocking triangles and circles, and they seem to repeat the same phrase: *"When the last drop falls, the first shall rise."* Whether this is a warning, a promise, or a ritual is unclear.

This is Ironmire, a place where water is controlled, vegetation is synthetic, and the earth itself is a machine. It is a landscape at the end of history, where the natural has been replaced by the functional, and the only life is the quiet persistence of process. It exists because it exists, and in its unyielding, rhythmic labor, it has become something more than the sum of its parts—a monument to the strange marriage of erosion and construction, darkness and industry, stillness and roar.
