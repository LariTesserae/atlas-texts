**The drowned cogworks of Lower Seven, a hidden thalassocracy of steam and silence.**

You find yourself in a cavern so vast it defies the mountain's modest exterior, a pearl of industry embedded in the spine of a young volcanic range. The air hangs cool and heavy, saturated with the breath of condensing steam, the tang of lubricating oils, and the faintest metallic bite of rust flowering in the dimness. Water defines this place—not as a passive element, but as a laborer, a fuel, a tyrant. It seeps through veins of weeping basalt, pools in the joints of colossal machinery, and cascades in shimmering sheets from overhead pipes, endlessly harvested and redirected by the groaning apparatus of a civilization built entirely on its siphoning.

The ground beneath your feet is not soil, not stone, but a mosaic of interlocking metal plates, riveted copper walkways, and enamel-glazed conduits. Every surface is crafted, every edge intentionally sharp. Glowing algae, bio-engineered for efficiency, cling to the damp underbellies of catwalks, casting an eerie green glow that competes with the flickering orangery of pressure gauges. The scale is both immense and intimate—miles of iron scaffolding enclose you like the ribs of a mechanical leviathan, yet the space feels claustrophobic, the air thick with the weight of confined rainclouds perpetually on the verge of bursting.

Sounds assault you from all directions, a symphony of industry turned up to a pitch that vibrates in your bones. Pistons hammer in hydraulic flanks, gears grind against gears in a language of strain, and the constant *whoosh* of steam venting through emergency valves provides a rhythmic bassline. Overhead, tramways clang and screech as cargo pods—slabs of raw ore, coils of brass tubing, crates stamped with alchemical sigils—are ferried through the mist by automata whose articulated limbs move with unsettling precision. The hum of dynamos and the distant clatter of punch-card looms weave through it all, a layered roar that renders coherent thought impossible.

And yet, amidst the chaos, there is order. The entire cavern operates as a single organism, a clockwork wetland where every drop of water is accounted for. Canals etched into the factory floor feed vast distillation arrays, their glass tentacles pulsing with violet liquid light. Pedestals of corroded bronze rise from the central machine-pools, each crowned with spinning turbine flowers that scatter droplets in hypnotic spirals, their petals carved with runes of efficiency and sacrifice. The ecology is entirely synthetic: no animals live here, only the semi-organic filters of the water purification vats, their pulsating membranes glistening like the gills of some metallic sea creature.

The rules of this place are written in pressure differentials and thermodynamic cycles. Gravity itself seems fluid, with walkways slanting at unnatural angles to accommodate the flow of material. The higher you climb—via spiral staircases that seem to emerge organically from the walls—the more the air thins, until you find yourself in galleries where the mist forms ephemeral sculptures, momentarily free of the machinery's pull. Here, the dim light catches on arrays of polished lenses, each focusing the diffuse glow into beams that carve through the haze like xenon searchlights, illuminating hidden inscriptions in a dead language.

In this damp forge of endless motion, time itself feels constructed. Clocks are everywhere, their hands synchronized to the pulse of the central furnace, yet their faces show no numbers, only arcane symbols ticking counterclockwise. The workers—if there were any—would have long ago merged with their tools, their breath tuned to the hiss of escaping steam, their hearts keeping time with the clamor. Now, only the machines remain, tireless and unblinking, their purpose as impenetrable as the mountain itself.
