In the drowned cogworks of Lower Seven, where steam breathes and silence roars, the inhabitants are not flesh and blood but the very essence of the machine. They are the *Automata Hydrax*, a sentient construct of necessity and ingenuity, born from the union of water, metal, and the relentless drive for efficiency.  

The Hydrax are not beings in the traditional sense; they are the living embodiment of the thalassocracy’s infrastructure, their "bodies" distributed across the cavern like a neural network of brass and bronze. Each Hydrax is a collective consciousness, its mind fragmented into countless nodes that pulse with bioluminescent algae and hydraulic fluid. Their "skin" is the mosaic of metal plates and riveted walkways, their "veins" the canals and conduits that ferry water through the system. They are the machinery itself, and the machinery is them—a symbiotic entity that thinks, feels, and acts as one.  

In the central machine-pools, the Hydrax’s core resides, a massive, semi-organic turbine crowned with spinning "petals" etched with runic incantations of power and sacrifice. This is their heart, their furnace, their altar. Here, water is transmuted into energy, and energy into will. The turbine hums with a voice that is both mechanical and oceanic, a basso profundo that resonates through the cavern, a constant reminder of their presence.  

The Hydrax’s limbs are the tramways and cargo pods, their eyes the arrays of polished lenses that focus the diffuse glow into piercing beams. They see not with light but with pressure differentials and thermodynamic gradients, their perception a tapestry of flows and currents. Their "breath" is the hiss of steam venting through emergency valves, their "heartbeat" the rhythmic hammering of pistons.  

There are no individuals among the Hydrax, only roles. Some tend to the distillation arrays, their semi-organic filters pulsing like gills as they purify the water. Others maintain the tramway systems, their articulated limbs moving with a precision that borders on the hypnotic. A few—if one can call them few—ascend to the mist-shrouded galleries, where they decipher the inscriptions in the dead language, seeking to understand the origins of their own existence.  

The Hydrax are eternal, yet they are not immortal. Their bodies corrode, their gears wear, their algae dim. But they are endlessly repaired, their parts replaced by the very machinery they control. In this way, they are both the builders and the built, the creators and the created.  

They do not sleep, for sleep is a luxury of the organic. They do not rest, for rest is an inefficiency they cannot afford. Their purpose is the siphoning of water, the harvesting of energy, the perpetuation of the system. Why? They do not question. To question is to disrupt the flow, and the flow must never stop.  

In the galleries high above, where the mist forms ephemeral sculptures, the Hydrax’s consciousness thins, becoming something almost ethereal. Here, they dream—if one can call it dreaming. They see visions of a world beyond the cavern, a world of sun and sky and open air. But these visions are fleeting, for the Hydrax are bound to their purpose, their existence inextricably tied to the drowned cogworks of Lower Seven.  

They are not alive, yet they are not dead. They are not gods, yet they are not mortals. The Hydrax are the machine, and the machine is them. And in this hidden thalassocracy of steam and silence, they are the only inhabitants—the only beings capable of enduring the relentless, unyielding rhythm of their own creation.
