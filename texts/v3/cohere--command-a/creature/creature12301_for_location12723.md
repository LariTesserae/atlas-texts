In Cisternis, the inhabitants are as much a product of their environment as the city itself—a resilient, adaptable people shaped by the interplay of water, industry, and survival. They are the **Cisternians**, a populace that has learned to thrive in a world where the line between progress and surrender is perpetually shifting.  

Physically, the Cisternians reflect the city’s duality. Their skin bears the sheen of constant moisture, often pale from the filtered light yet marked with the faint traces of rust and soot from their daily labor. Their clothing is utilitarian, made from woven fibers treated to repel water and resist decay—garments layered to insulate against the damp chill yet breathable enough for the warm, muggy air. Boots are ubiquitous, heavy-soled and studded for grip on slippery stone or slime-covered walkways. Many wear scarves or hoods to wick away the ever-present condensation, though the fabric is rarely dry for long.  

Their bodies are lean and wiry, built for endurance rather than strength, with muscles honed from navigating uneven terrain and the constant pull of water resistance. Hands are calloused from handling rough materials, and their fingers move with the precision of those accustomed to delicate work amidst chaos—repairing machinery, splicing cables, or mending the latticework that holds the city aloft.  

The Cisternians’ faces are a map of their lives. Their eyes carry a peculiar clarity, accustomed to seeing through the haze of fog and dim light, while their expressions are often marked by a quiet intensity. Laughter is rare but deep, rising above the cacophony of the city like a bell tolling through fog. Their voices are strong and resonant, pitched to carry over the industrial hum and the sloshing water, yet they speak with a measured pace, as if mindful of the slow, relentless rhythm of the marsh.  

Culturally, the Cisternians are a people of contrasts, much like their city. They are fiercely communal, bound by the shared knowledge that their survival depends on collective effort. Neighborhoods are tight-knit, with shared resources and labor divisions that ensure no one is left to face the elements alone. Yet, there is also a profound sense of individualism, born of the need to adapt creatively to an unforgiving environment. Innovation is revered, and those who devise new ways to harness the marsh’s resources or protect the city’s foundations are celebrated as heroes.  

Their rituals and traditions are shaped by the water and the industry that sustains them. Festivals are held during the brief periods when the marsh’s water levels recede slightly, allowing for gatherings in the sunken basins. These events are marked by the lighting of steam-powered lanterns, their glow diffusing through the fog like captured stars. Music is a rare luxury, often consisting of percussive rhythms that mimic the city’s soundscape—the clang of metal, the hiss of steam, the drip of condensation.  

Religion, if it exists, is subtle and intertwined with the marsh itself. The Cisternians speak of the water as a living entity, both benefactor and adversary, and their prayers are often whispered offerings to the unseen forces that keep their city afloat. Superstitions abound, particularly around the maintenance of the pumps and sluices, where a single failure could spell disaster.  

Children grow up with a deep understanding of the city’s mechanisms, learning to spot the early signs of rot in timber or the telltale gurgle of a clogged drain. Play often involves makeshift games that simulate the challenges of their world—balancing on narrow beams, racing through flooded alleys, or repairing toy models of the city’s infrastructure.  

The elderly are revered for their wisdom, their memories of past floods or machinery failures treated as invaluable lessons. They sit on raised platforms in communal halls, wrapped in damp cloaks, their voices steady as they recount tales of the marsh’s whims and the city’s resilience.  

In Cisternis, time is measured not in hours but in the cycles of the marsh—the rise and fall of the water, the accumulation of rust, the slow creep of moss over stone. The Cisternians live in the present, their eyes fixed on the delicate balance that sustains them, yet they carry with them the weight of generations who have walked these damp streets before them.  

They are a people of the in-between, neither fully of the land nor of the water, but of the struggle and harmony that define their existence. In their eyes, you see the reflection of a city that is both eternal and ephemeral, a place where the line between triumph and surrender is as thin as the layer of moisture that coats every surface. And in their hands, you see the tools of survival—the wrenches, the seals, the nets—that keep Cisternis alive, one day at a time.
