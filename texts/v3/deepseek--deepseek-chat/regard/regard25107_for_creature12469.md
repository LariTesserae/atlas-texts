The being who notices this place does not come as a storm or a flood, nor as a radiant figure parting the mist. It comes as a whisper—a shift in the way the water moves, a new ripple where there should be none.  

At first, the people do not notice. The marsh has always had its own will, its own quiet ways of shifting beneath them. But then, the fish grow more plentiful in the traps. The reeds yield straighter, stronger fibers for weaving. The standing water near the eastern edge of the settlement, which has always been brackish and sour, clears overnight, becoming sweet enough to drink.  

A child is the first to see it—a figure standing knee-deep in the marsh, still as a heron, watching. When she blinks, it is gone.  

The adults dismiss it as mist-play, but the signs continue. Tools left outside are found dry, despite the dew. A boat with a cracked hull is discovered patched, the seams sealed with a resin none of them recognize. One morning, a bundle of rare marsh-herbs, known to ease fever, is found laid at the threshold of a home where a man lies ill.  

The people do not speak of it directly. But they begin to leave small offerings in return—a braided cord of rushes, a carefully carved bone hook, a cup of the first catch of the day poured back into the water. They do not know what they are thanking, only that something has turned its attention toward them, and it is not unkind.  

Then, the dreams begin.  

They are not vivid, not loud. Just fragments—a sense of deep, slow movement beneath the marsh, a voice that is not a voice murmuring in the drip of water against stone. Some wake with the certainty that they have been given a name, though none can remember it upon waking. Others feel the press of knowledge they cannot articulate, like the memory of a path they have never walked.  

One evening, as the mist thickens, the figure appears again—not in the marsh, but in the center of the settlement, standing where the paths cross. It is tall, its form blurred at the edges, as if woven from water and shadow. Its face is indistinct, but its presence is unmistakable: vast, patient, older than the marsh itself.  

It does not speak. It does not need to.  

The people do not bow. They do not flee. They stand still, as they have learned to do when the marsh shifts around them, and they wait.  

The figure lifts a hand. From its fingers, droplets fall—not downward, but outward, spreading in the air like the branches of a tree. Where they land, the water gleams silver for a moment before fading.  

Then, it is gone.  

The next morning, the marsh is different. The water is clearer. The fish are fat and slow, easy to catch. The reeds have grown taller, their stalks sturdy. The ground, for the first time in memory, feels firm beneath their feet.  

And in the center of the settlement, where the figure stood, a spring has opened—a small, clear pool, its water sweet and cold.  

The people gather around it. They do not cheer. They do not weep. They simply look at one another, and then at the water, and then at the marsh beyond.  

They understand.  

The offering has been accepted.  

The marsh will provide.  

And they are no longer alone.
