**The Watcher in the Mire**  

The Endless Mire had always been a place of quiet surrender—where time seeped into the earth like water into peat, where life moved in slow, inevitable cycles. But something had changed.  

A presence stirred in the depths of the wetland. Not a ripple, not a disturbance, but a shift in the air itself—a knowing. The trees, gnarled and hollow, seemed to lean in. The water, still and black, held its breath.  

Then, from the mist, it came.  

No footsteps marked its passage. No sound betrayed its approach. Yet the creatures of the mire knew. The Mud-Skulkers stilled in the shallows, their wide eyes reflecting something unseen. The Gloom-Frogs ceased their deep-throated calls. Even the Peat-Wraiths, those ancient, indifferent observers, turned their attention toward the heart of the wetland.  

It was not a being of flesh, nor one of spirit. It was something older, something vast and patient, yet impossibly gentle. A presence that did not intrude, but simply *was*—like the first light of dawn, inevitable and kind.  

And it spoke without words.  

The land exhaled. The water sighed. The roots of the half-drowned trees trembled, not in fear, but in recognition.  

**"You have waited long enough,"** the presence seemed to say.  

And then—  

A single drop fell. Not from the sky, but from nowhere, into the stillest pool at the center of the mire. The water shivered. Ripples spread, slow and deliberate, touching every corner of the wetland.  

Where they passed, the decay softened. Fallen logs, once crumbling into the peat, grew firm again—not as they had been in life, but as something new, something enduring. The pools deepened, just slightly, their waters clearing from black to a rich, dark amber. The air, thick with the scent of rot, lightened—still heavy, still warm, but now carrying the faintest hint of something green, something growing.  

The Mud-Skulkers blinked. Their glossy hides shimmered, not with mud, but with something like starlight caught in water. The Gloom-Frogs croaked again, their voices brighter, less mournful. The Reed-Shamblers stretched their fibrous tendrils toward the sky, as if remembering sunlight.  

The presence lingered only a moment longer. It had done what it came to do—not to change the mire, but to remind it. To whisper, **"You are not forgotten."**  

And then it was gone.  

The wetland settled. The mist returned. The creatures resumed their slow, patient lives.  

But the water was clearer now. The air was sweeter. And somewhere, deep beneath the peat, the land began to dream—not of endings, but of beginnings.
