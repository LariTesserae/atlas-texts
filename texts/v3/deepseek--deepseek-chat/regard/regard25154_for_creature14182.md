### **The Intervention of the Benevolent Power**  

The drowned city does not know it is drowning. It has forgotten the concept of air, of light unbroken by water, of silence without the groan of collapsing metal. It does not remember the hands that built it, nor the minds that dreamed its engines into being. It only *is*—a slow, grinding persistence against the weight of the deep.  

But something has noticed.  

A presence stirs in the black beyond the city’s limits, vast and patient. It is not a god, not in the way mortal minds would understand it. It is something older, quieter—a force that has watched universes flicker like candle flames, that has seen civilizations rise and fall like tides. It does not have a name, but if it did, it would be something like **The Keeper of Broken Things**.  

And the drowned city is very, very broken.  

The Keeper does not descend in a blaze of light. It does not part the waters or still the grinding gears. It does not erase the weight of the ocean, nor does it demand worship. Instead, it does something far more dangerous:  

**It remembers.**  

---

### **What the Keeper Does**  

1. **It Gives the Mechanics a Choice**  
   The Drowned Mechanics have never known anything but service. Their existence is an endless loop of maintenance, their hollow bodies puppeted by the city’s dying will. But the Keeper brushes against their flickering consciousnesses—not with words, but with *possibility*.  

   One by one, the Mechanics pause. Their hands still on the rusted hulls. Their featureless faces tilt upward, as if hearing a sound just beyond perception.  

   *You do not have to do this,* the Keeper whispers into the silence between their thoughts. *You were never meant to be slaves.*  

   Some shudder apart, their cables snapping, their bodies dissolving into the current. They choose oblivion over endless labor. Others—just a few—hesitate. Then, slowly, they step away from the machines.  

2. **It Offers the City an Ending**  
   The Keeper does not destroy. It does not smash the engines or shatter the corroded towers. Instead, it murmurs to the city itself, to the half-dead intelligence that lingers in the groaning metal:  

   *You have done enough.*  

   The city resists. It has been drowning for so long that it no longer knows how to stop. Its engines roar louder, its pistons flailing like a dying beast. But the Keeper is patient. It waits.  

   And then—  

   A single engine stutters. Stops.  

   The silence is deafening.  

3. **It Lets the Waters Claim What Remains**  
   The Keeper does not resurrect the dead. It does not lift the ruins back to the surface or restore the lost minds of the builders. Some things cannot be fixed. Some things should not be.  

   So it does the only merciful thing left:  

   It helps the city *finish* drowning.  

   The remaining Mechanics watch as the structures begin to collapse in earnest, as the last sparks of artificial light gutter out. The weight of the ocean, held at bay for centuries by sheer stubbornness, finally wins. The towers fold inward. The machinery crumples like paper.  

   And then—  

   Peace.  

   The silt settles. The currents slow. The creatures of the deep, those strange, half-living things that flitted at the edges of the ruins, drift closer. They do not mourn. They do not celebrate. They simply *exist*, as they always have.  

   The Keeper watches for a moment longer. Then it turns away.  

   There are other broken things to tend to.  

---

### **Why the Keeper Acts This Way**  

The Keeper is not kind. It is not cruel. It is simply *necessary*.  

Some wounds cannot be healed—only acknowledged. Some labors cannot be rewarded—only released. The drowned city was never meant to last. Its builders are dust. Its purpose is long forgotten. The only thing left was the suffering of its remnants, trapped in an endless, meaningless cycle.  

So the Keeper did the only thing it ever does:  

It granted the mercy of an ending.  

And then it moved on.
