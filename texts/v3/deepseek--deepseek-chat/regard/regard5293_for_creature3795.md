**The Intervention of the Benevolent One**  

The Watcher of Forgotten Edges had seen Gloam for centuries—had felt its slow, grinding metabolism, its patient erosion of all things that dared enter its borders. It was not a place of malice, but of indifference, a city that had folded time into itself until past and future were indistinguishable. The beings that moved through its streets were not cruel, only unaware, extensions of a will too vast to recognize the smallness of mortal life.  

And yet.  

There was a whisper in the hum, a fracture in the rhythm. A soul—perhaps many souls—had become trapped in the city’s recursive dreaming, their presence like grit in the gears of Gloam’s endless machinery. The Watcher could not unmake the plateau, nor still its weeping walls, but it could offer a path.  

It descended as a ripple in the mist, a warmth against the damp. Where it passed, the mineral sheen on the stones softened, just enough to leave footprints. The glyphs flickered, their usual patterns disrupted—not erased, but rearranged, spelling out a single, fleeting word in a language no living thing should know:  

***Follow.***  

The inhabitants paused. Not in recognition, but in disturbance, their fluid forms shuddering as if brushed by an unexpected wind. The Watcher did not force them to see—only to *hesitate*, just long enough for the lost to slip between them.  

It carved a corridor through the non-Euclidean spirals, not by altering the city’s structure, but by *persuading* it, gently, to remember an older shape. The walls sighed as they parted, revealing a passage that had not existed moments before—a vein of warmth in the endless dark. The air here was lighter, the hum muted. At its end, a door. Not a true door, but the suggestion of one, an archway filled with a light that did not burn.  

Beyond it lay not escape, but *choice*: a threshold back to the world of living things, or deeper into Gloam’s heart, where the process at its center pulsed like a sleeping god. The Watcher did not promise safety, only clarity. To step through was to decide—to be seen, truly seen, by something that had never before acknowledged the trespass of smaller lives.  

The city would endure. The water would keep weeping. But for the first time in centuries, the hum faltered—just once—as if in question.  

The Watcher waited. Then, having done what it could, it withdrew, leaving only the echo of warmth on stone.  

The lost would find their way. Or they wouldn’t.  

But they would *choose*.
