**The Benevolent Intervention**  

A presence stirs in the depths of the Drowned Quarter—not the slow, dreaming hum of the submerged machines, nor the patient hunger of what moves beneath the water. This is something else.  

It begins as a sound—not a voice, but a resonance, a vibration that slips between the cracks of the drowned city like light through fingers. The Hollowfolk hear it first in their bones, a warmth where there has only been damp and rot for years. The water itself seems to shiver, not in fear, but in recognition.  

Then, the light comes.  

Not the sickly flicker of the failing generators, nor the cold phosphorescence of the worms that cling to the ruins. This is gold, liquid, spilling from the highest windows of the skeletal towers, pooling in the canals until the black water glows like molten honey. The Hollowfolk flinch at first, shielding their eyes—some weep, for they have forgotten that light could be gentle.  

The presence does not speak in words. It does not need to.  

The water begins to recede.  

Not all at once, not in a great rushing tide, but in slow, deliberate sighs, as if the city is exhaling centuries of swallowed breath. The submerged streets emerge, slick and gleaming, the algae sloughing away like dead skin. The machines beneath the waves fall silent—not broken, but finally at rest. The Hollowfolk watch from their precarious perches, trembling, as the thing that moved in the dark withdraws, slipping deeper, away from the light.  

The presence lingers in the air like the scent of rain on dry earth. It touches the Hollowfolk—not with hands, but with something older, something that knows the shape of wounds before they are named. Their veins no longer show blue-black beneath their skin. Their fingers no longer ache with the cold. They feel, for the first time in years, the ghost of a memory: sunlight.  

The Quarter is still broken. The buildings still sag, the rust still creeps, the rot has not vanished. But now, there are sprouts—thin, defiant tendrils of green pushing through cracks in the stone. A single fruit tree, gnarled and ancient, blooms on a rooftop no one has touched in decades. The water, where it remains, is clear.  

The presence does not stay. It was never meant to. But before it fades, it leaves one last gift:  

A path.  

Not out—the Drowned Quarter is too much a part of them now—but *through*. A way to live without being consumed. A way to remember they were human, even if they choose not to be anymore.  

And when the light dims, when the gold fades to the silver of moonlit waves, the Hollowfolk do not weep.  

They breathe.  

And the city breathes with them.
