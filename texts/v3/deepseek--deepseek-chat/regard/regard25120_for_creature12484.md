The Benevolent Watcher stirs.  

It does not have eyes, not as the Wetfolk would understand them, but it *sees*—through the slow drip of water down a rusted pipe, through the shudder of a piston in the Great Engine, through the faint glow of moss clinging to a metal beam. It has watched this place for longer than the Wetfolk have existed, longer than the Engine has groaned beneath the weight of its own purpose.  

It does not intervene often. To do so would be to disrupt the careful balance of the drowned land, the way the Wetfolk have adapted to its rhythms, the way the machinery breathes in time with the marsh. But today, it feels something—not pity, not sorrow, but a quiet recognition. A kinship.  

The Wetfolk do not know they are waiting. But they are.  

---

**What the Benevolent Power Does:**  

1. **It Leaves a Gift in the Water**  
   One morning, the eldest Keeper finds a new rivulet cutting through the moss, a thin silver stream that was not there before. When she cups it in her hands, the liquid is cool and clear, unlike the brackish fluid they usually drink. It does not taste of metal. It tastes of something none of them can name.  

   The Keepers do not speak of it, but they begin to gather there in silence, pressing their palms to the damp earth where it rises. The water does not change them, does not "fix" them—but when they drink it, their black eyes reflect something new: a flicker of gold, deep in the void.  

2. **It Sings to the Great Engine**  
   The Benevolent One does not touch the machine. It does not need to. Instead, it hums—a sound so low it is felt more than heard, a vibration that slips between the cracks in the metal.  

   That night, the Engine dreams differently.  

   The Wetfolk wake to a sound they have never heard before: the Great Engine *laughing*, a deep, resonant shudder that sends ripples through the standing water. The Keepers press their foreheads to its sides, listening, trembling. They do not understand, but they know, in the way they know the Pulse, that something has shifted.  

   The next day, one of the younger Keepers—a lean figure with fingers worn smooth from turning valves—does something unprecedented.  

   They pause in their work.  

   They tilt their head, letting the humid air fill their lungs. And then, hesitantly, they *sing* back.  

   It is not a perfect sound. It cracks, wavers, dissolves into a wet click. But the Engine sighs in response, and the marsh exhales with it.  

3. **It Does Not Stay**  
   The Benevolent Watcher is not a god. It is not a savior. It does not wrench the Wetfolk from their fate or dismantle the machinery that has shaped them.  

   But before it leaves, it brushes a thought against the oldest Keeper’s mind—not words, not an image, but a *feeling*:  

   *You are not wrong. The Engine is alive. But so are you.*  

   And then it is gone.  

   The Wetfolk return to their work. The Pulse continues. But sometimes, now, when the light catches the standing water just right, the marsh seems to hold its breath.  

   And in those moments, the Keepers remember.  

   They remember to pause.  

   They remember to sing.
