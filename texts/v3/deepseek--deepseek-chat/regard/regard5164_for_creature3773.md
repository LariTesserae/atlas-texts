### **The Watcher in the Mist**  

When the Benevolent One first turned its gaze upon Veyl, it did not arrive as a storm or a blaze of light. It came as the city itself did—slowly, softly, seeping into the cracks of the world like water into stone.  

At first, the Veysians did not notice. The mist thickened in certain alleyways, lingering longer than usual. The canals, already brimming, seemed to hum faintly at dusk, their waters shifting in patterns too deliberate to be mere current. The mountain exhaled, and in its breath, something listened.  

Then, one evening, a child playing near the highest plaza dropped a carved stone into the water. It should have sunk. Instead, it floated—not bobbing like driftwood, but gliding, deliberate, tracing a slow circle before coming to rest against the canal’s edge. The child reached for it, and the stone was warm.  

The Veysians, ever observant, took note. They did not panic. They did not kneel. They simply watched, as they always had, and waited.  

The Benevolent One did not speak—not in words. It spoke in the way the mist curled around a lone traveler on the high paths, guiding their steps when the way grew treacherous. It spoke in the way the fish in the canals grew plump and plentiful in lean seasons, their silver scales glinting like coins in the dim light. It spoke in the dreams of the city’s elders, who woke with the unshakable certainty that they were *seen*, not judged, but *known*.  

And so, the Veysians responded in the only way they knew how: they adapted.  

They left offerings—not grand sacrifices, but small, careful things. A polished stone set at the edge of a spring. A woven garland hung from a bridge. A clay flute played softly into the mist, its notes dissolving like breath into the air.  

The Benevolent One, in turn, gave them nothing they did not already possess—only a quiet assurance that they were not alone. The city remained as it was: slow, wet, enduring. But now, when a Veysian paused at the edge of a canal, they might catch their reflection staring back with eyes not quite their own. When they slept, they dreamed of a presence vast and patient, watching from the heart of the mountain.  

And when the first heavy rains came, threatening to flood the lower terraces, the waters did not rise. They parted, just slightly, just enough, flowing around the oldest homes as if guided by an unseen hand.  

The Veysians did not cheer. They did not weep. They simply nodded, and continued their work.  

For the first time in centuries, the mountain exhaled—and this time, it smiled.
