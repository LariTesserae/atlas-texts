A benevolent and wise power observes this place—this high, windswept plateau, its slow-breathing stone-walkers, its patient people—and does not interfere. Not in the way one might expect.  

It does not reshape the land with a thought, nor does it whisper divine commandments into the ears of the people. It does not demand worship or alter the rhythm of the stone-walkers’ steps. Instead, it **listens**.  

It listens to the deep, resonant hum of the stone-walkers’ movements, the way their passage stirs the earth’s memory. It listens to the people’s quiet observations, their stories passed down through generations—how they learned to read the land by watching the stone-walkers, how they know when to move their settlements by the way the lichen grows thicker on the creatures’ northern flanks.  

And then, in the way of such powers, it **gives back**.  

It does so subtly, almost imperceptibly:  

- **A shift in the wind.** The cold gusts that once bit too harshly now carry, just occasionally, a warmth from some hidden crevice—enough to ease the worst of the frosts when the herds are calving. The people do not call it a miracle. They say the plateau breathes differently this year, and adjust.  

- **A softening of stone.** Where the stone-walkers pass, their feet now leave behind not just cracked earth, but faint, glistening veins of mineral-rich dust. The grasses that sprout in their wake grow a little greener, a little thicker. The shaggy herds grow stronger. No one notices at first. Then they do, and they nod, as if they’d expected it all along.  

- **Dreams in the dark.** On nights when the air is especially clear and the stars press close, the children dream of walking alongside the stone-walkers, their small hands brushing against lichen-covered hides. They wake with their palms stained faintly green, smelling of damp earth. The elders say nothing, but their eyes gleam with something like recognition.  

- **A song beneath the earth.** The stone-walkers have always been silent. But now, when one comes to rest—when it settles into the earth and becomes a hill—those who sleep nearest to it sometimes hear a sound, low and resonant, like stone humming to itself. The next morning, the springs at its base run clearer.  

The power does not stay. It is not a god to be petitioned or a force to be bargained with. It is a passing presence, a breath against the plateau’s cheek.  

But the land remembers.  

And the stone-walkers, ancient as they are, lift their heads as if greeting something unseen before they continue their slow, eternal walk.  

The people, wise in their own way, simply go on living.  

But the next time a child leaves a polished stone in the path of a stone-walker, the creature pauses.  

And for the first time in memory, it bends—just slightly—to brush its massive brow against the offering before moving on.  

The plateau grows.  

And the earth dreams.
