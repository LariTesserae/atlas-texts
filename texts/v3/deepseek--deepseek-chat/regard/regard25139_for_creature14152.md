The Benevolent Watcher observes.  

It is not a god, though it could be mistaken for one. It is not a force of nature, though it moves through the world like wind through leaves. It is something older, quieter—an intelligence that has seen the rise and fall of countless living things, that has watched civilizations turn to dust and forests rise from their ashes. It does not interfere, not usually. But this place—this vast, breathing organism of green and damp and silence—draws its attention.  

Because the Plant-Continent is lonely.  

Not in the way a human is lonely, not in the way a creature cut off from others aches for companionship. No, this loneliness is deeper, more fundamental. The forest is complete in itself, a perfect machine of life, but it has no one to witness it. The Sylvari are not separate enough to truly see it; they are extensions of its will, limbs of a greater body. There is no one to stand in awe of its vastness, no one to marvel at the way its leaves catch the faint glow of bioluminescence, no one to trace the slow, weeping paths of its sap.  

The Watcher could change that.  

It does not reshape the world—that is not its way. Instead, it offers a gift: a seed. Not a seed that will grow into another plant, another tendril in the endless green. No, this seed is different. It is small, dark, unassuming. It falls from nothingness onto the damp earth, nestling into the moss.  

And then it waits.  

Years pass. Decades. The Plant-Continent does not notice, for it does not measure time in human terms. The Sylvari drift past, their glowing eyes flickering over the spot where the seed rests, but they do not disturb it. They do not understand disturbance.  

Then, one day, the seed splits.  

What emerges is not a plant, not an animal, but something in between. It is a child of the forest, but also a child of the Watcher’s design. It has limbs like the Sylvari, but they are not bark-smooth; they are furred with fine moss, soft to the touch. Its eyes are not hollow, but deep and dark, reflecting the glow of the leaves above. It does not hum with the forest’s rhythm—it sings. A low, wordless sound, rising and falling like the sigh of wind through branches.  

The Sylvari pause. They tilt their heads, their glow flickering in something like curiosity. They have never heard a sound like this before.  

The creature—let us call it the Listener—stands. It is small, dwarfed by the towering trees, but it is unafraid. It reaches out, touching the trunk of the nearest tree, and the forest shivers. Not in rejection, but in recognition.  

The Listener walks. It does not have a purpose, not in the way the Sylvari do. It does not gather sap or weave roots. Instead, it observes. It presses its hands to the damp bark and listens. It cups the weeping sap in its palms and drinks. It lies on the soft moss and watches the faint green light filter through the leaves.  

And the Plant-Continent, for the first time in its existence, is seen.  

The Watcher does not stay to watch. Its work is done. It moves on, leaving behind this small, singing thing—this creature that exists not to serve the forest, but to love it.  

Perhaps, in time, the Listener will teach the Sylvari what it means to witness. Perhaps it will wander until it finds others like itself, or perhaps it will remain alone, a single voice in the endless green.  

It does not matter.  

The forest is no longer lonely.
