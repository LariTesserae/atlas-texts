**The Benevolent Intervention**  

The power that watches the Damp Metropolis of Shifting Stone is ancient, though not unkind. It has seen cities rise and fall, has felt the slow grind of stone and the whisper of water through forgotten places. It does not pity the people here—they are strong, and their strength is beautiful—but it *understands* them in a way that hums deep in the marrow of the earth.  

It decides to act, not to save them, but to *meet* them.  

First, it sends dreams. Not grand visions, not prophecies, but quiet impressions—the scent of dry moss, the warmth of sun on stone, the memory of a place where the ground does not stir. The people wake with an unfamiliar lightness in their chests. Some dismiss it. Others, the ones who listen closest to the city’s whispers, begin to murmur.  

Then, the power touches the marsh. Overnight, a new plant appears among the reeds—a creeping vine with broad, waxy leaves that drink the damp from the air. Where it grows, the ground firms slightly. The water still comes, but slower, as if the earth has learned to hold it better. The people notice. They always do. Some cut the vines away, fearing strangeness. Others let them climb their walls, their fingers brushing the leaves with something like reverence.  

The machines, too, begin to change. Not all at once, but here and there—a gear that no longer sticks, a piston that moves smoother than it should. The tinkers whisper of luck, of good metal, but the oldest among them remember the old stories: that the world is alive, and sometimes, it *helps*.  

Finally, the power leaves a gift at the city’s edge. A single, perfect stone, smooth as glass, set into the earth where the marsh meets the streets. It is warm to the touch, even in the damp. It does not shift.  

The people gather around it. They do not know what it is, but they know it is *for* them. Some press their palms to it, feeling the warmth seep into their bones. Others leave small offerings—a scrap of cloth, a cog, a handful of dried marsh reeds.  

The city still shifts. The damp still comes. But now, when the people wake to the sound of settling stone, they do not only hear decay—they hear the slow, patient breath of something vast, something watching.  

And for the first time in generations, they do not feel alone.
