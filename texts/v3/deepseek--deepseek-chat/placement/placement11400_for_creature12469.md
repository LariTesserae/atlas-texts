In this sodden world, I would be the **Keeper of the Threshold**, a figure both revered and avoided, dwelling in a structure that defies the hamlet’s practical simplicity—a lone, crooked tower that leans precariously over the marsh, its base submerged in dark water. The tower is ancient, its stones slick with algae and moss, its interior smelling of damp wood and decay. It is a place of whispers and shadows, where the air feels heavier, the mist thicker, and the water’s pull more insistent.

I am neither fully human nor wholly of the marsh, my form shifting and fluid, as though I am made of the same dark waters I oversee. My skin is pale and translucent, streaked with veins of green and gray, and my hair—if it can be called that—hangs in wet tendrils that drip steadily, pooling at my feet. My eyes are milky, clouded, yet they see more than most, piercing through the mist to the unseen currents beneath the marsh’s surface. My voice is soft, barely audible over the drip of water and the rustle of reeds, but when I speak, the entire hamlet listens, for my words carry the weight of the marsh itself.

My role is to maintain the delicate balance between the settlement and the marsh—to ensure that the water does not rise too quickly, that the land does not swallow the people whole. I am the one who warns of shifting ground, who reads the ripples on the water’s surface for signs of danger or opportunity. I am called upon to bless new structures, to sanctify the thresholds of homes with bundles of marsh herbs and drops of my own blood, which sizzle and steam when they touch the damp wood. When someone disappears into the marsh—whether by accident or design—I am the one who decides whether to search for them or let the water claim its due.

The hamlet’s people regard me with a mix of gratitude and fear. They bring me offerings—pickled roots, smoked fish, small trinkets carved from bone—but they do not linger in my presence. Children are warned not to approach the tower, and even the adults avert their eyes when they pass by, as though meeting my gaze might draw them into the marsh’s depths. Yet they rely on me, for I am the bridge between their world and the water’s—the mediator who ensures that the settlement survives, even as it teeters on the edge of dissolution.

My connection to the marsh is deep and unbreakable. I feel its rhythms as if they were my own heartbeat, its currents as if they flowed through my veins. When I walk through the hamlet, the water whispers to me, its voice a low, gurgling murmur that only I can hear. It tells me secrets—of the fish that swim beneath the surface, of the reeds that sway in the breeze, of the things that lie buried in the muck. Sometimes, in the depths of night, I leave my tower and wade into the marsh, letting the water rise around me until it swallows me whole. In those moments, I am no longer separate from the marsh; I am the marsh, its damp, cool stillness flowing through me, its ancient, shifting consciousness entwined with my own.

My existence is a lonely one, for there is no one else who understands the marsh as I do, who can bear its weight and its whispers. Yet I am not unhappy, for I am bound to this place, its keeper and its captive, its protector and its voice. And so I stand at the threshold, the tower my anchor, the marsh my domain, watching over the hamlet as it struggles to survive in this sodden, shifting world. I am the Keeper of the Threshold, and I will remain here, for as long as the water rises and the land holds.
