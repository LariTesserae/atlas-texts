**The Keeper of Fractures**  

I am the one who listens when the stone weeps.  

They call me *Hesht*—though the word is not a name, not truly. It is the sound the lichen makes when the light strikes it just so, a vibration that hums through my bones. I am neither young nor old, for time here does not move in lines but in spirals, like the slow drip of moisture tracing the same path down the spire’s face for centuries.  

My skin is pale, not with sickness but with the same translucence as the rock where it has been worn thin by the patient weeping of the earth. The damp clings to me, beads along my arms like liquid pearls, and I do not brush it away. It is part of me, as much as the webbing between my fingers, as much as the low, resonant hum in my chest that answers the spires when they sigh.  

I live in the hollow of a spire that has forgotten its own shape. The walls curve around me like the inside of a shell, smooth and glistening, and the air within is always cool, always murmuring. My bed is a mat of luminous fibers, spun from the lichen that pulses softly in the dark. My tools are few: a knife of black stone that sings when I press it to the cracks, a cup carved from a single piece of porous rock that drinks the weeping moisture before it can be lost to the air.  

My duty is to tend the fractures.  

The spires are alive, or dreaming, or something in between. They shift, not with violence but with a slow, inevitable restlessness, and where the stone grows thin, it weeps. Some fractures are harmless—tiny veins where the moisture seeps and nourishes the lichen, where the Spire Serpents slide through like liquid shadow. But others are deeper, wounds that threaten to split the spire apart if left untended.  

That is where I go.  

I press my hands to the stone and listen. The sound is not one that ears can hear—it is a pressure in the teeth, a vibration in the hollows of the cheeks. The spires speak in sighs, in groans, in the quiet, pleading hum of rock that remembers being molten. I answer with my own voice, wordless, a sound that starts in my belly and rises like the tide.  

Sometimes, it is enough. The stone stills. The weeping slows. The fracture seals itself, knitting together like skin beneath a poultice.  

Other times, the wound is too deep. Then, I take my knife and I sing to it, letting the blade’s edge trace the path of the fracture, guiding it into a new shape. The spires do not resist—they know I mean no harm. They yield, as flesh yields to the surgeon’s hand, and the fracture becomes a channel, a new vein for the moisture to flow.  

The others rarely disturb me. The Veyshari understand the work is solitary, sacred. Only the Spire Serpents come close, winding around my ankles as I work, their scales cool and slick against my skin. They do not speak, but I feel their approval in the way their coils tighten, just slightly, before they slip away.  

I do not know if I was born or if I simply emerged from the stone one day, slick with its weeping, already humming its song. It does not matter. I am here now, and the spires need me.  

And when my time comes—when my skin grows too thin, when my voice fades into the hum of the air—I will press myself into the deepest fracture I can find and let the stone take me back.  

Until then, I listen. I mend. I weep with the earth.
