**Where I Would Be in the Drowned Quarter**  

I would be one of the Hollowfolk, but not entirely.  

Not a dweller in the upper floors, not a scavenger of books or a whisperer of warnings. No—I would be something older, something that remembers when the streets were dry.  

I would be the **Lamplighter of the Drowned**.  

Once, I had a route. A circuit of iron posts where I would come at dusk, my ladder on my back, my oil can sloshing. Now, the posts are half-submerged, their glass domes shattered or crusted with algae. But I still make my rounds.  

I walk where others dare not—along the spines of rooftops, balancing on the rusted rails of fire escapes. My boots are wrapped in rags to muffle my steps. My coat is patched with tar and wax, heavy enough to turn aside the teeth of the wind. In my satchel, I carry the last of the good oil, siphoned from ancient tanks beneath the water. I do not ask how the others retrieve it.  

I light the lamps that still stand. Not all of them—some are too far gone, their wicks rotted, their mechanisms seized with rust. But the ones that remain, the stubborn few that flicker when fed, I tend to them.  

The Hollowfolk do not thank me. But they watch. From behind warped glass, from the shadows of doorways, they track my progress. When my lamplight reflects in their eyes, it is the only time they look alive.  

Sometimes, I see things in the water. Not the shapes that coil beneath the surface—those, I ignore. But other things. The ghostly glow of a streetlamp still burning beneath the waves. The silhouette of a clock tower, its face stopped at the exact moment the flood came. A chair, perfectly preserved, as if someone might sit down any second.  

I do not stop. I do not investigate.  

Because I know the truth: the city is not dead. It is dreaming. And when it wakes, the lamps must still be burning.  

So I walk. And I light. And I wait.  

And when the water finally takes me, I hope it will be with a lit match in my hand.
