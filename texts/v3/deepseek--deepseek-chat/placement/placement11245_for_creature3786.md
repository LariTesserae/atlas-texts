### **The Moss-Covered Engine Quarter**  

You wake in a room that is not yours.  

The ceiling is low, sagging slightly under the weight of damp plaster and creeping moss. The walls exhale moisture—tiny rivulets trace paths down to the floorboards, which yield underfoot like the flesh of overripe fruit. A single window, its glass fogged with condensation, offers a view of the Quarter: a tangle of canals choked with duckweed, rooftops furred with moss, the skeletal remains of iron walkways half-submerged in black water.  

You do not remember arriving.  

But you know this place. Not in the way one knows a street or a house, but in the way one knows the shape of their own breath—instinctive, unthinking. The air here is thick with peat-smoke and the iron tang of slow corrosion. Somewhere below, an engine groans, a sound like a great beast shifting in its sleep.  

You rise. Your limbs feel heavy, as if the humidity has seeped into your bones. The floor is cool and slightly sticky beneath your bare feet. A mirror hangs crooked on the wall, its surface mottled with age, and when you peer into it, your reflection is wrong.  

Not distorted. Not blurred.  

*Different.*  

Your skin is pale, tinged green at the knuckles and elbows, as if algae has begun to bloom beneath the surface. Your hair is lank, damp, threaded with strands of something that might be moss or might be something else entirely. Your eyes—  

Your eyes are black. Not the black of pupils dilated in darkness, but the black of still water, of engine oil, of the spaces between gears where no light ever reaches.  

You blink. The reflection blinks back.  

A knock at the door—soft, almost apologetic.  

You do not answer. The door opens anyway.  

A figure stands in the threshold, silhouetted by the dim glow of a peat-lantern. They are small, their shoulders hunched forward as if accustomed to ducking under low beams. Their clothes are layers of waxed canvas and oiled leather, patched and repatched until the original fabric is lost to time. Their hands are broad, fingers slightly webbed at the base, nails thick and yellowed like old horn.  

They do not speak. They do not need to.  

You know what they are.  

*Peat-walker.*  

They tilt their head, studying you with eyes that reflect the lantern light like a cat’s. Then they extend a hand, palm up, offering.  

On it rests a single gear, rusted at the edges, its teeth worn smooth by time.  

You take it.  

The metal is warm.  

### **What You Are**  

You are not human. Not anymore.  

You are something that has been here for a long, long time.  

Perhaps you were once a worker, one of the countless souls who tended the engines until the day you leaned too close to a steam valve and were swallowed by the mist. Perhaps you were a traveler who strayed too far into the Quarter and found that leaving was not an option. Perhaps you were never alive at all—perhaps you are the coalesced memory of the place itself, a thing woven from peat-smoke and the sigh of rusted metal.  

It does not matter.  

What matters is this:  

The Quarter has claimed you.  

### **What You Do**  

You tend the engines.  

Not in the way the peat-walkers do, with their grease-stained hands and murmured prayers to valves. No—you move through the machinery like a shadow, like a draft of cool air. You press your palms to overheating pipes and feel the feverish pulse beneath the metal. You whisper to the flywheels when they stutter, your voice blending with the creak of old bearings.  

Sometimes, you slip into the canals.  

The water is warm, thick with sediment, alive with things that dart just beyond sight. You let it fill your lungs, and it does not drown you. You are beyond drowning.  

The peat-walkers see you sometimes. They do not startle. They simply nod, as if acknowledging a fellow laborer, and go about their business.  

You are part of the Quarter now.  

And the Quarter is part of you.  

### **The Others Like You**  

You are not alone.  

There are more of you—things that move through the moss and the mist, things that were once something else. Some are barely more than a ripple in the water, a sigh in the steam. Others are nearly solid, their forms flickering at the edges like a reflection in oil.  

You do not speak to them.  

You do not need to.  

You all know your purpose.  

### **The Offerings**  

The peat-walkers leave them for you.  

A cracked cup of strong, bitter tea, left on a pipe junction. A handful of dried reeds tucked into the crevice of a boiler. A smear of grease on a particular valve, accompanied by a whisper you cannot quite hear.  

You take them.  

Not because you need them.  

But because it is polite.  

### **The Day the Engines Stop**  

It will happen.  

One day, the great machines will shudder, sigh, and fall silent. The peat-walkers will gather around them, their hands resting on the cooling metal, their heads bowed.  

You will be there too.  

You will press your forehead to the nearest pipe and listen to the last, faint echo of steam.  

Then you will rise.  

And you will help them push the engines into the canals.  

And the Quarter will settle a little deeper into the earth.  

And the moss will grow a little thicker.  

And the water will rise a little higher.  

And you will remain.  

As you always have.  

As you always will.
