In the Weeping City of Vashal, I find myself drawn to the canals—those veins of sluggish water that wind through the city like threads of a broken tapestry. My name is Evrin, though few know it anymore. I am a dredger, one of the many who toil to keep the city’s watery arteries from clogging completely. My days are spent waist-deep in the murky runoff, scooping silt and debris into iron buckets that groan with the weight of their contents. The work is endless, thankless, and essential. Without dredgers like me, the city would drown in its own filth.

My body has adapted to the dampness. My skin is pale and slick, like the stones beneath my feet, and my fingers are webbed with calluses from gripping the handles of my tools. My lungs have grown accustomed to the thick air, though I still cough up black phlegm at the end of each day. My boots are reinforced with steel to keep me from slipping on the slick cobblestones, and my coat is heavy with oil and wax to repel the constant mist. I move slowly, methodically, my rhythm matching the city’s ceaseless hum.  

I live in a small room above a foundry, its walls stained with soot and damp. The room is spare—a cot, a table, a single chair—but it is mine. The foundry’s heat keeps the worst of the dampness at bay, though everything still feels slightly damp to the touch. My meager possessions are few: a tin cup, a spare set of clothes, a small shrine to the Spirits of Steam and Iron. I leave offerings of coal and scrap metal, praying for protection from the machines that both sustain and threaten us.  

Few dredgers last long. The work is brutal, the air is toxic, and the canals are treacherous. Those who do survive often succumb to the city’s darker pull, the whispers of the Deep Engine that echo in the silence between the pounding of pistons. I have heard them too, felt their pull in the marrow of my bones. They speak of something vast and unseen, something that hums beneath the city’s foundations. I try to ignore them, to focus on the rhythm of my work, but they linger in the corners of my mind, insistent and unsettling.  

Sometimes, when the city is quiet—rare moments when the machinery pauses, and the hum recedes—I venture into the forgotten places. The crumbling basements, the rusted catwalks, the derelict factories where the air is thick with the scent of decay. These places are dangerous, but they are also alive with mystery. I have scavenged gears and pipes, scraps of metal that I trade for food and coal. I have also found strange things—shards of glass that glow faintly in the dark, fragments of machinery that hum with a life of their own. I keep these hidden in a box beneath my cot, though I do not know why.  

I am not alone in my curiosity. There are others like me—scavengers, tinkerers, those who cannot help but delve into the city’s secrets. We do not speak of it, but we recognize one another in the way we move, in the glint of our eyes. Some disappear, swallowed by the city or the whispers of the Deep Engine. Others persist, driven by a need to understand the place we call home.  

I have no family, no ties beyond the work I do and the city I serve. Yet, I feel tethered to Vashal in a way I cannot explain. Perhaps it is the rhythm of the machines, the pulse of the Deep Engine beneath my feet. Perhaps it is the dampness that seeps into my bones, the rust that clings to my skin. Whatever it is, I am a part of this place—a cog in its vast, incomprehensible machine.  

And so, I dredge. I scrape the silt from the canals, I remove the debris that clogs the city’s veins. I endure, as the city endures, patient and unyielding. The work is endless, but so is the city. And always, always, the water returns.
