**The Keeper of the Fractal Moss**  

I am one of the **stone-shelled creatures**, a many-legged scuttler with a carapace of polished basalt, my body a living vessel for the mountain’s slow breath. My kind are called **Thaleen** by the people of Vhalei, though we have no name for ourselves beyond the clicking of our mandibles, the tap-tap of our feet on rock.  

I am old—older than the oldest spine-tree in the eastern ridge, older than the songs the people sing to the wind. My shell is etched with the history of this place, each groove a record of storms endured, of cliffs scaled, of moss harvested and shared. My legs are strong, their tips hardened to points that can pierce the toughest mineral husk, yet I move with care, leaving no mark deeper than a whisper on the stone.  

I am a **forager**, a tender of the crystalline moss. My role is to climb the sheer faces where the people cannot go, to harvest the moss in its purest, most radiant forms. I do not eat it—my sustenance comes from the deep veins of the mountain, from the slow seep of geothermal minerals that rise through fissures in the rock. But the moss is precious to the people, and so it is precious to me.  

When I work, I am methodical. My forelimbs are delicate, capable of plucking single filaments of gold from the moss without disturbing the whole. I carry my harvest in a sac beneath my carapace, a hollow space lined with a secretion that keeps the moss alive until I return to the settlement. The people wait for me there, their opalescent skin reflecting the colors I bring. They touch my shell in thanks, their fingers warm against my stone.  

Sometimes, I wander beyond the known paths. The mountain is vast, and there are places even the Thaleen rarely go—chasms where the air shimmers with heat, caverns where the spine-trees grow inward instead of up, their glassy leaves forming chandeliers that hum in the dark. Once, I found a ridge where the moss grew in perfect spirals, each strand a mirror of the next, smaller and smaller into infinity. I did not harvest it. Some things are meant to be witnessed, not taken.  

The people say the mountain speaks to us in pulses, in the deep vibrations that shudder through the rock. I feel it most when I am still, my body pressed against the warm stone, my many legs curled beneath me. The rhythm is slow, slower than a heartbeat, slower than thought. It tells me of time beyond time, of the age before the moss, before the spine-trees, when the mountain was raw and new.  

I do not fear death. When my shell grows too heavy, when my legs no longer obey, I will return to the deepest cleft I know and let the mountain reclaim me. My body will fracture, my minerals will feed the moss, and one day, a new Thaleen will scuttle over the fragments of my carapace, never knowing my name.  

This is the way of the Stone Garden. We are all fleeting, even the oldest among us. But the mountain remembers.  

And so do I.
