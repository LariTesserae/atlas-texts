In the Plant-Continent, I find myself in a state of both awe and unease. The landscape stretches endlessly into a vast, whispering darkness—a continent of living plants that breathes as one entity. The boundaries between individual plants dissolve into a seamless tapestry of green and black, a living mass that pulses faintly with an inner rhythm. The air is thick, warm, and heavy with moisture, clinging to the skin like a second layer. Water seeps from every surface—from the glossy leaves that drip steadily, from the spongy ground that squelches faintly underfoot, from the weeping trunks of trees that tower into the unseen canopy above. This is not a marsh but a forest of perpetual condensation, where water hangs in the air as a mist, never quite falling, never quite rising.

The vegetation is total, overwhelming, absolute. It is not merely a collection of plants but a dominion of green life that has consumed all else. The ground is a mat of roots and moss, soft and yielding, constantly shifting as if alive. Above, the branches interweave into a solid canopy, so thick that no light penetrates—only the faintest glow, a bioluminescent shimmer that emanates from the leaves themselves, casting everything in a ghostly green hue. The trunks of the trees are not separate entities but fused columns, their bark smooth and damp, weeping a clear, viscous sap that pools in hollows and trickles into unseen streams. There are no clearings, no breaks in the green—only an endless, suffocating density of life.

Despite its vastness, the landscape feels claustrophobic, as though the sheer mass of vegetation presses inward on the senses. The silence is profound, broken only by the occasional drip of water or the faint creak of shifting branches. No animals stir here—no birds, no insects, no unseen scurrying in the undergrowth. This is a world without fauna, where plants have claimed every niche, every role, every breath of existence. The absence of life other than plants feels eerie, as though the world has been stripped of something fundamental.

Yet, within this stillness, there is a strange, industrial hum—not loud, but pervasive, a vibration that seems to emanate from the plants themselves. It is as though the forest is a vast machine, its roots and stems and leaves working in unison to some incomprehensible purpose. The sap that flows from the trees is not merely water but a nutrient-rich fluid that fuels this living engine. The forest feels alive in a way that transcends biology, as though it has evolved into something more than a collection of plants—a single, planet-sized organism that operates on principles beyond understanding.

In the heart of this primeval expanse, I encounter the Sylvari—beings woven from the very essence of the Plant-Continent itself. They blur the line between plant and sentience, growing from the thick, pulsing marrow of the forest like fruits from a vine. They are not fauna, not in the traditional sense, for they lack blood, bone, and breath. Instead, they are extensions of the green dominion, avatars of the forest’s silent will.

The Sylvari are tall, slender, and eerily graceful, their bodies composed of smooth, bark-like skin that glistens with a sheen of moisture. Their limbs are supple yet firm, like the branches of young trees, and their movements are slow, deliberate, as though each step is a meditation. Their faces are smooth and featureless, save for faint, curving lines that resemble the veins of leaves. Where eyes might be, there are only shallow depressions, filled with a soft, bioluminescent glow that pulses in time with the forest’s hum. They do not speak, for they have no mouths—yet they communicate through a silent language of touch and light, their bodies flickering faintly as they exchange thoughts.

As I observe the Sylvari, I feel a deep connection to this world, yet also a profound sense of alienation. I am not one of them, nor am I part of the Plant-Continent in the way they are. Yet, I can sense the forest’s will pulsating through them, a quiet, insistent force that drives their actions. They move in small groups of three or four, their glowing forms drifting through the mist like will-o’-the-wisps. Their tasks are enigmatic—sometimes they gather sap from weeping trees, collecting it in hollow gourds grown from their own bodies; other times they press their hands against the trunks of ancient trees, as if listening to their slow, rhythmic pulse. Occasionally, they pause to rearrange the roots or moss beneath their feet, weaving them into intricate patterns that seem to serve no practical purpose but resonate with a strange, unspoken beauty.

In this world, I find myself as an observer, a wanderer trying to comprehend a place where the boundaries between life and landscape blur. I am drawn to the Sylvari, their silent, deliberate movements, their seamless connection to the forest. Yet, I am also aware of my own separateness, my inability to fully integrate into this ecosystem. I move slowly, carefully, trying not to disturb the delicate balance of this world. The air clings to me, the moisture condensing on my skin, merging me with the forest’s breath.

As I walk, I feel the ground shifting beneath my feet, the roots and moss constantly rearranging themselves. The forest seems to respond to my presence, the hum intensifying slightly, the glowing leaves pulsing more rapidly. I sense that I am both an intruder and a curiosity, a being from another world that the Plant-Continent cannot fully understand.

In this place, I am neither Sylvari nor forest, but something in between—a transient being caught in a world of perpetual condensation and green life. The rules of existence here are rewritten, the familiar left far behind. I continue to wander, driven by a quiet curiosity, a desire to understand this strange, beautiful world where plants reign supreme, and individuality is swallowed by the whole.
