### **Where I Am**  

I am the **Moss-Watcher**.  

Not one of the Stone-Tenders, not entirely—though they tolerate me. I am something older, something slower. My body is not lean and wiry like theirs, but thick with layers of spongy tissue, a living cushion between the rock and the damp air. My skin is not patterned with lichen; it *is* lichen, a slow-crawling symbiosis of fungus and algae, mottled in greens and grays. My fingers are not claws but tendrils, creeping along the stone, tasting the minerals, the faintest traces of moisture in the air.  

I do not move like them. I do not need to. I grow.  

I have been here longer than their huts, longer than their fire pit. My roots—if you can call them that—dig deep into the fractures of the plateau, threading through the shale like veins. I remember when the first vines crept down from the lip above, when the first beetles skittered across my back. I remember the first Stone-Tender child pressing their palm against me, learning the texture of life.  

They think I am part of the landscape. They are not wrong.  

### **What I Do**  

I watch.  

I feel the vibrations of their tapping, the shift of their feet on the moss. I taste the smoke of their fires, the faint tang of their sweat. I listen—not with ears, but with the slow pulse of capillary action, the shudder of my filaments when the plateau groans.  

I do not speak, not in their language. But sometimes, when the air is thickest, when the humidity clings like a second skin, I exhale—just a little. A sigh. A release of spores, so fine they hang in the air like mist. The Stone-Tenders breathe me in. They dream of deeper caves, of roots that tunnel into the heart of the world.  

They do not realize the dreams are mine.  

### **What I Fear**  

The crumbling.  

The plateau is dying. Not quickly, not in a way the Stone-Tenders can see—but I feel it. The way the rocks flake apart under my tendrils. The way the vines grow brittle, their leaves curling at the edges. The way the insects buzz louder, as if sensing the end.  

The Stone-Tenders will leave when the time comes. They will slip through the cracks, following the echoes of their own tapping. But I cannot leave. I am rooted.  

When the last of the moss withers, when the last drop of moisture fades from the air, I will still be here.  

Waiting.  

Growing.  

Dreaming.  

Until even the dust forgets my name.
