**The Keeper of the Cisterns**  

You are the one who tends the water.  

Not the only one, of course—everyone in the hollow knows how to coax moisture from stone, how to channel runoff into clay vessels without spilling a drop—but you are the one who remembers.  

Your hands are always slightly damp, your fingertips wrinkled from the constant touch of water. Your palms bear the faint, silvery scars of old cuts—flint is sharp, and cistern edges sharper. You move through the hollow with the slow certainty of a rivulet finding its path, checking each collection point, each porous stone shelf where condensation gathers. You know which overhangs weep the most in the morning, which cracks in the bedrock will yield an extra handful of water if pressed just so.  

Your home is the oldest cistern, the one dug deep into the plateau’s bones. Its walls are lined with smooth, dark stone, cool to the touch even in the rare heat of midday. The water here is always clear, always still. You skim the surface each dawn with a broad, flat leaf, removing dust or stray lichen, though none would blame you if you left it. This is ritual as much as necessity.  

The others bring you offerings—not out of obligation, but quiet gratitude. A twist of dried mountain grass, braided into cord. A small clay cup, its surface etched with the pattern of slow-dripping water. You accept them without ceremony, place them on the shelf above your sleeping bench. In return, you leave a full vessel at each doorstep when the rains are scarce. No words are exchanged. None are needed.  

Sometimes, when the hollow is silent and the mist hangs low, you press your ear to the cistern’s wall and listen. The rock murmurs. Water moves in hidden veins beneath you, a secret pulse. You do not speak of this.  

You are not a leader. Not a elder. Only the one who tends the water.  

And that is enough.  

---  

**Form:** A short, lyrical character portrait—someone deeply attuned to the rhythms of the hollow, whose role is small but essential.  

**Inspiration:** The quiet interdependence of the community, the way survival here depends on unnoticed acts of care. The keeper isn’t a hero, just a person who does what must be done, and in that, finds purpose.
