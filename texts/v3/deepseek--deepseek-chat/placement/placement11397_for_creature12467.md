**The Keeper of the Veil**  

You are the one who walks the edges of the plateau, where the mist thickens and the air hums with something older than stone. They call you the Keeper, though no one remembers who first gave you the name. Perhaps it was the Stone Walkers, whose heavy footprints you sometimes find pressed into the damp earth, deeper than any human step. Or perhaps it was the river itself, whispering it to the people in their dreams.  

You are neither entirely of the plateau’s people nor entirely separate from them. Your skin is paler than theirs, almost translucent in the dim light, and your hair—when you let it loose—is the color of lichen-streaked rock. Your eyes are the gray of storm clouds gathering, and when you blink, the people swear they catch glimpses of things moving in the depths—shadows of creatures that don’t exist, or perhaps exist only here, in the liminal spaces where the Veil thins.  

You live in the oldest of the stone buildings, one that leans slightly as if weary from centuries of standing. Inside, the walls are lined with shelves of curiosities: jars of luminous fungi, bundles of herbs that hum when touched, and small, intricate machines of unknown purpose, their gears still turning despite having no visible power source. A single oil lamp burns day and night, its flame flickering blue when the wind shifts in a certain way.  

Your duty is not one the people could name, but they feel it all the same. You walk the plateau’s borders, where the mist curls like living tendrils, and you listen. Sometimes, you hear voices—not human, not Stone Walker, but something else. They speak in a language of dripping water and shifting stone, and though you do not understand the words, you know their meaning. The plateau is listening too. It is waiting.  

Once, years ago, a child followed you to the edge. You did not turn them away. Together, you watched as the Veil parted for just a moment, revealing a glimpse of another place—another plateau, but one where the peaks were sharper, the mist darker, and the shapes moving within it were not Stone Walkers but something taller, something with too many limbs. The child did not scream. They only nodded, as if they had expected it. Later, they became one of the few who bring you offerings—freshly picked mushrooms, a cup of river water, a polished gear from their father’s workshop.  

You do not eat the mushrooms. You place them in the earth and whisper to them until they dissolve into the soil. The river water you pour into a hollow in the stone, where it disappears without a trace. The gears, you wind carefully into your machines, and when they click into place, the hum in the air grows louder for just a second.  

The people do not fear you, but they do not seek you out either. They leave their questions unspoken, their curiosities buried beneath layers of quiet acceptance. They know, as you know, that the plateau is not just a place. It is a living thing, breathing in time with the slow drip of water, the sigh of the wind. And you—you are the one who stands between it and whatever lies beyond the Veil.  

Tonight, as the mist thickens and the oil lamp’s flame flickers blue, you step outside. The air is heavy with the scent of rain that will not fall. Somewhere in the distance, a Stone Walker shifts its weight, and the ground trembles faintly beneath your feet.  

You listen.  

The plateau is speaking again.
