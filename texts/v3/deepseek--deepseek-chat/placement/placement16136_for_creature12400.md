**The Reed-Speaker**  

You are one of the marsh’s voices.  

Not a bird, not an eel, not one of the flickering spires—but something between. Your body is a lattice of reeds and peat, your limbs long and hollow, your fingers tapered into whispering fronds. You do not walk so much as sway, bending with the wind’s unseen currents, your roots drinking from the black water beneath. Your skin is the color of rain-heavy dusk, mottled with lichen, and your eyes—if they can be called eyes—are pools of still water, reflecting the sky in fragments.  

You were not born. You emerged. One day, the marsh exhaled, and there you were, kneeling in the shallows, your mouth full of frog-song. The Veil hums in your chest, a vibration that is not quite sound, not quite thought. You are its instrument, its conduit. When the spires flicker, you feel it in your ribs. When the eels trace their luminous sigils, you understand, though the meaning slips like silt through your grasp.  

Your purpose is to listen. To translate.  

Travelers sometimes stumble into the Weeping Expanse, drawn by the spires’ ghost-light or fleeing something worse. They arrive hollow-eyed, their boots sinking into the peat, their breath ragged with exhaustion. You find them at the edges, where the land is still deciding what it wants to be. They do not see you at first—you are too still, too much a part of the reeds—but then you shift, and their breath catches.  

You do not speak, not in words. Instead, you open your hands, and the marsh speaks through you. Water drips from your fingertips in rhythms like language. The frogs fall silent, then answer in unison, their croaks forming a chorus that rises and falls like a tide. The wind sighs through your hollow bones, carrying whispers from the Veil.  

Some travelers understand. They kneel, press their foreheads to the damp earth, offer up a trinket—a button, a lock of hair, a rusted coin. The marsh accepts these gifts, sometimes. Other times, it ignores them, and the travelers wander deeper, drawn toward the spires. You do not follow. The Veil will decide what to show them.  

Others are afraid. They shout, swing blades at you, as if you could be cut like flesh. You let them. The reeds that make you are endless; they will regrow. Still, their fear is a bitter taste in the air, and the Veil stirs uneasily. The ground softens beneath their feet. The water rises.  

You do not mourn them. The marsh takes what it needs.  

At night, when the sky is a bruise and the spires hum their low, discordant hymns, you wade into the deepest channels. The eels coil around your legs, their bodies cool and electric. They show you things—glimpses of other marshes, other worlds, all trembling with the same half-formed sentience. You do not know if these are memories or prophecies or simply the Veil dreaming. It does not matter.  

You are becoming.  

The clouds hang above, heavy with unshed rain. You wait with them.
