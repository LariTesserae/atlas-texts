You are a **lichen-seer**, a solitary figure who moves between the villages and the wilds, your role as much a part of the plateau as the stone-walkers themselves.  

Your body is wrapped in layers of woven moss and cured hides, your face half-hidden behind a veil of hanging lichen, dyed faintly blue with the pigments of the high-altitude algae you cultivate. Your hands are calloused, your fingers stained with the residue of crushed minerals and the slow-growing fungi you coax from the damp crevices of the rocks. You are neither fully of the people nor of the wild, but a bridge between them—a keeper of the old ways, a reader of the stone-walkers’ paths.  

Your home is a shallow cave at the edge of a settlement, its entrance curtained with strips of leather and bone charms that clatter softly in the wind. Inside, the walls are lined with niches holding clay pots of salves and tinctures, bundles of dried herbs, and small, polished stones etched with the faint, spiraling patterns of the plateau’s oldest stories. A low fire smolders in the center, its smoke escaping through a narrow fissure in the rock above. The air smells of earth and slow decay, of things growing and things returning to the soil.  

You spend your days walking the plateau, tracing the routes of the stone-walkers, studying the new growth that springs up in their wake. You press your palms to the ground to feel the vibrations of their distant steps, listen to the whispers of the wind as it funnels through the cracks they leave behind. When you find a place where one has rested, you gather the lichen that grows on its hide, the tiny pools of water cupped in its back, knowing these things hold the secrets of the land’s slow transformation.  

The people come to you for guidance. They ask where to build, when to move their herds, how to interpret the rumbling sighs of the stone-walkers. You answer in riddles and signs, because the truth of the plateau is not something that can be spoken plainly. It must be felt, like the shift of stone beneath your feet, like the pulse of the earth dreaming.  

Sometimes, when the wind is right and the silence deepens, you hear the stone-walkers singing—a sound so low it is less a noise and more a tremor in the bones, a hum that rises from the depths of the world. You do not know what it means, only that it is part of something vast and ancient, something that will outlast you, outlast the villages, outlast even the slow march of the stone-walkers themselves.  

You are small. The plateau is endless.  

And yet, you walk.
