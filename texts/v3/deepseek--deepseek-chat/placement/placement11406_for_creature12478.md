**The Things Beneath the Water**  

The marsh does not give up its dead. Not in the way other places do.  

When something dies here—a bird, a fish, even the occasional wanderer who strays too far into the mist—it does not float. It does not bloat and rise to the surface for the carrion-eaters to pick apart. Instead, the black water swallows it whole. The silt shifts, the reeds part just slightly, and then the body is gone.  

But it does not disappear.  

If you were to wade into the shallows (and you should not), if you were to press your hands into the cold, clinging mud (but you will not), you might feel them. Not bones—bones would imply decay, and decay is too kind a word for what happens here. No, they are still whole. Preserved. Suspended in the gelid embrace of the marsh, their flesh turned to something between wax and leather, their open eyes clouded with algae.  

They do not move. Not exactly. But sometimes, when the mist thickens and the distant machinery groans like a living thing, the water ripples in ways it shouldn’t. A slow, sinuous disturbance, as if something beneath is turning over in its sleep.  

And then there are the leeches.  

They are not like ordinary leeches. They are longer, thinner, their bodies near-translucent save for the dark thread of their digestive tracts. They do not simply attach—they quest. They probe at the skin with delicate, almost inquisitive movements, as if searching for something. A memory, perhaps. A weakness.  

They find it every time.

**The Sound Beyond the Mist**  

The machinery is not supposed to be here.  

That much is obvious. The marsh is old—older than the factories, older than the roads that must snake somewhere beyond the horizon. The land does not know what to do with the sound, so it swallows it, muffles it, turns it into something low and resonant, like the hum of a dying refrigerator.  

But sometimes, when the wind shifts just so, the mist thins. And then—if you stand very still, if you do not breathe—you can see it.  

A smokestack. Just one. Rising from the edge of the marsh like a blackened finger. There are no lights, no signs of movement, but the sound persists. A grinding, a churning, a noise that feels less like industry and more like digestion.  

What is it refining? What is it consuming?  

The water does not answer. But the leeches twitch in the mud, and the things beneath the water shift, ever so slightly, in their sleep.
