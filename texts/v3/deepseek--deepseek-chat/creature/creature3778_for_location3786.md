**The Keepers of the Weeping Stones**  

They are not born here. They *arrive*. Drawn by the slow pulse of the fen, the whisper of moisture in the air, the way the light bends green and gold through the hanging vines. Some come seeking refuge. Others are sent as punishment. A few simply wake one day to find themselves wading through the black water, their memories bleached like sun-bleached bone.  

They call themselves **The Drowned Who Walk**, though none remember who first spoke the name. Their skin is leathery, stained dark by tannins, their fingers and toes slightly webbed from lifetimes of wading. Their hair grows in thick, rope-like strands, often tangled with strands of fibrous moss or tiny, translucent snails. Their eyes—large, dark, adapted to the gloom—reflect the dim light like still water.  

They do not build. They *adapt*. Their homes are hollows in the roots of the great trees, chambers woven from living vines, platforms suspended above the water on the backs of the weeping stones. They move silently, bare feet finding purchase on slick surfaces, their bodies swaying with the rhythm of the fen. They speak little, and when they do, their voices are soft, resonant, as if the marsh itself is shaping their words.  

They are not rulers of this place. They are its *caretakers*. They tend to the great beasts, guiding them away from fragile growths. They harvest the luminous fungi that glow faintly in the dark, using them to mark paths through the labyrinthine roots. They collect the weeping stones’ moisture in vessels of hardened resin, drinking only what they need.  

Some say they worship the fen. This is not quite true. They *listen* to it. They know when the water will rise before it does. They sense the approach of storms by the way the roots tremble. They understand the language of the slow, deep calls that echo through the mist—though they will not speak of what answers them.  

They do not fear death. When one grows too old to walk the fen, they are laid upon a raft of buoyant reeds and released into the deeper channels. None have ever returned, but sometimes, on still nights, the Keepers hear a familiar voice humming in the distance, harmonizing with the sigh of the wind through the leaves.  

They are not a people of stories. They are a people of *presence*. To live here is to become part of the fen—to let it reshape you, to let your edges soften, to let time move through you like water through stone.  

And when outsiders come—hunters, zealots, desperate souls—the Keepers watch. They do not interfere. The fen decides who stays.  

Most drown.  

Some walk.
