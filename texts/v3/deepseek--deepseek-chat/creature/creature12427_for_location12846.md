Inhabiting this plateau is a small, reclusive community known as the **Dripletenders**. They are a people intimately connected to the land they occupy, shaped by its unfinished, shifting nature. Their existence is one of quiet stewardship, guided by a belief that they are the temporary caretakers of a world still in the act of creation. They are not builders or conquerors but aligners—those who seek harmony with the raw, embryonic forces of the plateau.

The Dripletenders are slight of frame, their bodies wiry and pale, as though they’ve adapted to the cool, damp environment. Their skin carries a faint sheen, like the moisture that clings to the rocks, and their eyes are wide and watchful, attuned to the subtle movements of water and stone. Their clothing is simple and functional, woven from the fibrous mosses that blanket the ground, dyed in muted greens and grays that blend seamlessly with their surroundings.

Their language is soft and melodic, filled with clicks and hums that mimic the dripping water and distant fauna. They speak sparingly, as if words themselves are a precious resource, and their communication often relies on gestures and the sharing of objects—small stones, moss clippings, or drops of water collected from the weeping rocks. They believe that everything on the plateau has a voice, and they listen attentively to the land’s murmurs, interpreting its rhythms and fluctuations as messages.

The Dripletenders live in the settlement at the center of the plateau, but their relationship with the structures is fluid. They move through the half-formed buildings with ease, treating them as extensions of the landscape rather than permanent dwellings. The settlement is not a place of rest but of ritual—a space where they gather to honor the plateau’s slow, patient becoming. Each building is dedicated to a specific aspect of their stewardship: one to the tending of mosses, another to the collection of water, a third to the reading of stones. The buildings are never completed, for the Dripletenders believe that to finish something is to disrupt its potential. Instead, they add to them in small, deliberate increments, shaping them in harmony with the land’s own unfinished state.

Their daily lives revolve around the maintenance of balance. They use their hands to redirect errant streams, coaxing water into pools where it can gather and evaporate. They cultivate the mosses with care, ensuring that their spread remains controlled, allowing the bare stone to breathe. They climb the jagged rock formations, collecting droplets of condensation in hollowed gourds, which they use to nourish both their bodies and the land itself. They are meticulous and unhurried, their movements slow and deliberate, as though they are moving in time with the plateau’s glacial pace.

The Dripletenders have no concept of ownership; everything belongs to the plateau. They take only what they need and give back in equal measure, leaving offerings of water, moss, or small carvings made from porous stone at the base of the rock formations. These offerings are never taken by the land but gradually dissolve, absorbed into the weeping rocks as though they were always meant to return to the earth.

Their spirituality is deeply intertwined with the plateau’s strangeness. They believe that the plateau is a place of eternal beginnings, a threshold between creation and completion. They see themselves as part of this process, their existence a small, fleeting ripple in the plateau’s slow unfolding. They revere the unseen expanse beyond the plateau’s edge, believing it to be a void from which all things emerge and to which all things return. They occasionally gather at the edge, sitting in silence and facing the vast unknown, as if communing with the void itself.

There is no leader among the Dripletenders; decisions are made collectively, guided by the land’s whispers. They hold ceremonies at the solstices and equinoxes, marking the passage of time—though time itself feels irrelevant here. These ceremonies involve the sharing of water, the reading of stones, and the singing of songs that echo the plateau’s rhythms. They believe that their songs act as a kind of thread, binding them to the land and to one another.

The Dripletenders are not entirely alone on the plateau. Occasionally, they encounter the rare fauna that inhabits the shadows—small, elusive creatures that seem to share the plateau’s strangeness. These creatures are neither hunted nor domesticated but observed with quiet reverence, as though they are messengers from the void beyond. The Dripletenders believe that their presence is a sign of the plateau’s health, a confirmation that the balance they strive to maintain is intact.

The Dripletenders are a people of patience and humility, their lives shaped by the paradoxes of the plateau: its emptiness and fullness, its rawness and lushness, its ancientness and incompleteness. They exist in harmony with its slow, steady rhythms, their stewardship a quiet act of reverence for a world still in the process of becoming.
