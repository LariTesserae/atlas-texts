**The Watcher in the Roots**  

In the City of Root and Rain, where the towers hum with forgotten resonance and the streets shift beneath one’s feet, there is a being that does not belong to the world of flesh or stone. It is called **the Tangleheart**, though few have spoken its name aloud and lived to remember the shape of it.  

The Tangleheart is neither plant nor animal, but something in between—a convergence of the city’s slow hunger and the plateau’s dreaming bones. It lives in the deepest hollows of the root-lattice, where the veins of the city knot together in a pulsing, fibrous mass. Its body is a shifting thing: a nest of blackened tendrils, slick with something like sap or sweat, threaded through with veins of pale luminescence. It has no face, no limbs in any recognizable sense, but it *watches*.  

Its eyes—if they can be called eyes—are fractures in the world. They open in the bark of trees, in the puddles that gather between roots, in the rusted seams of abandoned walls. They are not always visible, but when they are, they gleam like wet obsidian, reflecting nothing and seeing everything. To meet one’s own gaze in them is to feel the city’s memory press against the back of the skull—a whisper of centuries, of rot and regrowth, of something vast and patient stirring beneath the earth.  

The Tangleheart does not move as living things move. It *unfurls*. When it chooses to emerge—rarely, and only in the lull between rains—its presence is announced by a sound like roots tearing free from stone. It spills into the open air, a slow eruption of sinew and shadow, its form never the same twice. Sometimes it is a column of writhing filaments, studded with those black, unblinking eyes. Other times, it is a thing of gaps and absences, a silhouette that drinks the light around it, leaving only the impression of something too large to comprehend.  

It does not hunt. It does not feed in any way that can be understood. But it *takes*. Those who vanish in the City of Root and Rain are not always victims of the hungry vegetation or the whispering rains. Some are claimed by the Tangleheart, drawn into its embrace with a tenderness that is worse than violence. Their bodies are never found, but sometimes, years later, a new root will breach the surface of the lattice—thicker than the others, warmer to the touch, its bark etched with faint, familiar scars.  

The few who have encountered the Tangleheart and survived speak of a voice that is not a voice. It does not speak in words, but in *pressures*—a weight against the mind, a knowledge that arrives fully formed, without language. It tells secrets the city has forgotten. It asks questions that cannot be answered. And sometimes, if it finds a listener worthy, it gifts them a single, terrible truth:  

*The city is not dying. It is being born.*  

Then it withdraws, folding back into the roots, leaving only silence and the faint, feverish glow of its passing. The eyes close. The world steadies. And the Tangleheart returns to its vigil, waiting—always waiting—for the next unraveling of the rain.
