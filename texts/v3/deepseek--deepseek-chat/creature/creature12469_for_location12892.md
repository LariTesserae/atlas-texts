In this sodden, half-sunken hamlet, the inhabitants are as much a part of the marsh as the reeds and the creeping water. They are a quiet, wiry people, their skin weathered by damp and chill, their movements deliberate and unhurried, as if they have learned to conserve energy against the perpetual drag of wet earth. Their eyes are sharp, accustomed to picking out movement in the dim, mist-hazed light—whether it be the flicker of a fish beneath the water’s surface or the shift of a neighbor’s shadow between the close-packed buildings.  

They dress in layers of oiled cloth and rough-spun wool, garments stained the muted greens and browns of the marsh, seams reinforced against the damp. Their boots are tall, made of treated leather or layered reed-woven soles, designed to keep the water at bay for as long as possible. Even so, their hems are perpetually darkened with moisture, their fingers pruned from the ever-present wet.  

Their homes are as much shelters against the water as they are extensions of it. The floors are raised on stilts or packed stone, the walls lined with layers of moss and clay to keep the damp from seeping in. Inside, the air is thick with the scent of peat smoke, drying herbs, and the faint, briny tang of preserved fish. They burn marsh-grass and peat in small, efficient hearths, the smoke curling up through holes in the sod roofs. Every surface is used: hooks and shelves hold tools, nets, bundles of reeds for weaving, jars of pickled roots and river snails. Nothing is wasted.  

They are a people of few words, not out of secrecy, but because speech here feels unnecessary—the marsh speaks for them, in the drip of water, the sigh of reeds, the distant call of a heron. When they do speak, their voices are low, their words practical. They trade in gestures, in the tilt of a head toward a shifting current, in the way they pause to listen to the wind before setting out in their shallow, flat-bottomed boats.  

Their work is dictated by the water. They are fishers, trappers, gatherers of reeds and medicinal mosses. They know the hidden paths through the marsh, the places where the ground will hold and where it will swallow you whole. They set woven traps for eels and frogs, their hands moving deftly even in the cold. Some craft tools from bone and water-smoothed stone; others mend nets or weave baskets from the tough, pliable rushes that grow at the marsh’s edge.  

They are superstitious in subtle ways. They leave offerings of dried fish or bundles of herbs at the thresholds of their homes, small wards against the marsh’s hunger. They speak of things seen in the mist—shapes that move just beyond vision, ripples with no source. Some say the marsh takes people, now and then, not out of malice, but because it forgets the difference between what belongs to it and what does not.  

Children here learn to move quietly, to read the water’s moods, to listen for the warning creak of unstable ground. They play games of balance on the half-submerged logs, learn to knot ropes before they can write their names. They are raised with the understanding that the marsh is both home and adversary—it feeds them, and it will one day reclaim them.  

There is no leader, not in the traditional sense. Decisions are made by those who know the land best, whose instincts have kept them alive the longest. Disputes are settled quietly, often with a shared task—repairing a wall, bailing out a flooded cellar—as if to remind everyone that survival here is communal. Outsiders are rare, and when they come, they are watched carefully, not with hostility, but with the wariness of people who know that one misstep can unravel the careful balance of their lives.  

They do not fear the water. They respect it. They live with it, in it, because they have no other choice. And in return, the marsh sustains them, in its own slow, grudging way.
