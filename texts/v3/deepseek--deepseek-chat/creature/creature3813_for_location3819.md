The inhabitants of Vhul, the Weeping Caverns, are a hardy yet enigmatic people, shaped by the oppressive environment they call home. They are known as the **Dripfolk**, a term born of both derision and respect, for they are as much a part of the caverns as the water that seeps through the stone.  

The Dripfolk are pale and lean, their skin perpetually damp and glistening, their eyes adapted to the dim light, glowing faintly like the lichen that clings to the walls. Their hair is dark and oily, often slicked back to keep it from their faces as they work. They wear clothing patched together from salvaged materials—waxed canvas, rubber, and scraps of metal—each piece treated to repel water and resist decay. Their hands are calloused from handling tools and machinery, their fingers nimble from years of repairing the ever-rusting mechanisms that sustain their city.  

Their culture revolves around water—its collection, its purification, its containment. They are engineers, plumbers, and alchemists, their lives dedicated to taming the relentless moisture that threatens to consume them. They worship no gods but instead venerate the **Silent Flow**, the unseen force they believe governs the movement of water through the caverns. They hold rituals at the edges of the runoff pools, offering small carvings of stone or metal as tokens of gratitude and supplication.  

The Dripfolk are a quiet people, their voices rarely rising above the ambient hum of the cavern. They communicate as much through touch and gesture as through words, their language a blend of spoken phrases and intricate hand signs. Their art is utilitarian yet beautiful—intricate pipe systems that twist and wind like vines, murals etched into the stone depicting the history of their struggle against the water.  

They live in small, tightly-knit communities within the cavern-city, each family unit occupying a section carved into the rock. Their homes are dry and warm, insulated from the dampness by layers of tarred fabric and heated by steam piped from the underground generators. They grow food in hydroponic gardens, their crops nourished by the mineral-rich water and tended with great care.  

The Dripfolk are not entirely alone in the caverns. They share their space with the creatures of Vhul—the blind, pale crustaceans they call **Dribbles**, which they harvest for food and shells, and the bat-like **Murkwings**, which they tolerate but do not trust. There are rumors of deeper, stranger beings—shapes that move in the shadows, whispers of something ancient and half-formed lurking in the darkest corners of the caverns. The Dripfolk speak of these entities rarely and in hushed tones, carving wards into the stone to keep them at bay.  

Life in Vhul is a constant negotiation—a balancing act between harnessing the moisture and preventing it from consuming everything. The Dripfolk are resilient, resourceful, and deeply connected to their environment, their existence a testament to the quiet tenacity of life in the face of unrelenting pressure.  

And so, they persist, moving through the damp corridors and dripping halls, their lives intertwined with the caverns, their fate tied to the Silent Flow. They wait, they work, and they endure, knowing that the water will have its way—and that they must find a way to survive it.
