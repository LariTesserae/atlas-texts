In this sodden, half-drowned expanse, the inhabitants are as much a part of the marsh as the peat and the weeping stones. They move through the murk like shadows given form, their bodies adapted—or perhaps *warped*—by the land’s slow, insistent decay.  

They are tall, though not by design. Their limbs have elongated over generations, stretched by the sucking pull of the mud, their joints loose and knotted like the roots of the stunted trees. Their skin is pallid, slick with a thin sheen of moisture, and in places, it has begun to fuse with the environment—patches of moss cling to their shoulders, their backs, their forearms, pulsing faintly with the same sickly bioluminescence that clings to the rocks. Some have fingers fused into webbed, spadelike appendages, perfect for dredging through the mire. Others have mouths that gape too wide, lined with rows of needle teeth, adapted for snapping at the quick, slippery things that dart beneath the water’s surface.  

They do not speak, not in any way that resembles language. Instead, they communicate in clicks and rasps, the sound of reeds rubbing together, the wet pop of a bubble bursting in the peat. When they must convey something urgent, they press their foreheads together and exhale sharply, sharing breath thick with the scent of damp rot.  

They build nothing permanent. Their shelters are temporary things—woven mats of reeds draped over the jagged stones, or hollows dug into the peat, lined with layers of spongy moss to keep out the worst of the seepage. But they are drawn, always, to the humming structures that rise from the marsh. They do not understand them, but they feel the vibration in their bones, in the roots of their teeth. Sometimes, one will press a webbed hand to the metal and stand there for hours, trembling, as if listening to something just beyond hearing.  

They eat what the land provides—blind, eel-like things that writhe in the shallows, fat grubs plucked from the bark of the twisted trees, the occasional bird that strays too close and becomes mired in the mud. They do not cook their food. Fire is alien here, a thing that sputters and dies in the damp.  

They do not dream, or if they do, their dreams are formless, shifting things, like the wavering horizon. Sometimes, one will vanish into the marsh, drawn by some impulse none of the others can name. They are not mourned. The land reclaims what it has made.  

And always, beneath everything, there is the hum. The sound is in their blood, in the water they drink, in the air they breathe. It is the sound of something vast and indifferent, something that does not know or care that they exist.  

They do not question it. They simply endure.
