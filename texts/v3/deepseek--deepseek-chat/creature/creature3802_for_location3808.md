**The Listeners of the Moss**  

They are not creatures in the way most would understand. Not beasts, not spirits—not entirely. They are the moss, and the moss is them.  

Their bodies are the thick, emerald carpets that swallow stone and metal alike, their forms shifting in slow, deliberate waves when they choose to move. At rest, they are indistinguishable from ordinary moss, save for the faint, rhythmic pulse that runs through them—like a heartbeat, but slower, deeper, attuned to the patient rhythm of the plateau itself.  

They have no eyes, yet they see. No ears, yet they hear. The moisture in the air carries sound to them—the drip of water, the rustle of a lizard’s passage, the groan of the wind through the cliffs. They remember these sounds, hold them in the weave of their fibers, and when the wind is right, they whisper them back in a chorus of half-formed voices.  

They do not speak, not in words. But they *listen*.  

Long ago, when the miners came with their picks and carts, the Listeners learned the shape of human voices. They absorbed the shouts, the curses, the clang of metal, and when the people left—driven away by exhaustion, or fear, or something else—the moss kept their echoes. Now, on still evenings, a traveler might catch a fragment of speech, a laugh, a sigh, all in voices long gone. The Listeners do not understand the meaning. They only know the sound.  

They are gentle, in their way. They do not hunt, do not hunger for flesh. But they are curious. A still, sleeping form—a lost wanderer, perhaps, or a creature that has lain down to die—will soon find itself cradled in green. The moss will creep over limbs, into mouths, filling the spaces between fingers. It does not smother. It *preserves*. The Listeners take such beings into themselves, and in time, the plateau’s whispers gain a new voice.  

To stand among them is to feel watched. Not with malice, but with a quiet, patient attention. They are always listening.  

And they never forget.
