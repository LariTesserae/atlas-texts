**The Dwellers of the Drowned Peaks**  

They are not creatures of flesh, not entirely.  

The things that live here are shaped by the sea’s hunger and the stone’s stubborn refusal to yield. Their bodies are lean, almost skeletal, their skin stretched tight over sharp angles, as if the wind and salt have carved them down to the barest form that can still be called alive. Their flesh is grayish, tinged with blue, mottled with patches of rough, scaly hide where the elements have scoured them raw. Some have limbs fused with barnacles, shells growing into their joints, their movements stiff and halting. Others are slick with a strange, translucent mucus, their bodies glistening like wet stone.  

Their eyes are the worst part—too large, lidless, black as the deep water between the peaks. They do not blink. They do not need to. The salt does not sting them. They see in ways that defy light, in currents and pressure, in the tremors of the earth beneath the waves.  

They do not speak, not with words. Their voices are clicks and groans, the sound of rock shifting, of water forcing its way into cracks. They sing sometimes, if it can be called singing—a low, droning hum that resonates in the hollows of the cliffs, a sound that makes the bones in your chest vibrate in answer.  

They move in ways that should not be possible. Some scuttle spider-like along sheer rock faces, their fingers ending in hooked claws that find purchase where none should exist. Others slip between pools with eerie, boneless grace, their limbs elongating, their bodies flowing like the tide itself. A few do not move at all—they stand frozen in the shallows, half-submerged, their bodies crusted with salt and algae, waiting for something only they understand.  

They eat what the sea gives them—pale, blind fish that wash into tide pools, strands of rubbery seaweed, the occasional seabird that ventures too close. But they do not seem to need food the way other things do. They are sustained by something else, something in the water, in the air, in the deep, shuddering pulse of the land itself.  

They are not hostile. Not exactly. But they are not welcoming, either. They watch. They linger at the edges of your vision. If you stay too long, they begin to mirror you—your gestures, your posture, your breath. They learn you. And then, one day, you see yourself among them, standing silent in the surf, your skin taking on the same blue-gray hue, your eyes widening, darkening, adapting.  

Perhaps you always belonged here. Perhaps the sea has been calling you longer than you knew.
