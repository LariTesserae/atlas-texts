In this strange, contradictory plateau, the beings who inhabit it are as much a part of the landscape as the glassy plants and the smooth, shimmering ground. They are neither flora nor fauna, but something in between—entities that blur the line between the biological and the mechanical, the organic and the manufactured. They are the architects of this place, though their intentions are as enigmatic as the landscape itself.

These beings—if they can be called such—are difficult to perceive clearly. They seem to exist at the edge of vision, shifting and shimmering like heat haze or the faint distortion of light through glass. Their forms are fluid, changing with the angle of observation, but some patterns emerge. When still, they appear as tall, slender columns, translucent and reflective, their surfaces etched with faint geometric patterns that glow softly in the unchanging light. When they move, they dissolve into a kind of liquid motion, flowing across the ground like mercury, their shapes constantly reconfiguring into spirals, fractals, and other precise geometries. They leave no footprints on the smooth, obsidian-like surface, yet their presence leaves a faint residue in the air, a metallic scent that mingles with the tang already present.

They are solitary creatures, each occupying its own vast territory, much like the clusters of vegetation. They are never seen interacting, yet there is a sense of connection between them, as though they communicate through the faint hum that permeates the plateau. This hum grows subtly louder when they are near, as though they are amplifying it, orchestrating it into a kind of language or song that is felt more than heard. It is a sound that vibrates in the bones, a low, steady pulse that seems to echo the rhythm of the plants and the slow, inevitable processes shaping the landscape.

These beings do not seem to eat, sleep, or reproduce in any conventional sense. Instead, they appear to exist in a state of constant creation and transformation. They move among the clusters of plants, their fluid forms brushing against the glassy towers and fractal fronds. When they do, the plants respond, pulsing brighter, their colors shifting slightly, as though exchanging information or energy. The ground beneath them shimmers more intensely, as though it, too, is part of this exchange.

Their purpose is unclear, but their actions suggest a kind of stewardship or curation. They seem to be tending to the plateau, guiding its slow, mechanical evolution. They are not builders in the traditional sense—there are no structures or tools—but their presence feels deliberate, as though they are directing the forces that shape this place. Perhaps they are the embodiment of those forces, manifesting as caretakers or guardians of a process that is both natural and unnatural.

They are not hostile, nor are they welcoming. They acknowledge intruders only indirectly, their forms shimmering faintly in the distance, never approaching but never retreating either. The hum grows louder when a newcomer arrives, as though the plateau itself is assessing the presence, determining whether it belongs. There is no sense of danger, only a vast, patient indifference, as though the beings and the landscape exist on a scale of time and purpose that renders human concerns irrelevant.

Their existence is a paradox, much like the plateau itself. They are both part of the landscape and distinct from it, shaping it and being shaped by it in turn. They are alive, yet their vitality is so alien that it defies categorization. They are silent, yet their presence resonates in the air, the ground, the plants, and the hum that binds it all together. They are the unseen hands of this place, the quiet architects of a world in the process of becoming—a world that exists for reasons we cannot fathom and in ways we can scarcely perceive.
