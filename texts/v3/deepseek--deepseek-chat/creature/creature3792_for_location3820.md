**The Inhabitants of the Liminal Wetland**  

They call themselves the **Drownborn**, though they do not speak in any language that could be transcribed. Their voices are the slow drip of water from stone, the sigh of reeds bending in the wind, the resonant hum that thrums beneath the earth. They are the marsh, and the marsh is them.  

### **Form & Movement**  
The Drownborn are not solid, not entirely. Their bodies are shifting amalgamations of water, peat, and bioluminescent algae, held together by some unseen force. When they move, they do so in ripples—a slow, deliberate unfolding, like ink dispersing in water. One moment, they might appear as a humanoid figure, limbs elongated and translucent; the next, they dissolve into the marsh itself, becoming nothing more than a disturbance in the reeds or a ripple in the standing pools.  

Their faces, when they choose to have them, are smooth and featureless save for faint impressions—hollows where eyes might be, a suggestion of a mouth that never opens. Some wear masks of hardened peat, carved with spiraling glyphs that shift when not observed.  

### **Behavior & Purpose**  
They do not eat, nor do they sleep. Instead, they **tend**. The Drownborn move through the wetland like gardeners of some vast, living machine, adjusting the flow of water, coaxing the glowing moss to spread, guiding the paths of the lesser creatures that flit through the reeds. Their purpose is obscure, but it is tied to the hum beneath the earth—the deep, resonant frequency that seems to govern the marsh’s unnatural cycles.  

Occasionally, they gather in the shallows, forming concentric circles as if in ritual. Their bodies merge, becoming a single, undulating mass, and the water around them shudders, reflecting not the gray sky above but something else—a fractured, inverted world where the rules are different. Then, just as suddenly, they disperse, returning to their solitary wanderings.  

### **Interactions with Outsiders**  
The Drownborn do not seem hostile, but neither are they welcoming. They regard intruders with a detached curiosity, sometimes following at a distance, their presence marked only by the sudden stillness of the water or the faint glow of algae where none was before.  

Those who stay too long begin to notice things:  
- Their reflection in the water does not match their movements.  
- The air tastes of copper, and their thoughts grow sluggish, heavy.  
- The Drownborn begin to resemble them—their faceless forms subtly shifting, adopting the visitor’s silhouette before melting away again.  

It is said that if you sleep in the marsh, you might wake to find one standing over you, its body bending like a reed in the wind, studying you with whatever passes for eyes. And if you meet its gaze for too long, you start to hear the hum—not just around you, but inside you, in your bones, in your blood.  

Then, perhaps, you become part of the tending.  

### **Lesser Creatures**  
The Drownborn are not alone in the wetland. Other things live here, though none seem wholly natural:  
- **Glidemoths** – Winged, translucent creatures that skim the water’s surface, their bodies refracting light into prismatic shards. They leave no ripples.  
- **Peat Worms** – Serpentine, segmented beings that burrow through the sodden earth, their bodies lined with glowing nodes. They hum in harmony with the marsh’s deeper resonance.  
- **Echo Crawlers** – Small, many-legged things that scuttle across the weeping rocks. They mimic sounds—sometimes your own voice, sometimes something much older.  

### **The Unspoken Truth**  
The wetland is not a place. It is a **process**. The Drownborn are its custodians, its living components, ensuring that whatever ancient mechanism lies beneath the peat continues its slow, inexorable work.  

And sometimes, when the reflections in the water grow too clear, when the hum rises to a deafening thrum, you realize—  

You were never supposed to see this.
