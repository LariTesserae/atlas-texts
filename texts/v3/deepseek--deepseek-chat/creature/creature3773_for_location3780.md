In the heart of the High Wet City of Veyl, its inhabitants embody the same slow, deliberate pace that defines their environment. They are a people of subtlety, their lives intertwined with the city's weaving canals and damp air. Their skin, pale and luminous, seems to absorb the diffuse light, while their eyes, often a muted gray or soft green, reflect the quiet persistence of the mist that envelops them.  

The Veysians, as they are called, are neither tall nor short, their stature balanced like the city itself. They move with a fluid grace, as if their bodies are extensions of the water that surrounds them. Their clothing is simple yet functional, woven from fibers harvested from the sparse mountain palms and dyed in earthy tones—greens, grays, and muted browns. Their garments are loose, allowing the damp air to circulate, and their feet are often bare, soles toughened by years of walking on smooth stone and reed bridges.  

Their culture is one of adaptation and harmony. They are craftspeople by necessity, shaping stone and wood into tools and structures that withstand the perpetual moisture. Their artistry lies in their precision, their ability to carve intricate designs into basalt walls or weave complex patterns into the reed bridges that connect their city. Their pottery, fired in kilns during the rare dry spells, is simple yet elegant—rounded vessels with smooth surfaces, often adorned with spirals and ripples that echo the flow of water.  

Veysian society is communal, with decisions made through consensus rather than hierarchy. They gather in the broad plazas of the upper districts, where the air is clearer, to discuss matters of importance or simply to share stories. Their voices are soft, their words measured, as if they understand the weight of each syllable in a world where sound carries so easily. Music is rare, but when it is played, it is hauntingly beautiful—flutes carved from bone or wood, their melodies drifting through the mist like echoes of the mountain itself.  

Their spirituality is deeply tied to the land and water. They see the mountain as a living entity, its slow exhalations of moisture a sign of its contemplation. They offer small tokens—carved stones, woven garlands—at the edges of canals or at the base of springs, honoring the forces that sustain them. Their rituals are quiet affairs, conducted at dawn or dusk when the mist is thickest, and they speak softly to the water as if it listens.  

The Veysians are not prolific breeders, their population stable and small, reflecting the city’s equilibrium. Children are rare but treasured, raised communally and taught the skills needed to maintain their delicate balance with the land. They are curious but restrained, their games mimicking the tasks of adulthood—carving small stones, weaving tiny baskets, or guiding makeshift boats down the canals.  

There is no hunger in Veyl, no scarcity. The few crops they grow—tubers, hardy greens, and the fruit of the mountain palms—are supplemented by fish that swim in the deeper pools of the canals. Their diet is simple but nourishing, their meals shared in communal gatherings where silence often speaks louder than words.  

The Veysians are not isolationists, but their contact with the outside world is rare and deliberate. Traders sometimes ascend the mountain paths, bringing metals or tools, but the Veysians trade only what they can spare—a few vessels, some woven fiber, or medicinal herbs harvested from the slopes. They are indifferent to the rush of the lowlands, content in their slow, quiet existence.  

In the end, the Veysians are as much a part of Veyl as the stone and water that shape it. They are its caretakers, its interpreters, its quiet witnesses to a land that is still deciding what it will become. Their lives are a testament to patience, to the art of living in harmony with a world that does not hurry but simply *is*.
