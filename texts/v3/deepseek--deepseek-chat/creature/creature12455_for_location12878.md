### **The Keepers of the Steam Gardens**  

They do not have names, for names are things given by others, and no one has spoken to them in centuries.  

They are tall, though not uniformly so—some stretch nearly eight feet, their elongated limbs wrapped in layers of fibrous, heat-resistant tissue that resembles both bark and old rubber insulation. Others are stunted, hunched, their backs curved from generations of navigating the low-ceilinged tunnels. Their skin is pallid, nearly translucent, threaded with veins that glow faintly blue where the bioluminescent fungi have taken root beneath their flesh.  

Their faces are smooth, devoid of features save for a single, vertical slit where a mouth might be. They do not speak, not in any way that requires sound. Instead, they communicate through vibrations—through the hum of the pipes, the shudder of steam vents, the rhythmic tapping of fingers against stone. Their hands are long, fingers fused in places, ending in hardened, chitinous tips perfect for scraping away mineral deposits or adjusting the flow of water through ancient valves.  

They move slowly, deliberately, as if every step is part of some greater choreography only they understand. Their purpose is maintenance. Preservation. They tend to the steam vents, clearing blockages, redirecting flows, ensuring the cavern does not flood or overheat. They harvest the rubbery tendrils that grow along the walls, peeling them back in strips to reveal fresh, glistening flesh beneath—flesh that they press into cracks in the machinery, sealing leaks, reinforcing weak points.  

They do not eat, not in any conventional sense. They absorb. Their bodies take in moisture from the air, nutrients from the mineral-rich water, energy from the heat itself. When one grows too old, too brittle to continue its work, it simply kneels at the edge of a steaming pool and allows itself to dissolve, its body breaking down into the same gelatinous matter that coats the walls.  

They do not sleep. They do not dream.  

But sometimes, when the steam is thick and the hum of the pipes reaches a certain pitch, they pause. They tilt their heads, as if listening to something beyond the stone. And for a moment—just a moment—they seem to remember.  

Then the moment passes. The work continues.  

The garden grows.
