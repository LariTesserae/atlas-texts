In this place, the beings that exist are as much a part of the environment as the air, the water, and the crumbling structures. They are not separate from the world but woven into its fabric, their forms and rhythms dictated by the same forces that shape the land.

The inhabitants are spectral, their bodies translucent and faintly luminous, shimmering with the same iridescence as the fungus that clings to the twisted stalks. They are neither solid nor entirely ephemeral, their forms shifting and flowing like water, taking on new shapes as they move. At times, they resemble humanoid figures, tall and slender, with elongated limbs that ripple as though caught in an unseen current. At other moments, they dissolve into amorphous clouds of mist, drifting lazily through the air before coalescing again into something vaguely recognizable.

Their faces, if they can be called faces, are indistinct, composed of shifting patterns of light and shadow that suggest features without ever fully committing to them. Eyes flicker into existence, only to dissolve into nothingness; mouths form and vanish, leaving behind only the faintest echo of expression. They do not speak, at least not in any language recognizable as such. Instead, they communicate through subtle shifts in their luminescence, their forms pulsing softly in patterns that carry meaning known only to them. The air hums faintly when they gather, their silent exchanges reverberating through the dampness.

These beings move slowly, deliberately, as though navigating a world that resists haste. Their steps—if they even have steps—are measured, their paths winding and erratic, following channels carved into the muck or tracing the edges of the weeping buildings. They seem to be drawn to the fungus, pausing frequently to hover near the iridescent clusters, their forms blending with the soft glow. It is unclear whether they feed on the fungus or are somehow sustained by it, but their connection to it is undeniable.

They are attuned to the rhythms of the land, moving in harmony with the subtle shifts of water and earth. When the ground breathes—pooling and receding in its quiet, cyclical pulse—they respond, their forms shifting in ways that mimic the land’s movements. They seem to sense the presence of intruders long before they are seen, their bodies rippling faintly in acknowledgment, though they rarely approach directly. Instead, they observe from a distance, their attention felt more than seen, their presence a quiet weight in the air.

These beings do not age, at least not in any conventional sense. Their existence is tied to the fluidity of the world around them, their forms adapting and changing as the landscape itself transforms. They are neither alive nor dead but somewhere in between, existing in a state of perpetual becoming. They are the embodiment of the world’s mutability, its constant, quiet unraveling and reconstitution.

Their purpose, if they have one, is unclear. They do not build, farm, or create in any recognizable way. Instead, they seem to exist in symbiosis with the environment, their presence a part of the world’s slow, inexorable transformation. They are neither hostile nor welcoming, merely indifferent to anything that does not belong to the rhythm of the land.

These beings are the quiet guardians of this place, though “guardian” may not be the right word. They are its inhabitants, its expression, its soul—if such a thing can be said to exist here. They are as much a part of the landscape as the dripping walls, the brittle stalks, and the shimmering fungus. To encounter them is to glimpse the essence of this world: a place where the boundaries between life and death, solid and fluid, self and other have dissolved into a single, incomprehensible whole.
