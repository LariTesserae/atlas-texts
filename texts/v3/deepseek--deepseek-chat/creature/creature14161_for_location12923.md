In this hidden pocket of life, the inhabitants are as much a part of the landscape as the moss-cloaked rocks and gnarled roots. They are the **Stone-Tenders**, a reclusive people who have adapted to this strange, waterless oasis over generations. Their bodies are lean and wiry, their skin darkened by the faint, filtered light and etched with faint, lichen-like patterns—either tattoos or some symbiotic growth, it’s hard to tell. Their eyes are large, pupils wide to drink in the dimness, and they move with a quiet, deliberate grace, as if they, too, are carved from the same ancient stone.  

They wear little, only draped layers of woven moss and cured vine fibers, garments that seem to breathe with the humidity. Some adorn themselves with polished shale pendants or beetle-shell ornaments that click softly as they walk. Their hands are their most striking feature—long-fingered, knotted with muscle, their nails hardened into something between claw and tool, perfect for scraping lichen, prying roots, or etching marks into the soft rock faces.  

The Stone-Tenders do not speak, at least not in any way outsiders would recognize. Instead, they communicate through a language of touch—fingertips brushing moss, palms pressed to stone, knuckles rapping hollow points in the rock to send vibrations through the chamber. The walls themselves seem to answer; the vines shiver, the insects hum in response. They are attuned to the pulse of this place, listening to its slow, crumbling breath.  

They live in symbiosis with the life around them. The vines that coil up their huts are not just decoration—they are cultivated, their broad leaves harvested for moisture, their fibrous stems unraveled into cordage. The moss is their bedding, their insulation, their medicine. The insects are both pest and pantry, carefully managed, their larvae harvested from the softest patches of decay. Even the trees are partners—their roots guided along specific paths, their sap tapped in careful, ritualistic increments.  

At the center of their settlement, the fire pit burns not wood, but something denser, darker—compacted layers of ancient moss and fungal growth, slow-burning and smokeless. Around it, they gather in silence, pressing hands to the ground as if listening. Perhaps they are. The plateau is old, and old things remember.  

They fear nothing from the outside world because the outside world does not know they exist. But they fear the slow death of their home—the way the rocks crumble a little more each season, the way the vines grow thinner, the way the air feels heavier, as if the chamber itself is sighing toward its end.  

They are the last keepers of a place that was never meant to last. And when the final collapse comes, they will go quietly, leaving only handprints on the stone and the echoes of their tapping, tapping, tapping—a language no one else will ever understand.
