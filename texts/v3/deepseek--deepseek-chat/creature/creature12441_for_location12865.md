Here, in this quiet expanse of damp stillness, the inhabitants are not creatures of flesh or movement, but of slow persistence—entities that have adapted to the unchanging hum of the land.  

They are the **Glassbound**, though no one names them. Their forms are not organic in any familiar sense, but neither are they machine. They emerge from the black volcanic glass, their bodies a fusion of mineral and something else—something that thrums in time with the distant industrial drone. When the light strikes them just so, their edges shimmer with prismatic fractures, revealing networks of fine, vein-like filaments beneath the surface. They do not walk, but they are not entirely still. They shift, infinitesimally, over centuries, their glassy limbs extending toward the faintly glowing fungal colonies as if in some unspoken exchange.  

The fungi, too, are more than they seem. They are not alive, not in the way life is measured elsewhere, but they are not inert. Their pulsing glow is a response—not to light or heat, but to the vibrations in the air, the hum of the unseen engines. They absorb the resonance, metabolize it in some unfathomable way, and in turn, the Glassbound absorb *them*, drawing the faint energy into their crystalline structures.  

There are no predators here, no prey. No cycles of consumption, only an endless, silent transaction between glass and fungus, vibration and reflection. The Glassbound do not think, but they *remember*—not in thoughts, but in the slow accumulation of fractures in their forms, each crack a record of some distant shift in the hum, some tremor in the equilibrium.  

And sometimes, very rarely, one of them shatters.  

When it happens, it is not a death, but a dispersal. The fragments do not lie still. They slide, almost imperceptibly, toward the nearest pool of water, where they dissolve into the faintly luminous film on its surface. Days or years later, new Glassbound emerge—smooth, unblemished, their forms slightly altered, as if the land itself is refining them through some vast, patient alchemy.  

They are the only inhabitants. They do not speak, do not hunger, do not dream. They simply *are*, as much a part of the landscape as the black glass and the humming air. And the landscape, in turn, is shaped by them—their slow movements etching microscopic trails in the stone, their presence a silent answer to the unending industrial whisper.  

This is not a place for life as it is known elsewhere. It is a place of echoes, of adaptations to a world that has settled into perfect, eternal stillness. The Glassbound are not alive. But they endure.
