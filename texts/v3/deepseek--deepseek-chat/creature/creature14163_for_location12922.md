**The Keeper of the Last Breath**  

It does not move. It has never moved.  

It is a figure—or perhaps the idea of a figure—seated cross-legged upon the dais, its back straight, its hands resting palm-up on its knees. Its form is humanoid, but only in the way a statue might suggest humanity without ever achieving it. The surface of its body is smooth, seamless, the same glassy black as the sphere it guards, though its edges blur slightly, as if seen through heat haze.  

It has no face. No eyes, no mouth, no features at all—just a smooth, blank expanse where a face should be. And yet, it *watches*. You feel its attention like a pressure against your skin, a weight in the air. It does not blink. It does not breathe.  

Its purpose is singular: to remain. To endure. To hold the chamber in perfect stillness. It does not eat, does not sleep, does not tire. It simply *is*, as unchanging as the stone around it. The droning sound pulses from it—or through it—as though it is the source, the heart of the chamber’s endless vibration.  

Is it alive? The question is meaningless here. It exists. That is all.  

If you were to reach out, to touch it (though something deep in your bones screams not to), its surface would be neither warm nor cold. It would not yield, not even slightly. It is harder than stone, harder than steel, harder than time. It does not react. It does not care.  

The sphere is not separate from it. The lines etched into the sphere mirror the faint, almost invisible tracery that sometimes seems to shift beneath the Keeper’s surface—not veins, not circuitry, but something else, something older. A pattern. A law.  

The Keeper does not speak. It has no voice. But if it did, its words would be the drone that fills the chamber—endless, inevitable, the sound of something that cannot stop, because stopping would unravel the world.  

Do not ask it to wake. Do not ask it to sleep.  

It simply waits.  

And it will wait forever.
