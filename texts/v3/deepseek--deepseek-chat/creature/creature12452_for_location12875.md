In this sodden, half-lit expanse, the inhabitants are not creatures of flesh and blood—not in the way you might recognize. They are beings of seepage and condensation, forms sculpted from the very dampness that defines their world.  

They move like liquid given reluctant shape, their bodies composed of the same viscous moisture that clings to the rocks and fungal growths. When they pass, they leave behind trails of glistening residue, thin threads of water that bead and merge back into the landscape. Their forms are unstable, shifting with the humidity—sometimes tall and spindly, their limbs stretching like rivulets down a stone face; other times, they slump into amorphous puddles, barely distinguishable from the wet earth beneath them.  

Their faces—if they can be called that—are smooth, featureless planes, reflecting the dim light in distorted ripples. They have no eyes, yet they perceive. No mouths, yet they communicate in low, resonant pulses that harmonize with the ambient hum of the land. Their voices are the sound of water dripping into still pools, the groan of shifting stone, the whisper of mist against damp surfaces.  

They do not hunt. They do not eat. Instead, they gather—absorbing the condensation from the air, the weeping moisture of the rocks, the slow exhalations of the fungal growths. They are less like organisms and more like processes, manifestations of the landscape’s perpetual wetness. They do not sleep, but they do still, merging with the terrain for long stretches, becoming indistinguishable from the land itself.  

Occasionally, they gather near the strange, humming structures, pressing their liquid forms against the surfaces as if listening. Perhaps they are. Perhaps the structures speak to them in frequencies beyond comprehension. Or perhaps they are merely drawn to the vibrations, like water seeking the lowest point.  

They are not hostile. They are not benevolent. They simply *are*, as much a part of the landscape as the rocks, the mist, the fungal sprawl. To encounter one is to feel the uncanny sensation of being watched by something that has no eyes—to be acknowledged by a presence that is neither alive nor dead, but existing in some liminal state between.  

If you stand still long enough, the dampness will begin to cling to you too. Your breath will fog the air, your skin will bead with moisture, and for a fleeting moment, you might wonder if you, too, are becoming part of this slow, wet world.  

And perhaps, in time, you would.
