In **The Drowned Furnace**, the inhabitants are as much a product of the environment as the blackened iron and salt-crusted stone itself. They are not merely survivors of this sunken, steaming city—they are its inheritors, shaped by its decay, its heat, and its ceaseless, creeping water.  

The **Scrimshaw Folk**, as they call themselves, are a people of adaptation, their bodies and culture molded by the relentless damp and the corrosive air. Their skin is pale from the lack of sun, but hardened by exposure to brine and ash, forming a patina-like layer that resists the worst of the corrosion. Their hands are calloused and stained, their fingertips worn smooth from decades of handling rusted metal and scalding pipes. They wear layers of waxed cloth and rubber, scavenged from the remnants of the city’s industrial past, their garments pieced together like armor, stitched with threads of sinew and steel. Their faces are obscured by goggles with lenses of salvaged quartz, designed to protect against the acidic steam, and their mouths are often muffled by scarves soaked in fetid water, a desperate attempt to filter the bitter air.  

The Scrimshaw Folk are led by the **Irondrinkers**, a caste of elders whose bodies have been transformed by lifetimes of inhaling metal-laden air and drinking water thick with rust. Their skin has a metallic sheen, their veins visible beneath the surface like lines of ore in rock. They claim to hear the heartbeat of the city itself, a deep, grinding rhythm that guides their decisions. They speak in riddles and hums, their voices carrying the resonance of corroded bells, and they are both revered and feared for their ability to predict the city’s slow collapse.  

Below the Scrimshaw Folk are the **Ashborn**, outcast scavengers who live in the city’s driest corners—if such places still exist. They are emaciated figures, their faces gaunt and their eyes hollow, their bodies coated in a fine layer of ash that clings to them like a second skin. They are experts at navigating the labyrinth of submerged streets and collapsing buildings, searching for scraps of metal, coal, or anything that can be burned in the furnaces. They are whispered to be cannibals, though whether this is true or merely a myth born of desperation, no one can say.  

And then there are the **Pipewalkers**, a secretive guild of mechanics and engineers who dedicate their lives to maintaining the city’s failing systems. They are amphibious creatures, spending as much time underwater as above, their bodies protected by suits of cured leather and reinforced with brass plating. They wield tools forged from salvaged steel, their movements precise and practiced as they patch leaking pipes, clear clogged vents, and coax life from the groaning machines. They are the closest thing the city has to a priesthood, their rituals centered around the furnaces that still burn despite the rising waters.  

The creatures that live alongside the Scrimshaw Folk are as much a part of the city’s ecosystem as the people themselves. The **Rustmaws**, armored crabs with carapaces of oxidized metal, are both predators and scavengers, feeding on anything that falls into the flooded streets, including the unwary or unfortunate. The **Smokeswifts**, leather-winged birds with needle-like beaks, are drawn to the chimneys and vents, where they pick at the remnants of machinery and nest in the corroded hollows of the city’s tallest structures. And then there are the **Ironveins**, eels that writhe through the water, their bodies shimmering with oil-slick hues, their flesh said to carry a metallic tang that some of the Ashborn claim grants visions of the city’s past.  

Life in The Drowned Furnace is a constant struggle against entropy, a battle waged in the shadow of decay. The Scrimshaw Folk know their city is dying, sinking deeper into the mire with each passing year, but they persist. They persist because they must, because the furnaces still burn, because the machines still turn, because the city’s heartbeat—though faint—has not yet fallen silent. They are a people forged in the crucible of corrosion, and their existence is a testament to the stubbornness of life, even in the face of inevitable ruin.
