Here is the entity that inhabits this plateau:  

### **The Glass-Eaters**  

They are tall, slender figures, their bodies composed of the same volcanic glass that forms the plateau, though theirs is not static—it shifts, reconfigures, fractures and reforms in slow, deliberate motions. Their limbs are elongated, jointless, flowing like liquid obsidian when they move, yet brittle and sharp when still. Their faces are smooth planes, featureless except for a single vertical seam where a mouth might be—a thin crack that occasionally parts to exhale sulfurous vapor or to ingest the bioluminescent fungi in slow, measured bites.  

They do not speak, not in any way that sound carries. Instead, they communicate through vibrations in the glass beneath them, sending pulses of resonance across the plateau that others of their kind interpret as meaning. Their language is one of pressure, of fractures mended and new ones formed, a dialogue conducted in the slow grinding of tectonic patience.  

They tend the machinery. They do not build it—it was there before them, or perhaps it grew from the plateau itself—but they maintain it, their glass fingers tracing the etched patterns on its surface, coaxing it into operation with touches that seem almost reverent. The purpose of the machines is unclear, even to them. They only know that the hum must continue, the pulse must be sustained.  

They do not sleep. They enter periods of stillness, their forms settling into rigid sculptures, their internal processes slowing to near-stasis. In these moments, the faint green glow of the fungi seeps into their glass bodies, illuminating them from within in slow, rhythmic pulses, as if they are dreaming in a language of light.  

They are not the apex of this ecosystem. The winged predators sometimes descend upon them, their vast shadows passing over the plateau like storm clouds. The Glass-Eaters do not flee. They stand motionless, letting the creatures inspect them, their surfaces reflecting the predators’ own forms back at them in distorted, prismatic fragments. Sometimes, the predators take one—plucking them from the ground with hooked talons, carrying them into the amber sky to be shattered elsewhere, their remains left to glisten in some distant stretch of obsidian. The others do not mourn. They simply wait. New fractures will form. New Glass-Eaters will rise.  

They are patient. They are inevitable. They are the plateau given thought, given slow and deliberate motion. They do not wonder about the world beyond the edge of the sky. They only know the glass, the hum, the slow and endless work.  

They are waiting for something.  

Or perhaps they are what the world is waiting for.
