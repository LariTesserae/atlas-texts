**The Hollowing**  

At the heart of the Weeping Plateau, where the stone sweats its slow grief and the roots hum with buried sentience, there is a place the mist does not touch. A clearing—if such a word can be used for a space that feels less *emptied* than *excavated*—where the moss pulls back like a receding tide, leaving bare, blackened earth. The ground here is not dirt but something closer to skin, stretched taut over unseen contours. It breathes.  

At the center stands the Hollowing.  

It is not a tree, though it wears the shape of one in the same way a corpse might wear the memory of a face. Its trunk is a column of fused bone and petrified sap, webbed with cracks that weep a substance too thick to be resin, too dark to be blood. The branches are not branches but fractures in the air, splitting reality like glass struck by a hammer. They do not move with the wind. The wind moves *around* them, afraid.  

The Hollowing is old. Older than the plateau, older than the mountains that came before it. It remembers when the sky was different. It remembers the weight of something vast pressing down upon the world, before the world learned to shrug.  

Things gather beneath it. Not animals, not exactly. Shapes that flicker between solid and not, their edges blurred as if seen through warped glass. They press close to the trunk, their mouths—if they have mouths—open in silent communion. Sometimes, one of them reaches out, presses a limb (a hand? a claw? a tendril?) against the Hollowing’s bark. The cracks widen for a moment. Something inside shifts. The watcher shudders and is still.  

The air here is thick with the scent of ozone and old copper. Sound behaves strangely—words come out wrong, too slow or too fast, or they unravel into static before they can be heard. The few birds that dare fly near the Hollowing do so in frantic, jagged arcs, as though the sky itself has grown teeth.  

And sometimes, when the mist thins just so, the Hollowing *speaks*.  

Not in words. Not in sound at all. It peels back the layers of the world like rind from fruit, and for a heartbeat, what lies beneath is visible—a vast, luminous lattice, a network of veins threading through everything, pulsing in time to a heartbeat that is not a heartbeat. The watchers tremble. The plateau holds its breath.  

Then the moment passes. The cracks seal. The Hollowing returns to its vigil.  

But the air still tastes of opened flesh.  

And the watchers are always, always waiting.
