**The Keepers of the Drowned Spire**  

In the hollow chambers and labyrinthine passages of the Drowned Spire, **the Weavers** make their home. They are neither human nor entirely otherworldly, but something in between—creatures born of salt and steel, shaped by the tower’s slow surrender to the sea. Their bodies are sleek and sinewy, covered in a fine, iridescent layer of scales that shimmer like oil on water. Their hands are long and spindly, tipped with claws that can pry barnacles from metal or carve symbols into rusted walls.  

The Weavers are not builders, but stewards. They tend to the tower’s groaning engine, coaxing it to life when its rhythm threatens to falter. They do not understand its purpose—if it ever had one—but they feel its pulse as their own. They move through the spire’s depths with eerie grace, their bioluminescent eyes casting pale light in the shadows.  

Their voices are the sound of water rushing through pipes, the creak of corroded metal, the low hum of the engine. They do not speak in words but in echoes, communicating through vibrations that resonate through the tower’s bones. To them, the spire is alive—a sleeping giant whose dreams they protect.  

In the higher reaches of the tower, where the seabirds nest and the wind howls, the Weavers craft **the Nets**. These are not mere ropes but intricate webs of kelp, seaweed, and salvaged metal, woven together with a strange, adhesive secretion from their own bodies. The Nets hang from the spire’s beams like tapestries, shimmering in the stormlight. They serve no obvious purpose, yet the Weavers work on them endlessly, their claws moving with hypnotic precision.  

Some say the Nets are a kind of language, a way for the Weavers to encode the tower’s history—or perhaps its future. Each knot and twist seems deliberate, as if telling a story of the sea’s rise, the tower’s fall, and the things that dwell in the depths below.  

The Weavers are curious but wary. They watch the silver fish darting through the beams, the translucent crabs scuttling across the rusted surfaces, even the vast shapes that glide past the outer edges of the tower. They do not interfere, not directly. Instead, they observe, their luminous eyes tracking every movement.  

There is a sense of inevitability to their existence, as if they too are part of the tower’s slow decay. They do not fear the abyss below or the storms above. They know the sea will claim the spire eventually, just as it claimed the cities before it. But until then, they will tend to its groaning engine, weave their shimmering nets, and listen to its ancient, fading song.
