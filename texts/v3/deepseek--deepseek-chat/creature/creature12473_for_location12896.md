In this high, wind-scoured expanse, the true rulers are not the people—though they endure—but the **stone-walkers**.  

They are massive, slow-moving creatures, their bodies like living boulders, their limbs thick and stony, covered in lichen and moss that shift with the seasons. Their backs are cragged plateaus in miniature, their hides pocked with shallow pools where rainwater collects, tiny ecosystems of algae and hardy insects. They move with a tectonic patience, their footfalls sending tremors through the earth, their paths worn into the rock over centuries.  

The people of the plateau revere them, though they do not worship them. To do so would be like worshiping the sky or the mountains—pointless, when the thing you honor is indifferent. Instead, they observe. They learn the stone-walkers’ migration routes, the way they pause at certain fissures to drink from underground streams, the way they gather in the lee of the tallest peaks when storms roll in. The herds of shaggy beasts follow in their wake, grazing on the tough grasses that sprout where the stone-walkers have passed, their hooves breaking up the soil just enough for seeds to take root.  

The stone-walkers themselves do not eat, not in any way the people understand. They absorb minerals from the rocks they tread upon, their bodies growing imperceptibly over decades, their forms shifting as new layers of stone accrete. Some say they are as old as the plateau itself, that they were here when the land first rose from the earth. Others whisper that they are the land—that if you listen closely, you can hear the slow, deep pulse of their hearts beneath your feet.  

They are not gentle, but neither are they cruel. If a settlement is in their path, they do not deviate. The people know better than to build where the stone-walkers walk. Sometimes, a young one—still small, only twice the height of a man—will pause at the edge of a village, its deep-set eyes glinting like quartz in the dim light, and the people will leave offerings of salt or polished stones, though they do not know if the creatures understand the gesture. The stone-walker might linger for a time, its breath a low rumble like distant thunder, before moving on.  

They are the plateau’s silent architects, its patient gardeners. Where they pass, the land changes. New springs bubble up in their footprints. Cracks in the earth widen into valleys. And sometimes, when one finally comes to rest—when it settles into the earth and does not rise again—its body becomes a new hill, its bones the foundation for whatever comes next.  

The people say that when a stone-walker dies, the earth dreams.  

And the plateau grows.
