### **The People of the Engine Quarter**  

They call themselves *peat-walkers*, though the name is old, and few remember why. Their skin is pale where it isn’t stained with soot or algae, their hands broad and webbed with calluses from hauling sodden ropes, turning rusted valves. They wear layers of waxed cloth and oiled leather, garments that never fully dry, that smell of mildew and tallow. Their voices are low, murmuring things, words half-lost in the drip and sigh of the Quarter.  

They are not tall people. The ceilings here are low, the doorways stooped, and they have adapted—their spines curve slightly forward, as if always listening to the ground. Their eyes are dark, accustomed to the green gloom, and they move with the patience of those who know that haste is wasted in a place where everything sinks, where every step must be measured against the soft earth.  

They live in the upper floors of the moss-choked buildings, where the air is marginally drier, where the heat of their peat stoves lingers a little longer. Their homes are lined with shelves of ceramic jars, each one holding something preserved—fermented reeds, smoked eel, the rendered fat of canal-dwelling creatures whose names have no translation beyond the Quarter. They sleep on mats of woven rushes, and their dreams are slow, thick things, full of the murmur of water and the creak of settling stone.  

### **The Work of the Quarter**  

They tend the engines. Not to keep them running—no, that is a lost cause—but to keep them *breathing*. The great machines wheeze and shudder, their boilers fed not with coal but with compressed peat bricks, their pistons moving like the limbs of a sleeping giant. The peat-walkers whisper to them, press their palms against the warm metal, smear the joints with grease made from rendered fish oil. They do not command the machines; they coax them, soothe them. The engines are living things now, or near enough—their rhythms syncopated with the rise and fall of the water, their heat bleeding into the damp air.  

Some of them dredge the canals, pulling up armfuls of silt and algae to be pressed into fuel bricks. Others harvest the moss that grows in thick, spongy mats along the walls, drying it for insulation, for bedding, for the strange, sour tea they brew in copper kettles. A few still remember how to read the old schematics, their fingers tracing the water-warped blueprints, their voices murmuring half-remembered words: *pressure valve*, *flywheel*, *condenser*.  

### **The Spirits of the Place**  

The peat-walkers do not speak of them directly, but they leave offerings all the same—a handful of dried reeds tucked into a pipe joint, a smear of grease on a particular valve, a whispered word to the hissing steam. The Quarter is old, and old places accumulate ghosts. Some say the machines have them—that the groaning of the pipes is a kind of speech, that the way the algae swirls in the canals is not random. Others say the ghosts are older still, that they were here before the first brick was laid, that they simply folded themselves into the new structures as they rose.  

The peat-walkers do not argue about it. They simply nod, press their palms to the mossy walls, and listen.  

### **A Visitor’s First Night in the Quarter**  

You will wake to the sound of water. Not the sharp, clear sound of a stream, but the thick, wet noise of it seeping, dripping, sighing into itself. The air will taste of iron and old rain. The walls of your borrowed room will glisten faintly in the dim light of a peat-fire, and you will realize, slowly, that the dark patches on the ceiling are not shadows—they are colonies of something alive, something that pulses very slightly with your breath.  

You will rise, your feet sinking into the damp floorboards, and go to the window. Outside, the canals are silvered with moonlight, the algae glowing faintly where the water stirs. A figure moves along the opposite bank, their shape indistinct, their footsteps silent in the moss. They pause, as if feeling your gaze, and raise a hand—not in greeting, not in warning, but in something like recognition.  

Then they are gone, swallowed by the green dark.  

And the Quarter exhales around you, slow and deep, and you understand, suddenly, why the peat-walkers never rush.  

There is no hurry here.  

Only the settling.  

Only the moss, and the water, and the long, slow breath of the engines.
