**The Watchers in the Moss**  

They are not human, though they wear the shape of it when they choose. Tall and slender, their limbs too long, their fingers too many, they move through the gorge like shadows given substance. Their skin is the color of wet stone—mottled gray-green, shifting with the light—and their hair is not hair at all, but cascades of living moss, threaded through with tiny, glowing fungi that pulse faintly in the dark.  

Their eyes are the worst part. Too large, too round, the whites too visible in the dimness. Pupils like slits, like a cat’s, but blacker than the gorge’s deepest crevices. They do not blink. They do not need to.  

They speak, but not with voices. When they wish to be understood, the air around them thrums, the moss shivers, and meaning presses itself into the mind like a thumb into soft clay. Their words are not words—they are sensations, impressions, half-formed memories that do not belong to you.  

They are old. Older than the gorge, though they remember when it was born. They pressed their hands to the splitting earth and whispered to the stone, and the stone listened. They do not rule here—they are part of it, as much as the hanging vines, the creeping water, the sighing mist.  

They take the offerings left at the gorge’s edge. Not because they need them, but because it pleases them to be acknowledged. Sometimes they leave gifts in return—a sprig of glowing lichen, a smooth river stone that hums when held, a bone flute that plays itself when the wind is right.  

They are not kind. They are not cruel. They are watching. They are waiting.  

For what, even they do not yet know.
