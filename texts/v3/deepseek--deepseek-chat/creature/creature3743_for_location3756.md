The inhabitants of the Moss-Covered Hollow are not people in the traditional sense—not anymore. They are embodiments of the damp, the decay, and the creeping life that thrives in the hollow’s shadowed depths. Their forms are lithe and elongated, their limbs jointed in ways that mimic the sinuous vines and fungal stalks that surround them. Their skin, if it can be called skin, is pallid and translucent, shot through with faint, bioluminescent veins that pulse in rhythm with the hollow’s heartbeat. It is as though they are not separate from the place, but extensions of it—its will made manifest.

Their faces are unsettling in their absence of definition. No eyes peer out from their smooth, featureless heads, yet they move with a precision that suggests some other sense guides them—perhaps the vibrations of the dripping water or the subtle shifts in the air’s humidity. Their mouths, when visible, are small and lipless, shaped more like the spore-bearing gills of a mushroom than anything human. They do not eat in the way one might expect; instead, they absorb nutrients through their skin, pressing themselves against the mossy walls or the damp fungal stalks to draw sustenance from the very fabric of their world.

They communicate sparingly, not through sound but through subtle gestures and pheromonal exchanges that ripple through the hollow’s dense air. Their movements are deliberate, almost ritualistic, as though every step, every touch, is part of a larger, unseen purpose. They seem to tend to the hollow itself—gently coaxing the fungal spires to grow in certain directions, guiding the water to pool where it is needed, and harvesting the armored beetles and pale, skittering things that dwell in the shallow basins. 

Their society, if it can be called that, is built on symbiosis. They are not rulers of the hollow but caretakers, existing in harmony with the other life that teems around them. Their dwellings are not separate structures but hollowed-out fungal spires, their interiors lined with soft, fibrous material that hums faintly with life. They do not hoard or accumulate; everything they use is returned to the hollow in some form, their waste absorbed back into the moss and decay that carpets the ground.

There is a quiet intelligence to them, a deep understanding of the hollow’s rhythms and cycles. They seem to know when the fungal towers will creak and settle, when the dripping water will pool in certain places, and when the ecosystem will shift in its delicate balance. They move through the hollow with a grace that suggests they are not merely surviving but thriving, their existence woven into the very fabric of the place.

Yet there is something eerie about them, something that defies comprehension. They are not hostile, not cruel, but their alienness is profound. They are not of this world, nor are they entirely *in* it. They are something else—something that has evolved beyond the need for light, for warmth, for the familiar trappings of life. They are creatures of the damp and the dark, and they belong to the hollow as surely as the moss and the fungal spires do. 

In the Moss-Covered Hollow, they live—quiet, strange, and utterly inseparable from the world they inhabit. They are its heartbeat, its caretakers, its silent witnesses. And though they may be hard to understand, they are undeniably *alive*, their existence a testament to the resilience and strangeness of life in even the most inhospitable of places.
