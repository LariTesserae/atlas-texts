In this place, beneath the tangled canopy and within the slow, dark waters, dwell the **Mireghast**.  

They are not creatures of flesh, not entirely. Their forms are fluid, shifting between the solid and the spectral, as if the jungle itself has exhaled them into being. Tall and gaunt, their limbs stretch too long, their fingers tapering into tendrils that dissolve into mist when they move. Their skin is the color of silt and old bark, mottled with patches of luminous moss that glows faintly in the dimness—not enough to illuminate, just enough to be seen when they wish it.  

Their faces are hollow, their features indistinct, as though carved by erosion rather than birth. They have no eyes, only deep recesses where something flickers—reflections of the river, perhaps, or the slow pulse of something deeper. When they open their mouths, it is not to speak but to exhale the damp breath of the jungle, a sigh that carries the scent of wet earth and rotting leaves.  

The Mireghast do not walk so much as drift, their feet barely disturbing the ground. They are drawn to the river, where they wade into the black water and let it swallow them whole, dissolving into the current before reforming elsewhere. They are guardians of a kind, though what they guard is unclear—perhaps the balance of decay, perhaps the secrets buried in the silt.  

They do not hunt, but they are not passive. When something intrudes—something that does not belong—the Mireghast gather. They do not attack. They simply *watch*, their presence thickening the air until the intruder feels the weight of centuries pressing down, until their breath comes slow and labored, until they understand, on some primal level, that they are being measured against time itself. Most leave. Those who do not are never seen again.  

Sometimes, on nights when the river is still and the hum of the jungle deepens, the Mireghast gather in the shallows. They stand motionless, their forms merging with the shadows, and from them rises a sound—not a song, not a chant, but a vibration, a resonance that matches the slow pulse of the earth. It is the sound of roots growing, of water carving stone, of leaves surrendering to rot.  

The jungle does not fear them. The jungle *is* them. And they will endure, as the river endures, as the trees endure—patient, inevitable, and utterly indifferent.
