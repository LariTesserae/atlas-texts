**The Wetfoot Clan**  

In the High Fen of Everweep, the Wetfoot Clan endures—a people born of the mire, shaped by its rhythms and bound to its secrets. They are not tall nor broad, but wiry and lean, their bodies honed by lifetimes of navigating the sodden landscape. Their skin is pale, almost translucent, streaked with faint, moss-green veins that seem to mirror the fen’s labyrinthine waterways. Their hair, when not shorn short, grows in wiry tufts, often darkened by mud or threaded with strands of lichen. Eyes wide and perpetually wary, they gleam faintly in the mist, reflecting the bioluminescence of the fen’s fungi and the occasional flash of metallic sheen in the water.  

The Wetfoot Clan wears garments spun from the fen itself—waterproof cloaks woven from sedge fibers, boots stitched from the hides of shaggy-coated herbivores, and tunics reinforced with layers of peat-moss for insulation. Their clothing is drab, blending seamlessly with the murky greens and browns of their surroundings, but it is intricately decorated with patterns that mimic the fen’s hidden currents and the arcane symbols they carve into the peat.  

They live in stilted villages perched above the marsh, their homes constructed from the bones of ancient trees and the rusting remnants of the forgotten machinery beneath the fen. The structures are crooked and asymmetrical, lashed together with sinew and rope, their walls lined with moss to keep out the chill. Bridges of woven reeds connect the huts, swaying slightly with each step, while lanterns of bioluminescent fungi cast a faint, eerie glow.  

The Wetfoot Clan speaks in whispers and hushed tones, their language a series of soft clicks, glottal stops, and murmured vowels that blend seamlessly with the fen’s ambient sounds. They have no word for “silence”; their language is built around the nuances of the fen’s ever-present murmur—the gurgle of water, the creak of peat, the hum of ancient machinery.  

Their way of life is defined by adaptation and symbiosis. They forage for edible roots, fungi, and the occasional fish, but they also cultivate small gardens of moss and reeds, coaxing sustenance from the stubborn land. They hunt the fen’s herbivores with spears tipped with sharpened stone or rusted metal scavenged from the depths, moving with a fluid grace that leaves barely a ripple in the water.  

Yet, the Wetfoot Clan is more than mere survivalists. They are keepers of the fen’s secrets, caretakers of its strange balance. They believe the fen is alive, a vast, breathing entity that watches and remembers. They revere it as both mother and guardian, leaving offerings of moss and peat at the edges of black pools or chanting softly before stone outcroppings. They also tend to the machinery buried beneath the fen, though its purpose is long lost to them. They polish rusted gears, pour oil into ancient pipes, and whisper prayers to the thrumming engines—not out of understanding, but out of reverence for the fen’s ancient heart.  

The Wetfoot Clan is wary of outsiders, viewing them as disruptors of the fen’s delicate equilibrium. They move unseen when intruders stumble into their territory, watching from the shadows with unblinking eyes. When cornered or provoked, they defend themselves with cunning and ferocity, using the fen itself as a weapon—trapping invaders in sinking peat, guiding them into the paths of silent predators, or luring them into the fog until they are lost forever.  

To live in the High Fen is to become part of it, and the Wetfoot Clan is its truest reflection. They are the guardians of its weeping stones, the echoes of its ancient machinery, and the embodiment of its slow, patient decay. They are the fen, and the fen is them.
