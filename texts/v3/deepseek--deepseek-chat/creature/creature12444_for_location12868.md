**The Inhabitants of the Primordial Pocket**  

They call themselves the *Kharvathi*, though the word is less a name and more a vibration, a resonance that thrums through their segmented bodies like the pulse of the buried machines. They are the children of the pocket—neither born nor made, but emerged, piece by piece, from the slow marriage of dust and industry.  

Their forms are impossible to classify. Some are no larger than a human hand, their exoskeletons a patchwork of rusted metal and chitin, their many-jointed legs clicking against the mineral crust as they scuttle in ceaseless motion. Others are longer, serpentine, their bodies rippling with overlapping plates that flex and shift as they navigate the labyrinth of pipes and vents. A few are massive, slow-moving behemoths, their backs studded with crystalline growths that hum in harmony with the deeper machinery beneath the earth.  

They do not have eyes, not in any familiar sense. Instead, their perception is a web of vibrations—sound, heat, the faint electromagnetic whispers of the ancient structures around them. They communicate in clicks and shudders, their language a living algorithm, a code passed down through generations of scavengers and engineers.  

They are both the caretakers and the consumed. The smallest among them feed on the mineral dust, their mandibles grinding it down into something usable, something that fuels their internal furnaces. The mid-sized hunters stalk these grazers, their needle-like appendages piercing carapaces to siphon the molten ichor within. The largest, the elders, do not hunt at all—they are walking refineries, their bodies processing the dead, the broken, the excess, breaking them down into raw materials that seep back into the earth, feeding the machines that feed the cycle anew.  

They do not sleep. They do not rest. They move in ceaseless patterns, their existence a dance of maintenance and predation, a ritual as old as the pocket itself. Some tend to the structures, clearing vents of debris, reinforcing weakened supports with secretions that harden into new metal. Others burrow into the ground, following the hum of deeper mechanisms, their bodies adapting, changing, becoming more like the forgotten technology they serve.  

There is no hierarchy among them, only function. No leaders, only roles. And yet, there is intelligence—not individual, but collective, a vast and humming mind woven from a million tiny interactions. They do not wonder about the world beyond their pocket. They do not question their purpose. They simply *are*, as essential to the place as the air, the light, the unending hum.  

And when one dies, it is dismantled, repurposed, its essence returned to the cycle. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is lost.  

They are the Kharvathi. The keepers of the rhythm. The living breath of the machine.
