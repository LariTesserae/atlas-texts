**The Keepers of the Sunken Greenhouse**  

They are not gardeners in the traditional sense. They do not prune or weed—they *listen*.  

Tall, slender figures with skin like water-stained parchment, they move through the green haze with the slow deliberation of roots seeking purchase. Their limbs are elongated, fingers tapered into fine, probing tendrils that brush against leaves, dip into soil, trace the paths of moisture along pipes. Their eyes, if they have eyes at all, are hidden beneath the deep folds of their hoods, which are not fabric but layers of lichen-grown cloth, living and breathing with them.  

They do not speak. Communication is the press of a palm against damp brick, the tilt of a head toward a creeping vine, the way they gather where the water drips thickest and stand, motionless, as if absorbing the sound. Their feet, wrapped in fibrous bindings of moss and hemp, leave no mark on the sodden ground.  

No one knows if they were once human. Perhaps they were engineers, caretakers left behind when the cistern was abandoned, who learned the language of the green and forgot all others. Perhaps they are something older, something that was always here, waiting for the water and the dark and the slow decay of metal.  

They tend without touching. A wilting fern perks up as one passes. A struggling shoot straightens as another exhales—a breath that carries not air but spores, fine as mist. They do not harvest the crops that grow in abundance; they simply ensure the growth continues, unchecked, unfaltering. The food is taken, yes—left in baskets at the district’s edges, collected by unseen hands from above—but the Keepers do not acknowledge this exchange. Their work is the work of balance, of silent negotiation between root and stone.  

At times, they gather around the old boiler, now a great, corroded husk veined with luminous mycelium. They press their hands to its surface and stand there for hours, days, as if listening to a heartbeat deep within the metal. Perhaps they are.  

They do not sleep. They simply pause, now and then, folding themselves into the green, becoming still as the ferns, as the moss, until they are nearly indistinguishable from the life around them.  

Visitors are rare. Those who stumble into the Sunken Greenhouse District sometimes report seeing them—always at a distance, always half-hidden by the hanging vines. The Keepers do not flee, but they do not approach. They observe, they wait, and then, when the intruder lingers too long, they simply *step into the green*—melding with the leaves, the shadows, the dripping dark—and are gone.  

They are the stewards of this sunken lung, the silent midwives of its endless growth. They ask for nothing. They only ensure the water drips, the leaves breathe, and the green goes on.
