### **The Hollowfolk of the Drowned Quarter**  

They do not call themselves *people* anymore. The word is too heavy, too human, and they have shed much of what made them so. Their skin is pale, not from lack of sun but from the slow leaching of warmth, their veins mapping blue-black beneath the surface like tributaries of stagnant water. Their fingers are long, knuckles swollen from years of pulling rope, prying open rusted doors, testing the give of rotten wood before trusting their weight to it.  

They live in the upper floors of buildings that have not yet surrendered to the water, in rooms where the plaster has not yet sloughed away in great weeping sheets. Their homes are nests of salvage—mattresses stuffed with dried reeds, shelves bowed under the weight of scavenged books swollen with damp, oil lamps burning fish fat that stinks of brine and burning hair. They sleep in shifts, always listening for the groan of shifting foundations, the telltale creak of a beam about to give.  

They do not go into the water if they can help it. Not because they fear drowning—they are too used to that—but because of what *moves* beneath the surface. Sometimes, when the generators sputter to life and the submerged cables hum, the water ripples in ways that have nothing to do with the current. Shapes coil just beneath, shadows too large to be fish, too deliberate to be debris. The Hollowfolk do not speak of them, but they mark the places where the water is darkest, where the reflections do not quite match what stands above.  

They communicate in gestures, in the tap of knuckles against pipes, in the way they leave certain doors ajar or pile debris in precise formations. A chair tipped on its side means *unsafe floor*. A rag tied around a rusted nail means *someone drowned here*. A mirror angled toward the water means *something looked back*.  

They eat what they can—moss scraped from brick, blind fish caught in nets of frayed wire, the occasional rat that has not yet learned to fear them. Some whisper of a garden, somewhere high above the waterline, where a stubborn old woman tends to mushrooms that glow faintly in the dark. But most have given up on green things.  

They do not bury their dead. The water takes them, eventually. Sometimes, if the body is fresh, they weigh it down with stones, hoping to keep it from floating back up. But the Quarter is patient. The dead always return, in one form or another.  

And at night, when the generators fail and the last lamp gutters out, they listen. To the drip of water in the walls. To the slow, rhythmic creaking of the city settling deeper into its grave.  

And sometimes, if they are very quiet, they hear the machines below—still humming, still dreaming—waiting.
