**The Ones Who Dwell in the Weeping Expanse**  

They are not creatures of flesh, nor of bone, nor even of the rigid structures that define most living things. They are *movement* given form—shapes woven from the slow, deliberate flow of water, the flex of reeds, the shudder of sodden earth. To call them beings would be to impose borders where none exist; they are more like *patterns*, disturbances in the already unstable equilibrium of the Expanse.  

When they manifest, it is as ripples in the standing water, as a sudden shift in the density of mist, as the inexplicable bending of a reed without wind. They are not singular, but neither are they many—they exist in the liminal space between individuality and collective, their presence flickering in and out of coherence like reflections on disturbed water.  

Their bodies—if they can be called that—are composed of whatever the Expanse provides at the moment. Some take the shape of tangled vines, knotted into approximations of limbs, their forms dripping with condensed moisture. Others are columns of mist, vaguely humanoid, their edges fraying into the damp air. A few seem to be made of the weeping stones, their surfaces eternally slick, their movements accompanied by the sound of dripping water.  

They do not speak, not in any language of words. Instead, they communicate through the language of the Expanse itself—the creak of vegetation, the plink of droplets, the subtle shifts in humidity. A conversation might be the sudden synchronization of dripping water across a dozen leaves, or the way the fog thickens in response to an unasked question.  

Their purpose—if they have one—is unclear. They do not hunt, for there is nothing to hunt. They do not build, for the land is already building itself. Perhaps they are stewards, shepherds of the Expanse’s slow metamorphosis, ensuring that the balance of growth and decay remains uninterrupted. Or perhaps they are merely symptoms of the land’s strangeness, emergent phenomena given fleeting consciousness by the sheer density of life and water.  

They are aware, but not in the way that animals or even plants are aware. Their perception is diffuse, spread across the entire wetland, their senses indistinguishable from the environment itself. They know the press of roots through mud, the taste of minerals in the water, the weight of mist on leaves. They know time as the land knows it—in the lengthening of shadows, the slow dissolution of stone, the patient unfurling of fronds.  

They do not fear, but they *react*. When something foreign enters the Expanse—a creature, a disturbance, a change—they gather, not with hostility, but with a kind of osmotic curiosity. The water stills. The reeds bend inward. The mist thickens. They study the intrusion, not with eyes, but with the entirety of the wetland’s awareness. And then, inevitably, the Expanse absorbs it. The foreign thing becomes part of the cycle, its edges softened, its identity dissolved into the greater whole.  

They are old, though not in years. They are old in the way that the first rainfall was old, in the way that the first sprout breaking through mud was old. They were not born; they simply *are*, as much a part of the Expanse as the water or the reeds.  

And when the light fades, when the mist grows too thick to see through, they retreat—not to any shelter, but into the land itself. They disperse, their presence fading into the background hum of the wetland, until all that remains is the sound of dripping water, the sigh of shifting reeds, and the quiet, ceaseless weeping of the stones.
