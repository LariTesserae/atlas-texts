**The Dwellers of the Submerged Plateau**  

They call themselves the **Tideborn**, though no voice from the surface has ever asked them their name.  

Their forms are fluid, not quite human, not entirely fish—something in between, sculpted by the ceaseless currents. Their skin is translucent in places, revealing the faint pulse of bioluminescent veins beneath, glowing softly in the deep’s perpetual twilight. Their limbs are long and sinewy, webbed between fingers and toes, built for cutting through the water with effortless grace. Their eyes are large, dark, and reflective, adapted to the dim light, capable of seeing the faintest ripple of movement in the gloom.  

They live among the ruins—not the rusted metal husks of the forgotten world above, but the older ruins, the ones made of coral and bone. Their dwellings are towering, spiraling structures grown from living reef, fused with the skeletons of long-dead leviathans. The walls pulse with slow, organic rhythms, as if breathing, as if the entire plateau is alive beneath them.  

The Tideborn do not speak in words. Instead, they sing—low, resonant vibrations that travel through the water, shaping meaning in harmonics and rhythm. Their songs tell of the great migrations of whales, of the shifting of the currents, of the slow crumbling of the cliffs into the abyss. Some say their voices can call storms, that the churning of the waves above is their doing, though none can say for certain.  

They are hunters, but not cruel ones. They move among the kelp forests like shadows, their hands deft and precise, plucking fish from the water with a flick of their wrists. They do not waste. What they take, they use—flesh for food, bones for tools, scales for adornment. Their bodies are draped in woven kelp and the shells of deep-sea mollusks, iridescent in the shifting light.  

They remember the world before the drowning, though none alive now saw it. Their songs carry the echoes of it—the great cities that once stood where now only waves roll, the voices of the land-dwellers swallowed by the sea. They do not mourn. They do not resent. They simply *are*, as inevitable as the tide.  

Sometimes, when the water is still and the light breaks just right, they rise near the surface, their forms flickering at the edges of human sight. Sailors whisper of them—ghosts in the waves, figures half-seen in the spray. Some call them omens. Others call them guardians.  

The Tideborn do not care for names. They only sing, and the sea listens.
