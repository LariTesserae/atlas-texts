In the Drowned Halls of the Old Earth, life persists in forms that have adapted to the crushing pressure and eternal darkness. Here, the inhabitants are as much a part of the ancient, shifting currents as the stone itself—creatures of patience and simplicity, shaped by the unforgiving abyss.

### **The Blind Loom-Spiders**  
Loom-spiders are not spiders in the true sense, but their name sticks for their web-like structures. They are small, translucent creatures with bodies soft as jelly, their limbs long and delicate, adapted for feeling rather than seeing. They spin nets of silken mucus, anchored between the smooth stone ribs, to catch the sparse plankton that drifts through the currents. Their webs glow faintly, a pale blue-green bioluminescence that fades quickly when disturbed. The spiders themselves are blind, their eyeless heads adorned with chemoreceptors that taste the water for traces of food or danger.

### **The Silent Leviathans**  
These massive, eel-like creatures glide through the black waters with a grace that belies their size. Long and sinuous, their bodies are armored in overlapping plates of bone, smooth as the stone they navigate. Silent leviathans have no eyes, their heads dominated by vast, sensory pits that detect the faintest vibrations in the water. They hunt only rarely, their metabolisms slow as the grinding of tectonic plates. When they do feed, it is on the blind loom-spiders or the occasional unwary crab, their cavernous mouths swallowing prey whole with a ripple of movement. Their skin is iridescent, faintly shimmering in the dark, though the light is too weak for any but their own kind to see.

### **The Stone-Tenders**  
The strangest inhabitants of the Drowned Halls are the Stone-Tenders—creatures neither fish nor crustacean, but something else entirely. Small and crablike in form, they scuttle over the smoothed stone, their bodies covered in chitinous plates the color of slate. They carry no claws or weapons, but their front limbs end in brush-like appendages, which they use to sweep silt and debris from the stone. Their purpose is unclear, but their movements are deliberate, almost ritualistic, as if tending to the ancient rock is their sole purpose. They are communal, clustering in hollows or beneath overhangs, their faint clicks and shuffles the closest thing to a language this place knows.

### **The Whispering Jellyfish**  
Drifting in loose colonies, the Whispering Jellyfish are ethereal, ghostly beings. Their translucent bodies pulse faintly, each contraction releasing a soft, phosphorescent glow. They are not truly jellyfish, but their bell-shaped forms and trailing tendrils evoke a kinship. Whispering Jellyfish are passive, drifting with the currents, their tendrils brushing against the stone and the occasional loom-spider web. They emit faint, almost musical vibrations—a humming that echoes through the water, resonating with the stone itself. Some say the vibrations are a form of communication, while others believe they are merely the byproduct of their strange biology.

### **The Abyssal Worms**  
Burrowed deep into the silt and sediment, the Abyssal Worms are rarely seen but ever-present. They are long, segmented creatures, their bodies covered in fine, hair-like bristles that sense the slightest movement in the water. Blind and jawless, they feed on detritus, their slow movements stirring the silt as they burrow. Occasionally, they emerge from their holes, their bodies writhing in the currents, before retreating back into the dark. Their skin is pale, almost translucent, and faintly luminescent—a glow so dim it is visible only in the deepest blackness.

### **The Echo-Murmurs**  
These creatures are more rumor than fact, their existence debated even among the other inhabitants of the Drowned Halls. Said to dwell in the deepest fissures of the stone, Echo-Murmurs are described as shapeless, fluid beings that blend into the water itself. They are said to mimic sounds—the groan of shifting rock, the sigh of the currents—though whether this is a form of predation or mere coincidence is unknown. Some claim they are the remnants of the ancient gods who once ruled this place, while others believe they are mere anomalies, products of the abyss’s strangeness.

Here, in the Drowned Halls of the Old Earth, life is starkly simple yet profoundly mysterious. The creatures that inhabit this place are bound to the rhythms of the water and the stone, their existence a testament to the resilience of life in even the most forbidding corners of the world.
