**The High Wet City of Veyl**  

The city of Veyl clings to the shoulders of a young mountain range, its terraced streets and stepped canals carved into the raw, sharp stone of a landscape still fresh from the earth’s upheaval. The peaks are jagged, their edges unsoftened by time, their flanks streaked with runoff from the ever-present mist that drifts between them. The air is warm and thick with moisture, condensing on the dark basalt walls of buildings, beading on the broad leaves of the sparse but lush vegetation that spills from rooftop gardens and cracks in the cobbles.  

Water is everywhere—not in great rushing rivers, but in a slow, quiet saturation. It seeps from the rock itself, welling up in shallow pools between flagstones, trickling down carved channels that lace through the city like veins. The canals are narrow and precise, their edges still crisp, their beds lined with smooth, dark stones. At dawn and dusk, the air is so heavy with vapor that the streets seem to swim in a silver haze, the outlines of buildings blurring at a distance.  

The city is built for water, not against it. Houses rise on stilts or squat on raised platforms, their foundations dry only by careful design. Bridges arch delicately over the canals, their spans made of lashed reeds and treated wood, their surfaces worn smooth by generations of bare feet. There are no animals here—no birds, no insects, no hum of life beyond the occasional rustle of wind through the broad, waxy leaves of the mountain palms. The silence is deep but not oppressive; it is the quiet of a place where sound is swallowed by the damp air, where voices carry softly, where the dominant noise is the occasional drip of water from an overhang.  

The people of Veyl move slowly, deliberately. Their tools are simple—stone and wood, woven fiber, clay baked in the rare dry spells. They build in layers, their city climbing the slopes in staggered tiers, each level a little more exposed to the wind, a little less steeped in the valley’s clinging humidity. The highest districts are open, their plazas broad and airy, their sightlines stretching far over the mist-shrouded lowlands below. From here, one can see the whole of Veyl—the dark, wet heart of the lower city, the glimmering threads of its canals, the way the land itself seems to exhale moisture into the sky.  

Time here is slow, almost still. The rocks do not crumble; the water does not rise or fall. The city exists in equilibrium, its people attuned to the rhythms of a world that does not hurry. The air smells of wet stone and crushed greenery, of clay and slow fire. The light is diffuse, softened by the ever-present mist, casting no sharp shadows.  

Veyl is a place of patience, of quiet mastery over a land that is neither harsh nor yielding, but simply *present*, waiting, as if the mountain itself is still deciding what it will become.
