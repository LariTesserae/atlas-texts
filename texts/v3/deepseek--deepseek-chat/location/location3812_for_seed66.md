**The Glass Marsh of the Weeping Towers**  

The air is thick with condensation, a warm, wet breath that clings to everything. The ground is a vast expanse of fractured volcanic glass, its black and green shards smoothed by centuries of slow erosion, their edges rounded like the teeth of some ancient beast. Water seeps up from below, pooling in jagged hollows, turning the glass slick and reflective. The marsh is not alive—not in the way you’d recognize—but it is not dead, either. Something moves beneath the surface, something that is not plant nor animal, but a slow, mineral pulse, a shifting of crystalline structures too vast and patient to be called life.  

Rising from the marsh are the **Weeping Towers**, their forms like jagged spires of fused obsidian and rusted iron. They are not built, not exactly—they grew, or were grown, extruded from the earth by some forgotten industrial alchemy. Their surfaces weep constantly, rivulets of water tracing down their flanks, collecting in basins at their bases before seeping back into the glass below. The water is never still, never silent; it drips, trickles, murmurs in a language of echoes.  

The city—if it can be called that—is dense, its structures clustered so close they seem to merge into one another, their edges blurred by time and moisture. Walkways of pitted metal span the gaps between towers, their surfaces streaked with rust and the slow creep of mineral deposits. There are no plants here, no green things to soften the edges. Instead, the air hums with the presence of **fauna**—not creatures of flesh, but things of shifting crystal and liquid metal, skittering across the glass or slithering through the marsh’s hidden channels. They move in swarms, in tides, their bodies clicking and chiming against the stone.  

The sound is **loud**, but not chaotic. It has a rhythm, a pattern—the drip of water, the groan of settling metal, the distant hum of engines buried deep beneath the marsh. The city breathes, exhales steam from hidden vents, shudders as unseen mechanisms turn in the dark.  

Time here is **slow**. The towers erode, but over centuries, not years. The marsh shifts, but imperceptibly, its glassy surface reforming itself in cycles too long for mortal reckoning. The fauna do not hurry. They move with purpose, but not urgency, their existence bound to the patient pulse of the land itself.  

And above it all, the sky is a haze of condensation, refracting light into strange, prismatic shimmers. The air smells of wet metal and ozone, of something on the edge of burning but never quite catching fire.  

This is a place of industry without life, of movement without growth, of sound without voices. It is vast, it is old, and it is waiting—though for what, even the towers do not seem to know.
