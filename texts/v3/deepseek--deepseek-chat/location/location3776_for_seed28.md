### **The Amber Hollow**  

A pocket of lush, feverish life clings to the walls of a vast, hollowed-out chamber—an ancient bubble trapped in the crust of the world. The air is thick and warm, heavy with the scent of resin and damp greenery, though no water flows here. The walls weep a slow, viscous sap that pools in shallow basins, sustaining the tangled vines and broad-leafed ferns that cling to every surface.  

The chamber is enormous, yet intimate—its ceiling lost in a haze of golden mist, its floor a mosaic of cracked stone and fibrous roots. The light is bright but diffuse, filtered through a canopy of translucent amber sheets, remnants of some long-hardened exudation from the rock. The glow is constant, unchanging, casting everything in a drowsy, honeyed hue.  

There is no rain, no river, only the slow seep of sap—a substitute for water, thick and syrupy, sustaining a strange ecology. The plants here are rubbery and resilient, their leaves waxy, their stems swollen with stored moisture. Some curl like grasping fingers, others spread wide as sails, catching the faint currents of warm air that rise from below.  

The sound is overwhelming—a drone, a hum, a ceaseless vibration that thrums through the stone. It comes from nowhere and everywhere, as if the hollow itself is breathing, resonating with some deep, buried frequency. The noise drowns out all but the closest sounds, turning speech into a thing of gestures and touch.  

Few animals live here. Those that do are silent, slow, adapted to the sticky air and the deafening hum. Wingless insects crawl along the amber veins, their carapaces gleaming like polished stone. Something larger moves in the shadows—a blind, many-legged thing that navigates by tremors in the ground, hunting the sluggish, sap-drinking grubs that burrow in the roots.  

At the chamber’s heart, nestled among the roots, is a settlement—not a city, but a clustered hive of woven fibers and hardened resin. The people here (if they are people) build not with wood or stone, but with the very substance of the hollow itself, shaping the sap into walls, tools, even vessels. Their technology is simple—no metal, only chitin and resin and the occasional sharpened stone. They move quietly, their hands speaking where words cannot.  

The hollow feels eternal, settled into its own rhythm. The sap flows, the plants grow, the hum never ceases. Time here is measured in the slow hardening of amber, in the gradual creep of roots through cracks in the stone. The world outside is forgotten, if it ever existed at all.  

And yet—something stirs. The hum shifts in pitch. The sap drips a little faster. The roots twitch, as if sensing a change in the air. The hollow is not dead. It is waiting.
