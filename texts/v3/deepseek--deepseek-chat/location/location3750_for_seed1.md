**The Weeping Flats of Vorthax**  

The air is thick with the weight of unseen water, a perpetual dampness that clings to the skin without ever forming rain. The ground is a vast, low-lying expanse of blackened glass, smooth as obsidian but fractured in places, revealing veins of sluggish, mercury-like fluid that seeps upward from below. The horizon shimmers with heat mirages that twist into impossible geometries—buildings that were never built, towers that lean sideways into dimensions beyond sight.  

This is a place where the boundary between worlds has worn thin. The sky is a bruised purple, streaked with veins of phosphorescent green where the fabric of reality has frayed. The sound is a constant, oppressive hum—not mechanical, not organic, but something in between, as if the land itself is vibrating at a frequency just beyond comprehension. Occasionally, the hum splits into a chorus of distorted voices, whispering in languages that shift before they can be understood.  

There is no vegetation. No life as we know it. Instead, the ground is alive in its own way—shifting, fracturing, healing. Glassy tendrils rise like slow-moving serpents, only to collapse back into the surface. The air is thick with floating motes of something that might be spores, might be ash, might be fragments of broken time. They glow faintly, casting a dim, sickly light over the flats.  

The water here does not behave as it should. It pools in unnatural depressions, refusing to evaporate, refusing to freeze despite the biting cold. Sometimes it moves against gravity, creeping up the edges of glass formations in trembling, reflective sheets. Other times, it vanishes entirely, leaving behind hollows filled with whispers.  

Creatures move in the distance—shapes that flicker in and out of solidity. They are neither wholly animal nor machine, their forms a grotesque fusion of industry and biology: segmented limbs ending in rusted gears, torsos ribbed with pipes that vent steam, eyes that glow like furnace coals. They do not hunt, not in any way that makes sense, but they *consume*—not flesh, not energy, but something else, something intangible. Perhaps memory. Perhaps possibility.  

The land itself is ancient beyond reckoning. It has been worn down to its barest essence, yet it refuses to die. The glass plains shift infinitesimally, grinding against one another with the sound of a million distant engines. The air smells of ozone and burnt metal, though no fire has burned here in epochs.  

This is a place where the rules have dissolved. Where the world is caught between states, neither solid nor liquid, neither alive nor dead, neither past nor future. It is a wound in reality, weeping endlessly, waiting for something—or perhaps *someone*—to stitch it shut.  

Or tear it open wider.
