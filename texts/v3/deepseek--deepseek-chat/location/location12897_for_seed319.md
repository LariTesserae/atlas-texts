**The Weeping City of Vashal**  

The city rises from the high plateau like a great, rusted machine exhaling steam into the sky. The air is thick with condensation, a perpetual dampness that clings to everything—stone, metal, skin. The plateau itself is ancient, its edges softened by centuries of wind and the slow, insistent work of water seeping through cracks. The ground is never quite dry; the streets glisten with runoff, and the walls of buildings weep rivulets down their facades, leaving dark streaks of mineral deposits.  

Vashal is an industrial relic, a city built upon itself in layers of brick, iron, and concrete. Chimneys belch smoke into the warm, wet air, their plumes diffusing into a low-hanging haze that blurs the outlines of the distant mountains. The buildings are weathered, their edges rounded by time, their surfaces pocked with the slow decay of oxidation and damp. Bridges of blackened iron span canals where sluggish water moves between districts, carrying the runoff of the city’s ceaseless industry.  

Vegetation is scarce, clinging only where it can—lichen on stone, stubborn tufts of reeds in the canals, the occasional skeletal tree twisted by the wind. The land beyond the city is a patchwork of shallow marshes and exposed rock, where water pools in depressions and evaporates under the warm, heavy sky.  

The sound is overwhelming. The groan of machinery, the hiss of steam, the rhythmic pounding of pistons—all of it echoes through the streets, amplified by the damp air and the hard surfaces of the city. Voices are swallowed by the din, reduced to shouts and half-heard murmurs beneath the industrial cacophony.  

Few animals remain. What creatures do persist are small, quick, and wary—rats that dart through the alleys, insects that hum near the standing water, the occasional bird that wheels high above, avoiding the smoke.  

And yet, there is something strange here. The water does not behave quite as it should. It gathers in unnatural patterns, pooling in spirals before draining away, as if the city itself is breathing. The oldest residents whisper that the plateau is hollow, that the water is seeping into something vast and unseen below. The machines, they say, are not just powered by coal—they are drawing from something else, something that hums beneath the streets.  

Time moves slowly in Vashal. Changes come in increments—a new layer of rust, another crack in the pavement, the gradual sinking of foundations into the ever-damp earth. The city is patient. It has been here for centuries. It will remain.  

And always, always, the water returns.
