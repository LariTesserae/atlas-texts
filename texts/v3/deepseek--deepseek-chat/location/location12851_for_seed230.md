**The High Damp Hollow**  

A weathered stone town clings to the slope of a wind-scoured plateau, its walls the same muted gray as the rock beneath it. The air is cool and thin here, carrying the scent of damp stone and the faint, mineral tang of slow-moving water. The settlement is small—no more than a cluster of low, rounded buildings, their roofs sloped against the occasional rain, their foundations sunk deep into the bedrock as if grown from it.  

Water seeps through this place in quiet ways. It beads on the undersides of overhangs, gathers in shallow depressions in the rock, trickles in narrow rivulets down the channels carved by centuries of patient erosion. There are no rivers here, only the slow, sporadic gift of moisture—condensation in the morning, a brief shower at dusk, the rare storm that rolls in from the distant peaks and leaves the stones glistening. The people of the hollow have learned to coax life from this scarcity, channeling runoff into shallow cisterns, lining their walls with porous stone to draw in the damp.  

Vegetation is sparse but stubborn. Lichen clings to the northern faces of boulders, and wiry grasses sprout in the cracks between cobblestones. A few stunted trees, their trunks gnarled by the wind, stand sentinel at the edges of the settlement, their roots gripping the rock like fingers. There is no forest here, no thick undergrowth—only the occasional patch of green where the water lingers longest.  

The town itself is built for endurance. Its structures are squat and thick-walled, their surfaces smoothed by time and weather. The streets are narrow, winding paths of worn stone, following the natural contours of the land rather than cutting through it. There are no grand towers, no sprawling plazas—only the quiet, efficient geometry of a people who have long since learned to live within their means. The tools here are simple: chisels of flint and bronze, ropes woven from tough mountain grasses, clay vessels for carrying and storing.  

The air is still, the sounds muted. The wind murmurs against the stones, and now and then, the distant cry of a bird echoes across the plateau. The people speak softly when they speak at all, their voices blending into the hush of the place. Time moves slowly here, if it moves at all. The hollow has existed in this state for generations, neither growing nor shrinking, its rhythms as steady as the seep of water through rock.  

This is a place of quiet persistence. The land does not change, and neither do the people who live upon it. They are part of the equilibrium, as much a feature of the landscape as the lichen or the wind-worn stones. The world beyond the plateau may shift and churn, but here, in the high damp hollow, there is only the long, slow breath of stone and sky.
