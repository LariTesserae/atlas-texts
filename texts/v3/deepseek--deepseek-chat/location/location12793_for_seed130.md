**The Weeping Factory Marshes**  

A vast, lowland expanse of blackened water and rusted iron, where the earth exhales condensation in slow, laborious breaths. The ground is not ground at all—it is a lattice of corroded catwalks, sagging grates, and sunken platforms, all half-submerged in a shallow, stagnant sea. The water is everywhere, seeping up through the perforated metal, pooling in the hollows of collapsed machinery, dripping from the underbellies of sagging pipes. The air is thick with the scent of oxidized metal and damp rot, a cool, clinging mist that never quite lifts.  

The vegetation is sparse but stubborn—tufts of sickly reeds sprout from the gaps in the machinery, their roots tangled in the rust. Lichen blooms in slow, creeping patches across the steel, a dull green-gray that seems more like corrosion than life. There are no trees, only the skeletal remains of scaffolding, their crossbeams weeping with condensation.  

This place is ancient, not in the way of mountains or canyons, but in the way of a ruin that has settled into its final form. The machines are no longer breaking; they have finished breaking, long ago. Now they simply exist, their edges softened by time, their purpose lost to the slow creep of water and decay. The land is flat, but not empty—great, hulking shapes loom in the distance, the silhouettes of derelict factories, their chimneys choked with vines, their windows long since shattered.  

The sound is deafening, but not chaotic. It is the hum of a thousand unseen mechanisms, the groan of metal under its own weight, the rhythmic drip of water from a hundred broken pipes. There is no animal life—no birds, no insects, nothing but the ceaseless mechanical murmur of the place itself.  

Light is scarce. The sky, when visible through the mist, is a dull, bruised gray. What little illumination exists comes from flickering industrial lamps, their glow diffused by the damp air, casting long, wavering reflections on the water’s surface.  

This is a world that was built, entirely and without apology. Nature has not reclaimed it so much as dissolved into it, becoming just another part of the machinery. The water does not flood; it simply is, as intrinsic to the landscape as the rust. The air does not stir; it hangs, heavy with the breath of the dying engines.  

And yet, there is motion—not life, but process. Condensation forms, drips, evaporates, forms again. Metal expands and contracts with the faint shifts in temperature. Somewhere, deep in the guts of the decaying factories, a piston still shudders, not because it serves any purpose, but because it has not yet been told it can stop.  

This is a place that has forgotten it was ever meant to be anything else.
