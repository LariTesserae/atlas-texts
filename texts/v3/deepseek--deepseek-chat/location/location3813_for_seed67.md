**The Endless Engine-Marsh**  

A vast, lowland expanse stretches in every direction, a world entirely constructed yet saturated with the weight of water. The air is warm and thick, clinging to the skin like oil-slick humidity. The ground is a dense lattice of rusted metal plating, bolted and welded into an infinite grid, but beneath it, unseen, the water seeps and pools, rising in slow, weeping beads through the seams. The marsh is not organic—it is a marsh of industry, of condensation on cold pipes, of runoff collecting in the hollows of forgotten machinery.  

The landscape is young, freshly assembled, yet already corroding. Sharp edges of factory-fresh steel jut from the ground, but their surfaces are already pocked with oxidation, bleeding orange rivulets into the standing water. There are no plants—only the slow, mineral creep of rust, the fractal spread of crystalline salts left behind by evaporating puddles. The world is sterile, yet teeming with movement: great herds of mechanical fauna, their bodies a tangle of pistons and segmented limbs, graze on the condensation that forms on cold surfaces. They hum and click, their movements precise, their purpose inscrutable. Some are small, skittering things, like brass beetles the size of dogs; others loom, skeletal and towering, their frames shuddering under their own weight as they wade through the shallow waters.  

The sound is a quiet industry—no roaring engines, no deafening factories, just the constant, low thrum of machines at rest, the occasional groan of metal settling, the drip-drip of water from a fractured pipe. The light is diffuse, filtered through a haze of steam and suspended particulates, casting everything in a dull, bronze glow. The sky, if there is one, is hidden behind the perpetual mist, a ceiling of condensed moisture that never quite falls as rain but never dissipates either.  

The marsh is vast enough to have its own weather—not storms, not winds, but shifts in pressure, in humidity, in the way the condensation gathers and drips. Sometimes, a low fog rolls through, swallowing the mechanical herds for hours before revealing them again, their surfaces beaded with fresh moisture. The air smells of iron and ozone, of hot metal cooling, of standing water that never quite stagnates because the machines keep it moving, just barely.  

Nothing changes here, not truly. The water rises and falls, the machines shift and graze, but the landscape remains in perfect, eerie equilibrium. It was built this way, and it will stay this way, until the last bolt rusts through, until the last pool evaporates—but that moment never comes. The marsh endures, an eternal wetland of industry, a world where life has been replaced by motion, where growth is measured in corrosion, and where the only breath is the slow, damp sigh of engines at rest.
