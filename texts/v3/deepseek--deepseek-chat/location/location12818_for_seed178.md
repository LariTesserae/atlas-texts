**The Weeping Expanse**  

A lowland marsh stretches out under a sky choked with slow, heavy clouds, their bellies sagging with unshed rain. The air is thick—not with warmth, but with a clinging, gelid dampness that seeps into the bones. The ground is a quilt of black peat and silver puddles, trembling underfoot as if the earth itself is holding its breath. Water bleeds up from below, pooling in the hollows between tussocks of wiry sedge and moss-crusted hummocks. The vegetation is dense, a riot of reeds and leathery ferns, their edges sharp with new growth, their roots drinking greedily from the saturated earth.  

Strange structures rise from the mire—not ruins, not quite, but remnants of something that was never meant to last. Slender spires of a material like frosted glass hum faintly in the wind, their surfaces slick with condensation. They flicker sometimes, as if catching a signal from somewhere else, their cores pulsing with a light that doesn’t illuminate. No one built them, not in any way that makes sense. They simply *are*, like the marsh’s own crystalline exhalations.  

The sound is overwhelming. Not the roar of a storm, but the ceaseless chatter of water—dripping, trickling, sloshing, the wet slap of unseen creatures moving through the shallows. Frogs croak in overlapping waves, their voices distorted, too loud for their size. Something larger moves in the deeper channels, sending up gurgling ripples. The air thrums with an almost electrical tension, as if the marsh is constantly on the verge of rearranging itself.  

And it does. Pools swell and shrink without reason. Channels shift overnight. The spires flicker out of existence, only to reappear yards away, their bases dry for a moment before the water seeps back in. The rules here are fluid. The land is young, still figuring itself out.  

The cold is not the clean bite of winter, but a damp, insistent chill that coils around the ankles and wrists. The few creatures that thrive here—pale, long-limbed wading birds, eels that flicker like liquid shadows—move with a restless energy, as if they, too, are caught in the marsh’s constant renegotiation of itself.  

This is a place where the water is always listening. Where the ground might open beneath you, not to swallow you, but to show you something you weren’t meant to see. Where the air is so full of sound it becomes a kind of silence.  

And above it all, the clouds hang, waiting.
