**The Whispering Peaks**  

The land is a vast, high-altitude plateau, a realm of stone and wind, where the air is thin and sharp with cold. The sky is a relentless blue, stretched taut over the jagged teeth of distant mountains, their peaks glazed with eternal snow. The sun beats down with a brightness that borders on violence, bleaching the rocks to pale, weathered hues—ochre, bone-white, and iron-gray.  

Water exists here in fleeting, furtive ways. Dew collects in the hollows of boulders overnight, vanishing by midday. Thin rivulets trickle down from hidden springs, carving shallow grooves into the rock before disappearing into the dry earth. Occasionally, a sudden storm rolls in, drenching the plateau in a brief, furious downpour, leaving behind shallow pools that gleam like scattered coins before evaporating.  

Vegetation is sparse, clinging to existence in cracks and crevices. Hardy grasses, wiry shrubs, and lichen-stained stones are the only signs of life that do not move. The real abundance is in the fauna—great flocks of birds wheel overhead, their wings catching the light as they ride the thermals, their calls lost in the ceaseless roar of the wind. Small, fleet-footed creatures dart between rocks, their fur or feathers blending seamlessly into the landscape.  

The wind is the dominant force here, a constant, deafening presence. It howls through the narrow passes, whips around the standing stones, and hums across the open expanses. Sometimes, when the gusts strike just right, the rocks themselves seem to sing—a low, eerie drone that vibrates through the bones of any creature that hears it. The locals, if there are any, might call it the voice of the mountain, a sound older than memory.  

Time moves differently here. The land does not change, not in any way that matters. The rocks have been worn smooth by centuries of wind, their edges softened but their forms unyielding. There is no erosion in the traditional sense—no crumbling cliffs, no landslides—just the slow, patient work of the elements, reshaping the plateau grain by grain over millennia.  

There are no structures, no signs of human hands. If people come here, they leave no trace. The only tools are those carried by the occasional traveler—simple things, made of bone or chipped stone, easily lost or abandoned. The land does not welcome permanence.  

And yet, there is something *off* about this place. The way the shadows sometimes stretch too long, the way the wind carries whispers that almost sound like words. The way the birds sometimes fall silent all at once, as if waiting for something unseen to pass.  

This is a land of extremes—bright and barren, loud and empty, ancient and unchanging. It does not care for those who pass through. It simply endures.
