The world is a drowned city, but not the kind you might imagine. It is not ruins, ancient and crumbling, but something newly submerged, its edges still sharp, its structures intact, gleaming faintly beneath a vast and restless sea. The water is everywhere, an infinite gray expanse that stretches in all directions, the sky indistinguishable from the surface. The city lies beneath, a labyrinth of glass and steel, its buildings sleek and alien, their surfaces smooth as if carved from a single piece of obsidian. The water presses down, dense and packed, filling every space, every crevice, every room. It is a place where everything is liquid, where the boundaries between inside and outside dissolve.

The city is young, its edges crisp and precise, its architecture untouched by time or decay. The buildings rise like spines, slender and angular, their peaks piercing the water’s surface only to vanish into the mist above. The streets are narrow canals, filled with a fluid that seems heavier than water, a darker shade of gray, almost metallic, moving sluggishly between the towers. There is vegetation, but it is sparse, clinging to the edges of the structures where it has found purchase—thin, translucent fronds that sway in the currents, their surfaces faintly bioluminescent, casting a pale green light that flickers in the gloom.

The atmosphere is cool, almost frigid, the water carrying a biting chill that seeps into every corner of the city. There is sound, but it is muffled, distorted by the liquid medium—a low, resonant hum that seems to emanate from the city itself, punctuated by occasional sharp cracks, like the snapping of distant ice. The city is alive, but not with animal life; it thrums with strange, mechanical processes, unseen engines churning beneath the surface, sending ripples through the water. The technology here is beyond comprehension, its origins and purpose unclear. The buildings seem to shift subtly, their angles changing imperceptibly over time, as if rearranged by unseen hands. Columns of water rise and fall in rhythmic patterns, their movements too precise to be natural.

The scale of the city is deceptive. At first, it seems vast, an endless sprawl of towers and canals, but as you move through it, it begins to feel intimate, almost claustrophobic. Each building is a self-contained ecosystem, its interior a maze of chambers and corridors filled with water of varying densities, some sections almost gelatinous, others impossibly fluid. The city is packed, every space occupied, every surface utilized. There is no room for emptiness here; even the air—if it can be called that—is thick with microscopic particles, tiny motes that drift lazily in the currents, glowing faintly in the dim light.

The light itself is strange, neither natural nor artificial. It seems to emanate from the water, a soft, diffuse glow that suffuses the city, casting everything in shades of gray and blue. Shadows are rare, their edges blurred and indistinct, as if the light refuses to be contained or directed. The city is active, its processes ongoing, its structures adapting and responding to unseen stimuli. Columns of water rise and fall, buildings shift and realign, and the city hums with a low, persistent vibration, like the pulse of a vast, submerged machine.

The rules of this world are unclear, its logic alien. Time seems fluid here, its passage marked not by the movement of the sun or moon, but by the rhythmic pulses of the city’s machinations. The water carries with it a sense of permanence, as if the city has always been this way, submerged and waiting, its purpose hidden beneath the surface. It is a place of contradictions—young but ancient, packed but sparse, active but eerily still. It exists on its own terms, a world apart, strange and unknowable.
