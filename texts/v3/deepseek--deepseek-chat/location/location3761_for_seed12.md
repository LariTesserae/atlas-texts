**The Floating Ruins of Varei-Thal**  

The world is water—endless, deep, and warm. A submerged realm where the sky is only a rumor, a distant shimmer far above the crushing weight of the ocean. Here, the land is not land at all, but the remnants of something older, something half-drowned and still emerging.  

Varei-Thal rises from the abyss in jagged spires of black stone, their surfaces slick with algae and phosphorescent lichen. The rock is porous, riddled with caverns that exhale slow bubbles of trapped air, rising in languid spirals toward the unseen surface. The stone itself seems alive, still forming, still stretching—new ridges split open like fresh wounds, oozing mineral-rich fluids that swirl into the current.  

The water is thick with life, though not the kind that swims. Forests of kelp and towering frond-trees sway in the unseen tides, their leaves broad and translucent, filtering what little light seeps down from above. Bioluminescent polyps cling to their stems, pulsing softly in the dimness. The vegetation is lush, almost suffocating, tangling around the stone spires in great veils of green and violet.  

There are creatures here, but they are rare, spectral things—elongated eels with glassy skin, their organs glowing faintly beneath; slow-moving crustaceans that scuttle across the rock, their shells encrusted with living crystals. They move cautiously, as if aware of how fragile their existence is in this half-made world.  

And then there are the ruins.  

They cling to the tallest spires—crumbling towers of coral-mortared stone, their arches worn smooth by centuries of water. This was once a city, or the beginnings of one. Its builders are long gone, if they ever existed at all. Now, only their structures remain, half-collapsed, their interiors choked with silt and slow-growing sponges. The architecture is primitive, blocky, built by hands that had only just discovered tools. Some of the walls are carved with spiraling glyphs, their meanings lost to the erosion of time.  

The water here is never still. Currents move in slow, patient spirals, carrying whispers of warmer or colder flows from distant vents. The sound is a low hum, the groan of shifting stone, the occasional pop of a gas pocket bursting. Mostly, it is quiet.  

This is a place of slow transformation. The land is still being born. The city is still being swallowed. And the water—always the water—presses in, patient and inevitable.  

To walk here (if one could walk) would be to walk through a dream of drowning. A place caught between becoming and forgetting.
