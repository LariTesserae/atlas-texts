**The City of Root and Rain**  

At the edge of the world, where the air is thin and the sky presses down like a leaden hand, the City of Root and Rain clings to the bones of an ancient plateau. The stone beneath it is worn smooth by eons of wind, its edges softened into undulating waves of rock—a landscape so old it has forgotten its own violence. The plateau stretches vast and open, a highland realm where the horizon bleeds into mist, and the few scattered structures of the city seem like afterthoughts, swallowed by the weight of time.  

The city itself is not built, but *grown*. Towers of blackened iron and glass rise from the earth like gnarled fingers, their surfaces crusted with lichen and moss. Between them, the streets are not paved but woven—a lattice of living roots, thick as a man’s arm, that shift and groan underfoot. The buildings are half-alive, their walls threaded through with vines that pulse faintly in the dim light. Some say the city breathes. Others say it dreams.  

Water is scarce but not absent. It lingers in the hollows of leaves, drips from the rusted eaves of forgotten machinery, seeps up through cracks in the root-lattice to pool in shallow, silver-black mirrors. The air is cool and damp, thick with the scent of wet earth and decaying metal. When the rains come—brief, fitful, unpredictable—they fall in sheets of whispering static, as if the sky itself is unraveling.  

The vegetation is everywhere, relentless. It spills from broken windows, coils around lampposts long since gone dark, erupts through the seams of abandoned factories. The plants here are not passive; they *consume*. They split concrete, strangle pipes, digest the rusted husks of machines. Some have thorns that glisten with a viscous, amber fluid. Others bloom only at night, their petals opening like mouths to exhale spores that taste of burnt sugar and ozone.  

Life—animal life—is rare. What few creatures remain are spectral things: translucent lizards that scuttle across the root-paths, their bones glowing faintly under their skin; crows with too many eyes, perched on the skeletal remains of radio towers; something that might once have been a dog, its body half-fused with the undergrowth, watching passersby with a gaze too knowing to be animal.  

The city hums. Not with the sound of engines or voices, but with something deeper—a vibration in the stone, a resonance in the roots. Some say it is the sound of the plateau settling, grain by grain, into its final form. Others say it is the city remembering itself, whispering its own name in a language no living thing can speak.  

And sometimes, when the rain stops and the air goes still, the boundaries of the world grow thin. The shadows stretch too long. The reflections in the water show things that should not be there. The city exhales, and for a moment, it is not a place but an idea—an echo of something that has not yet happened, or already has.  

Then the wind picks up. The roots creak. The world stitches itself back together, and the City of Root and Rain goes on waiting.
