**The Endless Fen of the Weeping Stones**  

The world here is a vast, slow-breathing marsh, stretching beyond sight—a primordial basin where time has pooled and thickened like sap. The air is warm, dense with moisture, clinging to the skin like a second layer. The light is dim, filtered through a ceiling of vegetation so thick it has become its own sky—a living firmament of interwoven vines, colossal fronds, and ancient, sagging boughs. The canopy is not just above but *around*, a three-dimensional labyrinth of plant matter, roots descending like pillars, stems twisting into bridges, leaves forming terraces where other plants grow upon them. The whole place hums with quiet, wet life.  

The ground is a mosaic of black water and spongy earth, pocked with pools that reflect the dim green glow of the world above. The water is never still—not with waves, but with slow, organic shifts, the rise and fall of roots breathing, the occasional collapse of some sodden mass into the depths. The stones here weep. Not metaphorically—they seep moisture from unseen pores, their surfaces slick with condensation, their bases softened into strange, bulbous forms, like melted wax. Some are half-swallowed by the fen, only their rounded backs visible, as if they are sinking into a dream.  

Life is everywhere, but it moves slowly. Great, shambling beasts with hides like moss-encrusted boulders wade through the shallows, their breaths deep and infrequent, their eyes patient. Insects the size of hands drift lazily between the hanging gardens, their wings catching the faint light in iridescent shivers. There are no sharp sounds—only the occasional drip of water, the creak of a settling tree, the low, resonant call of something distant and enormous.  

The air smells of decay and growth in perfect balance—rotting leaves, sweet nectar, wet stone. Time here is not linear but cyclical, slow, inevitable. The land itself is ancient beyond reckoning, worn down to its essence, all sharp edges long since softened. The few signs of past builders are barely distinguishable from the landscape—a sunken arch of lichen-crusted stone, a few scattered bricks swallowed by roots, the faint outline of a path long since reclaimed by the fen.  

This is a place where the boundary between earth and water, plant and stone, life and landscape, has dissolved. The world is not built—it has *grown*, layer upon layer, until it has become something vast, patient, and utterly alive.
