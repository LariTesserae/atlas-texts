**The Weeping Plateau**  

A high, windswept land where the air is thick with the scent of wet stone and the musk of ancient roots. The plateau is old—older than the mountains that once stood here, now ground down to nubs by time. The rock weeps constantly, not with rivers but with a slow, pervasive seepage, as if the stone itself is sweating under the weight of millennia. Pools form in hollows, their surfaces oily with algae and the occasional iridescent sheen of something not quite water.  

The vegetation is overwhelming, not in height but in sheer presence—a tangled, breathing mass that clings to every surface. Moss carpets the ground in spongy layers, swallowing footsteps whole. Lichen crusts the weeping rocks in blues and grays, while sinewy vines twist up from the earth like the fingers of buried giants. There are no trees, only thick-bodied shrubs with bark like cracked leather, their branches bent into arthritic shapes by the wind. Somewhere beneath the moss, the roots knit together into a living net, a subterranean nervous system that thrums with slow, vegetative thought.  

The cold here is not biting but deep, the kind that settles into bones and lingers. Mist rolls across the plateau in slow, spectral waves, blurring the edges of things. The light is dim, filtered through a perpetual haze, casting everything in a twilight pallor. Shadows move strangely here, as if they have weight, as if they are not just absences but things themselves.  

Life thrives in the wet and the rot. Beetles with iridescent carapaces scuttle through the moss, their legs clicking like tiny gears. Birds—or things that might once have been birds—flit between the shrubs, their calls distorted, too resonant, as if echoing from somewhere else. Larger shapes move at the edges of vision, half-glimpsed through the mist, their forms indistinct but undeniably alive. The air thrums with sound: the creak of straining roots, the drip of water, the distant hum of machinery that shouldn’t be there.  

Because this place is not untouched. Not entirely. Somewhere beyond the plateau’s edge, the land gives way to the scars of industry—smokestacks belching gray plumes into the sky, the rhythmic thud of pistons, the groan of metal under strain. The plateau resists it, but the boundary is porous. Sometimes, the wind carries the stench of oil. Sometimes, the water in the pools tastes of rust.  

And sometimes, the plateau shifts. The ground shudders, not with earthquake but with something more deliberate, as if the land itself is rearranging its bones. The vines twist tighter. The mist thickens into something almost solid. The shadows stretch too long, and for a moment, the sky is not sky but something else—a vast, pulsing membrane, veined with light.  

Then it passes. The plateau settles. The weeping continues.  

But the air still hums with the memory of fracture.
