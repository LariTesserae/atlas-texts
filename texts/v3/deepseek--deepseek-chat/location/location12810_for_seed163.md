The place is a high-altitude plateau, vast and open, stretching in all directions like the roof of the world. The air is cool, thin, and sharp, carrying a dampness that clings to everything. Water is everywhere, not in the form of lakes or rivers, but as a pervasive moisture—condensation beads on every surface, gathering into rivulets that trace the contours of the land. The ground is a mosaic of wet stone and spongy moss, with patches of bare rock glistening as if freshly formed. The heat of some distant volcanic activity keeps the water fluid, though the air itself is chill, a paradox that makes the place feel alive and unfinished.

Vegetation dominates, but not in the way of forests or meadows. Instead, the plateau is a sea of plant matter—a single, interconnected organism that stretches as far as the eye can see. The plants are low to the ground, a mat of fibrous roots and overlapping leaves that form a living substrate. Occasionally, taller structures rise from the mat—columnar stems capped with bulbous growths, their surfaces slick with moisture. These structures seem almost architectural, as if grown intentionally, though their purpose is unclear. The plants emit a faint bioluminescence, casting a soft, pale green glow over the plateau in the perpetual daylight.

The plateau is sparse, with vast stretches of emptiness between clusters of plant life. The few structures that dot the landscape are industrial relics—rusted metal frames, crumbling concrete platforms, and the skeletal remains of machinery. These artifacts are scattered randomly, as if abandoned mid-task, their purpose lost to time. A faint hum fills the air, the remnants of some long-forgotten engine still straining to function.

Sound is overwhelming—a constant, deafening roar of wind sweeping across the plateau, punctuated by the rhythmic dripping of water from the taller plant structures. The wind carries no scent, only the cold bite of altitude and moisture. Fauna is nearly absent, though occasional shadows dart across the ground—small, delicate creatures that seem more insect than mammal, flitting between the plants before vanishing into the mat.

Time here feels slow, almost stagnant. The plateau is a place of gradual change—the slow creep of plant matter over stone, the erosion of metal by wind and water, the incremental shift of moisture across the surface. It feels like a landscape in the early stages of becoming, still raw and unformed, as if the world is still deciding what it will be.

The light is bright, almost blinding—a white glare that reflects off the wet surfaces and casts long, sharp shadows. The combination of light and moisture creates a strange prismatic effect, with rainbows shimmering in the mist that hovers just above the ground. The plateau feels otherworldly, yet strangely familiar—a place where the rules of the world are still being written.
