**The Moss-Covered Spires of Veldrin**  

The city of Veldrin rises from the low, damp hollows of an ancient riverbed, its foundations sunk into silt and time. The water here is not a presence so much as a whisper—a slow seep through porous stone, the occasional drip from overhanging eaves, the slick sheen on cobblestones after a mist rolls in. The river itself is long gone, diverted or swallowed by the earth, but its memory lingers in the cool, wet air and the way everything seems to exhale moisture.  

Veldrin is small, barely more than a cluster of towers and bridges, but it is dense with life—not the kind that moves, but the kind that grows. The city is built upon itself, layer upon layer of stone and iron, but the buildings are no longer distinct from the vegetation that clings to them. Moss carpets every surface, softening edges, swallowing doorways, turning walls into living tapestries of emerald and bronze. Vines thick as ropes twist through balconies, their leaves broad and waxy, drinking in the dim light. The rooftops are crowned with ferns, their fronds trembling in the faintest breeze, and in the narrow alleys between buildings, mushrooms bloom in phosphorescent clusters, their pale glow just enough to navigate by when the mist thickens.  

The city hums with industry, though it is a quiet, patient sort. Waterwheels turn in hidden channels, their mechanisms rusted but still functional, powering looms and presses that weave cloth from fibrous lichen or press oil from the seeds of rooftop orchids. Chimneys exhale thin streams of smoke, but the scent is not of coal or metal—it is the earthy tang of slow-burning peat, harvested from the bogs that fringe the city. The streets are paved with smooth, water-worn stones, their surfaces worn concave by centuries of footsteps, and the few people who move through them do so with unhurried purpose, their boots whispering against the damp stone.  

There is little animal life here—no birds in the eaves, no rats in the alleys—but the plants seem to compensate, shifting subtly in the absence of wind, their leaves curling or unfurling in response to some unseen signal. The air is thick with the scent of wet earth and crushed greenery, and the only sounds are the distant creak of machinery, the occasional plink of water dripping from a gutter, and the low, resonant hum of the city itself—a sound that might be the vibration of the waterwheels, or something deeper, older, rising from the stone.  

Veldrin is a place of balance, where the built and the grown have long since ceased to struggle against one another. The city does not decay; it adapts. The plants do not overrun; they integrate. And the water, though never abundant, is always present—in the air, in the stone, in the slow, steady pulse of life that defines this place.  

It is not a city for the restless. It is a city for those who understand that growth is slow, that industry can be quiet, and that even in the absence of great motion, there is always something alive.
