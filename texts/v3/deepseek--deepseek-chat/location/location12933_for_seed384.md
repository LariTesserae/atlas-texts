**The Drowned Quarter**  

A sunken city, half-submerged in black water, its rooftops and chimneys breaking the surface like the spines of drowned leviathans. The water is deep here, an endless inkwell swallowing brick and iron, leaving only the tallest structures to gasp above the tide. The air is cold, thick with the scent of rust and sodden wood, and the sky is a low ceiling of bruised clouds that never quite break into rain.  

The buildings are old—centuries of erosion have softened their edges, rounded their corners into smooth, water-worn forms. Their bricks weep algae, their iron balconies sag like the ribs of a starving beast. The streets are canals now, narrow waterways choked with the remnants of industry: floating barrels, tangled cables, the occasional bloated corpse of a machine, its gears rusted into stillness.  

It is a place of silence. The water swallows sound, leaving only the occasional groan of shifting metal, the drip of condensation from a broken gutter. No birds call here. The only movement is the slow sway of submerged curtains in second-story windows, the flicker of bioluminescent worms clinging to the undersides of walkways.  

The city is dense—every inch is claimed by something, even if only by rot. Buildings lean against one another for support, their upper floors bridged by makeshift catwalks of salvaged timber and rope. The few dry spaces are crowded with the detritus of survival: oil lamps flickering behind warped glass, bundles of wire and cloth, the skeletal frames of scavenged furniture.  

Beneath the water, something hums. The remnants of industry still cling to life—a generator coughing in a basement, a turbine turning lazily in the current. The power is sporadic, but when it comes, it lights up the drowned streets in flickering arcs of blue, illuminating the faces of statues half-sunk in the muck, their features worn smooth by time.  

The people here, if there are any, move like ghosts. They live in the upper floors, where the wood is dry enough to burn, and they speak in whispers, as if afraid the water will hear them and rise another inch. They say the city is still sinking. They say the machines beneath the waves are dreaming.  

And sometimes, in the deepest dark, something moves in the water—not a fish, not a rat, but something stranger, something that does not belong.
