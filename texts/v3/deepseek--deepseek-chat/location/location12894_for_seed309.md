**The Root-City of Mycel**  

Beneath the surface, in the damp and breathing dark, the Root-City of Mycel sprawls—a living, industrial labyrinth where the walls are made of flesh and the machines are made of bone.  

The air is warm, thick with the musk of wet cellulose and the slow exhalations of fungal towers that rise like cathedral spires from the cavern floor. Their caps weep a constant, glistening condensation that drips into shallow pools, where blind, skittering things drink and vanish again into the tangled undergrowth of root and vine. The water here is never still—it beads on fibrous walls, trickles through grooves worn by centuries of slow erosion, and gathers in cisterns of hollowed-out mushroom stalks, where it is pumped through pulsing, vein-lined pipes to feed the city’s endless metabolism.  

The vegetation is total. There is no stone, no metal that was not once alive—only the interwoven mass of stems, stalks, and mycelial cables that form the city’s skeleton. Buildings are grown, not built: bulbous chambers swell from the cavern walls, their interiors lined with spongy membranes that flex with the passage of creatures inside. Walkways are braided vines, taut as suspension bridges, swaying underfoot. The city is old, its forms settled into equilibrium, its structures softened by time into curves and hollows that seem almost deliberate, as if the ecosystem itself remembers every footstep, every shift in weight.  

And yet, it is loud. The air thrums with the deep, rhythmic throb of industrial digestion—vast fungal reactors breaking down organic matter into fuel, their chimneys exhaling spores in thick, iridescent plumes. The sound of chitinous limbs scuttling across fibrous floors, of dripping water, of the distant, chittering calls of the city’s overwhelming fauna. They are everywhere: six-legged menders scuttling along the ceilings, their many-jointed limbs adjusting the flow of fluids through the city’s veins; swarms of bioluminescent mites that flicker like faulty wiring; and larger things, too, things that move in the periphery, their bodies half-merged with the architecture, watching with too many eyes.  

Light is dim, but not absent. It glows from bulbous growths on the walls, from the bellies of translucent grubs that crawl along the ceilings, from the faint, shifting luminescence of the fungal towers themselves. The illumination pulses, slow and rhythmic, like the breathing of some vast, unseen creature.  

This is a place of industry, but not human industry. The machines are alive—gears of hardened resin, pistons driven by the contraction of sinew, conveyor belts of woven tendrils carrying bundles of nutrient-rich nodules to be processed. The city works, ceaselessly, but it does not work for anyone. It simply *is*, a self-sustaining cycle of consumption and regrowth, where the line between organism and infrastructure has long since dissolved.  

And above it all, the sound of movement, of life in relentless motion—the hiss of steam from a vent, the shudder of a vine adjusting its grip, the distant, echoing cry of something that has no name.  

Welcome to Mycel. Mind your step. The ground is alive.
