### **The Plateau of Whispering Stone**  

At the edge of the world, where the sky presses down like cold iron, there stretches a vast plateau—an ancient, wind-scoured plain of cracked basalt and lichen-stained dolmens. The air is thin here, sharp with the scent of wet rock and slow decay. The land is old beyond reckoning, its contours worn smooth by eons of whispering winds and the patient gnawing of meltwater. It is a place that has settled into its final form, a landscape that has forgotten the violence of its birth.  

Moisture clings to the stone in veils of mist, pooling in shallow depressions where black water reflects the bruised sky. There are no rivers here, only the occasional seepage from hidden aquifers, bleeding out in slow, silent trickles that vanish into porous rock. The dampness lingers, never enough to flood, never enough to quench—just enough to keep the stone slick with a sheen of condensation, as if the plateau itself is sweating in the cold.  

Vegetation clings in sparse, defiant patches—tufts of wiry grass, clusters of blue-gray lichen, and the occasional gnarled shrub, twisted into bonsai-like submission by the wind. The plants are hardy, deep-rooted, their leaves waxy and resistant to frost. They grow in the lee of boulders, in the cracks of fractured basalt, anywhere the wind cannot scour them away. Yet despite their scarcity, they are lush where they do take hold, their foliage thick and leathery, drinking in the weak sunlight with quiet desperation.  

There is no animal life. No birds call, no insects hum. The only movement is the slow creep of lichen, the shudder of wind through dry grass, the occasional distant rumble of shifting stone. The silence is vast, broken only by the hollow moan of wind through narrow canyons, the drip of water into hidden cisterns, the faint, almost musical vibration of basalt columns humming in the breeze.  

And yet—there is something wrong here.  

The plateau does not obey. The laws of time and space fray at its edges. Shadows stretch too long, then snap back like rubber bands. The horizon sometimes ripples, as if seen through heat haze, though the air is frigid. The rocks whisper to each other in a language of groans and clicks, and if you listen too closely, you might hear words in them—words you do not recognize, but understand anyway.  

In the distance, the ruins of a city rise—low, blocky structures of fitted stone, their edges softened by centuries of wind. There are no doors, only narrow slits in the walls. No smoke rises from them. No lights burn within. The city is empty, but not abandoned. It waits.  

The plateau is old. It is patient. It is watching.  

And it is not entirely of this world.
