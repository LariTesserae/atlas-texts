**The Shattering Spires**  

The world is a wound in the making.  

You stand on a high plateau, its edges jagged and raw, as if the land itself had been torn upward by unseen hands only moments before. The rock is dark, almost black, but shot through with veins of iridescent crystal that pulse faintly in the dim light, like slow, sleeping breaths. There is no water here—no rivers, no rain, no dampness in the air—only a dry, mineral stillness that crackles with static. The air tastes of ozone and powdered stone.  

The plateau stretches for miles, a fractured expanse of basalt and glassy obsidian, interrupted by sudden, towering spires. These are not mountains, not yet—they jut from the ground at impossible angles, some leaning like half-fallen monoliths, others still rising, groaning as they push skyward. The land is *unfinished*, caught in the act of becoming.  

And yet, it is lush.  

Between the cracks, in the hollows where the rock has split, thick carpets of vegetation thrive. Not leaves, not grass, but something stranger—fibrous, translucent growths that shimmer like wet silk, their surfaces shifting between colors as if reflecting a sky that isn’t there. They have no roots, or at least none you can see; they simply *are*, sprouting from bare stone, drinking nothing, needing nothing. Some are tall as trees, their tendrils weaving into the air like frozen smoke. Others cling low, pulsing softly, exhaling a scent like burnt sugar and iron.  

There is no animal life. No birds, no insects, no scuttling things in the undergrowth. Only the plants, and the wind—if it can be called wind. It does not blow; it *presses*, a constant, deafening drone, a sound like the hum of distant machinery or the groan of tectonic plates grinding against one another. It never stops. It is the voice of the land itself, speaking in a language of pressure and vibration.  

Scattered across the plateau are structures—or perhaps they are accidents. Half-formed arches of fused stone, leaning slabs that might have been walls once, before the ground tilted beneath them. Some are clearly made by hands, rough-hewn and simple, their surfaces marked with crude carvings that shift when you look away. Others seem to have grown from the earth, extruded like the spires, their angles too sharp, their geometries unsettling.  

The light is dim, sourceless. There is no sun, only a dull glow that seeps from the horizon, casting long, wavering shadows. The crystals in the rock flicker in response, their light uneven, as if struggling against some greater dark.  

And then there are the moments when the world *flickers*.  

A spire that was whole is suddenly split down the middle, its halves hovering inches apart. A patch of the luminous vegetation vanishes, replaced by a smooth, glassy pit, before reappearing a breath later. The sound swells, then fractures into something like voices, whispering in reverse. The plateau is not stable. It is not *real* in the way other places are real. It is a sketch, a draft, a thing still deciding what it will be.  

You walk carefully. The ground might open beneath you. The air might thicken into something you cannot breathe. The plants might decide to remember you—or forget you.  

This is a place at the edge of existence, where the rules are still being written.
