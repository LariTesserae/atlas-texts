**The Glass Hollow**  

Deep in the jagged peaks of a mountain range that scrapes the sky, there is a place where the stone has been hollowed out—not by water, for there is none here, but by some older, stranger force. The walls of the chasm are sheer, polished smooth as if by a god’s hand, their surfaces glazed over with a thin, brittle film of mineral deposit that catches the dim light in fractured glimmers. The air is cool, almost brittle itself, and carries the faint metallic tang of something not quite rust, not quite salt.  

The floor of the hollow is a riot of life—but not animal life. No scuttling things, no fluttering wings, no breath but the slow exhalations of the plants themselves. Thick, rubbery fronds unfurl from cracks in the stone, their surfaces glossy and dark, drinking in what little light filters down from above. They grow in dense, overlapping layers, each leaf pressing against the next, leaving no space uncovered. Some are broad and flat, others spined and segmented, all of them pulsing faintly with a slow, internal rhythm, as if they are breathing. Their colors are deep—forest greens tinged with blue, purples so dark they are nearly black, veins of iridescence that shift when viewed from different angles.  

Scattered among them are the remnants of industry: a rusted generator half-swallowed by creeping vines, the skeletal frame of a crane bent under the weight of time, a single brick wall standing alone, its mortar long since crumbled away. The machines are silent now, but their presence lingers in the hum of residual energy, a subsonic vibration that thrums through the ground. The sound is not mechanical, not anymore—it has been absorbed, transformed, woven into the strange ecology of the place. The plants seem to resonate with it, their leaves trembling in unison with some unheard frequency.  

The hollow is sealed, a pocket world unto itself. The wind does not reach here, nor does rain. The air is still, thick with the scent of chlorophyll and ozone. The only movement comes from the plants themselves, their slow, deliberate growth, the way they shift and adjust in increments too small to see but felt in the bones. Time here is not absent, but it is sluggish, caught in a loop of near-perfect equilibrium. Nothing decays, not truly. Nothing changes.  

And yet, there is something watching. Not with eyes, but with presence. The plants are too dense, too aware, their roots too deep. They remember the machines. They remember the hands that built them. And they are waiting—patient, relentless, utterly still—for whatever comes next.
