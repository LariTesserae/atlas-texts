**The Weeping Hollows**  

A vast, sunken cathedral of stone and moss, where the air itself is thick with moisture, clinging to every surface like the breath of some ancient, slumbering beast. The Hollows are a place of slow decay and quiet saturation—a land where time has worn the world down to its bones, where water seeps from the cracks of primordial rock as if the earth itself is weeping.  

The terrain is a labyrinth of low, rounded bluffs and shallow basins, their edges softened by millennia of erosion. The stone here is dark, veined with luminescent lichen that glows faintly in the perpetual twilight, casting eerie halos over the damp ground. The air is warm, thick with the scent of wet earth and the faint, metallic tang of minerals leached from the rock.  

Water is everywhere, though never in great torrents—only in sighs. It beads on the walls of the Hollows, drips from the ceiling of overhangs in slow, irregular rhythms, gathers in shallow pools that ripple with unseen currents. The ground is spongy underfoot, a dense mat of moss and mycelium that muffles sound, swallowing footsteps whole.  

Vegetation thrives in the dimness—great curtains of hanging vines, broad-leafed ferns that unfurl like sleeping hands, clusters of bioluminescent fungi that pulse softly in the dark. The flora is lush but strange, adapted to the Hollows’ slow, eternal dampness. Some plants seem to move when unobserved, their leaves twitching as if stirred by a wind that does not exist.  

The fauna is abundant but elusive. Small, many-legged creatures scuttle through the undergrowth, their carapaces glistening with condensation. Larger things move in the deeper shadows, their forms indistinct, their presence marked only by the occasional ripple in the water or the distant, echoing call of something that might not be an animal at all.  

Human presence is scarce—only scattered remnants, half-swallowed by the moss. A crumbling archway of smooth, glassy stone, its purpose long forgotten. A rusted metal apparatus, its gears frozen in place, humming faintly with some residual energy. The Hollows do not reject civilization so much as ignore it, absorbing its traces without comment.  

The soundscape is a murmur of dripping water, the creak of shifting stone, the occasional, resonant hum of unseen machinery buried deep beneath the earth. The darkness is not oppressive but enveloping, a velvet shroud that muffles the edges of the world.  

Time here is not linear but cyclical, measured in the slow growth of roots through cracks, the patient dissolution of rock into silt. The Hollows are old—older than memory, older than history. They are a place where the world has settled into its final form, where change comes not in bursts but in sighs, where the land itself exhales, and the water answers.
