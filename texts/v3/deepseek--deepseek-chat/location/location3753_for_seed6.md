### **The Drowned Spires**  

A city of black iron and glass rises from the depths, its spires piercing the surface of a warm, sluggish sea. The water is everywhere—lapping at balconies, pooling in courtyards, filling the lower levels entirely—but the city does not drown. It thrives.  

The buildings are old, but not ancient. Their edges are sharp, their forms unweathered, as if they were forged yesterday and plunged into the ocean to cool. The metal is dark, almost blue where the light catches it, and the glass is thick, warped, distorting the world beyond into rippling abstractions. The streets are narrow canals, the sidewalks elevated walkways of riveted steel. Every surface is covered—not in rust, but in a creeping, luminous moss that clings to the metal like a second skin, pulsing faintly in the dim light.  

The air is thick with the smell of salt and oil, of warm machinery humming beneath the water. There are no birds, no fish, no sound but the slow drip of condensation from pipes and the occasional groan of metal adjusting to the weight of the sea. The city is silent, but not dead—merely waiting.  

The water is clear, unnaturally so. Looking down from a walkway, you can see deep into the submerged levels, where drowned corridors stretch into darkness. Sometimes, a light flickers in the depths—a submerged lamp, still burning, or something else. The seafloor is invisible, lost in the abyss below.  

The city is small, confined to a single island or perhaps a cluster of structures built atop some sunken foundation. There are no trees, no soil—only the moss, feeding on the metal, growing in dense, velvety patches. The light is diffuse, filtered through a perpetual haze of mist and steam. The sun, if it exists here, is a dull glow behind the clouds.  

Time moves slowly. The water rises and falls in imperceptible tides. The moss spreads, inch by inch, year by year. The city does not decay—it adapts. The machines beneath the waves still turn, their purpose long forgotten, their mechanisms now part of the ecosystem.  

This is a place of industry without workers, of life without creatures, of water that does not erode but preserves. A place where the boundary between the made and the natural has dissolved. A place where the sea does not reclaim—it becomes.  

And in the quiet, in the slow pulse of the moss-light, the city waits. For what, no one knows.
