**The Dripping Warrens of Kharzul**  

Beneath the sun-scorched surface of a vast, cracked plain lies the subterranean warren-city of Kharzul—a labyrinth of damp stone and humming industry, where the air is thick with the smell of wet metal and the echoes of dripping water.  

The caverns here are **young**, their walls still sharp-edged, their ceilings jagged with stalactites like the teeth of some great buried beast. Water seeps through fissures in the rock, pooling in shallow basins or running in thin, erratic rivulets down carved channels. The moisture never floods, never dries—just lingers, condensing on pipes and machinery, beading on rusted iron like sweat.  

**Sparse vegetation** clings to the edges of the caverns—pale, stringy mosses that thrive in the dim glow of flickering electric bulbs, their roots threading through cracks in the stone. There are no animals here, no skittering insects or blind cave fish—just the slow, silent growth of these ghostly plants, feeding on the damp and the faint warmth of the machines.  

The city itself is **dense**, a claustrophobic hive of narrow tunnels and tiered chambers, all carved or blasted into the living rock. Buildings rise in crooked stacks, their foundations bolted into the cavern walls, their upper levels leaning precariously over the abyss below. Walkways of woven steel cables and riveted plating crisscross the open spaces, trembling underfoot as distant machinery thrums.  

This is an **industrial** place, but not one of roaring furnaces or towering factories. Instead, the industry here is small-scale, intricate—workshops where artisans coax reluctant steam engines to life, where copper wires snake along the ceilings, carrying erratic currents to flickering bulbs. The air hums with the sound of pistons and the occasional hiss of a pressure valve releasing.  

Light is **dim**, provided mostly by the fitful glow of electric filaments or the occasional lantern burning some thick, slow oil. Shadows stretch long and liquid across the damp stone, shifting as the bulbs sputter. The soundscape is **mostly quiet**, but never still—dripping water, the groan of settling rock, the distant clank of tools.  

And yet, despite its stillness, the warrens are **active**. The rock itself shifts minutely, new cracks forming as the earth settles. The moss creeps forward, inch by patient inch. The machines, though sluggish, are always working, always processing, always turning the latent heat of the depths into something usable.  

It is a place of **unusual** logic—a city built not for people, exactly, but for the slow, stubborn persistence of industry in a world that doesn’t quite want it. The air is close, the walls press in, and the water never stops dripping. But life—such as it is—goes on.
