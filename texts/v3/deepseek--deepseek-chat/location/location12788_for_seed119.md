**The Weeping Hall of the Drowned Builders**  

The air is thick with the scent of wet stone and slow decay. You stand in a vast, low-ceilinged chamber—not quite a cavern, not quite a cathedral—carved from some ancient, waterlogged material that weeps constantly. The walls glisten with moisture, their surfaces etched with the marks of crude tools, as if the entire space had been hollowed out by hand over centuries. The floor is a shallow basin, never quite dry, never quite flooded, just a film of water that ripples faintly with unseen movements.  

The chamber is dim, lit only by the phosphorescent glow of pale, slug-like creatures that cling to the walls in sparse clusters. Their light is weak, but enough to reveal the strangeness of the place: the walls are not smooth, but textured with countless handprints, pressed deep into the stone as if it had once been soft as clay. Some of the prints are human-sized; others are too large, too elongated, or bear too many fingers.  

The sound is overwhelming—not a roar, but a layered cacophony. Water drips from above in irregular rhythms, plinking into the shallow pools. Something unseen scrapes against stone in the dark, a slow, deliberate sound, like a tool being dragged. And beneath it all, a low, droning hum, as if the chamber itself is breathing.  

Life thrives here, though it is not the life of forests or fields. Blind, translucent fish dart through the water, their bodies so thin they seem like ghosts. Larger things move in the deeper shadows—things with too many legs, or none at all, their forms shifting as they pass through the dim light. The air is alive with the flutter of leathery wings, though you never quite see what makes the sound.  

This is not a place that was built and abandoned. It is a place that was built *into being*, and still is being built, by hands unseen. The water is not an invader here—it is part of the architecture, seeping through the walls as if by design. The chamber feels ancient, settled into its own slow erosion, yet also alive with motion, as if the builders never left, only became something else.  

You are not sure if you are standing in a ruin, a workshop, or a shrine. Perhaps it is all three. The only certainty is that you are not alone.
