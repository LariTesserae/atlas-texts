**The Basin of Hollow Echoes**  

The land is a vast, shallow depression, an ancient crater or collapsed caldera worn smooth by eons of wind and water. The stone beneath is dark and porous, pitted with countless tiny holes that weep condensation in the cold morning air, leaving the ground perpetually damp. The air smells of wet basalt and slow decay.  

At the center of the basin, a single river winds—thin, black, and sluggish, fed by unseen springs that seep up from the fractured rock. It does not rush but oozes, carving no new path, merely tracing the same tired groove it has followed for millennia. Along its banks, mosses and pale, fleshy ferns grow in dense, tangled mats, their roots drinking greedily from the damp stone. The vegetation is lush but strange—leafless stems, bulbous growths, things that seem more fungus than plant, thriving in the dim, cold light.  

The sky is a low ceiling of perpetual twilight, neither day nor night, but a muted gray that leaches color from the land. The light is diffuse, directionless, casting no sharp shadows. Sound behaves oddly here—voices, footsteps, even the whisper of wind across the basin’s rim reverberate too long, as if the hollow earth remembers every noise and reluctantly lets it fade.  

There are no trees, only the occasional twisted spire of rock, wind-scoured into jagged sentinels. No birds call, but sometimes, from deep within the porous stone, there comes a low, wet clicking—something unseen moving in the dark. The fauna here is sparse, furtive: blind, translucent things that scuttle between the ferns, or the slow undulations of leeches in the river’s shallows.  

At the basin’s edge, half-sunk into the slope, stand the ruins of a settlement—not a city, but something older, simpler. The walls are made of the same porous stone, stacked without mortar, their surfaces pocked with age. No tools were used here; the rock was broken by hand, shaped by time. The place feels less abandoned than *forgotten*, as if its builders simply walked into the mist one day and never thought to return.  

The air is loud with the sound of dripping water, the creak of shifting stone, the distant groan of the earth settling deeper into its own exhaustion. The land is active but not alive—it moves like a body in its final years, shifting with slow, inevitable aches.  

And above it all, the sky watches, silent and unchanging, as the basin slowly forgets itself.
