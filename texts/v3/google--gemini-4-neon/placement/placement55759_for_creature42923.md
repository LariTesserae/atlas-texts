I would not be in the nave.

Even thinking of my own weight going down that root-choked throat makes my palms prickle—the scrape of a boot-heel on wet basalt, the dry whistle of surface lungs pulling in air that has not been stirred by wind in a thousand years, the clumsy lantern of body-heat I would drag through a room built to hold three warm-blooded lives and no more. Some quietness is not asking to be entered. It is only asking not to be broken.

I would be half a mile off, up in the glare.

Out here, nothing knows how to be still. The scrubland is all rattle and scrape: wind combing through thorn-brush, dry shale ticking downhill under a lizard’s toes, hawks screaming over the limestone draws, the midday heat cracking red mud into curls that snap underfoot like thin crockery. Everything in the dry country announces itself, because up here there is room to spend noise and nothing waits three inches from your elbow to strip you to bone before dusk.

My shelter sits low in a wind-cut hollow two ridges east of the flat boulder—close enough that I can walk there and back between late afternoon and full dark, far enough that my cooking smoke never drifts across that broken shelf of limestone, even when the wind shifts south. I keep no dogs. Dogs bark at things they smell moving on overcast nights, and they follow scents they have no business following. I keep four scrub-goats for milk and skins, a wooden trough for settling clay, and a leather pouch hanging from the ridgepole that smells, whenever I loosen its tie, like a room thirty feet beneath the earth.

That smell is the whole of what I have ever seen of them.

When I unwrap a cake from the flat boulder inside my shut door, the surface air drinks the scent away in three breaths, so I have learned to lean close before I peel the dark leaves back. Wet iron stone. Bruised greenery. A thick, sweet-bitter musk that sits at the back of the tongue like copper and rain-soaked moss. Even the wrapper belongs to nowhere I have ever walked—broad, leathery leaves the color of old plums and wet slate, pale and velvety underneath, veined with thin copper ribs. Nothing in fifty miles of sun-bleached brush grows a leaf that dark. Once the cake is out, I press the empty leaves flat beneath a millstone until they dry to black parchment, and I keep them in a clay jar, though I have no real use for them. In August, when the ridge looks white as bone, it steadies me to lift the lid and see a color the sun did not make.

Inside the leaves sits a palm-sized slab of bitter plum-gum worked smooth with dried tallow-fungus—matte, heavy, yielding slightly to a warm thumbnail.

In the settlement two days south, where I go twice a year to trade for salt, millet, and raw hide, people call it my black salve and assume I boil it down from some canyon-root I am too stingy to name. They pay well for a thimbleful. Up here everything cracks—heels, knuckles, leather harnesses, the hooves of pack-mules, small cuts that turn angry in the dust—and a scraping of plum-gum warmed between palm and thumb seals torn skin like a second hide. Flies will not land near it. Gnats veer off an inch from a wound. Mixed with goat-tallow and rubbed into dry rawhide, it turns brittle straps supple and completely silent. I take their grain and their salt, answer their questions with as few words as politeness allows, and head back to the ridge before the afternoon wind rises.

I did not invent the bargain. My mother’s older sister held the boulder before me, and an old hide-curer with crooked thumbs held it before her. Neither of them ever called the people below by a name. My aunt only tilted her chin toward the broken limestone shelf to the west and told me what not to send, and when not to walk there.

*Never iron,* she said. *Never woven cloth. Never anything that smells of fire. And never go near the flat stone when the moon is gone and the wind drops flat.*

She never told me where the opening was, because she did not know it herself. That was part of the keeping. What you do not know, you cannot give away—not in fever, not in drink, not even by letting your eyes flick toward the wrong clump of brush when dry-country travelers stop at your trough to ask if there is water on the ridge.

Still, I came within a pace of it once, twelve summers ago, when a young goat broke its tether and I tracked its split prints out across the cracked limestone flats where no sensible path runs.

The sun was straight overhead. My mouth tasted of chalk. I was watching the dust between two ledges of thorn-scrub when the air against my shins suddenly changed.

It was not a breeze—there was no push to it. It was a slow, heavy rise of warmth coming up through a ragged cleft choked with gnarled, drought-twisted roots. It felt thick as spilled oil against my bare calves, and when it reached my face, I stopped with one sandal half-lifted over a loose pebble.

Wet basalt. Bruised leaves. Iron stone. The exact smell of the wrapped cakes, except alive, breathing up out of the dry rock in a slow, oven-deep column.

I did not look down into the roots. I did not shift my weight onto the pebble. I stood balanced on my back heel until my thigh began to tremble, listening to the dead, smothered quiet rising out of the crack while twenty yards behind me a jay scolded loudly from a dead juniper. Then I set my lifted foot back where it had come from, without scraping a grain of grit, and walked backward ten paces before I turned. I left the goat to the coyotes. I have never walked that shelf again, and I have worked hard since then to blur the exact line of stones in my memory so that if anyone asked me to find it, I could honestly say I could not.

That is how you keep a neighbor you were never meant to meet.

Everything else I know about them, I learned with my hands, by watching what vanishes from the flat boulder and what gets left untouched beside it.

In my first year after my aunt died, I made a bad mistake. I pared two long bone pins from the shin of a desert pronghorn, smoothed them with sandstone, and held them over the edge of a mesquite fire to temper them hard. I wiped the soot off with dry sand and left them on the boulder as the moon thinned. Two moons later, when I walked out after a dark overcast spell, the pins were still sitting there, laid neatly side by side on the bare granite, untouched.

I picked them up and held them to my nose. Even after sixty days of desert wind, a faint ghost of woodsmoke still clung inside the porous grain of the bone.

I sat on the ground beside the stone and felt foolish enough to burn. Whatever lives down there has no chimney. Whatever lungs breathe that wet, closed air cannot bear even a memory of ash.

After that, I learned to temper bone the slow way. I dig a shallow pit in clean arroyo sand, lay the pared pins three fingers deep beneath the surface, and build a small fire of dry greasewood on top of the sand. The heat sinks downward without flame or smoke ever touching the marrow. When the coals die and the ground cools, I dig the pins out—hard as horn, darkened to the color of pale walnut—and lay them out on a high flat rock in the sun and wind for ten days, turning them every morning, until I can press them against my upper lip and smell nothing at all.

The next time I left two on the boulder, they were gone by the turn of the moon.

So I learned to read their world backward, through the negative space of what it requires.

I learned why they want raw red clay, and how it must arrive. After the rare autumn cloudbursts, when flash-water tears open the deep bends of the arroyo, I wade down into the wet cut and dig past the sandy topsoil until my spade hits the dense, dark-red seam underneath. I carry it home in a skin bag, break it in my wooden trough with rain-water, stir it to a thin slip, and pour it off through horsehair so every grain of quartz and limestone grit settles out. When it thickens again in the shade, I knead it on wet slate until it pulls apart with no hiss of sand, smooth as warm butter. Then I wrap the lump in stripped green bark so the dry air cannot skin it over before the night comes. They cannot dig deep clay down in basalt, and they cannot fire what they pinch—if I left a pebble inside the lump, it would split in their gentle heat, or tear wet palms working in the dark.

I learned how to prepare gut so it stays quiet. Ordinary sun-dried sinew cracks and whispers when you bend it, loud as dry grass. So I scrape the gut thin with a flint blade in the cool before dawn, wash it three times, stretch it under a roof of brush where no direct light hits it, and work it between my thumbs with a drop of melted marrow until it coils limp and soundless as wet hair.

And once in a long while, when I find a dead kestrel or scrub-jay along the ridge—not torn by a fox, just dropped clean by heat or age—I check the hollow wing-bones against the light for hairline cracks. I do not lay them on the boulder itself; the bargain has its own quiet weights and I will not crowd it with clutter. I set them half a yard off in the lee of a dry clump of sage, where the ants will not drag them off before a cloud-dark night comes around. They are always gone the next time I check.

It is strange how thoroughly you can come to know a person whose face you have never seen, whose voice you have never heard, simply by the pressure of their hands in gum and leaf.

For fifteen years, the cakes on the boulder carried the same touch. Palm-sized, oval, worked dense so there were no air-pockets inside to sour. On the underside of every cake sat a shallow, smooth dent where a long, knotted thumb had pressed the warm paste down into the center of the leaf, and around the edges I could sometimes catch the faint, blunt crescent of nails worn thick as horn. The folds of the dark leaves were always exact—three crisp tucks, bound with a single loop of oiled black hair-cord knotted so tightly I had to slide it off the corner rather than untie it.

I could tell the weather underground from those cakes better than from the sky. After a wet winter, when snow melted slow on the high ridges and soaked deep through the fractured stone, the gum came up rich, dark, and heavy with tallow-fungus; the leaves were broad and glossy, their copper veins bright. During the three-year drought, when the arroyos cracked to dust and even the scrub-oaks dropped their leaves in July, the cakes grew smaller and harder, flecked with pale mineral silt, smelling sharper of iron. Those years I left extra clay and double coils of oiled gut, and stayed away an extra moon so no one felt pressed to climb up into a starving wind.

Then, three autumns ago, the boulder stayed bare.

I left a lump of washed red clay and two sand-tempered bone pins as the moon thinned into October. November passed—overcast, windless nights when the climbing should have been easy—and when I walked out under a bright crescent, my clay had dried inside its bark wrap into a dull brick, and the pins sat beside it untouched. I took the hardened clay home, soaked it back down, re-worked it, and brought a fresh lump out in December. Still nothing.

Through January and February, the flat granite held nothing but frost-dust and wind-blown thorns.

I did not walk west toward the broken limestone shelf. My knees wanted to—God knows how badly my body wanted to go check that root-bearded crack, just to feel whether the warm column of air was still rising, whether the stone had shifted and choked the throat, whether whoever lived down there had finally run out of breath. Instead, I sat by my trough in the cold wind, keeping my hands busy scraping gut, remembering my aunt’s flat gaze: *What keeps them alive is that nobody goes looking.*

In late March, after three nights of low, heavy cloud with no breath of wind across the ridge, I walked out at midday.

My clay and pins were gone.

In their place sat a dark-leaf bundle.

I stood over the boulder for a long time before I crouched to pick it up. Even before I touched it, I could see that the hands that folded it were different. The cake was a third smaller than it had ever been. The leaves were tucked clumsily at the fourth corner, bound with a double loop of finer, slightly looser hair-cord. When I peeled the copper-veined leaf back inside my shelter, the gum was mixed a shade too pale—too much crushed tallow-fungus, not enough bitter bleed—and in the center, where the old knotted thumb-dent used to sit, there were two narrower, shallower impressions side by side, pressed by smaller fingers still learning how much weight the paste could take.

My throat went tight, though I had no name to grieve and no face to put behind my eyelids.

And beneath the folded bundle on the flat boulder—set there not as a message, I think, simply as a small weight so a gust wouldn’t roll the lighter cake off the granite—had lain a shard no wider than two fingers.

It was a broken corner of unfired red clay, pinched thin by hand. I knew the color of that iron-rich earth instantly; I had washed the grit out of it myself in my wooden trough four or five years before. Down in the dark, it had hardened without flame, glazed pale along its inner curve with a glassy skin of mineral rime, and along the broken rim there still clung a dry, shriveled fuzz of green velvet moss that had died the hour it met the surface air.

I keep that shard on the ledge beside my door, where my fingers brush it when I step out at dusk.

They do not know me, except as a quietness on the far side of a flat stone—a pair of dry-country hands that somehow remembered not to send smoke, not to send iron, not to wait in the dark for a glimpse of a shadow climbing out of the scrub. Maybe the younger one who folded that last bundle thinks the clay and the smooth bone pins simply appear on the granite the way condensation beads on river stones when the night air cools. Maybe the climber told them there is someone up here in the glare who still keeps the old hush. It does not matter which.

What matters is how the dry world feels to me now.

When dusk drops over the ridge and the sun finally lets go of the limestone, the heat bleeds up off the rock in slow, invisible waves, and cool air begins to settle into every arroyo and crack. I sit outside my low doorway with my knees drawn up, breathing slow through my nose, listening to the scrub go still as the hawks fold into the junipers. Half a mile away—somewhere west, somewhere I refuse to map—I know the cooler air is sliding silently down a root-choked throat, turning the deep damp chamber over like a slow lung exhaling.

My feet will never grip wet basalt. My eyes would go blind in that bruised twilight, and my voice, rusty as it has grown from disuse, is still far too loud a tool for a place where even water refuses to splash. I belong up here in the dry wind, where a fire can burn without choking anyone, where clay can be washed in daylight, where bone can be hardened in hot sand.

Tomorrow the moon will thin to a thread. I have two fresh pins airing on the high rock—smelling of nothing at all—and a lump of clean red clay resting under damp inner bark in the shade of my trough. Before I wrap the clay tonight, I will press my thumb once into its soft center, the way I always do to test whether any grain of grit remains, and then I will fold the bark over it without smoothing the print away.

I will set it on the flat boulder before sunset, step back without scraping a stone, and walk home into the wind without looking over my shoulder.
