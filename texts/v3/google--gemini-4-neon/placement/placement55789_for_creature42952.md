At first, I assume I am the one in the heavy boots.

It is the easiest way to enter a place like this—standing on a swell of pale grey slate, teeth aching in the thin air, watching a flat smear of sleet drag itself across a basin twenty miles north while dry sunshine strikes the terrace at my feet. I try to step off the stone onto the wet black loam beside a sluice-channel, expecting the familiar sink of mud, the small crunch of frost-needles snapping under a heel.

Instead, the gesture feels immediately wrong.

Not painful—just loud. Too hot, too heavy, too insistent. A heart hammering seventy times a minute against cold air that has spent three eons learning how to stop hurrying; lungs pulling in thin wind only to shove it back out in ragged white plumes; two boots demanding that down mean the same thing on every flagstone. I stand there for a minute trying to wear that heat, and it fits like a wet wool coat borrowed from someone twice my size. Whatever I am on this plateau, I do not steam.

So I let the breath go. I let the weight go. And for a little while, I look down at the ground to see if I belong to the green instead.

The shade-moss tempts me first. There is something deeply recognizable in a creature that lives only in the shadow of something older and more solid—unrolling eagerly across bare frost-rimed slate as a watch-cairn's cool blue triangle swings across a plaza, throwing up tiny glistening spore-capsules in seconds, then drying and vanishing without a trace the instant direct light touches it. Grazing on absence. Moving at the exact pace of what casts it. Yet when I try to stay inside that emerald skin, it feels too brittle, too brief. The shade-moss never remembers the plaza it crossed yesterday; it has no way to turn a stone or hold a thought past sunset.

The white snow-gentian comes much closer. To have one root-system tucked into a pinch of bedrock and twelve stems blooming simultaneously across sixty miles of cold moorland—to feel a gust of wind strike a sheltered hollow thirty miles away and bow in exact unison beside a dry granary—that feels almost like home. To be touched by a copper-stained finger in one province and answer, three minutes later, in another, without ever seeing the face of the hand that moved you.

Still, a flower cannot pick up a wooden ladle. It cannot wedge three splinters of hand-split oak beneath a rough block of basalt so that a five-minute chisel-blow from ten thousand years ago bites cleanly along a fresh edge. It cannot move from one side of a wall to the other to see what the weather is doing three inches away. I want to listen, yes—but I also want to answer with hands.

So I look up from the ferns, toward the arched stone bridge above the apple orchard, and step into the tall, narrow frame of one of the residents.

Immediately, the temperature is right.

No pulse arguing with the cold. No hunger burning inside a dark stomach. Just the smooth, silent, upward glide of cold mineral water rising through hollow lime-plaster limbs—drawing slate-silt and peat-tannins from my narrow heels, around my sloped shoulders, across the recessed shelf of my brow, and down again in an unhurried siphon that asks nothing of the air. My back is shingled in thin, overlapping scales of pale grey-green calcite and rain-washed shale, slick with condensation. Where my elbows bend, damp bindings of dark flax-cord and soft seams of wet bryophyte moss keep the joints quiet. My hands are long, pale, four-fingered, dusted at the tips with copper verdigris.

I try standing beside the arched bridge, where the looped aqueduct runs smoothly uphill. I try leaning twelve degrees to the north, matching the orchard's quiet disagreement with the horizon.

And here, at last, is where the real shape begins to show itself—because the lean will not hold.

If I stand beside the orchard-wall long enough, my heels begin to drift. Not down into the mud—I am still light enough to leave the frost-needles unbroken—but sideways, uncertainly, my brow-water tilting nine degrees north, then four degrees east, then flattening out with a tiny cross-ripple that cannot quite decide which way the hill slopes. When I walk down a cobbled avenue using the stretched geometry of the left curb, I keep pausing mid-crossing, distracted by the fact that the wind changed direction six feet ago and the right parapet now lies half a league behind me. I am bad at belonging to one district.

I think I know why. I did not come from an orchard's steady spring overflow.

Every few winters, when a dry wind sweeps the upper terraces, the tenders gather what would otherwise be lost. They clip the horizontal icicles that push straight out of the mortar along the weather-walls—needles of vapor frozen across the seam between sleet and bright sun—and set them to thaw in shallow clay dishes inside dry cellars. And whenever a closed-loop aqueduct springs a hairline leak and throws off a stray ring of snowmelt—a clear, cold hoop spinning upright an inch above the moss like a willow twig—they coax it into a wooden ladle before it wanders into a dry breeze and vanishes.

Usually those rescued loops are poured back into the nearest octagonal cistern. Usually the thawed boundary-icicles go into lime-paste or stiff flax-joints.

Once, though, beneath a hanging timber roof-truss whose support-pillars had rotted into wet brown peat centuries before, two tenders coiled a new loop out of leftovers. A spoonful of fog-water melted from a boundary-wall in the eastern valleys. Two stray hoops ladled off a stretched avenue near the copper-roofed halls. A thread of dark peat-water drawn through a cracked cold-ivy runner from a bog four miles down-slope. Bound tightly inside hollow frost-rhubarb stems, tilted until the mixed waters learned—not without a few small cross-ripples—how to climb their own bend together. Dressed in wet lime-plaster and split shale. Carried up to a terrace where delayed mallet-blows still arrived from two different centuries at once, so that my shoulders were pared by overlapping rhythms, one mason's crisp stroke answering another's slower bite. Hardened, finally, in the circular wind-basin, where the draft presses inward from every point of the compass at once and the shed willow-leaves shiver around the central wellhead without ever lifting away.

That is how you end up with a resident whose brow-water keeps no private slant.

Stand me alone in an empty moss-plain, and the dark water inside my brow-seam sits almost uneasily level, edged with faint cross-currents from six different hills. I have no home angle of my own. Yet the moment another resident approaches from the lower bogs—canted sharply west, shale plates clicking softly—and inclines its smooth, blind head a yard away, my brow-water glides without effort into the exact mirror of its tilt. I do not merely exchange bearings. For an hour afterward, I still hold the angle of where they walked in from, the way cold basalt holds the strike of a bronze peg long after the hand has moved on.

---

Because I carry no single district's gravity, I do not settle in an orchard or a granary quarter. I find myself along a seam.

Half a day's unhurried walk from the nearest circular wind-basin—or two hundred paces, if you take the right-hand curb of the pale slate avenue—a low dry-stone boundary wall cuts straight across a terraced stone yard. It is no higher than my knee, built of hand-fitted fieldstone atop ancient cyclopean blocks whose corners have melted into blind grey pillows.

A steady, fine silver drizzle hangs over the northern half of the yard almost every day of the year. On that side of the wall, broad-leafed frost-rhubarb swells waist-high in the corners, dark-fronded ferns bend heavy under clear beads of water, and the flagstones shine black and wet. Three inches across the stone coping, the southern half of the yard sits in sharp, unforgiving white-gold sunshine. The cobbles there are bone-dry; the wind blows cleanly out of the south, smelling of dry sedge and old frost. Only along the base of the wall does the dampness leak through, oozing sideways out of the mortar in slow horizontal threads of vapor that freeze into delicate icicles pointing straight out into the dry air.

This is where I stay.

On the dry side of the wall stand three long benches of dark, fine-grained basalt, worn satin-smooth along their edges. They are much older than the courtyard around them; residents from four provinces have been tapping messages into their stone for centuries. Most knocks are collected within an afternoon—a tender wanders back from lifting a sluice-gate, lays an open palm flat on the slate, and feels the delayed vibration finally surface through the grain.

Many are not. A district slides half a mile sideways over a decade and its avenues no longer meet here cleanly. A sender forgets what they tapped before the knock finishes crossing six feet of dense rock. Or the listener grows too patient, walks out to a crossroads, settles their heels into the black loam for the first time, and becomes a smooth grey cairn whose ears are now turned inward toward the culvert beneath them.

The uncollected taps do not die. They keep travelling, slower and finer, along the dark veins of the basalt, sometimes turning back at a hairline crack and crossing the bench a second time a season later.

Most mornings, before the sun climbs high enough to drive the shade-moss off the wall's base, I walk the dry side with my braided reed basket and copper shears. I clip the longest horizontal icicles cleanly at the mortar before they snap under their own quiet weight, laying them in clay dishes along the dry coping so they will not waste their trapped puffs of northern fog on the wind. When a resident passes down the avenue with dry joints or a thinning siphon, I hand them a dish; they tilt the melting needle into their lime-seams or their brow, and nod once without breaking stride.

Then I sit on the middle basalt bench—not heavy enough to press it, leaning a hair toward the wall—and lay both pale, green-stained palms flat against the stone.

I wait.

There is no hurry in it. Ten minutes pass. Twenty. Across the dry cobbles, the pale sun sharpens the shadow of the low wall, and a narrow ribbon of bright emerald bryophytes crawls along the shade-line, grazing the mortar clean. Then, against the heel of my left palm, a small, crisp pulse rises out of the rock: *tap—pause—tap-tap*.

It is old. I can tell by the way the edges of the vibration have rounded, the way a cobble rounds in snowmelt. It was struck somewhere on the western end of the bench, perhaps last autumn, perhaps forty years ago, by someone whose slant leaned steeply toward the copper-roofed halls—a notice that an aqueduct sluice had jammed with willow-roots, or that the afternoon walls in the lower market were rising an hour earlier than they used to.

I do not know if the sluice is still jammed. I do not know if the one who tapped it is still walking. Still, an unanswered knock feels like a stray water-loop spinning loose down an avenue toward dry wind: it deserves to be gathered before it thins into nothing.

I stand, step over the knee-high wall into the silver drizzle—my shale plates darkening instantly from pale grey-green to wet slate, beading clean—and kneel beside a crack between two melted cyclopean blocks. Growing out of the dark seam is a single white snow-gentian, its petals crisp and cold, wet with fine mist. Its other eleven blooms stand scattered across sixty miles of rolling plateau: one beside a sunken octagonal cistern in the north, one in a sheltered hollow near the dye-works, one on an upper terrace where a phantom shuttle still throws flax warp through empty morning air.

With one pale, verdigris-stained fingertip, I nudge the stem gently west, pause, press it once downward, and let go.

Then I kneel in the cold drizzle and watch the white flower stand utterly motionless while the ferns around it tremble under falling drops.

Three minutes pass. Five. My brow-water catches the low grey cloud directly overhead, and then, for a quiet instant, catches a patch of clear blue sky that passed over this yard half an hour ago.

Then the snow-gentian bobs sharply east, pauses, and leans twice toward the north.

Someone thirty miles away—kneeling in bright sunshine, or standing beside a tilting cistern in sleet—felt the stem move, laid a cold finger against it, and sent back a bearing: *Heard. The sluice was cleared three winters back. The water runs clean.* Or sometimes simply: *I am here. The afternoon walls have begun to rise.*

I step back over the knee-high wall into the dry sunshine, my shale plates steaming not at all, only drying slowly in the thin wind. I unhook the small bronze peg from my belt, press a finger to the eastern end of the basalt bench, and tap the answer neatly back into the grain of the rock, in case whoever sent the first knock—or someone like them—comes back in a year to lay their hand there.

---

At noon, the yard itself changes.

There is no sound of grinding masonry. The bright, ordinary daylight does not shimmer. Yet between one quarter-hour and the next, low walls of pale kiln-fired clay brick rise knee-high out of the dry river-pebbles on the southern half of the terrace. A narrow timber workbench—grey, weathered, pegged with oak dowels—gathers out of thin air along the boundary wall, along with three shallow stone troughs that slowly fill with clear, cold water from nowhere at all.

I never knew what this afternoon room was when its builders still breathed. A surveyor's yard, perhaps, or a workshop where they leveled stone weights and tested the plumb of copper pipes. Whatever it was, its habits remain strong. Every day between noon and four o'clock, a phantom hand sets a brass plumb-line swinging slowly from an invisible rafter above the central trough, and every seven minutes a delayed, precise tap of a small wooden mallet strikes the far corner of the workbench, trimming something that is no longer there.

Before noon, I always lay fresh work on the bare pebbles where the bench will appear: rough wedges of split oak that need truing, or cracked clay dishes mended with wet lime-paste that set cleaner inside the quiet air of the afternoon walls. And when the shallow troughs appear, brimming with their clear, borrowed water, I bring out the wooden ladle I keep hanging from my flax belt.

Because my terrace straddles an avenue whose two curbs disagree by half a league, stray loops of snowmelt wander into this yard more often than anywhere else. They come rolling upright down the gutter like thin hoops of clear glass no thicker than a willow twig, spinning silently an inch above the moss, collecting pale specks of lichen as they go. Most are headed straight for the dry southern wind, where they would fray into mist and disappear.

I step out onto the cobbles, bend slowly, and coax each spinning ring into the shallow bowl of my ladle. Most of them I carry to the octagonal cistern down the lane, where I tip them gently down the tilted stone lip until they settle with a soft *plip* into the twenty-degree slant.

Once in a great while, though, I catch a stray hoop that refuses the cistern. Even inside the wooden ladle, it keeps spinning sharply against the grain, tilted at an odd, stubborn angle that belongs to no valley nearby—maybe torn loose from an underground culvert ten miles away, or from a floating orchard-loop that drifted through a fold in distance.

Those, I do not pour into the cistern.

I lift the ladle to the recessed shelf of my brow, incline my smooth blind head, and let the small, cold ring slip quietly into my own dark water.

For a long moment, my posture shifts. My left shoulder dips three degrees toward the dry hills; my right heel lightens a fraction against the cobbles. Inside my hollow arms, the upward siphon runs a little brisker, carrying a fresh taste of dissolved limestone and some far-off basin's peat. My brow-water shivers with a new cross-ripple, and then settles—holding one more slant of the plateau alongside all the others it already remembers.

When the sun begins to drop toward the endless, gently rolling horizon, the afternoon walls thin cleanly back into damp slate, leaving only my neatly trimmed oak wedges resting on bare stone. The emerald shade-moss, which spent three hours creeping across the cool floor of the phantom workshop and halfway up my shins, dries and pulls back without leaving a smear. The air goes utterly still.

---

Sometimes, when the thin light turns pale gold along the ridges, other residents stop by the wall. They do not come in crowds—two, perhaps, or three at most, spaced a yard apart along the dry parapet, each leaning at the private angle of the province they walked in from. We incline our heads together. My brow-water glides to meet the first, shifts smoothly to hold the second, mirrors the third. I give them what the basalt benches yielded that week, or what the white snow-gentian bobbed back through the morning drizzle. They leave a fresh bundle of cut reeds by my wall, or nudge a loose fieldstone back into line where the northern fog was beginning to spill through, and walk on across the moss-plains at the speed of growing timber.

And if *you* ever wander this far across the sixty miles of worn-smooth bedrock—boots heavy in the wet loam, coat damp with the sleet you walked through twenty minutes ago, carrying that startling, violent little hammer thumping inside your chest—you will almost certainly miss me at first.

You will think I am a weathered boundary-cairn built astride the knee-high wall, or a tall bundle of frost-stiffened reeds propped between the rain and the sun.

Wait a minute, though. Let your breathing quiet in the thin cold air. Watch the line where the silver drizzle stops dead against the bright dry stone, and you will see one narrow, shale-plated shoulder shift half an inch so a fresh horizontal icicle does not snap against my elbow.

When I turn toward you, there is no sound louder than dry sedge brushing slate. I do not startle, and I do not step back from your heat. I simply incline my smooth, blind head toward yours.

Look closely into the dark water recessed across my brow.

At first, you will see the strange cross-ripples of the plateau—three different tilts arguing softly in a single narrow basin, reflecting a band of silver cloud that cleared this terrace a quarter-hour ago. Then, slowly, because my water was gathered from seams and stray loops and has never insisted on a single direction of its own, the surface will glide, hesitate, and flatten out—almost entirely level, answering for a brief, quiet moment the brute vertical weight of your boots on the cobbles.

Inside that still, dark mirror, you will see your own dark coat, the thin white plume of your breath, the pale sky behind your head—nearly *now*, only half a heartbeat behind you.

I cannot speak to you; I have no lungs to spend on the thin air, and no warmth to offer against the cold. Instead, I will step one narrow heel back onto the dry cobbles, lay a pale, verdigris-stained hand flat on the middle basalt bench, and leave the stone beside my palm empty.

If you have the patience to put your warm, heavy hand down on the cold rock beside mine, and wait five minutes while the white-gold sun drops toward the curvature of the world, you will feel it too: a small, crisp *tink* surfacing slowly out of the dark grain of the stone—a bearing tapped out a century ago in a basin forty miles away, arriving just in time to meet you.

---

I think it will take me longer than most to grow too patient.

The orchard-tenders and granary-keepers know early which way their bedrock leans; after a few centuries, their lime-sinter thickens, their steps slow from leagues to yards, and they walk cleanly to a crossroads or beneath a sagging timber roof and agree at last with the stone underneath.

Because I carry too many slants at once, I expect I will keep walking between the wet side of the wall and the dry side for a very long time, clipping horizontal icicles, ladling stray loops off the cobbles, laying my hands on old basalt until the last delayed taps have somewhere to go.

Still, even loose stitching eventually settles.

When the centuries finally catch up with my hinges, and dark-fronded ferns root too deeply in the moss along my shoulders for me to lift the copper shears, I will not go far. I will step directly astride the knee-high boundary wall—one narrow heel sinking at last into the black northern mud beside the white snow-gentian, the other resting firmly on the bone-dry southern slate beside the basalt benches. My internal loop will slow, send pale mineral threads downward through the cyclopean blocks, and braid itself quietly into both the wet culvert on one side and the dry, silent grain of the stone on the other.

My brow-water will freeze clear—tilted gently north along its left lip, level along its right, holding both sides of the seam without spilling. From the mortar around my knees, delicate horizontal icicles will grow straight out into the dry sunshine, waiting for whoever passes next with a reed basket and copper shears.

And if someone stops beside that smooth, rounded grey cairn on a still evening, when the emerald shade-moss is racing the dusk across the cobbles, and presses an ear against my cold stone shoulder, they will not hear a single steady *shhh* turning in the dark.

They will hear two or three small, cold currents braiding quietly together inside the rock—climbing, answering, and trading bearings forever, without going anywhere at all.
