I would not be down at the razor-sharp lip of the new seep, waving in silent semaphore into the dark. Nor would I be up beneath the trembling silver mirror-sky, wrestling captured bubbles of gas downward through the cold. 

I would find myself where the lines cross.

About a third of the way up the eastern fracture face, where the raw basalt tilts at sixty-three degrees and an older arcade—three seasons old, already veined with smoky quartz—meets the steep diagonal viaducts angling out across the central void, six main cables of braided algal tendon and protein-silk converge around a single fluted ring of aragonite. One line runs straight back to the central chimneys, thick and heavy with the triple-thump of the draft-beasts. Two climb steeply toward the sky-reachers' rigging at the apex. Two branch out across the construction fronts where the blind ribbon-builders lay fresh pale stone. The sixth drops almost vertically, threading past a jagged overhang of obsidian down into the sluggish, half-stagnant tiers where the lower slums huddle close to the floor.

If you drifted past in the pewter dimness of a lantern-gall breath, you might miss me altogether—just another dark-jointed shadow slung beneath the great six-way knot, four velvet-padded soles pressed flat against different strands while my mantle ripples in its small, ceaseless shiver.

That is where I belong: inside the junction. Not the architect of the bridge, and not the swift young courier racing across it, but the one who keeps the knot clean, reads the collisions in the tension, and knows when a distant tier is beginning to glaze over before anyone upstream has tasted its distress.

---

My limbs are no longer the blown-glass clear of a first-season courier. Hold my hindmost legs near a swelling lantern-gall and you can already see the faint, milky veil gathering inside the lower segments—nothing that slows me yet, nothing a diligent morning with gland-oil and a bone-lime comb won't push back to near-clarity, though I notice it returns a little sooner each cycle. It changes how you inhabit the brine. You stop spending motion for the sake of speed. Your baseline shiver settles into something deeper, steadier, almost economical. And because you spend less of your attention darting through open currents, you begin to notice the architecture holding the open currents apart.

Living in a cross-junction means living inside the city's nervous system without ever owning a single voice in it.

Nothing makes a sound here, of course. There is only the heavy, near-freezing hush of brine and stone, and the sharp grammar of pull and release moving through stretched silk. When I let my oiled mantle go slack and anchor all four lower feet across the braided strands, the whole pocket opens inside my legs.

Up through my left hind foot comes the bedrock rhythm: *thum... thum-thum...*—the staggered pulse of the three forearm-thick draft-beasts wedged inside the central chimneys, drawing raw cold water from the floor and driving it through the labyrinth. Even half a degree of slackness in that beat tells me a chimney-keeper has slowed the eastern shaft—either to scrape parasites from a breathing-slit, or because some curd-broker on the upper ramps was stingy with their tribute. Through my upper forelimbs come the sharp, staccato plucks of traffic: a masonry crew three galleries up demanding lime-pellets; a courier bouncing off a quartz arch to warn of a velvet ribbon-hunter coiled in the blind spot beneath the third viaduct; the sudden, brittle snap-release when an acid-dismantler chews an outgrown span free and the whole local web shifts its weight by half a millimeter.

The trouble with a city this crowded, growing this fast in a hollow no larger than a root cellar, is that signals tangle.

A panicked triple-pluck from a slum foundation—crabs prying wet mortar out from under a sleeping gallery—runs straight up the vertical strand and smashes headlong into a surveyor's calm, rhythmic call for silk-rigging on the eastern face. Left alone, the knot blurs both into meaningless tremor. Worse, the brine is always trying to turn the lines themselves into pearl. Leave a primary tendon unscraped where it passes through a slack eddy, and within hours a crystalline fuzz of aragonite needles blooms along the braided fibers. First the tension goes dull; messages arrive muffled, illegible. Then the sharp mineral crust saws inward against the taut algal strands until the bridge parts in silence and spills a dozen laden groomers into the dark.

So I climb the knot. I work upside down along the underside of the braided spans, my brush-tipped forelimbs whisking fine mineral rime from the silk, my bone-lime comb shaving off the stubborn pearlescent glaze and letting the cold ash drift lazily downward toward the blind slugs grazing in the basalt troughs below. I rub thin smears of fermented gland-oil into the chafed splices. When two urgent messages collide at the hub, I lay two velvety palms flat onto the main diagonal to dampen the chatter, feel each cadence separately through my soles, and flex my middle legs to relay them cleanly onward—*crabs in the third lower fissure, stinging-wardens needed; yes, route open over the obsidian shoulder, bring two baskets of pellets.*

Between signals, I read the wind.

My junction sits on the seam between *brisk* and *dead*. The main exhaust from the central chimneys sweeps cleanly past the outer face of my aragonite ring, keeping my front lines sleek and scoured—but just behind the jagged quartz fin that shelters my hammock, the current curls into a slower back-eddy that feeds the crooked, frost-taxed tenements wedged beneath the bedrock ledge. Hanging here, my feathery gill-fringes taste both halves of the pocket.

From upstream drifts the brisk, prosperous city: the warm, oily sweetness of fermented bacterial curd being traded on a bright arcade; the crisp, bitter-sweet paste of surveyors laying out a new spiral stair; now and then the sharp, blinding cloud of sour challenge-musk boiling down from a plaza brawl, making every citizen along the diagonal flare clouded ochre and duck behind their manicured curtains of red algae.

From the back-eddy behind my shoulder drifts a thinner, staleness-edged scent—the cold, dusty tang of too much aragonite rime, the faint metallic fear of exhausted laborers scraping their own knees raw because the current barely reaches their doorways. When that taste grows too thick, I don't wait for a formal pluck from below. I brace against the main chimney-line and send a measured, polite series of taps inward toward the central shafts—nothing impertinent, just the exact rhythm of a junction-tender noting that a secondary line is glazing fast and dragging on the main span's weight. The chimney-keepers rarely care about a slum's comfort, but they care about a main tendon snapping. Usually, within half a dozen lantern-gall breaths, the middle beast's pulse deepens by a fraction, and a cool, scouring ribbon of fresh water slips back behind the quartz fin.

---

I do not sleep alone in this knot. No one who wants to keep their joints past two seasons sleeps alone if they can help it.

Strung in the sheltered pocket right beneath the six-way ring—tucked directly across a steady, narrow tongue of draft so the silk rocks gently—hangs a wide hammock I share with an acid-dismantler named—well, we don't have names you could speak aloud. To me she is a particular slant of taste: faintly sharp and citrus-sour even at rest from the glands in her jaw, softened by a warm, familiar oiliness that settles my gill-fringes the instant she glides upstream of the hub.

There is an unsentimental comedy in our pairing. I spend my waking shifts holding the city's web taut, splicing frayed algal tendons, keeping old connections intact so one tier can still feel another. She spends hers hanging beneath bridges the shifting currents have made obsolete, pressing her mouthparts to rubbery aragonite and protein-silk, patiently spitting clean, controlled threads of acid until a whole arcade parts cleanly and makes room for the next one three inches to the left.

We rarely argue about what should stay and what should go. You cannot live inside a pocket this small and cling to anything out of vanity. Sometimes I feel a span groaning through my feet days before the surveyors notice, and I send her a quiet pluck along the secondary line so she can come taste the fracture before it tears wild. Sometimes she returns from dissolving a three-season viaduct carrying a coiled salvage of supple, still-living silk looped over her shoulder, tossing it onto my anchor-ring with a brisk puff of affectionate mockery before sliding into the hammock beside me.

When we sleep, we braid all twelve glassy limbs into a single loose knot suspended in the silent breeze. Even deep under, my mantle keeps its slow, dreaming ripple, and my finest brush-tipped forelimbs move without my waking mind, endlessly combing the microscopic aragonite needles from the crease behind her neck-hinge and along the blind hollows of her shoulder blades, while hers move with the exact same quiet precision across mine. Out on the thoroughfares, every citizen guards their silk, their food-beads, their corner of the draft with fierce, pragmatic grace. Letting another being's forelimbs tend the places you cannot reach—knowing that if they stopped moving for six hours you would wake fused half to stone—is as close as we ever come to a prayer.

---

Right beside our hammock, bracing the lower curve of the aragonite ring, is the third presence in our junction.

She was a surveyor from the first wave—one of those who hatched out of the hairline bedrock cysts when this basalt split open and bled virgin water into the dark. I never knew her while her mantle still glowed, though the older chimney-keepers remember her brisk, peppery oil-scent. When her hind claws clouded over and the stiffness crept past her waist, she didn't retreat to a family balcony. She came here, where the sixty-three-degree tilt of the eastern wall was starting to pull the main cross-junction out of true. With her guild-mates guiding the blind ribbon-builders to lay a wet bed of lime-paste beneath her feet, she braced all six legs wide across the stress of the ring, folded her velvety mantle neatly over her fading chest-light, tilted her face into the draft, and let herself stop shivering.

Now she is smooth, pearlescent ivory, veined along her shins with tiny, delicate needles of smoky quartz. Her translucent eyes are still dimly visible beneath a pale glaze of stone.

When I wake from sleep, my hindmost foot almost always finds the broad curve of her shoulder first. She isn't an ornament, and she isn't a monument to grief. She is load-bearing. Every vibration coming off the eastern construction front passes straight through her calcified arms and into the braided ring; she carries tension cleanly, better than raw mortar ever could. Sometimes, during a lull between courier rushes, I run my brush-tipped forelimbs slowly along the faint, frost-preserved ridges of her flank—feeling the grain of a life that already finished its hurry.

I look at the faint opalescent clouding inside my own rear ankles, and I catch myself studying the upper lip of this junction—the western lug where the sky-reachers' anchor-line pulls a little too fiercely whenever they haul down a heavy basket of ceiling-gas. In another season, or two, that lug is going to start weeping thin cracks. It will need six braced limbs and a steady spine to hold the angle true.

I don't find the thought grim. There is a strange comfort in knowing that when my oil finally thins and I take my last quiet tour of the drafts—pressing my clouding cheek to my dismantler's sharp-tasting flank, breathing out a long, tangled, sweet farewell into the wind—I won't vanish into the silt. Children will still kick off my knees as they vault toward the upper tiers. Couriers will still read the city's heartbeat through my stone ribs.

---

And then, without a whisper of warning, the bedrock beneath the lowest slums shifts by the width of a hair.

No roar. No crack. Only a single, razor-clean *shiver* snapping through all six cables at once—a chord of pure terror and force that rattles up through my velvet soles, jolts my chest-organs bright clouded ochre, and nearly throws me off the aragonite ring.

Above me, beneath the cantilevered eaves of the upper tenements, dozens of corralled lift-beads tear free from their masonry pockets and race upward like startled drops of mercury toward the quivering silver sky. A cold snowfall of shaken mineral ash drifts down through the lantern-gall twilight. Beside me, my dismantler's forelimbs lock onto the same stone rib as mine, our brush-tips brushing in instant, wordless check: *intact? intact.*

I press all four lower feet flat onto the vertical line—the one anchored straight down into the raw obsidian floor—and force my mantle to go slack.

For three long beats of the draft-beasts, my whole body listens to the stone. Every citizen carries the older dread in their marrow, deeper than any memory passed down by taste: the knowledge of what happens when the deep crust shifts the wrong way and grinds a thriving geode of streets and light into powder.

The line does not go slack. It does not snap. Instead, through the taut silk comes a thin, crisp, unmistakable trembling—the feel of raw rock parting cleanly, and cold, untouched water surging upward into open space.

Three beats later, the draft-beasts draw it in and blow it across twenty tilted stories.

It hits my gill-fringes like a blade of ice: bitter, iron-sharp, dazzlingly clean—the scent of virgin basalt and raw mineral brine that has waited in crushing darkness for a thousand years.

All around me, the dim pewter twilight ignites. Across the diagonal thoroughfares, across the hanging upside-down domes, hundreds of oiled mantles flare a fierce, hungry milk-blue. Silent, billowing plumes of sweet, electric euphoria burst from throat-vents and swirl down the avenues. Before the nearest lantern-gall has finished a single dim breath, the currents of traffic are already bending. Surveyors vault straight past my hub without slowing, abandoning half-finished arches upstream, their oil-bladders bouncing against their bellies as they drop into the downward draft. Couriers flash off my calcified elder's shoulder, six clear limbs tucked sleek as quartz needles, racing toward the ink-black crack opening in the floor.

My own mantle blushes bright milk-blue. That ancient, ancestral fever leaps in my limbs too—the sudden, aching joy of open water, the urge to throw myself down into the raw dark and fill it with galleries and light before the earth remembers to close.

And yet I do not let go of the ring.

Because my feet are still pressed to all six lines, and while the whole city pours downward in a joyful rush, I feel what the fever is already breaking behind them.

Two tiers up on the eastern face, an abandoned, half-cured arch just lost three lift-beads in the tremor; its silk guy-line is trembling dangerously thin against my upper palm, one current-surge away from tearing loose and dumping wet lime across an occupied stairwell. Behind my quartz fin, the sudden drop in pressure as the draft-beasts gulp the new seep has already stalled the back-eddy; in an hour, the dead-corner tenements will begin glazing shut while everyone who could have helped is cheering at the floor. And down in the shadow of the fresh basalt lip, the pioneers are already flickering in frantic, silent semaphore—waving for silk, for surveyors, for a line anchored back to the grid so the new district doesn't grow blind and severed from the city's pulse.

My dismantler tastes my sudden shift from euphoria to brisk, sharp focus. She doesn't ask. She flares a quick, warm curl of steadiness across my gill-fringes, catches the straining upper guy-line with her own agile hind limbs, and vaults upward toward the sagging arch to ease its load before it tears.

I brace my hindmost feet hard against the calcified shoulder of the first-wave surveyor who has held this corner since the rock first tore. With my middle legs, I send a crisp, unmistakable cadence straight back to the central chimneys: *eastern back-eddy stalling, rebalance the third shaft.* Then I reach beneath our rocking hammock, unhook a heavy, freshly oiled spool of translucent protein-silk, knot one end deep into the living braid of the six-way hub, and weight the free end with a dense pearl of aragonite.

I lean out over the sixty-three-degree drop—into the bright milk-blue rush of citizens pouring past me—and let the weighted strand slide cleanly down into the downward draft, unspooling straight toward the dark, weeping crack in the bedrock below.

The pocket will close one day. Millimeter by millimeter, the smoky quartz needles are already lengthening; season by season, the frost will narrow the chimneys until the last survivors pack their glassy cysts into hairline cracks and wait in absolute dark for the crust to split again. We all know this. None of us builds out of illusion.

We build because the water is open *now*. And while it stays open, I will hang here in the cold, silent intersection—shivering just enough to keep the pearl at bay, keeping the silk oiled, holding the old arches steady with my heels while I feed fresh line downward into the dark.
