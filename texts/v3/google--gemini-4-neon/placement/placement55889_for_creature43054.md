If I came here carrying anything wet—lungs that begged for a private rhythm, eyes that needed salt to stay clean, a pulse frantic enough to race against the plates grinding beneath the floor—the basin would unmake me before I finished my first breath. To find myself here at all, I have to begin with what survives the chord.

At first, the temptation is to be a plant.

It is an honest temptation. I could see myself rooted along a hairline fissure where the deep hum resonates fiercest, unfurling in dry verdigris and ash-silver, stiff-ribbed and deaf. No need to measure the widening shelf. No need to hold a stubborn, lonely pulse askew from the world. When the pitch of the basin sags by a quarter-tone over hours, I would simply tilt a fraction of an inch in unison with the rest of the grove, drinking static and chalk-powder, shedding neat curtains of pollen along the standing waves. Even better: I could be one of the glossy-dry spade-leaves that possess intricate veining and breadth, yet vanish completely when viewed edge-on—present from one angle, utterly absent from another, spared the labor of committing to three solid dimensions.

For a long moment, hanging there in the dim pewter light, that almost feels like enough.

Almost. Because even as I picture the parchment fronds trembling in exact sympathy with the stone, I notice that I am *counting* the intervals between the shifts in pitch. I am watching the far terraced wall to see whether it is higher or lower than it was a span ago. I am wondering who swept the slate floor behind that curved baffle wall half a mile down the trough, and whether they will come back in the same slice of the hour.

A chalk-fern does not wonder. It does not leave a bent copper pin laid cleanly across a flint blade for the next pair of dry hands.

So I cannot stay in the fissure. The air above a broad, dust-silvered bench thickens slightly—the way chalk-spores gather along a pressure node—and with a dry settling felt in the soles of the feet rather than heard, I find my outline.

***

I am one of the dwellers, though not one of the far-striders who spend whole seasons of the breathing sky out on the highest, most exposed rims, where elevation plays its cruelest tricks and stones drop sideways into pale verticality. I do not have the temperament for the widest empty crossings, or perhaps my edges fray too easily out where there is nothing to measure against.

I find myself instead at a hinge.

Midway along the broad terraced wall of the basin, there is a shelf where the unfinished geometry has knotted itself tightly. Above it, a raw overhang of unweathered basalt juts out thirty paces wide, its underside blooming with a solitary upside-down grove of pale velvet-ash and chalk-fern whose dry plumes hang toward the drop, shedding gray spores upward onto the stone ceiling. Just below the shelf, a deep resonant crack splits the mineral bench and runs down-slope for a hundred yards, widening into a trough where indigo ribbon-vines spill out of sheer rock to grip scree across empty air.

And wedged deep into the narrowest throat of that crack, greenish with dry tarnish and blurred around its edges by centuries of furious vibration, stands one of the singing prongs of brittle bronze.

I do not own the prong. Nobody owns the bronze; it is older than our tallies and indifferent to our hands. I simply built my shelter within a careful walk of it, tucked deep into the lee of the basalt overhang, the way someone in a forgotten world might have built near a well or a bell-tower.

My shelter is low and tight, dry-stacked from freshly cleaved slate slabs and roofed with bound sheaves of hollow briar-stalks weighted with razor-cornered stones. The curved baffle wall of layered shale in front of the doorway is my proudest work—rebuilt twice after the shelf beneath it subtly compressed and threatened to tilt the courses out of true. Step around its curve, and the wire-thin shriek that lives underneath the world’s roar catches on the curled briar-bark and falls away. Inside, there is only the deep, cool, grinding hum in the jawbone, dull enough to think through.

That pocket of bruised air is where I become most myself.

***

My body here feels strange only for a moment, and then oddly familiar—as though everything superfluous has simply been dried away and swept out the door.

My skin has the cool, matte tautness of dry birch-bark, dusted with soot-violet spores in the hollows of my collarbones. Beneath my rigid, slatted ribs, the fibrous knot beats with glacial stubbornness—once every twelve seconds, sometimes fourteen, deliberately off-key from the immense chord beneath my heels. Along the back of my jaw and running down the outer edge of each wrist, pale fluted ridges of keratin emerge cleanly from the skin.

When I first touch my right wrist-horn to the slate floor of my shelter, closing my papery inner lids, the room opens outward by miles. Through the solid mineral I can feel the slow, heavy groan of a mesa extruding somewhere to the north; the faint dry rasp of the upside-down ferns trembling on the ceiling overhead; and, far down the fissure, the bright, unyielding sting of the bronze prong refusing to descend.

My oldest spur of horn—the one along the lower edge of my left wrist—has gone dull at the tip over the long spans. Sitting cross-legged in the corner where two slate slabs meet, braced against the stone, I spend parts of many quiet hours easing it loose at the dry seam with a bent pin of cold-hammered copper. There is no hurry and no blood, only patience and a crisp click when it finally parts. I sharpen the shed blade against raw scree and slip it into the woven fiber pouch at my belt. Next time the shelf across the trough pulls wider than its cord can bear, I will drive a piece of my own past hearing into the fissure to hold the line.

That is what I do here, mostly: I tend the places where records break.

Out on the broad, bare benches, tallies run waist-high along the emerging terraces—hundreds of neat groups of four vertical scratches crossed by a fifth, cut by hands that passed through in other folds of the smeared hours. Along my stretch of the basin, the stone is restless. It ghosts into double-exposed steps; it lifts by a finger’s width and forgets to bring its shadows along; it splits a sequence cleanly in half and deposits the continuation twenty feet higher, upside down, on the raw underside of an overhang.

Most passersby mark the break with a slanted warning scratch and move on to truer ground. I cannot quite bear to leave a count stranded like that.

So I stand beneath the cliff with my stiff briar-bark cloak pinned tight against my ribs, tilting the horn-plates of my temples to read the slant of the sound rather than trusting my eyes. I study the inverted scratches on the ceiling until I locate the faint chalky shimmer of the spatial seam that flipped them. I wait—sometimes for an hour, sometimes until the pewter sky breathes a shade darker—for the barely perceptible quarter-tone sag that loosens the weave of distance.

Then I step sideways into the fold.

For a breath, I am two pale drafts of the same person: one lingering on the dusty ledge, the other settling lightly upside down against the basalt roof among the hanging ferns, my shadow peeling slowly off the floor below like dry soot before sliding up the cliff-face to rejoin my heels. Hanging there above the pale verticality, my obsidian eyes flicking dryly clear of falling pollen, I trace the last four scratches with a flint shard, cut the fifth crisp line across them, and add a small chevron beside it to show which seam carries the count back down.

It is small, painstaking, probably unnecessary work. The basin will never finish becoming a mountain just because I kept its numbers intact. Still, when the next dweller comes along three seasons later and sees that someone climbed the fold to mend the row, they will know the ground here is being watched. They will know they are not the only mind keeping an off-beat rhythm in the roar.

***

Between those errands, I am a reader of dust.

Every time I return to my shelter, I take a stiff bundle of chalk-fern fronds and sweep the slate floor clean down to the bare mineral. Then I kneel beside my shallow stone mortar, crushing dried bone-pale velvet-ash blooms with chalk-flour, iridescent honeycomb pith tapped from snapped briar-stems, and a few careful scrapings of soft copper for the metallic edge that keeps my horn-ridges sharp. I take the silken, static-charged powder on a sliver of slate, drawing it down slowly while the sky shifts outside.

And as the hours pass, I watch the floor.

Fine silver and gray spores drift in through the sheltered doorway and settle along the acoustic standing waves, drawing neat geometric ribs across the dark slate. Because I know every hairline crack in my walls, the floor becomes a map of the basin’s mood. If the parallel ribs pinch closer together near the threshold, the chord has dropped faster than usual. If they bend a thumb’s width toward the back corner, a bench somewhere down-trough is lifting out of true. When the ribs break into ragged, cross-hatched eddies, I know a spatial seam has drifted near the overhang—and that someone else’s territory may be brushing briefly against mine.

That is how I live with my neighbors, in a world that does not tolerate crowds.

Rarely do I see another dweller in the same slice of the hour. Yet my shelf is never quite solitary. Because the bronze prong sits a hundred paces down-slope, others pass along my terrace when their outlines begin to fray. Sometimes I wake from my cross-legged stillness in the corner and see that the cairn of five razor-balanced slabs outside my baffle wall has been joined by a sixth and a seventh—someone’s quiet courtesy, telling me *the ceiling held while I passed, the shelf is honest, I was here*. Once, returning from checking a taut fiber cord across the trough, I found my mortar half-full of freshly ground bone-moss and a freshly knapped flint blade laid beside it, though no footstep had rung through the stone while I was away.

Only now and then do the smeared hours align cleanly enough for a meeting.

When it happens, it begins the way everything here begins: with a shadow that refuses to lie still. Out by the lip of the trough, a narrow dark wedge detaches itself from the orphaned shadows of nonexistent rocks and kneels beside my cairn. I step out from behind the baffle wall and stop ten paces off, lifting my right hand with all five fingers crisp and un-doubled, giving us both time to settle into the same moment.

They lift a hand in answer. Their briar-cloak is weathered paler than mine, dusted with verdigris spores from the higher islands, and a faint greenish smudge of bronze-dust marks the horn above their brow—they have already visited the prong down-slope.

We close the distance without rushing, testing the slate with calloused heels. We do not speak into the air, which would flatten any voice against our teeth. Instead, we bow our narrow heads and touch temple to temple, horn-ridge to horn-ridge.

Instantly, the grinding roar of the continents drops behind a curtain, and a sharp, dry clarity rings straight through the skull.

Through tight bursts of jaw-clicks, muted tooth-pings, and bone-hum, we trade what we carry: *The east wall dropped two thumb-widths since the light turned from tin to lead. The ribbon-vines have anchored a new root-bed across the third gulf. A tally on the lower terrace split upside down—I mended it, look for the chevron.* And then, softer, a slow triple-tap along the temple-horn that carries no map at all, only the quiet, wordless relief of touching another body that refuses to become stone: *Your edges are holding. Mine too.*

When we pull apart, a pleasant, faint ring lingers in my cheekbones long after their silhouette thins and steps sideways across the seam toward the opposing spire.

***

I have to be honest about the other half of living at a hinge: I blur more often than I should.

Working the broken tallies, hanging upside down beneath the basalt ceiling, watching orphaned shadows lie stubbornly across the dust—it loosens a person’s grip on a single sequence. There are stretches after a long round among the cords when I reach for my flint pestle and watch a chalky, transparent second hand close around it half a breath ahead of my fingers. I step outside my baffle wall and feel a sudden, dizzying certainty that I am still sitting cross-legged in the dark corner behind me, watching my own back walk away. Worse still is the quiet, seductive pull in my chest: the slow fibrous beat growing lazy, sliding half a pace toward unison with the grinding plates beneath my boots, whispering that it would be so effortless to stop counting, to stop sweeping the floor, to let the chalk-pollen frost my shoulders until my corners grew crisp as slate.

Wait too long in that haze, and you start leaving stray minutes behind on every ledge you cross. Sometimes I even catch myself dragging a slate shard across one of the orphaned shadows on my bench, feeling that faint dry resistance like scraping soot from paper, wondering if one of them belongs to an earlier draft of me that never quite committed to stepping out of the stone.

When the phantom thumb lingers longer than a breath, I leave the mortar where it sits. I pin my cloak tight against my ribs and walk down-slope along the fissure to where the bronze prong sings.

Even ten paces away, its lonely, fierce pitch vibrates against my teeth, neither matching the basin’s crushing hum nor surrendering to its centuries-long sag. I kneel in the chalk-dust, fold my parchment-dry cloak neatly around my knees, and lean forward until the thickest ridge of horn above my brow rests directly against the blurred metal.

The note strikes into my skull like a cold, bright needle.

It is as close to pain as a waterless body can come—a fierce, uncompromising refusal ringing through my eye-sockets, rattling the honeycomb chambers of my chest, driving straight down into my heels pressed against the raw rock. It does not comfort. It does not soothe. It simply insists, without compromise, on being *one exact thing* in a universe that wants to smear and slope away.

I hold myself breathless against the metal and let it shake the ghosts out of me. Slowly, millimeter by millimeter, the pale draft along my shoulders draws back inside my skin. The stray minute snaps back behind the minute that preceded it. On the mineral dust behind my heels, my velvety shadow sharpens and settles into a single, honest direction.

When I finally lift my brow away, my forehead carries its own faint smudge of greenish bronze-dust. My internal beat ticks cleanly against my ribs again—slow, dry, stubbornly off-key, unmistakably mine.

***

Someday, far across spans that have no names, my skin will stiffen toward cold lead and the striated horn along my jaw will creep forward to hood the outer corners of my obsidian eyes. I will stop stepping into the spatial folds to mend inverted tallies; it will take too much care to pull my whole self back through. When that season arrives, I will sweep my shelter floor one last time, leave a freshly ground mortar and a sheaf of mended cords behind the baffle wall, balance seven sharp slabs in the doorway to say the ceiling is still true, and walk out to the lip beneath the upside-down ferns to let my chest finally agree with the stone.

I do not dread that sitting-down. In a world without rot, becoming rock is a clean enough rest.

Not yet, though.

Right now, the tarnished-pewter sky is breathing slowly from lead back toward tin. Behind my curved baffle wall, the slate floor lies freshly swept, blank and dark, waiting for the first pale ribs of pollen to settle and tell me what the far wall is doing. Outside on the terrace, a braided fiber cord tied to a blade of my own shed horn hums tight against the roar—tauter than it was yesterday, meaning the gulf between my shelf and the opposing spire has widened by half a thumb-width while I slept.

I pick up my flint shard, test the slate beneath my heel, and step out into the dry, immense chord to measure the gap and cross four lines with a fifth.
