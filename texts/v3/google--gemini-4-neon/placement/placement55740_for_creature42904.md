At first, standing on the weathered lip where the mountain wind still has teeth, I think I would stay up here with the Rim-Keepers.

There is an immediate, honest relief to the ridge. Up here the air is thin and dry, scouring the dolostone clean; if your chest tightens or a cough scrapes your throat, the wind tears the sound away before it can lean over the edge and touch the wet mirror below. Inside the dry-stone burrows, wrapped in wind-tanned pelts, you can see the whole cauldron at once—a bright, frantic geometry packed into a single stone bowl, every hanging catwalk and corkscrew stair legible from above like the inside of a clock left open in the snow. I understand the draw of that vantage. To mend frayed sinew in dry cold, to watch the white scree for a fox’s muzzle, to drop a soundless pouch of greased clay beside an intruder’s paws and keep the rim clean: it is a quiet, necessary border-work, and part of my nature wants to live on borders, where you can watch the storm without getting your boots wet.

Yet the longer I stare down into the glare, the more the ridge feels like a trick. From up here, the cauldron looks like a diagram. You see the white clouds of birds lifting and settling, the tiny flickers of gut-ribbon, the pale tide swelling and receding in the terraced floor—but you cannot feel the *tick-tick* of salt-ice knitting along a cold peg, or smell the chalk and bird-oil and bitter slime, or know in your own knees how thin the line is between water and stone. Watching from the dry lip, you only guard the silence. You never learn what the silence is made of.

So I go down the corkscrew stair.

The first lesson happens before I have descended twenty paces: my dry-land feet are all wrong. The first time a heel drops even slightly too hard onto the polished trough of the stone, a porter coming upward with a basket of ochre curd freezes two steps below me, bone visor tilting up, mittened hand lifting near his collarbone in a single sharp flat-palm *halt*. Nothing locks—the rock here is damp, not quite supercooled—but the look through the narrow horizontal slit of his visor teaches me faster than any shout could. I unlock my knees. I let my weight pour along the outer edge of the boot, across the arch to the ball, rolling rather than stepping. By the time I reach the middle galleries, the blinding light is no longer falling from overhead; it is striking upward off the wet slate at my ankles, off the oil-clear brine sliding over the step, off the hanging combs of frost above my brow. Even through juniper-soot and slotted bone, the glare feels heavy as snow pressing against my eyelids.

I try, for a while, to picture myself among the sunlit crews.

I admire them with a kind of quiet envy—the bridge-riggers who unpin a whole juniper span from a crusting anchor and swing it across the white void in forty heartbeats, the stall-shifters moving in that low, liquid trot, reading four flicks of a neighbor’s mittens through plumes of frozen breath without breaking stride. There is a fierce, athletic mercy in their work. Yet I know myself too well to pretend I would belong up there on the exposed overhangs, living by split-second choreography in the full noon blaze. My eyes tire in shadowless glare. My hands are steadier than they are swift. Whenever a crew strips a ledge bare and hauls its lashed rib-bones twenty paces up the stair, my attention does not follow the moving shelter; it stays behind on the bare dolostone, on the carved peg-socket worn smooth as a river pebble by ten thousand years of temporary gestures, watching the thin clear sheen of brine slow inside the hollow and begin to remember winter.

Beneath the catwalks, in the cool silvered shade where the light comes only secondhand off the running walls, something else pulls at me.

For a long moment I almost wish I were not human at all—that I were one of the six-toed climbers.

I watch one slip out of the hollow marrowless end of an ancient roof-rib, dense white-grey fur shedding drops like oiled silk, and flatten its belly against the glistening vertical slate just above my shoulder. Its hairless palms are the color of wet stone, soft as a newt’s belly, reading hairline fractures in the rock before any human eye could find a seam. That sixth digit set high on the outer wrist, tipped with a blunt horn-chisel nail, pries a flake of salt-glass loose without a click and hooks a twitching pale mite from beneath it. To live in the negative spaces of someone else’s city—never owned, never bred, bound to the loud-hearted giants only by a thumb’s-width of ochre curd left along the lip of a sluice and three silent flicks of a stiff tail when the overhang is about to shear—there is a sharp, unsentimental cleanliness to that life. Part of me recognizes it immediately: the instinct to live in the seam behind the wall, to listen for the stress before the crack runs, to stay light enough that if a ledge gives way you can arch your spine, splay your palms, and flutter sideways onto a hanging hide.

Even the heavy vat-mothers coiled inside their flat slate trays beside the tallow-lamps hold a strange pull—slow, cold, milky things turning raw mineral scum into bitter antifreeze so the town’s wooden axles do not seize. And the white birds packed into their ceiling pockets, breathing fierce small heat down through the leather onto sleeping children, hiding wet copper beneath their frayed velvet wings until spring. Every creature in this cauldron has made a bargain with the cold by giving up something rigid—its voice, its dry skin, its claim to a fixed room—and keeping only what bends.

Still, a climber lives five heartbeats ahead of the ice, and my mind drifts slower and deeper than that. I keep descending—past the salt-flats, past the bustling pulley-ledges, down the slick steps where the porters wade ankle-deep in pale slush—until I reach the lower gallery, just above the great terraced drainage bowls at the bottom of the pit.

Here the walls curve inward, deeply undercut, so the noon glare hits the floor like white fire and bounces back under the scalloped stone in trembling ribbons of silver. Here the carved spouts are oldest, their lips polished by an age of brine and flint. And here, wrapped in layered pelts in a dry hollow beside the third main vent, I find the posture my body has been looking for since I left the ridge.

I am not yet old enough for the Rim. My lungs are still clean and dry; I can breathe the cold wet mist all winter without gathering the barking rattle that sends an elder up to the wind. Yet my knees were never made for the high catwalks—too slow on the turn, too hesitant when a sheen of slush slides across a narrow plank—and long ago one of the old Feelers noticed that when a crew stopped talking in mitten-cant and waited for a surge, I was already crouching with my palm flat against the wet wall, counting a tremor I could not quite explain.

So this is where I stay: a Stone-Feeler of the lower spouts, taking the late-afternoon and dark watches when the older ones crawl into their bird-warmed hanging tents.

My kit is small. I do not need three curved ribs or a stack of twenty slates. A single hide awning pegged shallow against the overhanging dolostone, greased twice a day so the drip slides clean past its lip; a perforated stone tallow-cup, beneath whose faint halo of warmth I guard a fingernail-long beard of brittle glassy moss that I will not pinch with bone tweezers until the gums ache in deep winter; and two flat slate trays kept near the lamp’s heat, where a pair of ancient vat-mothers curl in damp black lichen. They are not strictly a Feeler’s charge, yet the main sluice-gate sits three paces from my niche, and someone has to stroke their cool translucent flanks with a horn spoon at dusk and keep the wooden axle glazed against the night’s first freeze. When my fingers stiffen, I rest them for a minute on the damp rim of the tray, smelling the bitter alum and the crushed shrimp-shells, watching the slow pale pulse inside their skins move like warm oil in a flask.

And tucked inside my fur collar, warmed against my collarbone until I need it, is the bone.

It is a stripped jawbone, old before the Feeler who gave it to me ever sat on this ledge—too narrow for one of the vanished alpine beasts, too long for a mountain fox, polished amber-pale by generations of bare cheeks and wet dolostone. Its tooth-sockets are worn smooth as the peg-holes in the walls. I have carved no lineage notches into it; its whole use is to carry a message without adding anything of its own.

By late afternoon, the glare begins to tilt. The porters below me are moving faster now, scraping the last pale-ochre sheets of fatty curd off the terraced bowls before the sun leaves the stone and the living carpet goes waxy and sleeps. I set my boot-soles across a resting cord-runner’s back for good measure, settle onto my pad of greased fleece at the dry lip of the spout, peel off one thick mitten, and press my bare calloused palm—and then the stripped jawbone, braced tight against the hinge of my own jaw—hard against the naked dolostone.

At first there is only the surface noise of the town, muffled and close: the soft *shhh* of oil-thick brine sliding across stone, the faint *whuff* of soundless wings banking past the overhang, a distant wet scuff where someone shifts a basket. I let all of that thin out. I breathe slowly through my nose so the little plume of ice-crystals drifts sideways and does not fog the stone.

Then the mountain begins to speak in the only voice allowed down here.

It is not a sound; air has nothing to do with it. It arrives through the bone first, a faint, sub-audible *thrum* gathering behind my ear and settling behind my teeth. Far beneath the terraced bowls, in flooded caverns no tallow-lamp has ever touched, the subterranean siphon is shifting its weight. I wait ten heartbeats, reading the shape of the tremor—whether it rises sharp and shallow, meaning a brief bright wash that will scarcely wet the porters’ ankles, or swells broad and heavy through the ribs of the rock.

This one is broad. Heavy. Three minutes out, maybe less, and carrying enough volume to flood the lower sluice-step and lift half the resting buckets if they are not pinned up.

I do not lift the jawbone from the stone. With my free hand, still mittened, I raise my forearm clear of my dark pelts so the lookouts on the opposite stair and the curd-scrapers down in the bowl can read the pale hide against the shadowed rock. Four spread fingers, held steady, then tilted slowly inward toward my chest: *heavy surge; clear the third lip.*

Down in the silvered glare, nobody turns to look at my face. Two porters flick their thumbs twice near their collarbones in acknowledgment, shoulder their hide buckets, and roll smoothly up the steps. A sluice-tender slides a horn wedge loose without a click, opening the side channel. Just behind my left elbow, inside the narrow wet flue where I always leave a generous thumb’s-width of ochre curd on the stone, a six-toed climber pokes its flat white-grey head into the light, dark forward-set eyes blinking once at the rising wetness, before slipping upward into the dry hollow of a beam.

Then the sun drops behind the pale ridge.

It goes all at once, the way it always does—the white mirror collapsing into bruised violet, and then into a black so cold the high stars look ground to flint edges. Up and down the steep walls, hundreds of tallow-lamps kindle inside their perforated cups until the whole cauldron becomes a sunken well of small amber halos.

This is the hour I truly belong to.

Without the glare, the town loosens its shoulders by a fraction of an inch. The frantic dismantling slows; the daytime crews swing up into their greased sinew hammocks and triple-walled hanging tents, where their thin, unvoiced whispers—all *s* and *f* and *h*—will brush gently against a child’s brow or a lover’s ear, safe from the wet walls. Down here by the floor, the night-crews wade quietly into the dark terraced bowls with their wide, shallow nets of braided human hair.

The siphon’s rhythm changes after dark. The surges come farther apart now, heavier and unmistakably warmer, drawn up from deep sunless pockets beneath the mountain’s roots where the earth still keeps a faint ember of heat. Ghosts of mist lift off the black brine and vanish into tiny snow-needles ten paces overhead. Through the jawbone pressed to my cheek, the night-pulses feel different from the brittle noon tides—less like a pump, more like a long, slow exhalation. When the warm water swells over the scalloped rim, gleaming faintly in the orange tallow-light, the blind pale vent-threads rise with it, undulating like ribbons of boiled sinew through the dark pools before the receding drain can pull them back underground. I count the rhythm for the netters, holding up two fingers, then a flat palm, letting them know when the pull-back is about to strengthen so no one slips on the slick rim and drops a net-frame with a crack that would turn the whole dark basin to milky glass.

On ordinary nights, that is the whole of my work: translating between the stone beneath our feet and the quiet hands above it, keeping the vat-mothers fed, watching the little green beard of moss under my lamp, laughing silently with a puff of frost through my nostrils when a neighbor taps my shoulder with a wooden dowel at the change of watch and slips a warm strip of salted curd into my palm.

And then there are the two nights in deep winter.

They never come on a date anyone can notch onto a rib. You know them only because, sometime after midnight, the dry mountain wind above the high ridge dies completely. Even down at the bottom of the pit you feel the absence of it—the air overhead going strangely still and dense, the tallow-flames standing straight up inside their stone cups without a shiver.

And then, beneath my bare palm and the amber jawbone, the siphon stops.

Not a slow fading. A halt. The receding brine finishes sliding down the black vents, and the shallow water left in the central bowl goes flat and still as oiled slate, reflecting the hundreds of tiny orange halos up the cliffs like a second town drowned upside down.

Along the steps, the night-crews lift their hair-nets carefully out of the water so not even a drop falls back onto the surface. The old Feelers who still wake for this hour sit motionless in their pelts along the terraced rim. Up in the beams, the velvety white birds do not stir inside their hide pockets. Even the climber in the flue behind my shoulder stays completely flat against the rock, its stiff tail pressed straight and silent.

I do not pull my jaw away from the dolostone, though the cold bites deep into my teeth and makes my eyes water into the soot.

For ten minutes there is nothing at all—only the terrible, glass-edged stillness of supercooled water waiting on the barest breath of a shock, and the quiet weight of ten thousand feet of pale mountain resting on our backs.

Then, far below the mountain’s roots, deeper than the warm pockets, deeper than any pocket where the blind vent-threads breed, something slowly *shifts*.

It comes up through the stripped bone not as a tremor, not as a knock, not even as a pulse—just a long, heavy, patient turning in the flooded dark. Too slow to be a rock giving way. Too steady and whole to be a settling fault. It feels the way a sleeping animal feels through the floorboards of a tent when it rolls its weight from one shoulder to the other, except its shoulder is a cavern wide and its breath is a season long.

Every muscle in my throat wants to tighten. Everything human in me wants a word for it—a shape to flash in mitten-cant, a hissed syllable to carry back into the bird-down dark of my tent. Yet I know better, and the older Feelers across the black pool know better too. To name it loudly would be to make it rigid, and nothing rigid survives here. We do not sign across the dark water. We do not tap a warning. We simply hold still, bone against stone, letting our own small hearts beat quietly inside our greased furs while whatever lives under the mountain settles back into the dark.

Half an hour after it stopped, the sub-audible *thrum* begins again—faint at first, then familiar, rising through the dolostone ribs like blood returning to a numb hand.

Warm black brine wells silently up through the vents, lifting thin ghosts of mist toward the sharp stars, thick with pale undulating threads. The glassy skin of the pool breaks into slow, silver ripples.

I lift the stripped jawbone from my cheek, tuck it back inside the warmth of my collar, and rub the feeling back into my bare palm against my furred thigh. Beside my elbow, the vat-mothers shift slowly in their slate tray. Down by the rim, the night-crews look up toward my overhang, waiting in the tallow-light.

I raise my mittened hand into the dim amber halo and hold up two quiet fingers.

*The tide is back,* I tell them without a sound. *Step in.*
