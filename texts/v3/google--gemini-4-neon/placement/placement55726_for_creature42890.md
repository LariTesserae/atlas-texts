I would not wake up inside the steam. I would come to it from the cold side of the bulkhead.

Bolted against the windward gable—small as a vestry tacked onto a village church—there is a dry iron vestibule that the builders left behind when the cables went down the mountain. No coils run beneath its floor. Its walls are bare riveted plate, sweating frost on the inner heads of the bolts whenever a cloud-front scrapes the ridge. Inside it smells of dry wool, pine resin, linseed oil, and the faint iron tang of altitude. There is a narrow workbench with a brass vise, a rack of spare plate glass wedged between strips of grey felt, tins of heavy amber oil, coils of waxed twine, a canvas ledger whose cover has warped slightly from being carried past the door, and a shallow wooden box of white soapstone pencils.

That is where I would find myself: not a visitor who climbed up for an afternoon, and not a creature condensed out of warm brass and lime-water, but the one who stayed on after the crane-rigging came down. The keeper of the dry side. The hand that still remembers the cold weight of the spanner and the sharp sting of glass dust along the knuckles.

I have scars. That is the first thing the vault notices about me, before I even take three steps past the heavy sealed door. A pale hook across the back of my left thumb from a sheet-metal flange; a fine white line along my palm from pulling taut wire; dry, chapped cuticles that carry the grit of mountain stone. Beside the unmarked rivets and the brand-new porcelain baffles, my hands look strangely old—built for a world that rusts and chips and dries out in the wind.

At first, I would come through the door in thick mountain wool and nailed boots, carrying a putty knife, a brass feeler-gauge, and a pocket full of spare washers, convinced I knew what I was here to do. Someone has to keep the exterior seals tight against frost-heave. Someone has to refill the amber oil-cups along the ridge gantry, check the switchgear behind the slate panel when the mountain static builds, and stack fresh bone-pale mats of dry mineral wool into the end-wall magazine so the silent carriage always has another tray to lower. Someone, I would tell myself, has to finish bolting down that hanging span of the second catwalk and tighten the weeping flanges where vapor escapes around the hex nuts.

Then the heavy door swings shut behind me with a dull pneumatic sigh, and the vault immediately proves how badly I am built for it.

My boots ring too loudly on the sharp diamond grating. Every step sends a dry metallic shiver down the stanchions—nothing a person downstairs would notice, yet enough to make the thumb-sized amphibian on the third stanchion tuck its translucent toes a hair closer to the warm brass bezel. My wool coat traps the mountain chill for ten seconds and then turns heavy, sour, and suffocating in the stove-side heat. My breath comes too quick, my heart hammering a stubborn lowland cadence in my throat that fights the slow, steady thrum rising from the buried transformers.

I stand on the middle catwalk with my brass feeler-gauge cooling in my hand, sweating through my shirt, feeling like a dropped wrench in a room full of felt-baffled bearings.

It is the far gable that changes what I think my job is.

I kneel there—shins aching against the sharp stamped mesh—to inspect the last curved panes I bedded in fresh linseed putty two days ago. The smell of the putty is still sharp and oily, cutting cleanly through the boiled-rain scent of the steam. Right beside the glass sits the freshly bolted manifold where three copper pipes meet the ceramic box. I open my knife to clean the bright brass threads of the pressure valve, and stop with the blade half an inch from the metal.

A hair-fine filament of green moss has already crept across the cut of the brass. Below it, a single translucent root-tip has found the microscopic seam between two steel washers and anchored itself inside the warm metal join.

If I scrape it clean, I am obeying the drawings still pinned to my bench on the cold side of the door. If I leave it, I am obeying the room.

While I am kneeling there, undecided, the eleventh minute finishes overhead. A single soft wooden *clunk* drops down through the hanging root-curtains, followed by the hiss-free spill of warm condensate down the stepped porcelain weir. Mist billows up through the floor grates beneath my knees, erasing the far glass wall in milky glare. My canvas ledger curls slightly in my hip pocket. My spectacles fog solid white.

I take them off, wipe them once against my cuff, and realize it is useless—the cold lenses simply cloud over again before I can lift them to my face. So I fold them away and sit still in the bright blur, listening to the drops *plink* against broad leaves, waiting for the cold outer panes to pull the water back out of the air.

When the green and silver slabs of mountain light sharpen again, the resident is perched six feet away on an overhead feeder pipe, directly above the heavy brass unions.

I do not know how long it has been watching me. Probably since the first time I left white soapstone numbers—*4-B*, *FEED*, arrows pointing upward—on the pale-grey steel arches before the cistern was even filled. A cloud drifts past below the sill line; the copper expansion-rods along the ridge contract, the frosted louvers tilt open, and the pearlescent inner veil draws smoothly back from its water-pale eyes. It tilts its fluted brow a fraction of an inch.

It is not frightened of my putty knife, and it does not seem to resent the clang of my boots. It simply looks at the dry wool of my cuff, the brass button at my wrist, the rapid pulse visible in my neck, the way my knees press awkwardly into the sharp mesh without finding any true hold. It watches with the quiet, literal patience of a draftsman trying to read an unfamiliar instrument: waiting to see what clock I run on, and what part of the mechanism I belong to.

That is the moment I put the putty knife away for good—at least for the inside of the seams.

Over the weeks that follow, I do not become like the resident. I am too heavy for the trusses, my skin is too opaque to drink the mist, my fingers will never groove themselves neatly to the curve of half-inch copper pipe. I still live on the cold side of the heavy sealed door, sleeping under wool blankets beside the slate switchboard, boiling bitter tea over a spirit lamp while the night wind hammers itself flat against the outer iron.

Instead, I become the slowest clock in the vault.

The copper rods answer the sky in seconds. The fronds adjust their turgor in minutes. The high cistern tips every eleven minutes; the overhead gantry crawls at the pace of a minute hand; the matte-white snail takes an hour to rasp six inches of curing lime off the concrete curb. My own rhythm is measured in dawns, frost-lines, oil-levels, and the slow weeks it takes concrete to finish weeping into warm water.

I learn to enter the room without clattering against its clocks. Just inside the sealed door, hanging from a brass hook where the steam keeps it perpetually warm and limp, I leave a thin linen shirt and a pair of soft-soled felt shoes sewn over tough leather. Before I step out onto the central aisle, I shed the cold wool and the nailed boots in the vestibule. I learn to walk the catwalks along their smooth angle-iron rims rather than the biting diamond grating, shifting my weight slowly so the pipes do not shudder under the pressure gauge where the pale amphibian sleeps.

I stop trying to finish the second catwalk. Let the next span of iron decking hang suspended from its silent trolley. Let the brass plumb-bobs dangle over nothing on their taut waxed twine. Whenever I pass that unfinished edge, I check the knots for rot, and sometimes—if a draft from the door has set one swaying—I cup my hand gently around the brass weight until it hangs dead still again, vertical over the rising glow of the cistern coils. The resident has seen me do it. Once, coming back up from the lower weir, I watched it do the exact same thing with one wet, caliper-slender fingertip.

I keep my soapstone pencils, though I stop using them for assembly marks. Now I use them to keep a quiet, impermanent record directly on the pale-grey arches—tiny chalk hashes beside a copper busbar where a silver-veined climber first doubles its spiral, a dated mark on the concrete curb where the matte-white snail finally crosses a galvanized bracket, a note beside the manifold on the morning the curing lime stops clouding the water beneath the grates. The steam softens the chalk within a fortnight and washes it cleanly into the copper gutters. In a room with no soil and no rust, I find I do not want my handwriting to outlast the mist anyway.

My real work stays mostly at the margins. I climb the exterior iron ladder in the thin, brutal wind to check the double-glazed seals and press fresh linseed putty wherever the mountain freeze has pulled a hairline gap between glass and mullion. I keep the amber oil-cups topped up so the ridge gantry never develops a dry squeal. Twice a week, I carry fresh slabs of dry, bone-pale mineral wool through the small hatch at the gable end and stack them neatly into the gantry's feeder rack, feeling the dusty dryness catch in my throat before I latch the cover shut.

Then I come down into the green heat, with nothing left to tighten or scrape, and simply stay through a cycle or two.

Sometimes I sit on an overturned crate near the far gable, my back against a warm galvanized stanchion, pencil moving lazily across my damp ledger—not logging pressure readings anymore, just tracing the geometry of how the aerial roots part around a brass union, or timing the small, wet-dry click of the east thermostat against the slower rise and fall of my own ribs. My pulse has learned to drop when I sit here. After twenty minutes in the stove-side warmth, with condensation beading cleanly along my forearms, my heart slows until it almost falls into step with the buried transformer hum vibrating up through the soles of my shoes.

The resident no longer pauses six feet off to take my measure. It moves through its rounds with that felt-baffled continuity, brushing past my shoulder so closely I can feel the faint cool draft of water sliding down its fluted brow and dripping off its elbows into the troughs below. It does not touch me, and I do not reach for it. In a place this exact, reaching out without a reason would be like tapping the glass of a barometer just to watch the needle jump.

Only once in a while—when a thick mountain cloud rises from below the sill line and presses directly against the spotless corner glass at the far gable, dimming the whole vault to soft, pearled twilight—our two clocks fall briefly into the same minute.

The resident slides down from the upper trusses and settles neatly onto the ceramic manifold box, careful of the green moss threading the brass valve. It leans forward and presses the flat of its pale forearm straight against the freezing pane, sighing thinly as its warmth melts a clean, dry window straight through the curtain of running condensation.

I sit on the angle-iron curb two feet to its left. Without thinking much about it, I lift my right hand—knuckles scarred from wire and cold iron, nails rimmed faintly with linseed putty—and lay my open palm flat against the glass beside its arm.

The mountain cold bites straight through the half-inch double seal, sharp enough to ache in the bones of my wrist. Beyond my fingers, there is nothing at all: cold wool of cloud, empty violet sky, wind flattened to a dull pressure against the mullions. Beside my blunt, weathered handprint, the resident’s long, narrow arm cuts a second clear lane through the steam, both of us looking out into the vast, unheated nothing the vault was bolted together to keep out.

Neither of us speaks. Neither of us turns to look at the other. We simply hold our warmth against the freezing glass until the cloud peels away and hard, un-hazed sunlight slams back through the clerestory.

Above us, the copper expansion-rods lengthen with a whisper; the frosted louvers tilt shut; beside me, the milky membrane slides smoothly across the resident’s clear eyes while I squint against the glare. We lift our skin from the pane at almost the same moment. For three seconds, the clear outlines—one long and bone-pale, one broad and scarred—hang side by side on the glass, framed in running water. Then the warm mist heals the surface over cleanly, leaving no mark that either of us was ever pressed there.

That is where I would find myself in this world: neither native to the steam nor wholly banished to the cold stone outside it. I would be the seam-keeper—the scarred hand that feeds the dry wool to the gantry, checks the putty against the frost, and leaves the moss alone on the brass threads. Rooted nowhere in the trays, yet wedged cleanly into the narrow warm joint between iron and glass, right where the building and the garden forget that they were ever meant to be separate things.
