If I look for myself in this plain, I know first where I am not.

I am not the upright figure walking out onto the flats at midday. Everything about that posture—the tall shadow, the heavy heel striking tamped silt, the eye scanning the horizon for a landmark that never comes—belongs to a visitor who makes the world fold shut ahead of them. Stay vertical here and you only meet empty aprons and pegged reeds. To find myself in this basin at all, I have to give up height, give up speed, and let my weight come down until the horizon stops feeling far away and becomes simply the far edge of the floor I am touching.

At first, when I try to settle into the plain, I feel pulled toward the outer margin—that gleaming dark umber band where new ground is laid at dusk. There is a clean, absorbing beauty in the working edge: the long straightedge drawing a wet wave of silt backward, erasing every trace of your own knees, leaving a mirror behind you that catches the last light before it turns to linen and bone. It is easy to imagine wanting to be at the frontier of a world’s making.

Yet the longer I stay low and listen to the glare, the more I realize that is not where my hands belong.

I drift inward. Five miles back from the wet margin, then ten, crossing broad pale rectangles that have already finished their shrinking and bleached to chalky buff. Out here the work is less visible, and the air smells less of raw river-bottom than of dry lime-flour and warm mineral dust. Nothing is newly invented on these older flats. The slopes were graded seasons ago; the dikes sit firm; the cisterns have settled clear. What happens here is quieter, slower, and closer to listening than to building.

This is where I find myself: at a low junction where two older aprons meet, separated by a shallow retaining step no higher than my knee. Beside the step squats a sunken settling-pan, wide and rectangular, its chamfered clay lip smooth from a hundred rains. A few paces off, tucked into the narrow shade of the dike’s weeping toe, lies a covered sump sealed beneath a heavy disc of plaited sedge. And running straight past both—scoring the bone-white floor from one shimmer to the next—is a crossing of water-leveling trenches, two hand-cut silver-lined grooves meeting at a clean right angle, controlled by small removable dams of kneaded clay.

I am one of the older ones—not yet stiff enough to stop moving between dikes, not young enough anymore for the long backward haul across wet silt. My hip joints, which once held a flat-footed crouch through half a night without complaint, now complain faintly when the pre-dawn dew settles deep. Across my shoulders and the crown of my skull, the grey-ochre hide has thickened and webbed with fine, dry creases, like an apron two days into curing. I keep it washed thickly in pale lime-slurry, smoothed on with wet palms after every squall so that when I curl knees-to-chin along the dike’s shadow at noon, I am just another bolster of chalky clay waiting for the heat to break.

I am a mender of the settled ground, and a keeper of the crossing.

It is not a title—we scarcely spend breath on names, and a title would sit as awkwardly on the plain as a tower. It is simply what my days have narrowed to. A floor this immense is always trying, by fractions of a hair, to forget its own flatness. Sun bakes the clay; night dew swells it; salt creeps into the lower courses and pries gently at the mortar; beneath the older lifts, the packed silt compresses by the thickness of a thumbnail over the span of a year. If no one watches the cured flats while the young press the outer edge farther toward the horizon, the slope begins to sag, runoff hesitates, and the messages carried by the water lose their truth.

So I stay where the ground is already supposed to be finished, and I check its memory.

My day begins before the glare exists, in the cool dark when the basin has breathed out its heat and the air drops past its dew-point. I slide the sedge lid aside from the sump—quietly, always keeping the lip clean so no dry grit falls in—and lift water in a shallow clay dish, drinking in long, unhurried pulls. The open cistern twenty paces away is fuller and mirrors the paling stars, but I never touch it with a cup or a hand; that water belongs to the grade and the trenches.

Then I hitch low along the damp seams of the retaining step. Where moisture has lingered through the night, a delicate white crust of salt-bloom has risen along the bottom brick course. I bend and draw my tongue cleanly along the brick, taking the sharp mineral bite before the sun can erase it. Farther along, tucked in the permanent dimness under an overhanging mortar lip, a velvet strip of dark olive moss clings to the wet clay. With the flat, spatulate pad of a thumb—the nail set far back, the skin worn smooth as river-bone—I peel just a narrow ribbon of it away, leaving the rest to spread. At the trench-rim, I pinch the crisp, salty tips from a sparse tuft of creeping glasswort, and without thinking I press the damp clay back firmly around its base so the afternoon squall will bring it on again. Everything here lives by leaving enough behind to answer the next rain.

When the sun clears the ruler-straight horizon and the buff flats begin to throw light straight upward, my heavy brow-ridges do their work, casting a permanent shelf of shade over my eyes while my pupils narrow to fine dark threads. A thin milky lid wipes the lime grit away without breaking my gaze. Soon the bounce off the clay grows sharp enough to drive everything low. Before I fold out of the glare, I walk my junction once—knees bent, moving in that loose, low hitch that keeps my shoulders beneath the height of the dike—checking the basin’s plain grammar.

A brick rests upright on the western lip of the runnel. Someone passed here last night, traveling inward from the new work while I sat beneath the sedge lid, and left me word without waking the floor with a voice: *lime running thin on the west margin.* I move the brick flat again, and beside my heap of crushed shell-lime I turn two loaves of fresh clay crosswise to the dry wind so the next carrier passing west after dusk will know there is flour ground and ready here.

Then I slip down into the cool dimness of the sump, or stretch belly-flat beneath a doubled canopy of pegged reeds where the mortar beneath me stays faintly damp, and wait out the noon.

This is not idle waiting. This is when I tend the wood.

Because I live on the older flats where the sumps are deepest and rarely dry out, the others leave their resting screeds with me. Down on the cool damp clay of the sump floor lie three of them, wiped clean of silt, turned every few days so moisture moves evenly through their ribs and keeps them from warping. In a plain without trees, these straightedges are older than anyone alive—older than the oldest bone-white apron beneath us. Run a calloused palm down their length in the dark and your fingers drop cleanly into grip-troughs polished by ten generations of thumbs.

One of them arrived three weeks ago from the outer edge with a hairline split opening along its grain. You do not discard wood here; you listen to how it wants to keep serving. In the dim shade under the reeds, I brace the split screed across my knees and work patiently at it with a flat wedge of hard lime-stone, coaxing the fissure open cleanly along the fiber instead of letting it tear across. With wet river-silt and fine shell-sand rubbed between my palms, I spend whole hot afternoons smoothing the raw inner faces until the splintered beam has become two shorter, truer tools: a narrow straightedge for dressing the bottoms of leveling-trenches, and a palm-sized mortar float for a child’s hands. When I finish, I dust the tiny pale shavings into a mixing-pit; even the dust goes back into the bond.

While my hands work the wood, my cheek or the flat of my palm rests now and then against the sump’s masonry. The cured floor is a vast drumhead, and on these bone-white miles, nothing is muffled. Through the clay I can feel the whole basin breathing: the crisp *tick* of a brick cracking two hundred yards off as the sun climbs; the faint, slow vibration—so distant it feels like a pulse in my own wrist—of workers miles away drawing a screed across fresh umber; and by mid-afternoon, the deep, muffled weight of thunder against my ribs, rolling in from cumulonimbus towers rising thirty miles beyond the horizon.

When the air begins to cool and the first gray apron of rain detaches itself from those distant towers, I uncurl from beneath the reeds.

Sometimes the squall sweeps straight across my junction. Within seconds the chalky buff floor turns deep chocolate, and a sharp, fragrant mist of wet terracotta and warm chalk rises straight into my face. The lime-wash melts off my shoulders in pale milky streams, leaving my cool grey-ochre skin bare to the brief sky. I stay crouched beside the settling-pan and watch the plain do what it was made to do: no wild puddling, no frantic gullying—just a bright, milky sheet gliding imperceptibly down the grade, gathering cleanly into the chamfered runnels and pouring without a splash over the clay lip into the pan.

Other afternoons the storm misses me by four or five miles, spending itself farther west. Those are the evenings I like best, because that is when the trenches speak.

An hour after the distant clouds lift, I kneel on a folded reed pad beside the western trough, watching the dark dry clay. Then it arrives—not with a sound, only a sudden bright catching of the low sky: a thin silver thread sliding silently toward me across the buff miles. Someone four miles up-grade has cut a palm-wide notch in a dam to tell the plain the rain fell there, and to test whether the slope still holds.

I lower my face close to the trough, brow-ridge nearly level with the rim, and read the water.

For fifty paces it laps cleanly to the beveled edge, a gleaming unbroken line. Then, just short of my junction, along a shallow stretch where an elder was laid flat into the lift three seasons ago, the silver margin widens by half a fingernail—leaving a tiny crescent of darker, submerged clay where the surface has dipped as the lime cured and settled around the bones.

No one walking upright would ever see it. Even kneeling, you would miss it if the light weren't low and slanted.

I press my palm flat against the damp clay beside that slight hollow, feeling the cool solidity beneath it. There is no grief in it, only a quiet recognition: the dead hold the grade, and now and then the living smooth the seam above them so the work keeps carrying water.

After the silver thread has passed through my junction and started its slow creep toward the next worker miles east, I pull the small clay dam shut and wait for dusk to soften the air. From my covered sump I bring a bowl of fine shell-lime flour and wet river-silt, mixing it with the heel of my hand until it shines dark umber and yields like cool wax. Kneeling on two narrow wooden slats so my knees leave no dent in the softened margin, I press the fresh slip thinly into that fingernail-deep sag, feathering the edges out with my spatulate fingers until no seam remains. Then I take the newly pared trench-screed, face the wet gloss, and back slowly away across the older bone-white floor, drawing the wood smoothly toward myself until the drag-marks lie parallel and the faint hollow is gone.

Before full dark, I open the little dam again for a hand’s breath of water. The silver thread glides forward, catches the last pale sheen of the sky, and kisses the beveled rim without a waver.

Sometimes, once in a season, another of us arrives at my crossing just after dusk—coming in quietly from the outer edge, carrying a cracked straightedge to trade for a trued one, or bearing a young one slung flat against their cool belly in a wide cradle of plaited sedge. We do not speak. We sit shoulder-to-shoulder against the sun-warmed face of the retaining step, letting the last heat soak into our backs while the open sky turns dark and wide above us. Now and then we share a low, closed-mouth hum held deep in the chest—barely a breath in the dry air, clear and steady where our ribs meet the masonry. The young one reaches a small, soft-hided hand down to press a shallow scalloped print into the damp toe of the dike, learning how clay answers a palm.

I know where my own work ends, and it does not trouble me. One cool dark before dawn, seasons from now, when my knees finally refuse the crouch and my hide has dried past softening, two others will lay me flat inside a shallow mixing-pit or along the raw edge of a new lift out on the margin. They will pack me evenly in slaked shell-lime and dark river-silt, and draw one of the ancient screeds—perhaps even one whose split ribs I pared smooth in the dark of my sump—cleanly across the top until nothing rises to break the horizon.

By afternoon I will have turned raw linen. By the next season, chalky buff. And years later, when a faint crescent of water hesitates by half a fingernail above my ribs after a distant rain, someone else will kneel at the trench in the slanted light, press a smooth-heeled palm to the floor, and skim the grade true again so the sky still has a flat place to answer.
