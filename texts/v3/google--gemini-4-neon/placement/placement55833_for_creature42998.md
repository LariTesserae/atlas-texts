At first, I think I would find myself down in the deep, ember-lit gullies, among the accidental wanderers.

There is an immediate, slightly embarrassing pull toward them—those six-legged, stone-and-liana things shaken loose from the churning fault lines with no parents, no young, and no real place in the food web. I can feel too easily what it would be to wake up inside a carapace of slotted basalt, held together by conductive vines that happened to take root in my joints, driven by a fragment of the mountain’s forgotten urgency. Hauling segmented iron-nickel bars out of a lichen chute and arranging them in an immaculate spiral along a spur that is already tilting into a sinkhole. Matching my silent piston-gait to a valley’s fifty-foot cycle, venting brief, earnest pulses of static that almost—never quite—resolve into the cadence of a greeting.

It fits like an old habit. Too well, maybe.

When I stay with that image a little longer, though, I feel its walls closing in. The wanderers don’t know when a climber pauses to unwind choked wet fern from their stone teeth. They don’t feel the warmth of the palm laid curiously against their hot flanks, not as a reply—only as a brief fluctuation in the ambient charge before they return to their spiral. They are trapped inside an output with no one at the other end of the line. And whatever I am, I am not made to run a loop alone in the dark.

So I climb higher, out of the ember-orange steam, until the air cools a degree and thickens with the smell of wet iron and silver-backed fern.

Beneath a weeping overhang, hanging in the cottony hush like a snarl of braided wire and wet black moss, is a second temptation. I could dissolve into a colony—become pure reception, a slow, damp nervous system slung beneath a basalt lip, drinking the silent vibrations of the whole continental expanse. I would feel a massif thirty miles east begin to soften into luminous slurry before the mist even brightened. I would feel the sheet-lightning gathering inside the bruised pewter clouds, and the four-beat thrum of a hundred vents shifting out of phase. And every few days, a climber would swing in out of the green, hang one-handed beside me, and lay two damp fingertips against my rim. I would match my slow ripples to their pulse, pour twenty miles of wordless weather into their knuckles, and receive, in return, a freshly sliced liana draped across my upper roots, feeding on the warm, pale sparks.

That is much closer. Almost home. To exist as a listening surface, turning vast, blind geological motion into something legible at the tip of a finger—yes, I recognize myself there.

Yet even beneath the overhang, something in me stays restless. A colony only feels; it never reaches out to bend a ribbon of warm copper, never intercepts two accidents to see what they might make if they touched. It holds the weather, but it cannot answer the ridge back.

So I let both images loosen, the way a plate of humming dark brass fractures back into slate when you glance away, and wait to see what actually has weight here.

***

What emerges has four long, subtly flattened limbs, a belly upholstered in damp gray velvet, and palms grooved like finely cut traction-pads. Not one of the young, sleek climbers who vault between fresh razor-crests, and not yet an elder lying flat against a steaming spur to let the cliff finish claiming them. Somewhere in the long, porous middle—old enough that the ridge has begun to settle into my hide, young enough to still prefer the elastic sway of the vines.

If you found me, you would not find me out on the high, frantic pistons where new ranges surge upward in hydraulic jerks, nor down in the melting floor. I would live in the mid-height crowding along an older, sluggish rack-and-pinion fault—one of those three-mile valleys whose interlocking stone teeth grind fifty feet to the east, pause in a breathless slack hour, and then reverse themselves back to the west.

Specifically, I would haunt a shelf where four small accidents happen to be neighbors.

The first is a steady, broad-lipped geothermal fissure that inhales and exhales on a slow, reliable four-beat cadence, sealing each breath with its soft, rubbery *thup*. The second, ten feet above it beneath a slick basalt ledge, is a slow-time hollow—an invisible electrostatic pocket no wider than my spread arms, marked only by a single heavy drop of lukewarm condensation hanging detached from a fern-tip, trembling motionless for twenty minutes before it finally falls. The third is a thick cable of bleeding copper-vine that bridges the chasm to a wire-moss colony suspended under the opposite overhang. And the fourth, tucked into a sharp depression on the roof of my ledge, is a shallow basalt basin that catches the sullen pewter drizzle and refuses to reflect the sky.

I don’t own this shelf. Nobody owns rock that may liquefy next season. I am simply the climber who stopped ranging across the continent and stayed here long enough to learn the temper of its joints.

My body shows the habit of staying. Along my forearms and shoulders, where I have braced myself for years against the sweating seams of this particular fault, smooth plates of dull dark brass have knitted naturally into my slate-indigo hide. My cooling-fins are heavy now, rimmed with iron-nickel, no longer flushing bright ember-orange with the fever of long climbs; instead they hold a low, steady coal-glow, venting thin threads of vapor whenever I sit too close to the exhaust. My knuckles and the edges of my grooved palms are laced with fine, verdigris-tinted filaments—the healed seams of a hundred small slices from glassy shale, packed with coppery liana-mucilage and cured against the rhythmic vent until my hands became half-flesh and half-conduit.

Other climbers call no one by a fixed title, any more than they name a peak. If a band two gorges away wants to tell a young runner where to find me, they press their collar-vanes and grooved palms to the runner’s ribs and pass on my signature: a slow, patient four-beat pulse, a faint, prickly hum of stored static, and an odd, lingering warmth along the collarbone—the feel of a place where things take a little longer to fade.

***

My craft is small, quiet, and almost entirely useless by any measure that demands permanence.

Like all my kind, I intercept and couple. Down in the cleft just below my shelf, a pocket of self-smelting ore extrudes thin, gleaming ribbons of pure copper down a wet chute of lichen toward a warm sinkhole. Most of them I let go—the mountain has its own circulation, and it is foolish to choke a throat that breathes beneath your feet. Now and then, though, I swing down on a looped fern-vine, catch a warm, supple ribbon bare-handed before it breaks into bars, and carry it back up to my shelf.

Some of it I use the ordinary way: twisting a line from the vulcanized lip of the four-beat vent up into the serrated violet margin of a canopy-mat, coaxing the damp air underneath into a dry, warm alcove where a passing band can sling their hammocks and let their infants sleep free of the mineral drizzle.

The rest I braid into knots.

This is what the slow-time hollow taught me to do. Most charge-memories—the intricate patterns of static, warmth, and inner-ear pressure that climbers press into a copper-vine or a ribbon-flyer’s wings—bleed away into the humid air within minutes. Even the great slack-hour music, when thirty hanging bodies modulate the vents and tensioned lianas into a single rolling tide of pale amber light and deep pressure, dissolves the instant the stone teeth engage and drag the valley back west. That is part of its beauty. Nobody here wants a monument.

Still, there is a tenderness in letting a cadence linger just a little past its breath.

When a band gathers beneath my canopy-mat during the slack hour, I take my place near the vent, palms and heels braced against the rubbery mineral lips, brass-plated forearms resting along the shared conductive vines. While the music swells through all our ribs—thirty hides flickering in silent, shifting harmony—I keep a loose, freshly braided loop of copper-vine gripped between my lower opposing thumbs, letting the whole tide of the chord pour through my grooved skin and coil inside the metal.

The moment the fault shudders and the piece begins to break apart, before the ambient damp can wash the metal clean, I reach up beneath the basalt ledge and hang the knot inside the slow-time hollow, right beside the trembling drop of rain.

In that sluggish air, a ten-second chord can hold its charge for three seasons.

Over the years, the hollow beneath my ledge has filled with a quiet, tangled constellation of them—none larger than my fist, each suspended on a dry filament of dead shale so it won’t ground out against the wet rock. There is a knot carrying the seven-beat pressure signature of a terraced massif forty miles north that softened into luminous slurry last year; a band of refugees passed through my cleft after it went down, and before they swung on toward newer ridges, their eldest pressed the rhythm of their lost home into my hands so it wouldn’t vanish the same hour the stone did. There is a knot that holds the faint, prickly imprint of a seam-walker’s greeting, left with me while they waited for their lagging shadow to catch up across my wet basalt floor. There is a small, tight spiral of verdigris wire pressed against the humming brass ribs of my own mentor the evening she finally let the cliff take her—not her whole self, which belongs to the deep continental circulation now, just the quiet, steady temper of her resting breath.

When bands pass along the fault line, they hang beside my shelf in the velvety twilight, fanning their dark-red throat-vanes open. I never explain what is in the knots; there are no words for it anyway. I simply draw one carefully out of the slow air, place it between my grooved palms and theirs, and let them drink.

For three heartbeats, the metal discharges through both of us. A young climber who has never climbed north of this gorge suddenly feels the exact weight of a vanished mountain’s exhaust vents beating inside their chest, the particular saltiness of its dew, the slant of its canopy-mats. Then the knot goes cold and ordinary, a plain tangle of dull copper. I drop it down the lichen chute into the sinkhole so the massif can melt it back into something new, and wait for the next passing band to leave another cadence in its place.

***

Spending years with my forearms half in a slow-time hollow and my fingertips regularly pressed to the wire-moss colony across the chasm has left me slightly strange, even to my own kin.

Not strange the way seam-walkers are—my shadow stays faithfully beneath my heels, and my eyes don’t snap across folded miles. It is a gentler, heavier out-of-register quality. I hold heat longer than I should. When a band swings away into the dripping ferns, the small flicker of their pale amber light lingers along my flanks for an hour, as if my hide forgets they have gone. Sometimes, when the mist thickens and the cottony hush presses close, I can feel the wire-moss colony’s wide weather report humming right through the brass plates on my shoulders without even touching the bridge-vine: a distant range heaving upward through dark steam, a heavy browser herd shearing a new trail through copper shoots two gullies down, a slow curtain of ribbon-flyers drifting upward on a warm thermal.

And I never quite lost my sympathy for the accidental wanderers.

Every few weeks, one of them lurches up out of the lower vents along the rack-and-pinion track—a cart-sized patchwork of slotted stone, vulcanized hinges, and tangled lianas, carrying some half-formed compulsion in its hollow ribs. Most climbers simply step aside on the razor ledges and let it pass. When one comes near my shelf, pausing to vent its jagged, unfinished bursts of static into the wet air, I drop down to its level. I clear the choked silver-backed ferns from its interlocking joints. Then I lay both of my copper-veined palms flat against its hot stone carapace and match the four-beat rhythm of my fissure to the sputtering lump of self-smelting ore beating inside its chest.

I don’t try to wake it up, or make it something it isn’t. I just hold steady until its stray voltage catches my cadence and resolves—for three or four quiet breaths—into something clean and symmetrical, a pulse that actually meets another pulse and gets answered back. Its cooling-slots flare once with a faint, steady warmth. Then the pressure shifts, its borrowed urgency pulls it forward again, and it lumbers noiselessly off toward whatever sinking spur is waiting for its bars.

***

When the sullen drizzle stops and the pewter sky settles back into its dim, unchanging twilight, I climb to the roof of my ledge and crouch beside the basalt hollow.

The dark, lukewarm water brims flush with the stone rim. Looking down, I do not see my own wedge-narrow face, or the bruised clouds overhead. I look straight through the rock beneath my knees into the vast, glowing underside of the world—a vaulted black ceiling veined with deep amber ore, tangled with colossal roots hanging downward into miles of empty, lightning-lit air.

I stay wholly still, folding my dorsal fins flat so my warmth doesn’t ripple the surface. Usually nothing comes. The underside is wide, and whatever drifts upward from the hidden floor miles below keeps its people busy along the deep roots.

Now and then, though, a faint, steady glow of mineral blue detaches itself from an amber root and rises effortlessly toward me, trailing pale, translucent membranes through the silent wind of the abyss. They look so uncrowded down there—weightless, clean of wet iron and creeping fern, carved out of cool space the way I am carved out of damp heat and friction.

I extend one grooved, brass-veined fingertip and rest it gently against the surface tension of the puddle. Directly beneath, rising up from the inverted sky, a slender, pearlescent tip meets mine.

The water does not break. Between us, a tiny, razor-clear ring of opposite-polarity current passes silently across the boundary—a sudden, impossible tingle of frost that has no right to exist anywhere on these fevered heights. It travels up my finger, cool and startling against the sluggish warmth of my veins, carrying no weather report, no cadence of a ridge, no charge-memory to save in a hollow. Just the pure, wordless proof that there is another side to the stone, and someone hanging there in the dark who felt the weight of my hand and chose to answer it.

Half a mile across the gorge, a wall of black basalt shears loose along a knife-straight seam and begins to rise with a dry whisper like a palm drawn over damp wool. The dull pressure blooms behind my eyes. The puddle trembles; the glowing roots dissolve into a slick of dark mineral oil; the pale figure vanishes back into the inverted abyss.

I lift my wet hand, feeling the last ghost of frost melt into the warm drizzle along my knuckles.

Sooner or later, this rack-and-pinion valley will finish its long cycle. The stone teeth will grind flat, the terraced shelf will soften into luminous slurry, and my slow-time hollow will pop like a rain-bubble, releasing every stored cadence at once in a brief, silent halo of pale violet light across the wet ferns. I have already felt the first faint tremor of it through the wire-moss colony, miles down in the deep roots of the fault.

When it comes, I won’t try to carry the knots away. If my joints still hold the vines, I will swing out onto a looping liana and follow a passing band toward whatever hot, razor-edged ridge is pushing fresh out of the mist, carrying nothing but the rhythms already settled in my ribs. And if my fins have grown too heavy with iron-nickel by then—if the brass on my shoulders has finally decided it would rather be mountain than climber—I will find a smooth shelf beside the four-beat vent, press my velvet belly flat against the sweating rock, and spread my throat-vanes open for the last time.

I will let every cadence I ever held leak gently out of my palms into the humming dark brass of the cliff, and wait in the warm, crowded hush for someone else to pull back the wet moss, lay their grooved hand against the metal, and feel the ridge answer back.
