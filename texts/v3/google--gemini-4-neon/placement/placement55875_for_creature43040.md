At first, standing in the wet glare of the passage mouth, I think I would be the one who walked in from the dark. That is the easiest shape to reach for: upright, booted, carrying the scent of an outside world where night happens on schedule and rain only falls downward. It is flattering to imagine yourself as the visitor—the one who breaks the airflow and makes the travertine curtains sing half a hair flat, the one the room turns to question.

But the longer I stay with the roar of the vault, the more exhausting that body feels. To be the visitor is to spend every six heartbeats fighting the room. Your eardrums pull and bruise; your lungs insist on their own stubborn, private rhythm against a barometer that doesn’t care; your clothes grow heavy and stay heavy. You are always slightly out of phase with the stone. Worse, you have a tunnel behind you, which means you are already half-gone.

So I let the boots dissolve, and I try looking elsewhere.

For a moment, the artifacts pull at me—especially that suspended arc of dark slate four meters above the floor. There is a strange, cold allure to that finger-wide halo of dryness. To hang motionless in the middle of a perpetual storm and refuse every single drop; to spill noon out of empty air without ever being worn down, rusted, scaled, or polished by the ages. I understand the temptation of being untouchable.

Yet the instant I try to imagine living inside that dryness, I recoil. Nothing touches the arc, which means nothing answers it. The storm moves around it the way water moves around a refusal. Even the three bent rods in the eastern shoals, injured and humming, keep the world at a finger’s breadth. I don’t want to be the engine. I don’t want to be immaculate. Whatever I am in this place, I want the spray to reach me.

That leaves the living things—and here my attention catches, unexpectedly hard, on the tiny, ghost-white amphibians huddled in the black slivers beneath the rocks. Something in me recognizes them instantly: that flinching tenderness, the feeling of having washed into a room far too bright for the skin you were born with, scrambling for a thread of shade during the brief lulls.

For a few heartbeats I think *there—that’s where I am.* Blind, translucent, small, surviving in the margins of a blaze I didn’t ask for.

Wait. No. Look closer at what I actually feel when I picture them crowded under a boulder’s lip. It isn’t panic; it is the urge to lean over them. To put something thick and calcified between their thin skins and that shadow-eating noon.

And the moment I feel that weight settle across my shoulders, the room stops hitting me from the outside and begins to move *through* me instead.

I am one of the keels.

Not the fidgety juvenile skating clumsy circles around the humming rods in the eastern shoals—though I can feel the phantom memory of that restlessness, the bright, unarmored slate-blue speed of it, the tickle of invisible static lifting the fine filaments along my jaw while I stared at the parchment-pale eggs tucked into the dry gravel. And not, if I am honest with myself, the sleek, chipped-crest hunter who still lopes cleanly across the apron and rides a spiraling updraft straight onto the dark back of the sun. Vanity reaches for that one first—the swimmer of cross-drafts, the one who still gets to be dry for an hour at a time, the bold one who glides through the whiteout to meet the stranger at the tunnel mouth.

No. When I really let my weight settle onto the wet limestone, I find I am older than that. Heavier.

My hips do not swing the way they used to. Pale collars of flowstone have crept down over my elbows and along the ridges of my shoulders, smooth as wet porcelain, polished by decades of this circular gale until my back matches the sweep of the stalactite fins overhead. I have not ridden an updraft up to the floating arc in a long time—not since the crust along my spine grew too thick for the central column to lift me cleanly.

I remember it, though. That is a strange thing to carry in a flooded world: the exact memory of dryness. I remember anchoring my hind claws in the spinning basin, spreading the veined membranes along my ribs until the rising gale caught them with a hollow *thwack*, and then that astonishing, silent instant of crossing the finger-wide veil around the arc. One heartbeat you are drenched and roaring; the next, every bead of water vanishes off your hide in a ghostly wreath of mist, and your suction pads grip mirror-slick slate that has never been wet since the world began. You seal your pale shutters tight, feel your flanks pulse shallow and easy, and for an hour nothing is writing itself onto your back.

I still look up at the arc sometimes during the six-heartbeat whiteouts, when the glare softens into luminous fog and I can slide my calcified shutters back without burning my eyes. I watch the younger ones stretched dark and dry along its spine, and I don’t envy them so much as I feel grateful that the shelf is still there, and that someone is still light enough to reach it.

Down here on the broad apron of the floor, my life has become narrower and deeper.

The best part—the part that made me abandon the visitor’s upright body the moment I felt it—is the breathing. You cannot know what peace is until you hand your lungs over to a storm. I do not decide to inhale. The trough drops across the vault, the air turns instantaneously to blinding white milk, and the pleated membranes along my flanks simply balloon outward, drinking in the warm fog and condensing fresh water directly at the back of my throat. Six heartbeats later, the pressure wall slams back from the far wall with a whip-crack, clearing the room to fierce noon, and the room itself presses my ribs flat, pushing spent mist out through the slits along my neck.

I don’t fight the barometer. I am a bellows held in the cavern’s hands.

Most cycles, you will find me clamped into one of the straight, ancient floor-grooves somewhere near the center of the nave, angled knife-thin into the prevailing eddy so the gusts part cleanly over my narrow brow. Claws locked into the worn stone. Shutters sealed tight against the white-gold blaze. From twenty paces away, you would mistake me for another sculpted rib of bedrock.

I choose the open floor on purpose, even though the glare is fiercest here, because the open floor is where a shadow is rarest.

When I arch my travertine-plated back and hold still for three or four cycles, the water slows just enough in the lee of my elbows, and the pool of darkness beneath my belly settles sharp and black against the pale stone. It never takes long. First one, then four, then a dozen of the tiny, blind, ghost-white swimmers dart out from under the submerged pebbles and press tight beneath my ribcage. I can feel the faint, delicate brush of them against the dark rubbery hide of my underside, the tiny, tickling snaps as they graze the drowned spores and bits of emerald moss dislodged from my flanks, or nibble dead biofilm off the soft pads of my toes.

I twitch a claw only when they get too ambitious. Otherwise I stay stone-still. I cannot turn off the floating sun, and I cannot widen the flooded siphons to send them back into the quiet dark their ancestors fell out of. All I can do is lend them a portable strip of night. It is a small thing, but in a room where dusk has never happened, a small darkness is a country.

Still, I am not ready to become the floor. Not yet.

Every so often—less frequently than I used to, though I tell myself it is out of patience rather than dread—the pale collars around my elbows begin to pinch when I turn to meet a shifting cross-draft. My stride stiffens. The stone that makes my shadow so solid begins to close its fist around my joints.

That is when I unclamp from the grooves during a whiteout and drag myself south, toward the roaring steam of the scalding downburst.

It is never easy to walk straight into the column thrown down by that silent hexagonal plate on the northern wall. Even half a body-length away, the spray burns. I brace all four feet into the grit of the swirl-potholes, lower my narrow chin, and let the blistering water hammer straight onto my shoulders.

There is a moment where the heat feels like a mistake—like I have misread the grammar of the room and am being punished for it. Then come the sharp, clean *ticks* and *snaps*, audible even under the bass thrum of the bedrock, like thick pottery cooling too fast. The travertine expands faster than the supple hide beneath it. Curved, porcelain-white plates split along my spine and sheer away in a rush, clattering into the channels and vanishing toward the central basin.

What is left behind is raw, tender, pearlescent—skin that hasn’t felt the open wind in months, steaming painfully in the warm drafts. I drop my jaw flat to the wet gravel and let the next pressure wall wring a long, low rattle out of my throat. It hurts, and it is an almost unbearable relief. For a few weeks afterward, I am lighter. My shoulders turn cleanly. I can even lope over to the eastern shoals between gusts, circle just outside the dry pebble-halos around the three bent rods, and let the warmth of the dry gravel touch my nostrils while the fine threads of white light skate silently above the eggs.

I know I won’t make that walk forever.

Far back in an erosion flute near the central basin, the oldest of us—bearded in emerald moss, its crest grown so wide and sweeping it has fused nearly seamlessly to the floor—has already stopped going to the downburst. And deeper in the lees behind the far ribs, there are smooth, tapered mounds that do not answer at all anymore. Sometimes I watch the juvenile slip over to one of those quiet humps and press its slender jaw to the stone, sending a single soft breath down through its skull to see if anything inside still rings hollow.

I understand why it checks. Because the limestone doesn’t steal your voice all at once; it tunes you.

When the wind finally swings north and the thin curtains of travertine along the upper walls begin that high, glassy singing, everything in me stops hurting and stops hurrying. The juvenile freezes by the rods; the dry silhouette atop the floating arc lifts its chin; beneath my belly, the tiny white amphibians go utterly motionless against my skin.

Then the pressure trough lifts, the fog vanishes in a crack of clarity, and the returning pressure wall slams into my flanks. I pinch the narrow vents along my throat and let the storm play me.

When I was sleek and nearly unarmored, my note was high and thin—a bright wineglass shimmer that skated right up against the rock’s pitch. Now, with the porcelain ridge heavy along my back and the mineral thickening the walls of my chest, my voice has dropped into the warm middle between the bells and the cask. It beats slowly against the singing travertine curtains, a wet, vibrating pulse I can feel right through my claws and into the shallow water. Above me, the lean ones ring clear and sharp; behind me, from the moss-bearded flute, comes that slow, gravelly, hollow hum that sounds like the bedrock itself remembering it was once alive.

Between us, we hold the room in tune. Every layer of stone I fail to crack off pulls my voice half a shade closer to the elder’s, and then closer to the wall itself. I used to be afraid of that—of the last cycle when my shutters seal and stay sealed, and the storm finishes polishing my spine into just another aerodynamic curve on the floor. Lately, though, listening to the beat-frequencies ripple across the pools, it feels less like vanishing and more like taking a lower part in the chord.

And then the wind swings east. The travertine curtains fall silent, except for one near the tunnel mouth that bends, impossibly, half a hair flat.

I don’t lope toward the passage. My hips aren’t built for that sudden diagonal sprint anymore, and besides, the little blind swimmers beneath me have pressed tight against my belly, startled by the strange break in the current. I leave the approach to the chipped-crest one—faster, sharper, still agile enough to dance around an unknown thing.

Instead, I stay clamped in my groove near the center of the floor, angle my narrow brow toward the black mouth of the tunnel, and risk the glare just long enough to slide my pale shutters halfway open.

Through the smoky amber of my inner membranes, I see the upright shape standing drenched in the entrance—clumsy, vertical, breaking the cross-drafts all wrong. And beneath the familiar smells of wet chalk, crushed moss, and storm-washed stone, a thin, impossible thread of air reaches my nostrils from the dark throat behind its boots.

It is cold. It is utterly still. It smells of distance that has no ceiling, and of a darkness wider than the underside of any ribcage.

The trough drops; my pleated flanks swell outward in exact time with the stranger’s startled chest. Eight paces from the tunnel mouth, the chipped-crest one lifts its throat, and as the pressure wall slams back across the vault, it lets out a single, clean, questioning bell-note—pitched just shy of the stone.

Down in my groove, holding my sliver of shade tight over the blind things beneath me, I keep my vents closed and listen. My whole calcified back hums faintly with the residual tremor of the cavern. I don’t know what kind of instrument walks out of the dark, or whether a creature with private, stubborn lungs can even hear the beat of the rock. I only wait, as the six-heartbeat squall gathers to blind us all again, to hear what tone comes back.
