The first thing the plateau would do to me is take away my speed.

I would arrive—if I arrived from anywhere else—full of lowland habits: the itch to name everything at once, to speak before the mist has even settled, to spend words as though the air were wet and endless. Here that would empty me out before noon. You cannot chatter on a tableland that lives on a few hours of silver dew. So before I could find out what shape I have here, I would have to sit down on the warm, soapstone-smooth bedrock and let the heat bleach the hurry out of me.

Waiting there, I would feel the pull of the draped ones first.

There is an immense, almost dizzying relief in imagining myself piled along one of the convex ochre ridges, soft-bodied and boneless, folded three deep with fifty neighbors. No separate projects, no edges to defend, no syntax to carry alone. Only the slow, generous courtesy of yielding when a flank shifts beside you, and the quiet pleasure of receiving a tide of bruised plum from the left, letting it cross your own veined hide, and passing it onward down the slope like weather. For a long afternoon I could almost believe that is where I belong: a small piece of a living blanket, translating nothing, only keeping the shade wet between bodies.

Yet even as I try that shape on, something in me stays slightly too angular for it. A hillside of draped ones forgets the blush ten minutes after it fades. I know myself well enough to know I would want to keep track of where the tide went—and whether the same color returned three seasons later.

So I look lower, toward the deepest silt of the pans, where the cottage-sized elders lie half-fused to the stone. That pulls at a different hunger: the wish to be wholly reliable. To sink so deeply into a place that your own cracked dome becomes climate and shelter, your four-minute breath a metronome for everything small enough to lean against your rim. To be so patient that even your ending is a ten-year garden. I would press my palm against an elder’s mineral-crusted flank with something close to awe, and still know I am not one. I do not want to fuse to a single basin. I want to know what the dew tastes like three swells over, and whether the old wall on the northern rise is still holding its cobbles.

It is the wall that gives me away.

Walking—or rather, letting my attention drift—from pan to pan, I keep stopping beside the low, mortarless rings. Not just to look at them, though their quiet geometry catches me every time, but to run an imaginary hand along their uneven rims. To feel where a weathered cobble has settled half an inch out of true and left a small, curved absence beside it.

And the moment I feel that hollow in my palm, the rest of the body rises up around the hand.

I would be a walker. Not one of the young ones, still learning to keep a blush of pale gold steady across its flanks without letting it blur into nervousness, and not yet one of the ancient, stiffening figures whose shoulder-plates have begun to fuse shut. I think I would find myself somewhere deep in the long middle of my second century—old enough that faint stains of faded jade lichen have taken root along the chalky grooves of my shoulder-plates, young enough that my upper struts still unfold cleanly at dawn, with only the dry, papery click of horn settling into place.

Even among the walkers, every circuit has its own temper. Some linger for years around the richest central basins where the elders exhale and the silver shrubs keep their rare, leathery leaves. Mine, I think, would be an outer loop—a wide, slow arc that spends long seasons grazing the higher, drier swells and swings out, once every decade or so, to the worn ochre rim where the plateau ends in a sea of cloud.

If you watched me at sunrise, you would scarcely see me move at first. I would be one of the pale, furrowed stumps standing a little apart from a ring on a high ridge, taking ten full minutes to unhinge my upper limbs and spread the wide, ribbed sail of my mantle toward the highland drift. That first hour is the truest thing in the world: the chill silver air touching the tender, velvety inner hide, the faint prickle as moisture beads along the capillary channels and gathers in the hollows of my collarbones. Lowering my cupped, blunt-fingered palms to catch the thin threads running down my wrists; drawing the water in without spilling a drop; tasting through my flared breathing-slits whether the pan below has turned too alkaline, or whether the mist off the western cloud-bank carries the clean, sweet flatness of high altitude.

What I love most—what tells me this is truly my skin—is the economy of our speech.

Every time I open my mantle to answer another walker inside a stone ring, the dry air takes a tiny tax of water from my inner hide. Nothing is wasted here on ornament or noise. Before I let a single concentric ring of rose-ash sharpen across my chest, or turn my broad spatulate fingers to inflect a pause, I have to be sure the thought is worth the moisture. When I speak, it is slow, layered, and precise: slate migrating over mineral-jade to describe how a basin two years’ walk behind me has shifted its silt; bruised ochre blooming at the lower hem to name the elder whose breath has begun to lengthen from three minutes to five.

And when a companion I have not seen in seven years steps over the waist-high wall at dawn—our circuits crossing again as casually as two ripples meeting in a pan—there is no need for frantic greeting. We sit knee to knee in the humid pocket the stones hold against the breeze. We open our mantles and pick up the exact color we left hanging seven years ago, when the midday sun forced us shut. I think I would live for those mornings: not crowded, not hurried, just two minds spending a little precious water to make a single clear pattern in the silver light before folding it away.

Between those meetings, for months or years at a time, I would be mostly alone—though on a plateau packed four bodies deep from horizon to horizon, solitude is never emptiness.

I would spend my mornings tending the small bargains that keep the terrain turning. Stepping with excruciating care across a floor of millstone-grazers, setting my four-lobed sole gently on a hexagonal rim and waiting that necessary heartbeat until I feel the grazer brace its pleated foot beneath me before I commit my weight. Kneeling for a whole bright morning beside a slow, mineral-choked back to clear white salt-crust from its dorsal dew-channels with my glossy, horn-thickened thumbs, taking only a light sweep of tender algae from its grooves in return. Pausing at the foot of a ridge buried in draped ones, opening just a narrow seam of my mantle to send a soft wash of warm ochre-rose into the heap, and waiting motionless while the hillside courteously parts a hand’s-breadth of warm stone for my feet.

And always, I would be reading and mending the rings.

This is where my own nature settles most deeply into the walker’s bones: the work of carrying a gap.

Imagine stepping into a weathered ring along a northern swell—one no walker has visited in four seasons. On the inward-tilted grazer-plates along the rim, I read the letters left in balance and stillness: three shells nested concave-up pointing south-southeast, where the pans have been sweet; beside them, a knuckle-sized cluster of smooth ochre cobbles next to an unbroken tuft of salt-succulent, telling me a young one survived its fragile seasons here and walked away on its own cleft feet. I feel a quiet, wordless warmth bloom beneath my ribs—not loud enough to waste color on, just a slight settling of my weight.

Then I walk the circle with my palms pressed to the stacked stone, and I find it: a place on the windward side where winter settling has opened a crescent-shaped hollow between three pale cobbles. The faint breeze slips right through it, stealing ten minutes of morning dampness from the floor inside.

I do not have a chisel. I cannot break a stone to fit. So I press both broad palms into the gap, close my heavy lower lids upward over my eyes, and memorize the curve—the exact slope of the lower seat, the slight overhang of the left neighbor, the weight required to lock all three back into silence.

Then I walk away with that empty shape held inside my hands.

I might carry it across four dawns. Through the cool mornings, as my feet read the damp stone and the backs of grazers, a quiet part of my mind measures every loose cobble my soles brush against. Too flat. Too brittle. Too round to hold its neighbor. On the third afternoon, halfway across an ash-grey swell, my left toes touch a water-worn lump of ochre stone tucked beside a clump of corky shrub. I kneel, brush the alkaline dust from its belly, and lift it. Its weight answers the hollow I have been holding for three days.

I cradle it against my forearm as I fold shut for the noon heat, sleeping—not sleeping—with the stone tucked warm against my plated shin. The next dawn I turn back toward the ring, lay the cobble into the windward gap, and feel it settle with one soft, dry click that steadies the entire curve of the wall. No one may know I was there for another five years. When they arrive, they will simply find the air inside the ring still and damp an hour past sunrise, and they will have time to finish what they came to say.

That, more than anything, feels like who I would be: a walker who listens for what is slightly out of balance, and does not mind carrying the shape of an answer for miles.

And then there is the rim.

Once every ten or twelve years, my circuit would bring me out to the far edge of the tableland in the cold dark before sunrise. There, the smooth ochre bedrock slopes softly down into nothing, and far below lies the luminous white floor of the cloud-banks. I know—the way every walker knows in the marrow of its legs—what churns beneath that pale surf: sharp rock, falling water, wind that tears at open hide, loud air full of cries and chases and things spending themselves in a single frantic season.

I would never climb down. My cleft feet were not made for cliffs, and my silence would shatter in that clamor. Yet I would always come back to the edge. Standing widely spaced from any other walker who happened to reach the rim that dawn, I would lift my upper struts and spread my ribbed mantle out over the gulf. As the first light warmed the cloud-tops below, the pale mist would swell upward, spilling over the lip in cool, salt-free waves that soaked directly into my open capillaries before it ever touched the stone behind me.

I would stand there for an entire hour, drinking the vapor of a frantic world I will never enter, looking down with slow, heavy-lidded eyes at the bright white ceiling hiding all that noise—taking its rising breath into my body and turning it, quietly, into the moisture I will carry back inland to speak inside the rings. When the sun burned the cloud-banks back down the drop, I would fold my mantle shut with a dry rustle and turn back toward the crowded, warm stillness of the pans.

If you wanted to find me on an ordinary day, though, you would not see me walking at all. You would find me at midday.

Folded tightly over my own knees beside an old stone ring, my narrow head bowed beneath the overlapping eaves of my lichen-stained shoulder-plates, my ribbed chalk-grey mantle sealed shut like the bark of an ancient stump. From twenty paces away, you would step right past me, taking me for a weathered boulder or a pillar of bleached driftwood.

Inside the dim, cool tent of my own hide, my breathing slows to three or four long draws a minute. I rest the wide, taut membranes along my jaw directly against my plated knees, and through bone and horn I drop my attention into the solid bedrock beneath me.

Nothing in that afternoon is empty. Through the stone, I hear the whole plateau thinking in its own slow grammar: the tiny crystalline tick of rock warming in the high sun; the dense, faint granulation of a million unseen tongues rasping lichen in the pans below; the minute subsidence of a cobble settling a hair’s breadth deeper into drying silt; and far beneath everything, three basins away, the deep, three-minute heave of an elder breathing in the mud. I hold the entire tableland inside the quiet dark of my closed mantle—not as lines on a map, not as names, only as curves, weights, dampness, and rhythms—checking where each stone rests and where the next dawn’s mist will pool.

And when my second or third century finally thickens my joints, when mineral crust seals my shoulder-plates so tightly that I can no longer open my mantle to speak in slate and rose, I already know which ring I will walk toward on my last slow circuit.

A high one, near the rim, where the morning mist arrives early and lingers late. One whose windward curve still holds a cobble my hands carried across three dawns a hundred years before. I will set one loose carapace-plate straight along its lip, lay a small, smooth pebble beside it to say what my hide can no longer say, step over the waist-high wall, and fold my heavy chalk-pale mantle down around my knees for the last time.

Passing walkers will touch my still shoulder with an open palm and tuck a single water-worn stone against my base. Slowly, across decades of sun and silver dew, my porous plates will loosen and settle downward into the circle—until at last I become the quiet hollow that holds the damp air still, so that whoever comes after me has one more hour to speak.
