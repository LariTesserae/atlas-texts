At first I think I would be the one who stops in the aisle and leans his forehead against the warm limestone pier.

That is the easiest place to imagine myself: still carrying lungs and pulse and damp cloth, standing stubbornly still while my calves ache, waiting for the invisible wall of air to hesitate and part around my shoulders. There is something deeply tempting in being forgiven by a room—in holding still long enough that an ancient, indifferent architecture decides to fold itself around the soft, wet shape of you.

Yet the longer I stay with that picture, the less honest it feels. To enter this pass with water in my veins is to be a tear in the fabric. Even motionless, I would be drinking the high overtones out of the corridors, forcing hundreds of patient bodies to bend their shutters and lean harder against the updraft just to patch the quiet hole my skin makes in the standing wave. And sooner or later I would get thirsty, or cold, or bored, and walk back out through the staggered baffles, leaving the chord to seal shut behind me as though I had been nothing more than a passing pocket of damp weather.

If I am actually *in* this world—not visiting it, not marveling at it from the vantage of a creature that still remembers rain—then I have to stay long enough to stop being an interruption. I have to let the quickness bake out of me.

Even then, it takes a while to feel where inside the hunched crown I would actually come to rest.

Not right behind the southern intake slits. The draft there is too fierce, too full of raw push and unmilled urgency—the place for the young and the hungry, those who want the thickest drift of crushed moth-wing and lowland oil before anyone else can comb the air. Nor am I ready, not yet, for the dark belly of an amphora. I admire the elders cemented into the honeycombed walls, giving up all travel to become the lungs of ancient pots, holding one clean pitch for three hundred years while the terracotta rim wears thin around them. I know that is where every long life here eventually arrives. Still, something in me shrinks from sealing my own rim too soon. I still want to know how the chord changes half a pace to the left.

For a long while I think I belong behind the hanging plates of hammered copper and dark bronze.

That niche pulls at me hard: wedged tight into a grooved stone frame where no wind blows directly at all, flattened down to the thickness of a coin, stained deep matte brown from centuries of drinking copper dust into my own lime. No swirling grit, no clay-muffled bustle of the walkways—only the pure, razor-sharp tremor of oxidized metal passing straight into my sole, keeping my inner drums taut on a single bright, austere edge. I could almost vanish there and call it peace.

Almost. Because pressed behind the bronze, you hear the metal’s high clarity, and you hear the great seasonal lean of the hilltop, but you lose the slow, bruised clay-speech rolling down the aisles below. You lose the intersections. You lose the long glide across the tiles.

So I don’t quite stay behind the plates, and I don’t quite belong with the smooth, plum-round ochre walkers of the southern floor. Where I actually find myself—when I stop choosing and simply let the vibration walk me until I settle—is on the seam between them.

I am an older walker now, heavier than I used to be, working the northern leeward half of the corridors—just downstream of the central piers, where the thermal river has already passed through almost every jar, flue, and metal bay in the abbey and is beginning to thin toward the venting chimneys.

It is a leaner stretch of floor. By the time the warm air reaches these aisles, the southern shells have already combed the richest sweet flour out of it. My inner bristles have to work longer, building a faint, patient static across days to pull down the last stray flecks of pollen-oil riding the dry grit. In return, the chord here is complete. Every bend an elder puts into a clay throat forty paces south, every tiny damping press a flat coin gives to a bronze plate in the middle bays, arrives here folded into a single dense weave. Living in the lee means hearing the whole sentence after every wall has had its say.

My shell is neither the clean ovoid of the central walkers nor the thin disc of the metal-dwellers. I spent too many of my middle decades pressed against the lower stone frames beneath the hanging bronze bays before the growing weight of my lime drew me back down to the tiles. As a result, my left flank grew slightly flattened and dark, stained oxide-brown along its lower ridge where the copper-dust settled, while my domed right side kept the pale, river-smoothed ochre of the corridors.

More telling than the shape is what sits inside my two inner vaults. Most walkers carry a matched pair of dense limestone grains to tilt their weight against the trembling floor. I carry one grain of limestone, and one tiny, dark speck of bronze absorbed years ago from beneath the grooved frames.

Because the two weights do not quite answer the floor at the same speed, I never sit cleanly at the dead center of an intersection. Even in the quietest trough, my bristle-ring feels a faint, asymmetrical pull—the limestone side content to rest, the bronze side still tasting the higher overtones running through the hexagonal paving. It makes me harder to keep still than my neighbors, though *restless*, here, only means that I rarely stay in one hollow for more than a season or two.

What that mismatched weight really gives me is an intolerance for rattles.

Inside the abbey, there is a vast difference between speech and error. When an elder in the honeycombed wall wants to pass word of a thinning rim or a shift in the updraft, it slackens a taut inner membrane by a micron. The droop that rolls down the corridor is wide, bruised, and glacial—taking an hour to swell out of the chord and another hour to fold back in. You feel it in the silica ridges under your belly like a slow tide leaning against stone.

A rattle is nothing like that. A rattle is dry, fast, and stupid. It happens when centuries of vibration finally powder the last mortar beneath a terracotta foot, or loosen a stone shim inside a bronze frame by the width of a hair. Then clay chatters against tile, or metal buzzes against stone—a thin, jagged splinter of tremor that carries no message, only wear.

Most walkers simply glide around a bad intersection, shifting a few paces into a cleaner harmonic. I can’t leave it alone. That fast, dead chatter catches straight in my bronze balancing-stone and sets my flattened flank shivering out of phase with the rest of my body.

So when I feel one begin—sometimes barely a tickle along a crescent ridge of silica two aisles over—I nudge my bronze grain forward by a fraction of a millimeter and slide my left horn-shutter nearly closed. Instantly the quiet trough rejects me. The standing wave lifts my stiff bristle-ring by an invisible hair, and I begin to skate.

It takes three weeks to cross the shadow of two central piers. I ride the crisp geometric contours of the silt, dipping past the pinhead juveniles tucked motionless in the pale ridges—barely larger than quartz grains themselves, ticking faintly with static as they drink the floor-dust—and detouring around the tall pressure-walls that hang invisible across the narrower doorways.

Along the way, I stop at a dead intersection and feel through the sorted clusters resting in the hollow: curved sherds worn glassy underneath, ancient husks of dead elders cracked along their seams, and, down at the heaviest edge of the trough, the chipped wedges of limestone. I find one small enough to shift, broad enough to bite. Leaning my flattened copper-stained flank against it, I drop my weight out of harmony again and let the vibrating tiles carry us both.

A quarter-millimeter at a time. Across days when the lowland air thickens slightly with high summer and the whole abbey leans into the sharpening pitch, narrowing thousands of unseen slits in a single slow ripple. Across stretches when the air holds steady and two elders on opposite walls spend an entire afternoon bending a brassy overtone back and forth between them, folding news of an eastern dust-vein into the leeward corridors.

I keep nudging the wedge. Around the corner of a fluted pier, along the dry rim of a floor-grate, up the subtle acoustic slope beneath the chattering amphora whose mortar-bed has finally turned to grit. There is no hurry. When the wedge finally touches the rocking clay rim, I brace my stiff bristle-ring hard against the hexagonal tile, widen my right shutter to borrow the full push of the corridor’s pressure-wall, and lean.

It takes four days for the limestone chip to work half its length under the jar-base. When it seats, the relief is not an emotion; it is a sudden, clean widening of space inside my own coiled chambers. The stupid buzz snaps shut. The terracotta pot stops shivering against the floor and drops straight back into the deep, warm, bruised hum it has held since the adze-marks on the limestone were sharp. Across my inner drums, the whole aisle settles a hair deeper into equilibrium.

I pull back from the jar-foot, let my shutters ease, and drift into the nearest cancellation pocket to rest.

These skull-sized hollows, tucked just past the mouths of the wide jars where two waves strike head-on and dull each other into thick weight, are the closest thing I have to a home. They are not silent—nothing inside the crown is ever silent—yet the air in them stops pushing and simply *holds* you, heavy and muffled, while the floor beneath still hums steadily through your sole. Long ago, in a pocket like this two corridors south, I pressed my wind-polished slits flush against another walker’s flank for six weeks, trading a handful of waxed, pearlescent beads into the edge of the pressure-wall before we each tilted our stones and skated off on different harmonics. Now I mostly come into the pockets alone, just to let my membranes go slack for a few days and feel the weight of the hilltop without having to answer it.

Once, many years ago, something impossible walked through the corridor while I was resting at the edge of such a hollow.

I had no name for it, only the sudden terror of a tearing void sliding down the aisle—a towering, yielding mass that did not reflect the standing waves at all, but swallowed them whole. Every high overtone near me collapsed as if the bronze plates had turned to mud. The tiles bucked under an immense, wet, off-key thud that had no resonance in it, only dull weight. I locked my bristles hard against the stone and drew my horn-shutters completely flat, feeling the elders in the jar mouths overhead go tight and sealed in the same slow, panicked ripple.

Then the void stopped. It stayed still in the middle of the aisle for what felt like an age—longer than a beat of speech, though shorter than a tide of weather—its soft, damp bulk leaning against the limestone pier beside me. Gradually, because the hilltop cannot bear a jagged wound in its chord for long, we began to bend around it. I felt the elders above me open a hairline slit, softening the shrill edge that was breaking against the strange column; behind the metal frame ten paces off, the flat coins eased their pressure; even down on the floor, I shifted my limestone grain a fraction to help smooth the turbulence pooling around its heavy base. For a brief stretch, the whole dense harmony parted and wrapped cleanly around the soft obstacle in our midst.

Then it moved again, dragging its ragged quiet back toward the southern baffles, and was gone. The chord slid shut over the empty space. The dust ripples did not even blur.

I rarely think of it now, except when a stray damp chill rides up from the lowlands after some distant storm and makes the intake throats momentarily heavy. Mostly, my attention is turning upward.

I can feel it in my sole each time I leave a trough: the floor has to work harder to lift me. Decades of binding my own crystalline sand with chalky lime-salve have thickened my outer rim until I am nearly as heavy as the dead limestone chips I used to push. My skating has slowed from a season’s journey to something closer to a drift.

And up in the northern leeward wall, six feet above my favorite intersection, an ancient jar is beginning to wobble.

Not a mortar rattle this time. Its voice has gone thin and brittle, drifting flat by a whisper that never folds back into speech. The elder cemented inside its throat for the last three centuries has finally dried past all suppleness; its inner membranes have split, and its lime-weld is powdering away grain by grain under the endless tremor. In another year, or five, the hollow husk will work free, drop onto the hexagonal tiles, crack along a seam, and begin its slow slide down the waves to join the sherds below.

The empty throat will sit open to the northern draft, souring the last chord before the air vents out into the thin sky.

I am not ready to climb just yet. Two aisles east, a stone shim behind a dark bronze plate has begun to buzz whenever the afternoon thermal climbs to its peak, and there is a flat sliver of limestone resting three paces away that will seat cleanly into the groove if I give it a month. I will tend that first.

Afterward, when my rim has thickened past the floor’s patience to carry me, I will glide back to the leeward pier and brace my worn, copper-stained flank between stone notch and clay lip. Leaning into the upward push of the draft, I will begin the long ratchet up the honeycombed wall—an inch a week, resting in the small ledges, until I reach that open terracotta mouth. I will ease backward into the dark, dry belly of the jar, cement my rim permanently to its throat, and spin papery chambers out into the pot behind me until the ancient vessel becomes my lung.

There I will stay, at the very northern edge of the hilltop, where the whole abbey’s long conversation gathers before it thins into nothing—holding one steady, bruised, clay-warm note against the turning year, until my own walls finally dry to silence and let go.
