At first, I think I would try to stay near the heat.

Any newcomer’s instinct on a shelf like this is to crowd close to the one thing that feels like a hearth. Under the low, drum-tight canopy of oiled drifter-membrane, wedged into the shallow channel behind a smelter’s shoulder-gill, the world narrows to something survivable: four feet of headroom, layered mats of felted walker-hide, the steady dry stove-warmth rising through pitted iron-slag into your knees, and beneath it all the muffled, pressurized thrum of the vats and grinding plates. You learn the cadence of the beast through your hipbones. You learn to answer three slow knuckle-taps traveling along the dorsal plates with two of your own, without lifting your eyes from the brass puddle-wire you are knotting into a repair-mesh.

I can feel how much of me would love that work. The quiet economy of it. Sliding out through the creased membrane into the soundless sheet-wind with a borer-tooth scraper, crouching in the thin sour steam to clear crusted clinker from the cherry-lit lip of the gill. Kneeling in the wet scree beside the blind shovel-jaw while the giant pauses between scoops, wiping mineral scale from its recessed thermal pits with a damp wad of copper-moss. Tending a creature that has no eyes to know your face and nothing like gratitude, only a cleaner burn and a steadier stride when you keep its knees greased and its draft open.

There is a deep rightness to that. Maintenance without ownership. Warmth earned through attention.

And yet, as I sit beneath that imagined canopy—listening to the low, unvoiced murmurs of a household, feeling the iron-slag floor settle fractionally as the smelter shifts half a length forward—I notice my attention keeps leaking past the weighted hem. Out across the fifty-mile basin. Down to the hairline file of stork-hipped walkers moving along the opposite slope, and down further still, to the bare, frost-roughened gravel between herds where nothing has passed in years except wind and mist.

If I am honest with the shape of the place, I do not stay on the smelter’s back.

Not out of restlessness. The plateau has no patience for restlessness; anyone who wanders out onto the scree for the sake of wandering simply freezes, and leaves behind a coat that someone else will have to walk out to strip before the mites ruin the seams. You leave a smelter because a smelter cools, or because a household grows too heavy for one spine, or simply because your ears—and the pale, raised ridges along your inner wrists—keep turning toward the intervals between things.

I find myself twenty paces behind the last walker in a horizon-long file.

Not nineteen, not twenty-five. The walkers hold their own exact spacing across leagues of damp shale, each animal stepping on cushioned, felt-like hooves into the neat circular prints of the one ahead, and if you keep their measure behind the tail of the line, they fold you into their silence the way they fold the laminar wind. I am stooped slightly against the cold sheet of air, hooded in cured drifter-membrane rubbed dark with mite-oil until the mist beads along my shoulders without soaking in. Every seam of my coat is sutured with fine brass wire gathered from the seepage-pans, bleeding the overhead static quietly down into my vulcanized soles so my skin never pricks. My scalp is shaved close and slicked with dark grease. Beneath my ribs, tucked into a pouch of lined hide where my own slow, iron-heavy blood can keep it from stiffening, sits a small brood of flat, leathery joint-symbiotes packed in damp moss—meant for a herd three valleys east whose lead smelter has begun to drag a rear column dry.

Beside that pouch hang a few coils of bright puddle-wire, a borer-tooth stylus, and a narrow strip of copper-rich walker-bone bound to a rod of dried sedge-pitch and sinew.

I am a courier. Though even that word sounds too crisp, too official for what the plateau actually makes of you. Out here, five days from the last pillar of steam and four from the next, you do not feel like a messenger carrying an errand from one fixed point to another. There are no fixed points. Even the herds are moving, just at the speed of geology. What you are is a piece of the circuit that happens to have legs and a memory.

Wait—let that settle a little longer, because something quieter is underneath it.

Walking behind a file of walkers across a featureless saucer of ferric shale, you have nothing to look at for hours at a time except the greased dark hindquarters twenty paces ahead, the etched copper comb rising along its spine, and the flat pewter sky that never brightens into noon or darkens into night. Because thin air swallows sound and the wind has nothing jagged to rattle against, there is no weather to distract you, either. Your own boots make no crunch on the wet gravel. Even your breath leaves almost no vapor through the twisted bone behind the bridge of your nose.

In that kind of quiet, your senses rearrange themselves. Your eyes stop hunting for horizons—there is only grey stone meeting grey cloud—and your attention drops into your palms, your teeth, and the soles of your feet.

You learn the plateau the way a blind animal learns a burrow: by gradient. The walkers are following the buried inductive seams beneath the basalt, so by matching their path you begin to feel the unseen veins of the continent through the faint, steady pull in your wrist-ridges. You know when the seam narrows, because the file bends two degrees north without a single hoof faltering. You know when we are crossing a wet mineral fault, because the fine hairs inside my cuffs stir slightly before a pale-blue thread of charge flickers silently from comb to comb down the length of the line ahead of me.

And every few leagues, you find the pins.

The walkers pay no mind to the flush-mounted plates of weathered iron protruding an inch from the scree; they step past them at the same unhurried pace, leaving me a choice. If I stop, I lose the tail of the line—not for long, because the circular hoof-prints hold their shape in the damp grit for hours, and a single courier can make up half a mile of distance by cutting the chord of a shallow basin. Still, every time I spot that stubby ceramic collar choked with frost and black moss, my stride breaks before I decide to break it.

I kneel on the wet gravel. I take the bone pick from my belt and work slowly around the seam, prying out every thread of black moss, scraping the rime cleanly down to the pale glaze. Not out of reverence—nobody remembers who drove these pins into the bedrock, and if I ever wondered, the air sanded the question smooth years ago. I clean the collar for the same reason a smelter-household clears slag from a shoulder-gill: because a fouled seam dampens the draw, and because I want to hear clearly.

I wipe the dark iron with my oiled sleeve, pull the glove from my right hand, and press my bare palm flat against the plate. With the other hand I set the copper-bone tip of my rod to the metal and hold the far end lightly between my teeth.

For a long minute there is only the numbing cold creeping up my wrist. Then the surface falls away, and the whole shelf opens underneath my jawbone.

It is not a sound. It is a slow, deep, low-voltage weight—a tide moving through hundreds of miles of ancient basalt. Held there in the damp quiet, with the walker-file already thinning into a grey thread a quarter-mile ahead, I can feel the whole continent turning at once. Far to the south, thirty leagues beyond the terraced rim, a rhythmic, shallow sag in the telluric hum: a big herd has halted in a damp basin, and a file of walkers is kneeling flank-to-flank against their armored sides, pouring charge into the internal furnaces. To the east, a faint, steady tension pulling upward against the bedrock, tightening the air along the high ridges—barometric weight gathering beneath the cloud-deck, waiting for a drifter to drift low enough to bleed it off. And somewhere far out on the northwestern flats, almost too faint to distinguish from the pulse in my own gums, a sudden, soft tremor of static releasing in a long smooth arc: a sky-drifter’s tendril brushing a sister-plate, casting sharp blue shadows for twenty silent minutes over forty crouching figures I cannot see.

That is where I live, I think. Not in the heat of the smelter, and not quite in the open scree either, but in that brief, bone-conducted hinge where the vastness of the plateau becomes legible to a single pair of hands.

When I finally lift the rod from my teeth and stand up, my knees are stiff and my fingers are pale as wet ash. I tuck my bare hand inside my coat against my ribs to thaw beside the moss-wrapped symbiotes, and set out across the gravel to catch the trail of the walkers.

Along the way, I read the ground.

This is the other half of what I have become—something no one asked me to be, yet everyone expects when my dark silhouette appears along the rim of their basin. A smelter-household stays with its beast. Its memory is deep and circular: it knows every frost-heave, every rich ferric seam, every sour seepage-pan along its own four-year loop. What it cannot easily know is what is happening on the loops beside it, or what became of the herds whose trails crossed its own twenty years ago.

Because I walk the long chords between the valleys, I pass the old billets.

Sometimes I see one half a bowshot off the walker-track—a smooth, dark rectangle of refined iron, half-sunk in damp gravel, blooming with cold ochre rust and flat crusts of olive-black lichen. I step aside to kneel beside it. I tap its flank once with a knuckle-bone; the dull, deadened ring and the depth of the pitting tell me it has sat here twenty, maybe twenty-five years. With my thumb—the pale, sensitive pad trained to feel voltage through four inches of slag—I brush away the rime along its beveled corner until I find the hairline notches someone pressed into the metal while it was still soft from the vent.

Three marks, crossed by a fourth. Beside them, seated darkly into the top surface where the cooling bar gripped it, a tiny loop of braided puddle-wire tied in a double slip-knot I recognize.

I know that knot. I saw an old woman with stiff knuckles tying it six seasons ago beside a shoulder-gill in the western saucer, weaving repair-meshes with fingers that could no longer hold a scraper. The notches say her herd passed through this hollow when she was young, that the shale seam behind them was lean, that two people died in the cold damp here and were weighted with smooth stones at the margin of the seepage-pan to the north.

Five miles up the slope, a different herd is grazing slowly toward this basin. In another three years, a blind shovel-jaw will drag through this exact patch of scree, scoop this rusted bar into its dark maw, and melt her double slip-knot back into raw iron and acid steam.

I do not try to save the billet. You cannot carry a refined ingot across forty miles of shale, and even if you could, keeping iron out of the furnace would be like trying to keep mist out of the sky. Nothing here is meant to stay separate from the loop.

Instead, I simply take the mark into my head, the way I take the sag of the current from the grounding-pins, and keep walking. When I reach the eastern basin four days later—rising out of the mist two miles down the terrace, raising my copper-bone rod toward the pewter overcast and touching its tip to the grounded brass braid at my cuff to throw three paired blue stitches of spark across the grey air—I will wait until a single answering flicker leaps from the shoulder-ridge of the lead smelter.

Then I will walk quietly across the damp scree, climb onto the warm, pitted armor, and sit knee to knee beneath their membrane canopy.

We will not rush. First I will take the pouch from inside my coat and hand over the warm, healthy symbiote-brood, and watch two of their lean young adults slip out with rendered tallow to pack the dry rear hip-socket before the giant’s leg begins to grind. We will drink distilled vent-water that tastes faintly of wet iron and ash. Then I will lay my bare wrists against an elder’s wrists, press my forehead lightly to theirs, and send the plateau’s quiet accounting straight through bone in low, hummed vibrations and soft clicks of unvoiced breath:

*The northern walkers are shifting east along a richer seam. A drifter fell beyond the third rim last month; the tallow was good. The old smelter in the far western basin is running dim behind its gill—its household will need space for two young ones before the next loop closes. And twenty miles back along the old shale hollow, I found your cousin’s knot on a twenty-five-year bar. The borers have nearly got it, and the next herd is three years behind.*

No one weeps at that, and no one celebrates. The elder simply holds my wrists a breath longer, nodding once beneath heavy brow-folds, letting the knowledge settle into the warm dark beneath the canopy like condensation settling onto slate.

Later, when the smelter lowers its hindquarters into a damp hollow and extrudes a fresh billet—still hot enough to melt the frost beneath it, sending up a faint sour ribbon of steam—we will climb down together and crouch in a tight ring around the dark bar, tucking our oiled sleeves over our knees. We will thaw our stiff fingers and warm bladders of tallow-broth against its flank. When the freezing air finally grips the iron and it contracts with that single hairline *tick*—sharp and solitary across five miles of silence—we will all fall motionless for one slow breath.

Before the metal hardens completely, someone will hand me the borer-tooth stylus. Not because I belong to their household, and not because I am an authority, only because I am the one who walked in from another valley, and my hand carries the marks of places they have never seen. I will press three small, disciplined notches into the beveled edge, just deep enough to outlast twenty winters of frost and olive-black lichen, shallow enough to be eaten cleanly when the circuit comes back around.

And in the flat, duskless afternoon that follows—with a fresh bladder of mist-water at my hip, a handful of salted trilobite meat, and a spare tooth of etched copper-bone tucked into my belt—I will slip down off the warm dorsal plates while the herd is still grazing, step out onto the wet gravel, and wait beside a damp hollow until the next unbroken file of walkers rises out of the mist.

I know how it ends, too. Everyone on the plateau knows, without needing to speak of it. Eventually the cold gets into the hips and stays there, the way grit gets into an untended smelter-joint. Twenty paces behind a stork-hipped walker becomes too long a stride to hold for five days at a stretch. When that afternoon arrives, I will climb onto whatever warm spine has room behind its shoulder-gill, hang up my sedge-pitch rod, and spend my last seasons knotting fine brass puddle-wire with slow fingers, listening to the knuckle-taps through the iron-slag plates while younger couriers bring sparks in from the edges of the mist. And when my slow, heavy blood finally stops turning, they will take my wire-stitched coat and my vulcanized boots, lay me out on a flat slab of shale beside a seepage-pan with smooth stones resting lightly on my wrists and ankles, and sit with me until the last warmth leaves my brow.

Until then, you would almost certainly miss me if you stood in the middle of the basin.

There is far more wet slate and low pewter cloud than life of any kind. Two miles away, a barge-sized smelter shifts its weight with a thin ribbon of pale steam. High overhead, a single translucent drifter trails its silent tendril toward a distant ridge. And far down the opposite slope, twenty paces behind the last muffled hoof in a line stretching from horizon to horizon, a narrow pewter-colored figure pauses beside a flat iron plate half-buried in the scree, scrapes the black moss cleanly out of its ceramic collar, presses a bare palm to the cold metal, and listens for a minute to the low, steady hum of the world before stepping quietly back into the mist.
