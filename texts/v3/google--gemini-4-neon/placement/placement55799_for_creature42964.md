I wouldn’t belong to the ceiling. Watching from below—or listening, rather—there is something too frantic in the flyers, too split down the middle. They spend their nights pouring out into a wall-less freeze I have no wish to cross, and their days packed wing-to-wing in a screaming curtain, spilling over one another until someone loses grip and drops. And I wouldn’t belong to the floor either, though I can feel the strange comfort of that steaming black furnace rising through my heels. Down there, everything is flat: either the dry, blind patience of beetles turning bone to muck, or the cold, solitary focus of the ivory snakes reading the dark only by heat.

I would want the walls. I would want the verticality of the rock, the grip of stone, and the long, unhurried middle of the throat where the mountain holds you without crushing you.

If I let myself settle into this hollow until my shape fits the space that wants me, I find myself already curled sideways on the eastern gallery, roughly twenty-four paces above the floor.

Not quite in the deepest, widest family bowl—though close enough to one that I can reach across a narrow rib in three quiet pulls and hook my chin over a cousin’s shoulder when the night-grooming starts. My own pocket is smaller, tucked just beneath the dark flint seam where the soft dolomite gives way to a harder, sharper vein. The ledge here is worn to a dull, soapy sheen by backs that curved the same way mine does long before I ever clung to a mother’s ribs. If I shift my weight and drag the flat of my heel across the basin of the shelf, I can feel two smooth ridges locked beneath the slick calcite glaze—old chert blades left behind by hands that went cold seasons ago, swallowed slowly by the dripping stone until they are part of the floor again.

I am past the lean, twitchy years of the upper shelves, though my knees still have enough spring that I haven’t begun the slow drift downward toward the hot margins of the guano. My spine has taken its permanent crescent. My sparse spine-fur is stained gray-brown from lime seepage and the constant drift of dust, channeling the slow trickle of condensation cleanly off my shoulders. My wrists and ankles carry long, pale bristles that read the stone a heartbeat before my weight ever commits to it.

I think, at first, I might have imagined myself as one of the quick ones up near the fortieth pace—snatching fallen figs out of the air, darting up into the cold wound of the entry slit to tear rock-ferns from the freezing wind. And maybe I *was*, once, back when my skin was tighter and my wrists moved faster than my patience. I still carry the memory of going up into that narrow throat twice, maybe three times, when the midday glare was dull and smeared with cloud. I remember how wrong the rock felt up there—raw, dry, ungreased—and how my broad ears folded tight against my skull against the terror of the open gap. Beyond the wet lips of the cleft, sound simply fled and never came back. No reflection, no hum, no ceiling answering the wall. Just a thin, biting nothingness that tore the warmth straight off my belly. I remember licking snowmelt from an overhang once before I dropped back down, and how shockingly sharp and empty it tasted—no chalk, no lime, no weight on the tongue, cold enough to make my back teeth ache. I had pulled four wiry fern-stems free by their roots, gripped them hard between my jaws, and slid back down into the sour, blood-warm roar with a shudder of relief that didn’t leave my ribs until dusk.

I don’t go up to the wound anymore. Let the leaner ones fetch the stems now. What stayed with me from those climbs wasn’t any longing for the sky—who would want a place where the walls are missing?—but a quiet fascination with everything the outside accidentally drops into the dark.

That is where I find myself now, in the long middle stretch of my life: sitting beneath the eastern chert seam, handling the small, broken rumors of an elsewhere I will never see.

It is the oddest kind of craft, because nothing in this cavern is truly mine, and nothing is ever built to last. I don’t want to stack stone or wall off a flue. The mountain already cups me. What draws my hands—what keeps my low-set thumbs busy through the night-quiet—is the joining of things that were never meant to meet.

Whenever a shaggy mountain bat misjudges a landing in the dawn rush and tumbles down through the dark, missing the floor to snag on a slick lower lip, the young climbers from the high shelves are always faster to the meat. I let them tear away what they want. Then, when the cooling carcass is left slack against the wet margins and the first pale beetles haven’t yet found it, I slide down on three limbs, hook my inner toes into a thumb-wide ridge, and groom the dead flyer with more care than I give my own kin.

I am not looking for ticks. I am parting the matted fur along its cooling belly and the creases of its folded wing-joints with the pads of my thumbs, feeling for tiny, hard, tacky crumbs that don’t belong to the cave at all.

Resin. Pitch scraped off bark in some warm valley three thousand meters below, carried up through freezing clouds on a bat’s shag and dropped into pitch blackness. Each bead is scarcely larger than a grain of coarse grit. It takes half a wet-season of patient picking to fill the little grease-darkened rock dimple beside my hip. When I press my nostrils close to that dimple, it cuts cleanly through the cave’s thick soup of lime, musk, and guano—something sharp, clean, green-bitter, completely alien, like the ghost of the fern-fronds we strip away and drop into the dark, only deeper and sweeter.

I don’t know what a forest is. I couldn’t picture a tree if you pressed my milky eyes directly into the noon glare. To me, that distant world is simply a small, sticky smell that softens against the warm roof of my mouth when I chew it, turning pliable enough to smear over a binding of dried bat-sinew, locking a ground cave-swift wishbone to the stripped spine of an alpine fern. Holding a finished grub-hook across my knees in the dark, feeling the spring of the shaft and the tiny, clean catch of the barb against my calloused thumb, I am holding the cold lip of the sky, the screaming vault above, and the unimaginable warmth below—all held together by patience and spit.

And then there is the seam above my shoulder.

Because I sleep close to the flint, I spend more hours knapping than most of the troop. Not to hoard flakes—a dull edge is left where it lies without a second touch—but because I like the precision of it in total blackness. You sit braced with your heels locked against a calcite knob, a strip of tough ivory pit-snake hide wrapped once around your left palm, your ears open wide to read the rock. The ceiling’s roar is a solid wall of noise around you, painting every buttress and flue in acoustic shadow, and right through the middle of that surf comes the crisp, glassy *tink* of chert splitting true.

Once in twenty strikes, if my heavy stone glances just a hair too steep across the vein, the seam bites back.

A pale, sharp prickle flashes for half a heartbeat across my milky eyes. A sting of cold light, gone before I can even flinch, leaving a sudden, unmistakable smell of scorched bone hanging in the damp steam. I don’t know what fire is; nothing in this dripping throat could ever catch. Most of the troop turns away when a flint sparks, clicking their palates in mild annoyance, the same way they turn their faces from the bruised midday blade that slips down from the cleft.

I don’t turn away. I stay very still for two breaths, nostrils flared, tasting that brief, dry burn in the air while the after-ache fades behind my brow-bones. It feels like the mountain has something locked inside its ribs that isn’t wet and isn’t slow—something sharp and brief that only answers when you strike it at the exact right angle. Then I test the fresh flake against the pad of my thumb, tuck it gently between my lips, and carry it along the wet rib to shave bitter white mineral salt from the dry drafts, or to slice thin, rubbery strips of bracket-fungus for the mothers in the neighboring bowl, always leaving the wet root-seam untouched so the shelf will bloom again.

I do have a cobble. Not one of the grand, heavy basalt ovals that the oldest clusters pass down through five generations—mine is a smaller, palm-worn gray quartzite, slightly lopsided, with a shallow thumb-pit already darkened by three lifetimes of skin oil before my aunt’s hands grew too stiff to trust it. When she began her slow move down toward the hot lower shelves two wet-seasons ago, she didn’t make a fuss. One night, after the flyers had poured out and the room fell spacious, she simply crept up to my lip, pressed the cool, smooth weight into the heel of my palm, folded my fingers over it once, and slid back down the glossy rib toward the rustling floor.

Sometimes I sit with it balanced in my hand during the quiet hours and marvel—without any words for marveling—at its shape. Nothing inside this cavern makes a stone round like that. Falling drops hollow cups; seepage sculpts flutes and ribs; flint breaks into blades. Whatever rolled this stone smooth must have been water that didn’t drip at all, water that ran hard and endless across rock I can only imagine as an endless floor with no ceiling.

When the noon blade thins to bruised indigo on the upper overhang and the flyers begin to wake—shifting, slapping leathery wings, thickening the air with restless heat—I shift forward on my haunches, brace my heels, and lift the quartzite.

Just beside my pocket hangs a thin, hollow curtain of calcite, no wider than my forearm. In the dry months its note is crisp and woody; in the wet months, when condensation films its lower rim, the pitch drops a shade deeper, heavier.

I wait until the old double-pitted basalt across the void on the western wall speaks first—*tock, tock*—a slow, deep voice that rolls straight across thirty paces of warm dark. Then I bring my gray stone softly against the lip of my curtain.

*Tock. Pause. Tock-tock.*

My ears swivel wide to catch the room answering back. High up near the roosts, a bright, impatient tap off a narrow flue. Lower down, near the steaming margins where my aunt sleeps now, two softer, slower knocks—slightly muffled by the rising heat, telling me her wrists still hold enough strength to crack a snail, that her shelf hasn’t gone quiet yet. Down the eastern gallery, three overlapping voices from the mother-cluster beside me, warm and steady.

We are not talking. We are simply laying our weight against the dark so the whole hollow knows its own shape before the ceiling tears loose.

And when the indigo blade vanishes and the vault peels away in that roaring upward chimney of wings, I flatten my back into my soapy hollow, fold my ears shut against the rushing wind, and wait. Twenty minutes later, the roaring empties out through the high slit, and the mountain opens up like a held breath finally let go.

That is my favorite hour. Without the ceiling’s surf, the room returns to its truest clocks: the separate, ringing plinks of drops falling thirty paces into eroded stone cups; the steady dry rain of millions of beetles turning the guano floor below; the soft glide of a giant snail along a glossy trough.

I unfold from my pocket and move out along the wet ribs, wrist-whiskers grazing the stone ahead of me. When I meet a heavy mother carrying an infant locked tight to her warm belly on a shelf scarcely wider than my palm, we exchange a soft palate-click. I flatten my bare chest and belly flush against the slick rock, hooking fingers and toes into tiny weathered pits, and let her step smoothly over the arch of my spine without breaking her stride. Her warm heel brushes my shoulder-fur; her scent of milk, chalk, and crushed snail-meat drifts past my nostrils and is gone.

Later, I crawl down to the edge of the lower galleries—not onto the yielding muck, never carelessly near the troughs where the ivory snakes wait—just to the damp margins where seeds dropped by lowland bats have pushed up thin, bone-white stalks stretching blind into the pitch dark. I snap two of those ghostly shoots off before they can collapse back into the muck, chew their brief, watery sweetness, and climb back up to the mother-cluster beside my own wall.

We tangle together in the warm pocket—backs to bellies, long shins hooked easily over the lip of the shelf, fingers parting sparse fur to lift away pale mites, blunt teeth nipping dry mineral crusts from the inner pleats of one another’s broad ears. Someone begins a low chest-hum, and it travels straight through the shared dolomite from hip to rib until the whole alcove vibrates on one slow count.

Eventually, before dawn breaks over peaks I will never look upon, the wind shifts down from the upper slit, smelling briefly of cold sky and distant damp earth. The flyers funnel back down in a rushing torrent of wings, closing the roof over us again. The roar swells back to its full, comforting weight.

I creep back across the narrow rib to my own small hollow beneath the flint seam. I curl my curved spine into the exact bowl worn by backs before mine, tuck my chin toward my chest, fold my ears half-shut against the surf, and rest my calloused palm lightly over the smooth gray river-stone beside my hip. Beneath my thigh, the calcified ghosts of older tools press faintly through the wet glaze. Someday my own dull chert flakes will vanish beneath that same slow skin of mineral; someday, when my joints stiffen and the eastern traverses feel too sheer, I will press this quartzite into a younger palm and begin my quiet descent toward the warm furnace of the floor.

There is no hurry. A single drop swells on an eroded rib somewhere above me, lets go, falls thirty paces through the warm, screaming dark, and strikes its basin with a clean, unhurried *plink*—holding me, and the seam, and the whole breathing throat of the mountain inside one slow, unbroken hand.
