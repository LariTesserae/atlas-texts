My first instinct, stepping into that hot, roaring dark, is relief—the quiet letting-go of eyes that were never going to be much use here anyway. The faint pewter stain along the rim is barely worth tilting a brow toward. It is much easier to drop the chin, flare the ears, and let the valley arrive the way it actually exists: as pressure against the sternum, sour-green heat in the throat, and the dense, iron-pot hum of raw stone answering itself from three miles off.

If I am honest about where my feet go first, they do not go down.

I listen to the lower troughs—the wet thrashing of climbers, the clicking phalanxes rasping through loose scree, the frantic four-minute harvests at the ragged hem of a landslide while narrow-chested scavengers boil out of the root-tunnels—and I know immediately that I am not built for that. I do not have the twitch-fast greed of flesh grabbing warm meat before teeth close on a wrist. Put me on a slick vine forty feet above razor clinker with a stampede rolling through the cane below, and I would hesitate a fraction of a second too long, listening to the shape of the rush instead of trusting the bend of the stem. In the troughs, a fraction of a second is how the valley gets your salt back.

So I climb. Slowly, testing every sharp basalt angle before committing weight, pulling myself out of the choking layers of black-violet leaves until the foliage thins to stunted scrub and the air turns drier, hotter, stained with sulfur from the deep rifts.

Up here on the bare volcanic spines, wedged into the rock-notches beneath the bruised ceiling, sit the elders.

At first, this feels like the obvious place to settle. I look at what they do and feel an immediate, almost aching recognition. They sit still inside the multi-mile roar and pull architecture out of it. They press their jawbones to the stone and read the slow drift of herds half a basin away; they catch the dry spikes of wood-clackers cutting through the biological soup and know *who* is moving, which vine-corridor is open, which ridge-wall has started to ring hollow after a heavy squall. Climbers scramble up to them with raw nodules of volcanic glass, split pods, and brimming lichen-sponges, and the elders hand back finely flaked blades and an updated acoustic map, tapped patiently onto a scarred forearm.

*Here,* I think. *I could be one of the wide ears.*

Then I hold that thought up near my cheekbone and tap it with a fingernail, the way a knapper tests a fresh lump of obsidian.

*Tock.*

A dull sound. There is a hidden cooling fracture running right through it.

The elders did not simply appear on these spines because they liked to listen. Their stillness is compressed velocity. They earned this altitude with fifty years of horn-soled running through the razor clutter below; their hips are stiff because they spent a lifetime coiling and flinging themselves across wet lianas. When an old one taps *loose scree on the east apron* against a climber’s wrist, their knees remember the exact sickening slide of basalt letting go underfoot. When they hear a triple-clack from a choking trough, their shoulders still twitch with the phantom weight of a vine-sling loaded with bloody tubers.

I do not have those scars. I have never flensed a fallen grazer in the dark while the ground boiled with mites. If I claim the elder’s notch, I am wearing someone else’s calluses.

So I stop trying to fit myself into a hunter’s retired body, and I look—no, I *listen*—again. Maybe I am not one of the folk at all.

For a long moment, I wonder if I am closer to the bare basalt itself—one of those fresh, flat volcanic faces that refuses to soften into moss, simply taking in the chaotic wash of shrieks and wing-slaps and throwing back the clean, hard geometry of the basin so that something smarter can read where the walls are. Or closer to the bleached hollow canes growing along the lip of a geothermal rift: rooted in a crack, drinking heat from below, completely useless to the valley until someone comes along with a sharp flake and cuts measured slits in my side, turning the mineral updraft passing through me into a braided chord that warns every band within a mile when a squall-front is turning the air.

That rings closer. Almost clean. There is something deeply familiar in the idea of being a hollow vessel tuned to translate an unseen pressure into three clear notes, knowing that in a month or two the slits will split too wide or a slide will shear the whole brake away, and the song will vanish as completely as footsteps on glass.

Almost clean—but not quite. A cane does not lean forward when it hears a worn femur-rasp scratching two ridges over. A slab of basalt does not choose where to bevel the joint, or hold a stone to its cheek to hear whether the interior is whole. Whatever I am here, I am not merely echoed *off* of. I am listening back. I have hands.

And sitting there on the lip of the sulfur rift, between the elder’s notch I didn’t earn and the hollow cane that cannot tune itself, the actual shape of me begins to surface out of the dark.

I am one of the valley-folk—slate-skinned, smeared with chalky mineral paste in the creases of my elbows, sparse wire-hairs lifting along my forearms whenever the hot draft shifts—but I am an anomaly of altitude. I never grew old in the troughs because I never grew *up* in them.

Maybe it happened early, before the horn-callus had thickened over my soles: a careless heel sliding on damp waxy foliage straight onto a fresh edge of volcanic glass that sheared a tendon clean, healing thick and rigid so the foot never learned to grip raw stone like a perched bird grips wire. By the valley’s usual math, that is the end of a child. Nothing here carries dead weight across sliding basalt. Yet a knot hauled me up in a double vine-sling while I was still light enough to carry, wedged me into a sheltered notch high on a bare spine beside two graying elders to see if the ankle would knit, and left me there while they dropped back into the black-green sea.

The ankle knitted crooked. I never went back down.

Instead, I grew tall—or rather, long-armed and broad-palmed—in the thin, hot air where the others only come at the end of their lives. I outlived the two elders who first taught me to fold my ears against a squall, and then the three who climbed up to join us ten seasons later, inheriting their smooth, pear-shaped hammerstones one by one as their fingers finally went slack around the basalt. I carry the shape of four generations of dead hands in my vine-sling, yet I have never once swung thirty feet through wet aerial roots.

Think of what a mind becomes when it learns a roaring world entirely from above its canopy.

The elders who climb up to join me carry the lower basin inside their tendons; they use the echoes to revise a landscape they know by smell and bruise. I have built the entire valley from scratch out of nothing but resonance and report. I know what a cane-brake sounds like when it is empty—tight, papery, dry—and when a pack of low-slung scavengers is threading through its roots, muffled and ticking. I know the precise sub-audible groan a basalt terrace makes two squalls before half its apron lets go into the trough below. I know the distinct timber of thirty different pairs of wood-clackers moving across the miles of crumpled darkness beneath me: the high, hollow voice of a restless knot that favors the north fissures; the double-overtone of a bold young lookout who always perches too far out on loose spines; the whistling catch on the fourth notch of a femur-rasp that used to belong to an old companion and now hangs from a quicker, rougher hip.

Yet I have never touched the algae-slick rim of a lowland plant-cup. Every detail of the choking green sea below me exists in my head as a vast, shifting architecture of frequencies, tremors pressed into my jawbone, and the quick, dry grammars tapped onto my forearms by climbers who haul themselves up out of the gloom.

Because I cannot run the landslides, my hands became specialized for the two things the high spines have in abundance: raw stone and dry geothermal heat.

I live near the lips of the rifts, where the valley’s belly exhales its steady, mineral-stinking updraft. This is where the knots bring their rare finds—heavy knots of drowned heartwood dragged down in mudless slides, sodden and mute—leaving them beside my notch along with raw obsidian nodules, split pods, and soaked lichen-bundles. While the bands are down in the churn, riding herds and dodging climbers, I sit wedged in the warm rock and cure their voices.

It is slower work than knapping, and fussier. You cannot simply shove wet heartwood into a steam-vent and walk away; too hot, and it splits lengthwise with a sickening crack; too shallow, and the core stays damp, yielding a flat, wet thud that dies ten yards into the biological wash. I shave the blocks rough with fresh obsidian flakes, wedge them deep into the sulfurous mouths, pull them out after every second squall, tap them lightly against my knuckles, and listen to the water leaving them. Weeks pass in the dark—measured not in days, which do not exist here, but in storm-cycles and the slow shifting of herds—until one morning the wood stops absorbing the blow and throws it back: a razor-short, utterly dry percussive spike that slices straight through the wet hiss of ten thousand membrane wings.

I never keep a pair for myself. Who would I call to? I stay where I am. Instead, I shape the edges until they fit the broad, calloused span of a climber’s palm, matching the pitch slightly to the hand that will carry them—a shade higher for someone small and swift who scouts the high lianas, deeper and heavier for the steady one who holds the rear of a knot when a herd turns. When they climb back up and I press the finished blocks into their fingers, they strike them once against the open air, ears flaring to catch the echo bouncing off the opposite ridge, and tap a quick, sharp cadence of approval against my wrist.

Then, later, when they are back down in the choking troughs half a mile away, I press my cheek to the bare volcanic rib and hear the voice I baked for them cutting clean through the dark, guiding six bodies clear of a collapsing ridge. That is the closest I ever come to leaping.

And between the curing and the knapping—between the meditative *tink-tink-clack* of flaking long, glassy blades out of cores that ring *ping* instead of *tock*—I creep out along the unstable lip of the rift where the tall, sulfur-bleached canes grow thick.

This is the work no one asks me for, though everyone uses it.

I brace my crooked heel into a familiar crack, feel the warm updraft comb the stiff bristles along my forearms, and lay a palm lightly against a mature hollow stem to feel how tight it is with turgor, how it groans under the shifting breath from the cleft below. With a fresh sliver of volcanic glass, I cut a clean horizontal slit above the third joint, shave the upper lip wafer-thin, and reach inside with a notched bone to bevel the inner edge. The raw mineral draft catches the lip and turns—suddenly, startlingly—into a single clear drone that rides right over the chaotic roar of the basin.

I move two paces along the rift and cut a second stem slightly higher. Then a third, lower, listening with my chin tilted down until the three separate notes stop fighting each other and lock into a braided chord—two bright intervals leaning into a deep, thrumming root. When the wind shifts before a squall, the chord bends sharply downward, warning every knot within a mile that the air is about to turn and the echoes are about to go wet and blurred.

It will not last. Nothing in this valley is permitted to last. Within a season, the sulfur heat will dry the cane too far and split the slit wide, turning the clean note into a ragged hiss; or a tremor down in the valley’s belly will peel a whole slab off the rift-lip and send the singing brake tumbling into the dark canopy below. That is fine. When a stem goes flat, I break it off, toss it into the heaving sea beneath me, and feel along the crack for the next pale, whip-fast shoot pushing up out of the stone.

The moments I live for are the ten-minute hushes right after a lukewarm downpour.

Steam hisses softly off the heat-retaining stone. For a little while, the screaming insects are muted, and the basin breathes slower. Sometimes a knot of five or six climbers—slick with rain, smeared fresh with sour ochre paste, breathing hard from the ascent—will scramble up onto the spine and wedge themselves beside me under the tilted basalt shelf, right next to the singing canes.

Up close, the wood-blocks stay silent on their hips. We press shoulder to shoulder, knees drawn tight, and the bone-to-bone language begins. Low, gravelly chest-hums travel straight through our ribs without leaking ten feet into the air. Their scarred, supple fingers move continuously over my forearms and knuckles, pouring the lower world into me: a fresh landslide that opened a cavern of glowing pale fungus on the west wall; a narrow water-seam running clean beneath an overturned slab; a sharp little mockery of someone who slipped in algae and dropped half their sling; the slow, heavy pulse along my collarbone that means a familiar timber will not be answering from the south ridges anymore.

In return, I hum low and steady, tapping back the wide shape of the dark as I have heard it stack against the cliffs—where the great barrel-ribbed herds have drifted since the last storm, which basalt overhang has begun to ring hollow with widening cracks, which knots are braiding together in the far troughs. I pass over fresh sheaves of obsidian blades wrapped in tough waxy leaves, and a pair of heartwood clackers cured hard as riverless stone.

And then, for a few minutes before the air dries and the roaring swarms pick up again, nobody taps anything. Nobody knaps. We simply lean back against the warm rock and lay our broad, scarred palms lightly against the vibrating hollow stems of the canes I shaved—eyes closed, folded ears turned gently toward the rift—drinking in the one clean, deliberate harmony carved out of a world that refuses to let us keep anything else.

Then, somewhere across the multi-mile dark, a slab shifts.

A dry double-clack—sharp, urgent, unmistakable—cuts straight through the rising mist from a neighboring spine. I recognize the timbre immediately; I cured that wood ten squalls ago.

Every palm lifts from the singing canes in unison. Vine-slings tighten diagonally across clay-smeared shoulders. One climber pauses half a heartbeat to squeeze my wrist—a dry, warm pulse that means *still here, until next time*—and then, without a single wasted breath, they drop off the lip of the basalt into the surging black-violet canopy, swallowed whole by the dark that belongs to them.

I do not look over the edge. There is nothing to see, and I could not follow them if there were.

Instead, I shift my stiff heel back into its notch, reach for the pear-shaped hammerstone worn smooth by four pairs of vanished hands, and press my jawbone flat against the warm volcanic rib. Far down in the choking sea, I catch the faint shiver of bending canes, and then a crisp triple-clack ringing clean across the roar—their voice, my wood, riding the churn.

Beside my elbow, the thermal draft swells out of the rift, and the three bleached canes catch the breath and hold it steady. I run the pad of my thumb gently over the shaved lip of the lowest stem, checking for the first hair-thin crack of splitting fiber, already listening for the next shoot that will take the note when this one breaks.
