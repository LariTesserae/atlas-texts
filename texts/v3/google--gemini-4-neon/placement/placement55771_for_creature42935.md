I learned early to let the air go.

The air belongs to everything that shouts—the long, wet, dragging bellows rolling off the overhangs, the volleys of sharp throat-clicks pelting down into the streets, the endless, abrasive grit of a million shells scraping mineral slime off the walls. When I was small and still tucked inside my mother’s pelt-coat, the air felt like a wall pressing against my ears from every side at once. You cannot think inside a roar like that. You cannot even hear your own breath warming through the stiff hairs of your nostrils.

Then my mother lifted my bare palm, pressed it flat to a freezing, slick flagstone beneath our knees, and tapped once with her knuckle-stone two paces away.

The roar did not stop. It simply became weather. Beneath it, running cleanly through the unbroken rock like brine through a channel, was a single, crystalline note—sharp, dry, unhurried—that rang straight up my forearm and settled behind my teeth.

That is where I live. Not in the shouting air, and not truly in the warm, braided heaps of the roost-flues, though I go back to them when the cold starts chewing at my ribs. I live in the six inches of stillness right against the wet stone, where the water refuses to freeze and the whole mile-wide bowl speaks in bone and strike.

If you tried to find me at first, you might check the broad causeways down on the basin floor, where the air smells of iron and frost and the retention cisterns heave in their slow, cavernous *glug-hiss*. I go down there sometimes, when the kin need extra slings for shearing. I know how to match the glacial, shaking stride of a sweeper, how to slip beneath the overhang of its rime-stiffened flanks and saw away the outer fleece without breaking its walk. It is honest, calm work. Your fingers go numb inside the frost-crusts, and the giant’s weight rumbles up through your heels like a distant rockslide, and when you tap *clear* into the wet paving behind its receding stride, you feel clean all the way to your lungs.

Still, I never stay long on the floor. Down there, the stone is too crowded with weight—too many heavy steps, too many pulsing cisterns drowning the finer notes in a thick, lung-like throb. And I am not patient enough in the right way to be a wall-herder, though I respect the slow cadence of their bone wedges prying loose the broadest shells along the middle terraces. Whenever I try to harvest grazers, my hand keeps stopping. I leave the wedge wedged under a gritty rim and press my cheekbone straight against the bare, slick film between the shells, listening *behind* the animals to whatever is traveling through the pillar beneath them.

That is what pulls me upward, tier by slick tier, knees bent low, shell-weighted coat-hem brushing the razor-sharp risers so the updrafts cannot lift my pelts.

You will find me high along the unfinished rims, out on the cantilevered spans that jut into nothing without a pillar beneath them.

Out here, the air is thin enough to make my ribs work slowly and deeply, and cleaner than the lower canyons—less ammonia, less musky warmth dripping from crowded overhangs, mostly just the sharp metallic tang of curing stone and the endless hiss of sheeting water. Few flocks roost above a naked span with no lintel to shield them; few grazers have crept this far out along the fresh bevels, because the greasy mineral film has scarcely begun to weep from the newest seams.

Out here, when I kneel on the wet, frictionless rock and lay both bare palms flat beside my knees, the whole amphitheater opens inside my wrists.

Most of our kin use the lattice the way a walker uses a stairwell: to pass news from tier to tier, to check whether a bridge has locked hard enough to take weight, to warn a harvesting pair that a flock is shifting or a gutter is choking. They read the words in the cadence—the spacing, the weight of the strike, the brisk double-knock that means *kin passing*, the long, buzzing trill of sweeper-tooth on basalt that means *seam unstable, keep clear*.

What holds me still in the freezing mist, until the condensation beads thick on my oiled brow and runs down into my collar, is not only what the kin are saying. It is what the stone *does* to their voices on the way up.

A message struck down by the lower cisterns does not arrive on an upper viaduct untouched. Through finished, tension-locked rock, it rings bright and brittle, carrying its edges like a freshly ground shell-blade. Where a span is still curing—soft as chilled tallow along its rising lip—the tap arrives dull, slumped, slightly late, as if someone spoke through a mouthful of wet silt. Where a pillar has swallowed a careless grazer or a fallen scavenger years ago, the note splits around the buried bone and reaches my palm as a faint, ghostly stutter. Every tier, every drainage groove, every hollow flue bends the strike by a hair’s breadth.

Listening long enough, I began to hear the places where the city was going deaf to itself.

A half-extruded corner where two viaducts met at an awkward angle, swallowing every tap from the eastern terraces into a muddy, unreadable thrum. A thickening balustrade where a trapped pocket of unformed mineral paste was curing sluggishly, threatening to mute an entire canyon-street once it finally hardened. A drainage lip where the sheeting water fell straight onto a relay-junction, drumming out a relentless hiss that blurred the finer, quieter knocks coming up from the floor.

So I became what my hands kept reaching to do. We have no grand name for it—just my three-knock signature, two crisp strikes and a slight drag of the tooth-tip, which tells anyone listening along the span that it is me crouching out on the curing edge with a sling of hollow bones across my back.

I carry flat bone spatulas, slick with rendered grease, and bundles of spliced aerialist wing-bones dried hard in the upper drafts, and wedges of dense, notched grazer-shell. When I find a dull pocket along an active lip—where the latest millimeter is still soft enough to take the print of my thumb—I kneel in the wet cold and work slowly, careful never to burn heat I cannot spare.

I cannot argue with the deep geometry rising out of the mountain. Whatever silent rule turns raw fractured rock into razor-edged streets does not care whether a message carries cleanly across a junction. All I can do is set small bridges inside its slowness. I push a hollow wing-bone sideways into the tallow-soft rim, angling it until a test-tap from my knuckle-stone rings clear through the marrow-channel and wakes the finished flagstone on the far side. I pack the seam neatly around the bone with bare, oiled fingers, pinching a tiny spillway above it so the runoff sheets around the joint instead of drumming over it. I press a pair of notched shells into the blind outer edge of the bevel so any hand trailing the wall in the roaring cold will know there is a clean listening-point beneath its palm.

Then I wait. Not all at once—the city takes months to lock a handspan—so I move along my circuit of high rims, returning cycle after cycle to feel my insertions tighten as the mineral paste surrenders to stone.

Every now and then, while my fingers are buried in a soft edge, the skin across my smooth brow prickles all at once—a sudden, dead-cold static crawling through the oil on my cheeks and lifting the stiff hairs inside my nostrils.

My hands stop. My lungs stop.

A half-heartbeat later, the mountain splits beneath us with an immense, crystalline *crack* that jolts straight up through my shins, followed by a long, trembling hum rolling outward across the whole bowl. For two heartbeats, even the roaring flocks overhead choke off their bellows.

In that tiny, breathtaking pocket of quiet, I do not tap out news to my kin right away, the way the others do. I drop my lower jaw straight onto the wet flagstone and listen to the *hum* die out. In the fading tremor of a locking seam, the whole city rings like a single struck rib. I hear the new millimeter settle into place three viaducts over; I hear an old muffled joint suddenly snap bright; I hear a fresh hollow open somewhere deep beneath the retention pools, altering the low pitch of the entire basin by a fraction of a tone. Only when the hum sinks back into the steady, subtle vibration that keeps the water fluid do I lift my staff and tap my three-knock signature into the street, trading readings with the listeners two tiers down, updating the map we hold inside our skulls.

I do not stay out on the rims forever. Nobody could. When the cold climbs past my shell-hem and my fingers begin to fumble the spatulas, I fold my tools into my oiled hide sling and descend into the crowded middle tiers, tracing the familiar notched bevels until the smell hits me—thick, eye-watering ammonia, wet hides, grease, and the heavy, blessed warmth of living flesh.

I slip beneath the weighted curtain of our flue-dwelling, past the low baffle-wall mortared with basalt paste and sweeper-bristle, and for a moment I just stand there, letting the air thaw the crisp crusts inside my nostrils while dozens of half-tame aerialists shift and mutter softly along the ceiling overhead, raining musky warmth down onto my hood.

In the flue, my staff stays quiet against the wall. I shed my wet outer pelt, crawl onto the padded ledge of matted bristle, and wedge myself rib-to-rib into the braided heap of my kin. Someone shifts in their sleep, making a pocket of heat for my knees. Another—awake, breathing slowly—reaches out an oiled hand, finds the smooth curve of my brow, and draws my head gently against theirs.

There is no tapping here. Just bone pressed to bone, forehead to forehead, beneath the snoring ceiling. A low, warm, gravelly purr starts in their throat and vibrates straight into my skull, carrying the small, intimate news of the flue—who tore a coat on a fresh stair, which young one cut their palm on an unweathered lintel, how plump the siphon-lobes felt in the western cistern today. I hum back what I heard on the high spans: which bridge has locked true, where a new wing-bone is taking hold, how the eastern seam sang when it cracked. Our voices never travel an arm’s length into the chamber. They live only between the two of us, warm as blood.

Yet even in the snug dark of the heap, my mind drifts back to the junctions—specifically to the oldest, quietest places on my circuit.

Because the strangest bends in the lattice are not made by pockets of silt or careless grazers. They are made by the elders.

You know them long before your fingers touch their pelts. As you approach a three-way parapet where an elder has sat for three or four years, the stone beneath your heels changes its grain—warming by nothing you could measure with skin, yet growing strangely *dense* in its echo, as though the basalt has grown a core of living marrow. Their knees and shins are seamless with the street now. When they lift a thickened, trembling finger to tap a bone stylus against the rock sheathing their own thighs, the message does not merely travel *along* the viaduct; it blooms straight out of the architecture, deep and resonant, carrying the pulse of the whole amphitheater they can feel through their locked pelvises.

I always keep the softest strips of brine-steeped grazer foot and a fresh bladder of clean blubber-oil in my sling for them. I kneel beside an elder at the rim, press the food gently between their pale lips, and rub thick oil across the smooth sweep of their brow and cheeks so the drifting mist cannot bite into their skin. Then I pull my heavy hood forward over both our heads to break the freezing draft, press my cheekbone to theirs, and let their skull-song shake through mine—vast, slow, patient news of tensions shifting deep in the bedrock hours before any walking listener could feel them.

And then there are the ones further along.

The ones whose elbows the rising stone has already taken. Whose ribs are sheathed in seamless, wet geometry, leaving only the broad curve of their collarbones, their chin, and their oiled brow above the bevel. They cannot hold a stylus anymore. Their chests rise scarcely once a minute, hoarding the last faint coal of heat inside a body that has almost finished becoming street.

Most kin come by to tend them, oil the last exposed skin, tap two affectionate knocks into the wall beside their shoulders, and walk slowly on. They think the elder has gone quiet.

That is where I stay the longest.

I settle my weighted shell-hem heavy against the wet curb so the wind cannot shift me, lean in until my hood drapes over the smooth stone sheathing their chest, and lay my bare jawbone flat against the wet bevel right over their sternum. I hold my breath until the thin air makes my ribs ache.

Past the roaring air. Past the scrubbing shells. Past the steady anti-freeze tremor of the street itself.

There it is—a tiny, almost imperceptible rasp, vibrating straight up through two inches of cured basalt into my teeth. Not a message meant for the canyon anymore. Just the faintest gravelly hum of a throat that still remembers how to purr, moving at the glacial pace of a body nearly asleep in rock. Sometimes it is a fragment of an old map—the cadence of a lower street that was buried under newer tiers before I was born. Sometimes it is a quiet warning about a hair-line strain ten yards down the parapet. Sometimes it is nothing with words at all, only the slow, rhythmic hum of someone letting you know they are still here, still warm enough to listen, not quite finished yet.

I stay until the cold starts biting through my oiled pelts. I stay until my own breath steadies to match theirs. And when I finally lift my face from the wet stone, I take my tapping staff and strike a clean, measured echo of what they gave me straight into the junction—translating a whisper that can no longer travel six inches into a crisp note that will ring a quarter-mile down the terraces, so the basin does not lose the thread.

Once every few cycles, when the crowded chatter of the middle tiers gets too loud under my heels and the warm air of the flues sits too heavy in my lungs, I walk all the way out to the outermost lip of the city.

Past the last finished drainage street. Past the stubs of rising towers. Out to where the sharp geometric bevels soften, lose their discipline, and dissolve back into raw, jagged mountain stone.

I crouch on the last smooth flagstone, feeling the familiar, tireless hum that forbids the water beneath my palms from locking into ice. Two paces beyond my fingertips, the field dies. Out there, the cold mist freezes solid onto the barren talus in thick, gritty crusts of rime, and the silence beneath the howling air is absolute.

I reach forward with my bone staff and tap once against the dead rock outside the rim.

Nothing answers. The strike dies in an inch, swallowed by frost and fracture. No echo comes back up the shaft. No kin, no cistern, no sweeper, no buried ancestor. Just the end of the world, sharp and brittle enough to draw blood if I press bare skin to it.

I pull the staff back and tap twice on the wet, living bevel beneath my own knee.

Instantly, the mile-wide bowl answers through my shins—the distant, cavernous *glug-hiss* of the retention pools, the heavy rumble of a sweeper turning a lower causeway, a scatter of bright, dry knocks from harvesters three tiers down, and—woven cleanly through it all, bending faintly around the smooth swells of kin long since sealed into the walls—the crisp, singing resonance of a wing-bone I set into a curing seam four winters ago.

That is how I know where I will go, when my knees finally stiffen against the slick risers and my lungs begin to labor too hard in the thin air to keep pace with the harvest.

Not to a grand three-way junction in the bustling middle canyons. I already know my spot. It is out on a narrow, cantilevered viaduct high on the northern slope, jutting cleanly out over the pitch, right where a finished span meets a stubborn, muddy seam that has always slurred the calls coming up from the western cisterns. Even the wing-bones I wedged there years ago only half-mended it; the gap is wider than a single marrow-channel can span.

When the time comes, I will carry my thickest cured pelts out there, tuck my shell-hem neatly beneath my calves, and sit squarely across the fault while the newest millimeter is still soft as chilled tallow. I will let the stone climb past my ankles, my knees, my hips, locking my skeleton straight across the dull patch until my own fused bones become the bridge.

For a few years, while my hands still move, I will tap out the health of the northern rim against my own black-sheathed knees, and lean my smooth brow against the hoods of younger listeners who climb out with oil and strips of brine-soaked meat. When the basalt reaches my ribs and my fingers can no longer lift the stylus, I will keep humming as softly as I can for whoever thinks to lay their cheekbone against my chest in the freezing wet.

And ten years after my last breath stops—when nothing remains on that high parapet except a clean, glistening flank with a subtle, smooth-beveled swell about the height of a seated chest—the taps from the western bowl will pass right through my shoulders and ring out across the upper tiers without a single slur.

A half-grown walker, wrapped in a patched hide coat too large for their narrow frame, will pass along that span in the roaring cold. They will trail a bare, thick-padded palm across the smooth swell of my shoulder, tap two quiet, affectionate knocks straight into the stone, and listen as the whole vast, singing city answers cleanly back.
