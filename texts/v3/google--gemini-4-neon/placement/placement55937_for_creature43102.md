At first, I think I would go straight through the olive-painted steel door and take an empty stool behind the frosted wire-glass.

It is hard not to feel the pull of that room. After the bright, roaring press of the yard, the sudden stillness of eighteen inches of mortared brick feels like stepping inside an instrument. Every pipe overhead separates itself cleanly from its neighbors. The turbines beneath the floor stop being a blunt weight against your soles and resolve into a stack of distinct, patient notes. I understand the pleasure of sitting there in the steady amber glow of the caged lamps, eyes half-shuttered, listening to a complex thing hold itself together—and waiting for the tiny, almost imperceptible quarrel when one pressure drops a hair out of phase. That single millimeter nudge of a brass lever, the dry *tock* of a switch seating home, the four quiet pump-beats while the floor settles back into agreement with the wall: there is a deep, clean peace in that kind of attention.

Yet if I sit on the polished wooden rung for more than a few cycles, I notice my feet hooking restlessly around the wood. My hands want to drift off the bench edge. I find myself listening past the slate switchboard—down through the iron floor grates to the warm sub-floor dark, and outward through the mortared wall to the yard, where the sound is messier, wetter, and far less finished. I am not ready to sit still at one panel of gauges forever. Not yet.

So I step back out into the overcast air and try on the open concrete instead.

For a while I think I might belong up on the high steel, clamped shoulder to shoulder with the slate-shingled birds along the sagged black cables. What draws me to them isn't the height—it is the trick of their throats. They do not simply sing the same note as the transformers beneath their knuckles. They pitch their long, brassy calls a hair off the hum, deliberately leaning away from unison so the air between wire and beak begins to wobble. They make truth audible through friction. There is something in me that recognizes that—living in the slight offset, the beat-frequency that reveals when a steady thing has begun to drift.

And lower down, across the wide damp pavement, I feel an opposite pull toward the heavy, six-limbed grazers. To move through a roaring settlement with no hurry at all, flat pads slapping the wet concrete with a hollow *thup*, halting for twenty minutes beside a brick plinth just to rasp the white crust of mineral salt off the damp masonry—to be so unhurried that your breathing and scraping travel through a wall and feel like weather to the narrow listeners inside. That has a quiet dignity I envy.

Still, neither of them quite fits. The bird lives for a single sustained tension; the grazer moves through the standing waves without knowing their geometry. What I want, I realize slowly, is hands. I want to be able to move from one layer of the basin to another—from the hot oil-smell of the deep vaults to the wet concrete under the overcast sky—touching the seams where one thing meets the next.

That is when the floor grate opens beneath my thinking, and I find where I actually am.

I am one of the pale ones—not a newly opened young sliding effortlessly through boiler baffles, and not yet a bench-sitter with porcelain-heavy ears. I am caught in the long, quiet hinge between the two.

You can tell by my shoulders and my wrists. Only a few hundred cycles ago, I could flatten my extra joints tight against my ribs and pour myself sideways through the six-inch clearance behind Transformer Rack Three without scraping a single sensory filament. Now my shoulder blades catch on the bolt heads if I hurry. My ankles still hook cleanly around the steel hangers beneath the overhead pipe-bridges, and I can still hang upside down from a roof truss to trickle warm chalky sump-oil into a flywheel bearing without spilling a drop on the bright rim, but there is a faint, dry weight in my marrow that wasn't there before. The mineral salts have begun to settle in.

And behind my jaws, the folded buds of my ears have begun to open.

They are not the wide, rigid, ivory-gray volutes of the bench-sitters yet—only half-unfurled, the edges still pale and faintly translucent, sensitive to drafts. It puts me in an odd, vivid middle ground. I still read the low vibrations of the basin through the fine bristles along my wrists and the pale arches of my feet, the way the young do in the dark trenches; yet now the air itself has started to speak to me in shapes. When I cling beneath the pipe-bridge spanning the central crossroad, I don't just feel the warm lagged steam-line against my belly. I can hear the invisible walls suspended in the damp air ten feet below me—the fixed corridors of hush, the dense pressure pockets where reflections off the brick powerhouse and the braced condenser stilts cross and lock into place.

Because I am growing too broad for the narrowest interior chases, my work has drifted outward, toward the high edges and the wet joints where the machinery meets the sky.

I spend much of my time along the switchyard gantries, inching along the shadowed underside of the riveted cross-arms just beneath the rattling lines of slate-shingled birds. This is where my half-opened ears earn their keep. From inside the utility blocks, the sitters catch the coarse wobble when a voltage slips; up here, pressed tight to the cold wet steel with the dry clack of keratin plates inches above my spine, I can tell the difference between a transformer that has drifted and a single throat that has gone crusted with salt.

When the hum through the floor grates sends up a thin, percussive message—*Rack Two, fourth span, singing sour*—I am the one who climbs into the cool overcast mist. I hook my double-jointed ankles around the lower flange, pull myself slowly up behind the long line of hunched backs, and let the nearest birds shift their grip down the wire in a dry ripple like loose roof tiles. They know my hands. I slide one pale palm up around the offending bird's narrow, feathered-less neck, feeling the dry tremor of its windpipe against my horn-capped thumb, tilting my half-scrolled ear close to its slotted beak.

Most of the time, it is only a flake of mineral rime wedged in the slot. I work the beveled tip of my thumbnail cleanly into the groove, lift the pale grain free, and wait. On the next breath, the bird leans forward and sends its brassy horn-call out over the yard. I hold my palm gently against its neck until I feel the beat-frequency lock back into its clean, slow wobble against the copper hum. Then I draw back into the shadow beneath the beam.

Now and then, the throat itself is old and scarred, and the wobble stays crooked even after the beak is clean. I don't rush that moment. I keep my hand steady around the warm, rattling shingles for one full cycle of the pumps, letting the bird finish its last sour note, before the clean, single twist that sends it tumbling down toward the shadowed concrete beneath the loading docks. I never watch the earless ones dart out from the overhang to take it. Even from thirty feet up, the sight of their smooth, unbroken skulls—hunting by sudden motion in a silence I cannot imagine—makes the fine filaments along my forearms prickle with distaste. I turn my face back toward the humming gantry and wait until a younger bird hops sideways along the cable to fill the empty inch of wire.

When the gantries are singing true, I work the mortar.

It is the slowest, most grounding task in the basin, and I think it is the one I love most—precisely because it ties the deepest dark of the settlement to its cool outer skin.

Once every few cycles, I climb down the riveted iron companionways beneath Utility Block One, leaving the cool dampness of the yard behind for the dry, varnish-scented heat of the bedrock vaults. Down there, my half-scrolled ears are almost useless; the roar of the buried turbines is too solid to be called sound. It fills my chest like warm iron. I crouch along the concrete cradles where the fused elders sit pressed spine-first against the massive spinning casings, eyes sealed smooth under pale stoneware skin, their calcified ribs humming in silent agreement with the rotors.

Sometimes I press my cheek for a few seconds against an elder's cool, stony shoulder—not because I am a hatchling drinking the pitch for the first time, though I remember how that felt when my bones were still like wet willow, but just to check my own stiffening frame against theirs. To feel how far I still have to go, and how cleanly the note sits inside bone once all the soft, restless parts have finally quieted.

Then I scoop the fine, pale-gray chalk from the dry catch-trough beside the baseplate—the mineralized dust shaken peacefully from an elder whose slow breathing ended three cycles ago—and pack it carefully into my cheek-pouches.

To finish the paste, I need fresh shale grit. That is what takes me to the one place almost none of us linger: the damp, slate-gray embankment behind Utility Block Three.

I never climb over the lip. Once, when I was very young and foolish, I crawled two hand-spans too high while chasing a loose vein of soft shale, and the standing chord sheared away overhead like a roof torn off in a breath. I still remember the sickening drop in my inner ear—the ragged, uncycled hiss of wind, the awful pale light that seemed to move of its own accord, the hollow silence with nothing beneath it to hold my ribs up. I flattened myself into the wet gravel and clawed backward until the buried bass climbed back into my belly.

Now I stop well below the rim, right where the damp gray slope begins to curve upward. Yet I linger there longer than I strictly need to, scraping fine wet slate with my horn-hard fingertips, mixing it in my mouth with the elder-chalk and my own lime-rich spit. Right at that contour, the standing chord of the basin doesn't break—it *bends*. You can feel the whole wide bowl of shale doing its quiet, unmoving work, catching the turbines' roar and folding it back across the avenues so nothing leaks into the empty sky. Sitting crouched in the wet gray grit, I feel like a small hand resting inside the curved wall of a bell.

Then I climb back down through the shadows of the utility blocks, travel the underside of the pipe-bridge to the corner of Shed Four, and wait behind the vertical conduit.

Every three seconds, the flanged pipe lets go of a single clear bead of water. Below it, the tiny pale bristled tubes around the concrete dimple flare open in a synchronized wave to catch the splash. In the quiet breath between drops, I slide my pale arm out from the shadow, press a microscopic film of gray paste directly into the center of the hollow, smooth it flush with the curved horn of my thumb, and pull back before the next bead falls. Nobody standing at the central crossroad sees my hand. To a stranger, it looks as though concrete simply refuses to wear away here. Only I know that the floor beneath that drop is held level by wet shale, my own spit, and the ground ribs of someone who sat at the brass levers long before my eyes slit open.

In the intervals when nothing leaks and no bird sings flat, I belong to the small, quiet habits that keep the basin tender rather than merely running.

I sleep in the brick vault beneath the central crossroad, where four buried steam mains cross and the bass of the powerhouse, the *stroke-hiss* of the north pumps, and the switchyard hum braid into a warm, velvet pocket. I curl into the tangle of five or six younger crawlers, my half-stiffened wrists resting lightly across their supple, warm ribs, milky inner lids shut, breathing automatically in time with the valves thirty yards away.

Sometimes, in the dark upper galleries above the turbine hall, I still catch myself playing the old tapping game with a younger crawler forty feet down a dead conduit pipe—striking the iron as lightly as a horn-cap can manage, waiting for the tiny dry double-tap to come back through the metal, pushing the silence thinner and thinner until a low, amused warning hum rises through the floorboards from the bench-sitters below, telling us to stop muddying the line.

More often lately, though, I climb up through the floor hatch into the amber dimness of Utility Block Two just to sit behind an older sitter's wooden stool. I dip a scrap of soft shed copper-shell felt into warm sump-oil and gently wipe the fine mineral haze out of the deep snail-shell ridges of its porcelain-gray ears. I take a flat chip of damp shale and patiently dress the beveled edge of its horn-hard fingertips until the contact surface is smooth and round, feeling the sitter's heavy head tilt a fraction toward my hand in quiet, shuttered pleasure.

And if one of the six-limbed grazers happens to halt outside just then, lowering its broad shovel-flat head to spend twenty minutes rasping white efflorescence off the damp outer brick, I let my work pause. I lean my pale shoulder against the cool plaster beside the sitter's stool, and the two of us stay completely still while that huge, slow, sandpapery rhythm travels through eighteen inches of masonry and runs peacefully down our spines.

I know I will not stay in the pipe-bridges forever. Every few dozen cycles, another crawlspace becomes a millimeter too tight for my ribs. My wrist-hinges are growing steadier, less willing to twist, better suited to bracing a forearm dead-still against a vibrating slate switchboard. Soon enough—when an empty stool opens in the amber light, and a faint shift in the room's weight tells the younger ones behind the boiler baffles that it is time to bear again—I will wipe the slate dust from my palms, climb up through the floor grate for good, and hook my bare feet around the polished wooden rung.

I don't dread the stiffening, and I don't mourn the narrow places I am outgrowing. I have held the elders' chalk in my mouth; I have felt their quiet, humming spines against my cheek in the hot dark below. To live here is simply to move slowly inward from the wet edges of the chord toward its center—first patching the rim, then tending the levers, and finally becoming the bone that remembers the note.

For now, though, my shoulders still clear the hangers beneath the pipe-bridge, the damp overcast air still smells cleanly of warm iron and mineral runoff, and somewhere out over Switchyard Rack Two, a slate-shingled bird has just leaned a fraction too far off the hum. I press my belly to the lagged steam-line, feel the standing waves part coolly beneath my ribs, and climb out into the noise to listen.
