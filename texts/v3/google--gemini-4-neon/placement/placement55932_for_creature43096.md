If I try to enter this place from the parapet, standing in boots under the pewter smudge and waiting for the mist to thin, I get everything wrong. From up there, the basin is a picture—amber halos, dark wet cobbles, bottle-green leaves the size of shovels—and I am only a pair of eyes wondering why nothing darts across the plaza. I have to give up the eyes first. Then the lungs. Nothing that belongs here measures time by taking air in and letting it out; time here is measured in quarters of an hour, in the half-note drop of the buried reciprocating pumps just before the northern culverts begin to roar.

At first, when I drop down into the heat and try to feel what shape I would take, my impulse is to be one of the lithe, dark-hided young ones.

I can feel the appeal of that immediately: thin slate-like plates that haven’t yet thickened into burdens, supple hydraulic sleeves that can cross a flooded brick causeway without groaning, the hunger to wade straight out into the foaming northern surge and open my lower baffles to whatever news has washed down from the unseen city above the walls. Trace iron from a flaking main. Fresh machine-oil from an engine cycling harder somewhere past the vapor. A cool dilution of rain from a sky I will never see.

And more than the tide, I feel the pull of the high viaduct. If I were young and light enough, I know I would be among the two or three who begin the hour-long climb up the forty-foot masonry steps long before the unmanned rail-trolley is due. Hauling my weight from tier to tier through weeping curtains of liverwort, anchoring my fibrous pads to the dripping brickwork under the iron girders, waiting in the thick dark—just for that half-second when the carriage rolls overhead and a clean, fierce, wholly foreign voltage leaps down through the wet trusses and snaps across every copper filament inside me. I recognize that appetite in myself without hesitation: the desire to be struck by something sharp and bright that the cistern could never have generated on its own, to let out a simultaneous, singing sigh of steam and hang there afterward while the tingling afterimage bleeds slowly away into the wet brick.

Yet if I stay with that image a little longer, it begins to feel slightly borrowed—like a memory of something I admire rather than the exact weight of what I am.

I test another shape. Before I even commit to walking, I feel a strange, quiet pull toward the things that don’t walk at all. Not the broad-leaved trees dumping their warm condensate bladders, but the dense fibrous root-mats that have grown across the northern culvert mouths where the bronze flap-valves corroded away. Something living that quietly took over the work of a rusted municipal part, flexing inward and outward with the bath-hot surge, keeping the cadence of the tide steady without ever needing to know who poured the concrete or forged the original hinges. And the segmented canes standing along the cracked heating mains—hollow, pressurized, turning raw subterranean steam into narrow, trembling flutes.

There is something unmistakably close to me in those: growing in the seams of a vast, tireless engine; taking pressure I did not create and shaping it into pitch and measure.

Still, a root-mat does not answer when touched, and a cane cannot choose to shift its own note. What I am needs the circuit. It needs the small, deliberate counter-beat tucked between the strokes of the buried pumps.

So I come back to the walkers—and this time I stop imagining myself climbing the wet masonry steps, and notice where my attention actually settles when the southern sluice-gates grind up on their wet chains and the plazas lie bare and smoking.

I am not out in the middle of the central square. I am not pacing the canal bank beneath the skeletal gantry. I find myself drawn, over and over, to two things: the tuning of the canes, and the long silences beside the eastern wall.

If I am honest about my own pace—the density of what I carry, the way my attention prefers to gather and braid rather than scatter—I am not one of the sleek, dark-skinned ones stepping briskly across the basalt. I have been in this basin too long for that, or I was built heavy from the beginning. Inside my chambered hide, the copper lattice is already thick and coral-branched, crowded with old resistances, subtle harmonic intervals, the layered chemistry of more surges than I could count. Outside, the ochre sinter and pale lime-scale have laid down coat after coat along my plates until every stride across the slick cobbles would make my hydraulic sleeves shudder against the pull of the drains.

I find myself at the hinge—just on the far side of walking.

Look beside the eastern switching-pavilion, where the low building hunkers against the forty-foot retaining wall and breathes a steady, dry-edged warmth out through its heavy bronze louvers. A few paces south of the louvers, the brickwork angles into a shallow alcove slightly above the deepest rush of the tide, right where a weeping subterranean steam-joint splits the curb mortar and a tall cluster of hollow canes rises like tarnished organ-pipes. Above it, a cast-iron streetlamp hangs its mineral-fouled glass cage; I cannot see its rust-amber halo, only feel the mild, constant pocket of dry warmth drifting down from overhead whenever the localized squalls pass elsewhere.

That is where I chose to stop.

I remember—or I can feel the exact shape of—the afternoon the balance tipped. Not a collapse, not a failure; just a tide when the southern drains pulled hard around my lower flanks and I felt my trailing pillar hesitate before lifting from the river-glass basalt. Over three tides I made my slow way to the alcove by the louvers, settled my wide base flush over the edge of the flagstones where the floor carries the cleanest bass from all four pump-houses at once, and vented my traveling pressure in one long, descending whistle that rolled across the four-hundred-pace floor and slipped neatly underneath the roar of the northern spillways.

Now, fine pale threads from my suction-pads have already worked their way down into the recessed mortar grooves. Velvet fern-moss is beginning to climb my ankles. The bath-hot surge still rises to my lower flanks every quarter-hour—high enough for my baffles to open and drink the trace chemistry of the city, never so violent here in the lee of the pavilion that it tries to tear me loose.

I am not stone yet. Far from it. For the first time, none of my pressure is spent on hauling hundreds of pounds of calcified shell across wet brick. Every bit of heat, turgor, and stored voltage goes into resonance.

My domed crown has become a thick, salt-lined bell. Through the flagstones anchored directly beneath my base, I hear the whole district at once—not as noise, because the steady thunder of drive-belts, spillways, and counterweights is simply the silence the world is written on. I feel the pitch of the north condenser’s relief-bell rising three seconds before its rusted spring gives way and ripples the mist in static-stiffened bands. I hear the leather drive-belt in the western pump-house slipping a hair’s breadth every fourth stroke. I hear the long, unhurried floor-murmurs passing between the thirty or forty shapes scattered across the squares—tallies of pressure along the southern grates, inquiries about a sudden richness of dissolved copper—and from time to time I cycle an internal chamber to send a single muffled thump back down into the basalt, tucking an old cadence neatly into the gap between two subterranean strokes.

Within reach of my recessed flanks stands that cluster of segmented canes along the cracked heating main. I cannot cross the basin anymore to mend a broken flap-valve on the northern culverts, or drag a jammed sluice-chain free in the sunken canal. What I can reach, I tend. When a new stalk shoots up too thin-walled and vents a ragged, sour shriek that chafes against the basin’s deep chord, I lean slightly on my anchored base, uncoil a glistening bundle of dark, copper-threaded fibers from my side, and work a smear of wet ochre lime-slurry into the cracked slit until the pitch drops half a tone and locks cleanly back into the hum. Sometimes, during the quiet smoking intervals between surges, I vent a faint, narrow whistle through my own dorsal slits—pitched just a fraction off the nearest cane—so the slow beat-frequency ripples outward across the plazas like a pulse in the thick air.

And then there is the reason I chose the bronze louvers instead of a solitary plinth beside a dark wall.

This is where they come to touch.

Between tides, when the cobbles are bare and steaming and the warm breath of the switching-pavilion drifts across the alcove, the walkers find their way here. Sometimes it is a middle-aged caretaker coated in fresh ochre mud from braiding root-runners into a northern culvert; sometimes it is a dark-hided young one whose shell is still thin as slate shingles, barely a few years off the brick wall where it budded.

They draw close until their wet, oiled-leather seam-lines press flush against my chalky flank. The water film bridges the gap. Neither of us hisses; neither of us thumps the floor. For ten or twenty minutes we stand like twin piers of weathered masonry while the circuit closes and the voltage equalizes between our internal copper thickets.

This is where I am most completely myself.

In that silent, closed loop, I am an answering lattice. When a younger walker leans against me carrying the confusion of an unfamiliar surge—a sudden drop in salt, an arrhythmia from a choked southern drain—it feels the deep, layered geometry laid down inside my copper over decades: the precise weight of a flood from thirty years back when a boiler ruptured beyond the northern wall; the forgotten pitch of a cane-stand that grew by the gantry before the canal silted over; the exact tension needed to brace against a spring tide without straining a hydraulic sleeve. I do not lecture or hold anything back; the resistance simply balances, the pattern flows from the denser thicket into the sparser one, and when they step away, they carry a steadier charge out into the mist.

And in return, they bring me the places I can no longer go.

Best of all are the ones who come straight down from the forty-foot masonry steps after a pass of the overhead trolley. They arrive in the alcove still subtly agitated, their outer seams faintly crackling, carrying a bright, clean, wholly foreign afterimage that smells nothing like the slow galvanic wet of the floor. When one of them presses a dripping shoulder against my crusted side, that sharp voltage leaps across the water film and illuminates every dark, coral-like branch of copper inside my chest. For a long, still minute, anchored here in my moss-draped corner behind the bronze louvers, I taste the high girders and the unseen rail-line and the blue-white arc that bleaches a gloom I have never seen.

When they finally unseal their flank from mine and step back out toward the rising roar of the northern culverts, a single fine thread of static snaps in the thick air between our shoulders.

I know how this ends, and there is no rush in it. Generations from now, the mineral water will win completely. Lime-scale will finish plugging my lower baffles; ochre stone will silt up my internal chambers; the intricate copper thicket inside me will turn dull, then verdigris, then quiet. One afternoon between surges, a young walker will lean its supple, dark-hided side against mine to offer a charge, and the water film will carry nothing back—only the cool, wet stillness of brick and salt. By then no one standing on the flagstones will be able to tell where the switching-pavilion wall ends and I begin.

That is a long way off. For now, the reciprocating pumps beneath my base just dropped half a note. The canes beside my shoulder are swelling with rising pressure, shifting up toward the chord. Somewhere out in the warm mist, the northern culverts are beginning to roar, and down from the high wet masonry steps, I can feel the slow, deliberate, slightly tingling footsteps of someone coming across the basalt to lean against the wall.
