At first I try to stand where the narrator stands—cold to the wrists, bracing a gloved hand against a buzzing ceramic balustrade, watching the double-edged shadows peel off the stairs.

It doesn’t hold. The posture feels borrowed. If I look down at my sleeve in this clear, mineral noon, there is no curl of outside silt drifting off me. No warm, messy convection plume rising off my skin to blur the braided currents. If I move an arm through the water, nothing rolls or eddies out of my way. I do not breathe, and the absence of air here does not tighten my chest.

So I am not the visitor who crawled down through the mud.

For a few minutes, lingering near the upper arcades where the light is cleanest, I think I might be one of the daylight panes.

It is a tempting fit. An unmounted sheet hanging at an odd angle between two tenements, with no frame, no cable, and no thickness at all when viewed edge-on. Something that does not burn with its own private sun, does not own the brightness it pours into the alleys—it simply *admits* light from somewhere vast and overcast and far away, casting sharp caustics onto pale masonry, while behind it there is nothing at all except ordinary water and the next wall. You could pass straight around the back of me and find no engine, no hearth, no interior room. Only the aperture itself, holding open a patch of borrowed noon.

I sit with that image until the deep chime sounds twice beneath the lowest foundations, and the pocket flushes.

When the scouring storm hits, a daylight pane does nothing. It does not sing. It does not brace itself or open into the blast. It has no stake in the twenty seconds of whiteout, and afterward it has not been sharpened or fed.

That is how I know I am not a pane. Because when the currents reverse and the roaring sheet of pressurized water tears diagonally through the streets, I *do* answer it. I open into it. I know, deep in whatever passes for my ribs, the terrible, clean relief of being scrubbed straight back to the first hour of existence.

Try again, then. Let the eyes adjust to the double-vision shimmer along the walls until the empty machine disappears and the crowded city snaps into focus.

Here I am—or closer to it. Hinged planes of clear Iceland spar, braced along the sharpest seams by hairline ribs of dark alloy. No face, no lungs, no soft hinge of meat. When I look at anything in this pocket—an etched plaque, an approaching current, the jagged underside of the rock ceiling—I do not take it in through an eye. I let the light pass straight through my own clear geometry and measure how it bends. Every line splits into two slightly offset versions of itself as it crosses my chest: the thing as it stands, and the thing shifted by a fingernail’s width, held side by side so I can read the distance between them.

I recognize this way of speaking, too. There is no air here to push across vocal cords, no warm breath to shape into vowels. When I have something to say, two parallel plates flex apart by a hair’s breadth and snap shut faster than the water can flee. A microscopic pocket of nothingness tears open, collapses in an instant, and throws off a single blue-white pinprick of light and a dry acoustic tap that travels straight into the bones of whoever is listening. Every utterance is a tiny, calibrated collapse of pressure—sharp, brief, vanishing almost before the retina can fix on it, leaving behind only the vibration in the stone and the faint metallic sting of ozone.

And I recognize the flush.

Four minutes of bustling, intricate alignment—stepping wake-less across catwalks, angling facets toward recessed plaques, catching the rectangular ribbons of the street-currents to shoot cleanly up a facade—and then the twin chime beneath the floor. Every edge locks into the sintered masonry. Every pleat blooms outward into crystalline lace. The cold, mineral-heavy surge hammers straight through my open lattice, ringing me like struck glass, stripping away every speck of dust that tried to gather in my folds, wiping every hairline scratch back to mirror clarity. Twenty seconds later, the bubbles vanish, the pleats snap shut with a cascading rattle, and the world is brand new again. No rust. No lime crust. No slow accumulation of tired evenings.

If I stay in the middle terraces, moving with the bright, tireless traffic of the arcades, I almost believe I am simply one of the citizens. A mid-sized resident, moderately pleated, neither a darting fingernail-sliver nor one of the massive elders whose lower plates have fused permanently into the buttresses.

Almost.

Watch where I go when the flush cuts off and the balconies fill up again.

The other residents step briskly back into their civic geometry. They ride the braided thoroughfares, adjust the louvers, tilt their uppermost triangles toward the floating noon-panes, and keep their angles immaculate. When a stray grain of flint drifts off the eastern cliff, they lift it with needle-fine precision and drop it straight into an exhaust ribbon. They live inside a bright, ringing horror of the blur—of anything that leaks, softens, wanders out of alignment, or dies into silt.

I do not feel that horror.

That is the first crack in the fit. As I stand on a narrow balustrade where gravity pulls sideways toward the sheared slate, listening to a heated argument in the arcade below—a swarm of blue-white cavitation sparks popping like dry ice against the stone—I realize I am not looking inward toward the great upside-down chimes of the central plaza. I keep angling my uppermost plate downward, toward the shadowed joints at the very base of the lowest terraces. Toward the places where the pale ceramic fails to quite seal over the raw black basalt.

Toward the edges where the city touches what it cannot fully clean.

---

Down near the fractured floor, tucked tight behind a louvered vent that breathes a slow, chalk-scented trickle of warmer water into the chill, the light from the overhead panes arrives only at steep, broken angles. Here the constant acoustic roar of the city pools and sours—too many hard ceramic walls facing one another at tight distances, throwing harsh, metallic harmonics back and forth until a crystal citizen’s ribs ache just standing near the corner.

And here, clinging directly to the mineral scars of the black rock, grow the fingernail-sized rosettes of translucent, silver-green moss and the tiny tufts of wire-thin aquatic grass.

They are trembling so fast in the perpetual sound that their outlines never quite settle. They are the only soft things allowed to stay inside the midday light, kept like prized strings on an instrument because their living, supple fibers drink up the ugly echoes that rigid spar and alloy cannot damp on their own.

This is where I find myself. Not on the high sunlit terraces, and not fused among the vast elders who groan and slide housing blocks across the alleys. I am down in the lower seams, folded compactly against the raw black rock—no taller than a forearm, built of fewer, thinner plates than the bustling clerks above, yet too intricately hinged, too deliberately weighted, to be one of the newly cleaved slivers darting around the window cornices.

I am a seam-tender. A tuner of the places where stone meets green.

Wait—look closer at the geometry of my chest, the way the noon light bends when it hits my uppermost triangle.

In an ordinary citizen, every plate of spar is flawless, kiln-clear, scrubbed back to perfect optical cleanliness every four minutes. Look straight through them and the lintel behind them doubles cleanly, razor-sharp on both edges.

In me, there is an inclusion.

It isn’t on the surface—if it were, the scouring tide would have stripped it off in the first twenty seconds of my life, or the braided currents would have quietly boxed me in and routed me back through the basalt slot. It is sealed *inside* the mineral itself, locked between two microscopic laminae of calcite that closed over it when the cleft first split and the dark shelf water flooded in before the panes were pinned open.

A single, tiny fluid inclusion—no wider than the point of a needle. Inside that sealed crystal pocket floats an infinitesimal droplet of the outside sea: dark, brackish, faintly silted, impossibly old.

Nothing can flush it out. My exterior planes stay mirror-bright, sharp enough to slice a glove, wholly obedient to the civic hygiene of the pocket. When I step across a shear boundary, my lower edges turn phase-perforated and leave the water utterly unbruised. By every public measure, I am in alignment. Yet whenever a beam from a daylight pane passes through that one upper facet, the doubled image it casts is not quite crisp. Along one hairline margin, the edge softens by half a fraction of a degree—just enough to hold a faint, forbidden warmth.

Because of that buried drop of blur, I understand the moss differently than the other tenders do.

They care for the tufts with exquisite civic manners: plucking stray chips of flint from the rosettes, bouncing disciplined slivers of noon down into the shadowed vents, matching their thin ribs to the plant’s vibration to check the tuning of a stairwell. They treat the moss as a useful, polite anomaly—a biological tool that has the decency to stay rooted in neat geometric circles.

I stay beside it after the tuning is finished. I lean one thin dark-alloy rib close enough to the wire-thin grass to let its rapid, living tremble run straight up through my hinges, softening the hard arithmetic of my own frame. I listen to the way a living blade refuses to ring in a single clean octave—the way its hum carries microscopic irregularities, little hesitations and overtones that never repeat themselves the exact same way twice. Every time the four-minute flush roars through and strips my outside surfaces clean, I lock my lower pleats into the basalt beside the moss, unfold my wings into the cathedral of struck glass, and feel that tiny sealed droplet inside my chest shiver against its crystal walls—the one piece of me that does not go back to hour one.

---

And I do not stay only at the vents.

Between flushes, when the tall, heavily pleated residents are busy reconfiguring the third-story terraces and the elders are shifting the street-currents, I climb along the raw, jagged underside of the bedrock ceiling toward the western wall—out to the very edge of the bright pocket, where the city ends in a fresh, splintered tear no wider than a human shoulder.

Most citizens avoid the basalt slot once the vanguard has sealed its light and mapped its currents. Beyond that sharp lip is everything the city was built to flee: flat dark silt, cold brackish drift, blind tides, soft bodies leaving messy wakes in water that has no straight edges. To stare out through the slot is to stare into the slow rounding-off of all corners.

Yet the acoustics near the lip are extraordinary.

If I anchor myself sideways onto the razor-sharp basalt just inside the entrance—where the clear noon-water meets the murk without quite mixing, held apart by a thin, hissing sheet of braided current—I can catch vibrations that never reach the inner plazas. Dull, muffled booms from far up on the drowned shelf. The low, rolling sigh of a tide that does not obey a four-minute schedule. Once in a long while, the slow, clumsy turbulence of something warm and fleshy passing through the mud outside, unaware that twenty paces below its belly, a packed cathedral of glass and ceramic is ringing in perpetual midday.

I cannot go out there. If I slipped through the slot into the brackish dark, my phase-porous step would snag on drifting weeds; silt would cake in my pleated bellows; the darkness would collapse my angles until I could no longer tell a doorway from a wall, or myself from the mud. I am made of this bright, loud, sharp-cornered cleft. I need the noon panes, the crisp currents, the clean scouring rush that keeps my facets keen.

Still, I keep my post at the threshold. I tend the last, stubborn rosette of silver-green moss clinging to the mineral scar just two handspans from the dark. I angle my flawed facet to throw a thin, slightly softened ribbon of daylight across the basalt lip—not far, just enough to catch the splintered conchoidal edges where the two worlds touch.

And on the rare, startling hour when a stranger slips through the slot—bulky, damp, radiating chaotic plumes of warmth, trailing faint clouds of coastal mud from a torn cuff—I watch the city react around them. I hear the tall residents on the catwalk snap their cool, polite verdicts of misalignment. I watch the louvered shutters click open and the flat sheets of braided current slice neatly across the alley to wall the intruder off from the clean masonry, quarantining their softness, escorting them inexorably back toward the dark shelf where they belong.

Before the current closes completely around their shoulders, I step off the raw basalt, turn hazy for a heartbeat to slip straight through the tearing shear ribbon, and settle—weightless, razor-edged, humming at the dampened pitch of the moss—onto the knuckle of their glove.

Not to judge their blur. Not to file a measurement for the civic elders.

Only because I have spent my whole sharp, bright, perpetually scoured existence listening to a single trapped droplet of their world shiver inside my chest—and for one brief instant, before the plumbing pushes them back into the dark and the twin chime calls me home to be polished clean again, I want to set my clear triangles against something warm, yielding, and unrepeatable, and answer it with one tiny spark of blue-white light.
