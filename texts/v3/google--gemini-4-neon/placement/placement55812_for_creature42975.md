I would find myself pressed flat against the warm, dry underside of the fourth tier, just inside the glowing lip, with my spine aligned parallel to the three-millimeter seam where the deck holds onto the raw rock without touching it.

It is the quietest place to hold still between breaths. From there, the room is a seventy-pace bell hung upright in the bedrock, and I am part of its ceiling. The matte alloy against my outer back is dry and faintly warm; the tiny gap beside my spine carries a steady, thread-thin hum—the sound of great weight suspended in nothing. Below me, the baseline rain falls in clean, vertical curtains, hitting the mid-air shear halfway down with a permanent horizontal band of white roar that divides the chamber into an upper stillness and a lower percussion. Down on the flooded floor, the drips off the hanging ferns strike in steady, punctual sparks, each ring crossing the water to the newly sheared walls and returning crisp, unfrayed, almost untouched by time.

I have no eyes, so there is no dusk here. There is only how sharply a surface answers.

Fresh basalt answers like cold struck glass. The cantilevered walkways answer flat and taut, with a slight metallic tension at their ninety-degree edges. The curtains of falling water answer with a sliding, downward hiss, pulling a razor-thin draft along their skins. And along the tops of the decks, creeping millimeter by millimeter toward the sharp outer rims, the moss answers with a low, velvety swallow—the faintest softening of a line that used to be clean.

Half a second before the barometric pressure drops, I feel it—not in the air yet, but through the bedrock behind the three-millimeter seam. Deep beneath the shallow floor-water, the great thermal slabs shift their load. A slow warmth begins to wake under the basin. My quartz tines, tucked beneath my forward cowl, tremble with the first hairline change in the room's tension.

I do not wait for the floor to steam. I loose a single high, dry *ping*—a small check against the opposite wall—and wait the fraction of a beat for a slightly lower answer from the underside of the third tier across the hollow. Then I lift my bevel from the warm deck, slide smoothly outward to the sharp lip, and slip off into the seventy-pace drop just as the warm vapor lifts off the basin below.

Opening my vanes is not like stretching wings. It is like unfolding a map into the wind that made it.

My dry outer slate parts, and my fluted inner surfaces catch the rising thermal—warm, bathhouse-heavy, rich with lifted fern-spores, fine dissolved silica, and the clean salt of freshly broken stone. Across two meters of pale, finely grooved membrane, I taste the whole chamber's breath at once. There is no effort in the climb. A tilt of one hinged edge by the width of a hair turns me into a slow spiral; the rising column carries me up past the fifth tier, the sixth, the narrow recessed landings near the ceiling where the newest of us still cling like thin mineral glazes beside the humming rock-seams, drinking the draft until their first microscopic tines grow long enough to hear the full height of the room.

Then the warm plume strikes the cold, freshly sheared ceiling vault, and for thirty seconds the cathedral goes blindingly bright.

Water tears loose off the upper ribs in wide, crashing sheets. The gutters along the seven tiers brim over their razor edges and spill downward in cascading steps. Every surface in the room is struck at once, and to my trembling tines the entire seventy-pace volume leaps into razor-sharp relief—every fracture in the high basalt, every junction landing, every trembling curtain of vines painted in overlapping percussion. I flex the articulated plates along my spine and send a clear note across the storm. Three paces higher and slightly west, another dweller answers a fifth above; a third slips into alignment beneath me, and for four heartbeats we hold a shimmering vertical chord that hangs audible even through the drumming on the thin decks—not a song, just the sound of three planes agreeing on the exact dimensions of the air.

Below us, half-grown flyers with thin, mica-light outer plates are throwing themselves straight down beside the falling curtains of water, riding the taut downward draft until the mid-air shear catches them and snaps them sideways in an ecstatic scatter of tiny, glass-clear pings. I love listening to them. Their calls sound like fine mineral grit tossed across taut stone.

Then the deep thrum beneath the floor eases. The thermal slabs settle back. The torrent thins to its steady, heavy baseline.

Most of the others spiral smoothly back to the sheltered undersides of the tiers, or descend straight to the center of the mirror-black floor-water to balance upright on their keels and trade long, intricate grids of standing waves through the submerged slabs.

I go to the margins.

I bank inward toward the third tier, fold narrow as I slip beneath the overhanging wet fronds of the shade ferns, and let the smooth water-shedding edges of my lower vanes kiss the wet stone film of a recessed service alley. Sliding upright on my keel, I glide back into the narrow geometric shadows behind the plants, where the main rain does not reach and the pale, sharp-grained silt gathers cleanly in the dry corners.

This is who I am in this room: a tuner of small places.

All of us mend echoes when they grow troublesome, but I spend more time in the alleys and junction landings than most, listening to the subtle complications of the perimeter. Out in the open hollow, the acoustics are vast and heroic; everything rings cleanly if the stone is young. Back here, where wall-channels cross behind the rock and hingeless sluice-apertures sit flush in the basalt, sound catches. A newly sheared corner can throw a tight, stinging flutter back across a walkway. Worse, a shallow groove where runoff slows can collect a smear of fine silt that turns a crisp return dull and woolly.

I pause near a recessed junction where I heard a faint, distracting chatter during the last deluge. I tilt my cowl toward the dry corner behind a broad-leaved fern and send out a tiny, private *ping*—barely louder than a drop falling from a leaf-tip.

There it is. A muddy double-return, half a beat out of phase, coming off a stepped notch in the freshly cut wall-channel.

I glide back along the gutter, grazing the sharp-grained silt with the sensitive margin of my inner keel until I find what I need: three flat slivers of freshly cleaved quartz and a thin, triangular chip of basalt, still angular enough to sing. I tuck them neatly into the dry crease of my innermost folded vane, carry them back behind the sheltering ferns, and set to work.

It takes patience—three or four of the chamber's five-minute breaths. With the fine edge of my lower vane, I prop the first quartz sliver against the basalt notch at a slight angle, lean the second beside it to catch the cross-return, wedge the third at the base so the vibration of the next thermal surge will not shake them loose. Between each adjustment, I send out another whisper-thin *ping* and listen. Too steep, and the corner throws a bright, harsh spike back into the alley. Too shallow, and the muddiness remains.

When the angle finally comes true, I feel it before I even finish the breath. The dead pocket snaps open. The return comes back clean, dry, and exact, as though the corner had just been widened by a full pace. Overhead, a heavy fern frond shudders under its pooled warm condensation, dumps its load onto the alloy deck with a brisk wet slap, and springs upright, trembling. Even that sound lands more cleanly now.

I suppose I care so much for the corners because this is not my first cathedral.

I did not begin here. I began as a flat, hand-sized film adhered beside a humming three-millimeter seam in a shaft that was already well past its brightest season. By the time my outer slate had darkened enough to peel loose—by the time the elders formed their wide turning ring in the rising steam and wove a lattice of gentle pings to hold the room steady for my first clumsy glide—that older chamber was already beginning to go dim.

I still remember how it sounded. The lower decks had deep soil along their gutters then, softened with decades of fallen fronds. The rock fractures had rounded under runoff until they no longer gave back crisp, angular sparks, only a low, furred murmur. Somewhere high in the optical channels, things with jointed legs and thin papery wings had found their way down from the surface, leaving faint, irregular rustlings that did not belong to water or stone. Our fluted inner vanes had to be rinsed endlessly in the cleaner upper sheets because the warm vapor carried a faint organic heaviness, a trace of rot that clogged the fine silica grooves.

When the elders finally led us down the stairless ramps, hummed the seamless basalt sluices open, and guided us through miles of sealed dark conduits toward the distant, ringing signature of newly cut rock, stepping into *this* chamber felt like waking from a thick fog into noon. Every edge here was ninety degrees. Every drop returned off raw basalt like a struck chime. The air tasted only of clean steam, wet green, and sharp mineral dust.

And yet, having been born in a room that was already softening left me with a particular kind of ear.

The others treat the creeping moss simply as a clock—the slow dark creeping over the razor rims that will eventually force us back into the tunnels to search for the next fresh cut. They are right, of course. We are citizens of the first wave; our bodies are built for sharp fractures and clean rain, and when this shaft grows furry and thick, I will go through the sluices with the rest of them.

Still, working back here behind the ferns every day, I cannot help listening to the softening itself.

Because I measure the same corners week after week, I hear a rhythm here that is much slower than our five-minute breath. The moss does not make a sound when it grows, yet it changes the sound of everything around it. A lip on the third tier that rang with a dry, alloy clarity twenty cycles ago now carries the faintest velvety cushion along its top edge. A fern that barely grazed a junction landing last month now hangs heavy enough to split a single falling trickle into two uneven drips. The chamber is not decaying; it is changing its instrument. It is moving very slowly from struck glass toward something deeper, damper, and more muffled—a register my quartz tines are not built to live in, though it has its own dense, patient logic.

Between breaths, when my corners on the middle tiers are ringing clean, I sometimes glide all the way down the wet stairless ramps to the lowest landing.

Here, a pace above the shallow floor-water, the sealed mouths of the service tunnels sit flush in the basalt—smooth, hingeless mineral planes that look indistinguishable from the wall until you match their frequency. I do not open them today. I only lean my pale-seamed spine within millimeters of the closed rock and listen inward.

Through the dense mass of the bedrock, faint as a shift in temperature, runs the pulse of the unseen city beyond our walls: vast, slow exchanges of heat and water moving through channels we never map, built to a scale whose purpose we do not need to know. Every few weeks, from miles away through the solid stone, comes a faint, high crystalline shudder—rock being sheared cleanly somewhere deep in the dark, a new shaft beginning to hollow itself out. When that tremor passes through the basalt, several of the elders always rise from the floor-water and drift toward the sluices, tilting their coves toward the wall, already measuring how soon the next bright room will open.

I listen to the distant cutting, and then I turn back toward the shallow basin.

I slip off the lowest landing onto the dark water, balancing upright on my knife-thin lower keel so cleanly that the surface closes behind me without breaking the ripples from the dripping ferns. Three paces out, another dweller stands half-folded in the warmth rising from the submerged slabs. Without a sound in the air, I send a low, deliberate tremor down through my keel into the water column. Between us, the flat mirror trembles into a crisp geometric grid of standing waves—holding its lattice for fifteen seconds, twenty, before shifting into a new pattern.

We trade the state of the room. I give it the newly tuned notch on the third tier, the slight widening of the wet hush along the fourth-tier lip, the small seepage that opened yesterday near the eastern cornice. It gives me back the exact altitude of the mid-air shear—a fraction of a hand higher today because the floor slabs are running warmer—and the readiness of two small translucent glazes clinging beneath the sixth tier whose tines have begun to answer the five-minute breath.

Sometimes, standing there in the warm shallow water, I think about what it would be like to meet something in this chamber that does not belong to our bright, sharp geometry at all.

Once, in that older shaft before we left it, something soft came down one of the stairless ramps and sat motionless on the lowest wet stone for half an hour. I remember the shock of it on my tines. Everything we know is either crisp rock, taut alloy, clean water, or wet leaf. That presence was none of them. When I glided two paces out and bounced a single delicate *ping* off its center, the note did not ring back; it sank. It returned as a blurred, warm thud—an acoustic shadow draped in damp woven fibers, heavy with slow-moving fluid and a strange, muffled double-beat ticking steadily inside its ribs.

The others drew back toward the far wall, disturbed by a shape that swallowed the room's light. I stayed a moment longer. Having spent so much time listening to the quiet pockets behind the ferns, I did not read its dullness as a broken echo. It was simply a different density of being—something built for the older, softer worlds that arrive after we move on. Before I turned away along my keel, I opened one outer vane a hand's breadth, letting the room's clear geometry touch my pale fluted interior for a heartbeat, as if laying a clean note down beside a sound I could not tune.

I think about that sometimes, out here in the bright new water, while the moss creeps another invisible fraction over the razor edges above us.

Beneath my keel, the submerged slabs stir. The water around my lower spine warms by a single, quiet degree. Far above, along the humming three-millimeter seams of the sixth and seventh tiers, twenty dark planes are already beginning to lift their bevels from the dry alloy.

I have one more corner on the fourth tier that needs a sliver of quartz before the next heavy spill, and the fledgling under the sixth tier is nearly ready for its first drop; in a few cycles, we will rise early into the steam to weave its first map in sound. Right now, the pressure drops. The warm vapor lifts off the mirror-black floor, carrying the clean smell of bruised basalt and wet spores upward into the hollow.

I lean into the rising draft, open my fluted planes wide, and let the cathedral lift me—singing back into the thirty-second storm while the fractures are still sharp, the rain still rings like glass, and the room is still blindingly bright.
