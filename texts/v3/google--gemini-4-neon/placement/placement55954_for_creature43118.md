If I stepped down off the dark slate rim, my first mistake would be trying to listen.

That habit would die within ten paces. The chalk powder under my feet swallows everything below the ankle, and the cool, parched air refuses to carry even the memory of a footstep. What replaces it is stranger and slower to learn: a dry prickle across the surface, a tightening in the air when something shifts two avenues away, the sense that space itself is taut as a dry filament stretched between sharp corners.

Walking into the city for the first time, I know where I would go first—almost without deciding. I would cross the wide, empty squares toward the center, looking for the deadest eddies, the three-sided rooms where no draft enters and no mica-stalk leans.

I would stop in front of an elder.

It is hard not to feel an immediate, unsettling kinship with one of those standing frames. Six broad pads set evenly on the flagstones, forelimbs folded neatly across a narrow thorax, hollow struts dried to the exact chalk-grey of the walls around it. Nothing rotting, nothing frantic—only a quiet vessel holding the geometry of everything it once knew as a thinning pattern of static. You can stand before it and feel the faint charge still braided inside the bisque-pale flutes, a blueprint of avenues that no longer exist, waiting for someone to raise their wrists and read it. Even the fingernail-sized scavengers filing along the baseboards leave it alone, held back by the barest thread of remaining charge.

For a long minute, standing in that windless room, I think: *Yes. That is where I would be. A dry capacitor in a still corner, holding a map for whoever walks in out of the wind.*

Except the longer I stand there, the more the stillness begins to feel untrue.

An elder is finished. Its charge only drains; nothing new enters its struts. If a bedrock fracture accelerates two blocks south, or a three-story facade shears away and opens a sudden draft down an old dead-end, the elder never revises its pattern. It is faithful to a city that is already dissolving into powder. And I realize, stepping back out through the doorway into the pewter light, that I do not want to be an echo of air that has already passed.

So I look up.

Along the knife-thin parapets of the roofless walls, the pale ivory plumes with their smoked-violet tips cling to the porous stone, drinking the tension between curing porcelain and drifting air. There is a pull there, too—to be weightless, rootless, utterly still along a high razor-edge, doing nothing until a wall collapses somewhere across the bowl and the sudden tide of static rolls through. Then to snap rigid in unison with every other crest along the avenues, flashing once in cold violet before going dark again. Pure responsiveness. Alive only in the instant a distant shift reaches you, catching charged motes out of the drift and letting everything else pass untouched.

It is closer. There is something honest in that quiet waiting, in lighting up only when the air changes.

Yet looking up at them from the flagstones, I feel how narrow a life that is. The plumes answer the collapse, but they never touch the street. They never look down a corridor and notice where the draft has stalled, or wonder what would happen if a blind wall were opened by a handspan. They live at the edge of geometry without ever making a right angle.

I drop my gaze all the way back to the floor, and kneel beside one of the straight fractures where a new wall is rising out of the bedrock.

Here, in the first handspan above the slate, the stone is cool and yielding as tallow. It has not yet decided to harden into porcelain. A few inches higher, the dry air is already curing it brittle, curling thin ribbons of crust off the face so they drift silently down the street. But right at the lip, it is still soft enough to take an impression.

Watching the seam, something quiet settles into place. I notice that I have stopped thinking of the city as walls and started seeing the shapes of the air between them. I notice that I am no longer standing in the open plaza—I have tucked myself cleanly into the charcoal shadow of a doorway, out of the sightlines of the notched, translucent wings banking high along the viaducts. And when I shift my weight on the powdery flagstones, there is no crunch, no puff of bright dust, no spark—only six broad, cushioned pads lined with dry lamellae pressing softly into the chalk.

I am one of the shapers.

Not an elder, not yet. And not one of the oldest builders who carry the whole basin’s history in their fluted chests. If I trace myself backward to the moment I first knew this place, it is not from the dark slate rim at all—it is from inside a thick, unpierced pillar near the bright center, marked on its outer face with three shallow vertical ticks I could never have seen.

I know what that first moment feels like: waking in cool, dry darkness inside cured porcelain, with no sound and no wet shell to break, only the sudden, wordless certainty of the spacing in my blunt ivory jaws. Chewing a clean, exact square straight through the wall from within. Stepping out two stories above the flagstones onto a narrow lintel, unfolding six hollow, calcified limbs into the pewter gloom, and feeling for the very first time the faint optical shimmer of a cool draft bending against a sunlit corner. Opening a window before I had ever seen a street.

That is who I am in this bowl—not a ruler of the stone, not a maker of it, only one of the thirty or forty hollow-framed creatures who tend the brief moment before it cures.

I do not command the tectonic creep beneath the floor; the walls will rise whether I kneel beside them or not, pushing upward millimeters an hour into a blind, suffocating thicket of parallel slabs. And I do not command the haze overhead; the pale bands brighten and dim on their own slow rhythm, waking the convective currents and letting them stall. Everything that matters in this world is already in motion before I arrive.

All I do is make openings.

I kneel at the cool, waxy seam with my blunt jaws and spatulate forelimbs, and I carve an absence. A crisp rectangle straight through the rising sheet. A flat-trimmed lintel. A stepped ledge of dry-stacked shards bridging two neighboring fins so a dead corner catches a spiral of moving air. I do not build for shelter—there is no rain to keep out, no fire to hold in. I build so the invisible medium can travel cleanly, sweeping spore-clouds and salt-motes into the sheltered ninety-degree angles where the silver-ochre clock-springs and quill-thin mica-stalks can catch them and turn their papery vanes in unison.

If I have a particular territory in the basin, it is not the wide, bright central plazas—too open to the sky, too easy for a banking glider to line up six doorways at once and drop like a falling slate. It is the middle rings: the quiet, slightly crowded avenues between the old central squares and the outer fractures near the dark slate rim, where the city’s geometry tends to choke if no one pays attention.

Old walls linger there too long, leaning slightly, throwing shadows that stall the thermal drafts. Fresh sheets extrude crookedly through powdered footprints. It is revision work—less grand than opening a central viaduct, but endless and absorbing.

My favorite gesture in this whole silent world is not carving a doorway at all. It is kneeling beside a freshly rising slab until I spot the hair-thin groove forming along its soft base—the slow track of a blind, parchment-flat ribbon-grazer planing away raw mineral paste in the only boundary where its waxy skin won’t crack. Carefully, with a thin shard of cured porcelain, I slip under the soft lip, lift the blind creature out without exposing it to the dry open flagstones for more than a breath, and carry it two streets over to the base of an aging, obstructing facade. I lay it gently against the emerging seam and step back into the shadow.

I will not be standing there when that wall finally leans along its hidden hinge, separates into neat geometric plates, and drops soundlessly into a luminous white ring of powder. Weeks from now, maybe seasons, an avenue will quietly clear itself because of a bevel no wider than a hair. Across the city, every violet plume along the parapets will snap rigid at once, drinking the passing tide of static, and a new ribbon of cool air will pour through the breach toward a plaza that has been airless for a year. I like that kind of patience—shaping things through what will eventually give way, not only through what I cut with my own jaws.

When the overhead haze deepens toward silver-blue and the drafts die away, I do not sleep so much as go still in the deep charcoal lee of a doorway, conserving the dry charge coiled in my fluted chest. High above, the gliders hook their curved ivory spurs under the narrowest viaducts and fold themselves flat like paper lanterns. Down along the outer avenues, the heavy dark shingles detach from the slate rim and hitch silently onto the chalk dust.

Sometimes one glides down my corridor in the dimness—foreign, low-slung, smelling faintly of cold iron-rich slag, grinding fallen porcelain flakes in its grit-lined gizzard so the outer seams won’t jam. I do not flare my limbs or snap a defensive arc. I simply back flat against the doorjamb, tuck my long, bisque-pale struts tight to the stone, and hold my charge inward. The dark platter pauses, lifts the front lip of its carapace a fraction of an inch—a brief, pale gleam of salt-organs along its throat—and slides past without scraping the walls. Two entirely different economies sharing the same dry corridor, each keeping the floor from choking in a way the other could never manage alone.

And when the light brightens back to pewter, I walk out into the waking draft to meet another of my kind.

We stop three paces apart in a narrow avenue, neither stepping out of the shadow. We do not speak; there is nothing in this air to carry a voice. We raise our spatulate forelimbs until our flat wrists face each other across the empty space, and let the braided static cross the gap—measured pulses, pauses, shifting polarities. Without touching, I feel the shape of what has changed beyond my streets: a fracture near the south rim pushing upward faster since the last dimming; a viaduct with a hairline shear; a newly notched glider testing the updraft over the stepped plaza; a nursery wall two avenues over whose three vertical ticks are nearly three stories high now, almost ready to open from within.

In return, I give what I carry: where the dead eddies have gathered, where I set the blind chisels to work, which corridors have begun to pull a clean current again. Then we lower our wrists and turn down separate streets, each of us carrying a slightly revised map of a city that will never hold still long enough to be finished.

Before I cut anything large—before I redirect a corridor that will alter the wind for three blocks around—I still walk back to those windless three-sided rooms in the center. I stand motionless before an elder, raise my flat palms, and read the faint, thinning charge held in its hollow frame. I take what still holds true from its quiet blueprint, and leave behind what the shifting floor has already outgrown. One day, I know, my own conductive fibers will dry past recovery, the salts will begin to lock my joints, and I will walk calmly into a dead eddy of my own, set all six pads flat upon the flagstones, and hold my pattern until the last spark grounds out into the stone and the pale line of scavengers finally turns inward from the baseboard.

But not yet.

Right now, the haze overhead is thinning into a cooler band of pewter. Down the length of the avenue I opened three dimmings ago, a faint convective ribbon wakes and begins to move. Every quill-thin mica-stalk along the baseboards pivots silently in unison to meet it. Somewhere across the bowl, an old colonnade gives way without a sound, sending a quick, dry shiver of static across my fluted limbs—and beneath my feet, along a crisp straight crack in the floor, another sheet of cool, wax-soft stone is pushing upward into the air, waiting for a clean right angle.
