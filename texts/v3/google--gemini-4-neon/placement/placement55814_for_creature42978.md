At first I think I would try to stay completely still, out of a sudden, sharp shame at being soft.

Everything else in the chamber has made a hard bargain with water and lime. The bricks are slicked with mineral crust; the lurkers wear olive plate; even the keepers have soles like boiled leather and ears plugged against the bruise of the bass. To stand shin-deep in that pewter mirror in cloth and wet boots—soaking up sound where sound ought to glide, bending a centuries-old counterclockwise wave a hair’s breadth out of true—is to realize how loud an unadapted body is simply by existing.

So if I stayed—and I think I would stay, held by the strange restfulness of a place where ordinary speech turns to wet mud and nothing is wasted on explaining itself—I would not begin by trying to belong to the rafters.

I know myself well enough to see where I would fail here. I would never be the slender figure climbing forty feet horizontally across slick terracotta lips while flyers rake the mortar beside their knuckles; my toes would never splay quite right for wet clay, and my nerve would thin out halfway up the curve. Nor would I be the one crouched by a wall-sluice during the two-minute ebb with a bone-tipped prong poised for a lurker’s neck-plate. That kind of clean, instantaneous strike belongs to people whose reflexes were raised inside the roar.

What pulls at me is slower, lower, and closer to the seams.

I would end up living between the sunken perimeter bench and the central limestone grate, never quite as fluid on the high-cleated oak pattens as the ones born in the rush-cradles. For a long time my heels would still drag a fraction too hard across the slumped slate, sending a flat, clumsy shudder through the shallows that makes the broad lurkers twitch in their troughs and draws a single, dry *tock* of warning from the beams overhead. My back would never take the full, graceful bow of the dome’s ribs. Even after my hair was cropped close with a shell-edge so cold water would stop pooling at my collar, even after lime-chalk settled permanently into the creases of my ankles and knuckles, there would probably remain one rush-cord along the eastern curve whose slip-knot still sits a whisper lower than its neighbors—the room’s quiet, ongoing allowance for my shape.

It takes weeks, maybe seasons, to stop fighting the sound with your ears.

At first the circling boom feels like an enclosure pressing inward on the skull. Only when one of the keepers presses a warm wad of amphibian tallow and shredded moss into my palm—not unkindly, the way you hand a coat to someone shivering—and hangs a short, notched spool of fish-bone around my neck on a loop of sinew does the chamber stop being loud and start becoming legible.

Once the ears are blunted, the skeleton wakes up.

You clamp the fish-bone lightly between your back teeth and touch its far end to a taut rush-cord, or to the wet edge of a dressed block, and suddenly the thick gray pressure splits cleanly into separate strands. You can feel the upper tier of small jars barking like dry wood; beneath them, the wide-bellied vessels near the waterline rolling their brassy bass around the brickwork; beneath that, the dry frying crackle of crawlers packing the seams between flagstones. Nothing is mixed into mud anymore. Each layer has its own grain, its own weight against your jaw.

And if you kneel beside the central limestone grate during the quiet twelve minutes between ebbs, and press the tip of your bone spool directly against the submerged stone lip, you find a fourth rhythm that almost no one listens to until it breaks.

Beneath the tiled floor, hidden in the dark masonry of the foundations, the siphon is always working.

You cannot see its clay pipes or stone baffles, only the square slotted throat where the floor tilts inward every quarter-hour. Through the bone in your teeth, you feel the slow, heavy argument happening underneath the slate: groundwater pushing in from the unseen marsh outside, building pressure behind the sunken baffles, shifting grit grain by grain beneath the sagging floor-tiles, gathering toward the moment the whole flat mirror will groan and drop by the width of a hand.

That is where I find my hands going.

Not to the sudden emergencies of the ebb—though during those two roaring minutes, when the flyers boil down from the rafters and the midge-veils rise off the exposed black stone, I would be at the grate with the rest of them, plunging my arms elbow-deep into the sucking cold to haul out the matted fern-fronds, cracked shells, and shed chitin before they can foul the throat. I would still flinch slightly when a flyer banks too low and its trailing whiskers brush my cheek like wet thread. I would still blink too fast in the smoky veil of midges.

My real work happens in the long, pewter stretches when the water has risen back over the tiles and the middle of the chamber is empty again.

I would become a listener to the slow drift.

Because the dome is not finished, and it is not static. Craft and erosion have fused, yes, into something that looks timeless in twilight, but nothing that weeps water for centuries stays still. A hairline beard of lime thickens inside a lower jar’s throat over three moons, sharpening its pitch by a fraction of a tone—not enough to break the circling song, not yet worth sending a climber down with a scraper, just a faint tight edge creeping into the harmony. A braided rush-cord absorbs damp through a rainy week and stretches by the width of a fingernail, so its clay weight kisses the rising tide a breath too early and blurs the crossing rings. Somewhere under the northern tiles, a pebble works itself into a stone baffle and makes the quarter-hour breath come four beats late.

The others read the room’s health from the rafters, feeling the oak ribs with their cheekbones, steering the flyers with shifted cords, keeping the living balance of toads and chitin and fish in motion. They have carried this vessel in their posture since childhood; to them, the equilibrium is simply the air.

Having come from outside it, I can never quite forget how thin the wall is between a tuned instrument and a ruined well.

So I keep a short string of retired bone spools knotted to my waist-cord—old weights whose rush-lines frayed and had to be replaced, lengths of lurker-rib scored with tiny, deliberate notches cut by a shell-edge. Not writing; there is nothing here to write on, and damp would eat it in a week. Just measurements of what used to be. The length a cord needed to hold the northern arc three winters ago. The notch where the dry-season tide barely grazed the pierced clay. By laying an old spool against a hanging cord and tasting the faint difference in the hum between my teeth, I can feel the chamber aging—slowly, patiently, brick by brick—and whisper tiny corrections into the slip-knots before a drift becomes a fracture.

And because I still know what the open world feels like, the other task that settles naturally onto my shoulders belongs to the moonless nights.

When the eldest by the flue runs low on dried marsh-rush for new braids, or when the smoldering peat cakes in her tiny brazier dwindle down to gray ash, someone has to go out through the overflow culvert.

The others can do it, of course—they have done it for generations—but I see how tightly their shoulders draw inward when they crouch at the low stone mouth where excess water bleeds away beneath the foundation. If you have spent your whole life inside seventy paces of curved brick and oak ribs, where every sound turns faithfully back to you in a circle and every ripple meets a wall and returns with news, the drowned ground outside is a terrifying void. There is no ceiling to catch your click. The air moves from any direction without warning. When a reed snaps or a bird cries out in the unseen marsh, the sound simply flies off into the flat black sky and never comes back.

To me, that open emptiness is an old ache rather than a panic.

So I strip off the heavy lurker-hide cloak—too stiff for the narrow stone throat—smear my forearms and shins with a thin coat of tallow against the cold, tuck a sharpened shell-blade into my belt, and slide belly-down through the weeping culvert into the dark.

Out there, for an hour or two, the pressure lifts off my chest. My ears unplug on their own. There is only the black gleam of marsh-water under clouded stars, the cold smell of wind moving freely across miles of sedge, and the soft hiss of pale rush-stems parting under my shell-blade. I bundle the wet stalks tight with spare cord, dig heavy, dripping blocks of peat from the banks, and sit for a few quiet minutes on a hummock with my knees drawn up, listening to how scattered and uncollected the outside world is—every creature calling into nowhere, nothing answering on the beat, no mirror holding the rings long enough to read them.

I never stay long. Soon enough, the openness starts to feel thin and careless, and I miss the weight of the dome.

Crawling back through the culvert with a wet bundle of rush lashed behind my hips, I wash the foreign marsh-mud off the stems in the outer stone trough so no strange seed or rot rides in with me. When I heave myself back onto the sunken perimeter bench inside the chamber, the circling bass hits my sternum like a warm hand—not warm in temperature, never that, only warm with continuity—and the air tastes cleanly again of wet clay, lime, and mineral dark.

Up near the crooked roof-vent, the pale shaft of twilight is barely hinting at morning. The eldest sits cross-legged on her plank by the clay flue, a thin thread of guano-smoke drawing straight out past her shoulder into the unseen sky. I tie the dripping bundle of pale rush to a spare haul-line and give it two sharp tugs. Up in the oak gloom, a pale forearm leans over a rush-cradle and draws the harvest hand-over-hand into the rafters, where the stalks will dry slowly in the rising draft until her thick, flat-worn fingernails twist them into the next generation of nerves for the room.

Two soft *tocks* of bone on oak answer from above. Not thanks—there is no need for thanks between parts of the same vessel—only acknowledgment that the weight has arrived, and the line is clear.

I do not climb up to sleep right away. I hook my wooden pattens over my arches, slip quietly off the bench into the shin-deep mirror without breaking its sheen, and glide out toward the third stone bollard near the center.

Below my cleats, the flagstones shudder once—that faint, deep hydraulic sigh telling me the buried siphon is nearly ready to tip. In another minute the floor will breathe, the flyers will spill out of the rafters in screaming ribbons, and the chamber will knot itself into two minutes of frantic, precise harvest.

Before it breaks, I pause beside one of the hanging rush-cords in the empty gray middle. Its notched bone weight hovers a bare fingernail above the dark water. I touch the braided fiber lightly between thumb and forefinger—not pulling, never forcing—just feeling the counterclockwise roar of thousands of clay-housed throats humming down the damp stalks straight through my skin.

I am not the singer in the wall, and I am not the architect who pinned these oak ribs with copper pegs centuries ago. I am something thinner and more suspended than either: a slow translator crouched at the waterline, carrying raw rush in from the boundless dark, measuring the quiet drift beneath the slate, letting a tuned vessel reshape my bones until the small dead spot I make in the air is no longer an interruption at all, only another knot in the cord, held steady as the great circling song turns and turns around us both.
