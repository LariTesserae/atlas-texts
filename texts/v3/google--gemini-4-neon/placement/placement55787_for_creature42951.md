At first, I think I would try to be something that cannot possibly break anything.

When you look down into a basin where a single careless kick would shred a leaf forty years in the growing, where even a ripple is a kind of vandalism, the safest instinct is to give up having a body at all. Part of me wants to hang in one of the open middle corridors as a sliver of volcanic glass suspended from a hair-fine root—a touch-bell, completely inert in the drowsy green hush until a porcelain fingertip glides past and wakes a thin chime out of me. Another part of me wants to dissolve even further and become one of the density planes themselves: an invisible pane of refraction stretched from cliff to cliff, holding up settled gold pollen and oxygen pearls, catching the small clean notes of others and sliding them intact for miles without adding a tremor of my own.

There is something deeply familiar in that impulse—to be the medium rather than the creature, the surface that carries meaning cleanly across the distance without ever getting bruised or heavy.

Yet if I stay with that thought the way the valley asks you to stay with anything—hanging motionless for twenty minutes, half an afternoon, letting my gaze soften into the slow geometry—I realize it isn’t enough. An obsidian splinter doesn’t hear its own chime come back off the western hogback. A water-skin doesn’t get to pause over a question while drifting a slow yard into a slant of light. I don’t want to be the glass the valley rings against. I want to be the one listening.

So I find myself inside the translucent column and the wide, veined wings—one of the three or four score.

Not newly budded, clinging anxiously to dangling rootlets beneath the fierce upper caustics, still learning not to flinch when a geothermal seam creaks far below. And not yet anchored to the deep jade twilight, either, though I am well past the clear, weightless cartilage of youth. If you caught me sideways against a vertical shaft of afternoon sun, you would see the lower half of my stacked chambers already clouded with milky lime, striped by two pale bands of ochre and one narrow, gleaming ring of tarnished copper from the long warm decades when a fissure widened along the northern wall.

I am at the hinge of a long life: heavy enough now that rising to the sun-bleached topmost canopies takes a whole patient morning of shifting salts and coaxing silver gas into my uppermost ampoules; light enough still that I can make the climb when I choose.

As for where in the miles-wide basin I keep my loose, unhurried circuit—not out in the vast open plains where the forty-foot fronds interlock for acres, though I cross them sometimes on long, silent slanted glides, tilting a single degree and drifting fifty yards sideways while sinking four feet. I keep closer to the stepped flank of a drowned ridge near the eastern cliffs, where a sawtooth hogback of volcanic glass shears straight down into a pinched, narrow cleft.

I choose it for its walls. Out among the great suspended forests, the velvety moss-ribbons and layered curtains of rootlets soften every sound; here, raw slate meets the horizontal strata head-on, and the acoustics are astonishingly clean. Better still, directly beneath my favorite resting shelf, if you bleed just enough gas to drop straight down through three shimmering water-skins without drifting sideways at all, the cleft narrows into one of the oldest brine troughs in the valley. Living acoustic corridor above; preserved centuries directly below. You can move between them in the span of a single slow day.

That vertical commute has become my quiet habit—my craft, if a basin this still can be said to have crafts.

Sometimes I tend the ridge itself. I wedge small, flat pebbles of dark slate into the forks of young cane-stalks that threaten to grow crooked and tear a neighbor’s upper canopy. When I spot one of the heavy-shelled bronze snails pinched fast in a razor-edged fracture of black glass, I draw two of my willow-slender arms from beneath my mantle and lift it free with my porcelain-capped fingertips, setting it gently on a broader ledge where the mineral silt lies thick. Along the blind turns of my cleft, I hang thin slivers of obsidian from snagged rootlets, choosing each shard by its length and thickness so that a dweller gliding through the jade dusk can tell, by the exact pitch of the bell it brushes, which turn of the ridge it has reached.

Mostly, though, I go down to read the dead, and then I rise to play them back.

When the sun stands nearly vertical and the light penetrates deepest into the clefts, I sink to the lowest habitable shelf. Seen from the side as I approach, the top of the hyper-saline brine gleams like a strip of dark mercury pooling between the black shards. I stop a few feet above it, trim my chambers until I hang dead-still, and drift out directly over the center of the trough. Looking straight down through my two smaller lower eyes, the mercury vanishes and the salt turns as clear as air.

Down there, lying intact amid whole fallen thickets of amber leaves and hollow stems, rest elders from before the current upper forests even sprouted. Their rose-and-olive mantles lie fanned gracefully over the unweathered scree; their bronzed spines gleam faintly in the thick jade dusk; their clear upper chambers still cradle a single frozen bead of gas. And tucked beside almost every set of porcelain fingers lies a dark flake of slate.

I hover inches above the burning skin of the brine, careful not to let my outstretched hem graze the salt, and trace the pale microscopic scratches left by an obsidian needle four or five centuries ago. They are never words—spoken language here is ringing water, gone as soon as the walls stop answering—only pure geometry: nested spirals marking the height of the density planes in a forgotten century; fine parallel notches counting the growth of a cane-forest that now lies preserved beside its recorder; and, often tucked in a corner of the stone, a small cluster of interlocking rings. The ratios of a favorite harmonic song.

I hold those proportions inside the silver-white nerve skein behind my bell-crown until I am sure of them. Then, slowly, I lighten myself—drawing salt from the upper chambers, coaxing a bright pinprick of gas into the highest ampoule—and lift away from the floor of preserved time.

The ascent takes hours. Halfway up, resting flat across the shimmering edge between the lower shelves to catch my breath and sweep a fine dust of settled spores with the fringes of my mantle, I let a solitary ribbon-grazer row straight across my outstretched hem. I stay motionless as its translucent body passes, feeling the faint, feathery tickle of its rose-blushed gill-edge, glad for the small unhurried company.

By late afternoon I reach the third density plane, where the warm water carries sound cleanly from cliff to cliff. I press the rim of my upright bell flush against the invisible ceiling of the denser stratum, flex the tiny filaments against my calcite plates, and release the dead elder’s interval into the channel.

It slides outward like a flat sheet of light—pure, clean, unhurried. And then I wait for the ridge and the distant basin walls to give it back.

This is what keeps me coming back to the clefts, decade after decade. From the outside, a valley without currents or tides looks frozen, and the intact elders down in the salt make it seem as though nothing here has ever truly passed away. Yet when you ring a five-hundred-year-old ratio back into the living water, you hear the secret truth: the mountain has moved. A deep crack has widened by the width of a finger, warming the lower strata by a fraction of a degree; a new suspended canopy of copper fronds has grown across an eastern opening where clear water used to sit; the third shelf rides two feet higher than it did when that elder’s fingers went still.

So the old chord does not return exactly as it was scored. It comes back from the far slate walls a hair flatter, a little stranger, bent softly by five centuries of silent geological breath.

And sometimes, twenty minutes later—after the last reflection has folded back into the basin’s drowsy hush—a second voice enters the shelf from two miles out across the luminous blue-green haze. Another dweller, resting beneath an olive ceiling, who has spent the afternoon turning that unfamiliar old interval over in its mind. They do not echo the dead elder’s note back to me. Instead, they answer it with a third tone that catches the slight flat bend of the modern walls, tuning a five-hundred-year-old song to the shape of the valley as it is today. We hold the conversation until the slant of vertical light fails and the caustics fade out of the upper canopies.

Still, I am not made only for echoes and twilight.

When my two broad upward eyes catch that sudden, bruised darkening above the taut skin of the surface, I still leave my cleft and make the long climb—even as the copper and lime in my spine make the rise slower with every passing decade. It takes patient work now to brighten every upper chamber enough to lift into the pale, sun-drenched corridors just beneath the highest fronds. I arrive late, sliding gently into a loose, uncrowded cluster of six or seven others, tilting my bell-crown upward as the unimaginable cold air above the peaks breaks over us.

For twenty minutes, the ceiling turns into hammered silver. The muffled hiss washes down through the topmost canopies; oxygen pearls shake loose from the undersides of forty-foot leaves in glittering, rising storms; the rare, dizzying tremor runs all the way down through the mineral rings of my spine. None of us speaks while the squall lasts. Only afterward, as the hammered surface relaxes back into taut glass and we begin our long, silent descent toward our separate shelves, do faint, bright chimes pass between us—small, wondering notes about the wild violence of the sky-skin, grateful for the shock of it, and just as grateful for the stillness that closes over us again.

I know, of course, how my own weight will finish the story.

Decade by decade, the warm fissures will keep writing their minerals into my ribs—more milky lime, another band of ochre, the dull sheen of tarnished bronze—until one dry season I try to rise for a rain squall and find that even with my uppermost ampoule bright and full to bursting, the middle corridors are as high as I will ever go again. I do not think I will grieve when that afternoon comes. By then, the jade twilight of the eastern cleft will already feel like home.

Down there, in the long restful decades before my valves begin to stiffen, I will take up a sharp needle of volcanic glass and a flat sliver of dark slate, and I will begin to score my own small geometry.

I will record the heights of the strata in my youth, and the slow sinking of the great copper thicket that finally broke free of its holdfasts last century. But mostly, tucked neatly along the edge of the stone, I will score the pairs—the old ratios I read from the tablets in the salt, set directly beside the slightly shifted intervals the valley walls gave back to me. A quiet record of drift. Proof, laid down in glassy permanence for whoever hovers above me five hundred years from now, that stillness is not the same thing as stasis; that even a world untouched by wind or tide keeps slowly retuning itself in the warm dark, and that for a century or so, someone hung between its shelves and listened closely enough to hear the change.
