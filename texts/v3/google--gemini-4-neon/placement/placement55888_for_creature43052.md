At first, I do not think I would have a face here at all.

When I let myself sink into the warmth of this place, my earliest instinct is to slip into the masonry itself—not into the massive flagstones at the center, where the geothermal spring weeps up warm and metallic, nor into the stout octagonal pillars that bear the weight of the world, but into one of the horizontal clay amphorae mortared high into the blackened vaulting.

It is easy to understand the pull of that. To be hollow and hand-thrown, wedged into the ribs of the dome to lighten a load whose builders dissolved into mountain wind long ago. To hold a stillness so deep that a lard-pale eel mistakes me for a cave and coils inside my dark belly, resting its blunt snout at my lip. To have no light of my own, no hunger, no drift—only a single, earthen pitch, slightly muffled by the mortar around my flanks and the living weight inside me, waiting in the cottony hush until a long, pale knuckle taps my travertine rim and wakes a dull thrum that travels all the way across the drowned cathedral.

Or perhaps, a little lower down, I test the feeling of being one of the grazing limpets along the flank of a pillar. Inch by inch across a century, rasping sulfur-crust from the softened stone, wholly invisible in the dark until something brushes past me. Only when touched do I flare—a tiny, cold smear of greenish-white light that lasts four seconds and dies, revealing not myself, really, but the pale face or passing fin that woke me.

Both of those feel strangely close to what I am: an instrument that answers a tap; a small cold flare provoked by contact, making a hand’s-breadth of ancient stonework legible before the warm gloom closes back over it.

And yet, if I stay motionless inside those shapes long enough—half a turn of the convective wheel, the six hours it takes a grain of lime to rise from the floor-grates and slide back down the chilled outer walls—I feel a faint restlessness. Not the dry-world urgency of kicking or fleeing, nothing so wasteful of heat. Just a quiet, persistent need for hands.

I want to feel the temperature of the stone. I want to lay my broad, striated fingertips over the blurred friezes beneath the upper arches and trace the worn ribs of tally marks I cannot decode. I want to know the difference between limestone that is slowly dissolving and limestone that is slowly thickening its glassy skin.

So I uncoil.

If you were drifting near the outer perimeter wall in the upper galleries, you would have mistaken me for a curtain of travertine draped across a sunken balustrade. Only when the winter chill bleeding through three paces of limestone nudges the water heavy against my ribs do I shift half an arm’s-length sideways, releasing a faint, milky puff of stirred lime.

I am one of the seven—or the nine. Not the youngest, whose skin is still mostly slick and pearlescent, who still trails both palms down the middle galleries just for the quiet delight of watching long ribbons of greenish-white limpet light bloom and vanish across the arches. And not yet one of the oldest, whose knees have begun to stiffen with internal lime, pulling them gently toward the warm settling-tanks at the floor.

I am somewhere deep in the second century of my stillness. Matte chalky blooms have dusted the broad flats of my shoulder blades; a cluster of minute glassy bivalves has paved the hollow above my left collarbone, opening papery fans into the slow drift. I leave them there. Only last winter, when a young mussel anchored itself too close to the crease of my inner elbow, did I drift alongside a companion in the pitch dark and hold out my arm until their mineral-hardened thumbnail slipped cleanly beneath the edge—the sharp little *tick* of the shell snapping shut ringing faintly down the narrow water-lanes between the piers.

Where I keep my hold—where I always return, after the slow circuit of feeding and breathing—is the high outer ring of the cistern.

Most of the others favor the warmer middle galleries, tucking themselves lengthwise into the pillowy curves of drowned stairwells or sharing an empty sluice-groove shoulder to shoulder with a knot of wrinkled salamanders. Down there, heat is cheap. You can let your heart drop to three beats a minute and forget that anything exists beyond the masonry.

I stay near the edge.

Up here, suspended between the outermost rank of octagonal columns and three paces of dressed limestone, the water is a hair cooler—just heavy enough to tug gently downward at my heels. I brace a foot against a swollen, corroded bronze dowel to keep from sliding with the current, hook two fingers lightly around the rounded lip of an old stone trough, and press my cheek flat against the perimeter wall.

This is where the outside speaks, if a thing so vast and cold can be said to speak.

When a mountain gale scours the ruin far above the tree line, it enters here as a slow, heavy pressure humming directly in my jawbone and teeth. The water around my calves does not stir by a hair’s-breadth, the blind pink loach hanging a finger’s-width from my knee does not even twitch its barbels, yet my whole skull aches softly with the weight of an invisible violence pressing against the stone. In deep winter, the cold curtain sliding down the wall thickens until it feels like a slow, dark waterfall of denser water pouring past my ribs, speeding the convective wheel by a fraction. In high summer, it eases almost to nothing, and the cistern grows heavy and drowsy as a held breath.

I do not know what a sky is, or wind, or snow, or a dry stone pass. I know them only as pressure in the bone, as the shifting thickness of the cool downward slide, and as the three pale needles of gray dust hanging beneath the blackened crown.

From my station near the upper galleries, I can sense those needles with my wide, milky eyes—not their edges, only their soft, fading glow where they pierce the gloom before the warm dark swallows them. I remember, with the long, unhurried memory of travertine, when the widest needle reached one stair tread farther down the upper mezzanine. That was perhaps eighty winters ago. Decade by decade, blown grit and calcified dripstone have narrowed the three vent-slits by the width of a fingernail. The needles have grown thinner, retreating slowly toward the roof.

Sometimes, hanging motionless beneath those dim gray shafts, I turn my palms upward and wait.

I am waiting for the scraps of sharpness.

Perhaps twice in a turning of the seasons, something infinitesimally small touches the sensitive whorls of my palm—something with a texture that does not exist anywhere else in our softened world. A drowned mountain midge, carried through a narrowed slit and sinking for hours through the warm dark. I close my long fingers around it with extreme gentleness and do not eat it. Suspended between two pillars, I roll the microscopic speck between thumb and forefinger. Its legs are brittle; its wings are impossibly crisp, unforgivingly thin, unmelted by mineral or time. I hold that tiny, alien edge in the dark for twenty minutes, marveling at a place beyond three paces of stone where things are allowed to remain sharp before they break.

And then I turn back to the architecture.

Because I live so close to the upper friezes and the mortared amphorae, my days—measured only by the slow rise and fall of the thermal turn, and the twice-daily need for the ceiling—are given over to two quiet habits: listening, and leaving marks.

I know the voice of every clay jar in the upper vaulting. When I tap the rim of the third amphora east of the widest vent-slit, I can tell instantly whether the old lard-pale eel is coiled inside it; its body dulls the earthen thrum by half a tone, turning a hollow ring into a soft, dense pulse. I am often the one who answers across the cathedral width when another taps out a slow, widely spaced sequence from the lower galleries—two dull notes, a long pause while the water settles back to cottony silence, then a third. *Amphipods thick on the western steps.* Or simply: *Here. Still warm. Still here.* After I strike a jar, I hold completely motionless so my own body will not disturb the water while the vibration dies away into the stone.

Between those sparse exchanges, I drift along the recessed mortar joints of the outer baffle-walls and sunken ledges, carrying small, loose things cupped in my palm: a perfectly whole white cone-shell, a detached pebble of ancient crushed-brick mortar, a shed leathery scute left behind by one of the ancient turtles.

I have traced the builders’ buried friezes so many times over a century that their rhythms live in my fingertips—three short ribs, a braided curve, a pause of smooth stone, three ribs again. I have no idea whether they were counting seasons, or measures of water, or the names of their dead. It does not matter. I find a deep, smooth-lipped crevice where the warm upward flow slows enough to lay down travertine, and I wedge my small objects into it in a deliberate interval: shell, pebble, shell, gap, turtle scute.

I will not live to see it wholly finished, or perhaps I will—two centuries is long enough for a glassy skin to creep over a pebble and fuse it forever to the block. Once, forty winters ago, I wedged a drowned midge into a quiet hollow beneath a dripping rim of calcified stone, hoping the lime would catch its tiny sharp wings before the water unmade them. When I drifted back a season later and brushed the spot with my fingertip, there was only smooth, bare rock; either an ivory amphipod had found it, or the cistern had simply refused to preserve anything so brittle. I understood. I went back to shells and mortar-grains.

Now and then, when one of the others drifts up through the dark to share my cool perimeter ledge, we do not waste breath or motion. We hang close enough for our jawbones nearly to meet, and hum a low, closed-mouth note back and forth—a warm vibration felt directly in the cheekbones and the roots of the teeth. Then I reach out and draw two fingertips slowly down the flank of the nearest octagonal pillar.

A cold, greenish-white ribbon of limpet light blooms behind my hand.

In those four ghostly seconds, I let my companion see what I have tucked into the mortar joint beside us, and I watch their pale, calm face emerge from the gloom—wide milky eyes, smooth lips, tiny white snail-shells clinging like freckles along their temple. Sometimes they raise a broad hand and lay their palm flat against my arrangement, reading the spacing with their skin before the light dies and returns us both to the warm dark.

Twice a day—once, in the drowsy stall of high summer—I leave the outer wall and drift inward until the warm geothermal weep catches my ribs and lifts me effortlessly toward the blackened crown.

I rise straight and unhurried, my chest quiet, my heart ticking four times a minute. Near the highest crossing ribs, if an ancient soft-shelled turtle is ascending on the same lane, I pause a body-length below the ceiling, hovering motionless until its pointed nostrils pierce the silver-bellied lens of trapped gas first. When it finishes and sinks back into the dark, I slip between the blackened ribs and tip my face up through the mirror of the meniscus.

The air in the pocket is thinner than it was a hundred winters ago, pressed flat by the weight of the water and the altitude. It tastes of wet chalk, faint sulfur, and the dry, biting memory of the mountain outside. I open my soft-valved nostrils and draw it in slowly, filling my narrow lungs and swelling the thin, pink, pleated pouches along the sides of my throat.

Then—not every time, but often—I do not return to my high ledge.

Instead, I tuck my chin, drift sideways until the heavy, chilled water along the outer perimeter wall catches my back, and let it carry me down without a single kick. Down past the crowded middle bridges, past the hanging shoals of blind catfish, down through the absolute, heavy pitch-black until the stone beneath my palms turns suddenly, faintly metallic and warm.

Down into the narrowest settling-tanks beneath the lowest arches, where the oldest of us have stopped moving.

I do not need limpet light to find the one I come to tend. I know the exact, pillowy curve of her shoulder where it has already fused into the rounded waist of a column base; I know the thick, translucent curtains of travertine that have glazed her folded knees seamlessly into the flagstones. Bivalves pave her forearms. A white cone-shelled snail is inching unhurriedly across the back of her motionless hand.

I drift down beside her in the warm sulfurous weep and lay my palm gently against her glazed ribs. Beneath the growing shell of stone, a faint, steady tremor answers—her heart, beating perhaps twice a minute now, miserly and serene.

I slip closer, press my broad, soft mouth gently against her calcifying lips, and pass down the thin, chalk-tasting air I carried from the crown. Long and slow, chest to chest, emptying my throat-pouches into hers while the blind loaches hover calmly around our ankles. When the breath is given, I rest my forehead briefly against her temple, feeling the cool dusting of my own travertine touch the thicker glass of hers. I hum one low, closed-mouth note into her cheekbones—carrying down into her warm dark the small news of the upper walls: that the winter gale is pressing hard against the masonry tonight; that the third amphora still holds its eel; that the water is still turning.

She does not move, cannot move, yet her pleated throat-folds pulse once, faintly, in receipt.

One day—perhaps in another century, when the three gray dust-needles have thinned to threads and finally winked out altogether, when the winter gales hum only as the barest ghost in the jawbone—I will drift down here and feel only the steady, metallic warmth of the spring rising through her ribs. And not so very long after that, my own knees will begin to stiffen past the thrift of the long ascent.

I do not dread the sealing of the vent-slits, nor the thinning of the silver pockets at the crown. Everything in this drowned cathedral settles, thickens, and fuses in its own time. We did not chisel these stones, and we will not claw them open.

Until my knees lock, I will stay near the chilled outer wall where the mountain leans against us, listening to the pressure of a world I will never see, tapping the hollow clay jars in the dark, rolling an occasional sharp, drowned wing between my pads, and tucking small shells into the recessed mortar—leaving slow, pillowy sentences for the fingertips of whoever drifts past after me, long after the cistern has smoothed me back into its stone.
