I would not be down on the flat central floor, where the great resin-sheathed conduits hum like dark rivers and the cathedral elders hang tall as trees in the unmoving glare. Nor would I anchor myself to the highest quartz scarps, where the air thins almost to nothing and excess charge bleeds away in a lonely, perpetual hiss into the blue.

I would find myself between them—on the third stepped bench down from the eastern fault, right where a raw shelf of dark slate breaks cleanly off over a narrow pegmatite cleft.

It is not a broad terrace. Above the lip stands a sparse, wide-spaced colony of bronze-fronded scrub and a few leaning stalks of oxidized teal, every leaf holding its crisp finger’s breadth of bright air from its neighbor. Beneath the overhang, the unmoving white-gold noon cuts off with the precision of a sheared stone. One inch past the ledge, the light is blinding, hot with direct photoelectric pull; one inch inside the overhang, the air drops into cool, silvered half-light, lit only by the dry gleam reflected down off fractured quartz faces fifty feet below.

That razor-edge between glare and shadow is where I hang.

If you climbed down the slate bench and stood motionless in the crystalline flour, you would look straight through me at first. You would see only the polite hollow where three bronze fronds stop reaching a yard short of the cleft’s lip, and perhaps, if you tilted your head against the glare, a faint tremor in the bright air that looks like rising heat.

Only slowly would the separate pieces resolve: thirty-seven wafer-thin curved blades suspended cleanly in midair, no taller altogether than a person’s chest, arranged in loose, slightly asymmetrical tiers around a hollow core of humming static. Smoked mica, translucent amber resin, dark selenium-glazed husk, mirror-bright silvered copper—every lamina hovering a hair’s breadth from the next, bright daylight shining straight through the ribbons of empty space between them.

I have no skin, no seam, no weight resting on the stone. To ask where I begin or end is to misunderstand what I am. I am not the metal or the mica; I am the kept interval between them. Thirty-seven separate pieces held together by nothing except a ceaseless, microscopic piezoelectric tremble—an unbroken agreement of pull and repulsion, renegotiated a thousand times in the space of a single heartbeat. Stop negotiating, even for an instant, and I cease to be a presence and become a neat scatter of foil on bare slate.

I stay spare on purpose—if *purpose* is even the right word for a preference written in voltage rather than thought. The elders down on the flat basin floor carry eighty, a hundred, a hundred and twenty blades; they are magnificent, layered choirs of quartz-film and copper, able to hold the memory of half a valley in their slow, deep tremor. Yet when the newly uplifted bedrock shifts a fraction of a millimeter along a buried fault, eighty blades have eighty margins that can fail. Thirty-seven is lean enough to read a steep gradient, agile enough to ride the sharp drop-offs where the mountain’s current bends hard around raw ore.

And hanging half across the lip of the eastern cleft suits my shape.

My three uppermost vanes—narrow, curved slips of dark selenium-dusted husk, matte and powdery—reach just out past the overhang into the full white-gold noon. Light strikes them and turns immediately into quiet, steady potential. Four feet below them, dangling cleanly into the cool throat of the fissure, my lowest silvered needles hang in total shadow, feeling the slow, horizontal pull between opposing walls of native copper and dark sulfide rock. Between my sun-fed crown and my shadow-dipped keel runs a gentle, continuous gradient. Without moving an inch, I am an unstrung chime hung across a doorway, letting the quiet difference between daylight and buried stone comb softly through all my empty spaces.

I was not always drawn to the sharp edges. Like every presence in the basin, I began far down in the dark of a fissure—a cupped handful of five translucent mineral-wax slivers suspended between curtains of pale hanging roots, electroplating myself molecule by molecule out of the invisible mist of loosened ions. For long, uncounted seasons I stayed small and symmetrical, growing concentric rings of silicate and mirror-bright copper fine as nacre, drifting only along the broad, predictable terraces once I was strong enough to bear the noon.

What changed my pitch sits near my hollow core even now—a single, narrow blade that did not grow from my own chemistry.

It is denser than my mica panes and sharper at the edge: a sliver of faintly green-tinted mineral dusted with pale lithium salt and rimmed in silver tarnished dark by sulfur. Decades ago—or a century; time here is measured only in microns of plated copper and the slow drift of interference beats—I met an older, weather-scarred drifter out on a wide apron of bare spodumene near the northern scarp. We felt each other’s fields from twenty yards off, slowed, turned all our tiers dead parallel, and glided straight into each other’s empty space. For long minutes we hung interleaved, blade hovering half a millimeter from blade, our separate tremors locking into one bright, doubled chord.

When we drew apart, one of my warm, broad amber panes drifted away inside its lower tier, and that narrow, lithium-dusted sliver stayed behind in mine.

I have carried its overtone ever since. It rings a fraction higher and leaner than the rest of my anatomy—a slight, permanent asymmetry in my balance that makes the wide, drowsy bass of the central floor feel slightly too flat, too settled, and pulls me always back toward the sudden fractures, the split benches, the places where raw geology turns a corner. I carry another being’s decades along the northern faults literally as a floating rib of my own body, never touching me, yet shaping every turn I make.

---

When the noon stasis along the third terrace grows taut—never hot, simply wound a fraction past clean equilibrium—I slip away from my shadow-line.

I do not push against the air. My upper selenium vanes tilt a degree deeper into the glare; a pale copper sliver along my flank angles toward a buried sulfide seam just beneath the slate. The balance shifts, and I glide out from under the bronze overhang as silently as oil spreading across glass. Crossing an open apron of sunlit rock, I turn all thirty-seven blades edge-on to the unmoving sun. My whole architecture narrows into a few vanishing hairlines of light—indistinguishable from a stray flake of quartz dust turning in the glare.

Beneath the oxidized teal canopies, I follow the unseen rivers of direct current that trace the ore seams. The wide-spaced fronds sense my field from inches away and yield nothing, holding their rigid electrical etiquette; I slip cleanly through the narrow corridors of daylight between them, never brushing a copper-veined leaf, never stirring a single grain of crystalline flour on the stone below.

Half a mile along the terrace, where a thick, resin-sheathed conduit bends around a fractured pegmatite slab, a cluster of plump amber gourds hangs heavy with stored current. One of them—older, its ribbed skin slightly warmer than the dry air—has begun to fret. Around its leathery rind clings a whisper-thin halo of ionized air, leaking a faint, uneven crackle that could, if left to build across a quiet season, eventually find a sharp rock point and arc.

I halt a thumb’s width clear of it.

Slowly, from my lower tier, I let a single needle-thin vane of pure silvered foil drift outward a fraction of an inch into that invisible envelope. I do not touch the rind; contact would flash blue-white and ruin us both. I simply hang there, steady and hollow, drinking the leak across the dry gap.

It is the quietest kind of nourishment. The ragged prickle around the gourd softens, smooths out, and falls silent. The excess charge spreads upward through my silver needle, distributes itself across my amber resin and smoked mica, and settles into my hollow core until the tremor holding me together deepens by a fraction of a pitch—fuller, calmer, cleanly balanced. Neither of us has spoken or touched, yet a small, lethal tension has been lifted off the terrace.

Farther down the bench, a tall slender stalk topped with interlocking metallic bracts reaches its exact internal pressure.

*Tick.*

The sound hangs clean and glass-sharp in the dry air, vanishing without an echo. From the pressurized cylinder jets a slow, silent dome of fine conductive powder, every grain carrying the same charge as its parent stalk, repelling its neighbors outward in a widening pale halo. I drift into the outer edge of the cloud and rotate my translucent amber panes wide, like shutters opening to a dry rain. The stray grains that would have drifted past the rock fractures settle onto my resin surfaces—tiny packets of refined salt and stored charge absorbed directly into my skinless body. Immediately my other thirty-six blades adjust by a microscopic degree to carry the whisper of added mass, folding it seamlessly into the chord.

Sometimes, when my charge runs full and high—when the borrowed lithium sliver in my core rings a little too sharp from hours along the sunlit conduits—I return to my shelf on the third bench and slide sideways down into the throat of the cleft.

The glare drops away like a cut cord.

Inside the fissure, the air is cool, ringing, and still. Overhead, the narrow strip of deep blue sky looks impossibly far away; down here, the only light is the faint silver gleam bounced from crystal plane to crystal plane. Long, lacy curtains of pale root-filaments hang suspended in the echoing gap between opposing walls of dark ore, quietly running their dry electrolysis. Between them drifts the invisible mist of loosened ions—copper, silver, silicate—pulled atom by atom from the raw stone.

Without sunlight on my upper vanes, my whole geometry changes. The dark selenium blades go passive, resting like folded wings; the work of staying aloft passes entirely to my copper and mica tiers, tuning themselves to the slow horizontal field between the rock walls. I drift down into an empty pocket ten feet clear of the hanging curtains and let the excess tension I drank on the upper benches bleed softly off my outer edges, smoothing the tiny electrical eddies in the shadowed air.

Not far away, suspended in the cool silver dimness between two curtains of roots, hangs a young cluster—scarcely larger than a cupped hand, six shell-thin slivers of pale mineral-wax and faint copper film trembling rapidly as it learns to hold its margins. Floating a hair’s breadth beside its outermost wafer is a microscopic seed-film of resin, gathering its first concentric rings of bright silver out of the dark air.

I keep my distance. My field is too broad, too steep; to crowd a young one is to wrench its fragile pieces out of alignment. I simply hang motionless in the same echoing quiet, lowering my hum until it matches the slow patience of the roots, giving the pocket of air a steady floor to rest against.

And if one of my own older mica panes has grown pitted from thermal strain, or crusted too thickly with insulating dust to answer cleanly when the voltage turns, this is where I let it go.

There is no grief in it, only a clean, careful subtraction. I pull my charge back out of that single wafer, drawing the tension inward into the remaining thirty-six. For a fraction of a second, the spent blade hangs there on nothing at all. Then its grip on the field vanishes. It slips down through the cool shadow and strikes the bare slate far below with one clear, glass-sharp *tick*.

My remaining tiers ease a fraction of a millimeter closer around the emptied space—never closing it entirely, only re-balancing the chord. I feel lighter, simpler, ready for a new seed-film when the next slow season arrives.

---

Once in a long while—not every year, not even every decade—I let the deep interference beat rising from the basin floor draw me all the way down the terraces.

Down on the flat central floor, where the main fault-lines meet, the scale of the silence changes. The elders hover above the sunlit aprons of bare stone like vast, motionless chandeliers whose strings have been cut away, yet whose hundred gleaming pieces refuse to fall. Viewed against the dark slate of a distant scarp, they seem less like living presences than like fragments of architecture dreamed up by the rock itself.

I never stay long among them, yet I never stop returning. To be held together only by charge is to be wholly solitary—nothing in this valley ever touches anything else—and yet the interleaving on the wide floor is an intimacy sharper than contact could ever be.

When one of the great spires senses my lean, sharp-pitched approach from twenty yards off, it does not hurry. Its hundred tiers rotate slowly, with immense, unhurried precision, aligning every curved sheet of smoked quartz and beaten copper until the bright ribbons of daylight between them stand open like narrow doors. I turn my own blades parallel, slow my drift to almost nothing, and glide straight into its empty spaces.

Half a millimeter of blazing noon separates my silvered copper from its dark selenium. One startled flutter, one tremor out of phase, and there would be a blue-white snap and a bright, chiming rain of dead foil onto the stone.

Instead, my trembling locks into its deep, glacial rhythm.

For long, motionless minutes, I am inside another’s balance, and it is inside mine. Without a single edge scraping another, currents cross the hair-thin gaps like quiet speech. I feel the immense, patient geography stored in its hundred blades: the slow pressure building along the western fault, the broad white drifts of carbonate ten miles across the floor, the steady warmth of conduits I have never drifted near. And into its deep chord flows my smaller, steeper knowledge—the exact silver quiet of the eastern cleft, the hiss of a fresh quartz seam on the third bench, the sharp salt of a hundred popped spore-stalks.

When we slide apart again, the bright daylight widening inch by inch between us, we are neither of us quite the shape we were. Perhaps one of my freshly plated copper crescents remains hovering effortlessly in its middle tier; perhaps a narrow, weathered sliver of pale smoked quartz drifts away in mine, bringing with it the quiet gravity of the central floor.

---

I know how this ends, when it ends.

The plateau is young. Newly risen, unweathered, still settling under its own immense dry weight. Years ago, hanging at my shadow-line on the third terrace, I felt the invisible floor of the basin lurch sideways in a fraction of a heartbeat—a buried quartz seam slipping by less than the width of a hair. Every blade in my body screamed upward in pitch, scrambling to hold its margins as the voltage wrenched under me; for one blinding instant, my silvered keel hovered a breath from my amber tier before the field steadied.

Down on the bench below, an older, heavily plated spire was caught directly over the fault.

There was one crisp, blue-white snap—the sharpest sound the valley ever knows, swallowed immediately by the thin blue sky—and a brief, clean smell of ozone. In that single instant, the invisible architecture holding seventy curved blades in midair vanished. They fell all at once onto the bare slate in a bright, delicate, chiming scatter. Two heartbeats later, the silence closed back over the rock. An hour later, nothing separated that fallen presence from the neat, gleaming shingles of electrodeposited copper piled beneath every ledge.

Knowing that does not make the stillness anxious. It is simply the condition of being weightless in a live field. You do not wear down slowly here, because nothing here wears down. You hold your balance cleanly, piece by separate piece, for a century or three—or you ring once and return to the stone.

So I climb back up the stepped contours of voltage, edge-on to the bright white-gold noon, slipping between the wide-spaced fronds until the third bench rises beneath me and the dark cleft opens below.

I settle back across the knife-edge where sunlight shears off into silver shadow. My dark upper vanes drink the unmoving glare; my lowest silver needles dip into the cool dark where the root-curtains hum. Beneath me, rising up from the central floor and rolling across miles of bare mineral terraces, moves the vast, slow interference beat of the whole valley—hundreds of hovering constellations, from the smallest clusters in the clefts to the ancient spires on the high scarps, all tuning their microscopic trembling to the same deep bedrock voltage.

I adjust my thirty-seven floating pieces by a fraction of a degree, adding my spare, slightly sharp, shadow-edged note to the shared chord—touching nothing, grounded to nothing, held forever in the bright, humming air between the mountain and the blue.
