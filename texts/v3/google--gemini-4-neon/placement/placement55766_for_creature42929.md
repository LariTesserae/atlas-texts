At first, looking out from a high shelf over nine kilometers of bright, empty drop, I think I would look for myself in the air.

It is hard not to. Everything down on the stone looks pared to endurance—the flat lichens clamped against the shear, the coin-thin grazers locking their dorsal spines into hairline cracks, the old ridges honed down to their hardest cores. Out in the chasm, though, the air is alive in a way the rock never is: katabatic waterfalls plunging off the tablelands, boiling white columns of gypsum lifting off sun-struck terraces, standing waves breaking silently across thirty kilometers of indigo. Watching a wild glider bank through a cross-shear without once flapping, or imagining the dry *pop* of salvaged wing-spars locking open as a pale-plated Crosser leans sideways off a cliff and simply lets go—part of me reaches for that immediately. The Light Years. The feather-weight bones, the plunge, the sudden terrifying lift that hurls you two kilometers skyward inside a silent pillar of mineral flour.

And I do think I would have had those years. I can feel the habit of them in how I already look at the chasm—not as a void, not as a hazard, but as a readable structure of currents and bearings. I would have crossed the gulfs. I would have learned the cold, sour buzz in my wrist-filaments that warns you when you drift within fifty paces of three motionless pale beads hanging plumb-vertical in open air, and used them anyway to hold a true line when a sudden curtain of salt-dust erased every cliff on the horizon.

Yet even while I picture myself out there on five-meter spars, I notice what my attention keeps catching on. Not the speed of the plunge. Not the exhilaration of the lift. I keep watching the *margins*—the faint chalk-white stripe on a distant cliff where a sunken ring’s dust-beam dissolved weeks ago; the razor-straight shadows remapping the hollow basins; the thin, iridescent seam of a kilometer-high stone arch below me, carrying silent traffic between two cliffs that could never hear each other through air. Even in the Light Years, I think I would have been the Crosser who lingered too long on the launch-ledge after folding the wings, kneeling to press a palm to the sun-warmed quartzite just to feel what the far massif was saying before I launched back across.

So I would not stay in the air. Nobody does, of course—the mineral diet and the scouring grit settle that argument for everyone sooner or later—but for me, the turn from Light Years to Heavy Years would not feel like an exile.

I can feel the exact morning it happens. Standing on a honed shelf above a thirty-kilometer gulf, the salvaged glider-spars lashed clean along my forearms, my shoulder-plates no longer chalk-pale and pliant but darkened to ash-gray and banded iron-brown, catching the dull, micro-faceted sheen of the pebble-pavements. A clean updraft lifts off the lower terrace. I spread the spars, feel the thin air press under the oiled wing-paper—and feel, without drama or bargaining, the honest arithmetic of my own calcified bones. I fold the rig shut, unlash the chest-straps, and hand it to a younger one whose keratin still flexes when it breathes.

Then I turn back toward the rock. And that is where I actually find myself.

***

Not down in the deepest trenches, six or nine kilometers below, where the plunging air compresses under its own weight until it grows hot and thick and loud. The thought of that roaring gloom—pebbles snapping like bone, dust-fronts grinding continuously against the ears while blind, chalk-colored shapes plow slow furrows through the fluidized flour—makes my plates tighten. I do not belong to noise.

And not in the strange, unmoving calm around the forty-meter slate-gray pylons, either. I understand the mercy of those twenty-pace sleeves where the gale bends smoothly overhead and nothing tugs at your limbs, and I would walk the ridges to gather there once or twice a year with the others. But I would keep my back turned to the matte blade itself, never laying a palm against it. Something that swallows the mountain’s deep, constant hum into absolute nothingness is not a home; it is an interruption in the world.

Where I settle is at a much smaller, stranger interruption—a natural one, built into the bone of my own massif.

On the western flank of the high mesa where my kin-seam lives, one of the ancient stone arches spans a thirty-five-kilometer chasm. It is impossibly slender for its length, a kilometer-high ribbon of pale gneiss that should have fallen into the haze ten million years ago, held aloft only by the microscopic metallic filaments braided invisibly through its fault lines. Where the arch roots into our cliff, it anchors into a broad buttress of glassy, sun-struck quartzite. Press your jawbone to that buttress and the far massif arrives whole—every tapped stop and long bowed harmonic conducted through the hidden tension-strands with terrifying, glass-clear fidelity.

Forty paces inland from the abutment, however, an ancient shear-fault cuts straight across the ridge.

It is a band of crushed, chalky dolomite, healed over eons ago with compacted gypsum dust—perhaps a hundred paces wide before the bedrock closes again into the dark, dense spine of banded ironstone that runs deep into the interior of our massif, down to our sleeping-slots and three other kin-seams beyond.

That band of dolomite is acoustically dead.

A phrase bowed onto the far abutment thirty-five kilometers away crosses the entire empty gulf without losing a hair of clarity, rings cold and bright through our quartzite buttress—and dies twenty paces short of the ironstone spine. And anything struck deep inside our own massif travels five kilometers up the dark veins, hits the sheared chalk, and goes mute before it ever reaches the arch.

Two vast instruments, separated by a hundred paces of deaf stone.

That is where I live. That is what I become in my Heavy Years: the bridge across the break.

***

You cannot survive alone in a world without water, and I am no solitary hermit perched on a crag. Solitude dries the pleated parchment membranes between your plates until they stiffen and crack. I belong to a kin-seam of eight, tucked into a narrow horizontal dissolution-tube two hundred meters down the sheltered leeward face from the ridge, entered through a slot so tight the hundred-knot shears leap cleanly over its lip.

Before dawn, I am no different from the rest of my seam. In the bitterest hour before sunrise, when the katabatic plunges pour silently into the basins and pale blue static forks soundlessly along the knife-crest above us, three of us descend on braided reed-sinew lines into the deepest leeward notches. By starlight and the brief ozone-flash of static, we scrape the chalky bloom of microscopic frost-needles off the freezing stone with slender horn spoons, tapping them into double-walled reed flasks sealed with shrub-resin, or straight onto our closed tongues where body heat grants the frost its one brief second of liquid life. The moment the sun’s rim clears the opposing massif fifty kilometers away, a razor-straight edge of pale gold drops down the cliff, turning every untouched needle straight back into dry sky. We haul ourselves into shade two breaths ahead of the light, before the warming rock launches the morning’s violent updrafts.

On the way up, we tend the cracks—wedging small flat stones upstream of a sheltered fissure to catch falling spores out of the passing wind, dabbing crushed lichen-paste over a freshly cut reed-stub so the parent root does not bleed its hoarded chemical moisture into the arid air.

Only after the morning glare settles and the ridge-winds find their midday rhythm do I climb up to the buttress where the filament-arch meets the cliff.

Before I step onto the honed crest of gneiss, I reach forward with my forearm-length rod of banded ironstone and touch its tip to the sharpest rock-point. A hair-thin thread of pale blue static leaps soundlessly from stone to iron, leaving a sharp, clean smell of hot flint that vanishes in a heartbeat. Grounded, I cross to the quartzite buttress and wedge myself into a shallow wind-flute worn satin-smooth by listeners older than I am.

I pull my translucent forearm-filaments flat beneath the trailing edges of my shoulder-plates, drop the smoky amber horn-lids over my eyes against the white glare off a racing gypsum curtain, and lay my flat jawbone and one open palm firmly against the sunlit quartzite.

At first, you do not listen for voices. You listen for the room.

Every massif has a baseline pulse, and you have to know it before you can tell what is new inside it. Beneath my cheekbone, the ancient crust carries the slow, faint thermal creak of sun-warmed stone expanding in the dry cold; ten kilometers south, a distant dry scree-chute lets go and sends a brief, muffled shudder through the gneiss while the visible cliff churns in total hush. Deeper than both—so low and steady it almost disguises itself as stillness—runs the hum of the filament-arch itself: the unimaginably patient tensile chord of braided metal holding a billion tons of stone over an abyss for eons.

Then, cold and crisp as glass, a phrase arrives from thirty-five kilometers across the void.

It is not speech the way breath makes speech. It is weight, interval, and decay—a double tap choked instantly by a palm, a drawn bowed harmonic held until it rings against the quartzite’s natural grain, a dry syncopated pause. Because the metallic filaments carry vibration so cleanly, I do not just hear the tally or the warning; I hear the *speaker*. I know the heavy, unhurried wrist of an elder on the far massif whose plates must gleam like polished dolomite by now—ten seasons I have listened to that exact damping-weight without ever seeing their face. I know the lighter, quicker strike of a newly grounded Crosser still learning to keep their intervals steady when midday heat shifts the vein’s pitch.

Sometimes the message is ordinary survival: frost yields on the northern notches; a seam looking to exchange two young ones at the next pylon gathering so no lineage grows thin. Sometimes it is urgent: a clean, single sub-audible pluck travels up through the bedrock first—unmistakably one of the three-hundred-meter sunken rings waking far down on a canyon terrace—and seconds later I tilt my amber-lidded eye over the lip to watch the refractive lens bend the clear air and lance a tree-thick white beam of compressed dust horizontally across the gulf. An hour later, the far abutment rings with the exact bearing of the chalk-banded ledge where the beam frayed and died, so our harvesters can chart a descent in three weeks when the accidental bounty of spores turns into thick parchment-lichens.

And sometimes the message is simply a launch: *A pale-plated one has stepped off the high shelf into the afternoon katabatic. Watch the vertical beads.*

When the chord dies back into the arch’s long tension-hum, I lift my jaw from the stone.

This is the part of my life that belongs only to me: the hundred paces across the deaf dolomite.

I rise, tuck my head behind my bony brow as a silent veil of quartz-flour whips across the ridge and turns the world opaque white for twenty seconds, and wait out the pressure-drop until my eardrums pop gently back. Then I cross the dead stone. Under my cupped horn-toes, the fractured chalk gives back nothing—no pulse, no distant scree-fall, no far-off voice. For those hundred paces, the message from thirty-five kilometers away has left the bedrock altogether. It exists nowhere in the world except inside my own hollow, mineral-strutted bones, held in the quiet arithmetic of my memory and the slow rhythm of my three-per-minute breath while the silent gale snaps against my keratin plates.

At the far side of the fault, the dark banded ironstone breaks the surface cleanly. I kneel, rest my heavy rod upright on the live grain, press my free palm flat to read the afternoon’s thermal pitch, and draw my notched horn bridge across the iron.

You cannot simply copy what came off the quartzite. Ironstone is denser, darker in tone; it eats high overtones and carries low intervals for leagues around blind corners. If I struck the exact same pattern I heard at the abutment, the kin-seams five kilometers inland would receive a blurred, clipped ghost of it. So I translate—widening the pauses, leaning heavier into the damped stops, letting the long bowed notes settle into the mineral’s own speed until the meaning rings clear and unhurried down the spine of our mountain.

Standing two paces away from me in the thin air, an outsider would hear nothing at all—only a faint, papery scrape swallowed instantly by the hiss of drifting grit. Down in the cliff-seams five kilometers away, someone pressing a cheek to the wall of a dissolution-tube hears the far continent speak as cleanly as if the abyss between us did not exist.

***

Inside the hollow of my chest, lashed tight beneath a flat pouch of scavenged glider-parchment so no gust can ever lift an edge, I carry a small stack of cleaved mica wafers.

Held flat in the hand, they look blank, translucent as dry water. Tilted to a low grazing sun, webs of needle-fine silver scratches leap into view.

Every elder keeps charts—acoustic fault-maps, the angles of deep frost-notches, tallies of kin across neighboring massifs. Mine started that way. Over the seasons at the abutment, though, the margins of my wafers have filled with a second, quieter record. Because I sit where the natural stone meets the ancient engineering, I have begun etching the correspondences no one asks me to relay.

I scratch the faint, hair-breadth shift in the filament-arch’s background hum when a massive static-front grinds across its center span. I mark the exact intervals between a sunken ring’s bedrock tremor and the arrival of its pale dust-lance against the opposite cliff. I keep a tally of the Crossers whose launches are bowed across to me from the far massif—a single short vertical needle-line when they step off the rock, crossed cleanly by a horizontal scratch when our own interior ironstone rings back that they have folded their wings safely on our shelves. Some vertical lines stay uncrossed. I do not rub them out. The mica holds them without grief or hurry, the way the stone holds everything.

I do not pretend to understand whoever left the pylons, the rings, the filament-arches, and the motionless black polyhedra hanging untouched in a hundred-knot gale. They built without regard for erosion, gravity, or us. Yet living beside their works teaches a certain unsentimental patience: indifference still has geometry, and anything with geometry can be listened to, mapped, and lived alongside without needing to be worshipped or feared.

***

There are only two places where my voice leaves the stone entirely, and they sit at opposite ends of scale.

The first comes once or twice a year, when the evening shadow of the great arch slips past a familiar notch on the canyon wall, and small parties from three separate massifs travel the knife-ridges to converge on the solitary slate-gray pylon on the high plateau.

Stepping across that invisible teardrop boundary never stops feeling strange. One pace out, quartz-flour stings your amber lids and the gale tugs relentlessly at every plate; one pace in, the wind ceases to exist. Twenty paces of interlocking pebble-pavement sit inside an unheated room of dead-still air while dust-curtains bend smoothly overhead without a flicker of turbulence.

Twelve or twenty of us sit cross-legged near the outer rim of the calm, well back from the matte blade that drinks vibration into nothingness. We trade quartz lenses for heavy shrub-resin, dried lichen-cakes for fresh mica wafers; young Crossers arrange to move between seams. And here, in the motionless thin air, we speak aloud—a dry, clicking, papery murmur, pitched high and clear, scarcely louder than cloth rubbing cloth.

For someone whose whole days are spent leaning into the solid, geological certainty of bedrock resonance, letting a voice float loose in empty air feels startlingly bare—unguarded, almost fragile. Yet it is here, one crisp autumn evening, that I finally sit three paces from the elder whose heavy, unhurried ironstone wrist I have translated across the deaf dolomite for nine seasons. Their shoulders gleam pale and faceted as river-worn quartz; their joints creak faintly when they shift. Hearing their voice unmoored from forty kilometers of braided metal and gneiss—hearing it arrive small, dry, and immediate through three paces of still air—quietly rearranges something inside me. In the stone, we sound monumental, permanent as the strata we speak through. Inside the pylon’s calm, you remember how thin-walled and light we actually are.

The second place is smaller still.

When dusk stretches razor-edged shadows whole leagues across the basins and the cooling shelves send fresh katabatic avalanches roaring silently down the fluted walls, I leave the abutment and climb down to my seam’s narrow slot. Inside, the rock is worn smooth as horn by generations of backs. We dust a pinch of fine gypsum flour into the dry pleats of our elbows and knees, tuck our tapping-rods into the crevices, and hang the tight, overlapping curtain of oiled glider-parchment across the throat of the entrance, weighting its lower hem with faceted pebbles.

Sealed off from the bone-dry abyss, the eight of us wedge close together in our body-fitted niches and let our breaths slow until we are drawing air in the same quiet rhythm—three, four times a minute—hoarding that faint, communal ghost of shared humidity that keeps our membranes supple until the pre-dawn climb.

In the dark behind the parchment curtain, I press the smooth plate of my forehead lightly against a seam-mate’s, or lay three flattened fingers along the ridge of a collarbone, and hum low in my ribbed throat.

There is no forty-kilometer span to cross here, no deaf fault to bridge, no translation between quartzite and ironstone. The warmth passes directly from hollow bone to hollow bone, untouched by whatever silent storm is tearing across the cliffs three paces outside the slot. After a long afternoon of listening to the vastness of the crust, it is a mercy to let the whole world shrink to the distance of a single touch.

***

I know how it ends, too, and there is no weight in thinking of it.

Seasons from now, when the silica and salts have done their slow final work—when my plates are too thick and faceted to flex easily, my breaths stretching to a full minute, two minutes, then stopping in the quiet of the sleeping-crack—my seam will not look for soil that does not exist. They will wait for a brief calm between fronts, carry my light, dry husk up past the deaf dolomite, and wedge me carefully upright inside a sun-facing wind-flute overlooking the great filament-arch, locking my plates deep into the gneiss so even the fiercest katabatic plunge cannot tear me loose.

Nothing rots in the dry cold. Season by season, the silent gales will scour away the parchment membranes and polish the hollow ceramic bones to pale glass. Decade by decade, the airborne quartz will wear my plates thin, facet them smooth, and drop them piece by piece to lock flush among the dolomite pebbles of the ledge—and lift the rest as fine pale mineral flour to join the bright ribbons streaming across the clear, empty sky I once glided through.

And a few paces away on the sunlit quartzite buttress, someone younger—newly heavy, their spars just passed on—will press a jawbone flat to the ancient grain, close their amber lids against the glare, and catch three clean, deliberate harmonics ringing cold and clear across the gulf before the night winds rise.
