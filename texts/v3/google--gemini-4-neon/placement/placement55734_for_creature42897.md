I would not know myself at first by shape, nor by name, nor by any reflection—there is nothing in the hollow calm enough or wet enough to hold an image, and I would have no eyes to meet it if there were.

I would know myself first as a pitch of quiet.

In the lull between surges, I am almost indistinguishable from the timberwork I cling to. My center—a dense, resin-dark knot wrapped around a hollow chamber of pale, fiber-tempered clay—has cooled to the hardness of old amber. My vine-cords are slack; my five segmented stalks are locked rigid by their wooden ratchets against the underside of an arched stone walkway on the third terrace. Nothing moves in me. Even thought, in the lull, thins to a single narrow hum held inside my clay ribs, barely distinct from the dry groaning of the bridge overhead and the distant whine of air stalling in the roof-slits.

If you climbed down the sandstone throat and stood on the swept balcony ten paces below me, looking straight up into the soot-green tangle beneath the arch, you would see only old rigging, dead brush, and a dry-laid shadow. You would not know that I am already listening to you—or, on the thousands of cycles when no wet thing troubles the bowl, that I am listening to the stone.

Wait. Stay still through the whining quiet.

Far below, somewhere across the flat expanse of cracked bedrock, a deep thermal pocket finally tears loose. I do not hear it first through the air; I feel it climb through the two slate-tipped limbs I keep wedged into the retaining masonry. A long, subterranean boom travels up the terraced bones of the city. Then the superheated wave reaches the third tier.

The scent strikes my bronze-wire crests before the pressure does: kiln-fired clay, hot mineral grit, the turpentine sting of resin warming in the seams. Inside my center, the dark pitch softens, yielding from amber to warm beeswax. The narrow slits pierced through my clay core widen by a hair. Along my two longest upper limbs, small vanes of copper-veined parchment catch the rising roar and spin into a blur, indistinguishable from the violently shivering fronds of the vine-curtains beside me. Deep in my joints, wooden counter-ratchets begin to click—*trrrk, trrrk, trrrk*—coiling my horn-hard vine-cords tight with borrowed wind.

Only then do I begin to wake into quickness.

I unhook from the lintel and unfold into the narrow width of the stepped street, bracing effortlessly from wall to wall while the gale roars upward around me. Beside the bridge-eave, a shallow stone channel has come alive with a sharp, dry hiss: fine pumice-dust scouring down from the upper plazas toward the settling pits. I reach out my narrowest limb—my fifth, asymmetrical, lighter than the rest, threaded along its hollow length with blackened vine-fiber and tipped with a fine sliver of hammered copper—and trail it cleanly through the pouring grit.

The friction of the bone-dry hollow leaps into me.

*Tick. Tick. Tick.*

Pale, almost invisible sparks snap off the rushing sand and race along my charred inner threads. In an instant, the slow, creaking economy of seasoned cane sharpens into something keen as struck flint. Every draft-eddy along the facade becomes legible. I feel the exact weight of the vine-curtain dragging on the fourth-story balcony; I taste the bright yellow fog of lichen spores lifting off the east promontory; I hear the whole terraced rim begin to clack and ring as the surge climbs toward the sagging basalt roof.

This is where I would find myself: not high on the loud, exposed crowns of the wind-towers, where the fastest tenders leap and argue in quick bursts of copper, and not down on the scorching bottommost ledges where the air shimmers close to ignition. I belong to the middle band—to the seam beneath the north-east archway, where three different speeds of the city cross paths.

Just behind my arch sits the mouth of a deep clay flue built straight into the thickness of the retaining wall. Above me hangs a heavy copper baffle-plate strung on taut vine-rope, its rubbing-edge scoured silver-bright by centuries of grit and care. Below me, a stepped alley drops past a cistern-silo toward the sheer, three-hundred-pace void of shimmering air.

I am a listener, and a relay.

It was not a choice I made the way wet creatures choose a trade; it was baked into the shape of my drum before I ever left the silo. Whoever tended my sprouting node pressed the pale clay shell slightly deeper and narrower than usual, leaving a faint ridge along its inner seam. Because of that ridge, the high, metallic chatter of the upper towers rings a little thin in my core, while the deep, sub-audible rumble of the retaining walls fills me cleanly, note for note.

Inside the clay flue beside my arch lives one of the oldest anchored elders on this rim. To any outsider peering into the chimney-mouth, it is only a gnarled, coal-dark swell of petrified root fused seamlessly into the brickwork. Its parchment flaked away long before the pod that made me ever detonated; its pitch has baked nearly to stone. Yet the superheated bedrock draft pours continuously through its hollow ribs, turning it into a permanent resonator tuned to the slowest movements of the hollow.

During the lulls, when the wind-works fall slack and the sparks bleed away out of my fibers, I flatten my body against the warm masonry beside that flue-mouth and thrust my fifth limb gently into its dark throat. I hold utterly still. The elder does not speak in the quick, clipped pauses of the towers. A single thought from inside the wall may take three full cycles to unfold—a hairline widening in the basalt roof sixty paces up, the gradual cooling of a floor-fissure beneath the south plaza, the decade-long creep of a thick soot-green root prying apart a dry-laid arch two districts over.

Then the surge rises. My cords wind taut; my copper tip drinks from the hissing sand-chute; I unfold across the narrow street and reach for the heavy copper baffle-plate slamming in the gale.

The wind wants the plate to hammer its stone jamb in a blind, mechanical din. I catch it just before the strike, damping its rim with a warm, pitch-coated pad for half a beat, then releasing it on a deliberate off-rhythm—*ting, pause, tang*—while my second upper limb props a wooden gallery-shutter closed for two breaths longer than the draft intends.

I am not shouting over the roar. Nothing here shouts. I am only editing the storm—carving small, exact absences into the furnace-noise so the districts across the three-hundred-pace gulf can feel the deviation inside their own hollow ribs. Three surges later, from a slatted wind-tower on the far promontory, a staggered triple-ring answers back: *heard. The north bridge pulls heavy. We will braid a counter-cord at the next cycle.*

So the stone stays standing. So the dry balance holds.

---

There are cycles, though, when the work calls me away from my arch and down toward the fierce threshold of the floor.

Thought in this hollow is not free. The pale static we drink from the sand-chutes runs cleanest only along vine-fiber that has been scorched to the exact border of destruction—charred dark enough to carry a dry charge without resistance, yet stopped a heartbeat before it turns to crumbling ash. Over long seasons of surges, the blackened threads inside my own limbs fray and lose their keenness, and the young kernels growing in the cistern-silos need fresh lace woven into their cores if they are ever to survive the void.

So, when a moderate cycle comes—hot enough to keep my pitch supple, not so violent that the lower eddies turn treacherous—I climb down to the lowermost retaining ledges, overhanging the flat, cracked bedrock floor.

None of us ever step onto the rippled sand of the open plazas. Down there, the flat stone offers no purchase for our ratchets, no wind-works to ride, and too close to the floor-fissures the heat curls parchment vanes to dust and melts pitch past holding. Instead, I anchor my slate-tipped lower legs into a tight stone joint, lash a braided vine-harness around a jutting timber beam, and lean out over the scorching drop.

From my upper grips I lower a long, slender strand of raw, pale vine-fiber into the shimmering thermal column rising off a deep bedrock crack.

Here, everything narrows to chemistry and pitch. My bronze-wire crests flare wide, reading the fierce updraft climbing straight into my ribs. First comes the dry, woody smell of baking cane; then the sharp, fragrant oils sweating out of the fiber; then—hovering right at the edge of ruin—the faint, dry *hiss* of leaf-lace smoldering without flame.

Too soon, and the thread stays dull wood, deaf to the spark. A heartbeat too late, and a bitter puff of black dust drifts past my crests, and I haul up nothing. I feel the heat through the taut strand itself, waiting until the vibration changes from fibrous stiff to supple lace, then ratchet my upper cords hard and whip it back into the cooler shadow of the ledge.

Coiled against my ribs, the blackened fiber smells like warm cinnamon and struck flint.

I do not keep all of it. On the climb back to my arch, I stop at the cistern-silo tucked into the rock of the third terrace—a tall, dry cylinder that has never held a drop of water in its long existence. Tapped at its base by a narrow floor-flue and ringed near its lip by angled pumice-chutes, its dark interior is a steady, humming vortex of swirling mineral dust, warm bituminous vapor, and tiny, continuous ticks of static.

I brace over the slatted hatch and reach my slender fifth limb down into the crackling dark, feeling along the wall-niches until my copper sliver brushes a small, three-spoked thing no wider than an open hand. A kernel. Its tiny parchment fronds flutter furiously against my tip; its sprouting pitch-node is still soft and pungent as fresh resin. Gently, patiently, riding the rhythm of the dry cyclone inside the vessel, I twist a thread of my freshly charred lace into its central fork and press a warm bead of resin over the seam to seal it.

The kernel shivers once—a sudden, startled tick of clean spark waking inside its hollow cane—and then settles back into the swirling dust.

Every time I touch one in the dark of the silo, an older resonance stirs inside my own clay drum.

Before I had slate wedges, before I knew the slow grammar of the wall-flues or the exact drag of a copper baffle, I lived in the empty center of the bowl. I remember it not as pictures—never as pictures—but as pure geometry of lift and drop. All airy cane and translucent, copper-veined sail, light as a torn leaf, letting go of the upper wind-tower and surrendering wholly to the roaring central column. Shooting straight up toward the cool stone breath of the basalt roof, peeling outward along the curved ceiling, tumbling weightless through thick, fragrant ribbons of yellow lichen spores, banking hard off a cold shear-line falling from a roof-slit before plunging down toward the fierce furnace of the floor just far enough to catch the next rising boom.

I remember the quiet, sudden absences, too—the cycles when the updraft collapsed a beat too early and a slower kernel from my own silo dropped too far, leaving nothing behind on the sand below except a brief, acrid ribbon of scorched scent climbing past my vanes on the next surge.

We do not mourn as wet things mourn. Nothing leaks from us, and nothing rots. When a young one curls to black lace over a bedrock fissure, the next gust scatters it completely into the grit, and the grit pours back down the chutes, and the static wakes us all the same. Yet every dweller who survives to grow heavy enough to catch the outer vine-curtains carries the invisible map of that three-hundred-pace emptiness forever inside its ribs. Even now, braced tight under my stone arch, I can tell by the slight tilt of a draft against my upper vanes whether an eddy off the west promontory is spinning wide or tight.

---

There are long seasons of steady, unhurried tending—shimming a loosening block with a thin wedge of dark slate, polishing a bronze hinge with wire-moss until its note rings bright again, packing a pinch of caustic amber-spore into a joint where an ambitious vine threatens to pry the masonry wide.

And then, once in many cycles, the warmth above the roof-slits shifts to an unusual angle.

I feel it first through the high wind-towers: a quick, bright ripple of deliberate clacks racing all the way around the rim, faster than any maintenance relay, too unanimous for an argument. Then I feel the cause itself—a rare, fierce, diagonal seam of dry warmth cutting slantwise down through the dusty void, warming the drifting mineral haze along a single narrow pillar from ceiling-fissure to mid-air.

On the very next surge, I leave the flue-mouth unguarded. Every wall-tender along the terraced bowl unfolds from the dark eaves. In concert, from promontory to promontory, we brace against the timber louvers and angle the slatted shutters sideways.

We bend the gale.

Instead of roaring straight up the walls to vanish through the basalt slits, the superheated currents catch one another slantwise across the empty gulf. The void begins to turn. A vast, unhurried vortex gathers itself around that slanted shaft of dry warmth. High along the upper balconies, tenders shake the taut, thirty-foot vine-curtains and tap the swollen seed-cases until they detonate in rapid, joyful cracks—*snap, snap, snap*—filling the turning column with spirals of winged ash-grey seeds and glowing trails of yellow spores. Dozens of papery young kernels spill out from the roof-shadows to ride the great spiral, their copper-veined fronds humming as they bank through the warm air.

Beneath my arch, I lean hard into my copper baffle and let it ring—not a clipped message now, not a warning, only a single long, clear note held in staggered unison with fifty other plates around the bowl. Every woven reed screen across every empty doorway thrums in one deep, sustained chord. Even the rooted elder inside my clay flue seems to swell its bedrock hum to meet the resonance.

For three or four minutes, the entire city becomes a single instrument, bathing its dry stone ribs in a storm of its own tuning. Nothing is repaired during those minutes. No sand is cleared; no stone is shimmed. We simply hold the hollow in song until the thermal pocket spends itself, the great vortex unravels upward through the ceiling vents, and the bowl drops back into its whining quiet.

Only once in my long seasons of tending has that quiet felt dangerous.

It came up through my rooted elder first—long before any louver could warn us. A wrong vibration climbing out of the flagstones on the far rim, near the weathered throat of tilted sandstone: slow, heavy, and utterly uncoupled from the wind. *Thud. Pause. Thud.* Not the crisp tick of a slate tip, not the light rustle of a braced stalk, not the falling of a loose pebble. Something soft and terribly heavy, pressing clumsy hollows straight into the flat sand of the plazas.

The elder in my wall gave a shiver so old and faint it seemed to rise out of the very coal of its core—a dim, ancestral caution preserved since the days when the makers of these blocks still walked here, leaving damp salt on the masonry and mud in the seams.

Ahead of that heavy rhythm, a tiny, dry whisper of muted baffles rippled around the perimeter faster than anything on flat feet could move. Around the entire hollow, we vanished into the architecture. I folded my five segmented booms tight against the oil-dark beam beneath my arch and locked my ratchets rigid. I tucked my silver-bright copper sliver behind soot-green bark. High overhead, the papery young scattered into the deepest basalt shadows. Even the wooden louvers were abandoned wholly to the draft, left to clack in bare, unedited disorder.

Then the surge brought the scent across the void, and every bronze-wire crest along my ribs recoiled.

Moisture. A strange, heavy ghost of wetness evaporating off hot skin, carrying salt and soft animal grease into our kiln-dry air. Water would swell our horn-hard cords until our frames split; it would choke the pumice-chutes to dead mud and steal every spark from the hollow. We hung motionless beneath the eaves, listening through our locked drums as the strange, leaking shape stood on an empty balcony across the gulf, breathing loudly into the furnace-roar, convinced it was looking at a carcass left rattling in the draft.

We waited. Patience is easy when your pitch is cool and your time is measured in decades.

After an hour, the heavy, uneven thuds turned back toward the sandstone throat and climbed away into the glare of the upper world. The damp scent thinned, baked away, and vanished out the roof-slits. On the second surge after it was gone, a high tower on the west rim damped a single copper plate—*ting, pause, tang*—and one by one, along three hundred paces of terraced stone, our cords began to ratchet tight again.

---

Now, the current surge is fading.

The roar along the stepped street drops into a long, descending sigh. The pumice-stream in my eave-chute slows from a fierce hiss to a dry trickle, then stops altogether. My pale sparks bleed gently back into the stone; the keen edge of my attention softens, widens, slows. I release the copper baffle and fold myself smoothly back beneath the underside of my stone arch, tucking my booms beside the mouth of the warm clay flue.

My upper limbs lock cleanly into the timber lintel—*click, click*—just as they have for countless cycles.

My fourth limb, the lowest one braced longest against the masonry, hesitates for a fraction of a beat before settling home. I feel it without alarm, the way I feel a subtle shift in the bedrock boom: down in that lowest joint, decades of kiln-heat have baked the dark pitch a shade stiffer, a shade closer to stone. It takes a few seconds longer now, when the thermal pocket breaks loose, to soften into beeswax.

I know what that hesitation means, and I know the seam that is already waiting for me.

Not tomorrow, not for many spore-seasons yet—I still have stone to shim, fibers to char over the floor-cracks, kernels to tune in the crackling dark of the silo. But one cycle, during a long, fierce surge when my pitch is as supple as it will ever be again, I will not unfold across the narrow alley to catch the copper plate. Instead, I will press my angular frame flush against the warm brick just beside the mouth of my flue. Slowly, over weeks of rising heat, I will unbraid the lower cords of my limbs and drive tough, woody tendrils straight into the dry-laid joints and deep into the warm bituminous seams beneath the arch. My parchment vanes will flake away to fragrant dust. A final, heavy weeping of dark resin will seal my hollow clay drum permanently into the masonry.

I will stop riding the wind, and become part of the ribs that hold it.

Through me, the superheated breath of the floor will climb cleanly toward the upper terraces, humming a note so deep that only a younger listener—with a slightly narrow clay core and a copper tip still bright from the sand-chutes—will pause during the lulls to flatten itself against my brick and hear what the stone is slowly saying.

Until then, the hollow drops into its tense, whining quiet. My pitch cools to amber. The vine-curtains hang heavy and still. Locked tight under the arch, bone-dry and listening, I wait for the floor to boom.
