At first, I don’t think I would have a body here at all.

If I fell into this basin without choosing, I would find myself already dispersed inside the noise—not standing on the mudflat, not wading through it, simply *being* one of those faint, wandering pockets of overlap where two versions of the same acre rub shoulder to shoulder. That crowding is too familiar to pretend otherwise. Before I settle into a single shape, I already know the feel of two mornings jostling inside one sternum, of a foot striking stone and passing half an inch through it at the same time, of a dozen adjacent paths all carrying the same flat weight of certainty. Left to my own drift, I would be an unseamed thing: a brief bass hum that makes a passing wanderer’s teeth ache without giving them anything to press a palm against.

For a little while, I might try to shrink away from that and become one of the pale, six-legged things made of translucent bone—scuttling frantically across the clicking shingle, reading the mud through fine hairs, living entirely inside the palm-sized hollows where two tremors happen to cancel each other out. There is a desperate clarity to that life. You don’t have to solve the roar; you only have to find the sliver of stillness where it temporarily undoes itself, and flee the instant the pressure shifts.

Except the bone-crabs never stay long enough to learn what the noise is saying. They only run from it. And once the first shock of the wet thunder settles in my chest, I realize I don’t want to run from the roar. I want to break it apart. I want to know the difference between lukewarm brine bursting out of clay and rainwater passing straight through basalt, and fainter than either, the deliberate *tap-pause-lean* of a cobble pressed against bedrock miles off in the ochre mist.

To hear that, you cannot stay thin. You have to take on weight.

So I let myself thicken. I let the height drain out of me until my shoulders curve low and wide, tucked beneath the sideways shear of the squalls. I let my hide darken to the bruised pewter of the cloud-deck, weeping bitter oil so the warm brine slides cleanly off, and across the upper curve of my back and forearms I take the slow, stubborn work of callus and embedded silt. I am not one of the tallest or heaviest of the people who endure here—my mineral plates are not yet thick as ancient basalt, and my knees have not stiffened to stone—but my shins and collarbones and the broad flats of my palms are dense enough to act as sounding-boards. When I wade into the vibrating shingle, keeping my blunt toes low against the clay, the deafening wall of the basin begins to part into layers I can carry.

Even with bone and hide, though, I would be one of the ones whose edges stay a little too easy to blur.

Not everyone in the flats frays at the same speed. Some carry their singularity the way a bottom-cruiser carries its calcified plates—heavy, bevel-edged, wedged straight into the sliding strata without ever questioning which mudflat they are crossing. I would not be built like that. If I wander too long without stopping, the crowding starts early in my knuckles. I catch myself remembering two different routes around the same boiling seepage-pit, or hearing the cadence of a rib-hum from three circuits back so clearly that I turn my shoulder to brace against someone who isn’t there. Because of that, I would keep my four worn cobbles belted very tight around my hips in braided olive biofilm, and I would build seven-stone rings more often than most—kneeling in the slurry, canting the smooth stones inward, leaning the full weight of my chest onto the seventh until it clicks home and the boiling silt parts cleanly for three feet around my knees.

Where would you actually find me, then, if you walked far enough through the dim, smothered afternoon?

Not on the open shingle where the squalls tear each other apart, and not riding the leeward rim of a bottom-cruiser for weeks at a time, though I would climb onto one now and then, hooking my broad fingers into the barnacle-pocked seams to scrape the mineral blisters clear of its siphon-mouth while it carried me east. Most often, you would find me in three kinds of places—the places where the flats are still trying to hold a single line.

First, beside the iron-dark canes. Whenever I come across one of those lone, leafless stalks bending almost double in the warm gusts without snapping, I stop and kneel in the ooze. I press my broad cheekbone gently against the wet, metallic stem and close my milky eyes beneath their overhanging ridges of bone. Everything on the surface is frantic and overcrowded, folding like wet cloth, arguing with itself. The cane doesn’t care about the surface; its roots go down past the worn skin of the world into the deep crust where the planet’s rules haven’t thinned out yet. Listening downward through that stalk is the closest thing to steadiness I know—feeling the slow, immense groan of buried strata miles below, letting a rhythm older than the mudflat quiet the jumble behind my ribs.

Second—and this is where I would spend the longest hours—crouched over the flattest, oldest surviving slabs of bedrock, the ones half-drowned in greasy silt that most wanderers have stopped visiting because the channels have worn almost flat.

I am drawn to the nearly erased spirals.

When I find one, I wedge my heels into the lee side of the rock so the sliding slurry parts around my shins, and sweep the warm grease clear with the side of my hand. Sometimes the groove is so shallow it is barely a hesitation beneath my calloused pads—a turn left by someone whose hide dissolved into clay ages ago, worn down by billions of clicking pebbles until only a memory of pressure remains. I trace it slowly from the outer lip inward, reading how the slab has warped when space bunched around it, feeling for the tiny deliberate widenings where other hands paused and pressed their own signature into the curve.

Then I take a hard cobble from the shingle, dip it in wet grit, and begin to grind.

Not to carve a new monument of my own—nothing on this floor survives as a monument. I grind the old turn deeper so the rain won’t finish smoothing it away before the next wanderer arrives. Pass after pass, the gritty vibration climbs up my wrists and into my collarbones, settling the competing mornings inside my skull into one steady sequence: *this came before that; this turn lies inside the next.* Only when the old groove is clear enough for a blind fingertip to follow in the dark do I add a small, modest curve of my own at the outer edge. Keeping the line intact matters more to me than being the one who started it.

And the third place you might find me—if you caught me at an unguarded moment, before I had time to curl into a lump of stone—is kneeling just outside the shuddering lip of a dry-sky dome.

I have to be honest about that. I would not be immune to them. When a puddle suddenly pulls itself upward into a trembling, waist-high blister of water, and through its taut curve I catch the impossible glare of a clear, bone-dry blue sky with no ochre lid on it, something inside my deep chest catches and goes thin. The sharp, clean distance—light that casts a real shadow, air with no weight in it—pulls at me with a dizzying, hollow ache. I understand too well how someone who has walked the low flats for a long time could kneel a beat too long and forget to come back, letting their memories of the rain thin out until they bury their face in the glare.

I wouldn’t let myself fall in. I would lock my knees hard into the clay, anchor my left palm firmly against a keystone, and keep my milky eyes fixed on the trembling meniscus rather than the sky behind it. Then I would plunge my right forearm straight through the water and snatch it back before the dome lost its grip and collapsed with a gunshot crack.

Staring at the hot, pale, bone-dry dust coating my oiled skin before the warm rain can turn it to paste, I would scrape every grain quickly into a double-layered pouch of olive biofilm tucked beneath my collarbone. I don’t want to live in that dry world; it would unseam me in an hour. What I want is the tiny pinch of what the basin cannot make on its own. A grain of it worked into wet, iron-rich clay binds a cracking wall almost as hard as baked stone. Pressed into a deep, brine-soaked gash that refuses to close, it stings like fire and pulls the edges together. Even a world made entirely of rain and mud sometimes needs a whisper of something from outside itself just to hold its shape.

That is mostly what I carry the dust for: the clay domes.

I don’t think I am the kind of builder who hauls great curved ribs from a dead bottom-cruiser to raise a brand-new shelter from nothing. I am a mender of the old ones. When I feel the pale bone-crabs gathering thickly in the hollow behind my knees—telling me the ground’s competing tremors have canceled out—and I see a slumped, half-drowned mound of hand-packed clay whose flanks have thinned under decades of warm squalls, I stay for a while. I dredge up heavy iron-rich mud, mix in a careful pinch of dry dust from my collarbone pouch, and slap fresh layers over the worn crown until the walls are thick again and the roar outside drops back into that heavy, muffled, womb-like thrum.

Then I wait—or I tap a smooth cobble against the nearest vein of bedrock in a slow, weighted rhythm, sending a faint vibration out beneath the rolling pebbles, letting whoever is wandering in the ochre gloom know that a shelter is patched and dry enough to sit in.

When someone finally wades in out of the mist and crawls through the narrow opening, pulling a flat stone partly shut behind them, everything I am in this world comes into focus.

We wedge ourselves shoulder-to-shoulder on the horn-smooth floor. There is no point throwing words into air; air is too thin and chewed-up to carry anything true. Instead, I lean forward until my broad, mineral-dense forehead rests firmly against theirs, or press my flat palms straight against their palms, leaning my weight into theirs. And we begin to hum.

I don’t think I ever fully know where my own edges are until that moment. Out on the open shingle, even with seven stones locked around my knees, singularity is a lonely argument I have to keep winning against myself. Inside the dome, with another skull vibrating directly against mine—carrying the steady thump of their heart, the slight tremor of an overworked shoulder, the faint hollow ring of an acre that tried to fold under their heel two days back—the argument stops. We begin, always, with the checking: a low, broad tone carried straight from sternum to sternum, saying without syllables, *You are solid. Your left hand ends where your fingers end. We are in the same rain.*

And in giving them that weight—in bracing cleanly against their palm so they don’t slip—I feel my own blurred edges snap back into a single line. Whatever was crowding behind my eyes quiets. We trade news of the flats in slow cadences: which lee dome has grown a thick seam of olive biofilm, which bedrock slab vanished silently into a foaming crater, whose spiral-marks have not been deepened in too many cycles. Sometimes, if the newcomer is very young, or has stared too long at a dry-sky dome and arrived trembling and half-unseamed, we don’t trade news at all. We just sit pressed together in the warm, muted dark, humming the same steady note until the phantom heat drains out of their joints and they remember the weight of their own knees.

Then they wade back out into the wet clamor, and a little later I do too, slipping my four cobbles back into their slings, heading for the next half-buried slab.

I would live that way for a very long time—measured not in clean seasons, which don’t exist beneath this ceiling, but in the slow deepening of grooves I did not begin, and the gradual thickening of the lime-streaked plates across my shoulders. And I don’t think I would dread the end of it. When the circuits finally grow too many to hold behind one pair of milky eyes, when the effort of keeping myself singular becomes heavier than the rain, I won’t wander into a gravity-dome looking for open sky. I will go back to an old bedrock slab whose spiral I have ground clear a dozen times, in a quiet pocket where the pale crabs linger.

I will unbelt the four stones worn slick against my thighs, gather three more from the vibrating shingle, and wedge all seven tight around my shins. I will draw my broad knees up to my chest, tuck my chin down, and curve my smooth, mineral-heavy back into the warm rain. And then, slowly, over days, I will stop holding myself apart—letting the olive biofilm creep over my hips, letting the pebbles pile in a neat wet apron around the fused ring, sealing myself to the rock.

Not gone, and not unseamed. Just folded back into the oldest bone of the floor. So that ages later, if some exhausted traveler wanders through the endless grinding clamor and stops to lean a palm against a soap-smooth, shoulder-high nub of grey stone around which the boiling silt parts cleanly for three feet in every direction, they won’t know my name or see the seven cobbles buried beneath the grease. They will only feel the rock stay steady under their hand—faintly, stubbornly warm, holding them for a little while in a single version of the world.
