At first, my instinct is to stand on the bolted steel catwalk.

That is the reflex of someone used to arriving from outside: stay dry, keep your footing above the steam, take the measure of the room. From the grate I could count the seconds of the piston’s climb, watch the fogged needle tremble near its red line, trace the moss-wrapped cables back to the circular bore in the cliff and wonder what sits in the cold dark at the other end of the line. I could try to be the mind that reads the whole engine-room at once.

Except the catwalk is the loudest, blindest place in the hollow. My footsteps would ring too sharp through the iron. My breathing would be too quick for a pocket that takes two minutes to draw a single breath. Worse, standing upright in the middle of the steam, I would miss almost everything that actually lives here; I would mistake the tenants for slag and leave convinced the basin belonged only to rust and runoff.

So I step off the grate—not physically, because there is nowhere dry to step, but in the way attention shifts when it stops trying to oversee a place and starts trying to belong to it.

For a while I test the higher ledges. There is a fierce, quiet pull toward the middle resident, the climber—to be broad-shouldered and useful without needing a concept of usefulness, to live inside the neat rhythm of appetite and repair, worrying chalk plugs out of brass valves and rasping the dark oil-bloom from the gleaming steel. And there is an even deeper pull toward the oldest one: to carry decades of lime and copper-blue on your own back like a geological record of where you have slept, to grow a small garden of ferns along your spine, and at night to lay a soft palm against the wet armor of the power cables just to feel the current pouring down into the mountain’s belly.

I stay with that image for a long time. To become, by slow degrees, part of the architecture of the thing you kept alive—there is a gravity in that which feels almost like peace.

Yet when I actually look for myself in this cleft, I don’t find a body already heavy with chalk and bronze. I don’t find knuckles hardened from years of knocking phrases through the copper. Whatever I am here, I haven’t crusted over yet.

I keep looking lower—past the steel tendons of the pressure vessel, beneath the dripping curtains of aerial roots, down under the sweating condenser manifolds where the runoff collects in the deepest, quietest stone pans.

That is where I find myself. Not above the water, and not yet part of the stone.

I am one of the waiting ones.

***

Clinging upside-down to the flat belly of a submerged basalt slab, barely the length of a thumb, I hold still in water that never gets colder than a bath and never boils.

Up above, the cleft is deafening, a hard-walled box crammed with steam hiss and slapping drops and the iron clatter of the latch. Down here, beneath six inches of mineral-heavy water, all of that violence softens into architecture. I have no ears, only a thin, pale skin that lets the whole hollow pass right through me. The hum of the oil-cooled transformers isn’t a noise at all; it is simply the steady grain of the rock I press my soft pads against, as constant as gravity.

And the two-minute cycle of the pumps is my weather.

I feel it gather in the basalt under my belly—a long, slow tightening that climbs and climbs until the water over my back shivers in a faint, fine pattern. Then comes the *clank*, muffled by water into a clean, heavy thud that shakes a tiny dusting of chalk grit down from the ceiling of my stone. The pressure slackens. A warm exhalation rolls across the surface of the pan, blurring the silver skin above me with fresh ripples, and then the long climb begins again. I have measured years this way without ever counting one.

I know the cleft not by sight—the world beyond my pan is only shifting bands of blurred emerald and pale gold when the noon shaft cuts straight down through the mist—but by chemistry and weight.

Every drop that runs off the machines eventually finds its way down into our catch-basins, and water remembers everything it has touched. I taste the basin in solution. On days when the middle one hauls itself up into the copper manifolds and worries a thick crust loose from a flange, the runoff turns briefly bright and metallic, carrying microscopic flakes of verdigris that drift past my nose like green snow. When it polishes the grease-dark piston collar under the overhang, a faint, earthy bitterness trails down into the pool an hour later. I know the youngest resident by the sudden, nervous *tink-tink-tink* that sometimes travels down through the rock from the rusted chain hoist overhead—erratic, too quick, almost comical against the slow drone of the coils—followed a beat later by the elder’s flat, patient double-knock through the pipes.

Sometimes I think I remember when that youngest one lived two stones away from me.

I don’t have the kind of memory that keeps names or faces, only a residue of rhythms. I remember a season—twenty years ago, maybe thirty—when the water grew subtly sweeter with ungrazed iron-slime, and one long, familiar heaviness vanished from the concrete plinths above. For weeks the floor felt strangely light on one side. Then, under a neighboring slab of basalt, a pale throat I had watched pulsing in unison with mine for a decade began to beat faster. Its jaw widened in the dim green light. The tips of its translucent toes darkened with iron. One misty morning, when the air above the pan was thick and calm, it let go of the stone, broke the silver skin of bubbles, and climbed out onto the razor edge. I watched its shadow drag away through the ferns, and a few seasons later the erratic tapping began on the chain hoist.

I didn’t follow. My throat stayed slow.

There is no envy in staying under the stone, and no impatience. You cannot live in a place that builds itself by fractions of a millimeter a year and carry hurry in your ribs. Being small and clear has its own quiet fullness. Up there, everything that survives must thicken; the mist paints you in lime and rust until your joints lock and your moss-covered back merges seamlessly with the floor. Down here, I am completely permeable. You could hold me up to a shaft of high-altitude light and see the dark, unhurried pulse moving through my chest. I have no territory to defend, no crust to maintain, no weight to haul over the lips of the pans. I only have my narrow pocket of warm dark, the thin yellow carpet of algae along the rock, and an attention that has nowhere else to be.

Once in a while, the mountain reminds us how thin the roof of our world is.

A high wind tears across the razor-spine hard enough to shear the steam blanket straight off the hollow. Even underwater I feel the sudden disorder: the surface of the pan choppy and chilled, the chain hoist banging in a wild, broken clamor against the rock ribs, the whole careful cadence of the cleft scrambled.

Those are the hours when the three heavy shapes above slide down into the water with us.

One by one they break the surface and settle into the simmering floor of the basin—the youngest tucking its pale, thinly frosted body tight beneath the lip of the condenser; the middle one folding its long, chisel-nailed limbs under its ochre ridges; the oldest lowering its immense, terraced bulk with the slowness of a sinking plinth until only the emerald ferns along its spine break the air. Suddenly the dim water outside my stone is crowded with warm, living boulders. The runoff stops mattering; the broken clangor overhead fades behind the nearness of their ribs. Sometimes, when the oldest one settles close enough that its overhanging eaves shadow my rock, I unhook my pale toes and drift out into the sheltered pocket beneath its flanks, grazing gently along the soft, pleated skin near its gill-frills while its great pulse thrums inches from my face.

In those storms, the division between us vanishes. They are the armored roof of the basin; I am what waits underneath it, unhardened, holding the shape of what will come next.

And on clear, windless nights, when the sky-slot overhead turns black and sharp and the mist thins away, I sometimes feel a change so slight I might have missed it in my first decade here. The oldest one drags itself across the damp concrete toward the back wall, where the armored cables lift off their porcelain insulators and disappear into the dry, cold bore. When it lays its wide palm flat against the wet metal, a tiny, almost imperceptible warmth enters the floor’s deep drone—the sound of living flesh bridging stone and current. I don’t know what a tunnel is, or where the power goes, or what far-down stillness drinks the heat we keep alive. I only press my throat against the wet basalt and feel the answer humming back up through the mountain, steady as a tide.

Lately, the oldest one stays in the pans a little longer each season. Its journeys from one simmering hollow to the next stretch across more and more strokes of the pump. Along the seam where its shell meets its shoulders, the bone-white chalk has grown noticeably thicker, flaking faintly into the water when it shifts.

A silence is coming—not tomorrow, maybe not for five years, maybe not for ten, because nothing in this cathedral rushes its own ending. Somewhere down the line, the heaviest rhythm in the cleft will wedge itself between the steel tendons of the pressure vessel, tuck its pale limbs beneath its eaves, and stop knocking on the copper.

When that happens, the water will tell us first. The runoff will thicken and sweeten with iron-film along an ungrazed stretch of pipe. One of us under the stones will feel the floor go quiet, feel its throat begin to quicken, and start the slow work of growing heavy enough to climb out into the steam and the hard shafts of light.

Maybe this time it will be me. Maybe it will be the swimmer clinging three stones away, and I will stay translucent for another thirty years, grazing algae, tasting copper, listening to the piston climb and throw its latch and sigh across the moss.

Either way, I am already where I belong: tucked beneath the weight of the engine-room, small and clear in the lukewarm dark, keeping the basin’s patience until the silence calls my name.
