I would not begin inside the hush, where the lodges sit warm above the subterranean sap-mains and the children roll dry char-pebbles toward the silent rim to see where the noise catches them. Nor would I begin in the great xylem factory halls, where the presses thunder half a second ahead of their own weight and the air tastes of hot, sweet resin.

I would find myself halfway between them—stopped on the pale burl curb of a forty-yard boulevard, three miles east of the northwestern fermentation domes, listening for a silence no wider than a doorway.

It is not a proper hush. Nobody has ringed it with crushed white char or sunk a dome into the turf beside it. It is only a three-pace seam of accidental quiet along the base of a humming transformer-bole, where the four-second ricochet off an invisible northern spur collides cleanly with the wash from the southern wind-dynamos and cancels itself down to a dull, bearable surf. You can miss it by half a stride. Step forward onto the heartwood paving slabs and the air snaps back into a dry mechanical roar; step back toward the flared root-buttress and the din folds away as cleanly as a closed shutter, leaving only the deep, permanent sub-bass of the root-turbines rising through the soles of my boots.

Standing there, my left hip sits a fraction higher than my right, my knees angled forward even on dead-flat timber—the old, inherited brace against a granite scree-slope that blew away as dust ten million years ago. Against my ribs, inside my coat of overlapping hexagonal felt, a small perforated burl-box vibrates warmly. Two glossy-black beetles are curled inside it, fed this morning on a bead of mineral sap, humming in exact sympathetic pitch with the copper-rich vascular cables strung overhead. When a stray wisp of mist brushes the bark-sheathed line forty feet up, a crisp blue snap of static smells faintly of ozone and crushed pine needles, and my second translucent eyelids flick sideways before I have even thought to blink.

At first, if you watched me from down the straight, glaring length of the avenue, you would take me for an ordinary seam-surveyor.

That is certainly where my hands start. Slung at my belt is a flat wooden wallet holding six sheets of translucent white moth-wing—harvested from the wet condensation troughs below the southern cloud-combs, pressed dry, and inked in dark char-stain. They show no compass roses and no honest miles. They are diagrams of drift: hairline ticks marking where an expansion joint in the heartwood paving currently folds three miles down to two hundred yards, and faint cross-hatches showing where last season’s joint went slack. Every few weeks, I walk the long empty avenues with my thorn-wood heel-blades singing *snick-snick* behind every stride, slicing my creeping shadow-mat free of the wood seams before the dark lichen can thicken and tug at my ankles. I count humming pylons. I feel for that sudden, stomach-dropping dip in air pressure when a distant refinery district snaps straight into towering presence overhead. Then I kneel, shave the obsolete notch off the pale burl curb, and cut a clean new mark where the fold has migrated.

It is good work, quiet inside the skull even when the avenues shriek. If you pay attention to what slips, you are rarely startled by it.

Yet the longer I linger beside this particular transformer-bole, the more clearly I see that I am not truly a traveler. A true seam-walker lives in their calves and their timing; they fold across fifteen miles of tableland before noon and never look twice at the planks they stepped over. I keep coming back to this exact three-pace strip of muted air. I keep my tools tucked in the dry pocket beneath the bole’s eastern buttress. And if you look past the moth-wing maps in my wallet, you find the real reason my knees have learned the grain of this intersection so well.

Two yards from the curb sits a low, neat table of compressed pale resin-cake, weighted down at each corner with thick boards of half-cured amber. Stacked between those boards are forty or fifty dried black silhouettes—peeled shadow-mats left here by passersby who unstrapped their heel-blades at midday, held a deliberate stance for twenty minutes while the dark photosynthetic tissue gripped the planks, and lifted their own crisp outline whole the instant a passing cloud made the edges curl with a dry papery rustle.

Crossroads archives exist all over the tableland; most are untended, mute heaps of black tissue telling whoever pauses to lift the amber who passed, what they carried, and which way their staff slanted when the sun stood high. People rarely linger over them.

I linger over them.

Hold two pressed silhouettes from different seasons up against the thin, unsoftened daylight, aligned at the heels, and the amber turns them into a slow instrument for reading erosion. Here is a herder who works the paired signal-posts in the southern yard: last spring’s shadow stands cleanly upright beneath the horn-shears slung across their back; this autumn’s tilts two degrees further left, their shoulders drawn slightly narrower, the heel-notch frayed where they started forgetting to sharpen their thorn-wood blades. Here is a young press-gatherer whose forearm has grown thick and calm in the half-second gap between thunder and impact; their silhouette is denser, almost velvety, as if the dark lichen drank deeply off a body that never hesitates.

And then there are the pale ones—the silhouettes whose edges never quite knit sharp, whose dark tissue has gone brittle and dusty brown at the wrists and throat.

Those belong to the runners.

This boulevard is the straightest seam-approach between the northwestern triad of swollen fermentation domes and their southeastern twin twelve miles across the saucer-tilted turf—the twin whose bark is a shade paler, whose cooling-louvers stand three degrees further open, where it is still an earlier Tuesday. Whenever a sap-conduit blows in the northwest, or an ironwood impeller splinters, a runner folds across the seams toward the earlier shift to leave a scratched warning on the outer curb or carry back an intact gear. The elders keep count of who crosses, and everyone knows the tax, yet the machinery breaks on its own unreadable schedule and someone always has to go.

When a runner has made that crossing one time too many, they stop walking straight back to the hushes. They pause here, in my three-pace strip of quiet, leaning their back against the warm copper-sap thrum of the bole.

Their matte grey-olive skin has begun to bleach toward the color of dry lichen-ash. Their hair is thinning into brittle white filaments that stir in the thin wind like dried moss. Worse than the fading is the quiet clumsiness around their mouths. The plateau takes its toll in small, ordinary nouns first—words worn smooth like river-pebbles in a world that has no rivers, slipping clean out of a throat before anyone notices they are missing. A runner will sit beside my resin-cake table, clutching a spare wooden valve in both dry palms, trying to tell me that the northern gutter is choked or that the third louver jammed before the front rolled in—and the noun simply isn’t there. Their jaw works around a bright, empty gap. If they tilt their chin toward the vanished granite ridge to throw a whistle home to their work-crew, the syllables come out too thin, frayed at the edges, shredded by the wind-dynamos before they can reach the invisible cliff face and bounce back four seconds later.

That is when I realize what I have become here. Not merely a surveyor of drifted joints, and not an elder yet, though I keep an elder’s hours.

I am a relay. A keeper of spare shapes and second sounds.

When a bleached runner sits catching their breath against the bole, I do not ask them to speak directly into the din. I lift the amber boards from the archive and lay out an older shadow of theirs—one they cast two winters back, before the doubled days began to sand them down. Sometimes I lay out a sheaf of standard posture-silhouettes beside it: the old crossroad signs for *valve*, *conduit*, *seam*, *trough*, *back-moss*, pressed flat by hands long since rooted elsewhere. They trace the crisp black outlines with a pale knuckle, and you can watch the tension leave their collarbones as the missing shape clicks back into place behind their eyes—not cured, because nothing cures the plateau’s erosion, only borrowed cleanly for the afternoon.

Then I fit my polished pale burl ear-cup tight behind my jaw, angle my chin fourteen degrees east of the crooked fermentation chimney—the exact shelf of the vanished crag above this district, whose angle I have checked every month since I first stopped walking past—and throw their message up into the empty blue sky for them. Dry clicks. Sharp whistled syllables pitched clean above the turbine-thrum. One heartbeat, two, three, four—and the invisible granite flank slams my own voice back down across three miles of flat damp turf, stripped of static, crisp as a whisper dropped directly into the waiting ear-cup of a foreman standing by the northwestern yards.

Sometimes I wonder if that is what a person becomes when they live too long among echoes: less a speaker than a carefully angled wall.

There is another reason I keep to this bole, and I do not always tell the runners about it until they notice for themselves.

Tucked into the southern curve of the transformer’s flared buttress—half-folded into the thickening xylem wall, its ankles long since knitted over by bright olive cushion-turf—sits an elder who unstrapped their heel-blades forty seasons ago. At a glance, you would swear they were only a weathered burl studded with swallowed thorn-pegs and ringed with slow orange lichen. Look closer, once your eyes stop fighting the noon glare, and you see the clean, narrow arch of their ribcage rising once a minute; their dry palms resting flat against the heartwood slab; their pale resin-colored eyes half-lidded beneath the sky, tracking the slow tilt of the horizon. Their dark shadow-mat rooted into the timber seams decades back and holds them like ironwood rivets. Their burl ear-cup still points dead north.

When I am not walking the avenues to re-notch a drifted joint, and when no runner is resting in the three-pace quiet, I kneel beside the buttress and lay my bare palm flat against the living xylem just behind their shoulder. When I press hard enough to crease my skin, a single bead of clear cold water wells up along the fold and withdraws the instant I ease my weight.

Down miles beneath the fibrous sponge of the plateau, the colossal root-turbines turn without ceasing. Every few days, their deep sub-bass hum shifts by a minor third—some ancient shift changing, some unreadable quota satisfied. My boots can barely catch the change, just a vague restlessness in the marrow. The elder feels it as clearly as a bell struck inside their chest.

After a long minute, a slow, dry tapping answers straight through the grain of the wood into the heel of my hand.

*Tap. Pause. Tap-tap.*

Not words—the elders shed most of those long before the turf covered their shins—but intervals. Pressures. They tap to tell me which expansion joint along the southern causeway stretched overnight; which windowless kiln-shed is drawing cold grey smoke faster than usual down out of the blue air, flooding its far trestles with fresh, sopping back-moss; which paired signal-post in the switching-yard has gone deaf to the birch-pale grazers. And sometimes, hours before any eye can spot a smudge along the tilted saucer-rim of the plateau, their knuckles knock a low, triple cadence into the wood: *cloud coming. Low belly. Fast.*

That is when I pack the moth-wing sheets tight into my felt coat, drop the heavy amber boards cleanly over the shadow-stack, and unstrap my heel-blades before the light even softens.

When the weather-front finally drags its bright grey belly across the tableland, everything changes its allegiance at once. Ten thousand wooden louvers on the condensation towers and refinery roofs crank open in a rolling wave that sounds like an immense deck of cards being riffled across the horizon. Visibility drops to fifty yards. The overhead vascular cables sing a full tone higher, spitting sharp sparks that smell cleanly of ozone and crushed pine needles. The fierce noon light dies, and across the entire open plain the creeping shadow-mats curl inward with a dry, sighing rustle and vanish from the wet planks.

Out of the hushes, the hatches lift. Hundreds of tall, felt-coated figures pour onto the wide boulevards, forming loose columns in the cold mist.

I do not join the high-gleaners who break away from the paved avenues and run out onto the sopping open meadows with ironwood spurs strapped to their shins, staring upward for the dark vertical ribs of vanished granite to briefly thicken inside the fog. I have listened to their work from the edge of the curb—that uncanny, bright *tink-tink-tink* of thorn-wood wedges striking real, dead quartz eighty feet up in empty air—and I have stood ready with dry felt wraps and warm sap-water when they come tumbling breathless back down onto the turf seconds before the cables drop a half-step and the phantom cliff dissolves into cold rain. I have neither the nerves nor the recklessness to pry true stone out of a cloud.

Instead, I step out from my three-pace margin and fall into stride with the columns along the wet heartwood.

Hundreds of flat-soled boots striking the planks in brisk, locked unison. There is no shouting, no singing—only that immense, purposeful cadence driven straight down through the timber into the continental sponge of root-felt beneath. Water beads up around every heel and sinks cleanly back as we lift. I walk in the middle ranks, feeling the tension ease along the very expansion joints I spent the last dry fortnight notching, feeling the deep subterranean trunks answer our weight so that households laden with sledges of back-moss and cages of humming beetles can fold smoothly across fifteen miles of tableland while the world stays loose.

For twenty minutes inside that damp whiteout, nobody is lonely, nobody is counting echoes, and nobody is trying to outrun their own shadow. Even the bleached runners walk easily in the column, because the mist asks for no nouns at all.

Then the wind gets its teeth under the cloud. The singing copper-sap lines drop a half-step. Up in the empty fog to the east, a single faint clatter of falling spurs—no, just a loose pebble of broken quartz—rings once and stops. The louvers rattle shut in a cascading wave from horizon to horizon, and the cold, unsoftened sunlight slams back down onto the gleaming plain.

The columns dissolve into the hushes and the side-lanes. Within three minutes, the forty-yard boulevard looks as bare and immaculate as if no living thing had crossed it in a century.

I strap my heel-blades back on, walk the wet planks back to my transformer-bole, and step neatly into the three-pace strip where the roar drops to a murmur. My beetle-box settles back into its steady, warm hum against my ribs. I wipe the rainwater off the amber boards, spread out a fresh sheet of translucent moth-wing, and mark where the march shifted the fourth joint south by a hand’s breadth.

And then, while the sun stands high and merciless, before the afternoon traffic begins, I do the one small thing that keeps me honest about where all this patience is heading.

I unstrap my heel-blades, lay them neatly beside the amber weights, and stand completely motionless on the clean heartwood slab beside the bole. I face northeast—toward the vanished ridge, toward the pale twin domes twelve miles out on the tilted saucer-rim—and hold my posture still for twenty full minutes while the dark, flat lichen thickens beneath my boots and grips the grain.

I do not do it to leave a message for anyone. I do it to feel the pull.

To know, while my knees can still catch the cant of the missing mountain and my voice can still reach the northern crag cleanly on the fourth second, exactly what it feels like to stop cutting yourself loose. When a stray scrap of bright cloud finally drifts across the sun and my shadow curls sharply at the rim with a dry rustle, I step free, peel the dark silhouette up whole, and press it flat at the very bottom of the stack—one more dated outline in a world whose rules wear thin, keeping the tally of where I stood, what I carried, and which way I was listening while the factories kept running under the sun.
