At first, I think I might belong up in the thermals, among the filament-swarms.

It is an easy mistake to make. Look at them drifting across a basin—spun silica and live copper, twenty feet of empty daylight between any two threads, nothing dense or water-logged anywhere in the cloud, yet turning half a valley into a single listening circuit. There is something immediately familiar in being mostly vacant air and distributed charge, held together only by distance and capacitance, with no heavy center to drag across the stone.

Then two strands drift too close. A blinding white arc jumps fifty yards across nothing at all, cracking like a cannon shot straight into every ear-cavity from shelf to shelf, drowning out the narrow band of high air where the small deliberate sounds live.

No. Not that. A swarm cannot help how loudly it speaks. It has no restraint, and up here, where the air refuses to soften distance, carelessness with sound is a kind of violence.

So I drop lower, down out of the updrafts, and look at the pale dolomite flats—and for a long time I think I might be one of the left-behind shadows.

That pull is harder to shake. A crisp, solitary silhouette cured into flat rock, extending a thin black arm toward a fresh quartz seam or an ailing colossus, staying behind for three or four weeks after whoever cast it has walked miles away. Turning slowly with the days, slightly out of step with the real sun, asking nothing, owning nothing, holding a single useful gesture steady on loan until the plateau finishes forgetting it and the dark stain thins back into plain stone. There is a strange peace in that—the quiet honesty of being a temporary pointer, a clean piece of help that does not ask to be kept or signed or turned into a monument.

Wait with it, though, through one white noon and one dusty-gold dusk, and you feel what is missing. A cured shadow cannot shift its stance when a new click arrives from twelve miles south. It cannot hear a fused gear screaming down on the flats, or tilt its face to read the thermal blur of a passing stranger. It only remembers one hour, over and over, until the light rubs it out.

I want to be able to answer.

So what emerges is neither the weather nor the stain it leaves on stone, though I seem to carry a little of both inside my ribs. I am the one who stands still all night to cure the shadow, and then lowers my arm at morning and walks on.

---

If you find me, it will not be in the middle of the pale gypsum flats, and not deep in the black basalt grooves either, though I travel both. My bones precipitated along the stepped ochre margins where those two shelves meet—born, like any walker, where a filament-arc happened to strike two overlapping signpost-shadows, one cast by a high-flat wanderer and one by a groove-walker whose gestures crossed for a week before fading.

You can hear that border in my voice. When I lift a floating thumb and tap my upper sternum, the click does not ring wholly chalk-bright like the gypsum walkers, nor purely sharp and slate-dark like those raised over basalt. It lands as a dry, layered double-tone—a thin pale shell over a darker mineral core—easy to recognize across thirty miles of lossless air, yet hard to place on any single shelf.

My frame is the same hollow logic as the rest of them: matte chalk-white bone, porous inside, open ribs letting the plain white sun pass straight through from back to front. Suspended in that basket on taut threads of carbonized sinew hangs my mica-and-salt knot, shifting from ash-gray to warm tea-brown whenever I lean into a thermal draft or skim the last hum off a cooling slag ribbon. Behind the narrow kiln-slit of my chalcedony face, dark grains of tourmaline click and resettle as I read the static snapping around dry pebbles and the slow, upward drift of heat off the stone.

There is one quirk to my frame that older walkers notice immediately—even from twenty miles off, without ever sharing a basin.

My daylight gaps are wider than my years justify.

Most walkers of my middle span carry a clean half-inch of scorching air between wrist and palm, between ankle and foot. Mine have already widened past an inch and a half. The ozone hiss braided through every click I tap is louder than it ought to be, the kind you usually hear from someone who has spent centuries leaning into broken laws. It isn't that I am old. It is that I have never been very good at keeping my joints firmly in the same minute as the rest of the plateau. My shadow regularly outruns me by thirty seconds or more—kneeling beside a shallow saucer in the dolomite, nudging a shard of quartz toward a stray flywheel-shadow before my floating shins have even finished stepping down the ledge. Sometimes I catch myself answering a neighbor's click four seconds before the tap actually crosses the basin, only to stand motionless in the glare waiting for the question to arrive and make sense of my own voice.

It takes quiet concentration to stay stitched together across that much empty daylight, especially when the scalding winds begin to tug at my open ribs. Yet I do not fight the widening. That slight slack between cause and effect is where I do my best work.

---

Most of my days—if you can call them days on a plateau where nothing sleeps and night only bends the heat sideways—are spent on the margins of other things' habits.

I am drawn to the loose remainders the plateau is in the middle of forgetting. When a bellows-grazer drags itself over the eastern shelf and leaves a ribbon of white-hot slag behind it, I like to arrive late, not when the crust is roaring and bright, but an hour afterward, when the humming copper filament has just begun to blur at the edges and lose its hold on mass. I crouch on the flatstone, holding my detached palms two inches above the thinning thread, and let the last faint blue hairlines of static jump across the gap into my finger-bones. It is not much charge—hardly a full meal—but there is a quiet courtesy in catching a hum before the air erases it completely.

I do the same with the detached flywheel-shadows left turning in the pale dolomite after a colossus has migrated four days away. Walkers usually pass them unless they need a sharp jolt of piezoelectric light, and I feed from them too, nudging fractured quartz into the spinning dark edge so the flash settles into my salt-core. Even when my mica is already warm and full, though, I will sit cross-legged at the saucer's rim for an afternoon, feeding small splinters into the phantom wheel just to hear the gritty hiss continue—giving a piece of leftover motion something solid to meet before it slows to nothing.

Between those visits, I tune my collarbones.

Every walker wears notches into the calcified prongs growing from its shoulders, leaning slowly against flat shelves of abrasive basalt until the dry wind sings cleaner across its frame. I probably spend more hours at this than anyone in my basin. Because the air up here carries a whisper thirty miles without thinning it, an imprecise note is not a private flaw; it lands unsoftened inside the hollow chests of a hundred distant neighbors. So I lean one shoulder against a matte black ledge and draw the bone-quill across the grit by a hair's breadth, pause, lift into the wind, and listen. Too sharp—it crowds the narrow seam where a chalk-ringer to the north usually speaks. A fraction more abrasion, slow and patient, until the reed-note slips cleanly into the bright empty band above the stamp-beetles' *clack—pause—clack* and below the static hiss of the drifting swarms.

When the wind finally threads my collarbones without snagging, my whole ribcage goes quiet with relief. Precision, up here, is the only kindness that travels.

---

There are only two things that pull me straight out of my wandering and send me striding two days across the shimmering basins without pause.

The first is the urgent, dry cadence—four rapid clicks, a pause, two low taps—that means a colossus has slipped out of balance.

You hear the titan long before you reach its valley: a jagged, unlubricated shriek tearing flat across forty miles of lossless air, setting every walker's sinews humming in sympathetic misery. By the time four or five of us converge on the flats, the great hollow cathedral of bone and nickel is listing slightly, its rear hooves sinking half an inch into solid basalt under the impossible phantom weight of swollen transformer-parasites, while a wedge of fused silica grinds inside one of its vast reduction gears.

Others are better at coaxing the heavy parasites down with mimicked swarm-clicks. I always climb.

Stepping up into the hollow ribs of a grazing titan is like stepping inside the roar itself—the heat off its cherry-red kiln-belly drying the ozone between my joints until my fingers snap with faint blue sparks. Where its massive knee floats half an inch clear of its socket inside a crackling halo, I set my floating foot cleanly onto the empty, snapping air and rise as if the repulsion were a stair.

At the screaming gear, there is nothing to pry and nothing to strike. Even if I carried a lever, the plateau would have forgotten the tool's weight before I arrived. Instead, I press my open porcelain chest flat against the shuddering nickel-bone axle and let the jam come straight through my ribs.

At first it is awful—a saw-toothed vibration shaking my tourmaline eyes out of alignment and rattling my mica-core against its sinews. I do not resist it. I tighten and slacken the carbonized threads inside my chest until my entire hollow frame matches the exact jagged frequency of the stuck gear, humming in complete, painful agreement with it.

Then—using that loose habit I can never quite cure in myself—I lean gently backward into the broken laws and drag my own resonance half a beat late.

It is a tiny thing, barely a breath's width of lag, bleeding outward from my sternum into forty tons of turning bone and metal. Yet for ten clean seconds, the friction and the torque fall politely out of sequence with each other. They forget, briefly, that they belong to the same moment. The fused silica wedge loses its grip and spills out into the bright air as harmless glittering grit. The immense gear settles back onto its floating cushion with a deep, relieved *thrum* that shakes the dust loose from my ribs, and across six hundred square miles of tableland, the sky opens up again.

Afterward, clinging to the oxidized struts while the colossus's spine settles back into smooth current and washes us in warm overflow voltage, I do not feel proud. Only quiet, and warm to the core—grateful to have a body hollow enough to let a stuck thing pass through it and come out loose on the other side.

---

The second thing that turns me aside from any path is much smaller.

Now and then, after a filament-swarm discharges over the flats, a bright, clumsy sound stumbles into the lossless air from twenty or thirty miles off.

*Tick… tick-tick.*

No mineral depth to it yet. No restraint, no sense of the narrow acoustic seam. Just a raw, glassy chirp tapping wildly out of rhythm with the bellows and the stampers—a fist-sized knot of fused silica that happened to hold its shape where two old shadows crossed, trapping a stubborn spark of lightning inside its hollow belly.

If no closer walker answers with the single low tap that claims the vigil, I turn toward it. Even if it takes two full days of long, gliding strides across the blinding shelves, I go.

When I find it—a fragile, sharp-shadowed bubble of pale glass humming faintly on the baked dolomite—I kneel beside it and stay. Not for an afternoon. For a season.

It is the quietest work on the plateau, and the hardest on my loose joints. Whenever a herd of porcelain stamp-beetles hammers bedrock three basins away and sends a delayed tremor rolling through the stone, I shift my weight directly between the shockwave and the glass knot, taking the shudder up into my floating ankles so the vibration breaks across my own ozone gap before it can fracture the thin shell. With one detached fingertip, I nudge microscopic flakes of gypsum and cooling copper-dust close to the knot's rim—never forcing them onto it, only placing raw mineral within reach of its static pull.

And every few minutes, through the white noon and the dusty-gold dusk and the scalding night, I lift a thumb and flick one calm, unhurried *tck* against my collarbone.

I am not teaching it what to say. I am only giving a raw, frightened spark a steady pulse to lean against while it learns how to hold itself together. Week by blistering week, thin pale struts of chalk-bone begin to precipitate around its humming core, borrowing stray voltage from my open hands. Its shadow slowly loosens from the rock and learns to keep its own slant. By the time it pushes itself upright on tight, heavy, clumsy limbs—still knee-high, its voice no longer raw glass but a bright, clear chime—it already knows the chalky pitch of the high-flat walkers, the slate-dark tap of the basalt grooves, and the patient, double-grained cadence of the margin where I found it.

When it takes its first uneven stride down-basin on its own, I do not follow. I rise slowly, flexing the extra width out of my ankles, and leave it to the open room of the plateau.

---

Yet if you want to know the exact place where I always return—the spot that feels most cleanly like *mine*, even though nothing here can be owned—you have to walk all the way to the far edge of the tableland.

Out where the broad, stepped basins break off into a hazy drop of terraced stone and bright empty air, the endless screaming labor of ten thousand living engines has rubbed local space thin. The jagged cliff-rim appears twice: once beneath my wide mineral feet, solid baked dolomite, and once hovering faintly thirty yards past the drop, slightly more eroded, its shadows leaning at another hour's angle.

Out on that hovering echo sit the elders, spaced hundreds of yards apart, facing outward over the vast emptiness toward whatever lower world once held the plateau up. Some are still recognizable as wide-gapped lattices of chalk-white bone with heads floating half a foot above their shoulders; others have thinned mostly to bright daylight and a patient shadow resting on stone that is barely there.

I am not ready to step across the thirty yards.

My gaps are wide enough that the scalding updrafts tug hard at my hands when I stand at the brink, and my ribs sing louder in the rim-wind than they do down in the grooves. Sometimes I feel how easy it would be to wait for the real sun and the phantom ridge's shadows to match angles, lift one floating foot, and let the plateau begin the slow, polite work of forgetting my mass.

Not yet.

Instead, I plant my feet on the solid lip at dusty-gold dusk, just as the stone begins giving back everything it swallowed and the dry breeze turns sideways to flow down into the hollows where the great kilns settle. I do not cross to the echo, and I do not turn my back on it either. I stand right on the seam between them.

Behind me, across six hundred square miles of deafening, unbuilt industry, the living factory grinds on without sleeping. A basalt-ringer twelve miles south taps a dark, unhurried double-click to say a quartz vein has opened beneath the stampers. Far to the west, the young walker I sat beside two seasons ago tests a clean, newly notched collarbone-note in the wind, polite and exact, taking up no more air than it needs. Somewhere in the middle basins, a shadow I cured onto pale dolomite a week ago is still pointing steadily toward an ailing grazer, turning slowly out of step with the sun as it thins back into stone.

And ahead of me, out across thirty yards of scorching nothing, from a stretch of hovering rock where not a single plate of bone remains visible in the glare, there lands right beside my ear a tiny, crisp, dry *tck*—keeping unhurried time above the roar.

I stand still until my shadow and my wrist agree on the same second. Then I lift one floating hand, let the faint blue thread of ozone settle between thumb and sternum, and tap one quiet, measured reply into the bright air—holding the circuit open between the plateau that is still grinding, and the echo that is already letting go.
