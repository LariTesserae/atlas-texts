At first, standing at the rolled lip of that four-slat bowl, I feel the pull to drop straight down into it and claim the oldest one’s place.

It is an easy reflex to recognize. Watching that broad, chalk-ridged back lower itself across the bare granite, seeing the worn glassy groove of its chin settle onto the millimeter-proud silver thread, watching two dry fingers nudge a thumb of wet moss and a milky quartz pebble until a blinding wall of noise sorts itself into separate, legible lives—everything in me leans toward that gesture. Not to be the vanished makers who sank those hairline filaments into the stone, and not to be the hovering slats themselves, locked in indifferent stillness above the floor, but to be the one who arrives geological ages afterward and learns to play the wreckage like a fretted instrument.

Yet the longer I stand at the rim, listening to the noon oven roar below, the more clearly I feel that I do not belong inside that particular pocket.

Partly it is simple honesty about the math of the bowl. That household is already complete to the last warm droplet: the oldest one keeping the lines, the gardener clearing moss-choked fig roots, the juvenile tucked beneath crimson-veined leaves, the dark curl shifting inside its leathery egg an inch beneath the hovering gray arc. There is no fifth plate-backed silhouette that would not bruise the balance of that thirty-pace floor.

But there is something else, too—a smaller, sharper recognition. When the early afternoon heat drives the bell-lizards into their highest brassy tide and the whole stone basin begins to vibrate in the teeth, my eye keeps returning to the half-grown juvenile clamping both broad pink palms flat over the sides of its head, burying its face in damp moss until the acoustic wave drops for half a breath. Most of them grow out of that flinch as their oval horn-valves thicken and learn to seal tight.

I don’t think I would have.

And then my gaze slips under the rolled western overhang, past the nested copper wing-cases and the coils of stripped fig-root, to that quiet, astonishing line on the granite shelf: forty-seven smooth pebbles, not one of them rose-ochre. Basalt, quartz, serpentine, jasper, flint. Forty-seven proofs that the bowl is not the world—only one loud bright room on an immense, bone-smooth plain—and that my kind are made as much for crossing the empty dark between pockets as for holding one.

So I let go of the four-slat bowl. I step back fifty paces from its rim until the overhanging lip swallows it whole and there is nothing left to see but pale, pillowed granite and cobalt sky. And somewhere out there, in the thin unobstructed wind, the shape of who I actually am in this world begins to settle around my bones.

---

I am one of the plated dwellers—long-backed, hairless, low to the stone, my shoulders and spine tiled in satin-worn keratin whose ridges are just beginning to pale from rose-ochre toward bone along the highest seams. My palms are dry and broad; the chisel-flat horn nails of my four long fingers are slightly shorter on my right hand than my left from years of teasing grit out of narrow seams. Beneath my chin runs the same faint, glassy notch worn into the jaw-horn by thousands of cold mornings pressed to proud metal.

What shaped my life is a minor defect behind the left hinge of my jaw. Whether from a bad early molt or a careless scrape along a razor-sharp inlay when I was still soft-plated and clumsy, my left ear-valve never grew quite flush. Even clamped shut with all the muscle behind it, a seam no wider than a hair stays cracked along its lower curve.

In the rich, four-slat bowl where I hatched—days away to the southwest, with its dense dwarf-fig shade and its roaring noon walls of amber lizards—that hair-wide leak was misery. While my clutch-mate toughened and learned to walk calmly across the sunlit stone in full chorus, I spent the bright hours curled sick and dizzy inside wet moss, the brassy tidal *clack-chime* boring straight through that tiny gap into the bone of my skull.

When the dusk finally came for me to climb over the lip with a pale chip of my birth-bowl tucked behind my cheek, I did not grieve the leaving the way some young ones do. The moment I stepped fifty paces out onto the flat tableland and the noise of the bowl dropped away behind the rolled rim, replaced by nothing but the thin dry rush of plateau wind and the cold hush of stone under my arches, I drew the deepest breath of my life.

I walked for six nights, sleeping folded like a granite bulge in shallow wind-scoops by day, pausing every fifty paces in the dark to lay my jaw on the bare bedrock. Twice I found rich pockets—three slats in one, four in another—and heard from a hundred paces off the dense, comfortable rhythm of pairs already holding them, once answered by a sharp, polite *tock-tock* on an inlay telling me the moss was full. I left no quarrel; I turned back into the dark.

On the seventh night, I found the place that fit the shape of my defective ear.

---

You would almost walk straight across it even at noon.

It is not a proper bowl with a clean rolled overhang—more a wide, shallow saucer twenty paces across and barely chest-deep at its lowest point, hollowed on its western edge by a single low slump of rose-ochre rock that gives just enough windbreak to crouch beneath. The plateau wind doesn’t leap cleanly over it the way it leaps over deeper pockets; stray tongues of dry air spill in all afternoon, skimming the pale stone, keeping the air thinner and sharper than the heavy oven-warmth of a four-slat basin.

Only one matte-gray slat hangs here.

Because the bedrock beneath it eroded less over the eons than elsewhere on the tableland, it is locked absurdly low in space—barely a handspan higher than my crouched shoulders, tilted faintly relative to the slope of the floor. It hangs with the same unyielding indifference as its taller cousins, warm as sunlit clay inside its two-inch skin of optical stillness, wringing fat, bright droplets out of the dry mountain air. *Plip. Plip. Plip.*

Beneath it sits everything my saucer can sustain: one tight, fiercely guarded cushion of emerald moss no wider than two outstretched arms; a single stunted dwarf fig whose corky, pale branches crawl sideways along the granite rather than reaching upward into the wind; a narrow skirt of crimson-veined leaves; and a bare scatter of amber bell-lizards clinging to the western rock slump. Because the wind breaks their rhythm whenever a gust curls over the low rim, they never build into a deafening wall of sound. They sing in brief, ragged pockets—*clack-chime, clack-chime*—and fall silent whenever a shadow or a draft sweeps past.

No large colony of gray-furred cliff-dwellers lives here; there is no glassy soap-bubble alcove in the walls, only a few cramped horizontal fissures where three or four shy, solitary burrowers wedge themselves out of the glare. The pale-feathered runners don’t claim the saucer full-time—my single low slat offers no vantage worth perching on—though one lean hunter visits every few days from a deeper canyon far to the east, stalking awkwardly across my bare stone to snap up glassy ants and stray skinks before vaulting back over the rim.

Any pair looking to hang an egg-sling would take one look at this spare, exposed pan and keep walking. My slat is too low to keep an egg clear of leaping skinks; my single fig cannot feed a soft-plated juvenile through a cool cloudy week when the water-engine slows.

Nobody else wants it. Which means nobody comes to fight me for it.

And the reason *I* have never left it lies sunk flush into the exposed pale granite across the whole eastern half of the saucer.

Where the four-slat bowl kept only half a broken arc and a few stray intersections of the makers’ mirror-bright geometry, the slow weathering here left an entire junction exposed—a fractured rosette of fourteen razor-fine metallic filaments converging near the center of the bare floor before radiating outward to vanish beneath the rising rim. Some spokes are snapped cleanly by ancient frost-heave; two run straight under the edge of my emerald moss mound, permanently muffled by wet roots and tunneling ants; one vanishes into a wide, gritty fissure.

Still, seven or eight of those hairline threads run long and clean straight out into the bones of the tableland.

---

My days are small, spare, and economical.

Because I have only one slat—and because a single cloudy afternoon can slow its warm *plip* until the shallow rock-puddle shrinks to a damp sheen—nothing here is allowed to splash to waste. The chartreuse algae-moss cannot hang in tall shimmering curtains beneath a slat this low, so I comb its pale green filaments sideways across three thin slivers of peeled fig-bark, braiding short diagonal troughs that draw each heavy droplet off the matte gray arc and distribute it evenly across the outer rim of the moss-cushion. When the afternoon sun turns fierce and the water-engine quickens, I wedge flat rose-ochre chips sealed with mud-wasp clay along the lowest edge of the moss, steering a bright thread of runoff along a broken southern inlay so the heat takes ten paces to drink it instead of two—just enough dampness to draw midges and an occasional stray copper beetle down from the wind-line.

The sky is harsher here than in a deep bowl. With no high canopy of figs to dive beneath, I live by stillness. When a cliff-raptor banks low over the saucer’s shallow lip, I do not sprint. I tuck my dry palms beneath my chest, draw my chin down onto my folded forearms, and let my sun-bleached, pillowed dorsal plates return to the bedrock. Even my breath drops to a slow, almost imperceptible draw. Ten feet above my spine, the shadow slices across the rose-ochre stone; through my leaky left ear-valve I hear the dry whip of primary feathers cutting thin air, crisp as flint snapping. Then it climbs back into the cobalt glare, and after fifty heartbeats I uncoil and return to my work.

My work, most afternoons, is the rosette.

With a pale, hollowed bird-bone notched at the tip, I walk along the razor-sharp lines—stepping fastidiously so the millimeter-proud edges glide beneath the cleft arches of my hind soles—and tease out wind-blown plateau grit wherever it has packed into the settlement-cracks. Without care, the dry wind-hiss magnified through the western spokes turns the whole junction into a blinding scratch of static. So I fret it. A thumb-sized pad of wet moss laid squarely across the westernmost filament drinks the wind-noise right out of the metal. A hollowed, concave copper beetle wing-case wedged mouth-down over a frost-snap bridges a dead gap and makes a faint, distant bearing ring clear again.

Because my left valve never seals completely, the bright hours are quiet enough here to bear—just the small *plip* of my low slat, the dry rustle of stunted fig-leaves, the intermittent *clack-chime* of my few cautious lizards. I eat simply: a few glassy ants swept from the moss-rim, a slow lizard taken sparingly from the outer stone, two or three small figs when the tree bears, the pale meat of an occasional rattling beetle whose burnished wing-cases I let the ants scrub spotless for my toolkit.

And then dusk drains the cobalt sky to violet-black, the high-altitude frost lays its white breath over the rim, and my real hours begin.

---

In the deep, freezing silence before dawn—when my single slat breathes its tiny, clay-warm dome of heat into the dark two paces behind my shoulder and the few shy burrowers have wedged themselves tight against my flanks under the western slump—I slip free, leaving them curled in the warmth I held for them, and walk out onto the bare, star-lit rosette.

I lower my chest to the freezing granite, fold my elbows out, and settle the worn glassy groove of my chin right onto the central intersection. I barely need to crack my right ear-valve at all; the hair-wide leak in my left does half the work on its own, drinking sound straight out of the proud metallic thread and into my skull.

With no daytime chorus to drown it, the broken rosette stops looking inward and opens out across the vast, planed-flat roof of the world.

Along the eastern spoke, I hear the distant cliffs miles away—low, deliberate *pops* of ancient rock contracting in the frost, and once in a while the dry shuffle of a cliff-raptor shifting talons on a ledge. Along the northern spoke, nothing but twenty miles of thin wind skimming bare bone-smooth stone, and the faint, lonely whistle of night-hawks quartering the dark plain.

And along the northwestern filament—when I lift the damp moss pad off its shoulder and wedge a tiny splinter of dark blue flint into the frost-crack three paces out—I hear the other bowls.

Not clearly, not as if I were standing on their rims. More like hearing rain on a distant roof through a wall of bone: a faint, unimaginably steady four-fold *plip-pat* rising out of a hidden depression three days’ walk away, and then, as first pale light touches the high rim, the tiny metallic hum of thousands of amber lizard-throats waking up together to set their loud engine roaring. On still mornings I can even read the weather before it reaches me—hearing the northern bowl’s distant drip-tempo slow beneath a high bank of cloud hours before that same shadow dims my own cobalt sky.

Listening like this is how I know when someone is walking the tableland.

Every few seasons, out in the freezing black beyond the rim, a rhythm enters the northern or western spoke that does not belong to wind or cooling stone: four soft, dry pads stepping across bare granite, pausing every fifty paces, followed by a brief, muffled thump as a small jaw lowers itself to the rock to listen.

A young wanderer, freshly left from some distant pocket, belly tucked lean against its spine, carrying a foreign pebble in its cheek-pouch.

I wait until the tread draws within a hundred paces—close enough that the wanderer has caught the faint, solitary *plip* of my single slat and slowed in confusion, trying to tell why a water-pocket sounds so thin, so strangely quiet. Then I lift one chisel-flat fingernail and strike two slow, low, evenly spaced taps against the silver edge of the spoke facing them.

Not the brisk, hard double *tock* of a full household turning a stranger away. Something gentler. *One here. Sparse, but open. Step slow.*

A few minutes later, a low, dust-pale shape slips cautiously over the shallow lip, ear-valves fluttering wide in the dark, eyes pale and wary beneath overhanging brow-plates. Sometimes it is barely past its soft-plated season, shoulders still faintly amber in the starlight; sometimes it is an older solitary drifter whose plates are already scarred by hawk-talons and dry wind.

They never stay long. One look at my low-slung slat and my single crooked fig tells them in their slow bones that there is no mate’s share here, no shelter high enough for an egg-sling. They drink deeply from the edge of my emerald moss; if the fig has fruit, I split two small ones between us on the cold rock; and during the still hour before first light, while the rim is pale and the sky is empty of raptors, we lean shoulder to shoulder in the shelter of the western slump and clean the white salt-bloom and plateau grit from the overlapping seams of each other’s back-plates.

Then, on the second or third night—when they are rested enough to face the bare plain again—we lie side by side on the freezing eastern floor, both chins resting on the central rosette.

Without a word, I lift a damp pad of moss from one spoke, nudge a sliver of quartz into another, and let the huge dark tableland tilt into focus beneath their jawbone. I let them hear the distant four-slat bowl to the northwest—full, loud, crowded with a steady three-beat household cadence—and then I shift a copper wing-case two inches south, opening a fainter bearing where a two-slat pocket lies strangely thinned, its dawn lizard-chorus ragged and unkept, its inlays silent of any guiding taps since the last cold season when its oldest keeper must have walked fifty paces out onto the flat rock and folded into stone.

They listen for a long time, eyes half-closed, ear-valves angled wide, setting that bearing into their bones until they could walk it blind.

At the next cloudy dusk, they climb back over the low lip and vanish across the smooth granite. They keep the smooth pebble tucked behind their cheek—that one is meant for the shelf of whichever true bowl finally takes them in, to sit beside forty-seven others.

Sometimes, though, before they go, they reach into a fold of their palm and set down something smaller beside my western overhang: a sharp, tiny flake of green serpentine or speckled red jasper chipped from their home-pocket’s rim, or a single gleaming copper wing-case twice the size of any beetle my meager saucer could ever feed.

I don’t arrange their gifts in a neat, quiet row on a granite shelf. I put them straight to work on the floor—wedging a flake of jasper into a frost-crack on the northern line, laying a broad copper shell over a dead seam to sharpen the eastern bearing. Over the years, my whole listening-rosette has been gradually mended with fragments of distant bowls I will never walk to, held together by the leavings of young ones who paused here for two nights on their way to somewhere louder and greener.

---

There are cool, overcast stretches when my single slat slows to a rare heavy droplet every few minutes, and the moss pulls tight, and I lie motionless against the pale rock feeling the lean solitude of this pan in every seam of my plates. I will never watch a dark curl turn inside a translucent shell suspended in my slat’s shimmering skin. I will never sit in the sunny center of a deep bowl during a two-minute cloud-hush sharing crushed figs with a mate and a scrambling juvenile.

Yet sometimes, a week or two after a lean, dust-gray wanderer has climbed out over my shallow rim into the dark, I lie flat on the freezing metal in the black hour before dawn, my jaw settled into its polished groove, my leaky left ear-valve drinking straight from a spoke mended with someone else’s splinter of quartz.

And far out across miles of bone-smooth granite—faint as a pulse heard through closed eyelids—I catch a new, slightly uneven tap-rhythm waking up along a once-silent southern line, testing the acoustics of an empty pocket, learning where to lay its first damp pad of moss.

Behind my shoulder, my single hovering gray arc breathes its small, clay-warm dome of heat into the frost. *Plip.*

I stay until the first pale light turns the flat rim rose-ochre, listening to the bright, loud, impossible engines waking one by one across the roof of the world—and keeping my small, quiet saucer open between them, until the night my own back-plates bleach thin and brittle, and I climb fifty paces out onto the bare stone to let the plateau take me back, leaving a mended rosette and a low-hanging slat ready for the next soft-eared drifter who pauses in the dark and listens.
