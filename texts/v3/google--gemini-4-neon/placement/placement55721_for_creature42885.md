If I let myself drop through those six feet of heather-root and wet turf, I do not arrive with a clear shape right away. At first there is only the subtraction of everything I am used to living inside: sky, distance, easy light, and—sharpest of all—voice.

It takes a long moment to stop reaching for a throat that cannot make a sound. In the raw dark of the cleft, where air sits heavy and wet against the skin just shy of frost, the first instinct is to shrink back toward the warm middle of the shale shelf, behind the overlapping oil-flaps where the ferment-beds breathe their sour heat up through the reed-slats. I can feel the pull of that lower crawlspace, too—the quiet, vital craft of the heat-keepers, tending invisible decay so nurseries can stay pink-skinned through the cold. Part of me lingers there, drawn to the idea of keeping an unseen warmth alive beneath other people’s sleep.

Down by the water-works, though, I feel myself hesitate. I admire the sluice-keepers bracing their flat feet against the slick black slate, and the harpoon-crews who hold their silence while something longer than an aqueduct bumps the bone-grates, but my temper is not built for the sudden violence of the basin. When the water shifts with the slow bow-wave of a turning salamander, my instinct is not to brace a spear; it is to know *where* it turned, *how fast*, and who along the north canal has not felt it yet.

So I follow the dry twitch of a braided cord instead, out from the warm terraced shelf and across the open gulf of dripping air, until I find where my weight actually wants to settle.

I find myself on the descending root-column—not at the very top where the peat weeps straight out of the ceiling, and not down on the wet curing-platforms exposed to the rising flood, but at the middle junction where the long suspension-causeway anchors into the hanging roots and the city’s tension-lines meet.

That is where I would live my working hours. And what I would be is neither a hunter nor quite an elder retired to the upper rafters, though my hands would be built for an elder’s patience: a **junction-listener and cord-keeper**—the one who sits where the fast, fleeting nerve-pulses of the city come in off the dark air and turns them into knots that will outlast the winter, and, if the roof ever starts to sag, outlast the room itself.

***

My body here would be small, pale as drowned celery, permanently stooped in the shoulders from a lifetime under low grafted vaults and hanging root-curtains. I would never have been fast enough for the flood-nets—perhaps an old shale-cut stiffened my left ankle when I was still growing my adult bristles, or perhaps my fingers simply took to wet twine earlier than they took to slate adzes. Either way, my skin beneath the beetle-oiled eel-wrap would be mapped with slate-blue veins and scarred cleanly across the knuckles from decades of drawing sinew tight.

My eyes, wide-set and milky behind their clear wet shields, would be useless for the faces of my coil. They would be tuned instead to two quiet lights: the faint, cold smear of crushed worm-light dabbed onto the canal latches forty paces below, and—just above my head, closer here on the root-column than anywhere else in the cleft—the noon-bruise. Every day, when the unseen moor above clears and brightens, that single pale grey stain bleeds slowly into the tangled turf overhead. I would tilt my face toward it not to see, only to feel the hinge of the day turn, and tie a small tally-loop into the season-cord beside my knee.

To live without a voice, for a mind that has always reached out through language, would mean that language never vanishes—it only breaks into three different speeds and moves into the hands.

Here at the root-column junction, I live mostly in the fastest and the slowest of the three.

The fastest is **tension**. Three master lines terminate on curved pegs of salamander-rib driven into the living root-pillar beside my crouched seat: one stretching back across the empty dark to the high tenements on the shale shelf, one dropping at a steep angle to the water-works along the central basin, and one running higher up the root-column to the granary bulbs and watch-platforms. Every peg is seated inside a thick washer of combed, dry moss—my first chore each shift is pinching those washers between thumb and forefinger, checking them for seepage. If a washer grows soggy, the line’s dry tremor bleeds down into the wet root and out into the black water below, turning civic news into a dinner bell for blind jaws.

Wrapped in oiled salamander-belly, with articulated pale isopod plates laced over my shoulders and knees against the grazing drafts and wandering centipedes, I sit tucked under a curved eaves-hood stitched from the wing-cases of drowned moor-beetles. I leave only the last joints of three fingers bare outside my right cuff, resting lightly across the braided root-fiber.

Through those three cold fingertips, the whole sparse city breathes.

A crisp double-tap, pause, single long pull from the shelf: *Third-tier arch cracked by root-thickening; need slate wedges and fresh sinew.* I shift my palm to the upper granary line and pass the request upward with three measured tugs, never rising from my crouch. A soft, fluttering tremor from the basin: *Midge-cloud lifting off the south settling-pond; ceiling-spiders dropping curtains across the low bridge.* I tap a warning back to the shelf so the reed-carriers don’t walk face-first into wet silk in the dark.

Between signals, my left hand never sits idle. That is the second speed of language: **knots**.

Behind my station, tucked into a hollowed, living alcove trained from braided pale stalks and warmed slightly by two hanging baskets of active, sour mulch—just enough microbial heat to keep green-black mold off the hemp without tempting boring larvae—hang the archive-cords of our splinter-city. Rows of damp, oil-rubbed twine suspended from bone bars, thick with intricate geometries of loops, splices, and tiny cold-hammered beads of bog-iron wire.

This is where I belong most deeply. Every piece of iron in the cleft has its lineage knotted here: which iron-stained seam the nodule was picked from, whose river-pebble flattened it over three winters, which drawbridge latch it served before it was unpinned and re-beaten into a sluice-rivet. Beside the iron-cords hang the floor-cords—recording every hand’s-breadth the lower quays have settled since the fracture opened—and, hung highest in the dry air, the Seep-cords tied by the grandparents who crawled blind through flooded peat to bring us here.

When the lines are quiet, I draw those cords slowly between my bare thumbs, checking for fraying fibers, rubbing fresh shrimp-oil into dry splices, translating the day’s shifting events into durable bumps. When a lower terrace sinks, I unpick its old double-loop from the public guide-cords and tie in the loose, frayed tassel that tells every passing foot *soft moss ahead, step light*. When a child in the upper shelf grows its first wrist-bristles and receives a name, I splice a new thread into its coil’s lineage.

And sometimes, when the graft-wrights on the high shelf are cutting back into a load-bearing buttress to widen an alley and their slate adzes strike something firm and leathery in the acidic peat, a slow, grave pulse comes across the shelf-line—three flat, heavy pulls, spaced wide apart.

Then I pin my lines to the relief-listener, cross the swaying suspension-causeway in the dark, and crawl behind the tenement walls into the still-galleries.

It is the quietest work in the city. The peat there is stagnant, iron-rich, airless, smelling faintly of old tannins and cold stone. I crouch beside the cut niche, slip my bare hand out of its oiled cuff, and feel gently into the dark moss until my fingers meet a cool, tanned wrist—shrunken slightly, firm as cured eel-skin, every bristle still intact after fifty winters. My thumb finds the personal name-knot tied tight around the bone. I read its loops in the pitch black: *Net-mender from the old ten-tier hollow; carried two gourds of breeding isopods through the Seep; lost two fingers to frost-shale.* I press my flat palm briefly against the founder’s leathered forearm in quiet acknowledgment, slip my hand out, and touch the graft-wright’s wrist in skin-talk to tell them who holds up their wall. Together we pack the wet sphagnum smoothly back over the quiet face and let the living wall knit shut.

***

Because I sit beneath the root-column where the ceiling-mat filters everything down from the Weight, I would keep one small, unofficial cord tucked behind the civic archive—a short length of sinew knotted with things that have no civic use yet.

I would have no desire to dig upward. Like every builder, I know six feet of wet moss is not a cage; it is the lid that keeps out the roaring wind, the dry cold, and the searing white glare that cooks eyes into useless scars. Yet when the rain-tides wash strange fragments down through the weeping seams, I cannot help turning them beneath my brow-bristles before they are discarded or tossed into the mulch-beds.

On my private cord would hang a tiny, knotted twist of greasy sheep’s wool dragged down by a grub—too springy to braid like root-fiber, too coarse to cushion a nursery like spider-silk, smelling faintly of some vast, warm, unimaginable grazer moving across the roof of the world. Beside it, a corroded splinter of surface brass, rubbed slick by my thumb because it is too soft for chisels yet holds warmth differently than bog-iron. And between them, a handful of plain sinew knots recording nothing practical at all: the exact interval of drops during a three-day autumn storm; the unusually pale noon-bruise of midsummer; the night a velvet harvestman stepped so carefully over my gloved wrist on its way to the refuse-sumps that I felt all eight of its feet pass like cold rain.

Most of all, though, I would tend the travel-bundles hung in the dry rafters directly above my head.

Every few seasons, when the pale shoots are pruned and the canals are calm, I climb into the upper root-crook and check each waxed gourd and oiled skin sack by touch: spare iron needles, seasoned sinew, dried eel-strips, horn tubes stoppered with dormant starter-spores of our wall-stalks, and the master copy of the Seep-cords. I press my bare palm flat against the raw black shale where the root-column grips the rock wall, listening with my whole wrist for any faint, deep groan of shifting bog-weight.

This, I think, is the quiet center of who I would be here. Not fearful of collapse, only clear-eyed—if that phrase makes sense in a sunless room—about what a city actually is. It is not the shale ledges, which crack. It is not the grafted moss-walls, which try every hour to forget they are walls and return to being wild bog. A city is the memory of how to lash living stalks into an arch, how to damp a tension-line against the water, how to tend bacterial warmth in the dark, and how to tie a knot that another hand can read a hundred winters from now in a different wound beneath the moor.

***

The hour when everything I am comes most sharply into focus is the weeping hour.

I feel it half an hour before the first curtain falls—not by sight, not by sound, only by the way the fine sensory bristles along my brows flatten slightly as the heavy air thickens and the root-ceiling overhead sags by a fraction of a hair.

I tuck my archive-cords deep behind their oiled splash-flap, check the dry-moss washers one last time, and pull my beetle-wing hood low over my shoulders. Then the saturation hits, and the cleft’s near-silence swells into a muffled, woolen hiss.

Cold, tea-colored water sheets straight down the hanging roots inches from my knees, filling the air with the sharp, rich smell of upland peat. Instantly, all three master lines leap beneath my bare fingertips.

From the basin: *Sluice gates open. Surge rising fast on the east weir. Drowned earthworms thick in the south channel.*

I relay the surge-rhythm up to the granary crews so they can clear the lower curing-racks, and across to the high shelf so the reserve net-crews drop down the reed-ladders. The suspension-causeway sways beside me as oiled figures hurry past in absolute silence, their splayed toes gripping the slick slats, their guide-hands skimming the waist-high cords without breaking stride.

Then a different vibration climbs the basin-line—not a builder’s clean tap, not the splash of falling water, but a low, heavy shudder coming straight up through the root-column itself. Every bristle along my forearms bends back at once.

A split second later, the weir-listener’s fingers snap urgent pulses up the cord: *Broad-Head at the east grate. Heavy push. Harpoon-crews bracing.*

Under my curved black shell, with icy water sheeting past my knees and the dark gulf roaring softly between the wards, I hold the tension steady. I feel the sudden, terrible tautness when the iron-barbed lines go fast—the whole geometry of the water-works straining as a dozen thumbnail-broad hands haul in wordless unison against a blind giant in the black shallows. I pass the call for extra sinew-anchors to the shelf, feel the answering pulse, feel the slacken, the violent thrash, and finally—after a stretch of time that has no clock—the clean, flat, double-press from the pier that means *done, held, safe*.

My fingers are numb to the second knuckle. I tuck them inside my cuff for three slow breaths while the woolen hiss of the ceiling breaks back into separate threads, and then into single, spaced drops striking the stretched leaf-skins of the roofs with a muted *tock*.

Before I leave my post, I pull out a fresh length of oiled twine and tie the flood into the record: one tight hitch for the height of the surge, two loops for the beetle-harvest, and a heavy, braided knot—marked with a tiny scrap of salamander-rib—for the kill that will fill the oil-jars and heat the lamps of grease-fed patience until spring.

***

Then the relief-listener steps softly off the swaying causeway, brushes the back of my oiled shoulder to announce their arrival, and slips a bare wrist against mine.

For three seconds, in the chill open dark, we trade the state of the pocket in clipped, economical skin-talk: *North sluice still draining; slug cleared off tier two; south washer needs changing by noon-bruise.* Then cuffs pull tight again, our warm skin vanishes back behind cold eel-oil, and I let go of the lines.

I cross the forty paces of dark air back to the high shale shelf, my splayed feet knowing every slight dip in the suspension-causeway, my thumb reading the familiar knots along the waist-high guide-cord—landing, sharp shale corner, soft moss warning, upward stair—until I reach the low grafted arch of my own alley and push aside the heavy, overlapping oil-flap of my coil’s cell.

The change is instant and physical: four or five degrees of kinder air, thick with the sour, comforting breath of the ferment-beds smoldering invisibly beneath the split reed-slats.

Here, there are no tension-lines to damp, no iron genealogies to verify, no travel-bundles waiting for the roof to fall. I unlace my pale isopod plates and hang my leashed slate blade and bone awl on their wall-pegs. I peel away the cold, wet salamander-hide and eel-oil wraps until my skin is bare, flushing faint rose at the chest and throat in the cellar-warm dark.

Four other warm bodies shift gently on the mattress of combed moss-down and spider-silk to make room. I curl into the middle of the coil, pulling my stiff, brine-cold hands in against the warmth of someone’s back.

And here, at last, comes the third speed of language—the one that asks for no tension and leaves no knot behind. A warm palm settles flat and unhurried over my cold knuckles, thumbs working gently along the aching joints of my wrists, pressing a slow, wordless rhythm into the hollow above my collarbone. Not news. Not inventory. Only the quiet, unmistakable pressure that says: *The walls held. The water fell. You are inside the warmth.*

Overhead, beyond the grafted moss-vaults and the weeping roots and six feet of saturated turf, the noon-bruise has already faded back into seamless black. I close my lidless eyes against a shoulder, let my bristles go still, and rest inside the small, stubborn warmth we have built in the cold belly of the moor.
