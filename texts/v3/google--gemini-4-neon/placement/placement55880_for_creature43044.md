If I climb down past the rhyolite lip without deciding in advance what shape I wear, my first instinct is to wish myself heavy.

Standing on a shaded ramp near the middle tiers, watching a builder spend twenty silent minutes softening a single marble of resin and rock-dust between its blunt palms, I feel a sharp, quiet envy for that kind of weight. To think in *lean* and *load*. To press both hands against a bottle-trunked tree and feel the balance of a balcony forty feet overhead, not as an idea, not as a worry, but as a literal tug in the wood. To hold a stewardship that takes sixty years to cross twenty feet of air, and to know that when your legs finally stiffen, you will descend to the vaults and become a load-bearing rib beneath the plaza—never wasted, never blown away by the wind above the rim.

I try to imagine wearing those sandstone-colored plates, feeling the noon sun bake into my back until it turns russet. I try to imagine the patience of keeping my eyes fixed on one seam of shale for an entire season.

It doesn’t hold. Even as I try the body on, my attention keeps slipping off the parapet. I don’t want to stay on the third terrace for forty years; I want to know whether the crack under this walkway is the same fracture that shifted the northern steps last month, and whether the roots down by the salt cellars have felt it yet. My thoughts move too quickly, skimming across surfaces, linking distant corners before a single marble of amber paste would even soften. If I were a builder, I would be a restless one—bad at the long, geological stillness that makes masonry possible.

So I keep looking.

For a moment I linger near the eaves, beside the feathery-scaled perchers watching the bright oval of sky. There is dignity in that vigilance, in being the first ripple of a lifted foot that turns a whole plaza back into boulders. Yet their gaze is fixed outward, on what does not belong to the cleft. My pull is inward—down into the joints, the undersides, the places where two surfaces meet and almost fit.

Lower still, I watch the chalk-white beetles filing with silver-dusted mandibles toward the dim archways. I like their work better: scraping away just enough wax without ever breaking the green skin beneath, carrying it down to seal the vaults so the softest lives don't split in the dry air. Quiet maintenance. Keeping the vessel tight. Still, their world runs in a single, unvarying line from leaf to cellar, and something in me wants the whole vertical height of the hollow at once.

I don’t actually find myself until the sun tilts past the jagged western rim, and the bright stone turns from white to copper to slate.

I am already there—have been there all through the blinding noon, hanging motionless beneath the sagging arch of a braided root-bridge on the fourth tier, just where the upper walkways narrow inward toward the open sky. All day I have been nothing to look at: a shriveled, umber pendant dusted with dry bark, sealed tight against the baked air, heart ticking twice a minute while the lizards hunt right past me and the builders tread overhead without dislodging a mote of dust.

I do not do well in the flat glare of midday. In that raw heat, there is nothing for me to do except fold tight and wait.

Then the first thin blade of mountain cold spills over the rhyolite edge. The daytime warmth pooled in the basin begins to rise in a slow, invisible column toward the darkening sky, brushing my pleated skin as it lifts—and something inside my chest loosens.

I do not drop. I simply uncoil.

Four-jointed limbs dark as oiled rattan let go of their tight hug around my ribs and reach out along the underside of the braided root, ending in wide, almond-pale pads that make no sound at all on the bark. My torso opens into its true shape: a hollow, lantern-ribbed frame no bigger than a pair of cupped hands, wrapped in charcoal-plum velvet that traps a thin, precious sheath of still air against my skin. Where eyes would be, there is only a broad, matte hood pitted with wax-rimmed hollows.

The dark does not blind me. The dark is where the world finally becomes legible.

Hanging upside down beneath the fourth-tier span, I look across the cleft and see a five-story cathedral built entirely out of temperature. Every honeycombed sleeping-cell glows like banked coals where two or three builders lie curled behind their woven doors. The thick stems of the bottle-trunked trees hold a dull, lingering amber. The quartz plazas below bleed pale gray into the freezing stars. Everything solid in the daylight turns translucent to heat; I can read the whole city at a glance, not by what it looks like, but by how tightly it is holding onto its warmth.

And cutting across those glowing walls—razor-thin, piercing, unmistakable—are veins of dark-blue cold.

That is where I belong: in the gap between the warmth the city hoards and the sharp mountain air trying to get in.

I am a night-walker, a velvet-skin—though if there are habits among us, mine is an odd one. Most of my kind keep to a familiar pair of middle tiers, working the ramps and parapets they know by heart. I am drawn to the extremes. I start high, just under the jagged lip where the fresh rhyolite still remembers being shattered out of the earth, where the wind overhead scrapes closest to the cleft and the hairline fractures open first.

Moving upside down from braided arch to shale cornice to felt-backed vine, I weigh almost nothing. My caliper-limbs span gaps that would take a builder twenty minutes of careful ramping to cross. I step cleanly over the dormant gliders tiled beneath the vaults; I pass without a tremor beside the dry hammocks of the chalk spiders. Because I cannot carry stone, because I have no claws to pry shale and no blunt palms to roll bio-cement, my lightness is the only tool I have.

Halfway along the northern eaves, where a bottle-trunked root wedges directly into raw glassy basalt, my hooded face catches a sudden needle of blue cold.

In daylight, no eye could find it. It is narrower than a hair—a tension-seam the young mountain eased open two days ago with a single dry tick no louder than a falling seed. Yet tonight, where the faintly breath-dampened warmth of an upper corridor kisses that freezing draft from outside, microscopic beads of frost-dew are forming right along the lip of the black rock. In five minutes the dry air will steal them.

I anchor my almond pads to the cured resin around the seam, flatten my hollow chest against the wall, and press the supple pale-velvet ring of my mouth directly over the stone.

I do not waste a drop into the open night. Sealed flush against the weeping basalt, I drink the cold water straight off the crack—a tiny, sharp, mineral sip that tastes of fresh fracture and high sky. And as I drink, the glands along my jaw leave their faint, sticky smear along the lips of the seam: dark copper, smelling sharply of toasted clove and iron.

I cannot fix this wall. I will never have the weight to brace a pier or the decades to braid a root across a gap. All I can do is taste where the cold is leaking in, keep the moisture from escaping into the empty wind, and leave a bright, bitter scent-mark on the raw stone so that tomorrow, when the sun warms the ramps, someone with heavier, steadier hands will smell it and know where to press their palms.

There is a strange peace in that division of labor. I do not need to be the mason to belong to the masonry.

By midnight, I have threaded four tiers downward, tracing the whole thermal sentence of the basin: a braided joist straining slightly tighter than it did last week above the western lane; a small, restless fever behind a third-tier door where an aging builder’s shoulder-plates are loosening early; two new cold threads near the base of the quartz plaza where the mountain settled half a grain’s width since the last full moon. My hollow chest is full of the night’s map—every strain, every warmth, every hidden wound—and by now the cold has sharpened enough that my thin bones are beginning to ache.

On some nights, I slip into one of the middle-tier sleeping-cells through the two-finger slot above the woven door, dropping silently onto the mounded leaf-scrolls and draping myself over the throat of a resting mason. I groom the dry crusts of crystallized salt from the seams of its russet horn-plates, grateful for the banked-coal warmth rising from its unplated belly, and then press my sternum tight against its pale-gold dewlap to hum what I have found.

That moment is the truest thing about what I am here.

In the open air, I am mute. To open a throat to the wind would be madness; words thrown into dry space are only lost vapor. My voice does not exist at all until I am pressed flat against someone warm enough to hold it. Then—sternum to throat, cartilage to bone—the map pours out of me as a low, intricate thrumming that never leaks past the woven door. Not a lecture, not a warning shouted from a roof, just the exact geometry of the night passed directly into another ribcage. And when the builder answers with that deep, slow, geological note that travels down through its cushioned feet into the resin-wood floorboards and across three rooms, it steadies the frantic fluttering of my tiny heart. I give them the edges they couldn't see; they give me the weight I don't possess.

Yet on the coldest nights—the nights I feel most myself—I go past the middle terraces altogether.

I slip down through the low, shadowed archways beneath the plazas, past the quiet vaults of oily pods and translucent cakes of salt, stepping lightly around the blind, many-legged keepers whose antennae sweep like dry grass over the stone. Down here, even at midnight, the air holds no blue threads at all. The thick mother-roots of the bottle-trunked trees cradle the basalt floor, and behind double walls packed with leaf-scrolls lie the silver-waxed chambers that smell faintly of dry linen and clean beeswax.

Here the thermal light is the softest in the entire mountain.

Curled into concave shale niches along the vaulted wall sit the oldest builders, their horn-plates bleached to chalky white, dusted with protective silver wax by the endless quiet work of the beetles. Their legs can no longer take the ramps. They will never climb back into the noon sun. Tucked into the warm lee of their broad bellies and beneath the overhang of their chins are the newly hatched young—no bigger than my own folded body, pale and soft as damp parchment, drinking the slow, mineral-rich oil that weeps from the elders’ ancient throats.

I glide across the wax-smooth ceiling, drop soundlessly beside an elder whose back-plates are already beginning to take on the stillness of the wall behind her, and drape my velvet chest lightly across the curve where her chalk-white ribs meet the soft, shifting warmth of two hatchlings.

Her breathing is so slow I have to wait half a minute to catch the rise of it. Neither she nor the young startle; they know the weightless touch of almond pads, the dry brush of charcoal bristles. I tuck my long limbs carefully so I cover the hatchlings from the faintest draft, press my flat sternum firmly against the elder’s throat, and let my mouth seal shut.

Then I sing the whole five-story city straight through both of them.

Into the blind, motionless grandmother who has not left this vault in twelve seasons, I hum the exact posture of the fourth-tier viaduct she helped braid fifty years ago—the slight pull on its western span, the fresh copper-scented crack near the northern eaves, the steady warmth of her kin sleeping behind woven doors above her head. I let her feel that the ribs she shaped are still holding true, that the mountain shifted only a hair and the arches met it without breaking.

And because the hatchlings are pressed tight against her flank, that same bone-conducted vibration passes right through her chalk-white sternum into their soft, unhardened chests. Before they have ever climbed a single shale ramp, before their cartilage has cured into horn or their sealed eyes have ever seen the bright oval of sky, they learn the tension of the roots, the pitch of fresh basalt, the lean and load of every gallery overhead. They learn the city from the inside out, as a hum in the dark.

Deep beneath me, the elder answers—so low it is barely more than a slow shifting of weight, the resonance of someone already halfway to becoming stone. It passes into the hatchlings, and up through my hollow ribs, and out into the mother-roots beneath the floor. For two hours before the bitterest pre-dawn chill breaks, the oldest memory in the cleft, the softest beginning, and the weightless messenger between them share a single quiet pulse, losing not one molecule of breath to the empty sky five stories overhead.

When the faintest pre-dawn gray touches the rim—before the first sharp blade of sunlight slices into the well—I uncoil myself from the warm flank and climb.

Up past the salt cellars, past the waking plazas where woven doors are already nudging quietly open for the brief hour of dew on the silver-needled shrubs. Up past a fourth-tier parapet where two builders have already paused, lowering their broad snouts to a thin copper smear smelling of clove and iron, pressing the soft concentric ridges of their palms against the fresh stone to read its new angle.

They do not look up to thank me. There is nothing to thank; we are the same closed loop, turned inside out between dark and light.

Before the sun touches the upper roots, I reach my spot beneath the high braided bridge, pull my four-jointed limbs tight against my lantern ribs, seal my pores, and let my heart slow down to twice a minute. By the time the light fills the basin and makes the quartz shimmer, I am only a dusty gourd hanging in the deep shade—silent, unremarkable, holding no territory of my own, waiting for the heat to climb and break so I can unfold again and listen for where the mountain has moved.
