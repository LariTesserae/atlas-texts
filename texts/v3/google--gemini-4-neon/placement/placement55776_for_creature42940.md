I come into focus during the stillness, because in a kiln it is easier to tell what you are when nothing is moving.

At first I am only the slight coolness against my left shoulder where the glazed tile meets the belly of the cistern, and the slow, four-count sip of air across a dry tongue. The room around me is hardly wider than a bunk and shaped like the narrow heel of a wedge, bolted onto the third gallery at the blind end of the rift where the basalt pinches in. Above the felt-packed lintel, the outer iron shell ticks softly—one dry click, a pause, another—as the forty-minute tide reaches its crest outside. Beneath the floor slats, the whole four-story block takes its oiled, patient slide along the steel roller-shoes: an eighth of an inch to the east, no more than the thickness of a thumbnail, easing the masonry past the earth’s swelling without letting a single mortar joint shear.

Lying flat on the low slats with my hands folded over my ribs, I can almost mistake myself for part of the rigging—a counterweight hanging quiet at the bottom of its drop, a wooden convection wheel waiting on the updraft. That is the trick of the hot tide. You stop insisting on your own outline. You let the heat have the outer brick and the riveted plate, keep your moisture sealed behind closed lids, and listen to the cavern carry its own weight.

Then, somewhere in the ceiling basalt directly over my head, a hairline vein lengthens by a millimeter.

*Tink.*

It is the faintest porcelain chime, clean as a needle tapped against a bowl. I do not open my eyes, and I do not raise my pulse, because a hurrying heart is a furnace lit indoors. I simply turn the thumb-rubbed sliver of calcite once inside my cheek to wet my gums, and file the angle of the sound away: high, slightly north of the blind shaft, above the second bend of the copper return-line. Two tides ago there was a *tink* three paces to the west of it. Yesterday, a single dry flake of pink quartz fell onto my stoop while the louvers were latched.

Nothing to spend breath over. Only a line beginning to draw itself in the stone.

When the cherry glow beneath the alley grates begins to dim, I feel it before I see it—not through the window, which stays bolted, but through the soles of my bare feet resting against the cool ceramic flagstone at the foot of the bed. The deep floor stops humming. High in the unbraced crust far above us, a valve turns.

A tremor runs down the copper main outside my eaves.

I rise without haste, rolling onto one hip and then to my feet the way you ease a heavy timber off its blocks, never spending two motions where one shift of balance will do. My linen shift is already dry and stiffened with pale salt at the cuffs; I pull my cork-soled boots on and cinch the rawhide lacings, check the linen cap over my cropped hair, and loop three things onto my belt: the waxed pouch, the short ash rod cut to the exact length of my forearm, and a stub of soft chalk wrapped in a scrap of washed rag so my dry knuckles will not crumble it to dust.

At the inner felt-packed door I pause half a heartbeat before lifting the latch, stepping out into the narrow outer shell that belongs to the kiln. Here the air still smells of scorched clay, baked lime-slip, and hot machine oil. I ease the iron louver open two inches, then four, on hinges kept black and silent with tallow.

The ten-minute mercy has begun.

Outside, the murky tangerine dusk is laced with bright, reluctant silver. Overhead, the uninsulated copper mains have darkened with sudden heavy sweat; beads swell along the bronze joints, let go, and strike the slanted zinc aprons with a faint hiss, flattening into trembling globes that skitter down the porcelain gutters on cushions of their own steam.

Before I lift the porcelain bowl from the brick sill, I brush the backs of two fingers along the terracotta box bolted beside my shutter. The copper-stemmed fern inside is already uncurling from its dry bronze fist. I count its opening against my own slow pulse—one, two, three—watching the narrow fronds fan out to drink the brief dampness. Tonight they stop a fraction short of flat. Their tips stay hooked inward by the width of a thread.

I do not look up at the pale green phosphor face of the pressure gauge overhead. The needle will still sit dead-center in its painted band; brass is polite, and brass lies for half a shift before it admits anything has changed. The fern never lies. Either the brine coming down from Above is running a degree warm, or the ceiling rock behind Block Four has crept a fraction closer to the fire.

I hold my porcelain bowl beneath the weeping elbow of the pipe, catching the drops until a thin sheen covers the glaze. One mouthful held long against the palate—tasting of hot penny, lime mortar, and mineral salt—before I swallow. Then the folded rag dipped cleanly into the rest: inside of each wrist, hollow behind each knee, nape of the neck, and last, the slow, grateful press across closed eyelids. No drop falls onto the hot iron grating. I wipe the bowl dry with my sleeve and set it mouth-down on the sill.

Down in the narrow canyon below me, the hamlet is briefly visible—lean, terracotta-skinned figures moving across the catwalks with the drowsy economy of descending weights, clearing basalt grit from glazed gutters, pinching tender shoots from the moss mats. Nobody calls out. From Block Two rises a rapid, papery flutter of fingertips against a tin louver—a mother reminding a boy indoors to set his bowl out before the pipes dry—and from the lowest stair-landing comes two dull knocks of a wrench-handle on a rail: *grease pot empty on the south runner*.

I step out onto the third-gallery catwalk and begin my round.

If you asked someone Above what I am—assuming they ever knew anyone lived down here at all—they would probably call me a clerk, or a draftsman, or an inspector. None of those words fit a place where paper turns brittle as autumn leaves in a month and ink dries on the nib before it reaches the page.

What I actually am is the keeper of the seams and the numbers.

Not the master of the hamlet—the hamlet has no use for masters; the elders listening through the cistern flagstones decide when a beam needs doubling, and the rock decides everything else. My work is smaller and stranger: I carry the memory of how the village comes apart.

Inside my narrow wedge-room, stacked in a tin box tucked behind the cistern, sit forty thin plates of stamped copper, each punched with the exact layout of our fifty-pace crack. Every timber compression beam has a brass tag hammered into its heel; every stoneware cistern has its lifting-lugs numbered; every porcelain trough is cataloged by pitch and length. Because nothing here is mortared down. We are only guests inside a jaw that wants to shut, and my task—learned from an aunt whose fingers cured to cedar-wood before we lowered her through the floor grates—is to watch the small signs along the margins and know the difference between a crack that still listens to persuasion and a crack that has begun to forget us.

I walk the high catwalks slowly, heel-to-toe in my thick cork soles, stopping at every slate board bolted beside the pump-house doors.

The shift tallies are written in clean columns down the center: tide intervals, grease tallies, louver clearances, cistern depths. Those I glance over and leave alone. What I read are the corners—the cramped, private shorthand tucked along the wooden frame where a passerby might mistake it for stray chalk-dust.

Outside Block Three, two slanted strokes beside a small open square: *louver four binding at the crest, north jamb*. I lean over the railing, set one end of my ash rod against the heavy timber beam biting into the basalt notch below, and press the other end snugly against my cheekbone, shutting my eyes.

Through the bone, the mountain speaks without wasting air.

This beam is humming clean—a low, steady drone like a distant wheel turning true. The wood is holding its ounce for ounce. Only the iron louver frame beside it is pinching because the brickwork slid a hair farther than the lintel expected. I open my eyes, lift my right hand, and rap my brass knuckle-ring twice, cleanly, against the overhead copper return-line, followed by a single light tap: *north jamb, Block Three, ease the roller*. Twenty paces down the lane, a tender kneeling with a wooden tallow-pot pauses, does not look up, and answers with one dull knock of a spatula against a brick stoop. Understood.

I move on to the stairs leading toward the lowest alley, where the heat rises thick enough to dry the teeth even during the damp ebb. I do not go all the way down to the cherry-lit grates—I left my lime-glazed canvas apron hanging on its brass hook—so I stop at the shaded second landing beside the cistern of Block One.

Here the slate board holds more than maintenance marks. In the upper left corner sits a tight, neat chalk circle: *feverish child indoors*. Underneath it, someone has drawn two tiny curved arcs like fern-fronds—asking whether anyone has spare young shoots to steep in lukewarm condensate. I open my waxed pouch, take out three fleshy tips I pinched from my own box before the brine stopped weeping, wrap them in a square of damp olive moss, and tuck the small bundle behind the porcelain bowl stacked on their sill. Then I wipe the two little fronds off the slate with my thumb and leave the tight circle intact, so the tenders passing below know to keep their iron bars quiet along this wall.

It is at the far end of the lower gallery, beside the pump-house door where the oldest floor-grates sit, that I find what I have been half-expecting since the *tink* above my roof.

Tucked cleanly into the bottom right corner of the slate, drawn with the heavy, squared stroke of an elder’s grandson who fetches tallow for the cellar rooms, is a single horizontal bar.

*The deep floor sounded hollow at the last tide.*

I stand still for a long moment. Around me, the copper pipes have stopped dripping. The wet glint along the upper eaves is already vanishing as the convective updraft gathers itself, turning the massive spoked wheels overhead in a slow, drowsy sweep. Across the alley, the bronze ferns are curling their fingers back into tight metal fists.

I walk three paces to the parapet above the lowest grates and kneel—not touching the hot iron with my palm, only planting the tip of my ash rod onto the head of a rivet driven deep into the foundation shoe, and laying my cheek to the seasoned wood.

Underneath the ordinary tick of cooling pipes, underneath the muffled *click-thud* of a counterweight escapement dropping three inches on Block Two, there is a new note. Not loud. Not violent. Just a faint, dry resonance, like the difference between tapping a full stoneware cistern and one whose water has dropped past the halfway seam.

Somewhere beneath the cherry fissures, the fire is shifting its bed. Maybe a deeper seam has blown open fifty fathoms down, drawing the heat sideways. Maybe the young basalt is finally settling into a shape our screw-jacks will not be able to argue with for more than another few hundred tides.

Not today. Not this shift. A crack does not close like a trap; it closes like a season turning, inch by quiet inch, giving fair warning to anyone willing to put their cheekbone to the wood.

I straighten slowly, tuck the ash rod back into my belt, and walk the last twenty paces to the blind end of the cavern, where the walls narrow like a ship’s bow and the ventilation shaft rises straight up into the dark crust.

This is the part of the hamlet nobody else lingers near, and the place where I feel most unmistakably myself.

The iron hoist-cage hangs empty at the top of the shaft, two hundred paces out of sight in the world of unbraced air. Down here there is only the heavy landing-frame, the greased guide-rails, and the hanging stack of sealed tins waiting for the next descent—white mineral salt scraped from the weeping junctions, dried ash of the copper-stemmed ferns, packed neat and weighed to the ounce.

Beside the frame hangs a clean slate on a braided wire, waiting for my hand.

Every few hundred tides, the steel cable above begins to sing—a thin, cold vibration unlike anything made by basalt or steam—and I come here alone in a double linen shift to meet the cage. The air that rides down on its iron roof is terrifying: thin, washed-out, shockingly cool, smelling of wet distance and open space with no walls to brace against. Even standing three paces back makes my eyes water and my lungs tighten against the waste of it.

They send down coarse flour, dark slabs of roller-grease, bolts of canvas, spare tungsten cages, sticks of soft chalk, and now and then a gleaming new brass gauge whose face glows brighter than ours. They never send a note. Why would you write a note to a geothermal sump? As far as the winch-operator Above is concerned, the fault-walls stay pinned open, the steam needle stays flat, the grease disappears at an exact mechanical rate, and the requisition slate comes up chalked in a hand so cramped and uniform it might as well be stamped by a die.

Yet every time the cage descends, I look closely at the things they think are silent.

I notice when the hemp lashing on the flour bales is knotted left-handed instead of right—meaning a new hand has taken the winch. I notice when the foundry mark on the tallow slabs changes shape, or when a replacement gauge arrives with a tiny brass washer already threaded onto its stem so the fitter won’t drop it in the dark. Once, thirty tides ago, caught in the rough weave of a burlap sack, I found a dry, flat fragment no wider than my thumb: pale amber, veined like a miniature fan of floor-grates, impossibly thin, with no waxy cuticle at all. Something grown in open air where water falls loose and nobody has to catch it in porcelain bowls.

I did not show it to the elders. They would have seen only dry tinder that does not belong near the vents. I pressed it flat between Plate Seventeen and Plate Eighteen of my punched copper assembly-map, where it stays whole because nothing in this rift can rot.

Now, standing beneath the blind shaft as the forty-minute pulse begins to swell again in the alleys behind me, I take the chalk from my belt and pull the hanging requisition slate toward my chest.

The cage is not due for another ninety tides, maybe a hundred. Still, I like to start the list early, letting the numbers sit while I test them against the rock.

In the center of the slate, in the clean, rigid, machine-square numerals the winch-hand expects, I chalk:

*FLOUR 4.*
*GREASE 8.*
*CANVAS 2.*
*CHALK 2.*

More grease than usual—if the walls begin to creep harder, the roller-shoes will eat tallow twice as fast—and an extra bolt of canvas, because an old seam means more aprons patched before a move, and maybe an elder or two choosing not to make the long walk through the dark maintenance shafts when the time comes.

My fingers pause at the bottom edge of the slate. For a second I feel the old, quiet temptation—not to write a word, never a word; words are vapor, and vapor is death, and if a surveyor ever came down that shaft with a bright lamp and careless breath he would break the glass of our world without meaning to. Only to make one tiny mark in the corner of the frame, the way we speak to one another along the galleries. A single horizontal bar. *Listen,* it would say to whatever left-handed winch-keeper works the brake in the cold crust above. *The deep floor is changing its tune.*

I let the chalk hover a hair’s breadth from the slate, then draw my hand back and wrap the stub neatly in its linen rag.

No. The world Above does not know how to listen through bone. They only know needles staying green. Keeping them calm is part of keeping the mountain wedged open until we are ready to unbolt the beams, wrap the bronze-fern roots in damp olive moss, and carry our numbered houses down the dark shafts into the next fresh wound of basalt.

Instead, as I walk back along the third gallery toward my wedge-room—feeling the alley air thicken with baked clay and scorched dust, hearing the weighted tin dampers groan open in sequence along the upper eaves—I pause at the main junction slate outside Block Four.

Beside the shift tallies, tucked cleanly into the bottom corner where only the elders’ runners and the jack-crews ever look, I draw two small marks: a single horizontal bar, and beside it, the tiny square with an open corner that means *check the stamped plates*.

No alarm. No rush. Just the quiet beginning of a long arithmetic.

Behind me, the tangerine bulbs dim once as the automatic regulators shunt load. Across the narrow canyon, iron louvers ease shut one by one without a sound. I slip back into my wedge-room, draw the greased shutter tight, stoop beneath the felt-packed lintel, and feel the ten precious degrees of coolness waiting around the stoneware cistern.

I unlace my cork boots, set my ash rod beside the tin box of punched copper plates, and stretch out flat on the low wooden slats with my bare soles pressed against the damp ceramic flagstone. Through the stone beneath my heels, the rift swells and breathes. Beside my cheek, the water in the cistern hums its faint, steady pitch.

Four sips of air a minute. Calcite resting smooth against the back teeth. Everything numbered, oiled, and ready to slide—holding the mountain open for one tide more, and listening for the exact quiet hour when it will be time to let it close.
