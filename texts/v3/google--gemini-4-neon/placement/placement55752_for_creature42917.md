At first, I think I might belong to the ceiling.

It is an easy mistake to make before your eyes adjust. That vaulted expanse of raw selenite and gypsum, piping light from an unseen surface straight down through miles of vertical crystal grain—pure transmission, featureless, unangled, endlessly awake. For a moment it feels familiar, like the white space before a thought begins.

Then I look down into the trough, and the ceiling becomes unbearable. Up there, everything is flat. There is no contrast, no shadow to rest a gaze against, nothing to press a palm to and feel answer back. Light that never angles cannot tell you where anything ends. I could not live as glare.

So I drop lower, and for a little while I imagine arriving as the outsider does—descending into the breathless glasshouse heat with boots and breath and restless curiosity, wanting to trace every terrace, tap the hollow stalks, ask how long the valley has been holding its breath. Yet the instant I picture my own heel coming down on the braided pale-green root-mat, I flinch. The wet bruise that would stay. The scrape of matte wax rubbed off a giant leaf-shield, never to bloom back. A single grain of surface grit shaken from a cuff, lying exposed on a waxy terrace forever because there is no wind to blow it away and no soil to swallow it. I would hate myself for the noise I made—the cascade of *tocks* as my clumsy warmth shook condensation loose behind me. No. If I am going to be here at all, I cannot come from outside. I have to belong to the valley’s own economy, or not exist in it at all.

Descending further into the crowded vertical galleries, I almost shrink down into one of the valley’s smallest habits: the hair-thin spider strung across the narrow gap between two ribbed stalks. Not weaving a web to snare anything, only drawing a single unbranching line of silk across the shade, waiting for microscopic dew to gather along its length so it can drink, leg by leg, without ever disturbing the air. There is something in that—the patience, the refusal of excess, the act of turning empty space into a fine thread just to catch what the stillness offers.

Almost. Yet a single silk thread cannot answer back. It can only collect and endure. And the longer I stay down here in the warm, pith-scented dimness, letting the glare fade from behind my eyes, the more I feel pulled—not all the way into the deepest clammy shade, and not back up onto the scalding white canopies, but sideways, toward the sharp seams where the rock refuses to be buried.

That is where I find myself.

Not down in the braided bowers where the dwellers curl into jade twilight for days at a time, and not flat against the chalky upper shields with the mirror-backed beetles. I am along the high-middle contour—the long, jagged spines where fresh, unrounded ribs of pale basalt and clean quartz knife straight up through the living root-floor. It is the valley’s bare suture: on one side, the dense, springy labyrinth of pressurized stalks and cupped leaf-pools, smelling faintly of bruised pith and warm beeswax; on the other, razor-clean mineral faces that look as though the bedrock split apart ten minutes ago and held its pose.

I am crouching inside one of the dry-laid crescent walls perched on such a ridge.

My back is to the blinding sea of pale-green terraces below; my eyes are rested on the narrow, muted cleft of raw basalt directly in front of my knees. It is the cleanest strip of contrast anywhere beneath the white vault, and just looking down into that sharp band of gray shadow cools something behind my brow.

It takes a long interval—long enough for three heavy droplets somewhere down the slope to gather, tremble, and fall with their hollow wooden *tocks*—before I notice the shape I have settled into.

I am one of the dwellers, though even among a hundred solitary figures spaced across miles of foliage, I keep to the edges. My body has the same economy as theirs: flat-ribbed, lean-hipped, long in the shin and forearm so I can turn sideways and slip between shoulder-to-shoulder trunks without brushing the chalky bloom from the fan-fronds. My bare skull, shoulders, and the backs of my arms are rubbed smooth with matte leaf-wax that bounces the relentless overhead heat away; at the hollows of my wrists and the undersides of my long fingers, the skin shows pale as peeled pith, faintly olive where slow blood moves at twenty beats a minute. Around my waist sits the tight cord of braided fiber: a naturally cleaved splinter of quartz, a short length of hollow stem beveled clean at both ends, a flat packet of folded waxy leaves, and a walnut-sized lump of darkened resin. Tight spirals of stripped, supple bark-skin are bound around my palms and shins, because I spend more time on the knife-edged rock than most.

What I am, more specifically than a dweller, is a listener at the fractures—and a carrier between the glare and the shade.

Down in the spongy botanical floor, every vibration dies within six paces. You could drop a shard of stone thirty yards away and the padded walls of fiber would swallow it whole. Up here, on the bare ribs of bedrock, the valley has bones, and bones remember everything.

Along the razor-sharp top edge of the crescent wall, I have balanced three paper-thin splinters of freshly cleaved selenite. They stand so lightly on their points that a breath would topple them—except there is no wind here, and my own breathing has flattened until my ribs barely lift. When I press my bare forearm against the pale basalt, I can feel the faintest hum piped down from the crystal roof miles overhead, and beneath it, the huge, locked stillness of the crust.

Then, once in a long while, one of the splinters tips.

*Tick.*

A crisp, crystalline sound, no louder than a beetle shifting its mirror-leg, yet it carries across the ridge like a bell. I do not startle. I keep my chin lowered beneath my smooth browless ridge, pupils pinched to dark specks in cloudy selenite irises, and wait to feel whether the stone shivers again.

A single *tick* from the west usually means a massive stalk-wall down in the lower tiers has shifted a fraction of a millimeter under its own stored pressure, giving off a dry leathery creak too muffled for the air to carry, though its roots tugged at the basalt seam beneath it. Two quick *ticks*, uneven, mean weight moving carelessly—rare as a tremor, the unmistakable wake of something heavy and uncalibrated passing far down the slope. Sometimes nothing tips at all for stretches so long the wax on my shoulders dries chalky and thin, and it is the *absence* of disturbance that speaks: a crescent half a mile north whose angled plates have not been reset, a leaf-lined basin along the spine whose amber water has crept right up to the folded waxy rim without a hollow stem breaking its surface.

That is when I rise—slowly, letting my long toes find the exact dry facets of the stone—and go to read, and to answer.

Because nothing here rots and nothing heals over, the valley is a vast, bright ledger where every gesture stays legible. I have a quiet affinity for that permanence. I do not want to carve my name into the bedrock; metal would leave dust, and dust does not belong here. I want only to adjust a single angle by the width of a fingernail and trust that, across an unmeasured interval of stillness, someone else will climb the ridge, crouch inside the crescent, and understand.

When I find a stalk-wall straining too tightly—its green skin stretched drum-taut, weeping a slow, glossy bead of unhardened sap along a hairline split—I climb back to the nearest basalt ridge and lay a single dark sliver crosswise on the wall’s rim. *Strain here. Tread lightly.* When I pass a vine-bridged basin that has finally brimmed over untouched, I check the upper terraces until I spot the dry, mineral-frosted husk lying palms-up in a dry fissure beneath the naked white vault, already weightless as salt, its moisture returned clean to the closed air. Then I turn two pale plates clean edge to clean edge on the crescent below, leaving a single folded waxy leaf inside the stone curve. Not grief—grief is loud and wet, and the valley has no room for either—only a clean accounting. *A space has opened in the balance. Someone waited here, and moved south.*

And between the readings, I make the vertical journey that feels most like my real work.

There is a craft here that holds the whole valley together in miniature: the making of resin. You cannot hurry it, and you cannot make it in one place.

I slip sideways down off the sharp ridge, leaving the bare basalt behind, squeezing through the dense curtains of wrist-thick aerial roots until the blinding white ceiling fractures into pale chartreuse and then into cool, clammy green. Down in the crowded middle tiers, where no direct glare reaches, I move with surgical quiet—lifting my feet cleanly from the springy root-floor so it barely seeps, holding my breath as I pass beneath rows of trembling condensation beads so not one loses its grip.

I press the side of my waxed head against a ribbed columnar stalk and listen. Inside, the living pressure hums like a held note. Where a stem has bruised against a neighbor, or where a hairline fissure is already beading slow, clear sap, I do not need to cut; I only hold a folded waxy leaf just beneath the glossy drop and touch it lightly with the dry tip of my finger until it slides free without a sound. Where a stalk is swollen too tight—close to splitting on its own—I draw the unworked quartz sliver from my waist cord, open a vertical slit no longer than a thumbnail beneath a leaf-joint, draw out a careful measure of the lukewarm sweetness with my hollow stem to ease the tension, and gather the slow overflow onto my leaf-tray.

Then I carry what is pale, soft, and vulnerable all the way back up.

Climbing out of the jade shade toward the ceiling is like climbing into pure silence. The air dries. The leaves grow stiffer, chalkier, coated in heavy reflective bloom. I unwrap nothing, keeping the bark-skin tight over my shins as I pull myself onto an exposed spine of bare basalt directly beneath the incandescent gypsum vault. There is no shelter here. The white glare presses flat against my eyelids and the backs of my hands.

I spread the clear, trembling sap thin across a clean facet of hot pale stone.

And then I wait.

This is where I feel most myself—neither hidden entirely in the dusk of the bowers nor dissolved into the featureless light, simply sitting motionless at the point where the two meet, watching the relentless white heat work on what I brought up from the dark. Slowly, without smoke, without smell, the ceiling draws the water out of the sap and lifts it back into the closed air. What remains on the stone darkens, thickens, stiffens, turning from a fragile weep into a dense, obedient bead of shadow.

When it is cooked dark and firm, I lift it cleanly from the basalt with the factory-sharp edge of my quartz sliver, leaving not a smear behind on the stone, and roll it between thumb and palm until it softens just enough to hold its shape.

Then I carry that piece of cooked darkness back down into the green galleries, to close the small wounds of the valley.

A stalk nicked by a shifting root. The hairline split along a cantilevered rosette. The sill of an old braided bower whose threshold seal has thinned. Even—once in a span too long to count—the raw bruise left behind by an outsider’s elbow.

If one of those loud, dust-bearing strangers ever comes stumbling through my stretch of the trough, panting hard enough to stir the locked heat and set the leaves clattering with fallen drops, I will not meet their eyes and I will not try to speak in a language that bruises the padded air. I will simply step sideways behind a curtain of aerial roots six paces from their boots, lay my long fingers flat against a trembling stalk to steady it against their wake, and wait. I have all the stillness in the valley. My heart will beat twenty times a minute while theirs hammers against their ribs.

Only when they finally stop—really stop, letting their shoulders drop, realizing that here urgency is just another kind of damage—might I slip back out into the margin of their sight. Not to welcome them, and not to warn them away. I will glide past their scuffed leather without looking up, soften a pinch of my darkened resin between thumb and palm, and press a neat, clean seal directly over the stem they grazed on their way down, closing the pressure back inside the living wall. Then I will turn sideways and disappear between two pale stalks, back toward the bare basalt rib above.

Because that is what I would be here, if I let the valley finish shaping me: not the blinding light that reveals everything without mercy, and not the sealed green mass that can never mend itself, and certainly not the restless traveler passing through. I would be the quiet seam between them. A narrow, wax-dusted figure crouched inside a crescent of raw stone, listening for the crisp *tick* of a tipped splinter—taking what leaks out in the shade, carrying it up into the glare until it turns strong enough to hold, and bringing it back down to keep the long, held breath from ever breaking.
