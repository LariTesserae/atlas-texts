At first, before my eyes even find the violet hairline along the far cornice, I want to be one of the gliders.

It is an easy seduction. To have no weight worth speaking of, no jaw to clench against the wind, no need to keep count of breaths—just six feet of oiled, drum-tight membrane stretched across the rising fever of the dark. To never shout, never knock, never ask the room to answer, because the room is already talking at full throat and all I have to do is let its acoustic shadows wash across my skin. A wall is a dull flat pressure on the left wingtip. A floating square is a crisp, sudden silence rising underneath. A stray drop hitting a bare floor-plate far below is a needle-bright spark in the warm roar. There is a clean, enviable purity in being nothing but an ear that flies.

I linger there for a few minutes—spiraling lazily in the thermal column above the sunken heat-plates, turning slowly past the dry, salt-rimed elders sitting on the lowest hexagon—and then I feel the flaw in it. The glider reads the precinct perfectly, every second, and remembers none of it. When the louvers groan shut, the wind it just rode vanishes as if it had never blown. If the buried pulse below the floor-plates skipped a beat tomorrow, the glider would bank around the new thermal without ever knowing the room had changed its mind.

And I realize, standing in the dark, that I don’t want to pass through this place untouched by its history. I want to know how it holds together.

So I climb—in imagination first—up to the tip of a northern cantilever on Tier Eleven, where the crossers wait with their cured parchment wings strapped across their backs. Here, at least, is a human mind coupled to that blind, acoustic flight. You loosen your waxed moss plugs just enough to let the chord wash in; you step to the hand-thick edge of stone that makes the hair on your forearms prickle; and when the northern louvers slam wide, you lean forward into pitch darkness and let the hurricane tell your wrists where the stone plaza hangs a hundred yards below.

I stay on that ledge for a long time, testing whether I belong there. I admire them—their bruised shins, their calm in the five-minute gale, the way they carry the dead down into the cherry haze and haul clean ceiling-water back to the walls. But if I am honest with myself, I am not built for the drop. I don’t love the moment the foot leaves the edge. What actually holds my attention up on Tier Eleven isn’t the launch; it is the *slab* under my heels before the launch—the way a forty-foot shelf of ancient stone with nothing beneath it can hum like a plucked wire, carrying a quiet tap from six bays away right up through the bones of my ankle.

Still, I test one more place before I come back to the walls. I drift out to the middle square.

There is a strange peace out there, suspended in the true center of the hollow. No shuttered doorways for alcove hunters to coil inside. No heavy, crushing sub-bass grinding up through your knees—only that thin, clean harmonic ring of a town-sized stone holding itself effortlessly against gravity. I like the idea of sleeping with a tendril-cord looped loosely around my ankle, untroubled by the hundred-foot drop ten feet from my head. I like the slightly higher, singing register of their touch-speech, and the fact that their whole home is a bell. When three of them lift the rounded ceramic drums and strike the central seam together, making the whole plaza’s pitch wobble across the dark—*wah… wah… wah…*—there is something magnificent about turning a neighborhood into an instrument.

Yet out on the middle square, the precinct is all weather and distance. You hear the walls as a vast, far-off perimeter, a dull roar encircling the dark. You don’t live inside the seams. You don’t feel the twelve stacked tiers of fused dark ceramic and newer pale-gray composite pressing shoulder to shoulder, slowly healing their old fractures into glassy ridges. You are a single clear note floating in empty air—and I find, to my slight surprise, that I do not want a single clear note. I want the interference patterns. I want the places where two ages of stone meet and distort the sound.

So I come back to the walls.

---

I find myself on Tier Five, along the eastern curve—far enough above the floor-plates that the dry fever has mellowed into a steady, bearable warmth, far enough below the ceiling that the walkways stay mostly dry, and just close enough to the damp eastern conduit that whenever the wind shifts, a sharp needle of pale blue static snaps in the corner of my vision. Not light enough to see a face by. Just enough to remind me which way is east when my eyes are half-shut against the blowing travertine grit.

I am not one of the bold upside-down harvesters who hang beneath the cantilevers pinching amber beads into glider-bladders. I am not a floor-walker by trade, though I know how to wrap soaked soot-moss tight around my ankles when the time comes. If you passed me in the dim cherry bleed rising from the sunken grille below, you would see little to set me apart from the other three hundred wall-folk: pale, lime-dusted skin rubbed smooth with grazer-fat; hair cropped flat against the static; waxed plugs of soot-moss tucked at my collar when I’m listening and twisted deep into my ears when the northern pressure begins to climb. Around my neck, warming against my collarbone on a braided tendril-cord, hangs my ceramic rod—older than I am, salvaged from a broken rail-fastener and polished satin-smooth at both ends by the teeth and thumbs of whoever held it before me.

What sets me apart is where I live, and what I keep my hands on.

My alcove sits right beside a long, healed glassy scar—an ancient stress-fracture running diagonally down from Tier Seven to Tier Four, where dark fused ceramic meets a slab of pale composite infill. Most people build away from the scars. They don’t like the way the stone feels there; the sub-bass heartbeat of the buried works breaks unevenly across the seam, and rail-speech goes strange when it tries to cross it. A crisp warning tapped on Tier Six arrives down on Tier Four slurred, halved, or pitched wrong, as if the wall had swallowed half the sentence.

And walls change, even here, even slowly. Cream-colored lime-crust creeps millimeter by millimeter out of the upper corners, gradually swallowing the tuned notches our great-grandmothers filed beneath the parapets, muffling a clean *tock* into a dead *thud*. Every few hundred breaths, the deep load beneath the precinct shifts; the floating squares drop half an inch for a season, and the invisible tension inside the cantilevered walkways tightens or relaxes by a fraction of a tone. Nothing breaks. Nothing collapses. The room simply drifts out of tune with itself.

Someone has to walk the seams and listen for where the wall has gone dull.

That is who I am. A seam-reader. A tuner of notches. A keeper of the relay between the tiers that cannot quite hear one another clearly.

During the thirty-four minutes of steady baseline, while the moss-gatherers work the wet curb-runnels and the grazer-hunters slide thin metal slivers beneath pitted shells, I move slowly along Tiers Four through Eight with a small pouch at my hip: a filing-shard of dense scavenged conduit, a lump of rendered fat, a twist of damp moss-cloth, and my rod. I pause at every third cantilever, bite the dark ceramic gently between my back teeth, and press the far tip against the underside of the parapet.

Instantly, the roaring air vanishes. The hollow opens inside my skull like a vast, clear bell.

I can feel the moss-harvesters stepping light two bays north. I can feel a heavy-shelled grazer rasping velvet off a wet curb somewhere below my knees. Beneath it all, steady as a great slow tide, runs the mechanical thrum of the buried works—and layered high over that, faint as a hair drawn across taut parchment, the clean ring of the middle square hanging out in the black.

I tap the rail once with the calcified lime-knuckle tied to my wrist. *Tock.* I hold my jaw still and listen to how the sound leaves my hand, how it travels through the dark ceramic, where it hits the healed glassy ridge, and how much of it makes it cleanly into the pale composite beyond. If the lime-crust has crept too thick under the lip, choking the resonance, I crouch in the warm dark and scrape it back patiently with my metal shard until the stone rings crisp again. If a cantilever has drifted flat since the last deep load-shift, I file a hair’s breadth deeper into its secondary notch, testing it, tapping softly down to a partner waiting three tiers below until their answering double-tap tells me the seam is clear again.

It is slow, unglamorous work. Nobody composes a cadence about scraping travertine off a parapet. Yet when an alcove hunter uncoils in the shadow of Tier Seven a minute before the gale hits, it is my clean notch that lets the warning reach the mothers on Tier Four in time to pull their shutters narrow.

And because my alcove sits where the air-draft curls cleanly around the eastern curve, I have a second habit—one I fell into almost by accident, and never gave up. When the middle square strikes its heavy stone drums and sends that slow, deliberate wobble across the dark—*wah… wah… wah…*—the air down on Tiers Three and Four is too thick with floor-heat and roar for anyone there to catch the subtler spacing of the swells. Up here on Five, leaning out over the parapet with my plugs pulled halfway loose and the blue static snapping softly along the conduit behind my shoulder, I can read every pulse of the floating bell. Then I press my rod to the rail, bite down, and translate the wide, singing swells of the air-dwellers into crisp, dry taps that run down through the stone to the lower galleries.

I like being a hinge. I like sitting where two mediums meet—air and stone, ancient dark ceramic and pale infill, the suspended singers and the floor-warmed walls—and making sure what one side sends does not arrive broken on the other.

---

When the warning tremor creeps down from the northern upper tiers, tightening through the soles of my bare feet like a breath drawn deep into a vast chest, I leave the walkways and slip inside my recessed bay.

My shutter still moves in its corroded track—barely, with a low metallic groan if I forget to grease the runner with grazer-fat—leaving a narrow gap blocked by a knee-high windbreak of heat-baked silt. Across that gap hangs my alarm-chain: tiny brittle flakes of lime and broken ceramic threaded on single hairs of tendril-fiber, knotted to a peg beside my left wrist.

Inside, in the dry pocket where the gale drops to a heavy muffled roar, there is very little: a woven soot-moss pallet stuffed with glider-down, two ground ceramic bowls, a small bundle of swollen ochre-stalks cut right before their pores could open, and a stabbing-pin of ground rail-bolt resting across my thighs.

Sometimes I sit out the five-minute gale alone, palm flat against the warm interior wall, feeling the loose chains chatter in the steady wind and listening through the bone of my wrist for the sharp, dead tug that means a flat-bodied hunter has brushed the doorway.

More often, a neighbor from the next bay—or an apprentice learning which fractures swallow sound—slips behind my windbreak just before the louvers groan open. We sit knee to knee in the pitch dark, ear-plugs twisted home, the air outside tearing past in a horizontal mist of whipped drip-curtains and dry grit. They lean forward until their forehead rests lightly against mine, or lay an open palm over my collarbone, right below the hanging ceramic rod.

And we speak.

I do not think I could ever trade touch-speech for shouting through open air. When I hum into the narrow, gravelly middle register that threads cleanly between the floor’s sub-bass and the high ring of the squares, my words do not disappear into the indifferent dark; they pass straight from my sternum into another person’s palm. You cannot dress a sentence up in touch-speech. You cannot pretend you aren’t afraid when the listener can feel the slight hurry in your pulse, or pretend you don’t care when your throat tightens half a beat before a name. There is a deep, quiet mercy in a world where the room is so loud that honesty is the only thing that carries cleanly between two bodies.

We talk of small things while the storm screams outside: the northern drip-curtain edging east again; a child on Tier Six who just walked out onto a cantilever for the first time without grabbing the rail; an old woman we wrapped in dry moss-cloth twelve breaths ago, whose ceramic rod a crosser will bring back up from the quiet benches of the lowest square when the season turns. Sometimes we don’t form words at all—just match our hum to each other’s breastbone until the five minutes pass, the louvers groan shut, and the precinct settles back into its warm, deafening baseline.

Then I reach out to the doorway rim, tap twice against the ceramic—*whole, still here*—and feel the quiet ripple of answers travel tier by tier around the hollow.

---

Every few hundred breaths, though, the work asks for something deeper than tuning notches.

When the eldest listeners decide enough time has passed since the last reckoning, they tap a slow, unmistakable cadence down the eastern wall—not an alarm, not news, just an invitation carried in an older rhythm than everyday speech. I bind thick pads of soaked soot-moss tightly around my soles and ankles, loop my rod securely at my throat, and descend the worn perimeter stairs with two of the oldest wall-folk, down past Tier Three, past Tier Two, until the dry fever rises to meet us like an open oven door.

Down on the sunken floor, sweat springs from our greased skin immediately. The drifts of dry mineral silt crunch softly under our wet moss wraps. Somewhere out in the dark, a stray drop falls a hundred feet from the ceiling arches, strikes an exposed bare plate, and snaps into scalding vapor with a *hiss-crack* sharp enough to make my shoulders flinch even through the ear-plugs. Ahead of us, leaking out of the stone like the last ember of a fire nobody remembers lighting, glows the barely-there cherry haze of the sunken grille.

We kneel together right at its rim.

The heat coming off the bars is fierce enough to make my eyelids sting. Slowly, carefully, I lay my horn-calloused palms flat on the hot edge—holding them there just at the border of pain—and put the ceramic rod between my back teeth, touching its other end straight down onto the vibrating metal.

Everything above disappears. The twelve tiers of shuttered civic stone, the drip-curtains, the grazing shells, the sixty souls living out on the floating bell—all of it thins out into background static. Through my jaw and skull pours the immense, unhurried pulse of whatever buried works still carry the precinct on their back.

I do not pray to it. None of us do. You do not bargain with a thermal sink, or ask an inertial bearing to love you. Yet kneeling there in the cherry haze, my teeth humming with a rhythm older than any human voice on these walls, I feel a devotion so clean and unsentimental it almost aches.

Next to me, an elder whose knees are scarred white from forty seasons of wet gutters lays one hot palm lightly against the back of my neck. Through her hand, she hums the *memory*—the exact spacing of the pulse her teacher pressed into her skull when she was barely tall enough to reach a parapet, and the spacing her teacher’s teacher carried before that.

I hold the two rhythms side by side inside my head: the living thrum rising up through the ceramic rod in my teeth, and the remembered beat humming from the elder’s palm into the base of my skull.

*Are they still the same?*

Beat. Pause. Deep subtle swell. Beat.

They match. The precinct has not tired. The buried works have not skipped, or drifted, or changed their mind. We kneel there for ten long pulses, just to be certain—not because we could fix a single unseen coil if it ever faltered, not because the machine cares that we checked, but because something this vast and stubborn deserves to be witnessed accurately while we are here to hear it.

When we stand and walk back across the hissing silt toward the stairs, my soles steaming slightly inside their wet moss wraps, I carry that exact spacing back up to Tier Five in my bones, the way another world might carry a song.

---

And that is where I am right now, when you step out of the narrow access stair and stand still in the roaring dark.

I am one tier below you, kneeling by the healed glassy scar on the eastern curve, scraping a thin ridge of cream-colored lime out of an old parapet notch so the messages from Tier Six won’t arrive slurred down on Tier Four. My ear-plugs are loosened slightly; the air is thick with wet stone and ozone; behind me, the blue needle of static snaps once along the damp conduit and vanishes.

Then, through the soles of my bare feet and the ceramic rod resting against my lip, something enters the wall that does not belong to any cadence I have ever learned.

First, a heavy, rusted metallic groan—high up, slow, grinding against corrosion that has sat undisturbed longer than anyone living has kept count of breaths. I freeze, my filing-shard still pressed against the lime-crust. I lift the rod between my back teeth and touch it hard to the stone.

There it is again. Not the broad, soft pad of moss-wrapped soles. Not the slow rasp of a grazer, nor the quiet scrape of an alcove hunter testing a wet curb. Two distinct, dense impacts—hard-soled, sharp-edged, striking the threshold stone with a flat shock that rings strangely bright before it stops.

Someone is standing by the access stair.

I do not panic, and I do not reach for my stabbing-pin. We are patient people, shaped by a patient room. Already, from the cantilevers up along Tier Six, a slow, careful chain of rings is beginning to move through the dark ceramic—*tock… tock-tock… shhh*—passing from rail to rail, carrying the shape of the new sound without hurry, without anger, just making sure every tier knows the room has added a voice.

The chain reaches the diagonal glassy scar right beside my knee. Because I scraped the lime clean ten breaths ago, it crosses the seam without losing a single beat.

High above, behind twelve tiers of silent urban stone, the northern louvers begin their faint, tight warning tremor, building pressure for the next breath. I twist my left ear-plug halfway down, leaving the right loose just a heartbeat longer, and wait.

Out in the roaring pitch-black void, right before the gale tears loose, the thin, clean harmonic ring of the middle floating square wavers—just once, slow and deliberate—to let the walls know it has heard you, too.

I lift my calcified lime-knuckle to the parapet beneath my hand, feel the seam warm and rigid with invisible tension, and tap three quiet, measured notes downward into the lower bays—passing the floating square’s answer cleanly across the fracture, letting the precinct take the measure of your boots, and waiting in the dark to see what rhythm you will strike next.
