I do not find myself in the center of the city, nor out on the sharp basalt rim where the surveyors carry charged staves against the husk-stalkers.

When I let the light settle around me—that cool, shadow-soft brightness filtering through miles of papery canopy—I am somewhere halfway up.

Not on the ceiling gantries, where the arc-ribbons burn behind their parchment leaves, and not down on the springy silver-ochre turf where the spindle-grazers crop the rail margins. I am standing on the third tier of an unfinished transit pylon, thirty meters above the meadows, right at the line where the climbing wood stops. Below my boots, the blackened iron skeleton is already swallowed in pale, hollow-celled bark, warm to the touch and faintly humming with the sixty-cycle pulse of the geothermal mains. Above my shoulders, the bare steel rises stark and cold against the chalky light, its ceramic collars still waiting for the cream-colored tendrils to reach them.

That seam—the exact height where living architecture meets raw iron—is where I keep finding myself.

At first I think I am a framer, one of the crews who bolt the armatures and guide the young sclerophyte shoots into their joints. It would make sense: I have the long, spare reach of someone raised under this high ceiling, hollow-boned and buoyant enough that stepping onto a narrow crossbeam barely shifts my weight. My boots are cork-soled, fitted at the heel with a flat tongue of braided copper that whispers against the iron as I shift my stance.

Wait longer, though, and the image changes. I am not carrying bolts, nor a sack of structural vine-seed. Tied to my belt-loop are three ceramic strikers of graduated weights—one pale and dense, one porous, one thin as a reed—and slung at my hip is a small perforated clay jar with a cedar lid. Inside, curled motionless in the dark, sits a single pale-amber coil-spinner no wider than my thumb.

I am not here to build the frame. I am here to listen to how it is taking.

---

In a valley barely past its first generation, nobody quite knows yet how a wide-open city sounds as it grows.

The elders—the Seeders who climbed up out of the ancient, deep hollows—know the acoustics of cramped vaults. They know five-story-deep timber labyrinths where every root has been knitting into the bedrock for centuries, where static has a thousand old copper veins to follow downward, and where a tapped message travels short, familiar distances through dense, seasoned wood. They still walk near the edges of the causeways here, brushing a dark, creased palm against a pylon every dozen paces just to be sure the wall hasn't wandered off.

I was born in the first lean years after the basin opened, when the overhead canopies were still narrow strips of scaffolding and half the valley floor was raw, glittering gypsum scree. My parents were Seeders; my limbs are Open-Born. I can cross a kilometer of bright silver turf without feeling the vertigo that makes the elders keep their eyes on their boots, yet I still catch myself laying a bare palm flat against every pillar I pass. I do it without thinking, waiting for the quiet tremor beneath the bark.

And because I grew up between the cramped dark and the wide light, I drifted into a trade that barely had a name in the old caverns: I walk the seams of the growing city and read its continuity.

Here is the problem with a young anhydrous valley. When a framing crew bolts a fresh iron skeleton to the bedrock, it rings clean and high—a bright, crystalline percussion that carries a signaler’s rhythm across an entire ward. Then the sclerophytes climb it. Season by season, the woody vines knit the metal into seamless walls of porous cork-bark, threading fine coppery root-veins through the cambium to bleed stray voltage safely into the stone below.

Most of the time, the graft is clean. The bark wraps tight, the copper finds the iron-rich floor, and the note of the structure simply deepens from steel-bright to warm, resonant oak.

Sometimes, it doesn't. A ceramic joint shifts half a millimeter during a cool twilight contraction. An acid-dusted tendril hits a dry pocket of slate instead of iron-bearing rock. A copper cambium-vein pinches shut inside a bend of bark, leaving a blind hollow where static cannot drain. From the outside, the refinery wall or viaduct pier looks flawless—pale, matte, immaculate. Inside, charge is gathering in a silent knot, waiting for a dry spore-wind to turn a stray arc into fire.

That is what I listen for.

I take the middle ceramic striker from my hip and tap the bare iron collar just above the bark-line: *tink—tink—clack*. Then I drop to one knee, lay the flat of my left hand directly over the pale cork-skin below it, and strike again.

Through my palm, the vibration tells me almost everything. A clean graft carries a long, even buzz that fades smoothly downward toward the turf. A pinched vein or a hollow pocket stutters—a dry, papery *thrum* that turns back on itself instead of sinking into the floor.

I let the translucent inner membrane sweep sideways across my eyes to clear a speck of chalk-dust, and watch the fine silver keratin filaments along the back of my bare forearm. Near the ceramic collar, they lie flat, calm as combed grass. Two hand-spans lower, over a smooth bulge of cream-colored bark, they suddenly lift and lean inward, trembling toward a point no wider than a coin.

There it is. A knot of trapped charge.

I unhook the clay jar from my belt, crack the cedar lid, and coax the coil-spinner out onto the dry tip of my striker. She is light as a dried leaf-vein, her long antennae twitching briskly in the charged air. I hold her just shy of the bulge in the bark. Before I can even breathe out, she touches her spinnerets to the iron lip above, drops a single gossamer strand of luminous silicate silk, and lets it drift across the seam.

The thread does not hang straight. Halfway down, it bows inward toward the hidden pocket of voltage, glowing faintly blue in the cool autumn light, taut as a drawn bowstring.

I murmur to her—a soft, rustling click high in my palate, backed by a gentle hum from the vocal reeds in my collarbone—and spool her safely back into the dark clay jar with a pair of dried chaff-beetles from my pocket. Then I take a bone-handled scraper, peel back a neat triangle of outer cork without slicing the living cambium beneath, dust the pinched root-junction with a pinch of mineral-acid powder from a stoppered reed, and press a thin strip of braided copper mesh into the crease before folding the bark-flap shut again.

My wrist grazes a sharp burr on the iron flange as I stand. No drop of moisture appears—only a neat bead of clear, pale-amber resin rising along the vellum skin of my forearm, smelling sharply of crushed juniper and warm pine-pitch. By the time I reach the ground, it will have cured into a smooth, glossy seal.

---

My days are made of long walks between these small corrections.

I follow the dark-gray rail beds across the uncluttered silver meadows, stepping silently on the springy mat while driverless freight bogies glide past on the viaducts overhead. Long before one comes into view, the pale tufts along my cheekbones lift and tilt toward the coming current, and I hear the polite breathy whisper of graphite bearings and the faint *tick-tick* of brass grounding brushes bleeding static into the track.

Now and then I fall into step beside a herd of spindle-grazers cropping the turf along a power avenue. They barely turn their narrow heads. Their six broad, padded feet make no more sound than my own cork soles, and the air around them smells cleanly of shaved cedar and dry sawdust pellets. Sometimes a pair of Open-Born youths raking loose felt from a grazer's flanks will click a greeting at me across ten paces, and I answer with a low wooden trill from my collarbone, too quiet to carry past the next porcelain pylon.

Because I travel between the mature central wards and the raw, half-framed perimeter, people use me the way they use the rails—as a carrier of small continuities.

A Seeder in a terraced cloister stops me at the edge of a causeway, pressing a folded scrap of pressed reed-cloth into my palm—nothing written on it, only three notched marks along the edge—and clicks softly that her granddaughter is out on the north gypsum survey crew, and asks if I will tap their family cadence onto the outer rail when I pass the fourth substation, just so the girl knows her grandmother's knees held up well through the last twilight drop.

Out near the rim, where the silver felt breaks into newborn crust-lichen and unrounded stone, I find the dark-gray rail ending abruptly in a pile of chalk-marked sleepers. I crouch, press my copper heel-strap firmly into the mat, and strike the steel cleanly with my heaviest ceramic rod: *clack—clack-tink—clack*. Far down the line, half a kilometer away across the hushed scree, a faint answering rhythm vibrates back up through the soles of my boots—bright, quick, laughing in metal.

I do not linger long at the rim. The raw basalt behind the chalk-lines has no hum to it, no living root-vein to soften its chill, and every time the wind stirs a heap of dried bark-shavings near the raw armatures, my filaments prickle with the old, sharp suspicion of a husk-stalker lying flat and breathless in the scree. Still, I check the newly seeded corner stakes anyway, listening to make sure the pioneer tendrils are biting cleanly into the mineral seams.

---

The moment I belong to this valley most completely comes every few hours, without warning from the sky.

I am halfway back across a wide expanse of plume-sedge meadow when the steady sixty-cycle hum beneath my boots drops—cleanly, unmistakably—to a deep forty-cycle thrum.

I feel it in my ankles first, then rising through my hollow shins into my pleated ribs.

Instantly, everything in sight lets go of motion. Far down the causeway, a brush-crew’s electric scooter glides to a halt and drops its grounding-pin with a dull *tok*. On the flank of a six-story resin-refinery to the west, an automated pruning gantry freezes mid-stroke, its ceramic shears locking open as a last feather of pale wood-shavings drifts down onto the turf. High up on a bare iron truss, two tiny figures in grazer-felt tunics stop climbing and lay their copper-strapped palms flat against the steel.

I drop to one knee on the springy moss-mat, set my striker across my thigh, and press both knuckles firmly into the pale silver-ochre substrate.

Across the cavern, the colossal recessed fans shift their pitch. A cool, deliberate breeze rolls out across the basin. Around me, whole acres of feathery plume-sedge bow together in slow, luminous waves and loose their clouds of buoyant ochre spores into the clear morning light. Overhead, the electrostatic collector-nets strung between the tall masts deepen their hum, darkening steadily from stark white to pale gold as they draw the drifting harvest out of the air.

For ten long minutes, nobody moves. Nobody taps a rail. Nobody clicks a word.

In that stillness, I can feel the whole valley as a single dry instrument. My forearm filaments lift slightly as the charged breeze passes, then settle flat as the substrate drinks the excess through my grounded knuckles. High above the drifting spores, a trio of vellum-gliders ride a warm column rising from a distant substation, turning in lazy, shadowless spirals like scraps of drafting paper that have forgotten how to fall. My chest expands sideways, slow and unhurried, pulling cool cedar-scented air through my honeycombed lungs and releasing a faint breath of toasted anise.

It is not fear that holds us still—or not only fear of a flash in the dust. It is the rare, quiet relief of stopping at the exact same second as everyone else in the world, grounded to the same living floor, listening to the sky clean itself.

---

When the hum climbs back to sixty cycles and the meadows clear, I finish my circuit as the ceiling arc-ribbons begin their long, gentle turn toward steel-blue twilight.

Far overhead, the papery diffuser-canopies curl inward along their gantries, shaking loose their silent snow of chalky chaff. It sifts down over the refineries, the porcelain pylons, and my shoulders—weightless, dry, dustless by the time a single breath brushes it away. Across the cooling wards, the residential domes and cork-ribbed workshops begin their evening conversation of wooden knocks, contracting gently around their iron bones as the temperature drops.

Before I head back to my own terraced cloister, I always cut through the central plaza.

It is the quietest place in the city at dusk, ringed by tall iron framing-pillars pierced with smooth, hexagonal alcoves. In the ancient caverns my parents left behind, every alcove along every main avenue is filled; the cured, cedar-light frames of the ancestors sit folded into the junctions while centuries of sclerophyte wood knit their hollow bones straight into the load-bearing ribs of the roofs. When you tap a pillar down in those deep vaults, the sound is low, complex, and deeply anchored—the voice of a forest that has already grown around its dead.

Here, almost all the alcoves are still open. Only three hold quiet, light figures wrapped in luminous silicate silk, pale cream-colored tendrils barely beginning to cross their cured ribs.

I stop beside one of the empty pillars, where the steel blue of the evening sky glows through the open hexagonal slots, and touch my lightest ceramic striker to the iron flange.

*Tink.*

The note is clear, high, and faintly hollow—the sound of a vessel waiting to be tuned by time.

Beside the pillar, a First-Seeder sits on a low bench of compressed cork, wrapped to the chin in pale grazer-felt, her skin darkened to the warm, seasoned walnut of old cedar. Her vocal reeds are too dry now for long speech, so she doesn't try; she only lifts two stiffened fingers in quiet recognition as my filaments stir faintly in her direction. I nod back, knowing without asking which alcove above her shoulder has been left clear, and how calmly she listens to the pillar's note each evening while her own joints thicken toward wood.

---

By the time I climb the steps to my hexagonal dwelling, the pleats along my wrists and ankles feel taut from twenty hours of cool, chalky air.

Inside, the floor radiates a gentle, dry warmth from the geothermal ducts beneath the cork. Amber-resin windowpanes hold a calm honey glow against the deepening blue outside. Supper is unhurried: brittle spore-flour wafers warmed on the induction plate until the room smells of toasted grain, a crumbled cake of juniper-spiced starch, and two translucent lozenges of refined seed-oil held under my palate until they dissolve clean. When I am done, I brush the dish spotless with a stiff tuft of dried moss and set it in its cedar rack.

Then I take down the stoneware flask of fresh-pressed seed-oil, warm a few drops between my dry palms, and sit cross-legged on my woven silicate-silk mat. If my partner is home from the substation circuits, we sit knee to knee and work the oil patiently into the creases of each other’s shoulders, wrists, and heels, easing the dry tension of the day without needing a single clicked word. If I am alone on an off-shift, I tend my own knuckles slowly while the living timber ribs of the roof give their familiar midnight settling sounds overhead.

*Tok*, says the north rib above the skylight.

I pause, listening, fingers glistening faintly with warm oil before the matte vellum skin drinks it completely dry.

That seam is a little tight. Tomorrow, when the arc-ribbons brighten back toward cool autumn daylight, I will climb up with a pinch of acid-dust, a thin strip of copper, and a pale-amber spider in a clay jar, and listen to it again.

That is who I am in this place: someone born on the hinge between the deep vaults and the open sky, carrying a striker at my belt and a quiet ear for the joints—walking the seams of a young city while its iron is still learning the weight of wood, and while its pillars are still waiting for the sound of its first ancestors to settle into the grain.
