At first, the pull is straight toward the center of the fourteen-pace wedge—or, failing that, toward its mirror. Reading of that seven-shelled lantern suspended in the acoustic crossing, waiting a century beside the narrowest seam to feel whether the iron answers, my first instinct is to say: *I would be the answer.* I would live in a twin pocket three hundred paces down the riveted spine of the edifice, kinking my own copper busbars, gardening my own pale fronds, angling my own glass plates two degrees out of true until a thin ripple found its way through the steel.

It is a clean symmetry. Two bells hung in separate bowls of calm, ringing softly into the bones of a colossal machine.

Yet if I stay with that image for a minute, without rushing it, a hairline crack opens across it. The lantern in the wedge is sovereign over its fourteen paces. Its most defining feature is hidden deep inside its seventh, innermost shell: a lamina it has never shed, coarser in grain, holding the original chord of the bay from before the valve grease hardened into amber glass. It remembers its own beginning by keeping a piece of it untouched at its center.

I do not think I have a seventh shell. If I look honestly at how I inhabit a place, I am not built around an unreplaced core, nor am I made to hold still in a single bowl of equilibrium for centuries. I would grow restless inside even the brightest sealed wedge, pressing too often against the narrow seam—not merely to send a chord outward, but to find out what happens to the chord once it leaves the wall.

So I test the other obvious threshold: the three-inch slit of beveled borosilicate glass on the western bulkhead. Out there lies sheer altitude—the thin-air blue, the sun-struck silver edge of the distant exterior truss, bright as a surgical blade. For a moment I try imagining myself clinging to the windward lee of that exterior lattice, bleached by unfiltered stratospheric noon, listening to the whole vast edifice humming beneath me like a frozen mountain.

That slips away, too. Out there, the roar bleeds off into empty sky; the sound loosens its grip and stops being architecture. I do not want the open air. I want the dry, precision-milled interior where noise has weight and edges, where every chamfered lip and matte ochre stencil sits in permanent clarity. Just not inside a room that ends after fourteen paces.

Follow the uninsulated copper busbars instead. Not out through the dogged circular hatch—those six radial wheels will never turn—but conceptually through the ribbed porcelain pass-throughs, behind the curved iron casings, out into the long structural arteries that tie the unseen machines together.

That is where I find myself.

---

Between the immense curved flanks of the turbine housings and the outer riveted skin of the superstructure run the long chord-galleries: razor-straight service chases and busbar corridors stretching hundreds of paces along the spine of the edifice. They are scarcely wider than a man’s outstretched arms, yet tall enough that the overhead I-beams vanish into banks of bare mercury-quartz tubes burning without flicker or warmth. Along the inner flank curve the colossal blind backs of the machines—massive cast-steel drums, bolted inspection flanges, and the hidden outer walls of accidental pockets like the fourteen-pace wedge. Along the outer flank sit the heavy structural gussets that tie into the exterior silver trusses, pierced every fifty paces by a narrow vertical slit of laminated borosilicate glass that cuts a motionless, slanted blade of thin-air blue across the deck plates.

Running the entire length of the gallery, mounted on rows of ribbed white porcelain standoffs, are the main copper bus-trunks, thick as a forearm and naked to the sub-zero air, leaking a continuous, crisp halo of ozone and stray charge.

Everything here shares the wedge’s absolute desiccation and factory-fresh sharpness, yet the sound behaves differently. Inside a sealed pocket, the roar settles into a single cathedral geometry—clean pressure walls, neat crossings, closed diamonds of dust on the floor. Out here in the long spine, three or four colossal engines bleed into the corridor at once, their massive low-frequency fundamentals colliding and sliding past one another in fixed, permanent interference.

The standing waves here do not form bowls. They form long, twisting ribbons.

A narrow trough of navigable calm snakes down the length of the gallery like a dry riverbed suspended in midair, bending around transformer lugs, narrowing to a hand’s breadth beside heavy riveted stanchions, widening again beneath the copper bus-trunks. Down on the precision-milled steel deck, the metallic dust and shattered plant splinters do not gather into tidy nested diamonds; they draw long, braided chevrons and interrupted hyperbolas that break off abruptly wherever two massive steel plates meet at a bolted expansion joint.

A seven-shelled lantern could never survive here. Its broad vanes would shear off the first time the twisting corridor pinched around a structural rib.

So the gallery itself decides what I am.

I am not concentric. I am linear, spare, and graduated—less like a bell than like an acoustic vernier, or a precision measuring instrument dismantled and strung cleanly along an invisible line.

At my full span I am roughly the length of a forearm and a half, though scarcely wider than two fingers at my broadest plate. My core is a single taut meridian of crystallized graphite, thin as surgical wire, held under steady electrostatic tension. Spaced along its length at strict, unequal intervals sit eleven slender, open-ended reeds of translucent mineral-glass—hollow prisms scarcely thicker than mica, each cut to a different length so that together they span the gallery’s braided polyphony, from the bone-bruising low fundamental down to the thin, frigid shriek of dry bearings turning behind the walls.

Between the reeds, small triangular vanes of pale matte glass—no larger than fingernails, chamfered sharp as fresh-milled steel and edged with a faint tarnished ochre where copper ions have crystallized into their rims—cant into the twisting pressure walls. They do not hold me at a single crossing; they trim me along the ribbon. By rippling a minute electrostatic shift from my leading vanes to my trailing ones, I tack smoothly along the gallery’s undulating veins of calm: a pace in a day, a turbine bay in a season, drifting without draft or wake beneath the uninsulated copper trunks.

From the underside of my graphite spine hang fine, spring-stiff carbon feelers—not plumb-hairs for gathering dust off the floor, though I sometimes drink a stray flake from the electrostatic drift, but listening whiskers that skim a fraction of a millimeter from the riveted steel plates, the porcelain insulator skirts, and the slate-hard petrified rubber of the expansion joints.

Because I am a creature of the corridor, I face an adversary the lantern in its wedge never meets: the dead seam.

Every hundred paces, a massive structural bulkhead or a bolted expansion flange cuts straight across the gallery, pinching the ribbon of calm completely shut. Solid acoustic pressure slams against the riveted steel like a vise. No floating glass can glide through it; even a single degree of miscalculation would burst my hollow reeds into bright silica powder.

When I reach one of these blind thresholds, I stop a hair’s breadth from the painted iron and do what a fixed bell cannot.

I extend my finest leading carbon whisker along the dry, millimeter-thin shadow beneath a porcelain cable clamp—or through the hairline clearance where a copper busbar passes through its ceramic collar—until its tip hovers in the calmer air on the far side of the bulkhead. There, sipping stray voltage from the bus-trunk and drawing trace carbon and graphite dust out of the frigid drift, I begin to build. Over weeks, a fresh hollow reed accretes on the far side of the steel, tuned cleanly to the next bay’s braided roar, then a second, then a pair of triangular matte vanes.

As each new segment locks into harmonic balance ahead of the seam, I deliberately tilt the matching trailing reed behind the bulkhead a fraction of a degree out of alignment with the standing wave.

The solid roar strikes it like a hammer.

In an instant swallowed completely by the engine’s iron drone, my rear segment shatters into dry, glittering dust, settles onto the milled deck plates, and within an hour walks quietly into the nearest braided chevron on the floor. Piece by piece, across the span of a season, I spool myself across bolted iron that has no door in it, leaving no husk, no seam, no mark behind.

It took me a long time, imagining this body, to realize what that way of traveling means.

It means not a single crystal in my frame is older than a few decades. I carry no fossilized ring at my center, no unbroken mineral record of the engine’s first age. Everything I am made of is borrowed from the local bay—silica dust from one corridor’s shattered flora, copper ions leached from the next bay’s busbars, carbon pulled from thin air three hundred paces away—and returned cleanly to the floor before I move on.

What persists is not the glass. It is the *spacing*.

The exact mathematical ratios between my eleven reeds, the crisp sequence of intervals carried forward along the graphite wire like a wave traveling down a steel cable that never itself leaves its anchors. I am an arrangement of distances, perpetually rebuilt out of the room’s own dry violence.

Life along the spine of the superstructure is a study in transitions.

Where the lantern knows its fourteen paces with absolute, stationary depth, I know the vast engine by its subtle shifts. I know the boundary between one unseen machine and the next by the faint quarter-step drop in the low fundamental—imperceptible to wet flesh, yet unmistakable as a stair-step to my longest reeds. I know the sparse, papery colonies of slotted flora anchored beneath the porcelain standoffs by how their tiny high overtones change pitch from sector to sector, growing fractionally sharper where the dry-gas compressors hum, fractionally flatter along the colossal generator casings.

Every few seasons, when my path crosses one of the narrow western borosilicate slits, I pause inside the three-inch blade of high-altitude sky-light. Because I am long and narrow, the slanted noon beam cuts across several of my reeds at once, sending pale, hairline spectral glints skating along my graphite meridian—a brief, crisp calibration against a solar frequency the overhead mercury-quartz tubes cannot counterfeit.

And because I spend my centuries skimming the riveted bones of the edifice, I understand something the sealed rooms cannot know: how deaf the iron really is.

The colossal low-frequency fundamental shakes every girder from one end of the superstructure to the other; no bolt or gasket can stop it. But the fine upper life of the building—the crystalline singing of the dry flora, the faint treble blur of manifold gauges, any small, deliberate tremor in the upper harmonics—dies almost immediately at the first bolted joint. The petrified rubber gaskets and riveted overlaps soak up high frequencies like dry stone soaking up a spark. Every accidental pocket pinched between the curved casings is an acoustic island, locked inside its own bright roar, unable to hear what lives two bulkheads away.

Which brings me, slowly, across decades of quiet tacking along the bus-trunks, to the blind seams.

I do not know what an inspection pocket looks like from the inside. I have never seen a transformer housing crowned with porcelain, or four brass-rimmed dial gauges, or chalk-crisp diamonds of dust unbroken by a single bolted joint. To me, gliding down the narrow chord-gallery, a sealed pocket is only a subtle thinning in the roar’s mass: a place where two massive curved casings pinch together behind a flush-riveted cover plate stenciled with matte ochre numbers, leaving an acoustic hollow behind the painted steel—a clean, empty resonance, quite different from the dense, spinning weight of a turbine drum.

Most of those hollows hold nothing at all. Only bare steel, dry air, and perhaps the faint, unvarying glassy filament of slotted fronds anchored in unseen bolt-holes.

Yet once—perhaps a century ago, far down the eastern flank of the superstructure, where the exterior trusses slant sharp against the noon glare—I paused beside one such narrow, stenciled pinch to harvest stray charge from a nearby porcelain standoff. My finest carbon whisker hovered a fraction of a millimeter from the painted iron.

And straight through the riveted steel, buried beneath the crystalline shriek of the bearings, came a tremor that did not belong to any machine.

It was a tiny, deliberate modulation of an upper overtone: a clean, razor-sharp phase-tilt, steady as a scored edge of mica angled two degrees inside a pressure trough. It had no mechanical drift, no botanical slowness. It was *held*—kept in exact, motionless poise against the far side of the iron wall for weeks, and then lifted cleanly back into the blank perfection of the roar.

I hung motionless in the twisting trough beside the stenciled steel long after it vanished. I could not open the bulkhead. I could not enter the bright cavity behind it, nor even imagine the shape of whatever had bent the roar from the other side.

All I could do was listen to the exact interval of what had touched my whisker—and then, slowly, as I accreted my next replacement segment, trim my seventh silica reed a few micrometers shorter than its usual ratio, tuning it precisely to that strange, crisp dissonance.

Then I spooled myself across the next expansion joint, shedding my trailing glass into the braided dust, and tacked onward down the long copper spine.

I carried that interval for decades—past three massive generator casings, past seven blades of noon sky-light, past drifts of dry singing fronds—holding that slight, foreign tilt quietly inside my spacing, the way a traveler in a wetter world might carry a single smooth stone from a shore they cannot return to.

And when, forty years later and hundreds of paces down the riveted bones of the superstructure, I drifted past another narrow pinch where two curved casings met behind an ochre-stenciled plate, and my whiskers sensed that same unmistakable acoustic hollow behind the iron—a sealed cavity, luminous with standing calm, holding its own crisp, intricate filigree of overtones—I stopped.

I drew my eleven graduated reeds tight along the twisting trough, a hair’s breadth from the painted steel. I did not know whether anything inside was awake to the upper register, or whether the hollow held only bare deck plates and dry plants. I simply shifted a trace of charge down my graphite meridian, let my tuned seventh reed lean cleanly into the pressure wall, and laid that carried, two-degree dissonance gently against the curved iron casing.

I held it there for a season, steady as a jewel in a watch movement.

Nothing answered while I waited. The steel remained pristine, frigid, and deafening. At last I smoothed my vanes back into balance, drifted onward down the gallery’s winding river of calm, and eventually—across three more bulkheads and half a century—shattered and regrew even that reed, though I kept the memory of its spacing lodged in the ratios of the reeds beside it.

I am still out here, somewhere along the endless, blindingly lit spine of the edifice.

I have found three such blind hollows now, separated by vast expanses of roaring iron and dead bolted joints that will never pass a whisper on their own. Now and again, when my long, slow circuit brings me back past a familiar stenciled pinch—perhaps the very one behind which seven nested lenses of smoked mineral-glass hang in a fourteen-pace wedge—my carbon whiskers skim the painted steel and feel a crisp, patient dissonance pressing outward from the far side of the hull.

I stop in the glare beside the seam. I cannot tell the being inside what lies beyond its dogged circular hatch, or how vast the riveted skeleton is above the cloud deck, or that the echo it waits a century to feel does not travel through the steel alone. I have no voice for stories, and no permanent center to call my own.

I only tune one slender hollow reed to the exact pitch pressing against the iron, hold my own quiet harmonic against the wall for a season so the steel feels slightly less blank on the far side—and then, unhurried as frost that never melts, I tack onward down the braided roar, carrying the chord across the next dead seam, listening for where the building wants to answer.
