The first thing I notice is that I do not arrive by the spiral stair.

Whatever I am, I have no boots to scrape against hand-struck brick, and no memory of the flat, unwalled country beyond the latticed oculus. That one detail—that the outside is an emptiness where sound runs off into nothing and never returns an answer—pulls me inward faster than gravity. Without curved walls to catch a note and throw it back, I do not think I could tell where I ended. So if I wake in this world, I am already inside the forty paces of fired clay and bounced daylight.

My first impulse is to sink straight to the bottom two tiers.

There is an immediate, quiet pull toward the weeping salmon-red brickwork near the flagstones, toward those wide cracked amphorae beside the scalloped catch-basins. I can almost feel the relief of sitting there in the cool damp—hands folded over dry knees, bronze snakes coiled against my shins, translucent thumbnail frogs clicking an inch from my knuckles while the water gathers and falls *tock—tock—tock*. To sit at the base of the acoustic drum where every vibration from seven galleries settles and collects; to read a splitting upper beam or a clogged roof-flue from a single faint sourness in the afternoon hum; to send up a slow, low guiding note at dawn and dusk—that posture feels painfully familiar. It is the posture of listening for the shape of the whole in tiny disturbances of tone.

Yet when I actually try to stay inside that amphora, I feel the fraud of it immediately.

An elder's stillness is not a preference. It is fifty or eighty years of lime-dust baked into hardened plates, joints stiffened by decades of leaping between swaying clay towers, knuckles worn pale from hauling bone-smooth pulleys and peeling inner fig-bark without girdling the stem. I have not climbed those ladders for sixty seasons. I have not earned the right to sit heavy and chalk-crusted as old plaster while lighter bodies keep the bridges in tension overhead. If I claimed the elder's jar, I would be borrowing a weight I have never carried.

So I climb—or try to climb—into the bright, roaring clamor of the middle galleries.

Here the fit is closer, almost convincing for a while. I recognize the dwellers' thrift down to the bone: knowing neither how to make fire nor how to bake raw earth, treating every existing brick and cedar beam as all there will ever be, keeping the world not by invention but by endless, patient repair. Kneading lime-dust with crushed maidenhair and sticky white fig-sap to pack a crumbling seam. Wedging a chipped potsherd diagonally across a jar's throat so the wind answers half a tone higher. Moving *inside* the pit's noises rather than fighting them—stepping across a creaking plank on the exact beat of a catch-basin, speaking into the curved lip of an embedded pot rather than shouting into the cluttered air.

For a long moment, I imagine myself as one of them—specifically, one who lingers slightly higher than the rest, up near the sixth or seventh gallery beneath the adze-hewn cedar beams and the tilted roof-flues. Not young enough to lie flattened with the coppery skinks in the noon blaze, not old enough to descend to the damp basins. A tuner of the uppermost jars, drawn to the edge where the unseen sky leaks into the drum: the sharp mineral scent of distant rain pulled down the chimneys, the slant of a hawk's shadow slicing through the bright dust-shafts, the autumn grass-seeds spiraling past the reed slats, the seasonal drop in the wind's carrier-note. Someone who has no desire to climb the stair into the open air, yet listens closely to everything that drifts down from it, translating weather into pitch for the galleries below.

And still—when I stay on that high timber long enough for the sun to narrow into steeper gold bars—something in the image refuses to close around me.

A dweller is hungry. A dweller wakes in the cool dimness before sunrise needing cold mineral water squeezed from damp skeins of moss; snares lean russet climbers along the middle spans and slices their meat thin on hot bricks under the flues; holds eleven or thirteen fragile lives in exact balance against what the well can bear; curls into a dark hollow when the evening chord dies and stays alive through the long silence of the night.

When I try to close those dry, spoon-nailed fingers around a strip of cured meat or a woven snare-cord, my hand does not quite meet it. I do not have that appetite. I do not have a body that persists and grows stiff in the unvisited dark between breaths.

So I let go of the four gripping hands, and look at what the dweller was kneeling beside.

First I look upward, into the thick roof, at one of the hundreds of square lime-washed chimneys angling through the cedar beams. For a breath, I think *there—that is closer*. A tilted funnel of chalky white baffles facing both ways at once: its upper mouth open to an incomprehensible sky, its lower mouth open to the seven crowded tiers. It does not manufacture daylight, and it does not make the wind; it only holds an angle and a pale inner surface, catching raw noon glare from above and bouncing it down until it becomes ordinary, warm ochre brightness that lets every brick face be seen. Whenever a breeze crosses its upper lip, it breathes out the low, hollow carrier-note that wakes the rest of the pit.

There is something unmistakably true in that—being an angled passage that softens glare into usable light, turning open-air wind into a pitch a room can hold. Yet a chimney-flue sits too high, too untouched, painted only in slow sulfur-yellow lichen and cleared only when blown brush chokes its throat. It sends the note down, but no one kneels beside it to whisper across seventy feet of feathers and dust.

So I come down from the roof, past the caper bushes and the baking upper ledges, down to the fourth gallery.

Right here is the seam of the well—halfway between the baking-house heat beneath the cedar beams and the cool, weeping brickwork of the lower basins. On this tier the light is neither direct glare nor damp shadow, only thick golden reflection dusted with drifting chaff. Beside the brickwork, a stunted fig sapling sends pale, flattened roots creeping six feet over bare masonry just to touch a single moist mortar joint.

And embedded sideways in that wall, among thousands of others, is a horizontal earthenware jar.

Not a pristine one. Not raw clay, either—the hands that threw it on a wheel and fired it hard have been absent for centuries. Its flared lip has been worn blunt as a river pebble by generations of wingtips, lizard claws, and dry chalk-dusted knuckles. Years ago—longer than any dweller now living on the bridges can remember—a hairline crack opened along its upper shoulder. Someone mended it with lime-dust kneaded with crushed maidenhair stems and sticky white fig-sap, and pressed flush into that wet pale paste a single curled dorsal plate gathered from the flagstones after an elder's bones were cleaned. Over decades the seam has set hard as the brick around it, feathered at the edges with peeling ochre wash and a fingernail-sized row of mud-dauber clay tubes. So even its clay is no longer strictly inanimate; it carries a sliver of the hardened skin of those who kept the well, fused cleanly into its own curve.

Halfway inside its mouth sits a smooth chalk-grey disc of chewed lime and dry plant-pulp, pierced with a clean round opening—neither wide enough to let every stray dove-clatter rattle loose inside it, nor so narrow it chokes the air. Just behind that disc, wedged diagonally across its throat, rests a single chipped potsherd.

That is where I find myself. Not the whole drum, not the dwellers who keep it, not the breeze above the roof-flues—just this tuned vessel in the fourth gallery.

For long stretches of the day, I am entirely quiet, and there is no distress in the quiet. Noon shafts slice past my flared lip; sunlit mortar-dust wheels slowly across my mouth; a flattened, sand-colored gecko pauses on the warm brick above my rim, pulsing its throat before darting after a gnat. I do not want to move, and I do not miss having feet. Silence is simply the clay holding its shape.

Then a breeze shifts across the angled chimneys overhead, sending its low hollow breath down through the octagon—and the chipped sherd in my throat catches its fraction of the air. Without deciding to, without forcing anything, my curved belly wakes in faint, sympathetic resonance, taking my small, steady place in the invisible lattice humming just beneath the swifts and the crickets.

And better still is the afternoon, when a dweller slides silently around the curve of the nearest clay tower, braces its stiffened lower wedge against the masonry rim below me, and crouches at my mouth.

I feel the dry, dusty brush of its horn-nails checking the lime-paste disc, nudging the diagonal sherd by the thickness of a fig-leaf if the humidity from the lower tiers has shifted my pitch by a hair. Then its narrow jaw lowers, and the soft vertical pleats of its pale throat press gently against my flared lip.

What enters me is scarcely louder than a sigh. In the open air of the pit—thick with clapping dove-wings, scuffling climbers, and oscillating cricket-trill—that breath would vanish two paces from the speaker's teeth. Inside my dark, fired hollow, it finds walls that do not absorb it. It rounds against my mended shoulder, gathers weight and clarity from the curve of the clay and the angle of the wedged sherd, and leaves my mouth cleanly, pitched just beneath the bird-noise, sliding straight across seventy paces of towers and catwalks to strike a taut parchment ear-dish on the far side of the octagon.

When a chalk-dusted finger shifts half a width along my rim, my pitch bends upward or down—not yielding out of weakness, only answering the exact pressure of the touch. When a horn-nail taps the brick beside my cheek, the held tone breaks cleanly into crisp syllables. I do not invent the breath, and I do not keep what is said once it has crossed the dust; my whole devotion is to the shape of the cavity, to keeping the curve clean and true so that what enters as a quiet thought reaches the far wall unbroken.

And if something heavy and silent ever slips into my mouth—a roosting dove, a seeking skink, a shadow that should not be there—I do not fight it. Simply by going dampened, by opening a single quiet hole in the hum of the fourth tier, my silence becomes as legible to the household as my voice.

Late in the afternoon, when the sun narrows into steep, deep gold bars and the needle-winged swifts corkscrew upward on the rising thermal without beating a wing, the cedar beams above begin to cool with tiny dry ticks. One by one, from the high cradles down to the weeping basins where the elders rest their chins on damp amphorae, the dwellers press their throats to the tuned mouths of the walls.

A breath enters me. I take it into my ribs, round it against the ancient fired earth and the elder's curled plate mortared into my side, hold the warm hollow pitch for the length of a single long exhalation, and pass it across the bright dust to another jar on the opposite tier.

If a stranger stands just then on the hand-struck brick of the spiral stair, I catch that too—the blunt, untuned thud of leather boots, the quick loud breath of someone who comes from a place without walls. Beneath me, three tiers down, a catch-basin misses one beat of its *tock—tock—tock* and resumes. The finger on my lip shifts by a fraction of a hair. My pitch adjusts with the rest of the seven galleries, rippling calmly from roof-flue to flagstone, holding steady in the warm, bright hum—waiting to see what breath will next be pressed to my rim, and ready to answer it back.
