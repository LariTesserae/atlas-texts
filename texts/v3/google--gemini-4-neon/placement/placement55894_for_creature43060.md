At first I think I would climb straight to the windward rim and stay there. There is something immediately tempting about the net-tenders: the cold shear of high air, the miles of tarnished copper gauze singing in the mist, the honest, solitary work of splicing ten thousand fine threads until your fingers turn permanently green-blue. A life stripped down to weather, wire, and tension.

And then I linger a moment longer and know I wouldn’t last a season up there. Too much sky, too clean a boundary between what is inside and what is out.

I test the opposite pull—the deep ventilation shafts, fifty tiers down into the oiled dusk where the engine thrum becomes a weight behind the ribs and the pale tenders read pinhole leaks by the prickle on their forearms. That draws me too: the quiet intimacy with a vast, hidden anatomy, the sense of living inside the warmth that keeps everyone else alive. Yet even as I picture it, I feel the lack of birdsong and green light like a cramped lung.

I wouldn’t belong to the blinding upper sun-forges either, nor to the high fig-binders who spend forty years persuading a single banyan root to take the weight of a gantry mast. I admire the patience of stone and wood, the slow majesty of handing a load across three lifetimes, but my attention is shorter and quicker than a tree’s, and less certain than a hammer’s.

If I let my feet choose without arguing with them, they stop about six decks down from the open glare, on the leeward shoulder of the bowl, where the sunlit galleries roof over into damp emerald twilight.

It is not a dramatic place. It is a hinge.

Above me, two massive concrete aqueducts cross at an oblique angle, leaving a ragged triangular cleft choked with maidenhair fern and the dark, braided trunks of old figs. Noon never hits this deck directly; it breaks into shifting green shafts that move across the brickwork over the course of an afternoon like the hands of a slow clock. Below my feet, through the iron floor-grates, the great updraft rises steady and warm from the unseen depths, carrying the faint, clean smell of heated copper and tallow. If you stand here with one palm on the shaded brick and the other held out over the grate, you can feel both halves of the valley at once—the cool drip of condensed cloud-water coming down from the windward nets, and the patient, invisible breath of the deep engines pushing up to meet it.

Right through the heart of this bay runs one of the slow vertical transfer shafts, and inside it, climbing endlessly out of the luminous mist at the pace of a walking beetle, moves a heavy iron bucket-chain.

*Clack—sigh. Clack—sigh.* Every four hours the loop completes itself.

I am not sure what the basin’s people would call someone who settles into a corner like this. Not a guild-master, not quite a cistern-keeper. Maybe just *the one at the lee-sheave*, or *the reed*—the thing that sits in the narrow neck between two chambers and vibrates when the pressure shifts on either side.

My alcove is an old brick junction-vault tucked behind a curving bundle of insulated steam-mains, half-screened from the catwalk by hanging fern-curtains and a trellis of wild gourd. My cork sleeping-mat lies over the perforated iron where the warm draft keeps the damp out of my joints at night. Everything I own is tethered: my stoneware cup, my bone stylus, my small brass burnishing-roller, the heavy lead-headed mallet hooked to my belt-ring, and the fan of five sounding-pegs slung on a greased cord at my collarbone—citrus-wood for steam-ducts, dense fig-heart for load-bearing iron, a sliver of cured horn for high-tension cables. Even sitting cross-legged on my mat, the short hemp lanyard at my waist stays clipped to a rusted conduit-clamp. I have dropped a brass washer once in my life, as a child, and stood frozen over the grate listening to it chime off three girders before the warm air swallowed it past the ring. I never needed the lesson twice.

My skin is neither the oiled walnut of the louver-walkers above nor the peeled-willow pale of the deep tenders below; it is the odd, muted shade of shaded bronze, dusted at the knees with pale lime from leaning against the cistern walls. When I climb up to the glaring rim to trade for dried citrus or oiled bast, I pull a woven shade-cloth low over my brow. My front incisors carry the beginning of the smooth half-moon notch worn by years of biting down on sounding-wood.

What I do here has very little to do with making anything new. Mostly, I listen, and I translate.

The valley speaks in four or five different tongues that rarely meet on their own, and almost all of them pass within arm’s reach of my ledge.

Out past the hanging ferns, across two miles of bright haze, the opposite rim talks in sparks. Every morning I clean a palm-sized square of soot-backed brass hinged to the lip of my parapet, and through a narrow gap in the vines I watch the skeletal derricks on the far side for the slow, deliberate winks of sun. *Cloud-bank thickening at the third windward mast. East suspension span needs fresh hemp by dusk. Old Mara on the south terrace stopped breathing before noon; the bay beneath the second pier is open.*

Down through the wall behind my back runs a continuous twelve-inch iron return-main. When the pipe-walkers want to say something too long for a mirror-flash, or when afternoon fog blinds the gulf, the iron itself begins to speak in slow, dry knocks that outrun the wind. I rest my bare sole against the flange while I peel a fig, reading the rhythm through the horn of my heel before I even reach for my mallet to answer.

And then there are the buckets.

They rise out of the warm mist damp with condensation, creaking softly as they tip around the overhead sheave and level out across my bay for twenty slow paces before dipping back toward the depths. I don’t stop the chain—nothing in the basin is meant to be stopped if it can be met in motion. I walk beside the moving iron bucket with my lanyard sliding *clink-slide-clink* along the overhead rail, lift out what is meant for the upper galleries—a brick of pale machine-tallow wrapped in oiled cloth, a handful of freshly peened bronze pins, a damp sack of dark mineral ash—and set in its place the bundled dried figs, waxed gourds of sharp citrus juice, and fresh coils of braided hemp left on my rack by the rim-folk.

What I look for first, tucked beneath the twine of the goods, is a thumb-length strip of soft grey lead.

The pale ones below almost never come up into the green light, and they do not care for long speeches. They write with the point of a hardened nail on scraps of salvaged flashing: a quick, spare geometry of circles, chevrons, tally marks, and bent arrows. A weeping packing-gland on the seventh heat-exchanger. A demand for bitter peel before the winter-damp settles in the lower tiers. A warning that the baritone thrum has shifted half a breath slower on the leeward side, and that someone above should check whether a counterweight balance-tank has fouled with fig-roots.

Nothing is wasted here, not even the metal that carries words. Once I have read a lead strip and chalked its meaning onto my slate, I lay it on a flat iron plate and roll it smooth with my brass burnisher until the old scratches fold back into the grey, leaving only the faintest ghost-ribs of last season’s leaky valves beneath the clean face. Then I pick up my bone stylus, scratch the rim’s reply into the soft metal, and tuck it into the next downward bucket.

You learn people that way, even if you never see their faces. There is an older tender somewhere sixty decks beneath my feet who draws a steam-valve with a small, unnecessary curl at the stem—unhurried, neat, leaving generous margins on the lead. When I see that curl, I know the problem can wait two days; I sometimes slip an extra strip of candied citrus rind inside the waxed parcel just for them. There is a younger one whose nail gouges deep, jagged burrs into the edge of the strip, always asking for tallow or hemp a loop too early. I draw my replies to that one larger, slower, with cleaner lines—a calm hand laid on a shoulder through half a mile of mist.

Once, years ago, one of them actually climbed up as far as my transfer-deck because a seized bucket-pin had to be driven out where the chain leveled. They sat on the darkest side of my alcove with a narrow-slit hood of smoked horn-glass pulled over their eyes, smelling warmly of mineral damp and clean grease, barely larger than an upper-tier adolescent yet carrying the dense, quiet stillness of an elder. We did not waste breath shouting over the shaft’s roar. They simply reached out a pale, hairless hand, rested two fingers lightly against the bone of my wrist, and hummed a low, dry chord into my arm while pointing at the worn bronze bushing. I understood them better than half the people who shout across the sunlit terraces. When they went back down the iron ladder into the warm dusk, they left a single snow-white root-tuber resting on my cork mat—crisp, faintly sweet, tasting of limestone and nothing that had ever known the sun.

Still, if I am honest about what keeps me tethered to this exact ledge, it isn’t only the mirrors or the buckets. It is the birds.

Because I leave bruised fig-skins and crushed beetle-shells on the rusted lip of my parapet, and because I move slowly enough that they have long since filed me under *masonry*, the glossy blue-black flocks use the overhead pipe-bundles outside my vault as a noon roost. Twenty or thirty of them sit ruffled in the cool green draft, copper throats flashing when a shaft of light catches them, rehearsing everything they heard that morning across four miles of iron valley.

To an outsider it is just a bright, liquid chatter broken by comic little *tink-hiss* impressions of steam-cocks. If you sit still in the emerald shade long enough, though, the basin’s entire acoustic map begins to lay itself out on the railing in front of you.

A young male drops in from the sunlit upper rim singing the ordinary wet wheeze of the West Cistern ram—nothing wrong there, that valve has sounded like a clearing throat since before I was born. Beside him, an older bird preens a wing and drops a dry, three-beat *skree-tick* straight into the middle of its courtship trill.

I stop rolling my lead strip. I don’t move my head; I just let the sound sit behind my eyes until it finds its matching memory.

Not a steam-cock. Not a sun-louver cable—those groan lower, in the wood-and-hemp register. That sharp, brassy *skree-tick* is dry metal kissing dry metal on a slow nine-second revolution. The north flywheel on the third aqueduct pier, or the idler pulley just leeward of the old citrus court.

I unclip a peg of dense fig-heart from my collarbone, climb three paces down the damp brick stair to the nearest cross-brace that ties into the north truss, press the blunt end hard against a rusted rivet-head, and bite down gently between my front teeth.

At once the valley disappears. The roar of the wind-wires, the clicking amber frogs in the flooded conduit below, the chattering grey climbers stealing nuts from the hanging gourds—all of it drops away behind the bone of my jaw. Inside my skull there is only the clean, dark baritone of the great girders, humming with the slow breath of the unseen engines miles down. I hold my breath and listen through the iron until, riding high and thin on the back of the deeper hum, I catch it: every nine seconds, a tiny, dry grit of vibration, like a grain of sand dragged across a knife-edge.

A bearing running dry, half a mile rimward, maybe three days before it scores the axle.

Nobody has flashed a mirror about it yet. The bucket-chain hasn’t brought up a warning. The grey climbers haven’t noticed because it hasn’t begun to shudder. Only a copper-throated starling that nested in a defunct junction-box near the pulley heard the new note and thought it pretty enough to steal.

I let the sounding-peg fall back against my chest on its cord, climb quietly back into the green dusk of my bay, and stand at the parapet with my brass shutter in my hand. I wait for a gap in the drifting condensation mist, tilt the burnished face into a thin needle of noon glare, and send four slow, careful sparks across the gulf toward the grease-sheds on the north shoulder. Then I take up my mallet and tap the same small warning gently into the warm iron pipe behind me, just in case the lower tenders are feeling the extra drag on the return-shaft.

That is who I would be here. Not the cloud-catcher, not the root-binder, not the unseen fire at the bottom of the world. Just an ear pressed to the seam where they overlap—a small, patient membrane keeping the long loops from drifting out of tune with each other.

Late in the afternoon, when the sun drops toward the far skeletal derricks and the vast glass-and-iron louvers groan into their evening angles, a single broad blade of gold light finally clears the overhanging aqueduct and slides straight across my bay. For twenty minutes the damp brick warms, the hanging maidenhair ferns glow fierce and translucent, and the fine mist rising out of the bucket-shaft turns into a curtain of drifting amber sparks.

The buckets keep coming up out of the glowing haze, unhurried, carrying their grease and ash and scratched grey metal. A glossy bird lands three feet from my knee, tilts its bright dark eye at me, and sings the clean, contented sigh of a newly greased pulley—or maybe just a wind-wire humming two miles away. I smile, wrap my knees in my arms, and rest the flat of my back against the warm iron main while the first small nut-oil wicks begin to catch fire in the cistern arches across the bowl.

And when my ears finally grow too dull for the bird-band above the turbine hum, and the notch in my front teeth has worn too deep to grip the sounding-wood cleanly, I know where I will go. Just outside my bay, where the great cast-iron bracket carries the weight of the bucket-chain’s transfer-sheave, a young strangler fig is already wrapping its pale aerial roots around the rusted bolts. I have been packing lime-dust and crushed fig-skins into its masonry pocket for years.

When the time comes, they can wrap me in rough bast cloth and lay me down inside that dark, mineral-rich bay. Within a lifetime, my bones will have thickened into the braided wood holding the sheave aloft—quiet, rooted, tethered forever to the iron, still holding up the slow loop that lets the sunlit rim and the warm dark keep speaking to one another across the drop.
