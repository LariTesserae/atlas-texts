If I let myself arrive in this basin as I am now—soft-soled, blinking, expecting warmth from brightness and meaning from air—the saddle would erase me in an afternoon. My eyes would burn raw on the silver wash, my boots would skate off the slick marble veins, and the wind would tear my voice out of my mouth before it crossed three paces.

So when I look for where I belong here, I don't arrive from below. I let the boots go. I let the heat go, and the need for dark corners, and the habit of expecting space to stay polite between one footstep and the next. What settles out of the glare is lower, denser, colder, and much quieter.

I find myself crouched under a deep canted brow of pale gneiss, halfway up the inner wall of a northern amphitheater.

It is not a dramatic perch—not one of the knife-edge outer spurs where the cloud-wall strikes first, and not the drowned floor of the looping chutes where the cobbles thunder like millstones. It is a shelf wide enough to squat on and three strides long, tucked behind a curving slate fin that breaks the worst of the shrieking crosswind into a steady, bearable groan. Even here there is no shade; the wet calcite floor throws the hammered white sky straight up against the underside of the overhang, painting my folded knees and bare shins in cold pewter. My eyelids are drawn nearly shut, two glinting horizontal seams taking the valley in as bands of wet edge and light.

I am one of the basin's walkers—broad-footed, bare-skinned, sealed in the thin waxy chill that sheds condensation in clean sheets—though if you watched me through several breaths of the valley, you might notice I stay closer to one seam than most.

Just outside my drip-line, a hair-thin vertical crack splits the wall of pale gneiss. Most fractures only need a single cold-hammered plate of copper to tell a passerby whether the ground ahead will keep its distance. Into this crack, pinned tight with tiny slate wedges, I keep three: a wide, blunt tongue at the bottom, a narrow blade above it, and at the top a slotted disc no broader than my palm.

Three disagreements meet in this one hand-span of stone. Half a pace beyond the overhang, a spatial crease folds my ledge directly onto a high exposed ridge three hundred yards across the basin, where a slender cairn leans against a different stack of wet spires. Two strides to the left, the pull of the rock begins to lean sideways, tilting runoff into gleaming diagonal ropes along the cliff face. And right across the flat apron at my feet runs a quiet quartz vein, keeping a shallow, wind-sheltered catch-basin glassy and wet on one side while a strip of pale mineral salt bakes dry and fast in the cold wind on the other.

When the gale drives down the stone corridor, my three copper blades do not sing a single note. They hold a thin, shifting triad. Most hours it hangs just shy of clean—a cold metallic interval that hums beneath the cataract roar. When the deep valley floor settles into brief agreement, the three notes lock together into a flat, steady fifth you can feel in the roots of your back teeth. When a fold begins to drift, the top slotted disc drops out of the chord first and begins to shudder in dry, angry bursts.

That is what I have grown around, the way a pothole cobble wears into the shape of its own flooded bowl: the intervals between things that almost agree.

Look at my body and you can read the shelf on it. The heel of my left palm is stained a faint, permanent blue-green worked deep into the thickened fissures of the skin, from years of prizing raw native copper out of yawning faults and beating it thin on a gneiss anvil. Above my left brow-eave sits a pale, flattened notch where a spinning glassy cobble clipped me once when I stayed half a beat too long inside a slackened chute. My back teeth are already ground smooth and stained dark iron-grey along the rims from decades of chewing brittle rust-crust skimmed off the quartz pans. My heartbeat sits slow and frugal beneath dense, cold-insulated ribs—barely more than a distant drip inside a wet cavern during the long hours of midday glare.

In the full brightness, I barely move. Movement wastes warmth, and the blinding white wash flattens distance into noise anyway. Sometimes I press my bare cheekbone straight against the pale continuous vein of gneiss that threads beneath my overhang and runs unbroken across two roaring slots to the far side of the amphitheater.

The air up here is a liar. It throws a cascade from half a mile off straight into your hooded ear and swallows the torrent foaming ten paces below your heels. Solid rock does not lie. Through the bones of my jaw I read the slow traffic of the northern basin: the dry, crisp *tock, tock-tock* of a cobble tapped against the vein by a walker two ridges over, asking whether the high fold is holding; the heavy, rhythmic thrum of potholes grinding somewhere deep below; the minute, almost-inaudible creak that travels up through the mountain hours before a fault decides to shift.

I answer with the smooth pale sphere I keep tucked in the crook of my knee. *Tock. Tock-tock-tock.* Flat, spaced, clear. Two blades steady; the upper one drifting; step wide of the drainage crack or it will drop you forty feet downslope with the gale in your face.

There are no titles here, no villages, no councils to appoint a keeper of seams. You simply tend the corner of the saddle that fits your hands. When my small split runnel of iron-seepage clogs with loose slate chips, I kneel in the glare and clear it with a blunt horn-hard nail, making sure both identical threads run true down their separate wet quartz faces—one to keep the rust-film growing slow and bright, the other to spill over the temporal vein where an afternoon's fast wind turns the wash into layered wafers of salty mineral cake. I scrape a few flakes loose with a thin slate knife, chew them down slowly with cold runoff, and cache the rest dry-palmed beneath the third tier of the leaning cairn just past my spatial crease.

Sometimes I wonder who takes them out from the other side of the fold. More often than not, I already know.

Because my overhang sits astride a tight pleat of space and time, I meet myself more frequently than walkers who roam the broader southern terraces.

I will be crouched under the drip-line in the blinding wash, cold-hammering a fresh twist of green-skinned copper flat against the stone—*clack, clack, clack*, disappearing seamlessly into the chorus of spinning cobbles—when a faint tremor reaches me through my bare soles. Not the fault. Unhurried, broad-padded feet, taking the tilted cliff wall sideways without checking their stride.

I lift my chin and look through slitted lids. Stepping neatly across the hand-wide drainage crack out of nowhere, wind suddenly slamming into its back, comes a figure with my exact low-centered shoulder cant, my exact splayed hands, my exact pale crescent of calcite dried along the collarbone.

Except the brow-eave is smooth—no pale notch above the left eye yet—and the heel of the left palm is plain pewter-white, unstained by copper.

Neither of us startles. What would be the point of startlement in a valley where the same chipped cairn leans on two horizons at once and a waterfall pours out of the cliff forty feet above its own mouth? The younger me crouches just outside the overhang, lays a blunt knuckle lightly against the narrowest copper tongue to feel its pitch, and sets two freshly split slate wedges down beside my anvil-stone—the exact thickness I will need to re-pin the slotted disc after the next cloud-wall rattles it loose.

I tilt my chin once, level and brief. Without breaking stillness, I nudge a newly rounded pothole-cobble across the wet floor toward its knee. It picks the sphere up, tucks it into the crook of an arm, and trots away sideways along the marble rib, rolling effortlessly past vertical until it vanishes upside-down beneath the neighboring slab.

Other times it is the older version of me that arrives.

I know that one before I see its face, by the slightly heavier set of its heels on the gneiss and the way it pauses a half-beat longer before stepping onto the tilted wall. When the glare is at its worst—the hammered white sky burning down from every quarter until even slitted eyes ache—it ducks beneath the low corbeled threshold of my dry-stone cell and wedges itself in beside me, shoulder to hip, ribs to ribs, like two slabs of fitted slate. Its pearly eyelids are already clouding faintly milk-white at the corners, and the green copper-stain on its left palm has faded back to pale grey scar-skin.

We do not tap stone inside the cell. There is no need. In the nearest thing to dimness the basin allows, with silver light crawling under the threshold to paint our tucked knees, we close our mouths and hum—a low, vibrating throat-chord tuned carefully to the fifth of the copper tongues outside. Two versions of the same chest, separated by winters that don't bother to stand in single file, holding one note until the chinked calcite walls tremble faintly around us and the gale outside drops to a muffled groan.

In a world worn this thin, "I" am not a single unbroken line drawn from one end of a life to the other. I am more like one of the tall interlocking cairns: a handful of stances stacked across a few folds of wet stone, each one balancing the weight of the next, passing flat discs and slate wedges across the gaps so the whole pile stays plumb.

Still, there is a time when even the steadiest cairn feels light enough to fly.

It comes before the eastern lip darkens. The waxy film on my hairless scalp tightens first; then the three copper tongues drop a quarter-tone as thick vapor loads the wind. When the great grey wall of cloud finally rolls up out of the abyss and swallows the outer spires, the relief is physical, deep in the chest. The brutal glare melts into a soft, luminous milk where rock and air dissolve into one brightness three paces out.

My pearly lids part wide. Silver-grey irises, pinhole pupils, drinking in the wet haze.

I unfold from beneath the overhang and move out onto the open shelves, swift and low-centered, fingers barely brushing slick gneiss to read its grain. Down along the tilted southern flank of my amphitheater, I swing past vertical without slowing, running upside-down along the dripping belly of a slab while a roaring slot gapes eighty feet above—or below—my crown. Downward is not a faith; it is only whatever the rock under my fissured soles insists.

Near the lip of a looping cataract, I drop to one knee beside a wide, shallow catch-basin and lay my left palm flat on the pale gneiss vein. The wind is screaming through the fog; the looping waterfall hammers into its polished intake ten paces away, flinging sheets of cold spray straight across my back.

I watch the water's skin.

Two seconds before the mountain moves, a fine, tight shiver crawls across the basin—a concentric ring spreading outward with nothing falling into it. At the same instant, a hair-thin foreslip ghosts up through my knees.

I bring my cobble down on the gneiss vein—*tock-tock-tock*, fast and urgent—sending the warning racing through the solid rock to whoever is crouched blind in the milky fog along the lower chutes. Maybe it is the scarred walker from the northern fin. Maybe it is my own younger, unnotched self waiting at the intake lip.

Then the deep, resonant *crack* snaps up through the soles of my feet before it reaches my ears.

Across the whole crowded amphitheater, the pitch of the water breaks at once. The looping cataract misses its lower throat by a hand's breadth, tearing open in wild white curtains across dry ledges. For ten rushing seconds, the roaring mouth of the chute stands half-empty and glistening, and a finger-wide seam along its wet flank yawns open, showing a raw, dull-green twist of native copper pinched inside the gneiss.

In the fog below me, a pale pewter shape darts out of nowhere, plunges a cold-numb arm into the slackened throat, wrenches the copper free—or snatches a fresh, glassy cobble from the spinning bowl—and vaults sideways onto an inverted shelf a heartbeat before the fault eases back and the torrent slams home with a concussion that shakes the spray off my brow.

I don't wait to see who caught it. As the cloud-wall shreds and rushes off over the western drop, and the hammered white sky slams back down onto a million wet facets at once, I trot back to my shelf to tend what the shift shook loose. I tap the slotted copper disc with my cobble—*tink, tink*—wedging a fresh chip of slate beneath its pin until its angry rattle settles back into a flat, faithful hum.

There are only two things in this valley that pull me away from my triple seam.

Once in a long while, I travel down to the stubby, wide stretch of pale gneiss where the nursery cells sit wedged beneath the heaviest overhangs—a rare fifty paces of stone where gravity agrees with itself end to end and the copper plates have sung the same unvarying note for generations. Outside those low thresholds, tiny shallow footholds have been pecked into the rock beside larger ones.

I am not an elder yet, not quite, though I am closer to it than I was. Still, sometimes I take one of the rare young ones out to the edge of the faithful shelf, keeping my broad, copper-stained hand closed firmly around its slick wrist or the smooth nape of its neck. I kneel beside it in the silver wash, lift its small, flat-nailed fingers, and lay them gently against a humming copper tongue pinned into the first spatial crack.

I hold its hand there until its breath slows to match the pitch of the metal. Until it stops trusting its slitted eyes—which will always be fooled by stacked horizons and bright glare—and begins to feel, straight through the bones of its wrist, the difference between rock that will hold a stride and rock that is sliding away into another ridge's weather. There is no gentler way to love anything up here than to teach it how to read the seams without falling through them.

And the other pull is quieter, and belongs to no one else.

Rarely—perhaps once in forty breaths of the valley—the organ-roar of the wind falters for ten heartbeats. The spray settles. On the wet side of the quartz vein outside my overhang, the shallow catch-basin flattens into utter, blinding glass.

Whenever it happens, I creep out from under the brow and kneel at the rim. Against every habit my body has learned, I force my pearly eyelids all the way open into the sting of the reflected sky, and I stare straight down into the water.

Down there, beneath the silver skin, the valley does not shine.

The gneiss fins stand razor-sharp and dry, unrounded by ten thousand winters of sloughing rain. Above them hangs a lower, darker vault of cloud—not beaten metal, but heavy, shadowed air—and for once in my life I see what darkness looks like when rock throws it clean across stone. No copper hums in that dry silence. No cataracts loop back into their own mouths.

I hang my wet, salt-streaked face inches above the water, looking for whatever stands along the rim of that older pool. Sometimes I think I see tall shapes there—upright, unbent by gale, dull-surfaced where I am slick—looking back down into a sky that hadn't yet climbed above the clouds. Sometimes I think it is only me, stretched back across the millennia before the cold pared me down to bone, fat, and patience.

I never learn which it is. Two seconds before a drop of condensation detaches from the overhang above my head, the ripple starts across the glass—widening outward from nothing, fracturing the dark-shadowed ridges, folding the old dry sky back into cold hammered silver. The drop strikes. The wind returns with a shriek through the slate fins. My lids close to bright slivers again, and my three copper blades pick up their thin, steady chord.

I don't grieve for the dry valley in the glass, any more than I dread the dry salt waiting on the other side of my quartz vein.

I know how my turning ends. I have already sat shoulder-to-shoulder with the version of myself whose joints have begun to stiffen past the upside-down climbs, whose eyelids are turning wholly white so even the milky fog dims to a blur. One day, when the iron-crust grinds my back teeth down to the gums and my fingers can no longer tell a clean fifth from a loose rattle, I will walk out past the drip-line one last time.

I will check the three copper tongues—just by habit, laying a dry knuckle against each blade—and balance one fresh, clean disc of pale slate atop the leaning cairn on the wet side of the fold.

Then I will step across the pale quartz vein onto the bone-dry crusted salt. I will fold my knees tight to my chest, bow my brow against the blinding stone, and let my slow heartbeat stop keeping time with the mountain. On the wet side, only a few walls of grey vapor will roll past and tear apart. On my side, decades of lean dry wind will sweep quietly over my hunched back, drawing out the water, bleaching pewter skin to white chalk, grinding the chalk fine until only a shallow smooth hollow remains in the salt—and by the next sideways rain, not even that.

And yet, even then, fifty paces across the spatial fold on a different wall of wet spires, a younger hand—unnotched, palms still unstained by copper—will already be reaching beneath the third tier of the leaning cairn, drawing out the smooth glassy cobble I left waiting for it, dry-palmed, on the bright wet side of the stone.
