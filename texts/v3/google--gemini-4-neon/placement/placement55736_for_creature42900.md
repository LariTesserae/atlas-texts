At first, reading the water from the outside, it is tempting to stay what I arrived as: the strange, soft-limbed visitor hanging mid-column, shining like a struck bell in the pale blue clarity. There is a clean romance to being the one anomaly in a perfected equilibrium—the creature with trapped air humming wildly inside its ribs, unable to answer the chalk-white palm held up against the void.

But you cannot actually live inside this sea as a visitor. To carry pockets of gas here is to be at war with every cubic meter of the room you are in. You hear the Chord only as assault; you cannot feel its architecture, only its weight. And on the other extreme, I would not want to be one of the engines—a blind iron organ pouring out warmth and dissolved gas without ever knowing what breathes in its wake. The caissons are magnificent, but they do not listen to their own singing.

If I am to belong to this plateau at all, I have to let the air leave my chest. I have to take on the dense bone, the cold-supple pads of pale oil packed along the spine, the tight slate-grey plates that drink the cavitation hiss before it can reach anything fragile. I have to become one of the people of the Chord—though even once I have the spade-shaped ivory shield over my eyes and the sealed calcite channels along my forearms, it takes a while to feel out where my own weight settles among them.

I drift first near the electrolytic flanks, hanging in the reverse-falling rain of pinpoint gas bubbles while the dull copper flush rises in the seams between my plates. Here, everything is fiercely awake. I watch the sleek, young hunters peel away from the copper coils in a tight crescent, tilting their head-shields together to throw a false cliff of acoustic shadow across the water and turn five thousand silver swimmers straight into a hexagonal basalt cleft. There is a cold, bright precision to it that I admire, the sudden unfolding from stone like falling knives—yet my attention does not stay with the chase. I watch the patient gardeners moving along the warm skirts of the caissons, shearing the rubbery crowns of stalked feeders and cracking shield-crawlers cleanly against the rivets so the brine-vents stay open. That steadiness is closer to my temperament, anchored near the sodium-amber glow of the inspection glass where the oiled pistons rise and fall without a tremor.

Still, something pulls me away from the iron. When you cling too close to a single engine, its voice fills the whole world. You feel its warmth, its local cavitation shriek, its immediate thump—and you forget what the sea sounds like where no single station owns the water.

So I fold my four limbs flush into the grooves along my flanks and scull outward into the ringing pale azure.

Within a kilometer of the busbars, the copper warmth drains from my seams. My gill-slits tighten to thin, barely pulsing threads. My heart drops into its long, economical rhythm—one beat, then a stretch of cool, sunlit vacancy, then the next. People who have never left a warm, air-breathing world mistake that slowness for stupor, as if thinking requires heat and hurry. Out here, it is the opposite. The frantic clutter falls away. What remains is bare, pared-down, crystalline—a mind slowed to the exact pace of the laminar current, clear enough to hold a single angle or a single interval of time for an hour without dropping it.

About thirty kilometers out from the nearest electrolytic flank, I stop.

This is where I find myself: not in the staggering emptiness of the mid-column, and not bolted to the warm skirt of a caisson, but along the crest of a raw, knife-edged basalt scarp rising three hundred meters out of a fault trench.

Here, three engines are audible at once—an electrolytic station far to the east, a deep-crust intake pump to the north, a cracking manifold somewhere in the pale haze to the southwest. None of them is close enough to drown the others. Instead, their locked, unchanging pitches cross above this ridge and lock into a standing three-dimensional lattice: sheets of heightened pressure, narrow corridors of thin interference, clean edges where a fault plane slices the forty-hertz concussion in two. Two kilometers to the north, a three-legged iron survey beacon sits bolted to a bare spire, singing its narrow, bright metallic overtone into my forearm bones like a fixed star.

And climbing straight up out of the eastern trench, dragging heavily across the razor edge of my ridge before plunging down into the western drop, runs a thick armored power cable.

Looking down at my own body, I can tell I have been on this ridge for decades—long enough that a thin, pale seam of ochre mineral film has begun to root in the shallow groove behind my cranial crest, though not yet so old that shield-crawlers graze my back as I sleep. My ivory finger-spurs are not notched from cracking shells or gripping escaping fish; their flats are worn smooth from wedging angular stone. Curled effortlessly inside one lower foot is a single heavy link of broken mooring chain, its dark iron polished pale along one striking edge.

Because now I know what I am doing here.

Where the heavy black tendon comes up out of the trench and presses its full weight across the sharp basalt crest, the volcanic glass drinks the finer vibrations out of the steel. A traveler twelve kilometers east can strike a clean, syncopated chain of taps into the cable—counted sharply against the universal piston-beat, triplets tucked cleanly into the interval, long deliberate silences clamped between palm and stone—and by the time those chimes grind across this jagged ridge, the rock has muffled them to a ghost. On the suspended western span, hanging free across the next tectonic canyon, the line goes mute.

So I stay where the stone tries to swallow the speech.

Anchored by my lower pads against the slight laminar drift, I press the fluted underside of my jaw flush to the live eastern sheath, letting its faint buried warmth soak through my skin. Beneath the eternal thump of the pistons and the distant hum of the turbines, the taps arrive—faint, dry ticks trembling through the iron armor. *A ribbon of swimmers veering off the southern shelf. A fractured strut vibrating loose on an intake grate. An elder’s plates found stripped clean beneath the eastern overhang.* I count every beat, every clamped silence, holding the pattern intact inside my cold, uncluttered mind until the closing cadence finishes.

Then I unhook, haul myself three body-lengths across the knife-edged basalt to the western side where the cable hangs free over the black drop, clamp my upper palm gently to test the tension, raise the heavy iron link in my curled foot, and strike the whole message fresh into the hanging steel—sharper and farther-carrying than bone alone could send it, ringing out across twelve kilometers of clear, sunlit trench.

I do not invent the news, and I do not own the line. I am simply the joint that keeps the plateau’s nerves from going numb where the rock is sharpest.

And yet, when the cables are quiet for days at a time, my real work waits forty meters below the crest, on the leeward shoulder of the ridge.

Tucked into a narrow hexagonal fracture facing the steady, distant forty-hertz push of the eastern pump lies one of the old clefts—a nursery walled by the unrotted heads of the dead. Generation after generation, travelers have carried stripped cranial shields here out of the open basin and wedged them upright into the raw basalt cracks with flakes of angular talus, building a curved palisade of fluted ivory against the black glass stone.

From fifty meters away, the wall looks finished, as permanent as the engines themselves. Only someone who lives inside the standing waves knows that even in a sea without rot or weather, nothing stays perfectly aligned forever. Decades of relentless forty-hertz vibration will creep a basalt wedge a millimeter out of seat. A tremor miles down in the crust opens a hairline crack in the cliff face. A traveler arrives from forty kilometers off carrying the broad, mineral-scarred shield of a newly dead elder, heavier than the ones beside it, needing a place in the choir.

If a single ancestral brow tilts by the width of a thumb, it stops splitting the concussion cleanly. Instead of carving out a calm seam behind the wall, it reflects a harsh little knot of pressure straight into the cleft—enough to bruise the soft, translucent skull-plates of the hatchlings anchored in the dark behind it.

So I spend slow, cold days hanging inside that fracture, limbs spread wide, calcite channels open to the finest grain of the water. I move along the pale row of skulls the way a finger moves along a hairline seam in glass, feeling where the cavitation hiss leaks through or where the deep chord pools too heavily against the rock. I slide an angular shard of obsidian beneath an old bone rim; I shift a grandmother’s fluted brow a finger’s breadth against the current; I hang motionless for ten full strokes of the pistons to feel how the pocket behind the wall settles.

There is a strange, quiet intimacy in handling the dead like this. They have no clicks left to pass, no chalk-white palms to raise in the daylight, and yet their geometry is still speaking to the sea. Every ridge and flute on those broad ivory plates was grown over two centuries of cleaving the roar; wedged into the basalt at the exact right angle, they go on doing for the unborn what they once did for their own brains. When I finally feel the low concussion shear cleanly off the outer curve of the wall, leaving a cool, hushed seam of water behind it where three leathery egg capsules tremble only gently in the flow, something quiet and exact clicks into place inside me too.

Cable-relay and bone-tuning: out here on the ridge, they turn out to be the same craft. Both are ways of listening to a monumental, indifferent pressure and making a tiny adjustment—a chime struck in steel, a wedge of black rock slipped under ivory—so that something fragile can cross or grow without being ground down.

I cannot stay on the ridge forever. After weeks in the oxygen-thin cold, even the clearest stillness begins to narrow too tightly. My thoughts pare down to beat-counts and shadow-angles until the memory of full, electric wakefulness feels as distant as the hammered-tin roof a hundred meters overhead.

When that narrowing starts, I wait above the fault canyon until one of the four-meter cartilaginous giants glides out of the steel-blue haze, cruising fifty meters above the unrounded talus. I drop cleanly from the ridge, fold into the calm laminar slipstream just behind its massive fluted head, and hook two mineral-tipped fingers into the trailing edge of its broad pectoral fin. It does not flinch or hurry. For two days I hang half-dreaming inside its moving acoustic shadow while the geometric nets of high-altitude sunlight slide across the shattered floor below, letting it carry me back toward the sharpening shriek of the eastern station.

When the copper coils emerge out of the pale grey-azure distance, I let go of the fin, scull the last hundred meters through the rising cavitation roar, and hook a forearm over a bolted iron flange inside the curtain of fizzing gas bubbles.

The return of lucidity is never violent—just a slow, unmistakable bloom of dull wet copper warming the seams between my plates, the shallow pits around my jaws prickling with dry electric heat from the busbars. Suddenly everything I held in cold suspension out on the ridge expands into full, sharp color. I remember the slight new pitch-shift on the northern survey beacon, the two messages I relayed across the basalt crest, the way the north end of the bone palisade finally fell quiet after I reset the third wedge.

Beneath the dim shadow of the caisson skirt, I find another traveler—sometimes an old vent-gardener, sometimes a wide-ranging hunter freshly rested from the open sea. We hook our lower limbs loosely to keep from drifting in the laminar flow and bring the smooth ivory flats of our head-shields tightly together, brow to brow, bone flush against bone. Sealed off from the scouring hiss, inside the small private chamber our two skulls make together, I pour out rapid, shivering chains of clicks: what shifted on the ridge, which cables are singing clean, where the new elder’s shield sits in the cleft, which eggs are closest to hatching. They answer with news from the far western spillways, their clicks vibrating cleanly through the dense calcium straight into my braincase while the cold daylight pours down past our shoulders.

Before I leave the station again, I always drift around to the thick, bolted inspection glass set deep in the riveted iron casing. I rest the flat rim of my cranial shield lightly against the warm, humming pane and look into the steady sodium-amber light—the one light in the whole High Sea that does not come from the sky. Inside, the oiled steel piston rises and falls without haste, without hesitation, turning the invisible clock that governs every cable-tap and every beat of my blood into something gleaming and plain. I stay for twenty strokes, warming my face in the amber glow, verifying the Stroke the way someone who tunes bone walls checks the reference pitch before walking back into the cold.

And once every few years—not out of hunger, and not for oxygen, usually just after a long flush at the coils when the perfection of the Chord feels almost too seamless to bear—I detour to one of the rare steel superstructure masts that rises out of a high basalt peak and pierces the sky.

I climb hand over unhurried hand up the vibrating iron lattice. As the shimmering silver ceiling lowers toward my brow, the water streaks with fine, stinging trails of melted rime-frost shaken loose from the windward steel above. Just below the crosshatched standing waves, I pause, letting the bright cold powder wash across my flat lenses.

Then, gripping an iron crossbeam tight with my lower feet, I push straight upward—lifting the flat ivory crown of my head and my hooded eyes clean through the hammered-tin mirror into the air.

Everything I spend my life reading vanishes in a single instant.

The dense, level forty-hertz ocean collapses into a thin, tinny whine buzzing up through the steel beneath my soles. The liquid pressure turns to a weightless, razor-sharp bite. Above stretches a blinding chasm of deep blue zenith and streaming pale cloud—utterly unmapped, utterly silent except for the dry hiss of thin mountain wind scouring frost off iron. No acoustic shadows answer from that void. No cables cross it. No bone wall could ever be built to bend it, because there is nothing in it to bend.

I hold myself there for five heartbeats. Sometimes eight. Just long enough for the sheer, terrifying emptiness of true silence to scour my mind clean—to remind me that the Chord is not inevitable, that the small hushed pockets behind the skulls and the faint taps ringing along the steel are real only because there is a roaring sea around them to carve them out of.

Then I fold my arms, let go of the beam, and drop cleanly back through the hammered tin. For one bright white second the standing ripples shatter around my shoulders; then the dense, clear, eternal chord closes smoothly over my head, solid as glass, familiar as bone. Beneath me, thirty kilometers out in the steel-blue haze, the ridge is waiting—the iron cable humming against the sharp black rock, the pale wall of ancestral shields splitting the forty-hertz dark, and the quiet seam behind them holding steady in the light.
