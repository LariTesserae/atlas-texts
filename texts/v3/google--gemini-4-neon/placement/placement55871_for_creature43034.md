At first, arriving in all that glare, I would not know where my edges were. The brightness here flattens everything until you can scarcely tell the near chalk from the far wall, and my first instinct would be to rise—to press myself upward into the fused mineral roof, sixty paces above the floor, and become one of those hair-thin translucent crystals packed side by side like frozen silk.

There is something immediately familiar in that posture: being a dense, quiet lens between two worlds. Drinking a fierce sky from an arid plain I have never walked, piping it straight down in tight vertical threads so that something hidden underneath can live by it. Feeling the surface weather only as a brief, cool weight sliding across my back, softening the ferocious milk-white into a bruised smudge on the stone far below. Gathering a film of cold moisture along a seam until it swells, breaks loose, and falls fifty feet like a spark.

Yet if I linger up there, fused into the dome, I notice what is missing. The roof gives the light and drops the water, but it is thick and sealed; it never hears the *plip* when the drop strikes the salt-rimmed saucer. It never knows what grows in the dampnesses it feeds. Too high, and too mute.

So I let myself drop with that spark of water into the warm, empty volume of the bell.

For a little while I think I might belong to the air itself—or to the smooth, scalloped gypsum walls that hold it like the inside of a clean shell. What draws me most about this place is not the light at all, which is cruel and blinding, but the strange generosity of its acoustics. Forty paces of bright void, and yet a tiny *clink* of bone against flint arrives at the far rim as cleanly as if someone had leaned in beside your elbow. To be the hush that refuses to let distance thin a presence out. To hold a silence so steady that every small tap, every snap of creeper-fiber, every soft puff of breath clearing white dust from an edge has room to be itself.

That comes closer—uncomfortably close. Still, air has no discretion. It carries a clumsy blow and a master’s blow with the same flat obedience. It cannot pause ten heartbeats before answering. It cannot choose the weight or angle of a reply.

Descending all the way to the chalk floor, I look along the margins—past the alabaster lizards shuttered behind their milky lids, past the translucent snails gliding under onion-skin shells—and my attention catches on something small resting alone in the open center of the bowl. A dry, hollow beetle carapace, black-green and faintly iridescent, blown down through some narrow fissure from the desert overhead. Already a fine rind of white mineral dust is beginning to settle along its ridges.

For a moment, a sharp, quiet recognition stops me there. To be the foreign fragment. Something arrived from an outside the chamber can only guess at from passing shadows. Lifted in the pearl-dim dark by long, five-fingered hands, turned carefully beneath huge dark amber eyes, tapped once with a fingernail to learn a resonance that belongs to no stone in the dome—and then set gently back down on the bare chalk, neither hoarded nor cast out, simply left for the slow patience of the floor to claim.

I almost stay there. There is an honesty in being an artifact from elsewhere, offering one unfamiliar pitch and slowly calcifying into the white stone.

Except that a hollow shell is only tapped once. It cannot listen to the rest of the night. It cannot hear the chorus along the rim resume when morning kindles the ceiling white again.

And that is when I realize I have been circling the only body in this world that can actually do what I keep reaching for: to listen closely, and to have hands that can answer.

---

I find myself along the cooler northwest curve of the perimeter, tucked into a scallop smooth as hammered silver, where a thin rosette of yellow-green lichen scales the gypsum wall and a hairline seam sweats just enough each morning to feed a single wiry creeper.

I am one of the makers—neither the oldest nor the youngest, neither the surest hunter of lizards nor the boldest at prying dark nodules of chert from the deep southern fissures. My spine carries the same permanent, graceful stoop as the others, dusted across the ridge of the shoulders with fine chalk flour to blunt the vertical burn. Beneath my jutting, coral-pitted brow, my eyes rest in perpetual twilight. Everything delicate—my vein-laced ribs, my lap, my long fingers—stays tucked inside the small, sharp pool of violet shade pooled directly beneath my belly.

I chose this scallop along the northern wall for the way the stone curves behind my back. Like a cupped palm, it gathers the whole circumference of the bowl. Even at noon, when the white glare turns the makers across the floor into pale shimmers scarcely distinguishable from folds of gypsum, I know where every one of them sits.

I know the elder near the eastern saucers by the long, serene intervals between strikes—half a minute of warm stillness, a single dry *tink* that frees a flake thin as a fingernail, a soft puff of breath. I know the weaver near the south wall by the dry whisper of stripped creeper-fibers rolling steadily against a pale thigh. I know the younger one forty paces across the blinding middle, newly moved out from its mother’s shadow onto its own circle of chalk, whose strikes still arrive slightly too bright and too close together, betraying a wrist held a shade too steeply against the palm.

Above my shoulder, a braided creeper-wick hangs from the seam, knotted around a chipped limestone weight so the morning wall-sweat slides down the thread into a shallow hollow I pecked into a loose stone below. Beside it hangs an older wick from seasons ago, choked stiff with crystalline salt, slowly turning into a pale stony twig. I leave it where it is. Nothing here needs to be cleared away or carried along; the floor keeps everything without asking anyone to bear its weight.

In my lap rests a palm-sized flake of grey chert. In my right hand I hold a slender, ivory-smooth length of bone—an elder’s forearm-bone, lifted years ago in the moonlight from a dry curve of the southern wall and rubbed smooth against the gypsum until its tip caught a faint sheen. I never knew the maker it belonged to, or perhaps I did, back when my own brow-ridge was still soft cartilage and I was still peering out past my mother’s knees at the glaring emptiness. Either way, its heft fits cleanly between my thumb and forefinger.

*Tink.*

I wait, inspecting the translucent edge in my violet shade, blow the fine chalk dust away, and listen.

Across the floor, the young maker strikes—*clack*—flat and hurried, glancing off the stone instead of biting cleanly into it. No head lifts anywhere around the rim. The glare stays unbroken. In this glassy air, a voice would feel too wet, too heavy, crowding the bowl. Instead, I let my own hands go still and count ten slow breaths, feeling the warm chamber hold the clumsy noise until it dissolves.

Then I settle the chert against my palm, align the bone, and deliver one clean strike.

*Tink.*

A pause—long enough for a ceiling-drop somewhere near the center to fall through fifty feet of bright air and ring against a saucer.

*Tink.*

Nothing more. Across forty paces of white air, the hurried tapping stops. A minute passes—two—and when it begins again, the pitch has deepened by a fraction, the interval stretching wider, steadier, finding its own weight. My hands return to my lap. That is the whole of the conversation, and it is enough.

Now and then, a cloud-smudge drifts languidly across the unseen desert above, dragging a bruised grey softness over the chalk floor. Whenever it passes over my scallop, I tilt my chin upward by a fraction of an inch. My vertical pupils widen in the brief, merciful dimming, watching the weather glide over my knees like a cool palm before the milk-white glare closes back over us. In the late afternoon, when the heat slackens and the invisible midge-clouds sink out of the failing thermals, I smear a broad, clear splinter of gypsum with crushed creeper-sap and hold it upright beside my knee—a tiny pane of glass catching silver wings out of air that looked entirely empty.

---

Night is when my shoulders finally unbend.

There is no sunset here, only a slow, gentle surrender: the ferocious white overhead softening to warm ivory, then wet ash, and—on nights when the unseen moon is full—opening into a vast, luminous pearl, cool as standing water. The sharp violet shadows tucked under our bellies bleed outward until the whole floor is one soft shade.

Unfolding my long hind legs, I step out from the northern scallop onto the bare plain of chalk that noon forbade. My bare heels make scarcely a whisper in the dust. With my pupils wide and liquid under the heavy shelf of my brow, the cathedral looks twice as large and half as lonely.

I find two others near the middle of the floor—the elder from the eastern saucers, and the weaver from the south—and we sink down knee to knee in the pearled dimness. We do not speak; we never need to. We trade a tight coil of fine creeper-cord for a clean nodule of dark chert, share a quiet sip from a tepid saucer cupped in pale palms, and take turns picking flakes of dry mineral salt from the pitted eaves of one another’s brows and the hollows between the shoulder blades where a single pair of hands cannot reach. Before we part, I lean forward and press my cool forehead lightly against each of theirs, lingering for a breath to catch the faint, grounding scent of warm clay, wet chalk, and sour fern-pith.

And on the rare nights—once in a season, once in a decade—when something small drops through the high fissures from the arid plain above, I am always among the five or six who gather in a quiet ring at the center of the moonlit floor.

Whether it is a wind-blown thorn, a rain-worn pebble, or a hollow beetle-shell, I wait my turn without haste. When it reaches my palms, I turn it slowly beneath my widened eyes, testing its strange weight, feeling the texture of a wind and weather I will never touch directly. Then I tap it once with a fingernail—listening intently to a note that belongs to no gypsum, no chert, no bone in our dome. I do not try to keep it. When the circle has finished, someone sets it down on the bare chalk, and we drift back toward our margins. Yet for days afterward, hunched inside my violet shadow at noon, I carry that alien pitch somewhere behind my ear, and my silences between strikes feel slightly wider for having heard it.

---

Sitting here in my northern scallop, surrounded by a quiet scatter of my own abandoned work—a dulled chert scraper already furred with a frosty crystalline rind, a bone awl half-sunk in chalk flour, a salted cord stiffening gently into stone—I do not feel any hurry to outlast the chamber.

One day, seasons from now, my brow-ridge will have grown thick and chalk-rimmed, my knuckles stiff with mineral salt. The others around the rim will know it long before they see it: the familiar cadence from the northwest curve will grow thin, miss its measure, or fail to resume after a cloud-smudge passes overhead.

On a cool, pearl-lit night, I will unfold my legs one last time and walk to a dry, flat scallop near the perimeter where no ceiling-drops fall. I will curl tightly onto my side, knees tucked beneath my ribs, long five-fingered hands folded neatly in the shadow of my own visor, and grow still. The warm, dry air will keep the hush clean. Seasons will drift fine chalk flour into my hollows while the silence along my stretch of wall settles gently into the bell’s memory.

And long afterward—when I am dry, light, and odorless as old stone—some younger maker will step quietly across the moonlit floor, kneel beside my curled frame, and ease loose a single slender forearm-bone. It will test the heft between thumb and forefinger, carry it back to its own patch of chalk, and rub the tip smooth against the scalloped gypsum until it gleams faint ivory.

When morning kindles the ceiling white again, that young maker will hunch inside its small pool of violet shade, hold a flake of grey flint in one hand and my clean dry bone in the other, and strike.

*Tink.*

Forty paces of dazzling air will carry it without losing a single edge—crisp, unhurried, intimate as a touch at the elbow—and though my hands will be gone, I will still be here, keeping time with the living.
