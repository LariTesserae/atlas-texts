I would not climb back up over the juniper rim.

If I started on the dished limestone stairs, watching my own breath hang white and slow in front of my chin, I would already know that the wind on the flats has nothing left to say to me. The plain above is all haste and scatter—sound running away until it dies of exhaustion, dust lifting from one place only to be dropped somewhere it doesn't belong. Down here, nothing leaves. Even a sigh stays in the air long enough for you to look at it and decide whether you meant it.

So I would go down. Not to the uppermost terrace, where the dwarf birches crouch and the restless ones sit with greased wool tucked in their ears, waiting for the tremor of open sky to bleed out of their shoulders. And not all the way to the lowest flagstone alleys, where the ninety-year-olds sit motionless in doorways with their lids half-dropped against the white glare, already practicing the stillness of the niches behind the walls.

I would stop two tiers up from the cistern floor, on the eastern side of the central cluster, where the houses lean into one another like shoulders pressed together against a draft that never comes.

There is a room there that most people pass without noticing—an L-shaped pocket tucked beneath a roof bowed under half a foot of emerald sphagnum, right where a pegged cedar footbridge lands against the bare karst wall. Its doorway is low enough that you have to dip your hood to enter. Inside, the back wall is not masonry at all, only the raw chalk-white limestone of the collapse, split diagonally by a broad vein of milky selenite. When the sun slides across the coin of sky at noon and strikes the bare face three tiers up, that vein wakes into a dim, pearly ribbon right inside my room, carrying a ghost of glare down through the stone itself while the single recessed horn pane stays cool and blue-grey.

I think, at first, I would just sit on the peat-warmed bench in that room and let my pulse learn the hollow’s pace. It takes time to stop hurrying when nothing is chasing you. You have to learn not to shiver—shivering wastes warmth and makes old felt rustle—and you have to let your eyes settle behind heavy upper lids until the shadowless white stops hurting and starts showing you its grammar: the faint blue-grey tint tucked under a lintel that tells you how deep an alcove runs, the duller sheen of dew where a flagstone sits half a fingernail lower than its neighbor.

Only after my hands turned that fixed, muted rose at the knuckles would I begin to know what I am here for.

Because nothing in the bowl looks ruined, and nothing looks newly made, a visitor thinks the truce between stone and water maintains itself. They see the settled permanence of river pebbles and assume the pebbles have finished shifting forever.

They haven't. They only shift so slowly that you have to live here forty years to catch them at it.

A saxifrage root thickens by the width of a hair inside a mortarless joint over six winters. A copper dew-pan settles unevenly in its worn stone ring. A cedar beam beneath the central cluster, adzed smooth before my grandmother was born, takes one winter's weight of wet sphagnum a fraction of a degree too far and begins—not to crack, not yet, only to hold its breath differently under the roofs it carries.

That is what I would be: the one who listens to the seams before they speak.

There is no name for it in the indoor whisper-tongue, no guild-mark on my sleeve. In a courtyard-wide pit of a hundred and eighty souls, you are simply whatever your hands know how to quiet. Mine know slope, grain, and pressure.

In the clear glare before midday, when the night's dew is finishing its last silver creep down the finger-wide grooves, I wrap my tools in oiled felt so flint never touches horn and slip out sideways into the narrow lanes. My soles are stitched from four layers of boiled wool; my hooded shawl is undyed grey, pinned at the throat with a sliver of hare-bone. I carry three flint points of different widths, a deer-antler burnisher worn glassy at its tip, a burl dish of wet chalk paste stiffened with a drop of frost-defying saxifrage sap, and a pouch of seasoned oak shims pared thinner than fingernails and rubbed with pale rendered fat so they will slide into a joint without a whine.

My work is to keep the water from learning how to fall.

In the howling world above the juniper, nobody would hear a single drop of dew breaking loose from a tilted stone ledge and hitting a slate sill once every forty heartbeats. Down here, a drip is a splinter in the silence. It gathers fat and shining on the underside of an overhanging stair, trembles, lets go—*tink*—and before the third drop falls, three stacked households have gone stiff at their baskets, their breath rising in short, high puffs near their collars.

When that happens, or better, a season before it happens, I kneel on a folded rush mat in the tight angle where two roofs lean against each other. You cannot tap a stone here to test whether it sits hollow; a knuckle knocked against limestone would freeze fifty pairs of reddened hands across the basin. Instead, I pull my hood back just enough to bare one cheekbone and press the side of my jaw flat against the cold chalk or the damp cedar, closing my eyes against the white bounce.

Through bone, the silent city is not silent at all.

You feel the slow, spongy give of boiled-wool heels crossing a footbridge two tiers overhead. You feel the faint, deep thrum of a covered cistern when someone lifts its mossy lid straight up and dips a birch-burl ladle into the black water below. And if you wait long enough—holding your breath so even the soft brush of vapor against your lips stops—you feel the tension in the timber itself: the tiny, dry strain where an oak dowel is taking more than its share of an upper balcony, or the micro-tilt of a flagstone lifted by a root.

Then I go to work. I never hurry. If a dew-groove has lost its imperceptible pitch, I draw the flint point along the chalk in long, smooth pulls—no scraping, no grit jumping—shaving off a paste of wet limestone no thicker than a moth's wing until the water stops pooling and remembers how to cling to the stone face. Surface tension is the real law of this place. As long as water holds to stone, it moves without a murmur. The moment it breaks free into air, it makes a sound.

If a cedar beam is tired, I do not wedge it in a day. I slip a single fat-greased oak sliver into the seating beside the stone corbel, press it home with the heel of my palm until the wood resists, and leave it for three days so the five stacked tiers above can settle into that hair's breadth without a groan. Three days later I return, kneel on the same mat, and press in a second sliver. A single beam can take a month of quiet visits. No one ever complains that I am slow. Down here, slowness is the only way to move a mountain without waking it.

And because water always runs downward from one terrace onto another, and every wall braces a neighbor's wall, my knees spend half their days on boundaries that two households have stopped speaking across.

I know the cold feuds of the hollow better than anyone, though I never ask after them. A grudge shows up in the masonry long before anyone is angry enough to set a clay jar down hard on a stair. You see it where a woman stops scraping the pale lime-crust from the last hand's-breadth of her condensation channel, letting the chalk build until the morning dew backs up and weeps slowly down onto the roof-moss of the family below. You see it in a doorway vent left packed with sour, spent moss so a ghost of peat-haze curls onto a shared landing.

I do not meddle with signs or whispered advice. I simply kneel where the chalk has choked the groove, pare it clean, and lay a tiny flake of translucent selenite at the lip to lead the bead cleanly back into the lower channel. Sometimes, while I am crouched there with my reddened fingers wet to the knuckles, both neighbors are sitting only six feet apart—one on her parapet above, one just inside her recessed doorway below—pretending not to look at each other's breath. When I finish and rise, I wait a moment, tilting my head slightly the way we do to read distance in the glare, and lay my bare thumb lightly across the back of the lower woman's wrist—checking her warmth, passing her the cool steadiness of stone. Then I climb the six dished stairs and do the same for the woman above. Often, before I turn the corner of the lane, the choppy white puffs rising between their eaves have already begun to round out again.

There is a second braid hanging in the dim hallway of my room, right beside my family's willow-bark cord.

My family's braid is like everyone else's: knotted with bone disks for births, dried birch catkins for dry autumns, a white snail-shell for the winter my uncle moved down into the dry fissures. You run your bare thumbs down it by the warm peat-bench at dusk and remember who held you before you could walk the stairs.

The second braid is mine alone. Nobody taught me to keep it; it just began one year when I realized my memory was too short for stone. It is a ledger of every seam in the sinkhole, braided from fine soaked willow-fiber and knotted in tiers from top to bottom. A tiny square of scraped slate marks a lintel I eased back into level on the fourth tier. A pinch of oxidized sea-green copper marks a dew-pan seating that shifts whenever frost needles form before noon. Three small pale snail-shells, spaced an arm's length apart, mark a hairline fracture in the eastern karst wall—not dangerous, not yet, only something I visit once every turning of the sun's tilt to lay a dry sliver of birch-bark against the stone and see whether the crack has widened by the breath of a hair.

At dusk, when the glare off the selenite walls drains into deep blue-grey and the dew begins its invisible condensation on every waxy leaf outside, I sit on my warm bench in the near-dark and let my fingers travel down that braid. I don't need light to read it. Under my calloused thumb-pads, the whole packed city rises up in miniature—every leaning wall, every pegged bridge, every hidden weight held in balance.

I think that is when I feel closest to the landlords under the flagstones.

Directly beneath the alley outside my threshold lies one of the oldest stone cisterns. Every ten days, when I lift its heavy moss-swaddled cover straight upward so the wood never drags against the curb, I hold my breath and lean over the black mirror. It takes a few seconds for my pin-pupiled eyes to open wide enough to see anything in the dark water. Then it emerges—not swimming so much as drifting into visibility like milk dropped into cold ink. Forearm-long, blind since before I was born, with wide feathery gills the color of pale rose quartz and one small notched curve in its left flank that I have known since I was small enough to sit in a felt bundle sorting selenite chips.

I drop two crushed moss-beetles onto the skin of the water and wait until the ghostly snout rises without a ripple and takes them down. Sometimes, after I have scraped the mineral crust from the stone lip where the wooden lid sits, I leave my bare fingertips resting just on the surface for ten slow heartbeats. The water is painfully cold, tasting in the air of chalk and crushed green stems. Deep below, I feel the faint, silky push of water as the pale creature turns beneath my hand, keeping the cistern clear, holding its own quiet end of the treaty.

Once in a while, when the noon glare outside has been unusually sharp off the western veins and my eyes ache behind their heavy lids, I slip down the lowest dished stairs, past the hushed doorways of the oldest living elders, and part the thick hanging felt curtains at the back of the deepest alley.

Inside the horizontal fissures of the karst, the air drops just below freezing and goes bone-dry. There is no moss here, no dew, no glare—only a pale, borrowed twilight leaking around the felt behind me, and the faint, clean smell of dry chalk and old wool.

I come partly for my work: I carry a pinch of fine dry lime-dust in my palm to check that no damp draft from the cistern alleys is creeping back into the seams where the dead sit. If moisture ever found its way back here, the long stillness would break. So I check the stone joints along the floor, breathing softly into my cupped collar so my own vapor doesn't settle on the walls.

And then, when I am sure the seams are dry, I unroll my rush mat and sit knee-to-knee with my grandfather's sister, who settled into her shallow carved niche twenty years ago, knees drawn modestly toward her chest, hands folded over her soft thinned felt, her face lightened to the pale translucence of scraped horn.

Down here, for a quarter of an hour, I let my breath go unmeasured.

Not loud—never loud. Just uneven. A little deeper than is polite on the terraces, or a little quick, rising white in the cold dry air where nobody across three tiers is reading its shape. I lay a papery white skeleton of a fallen birch leaf beside her boiled-wool shoe, and rest my palm flat against the dry limestone wall beside her shoulder.

Up above, through thirty feet of packed masonry and emerald moss, the city goes on holding itself together. A plump grey-backed bird shifts half an inch along an eave above a charcoal vent, keeping watch on the pale coin of sky. A hare chews waxy saxifrage with slow, soundless sideways motions of its jaw. Two women kneel over a copper pan, their white breath-plumes mingling softly in the glare. A newborn in a thick-walled room on the third tier draws its first startled breath, and a vibration so faint it is scarcely more than a change in temperature travels down through the leaning limestone into the flat of my hand.

I smile only with the corners of my eyes, and press my palm a fraction harder against the stone in quiet welcome.

When I finally stand, fold my mat, and slip back through the hanging felt into the damp bright hush of the alleys, my pulse is slow as a wintering badger's again. My hands are steady. Somewhere on the fourth tier, a root has probably pushed a flagstone a hundredth of an inch closer to a drip, and the oak shim I left under the cedar bridge three days ago is ready to be pressed a hair deeper.

I tilt my head slightly to read the blue-grey shadow under the stair, lift my wool-soled foot, and climb back into the glare without a sound.
