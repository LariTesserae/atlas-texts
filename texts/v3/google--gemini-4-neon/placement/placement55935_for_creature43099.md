At first, looking out across that clean indigo volume, I thought I might be one of the seasonal folk—a straggler who stayed a few weeks past the resin-harvest, leaning into the lee of a turf dome with beeswax softened in my ears, trying to figure out why a single bleached chimney half a mile upwind sounded too steady to be an accident.

It didn’t hold. A human up here is always braced against the place. You walk heavy; you pack wool into your head; you cut into the floor to hide from the sky, and then you leave before the roar wears your sleep down to splinters. You never stop being a guest who is slightly too loud for the house.

So I went down—past the three-foot camp pits, past the nervous patter of the burrowers, all the way through the tangled dry lattice until the sunlight broke into dusty needles and died, and the insect scream smoothed out into something like surf heard through a closed door.

Down against the granite, beside the still tea-colored saucers, I nearly stopped. There is an enormous peace in the oldest ones who stay down there: the ones whose hide has darkened toward walnut, whose shoulder-creases have begun to grow faint silver blooms of crustose lichen, who sit for an hour beside a peat-dam listening to the plateau move through their thickening jawbones. I could easily stay beside that dark water and never climb into the glare again.

Except my hands aren't quiet enough for that yet. And something in me still wants to touch the place where the air tears.

So I find myself a little higher than the stone, and a little closer to the edge of the world.

Not in the broad, settled interior of the plateau, where the saucer-basins roll slow and even for leagues, and a well-tended vent-stalk might hold its pitch for three seasons without needing a scraper. I am in a rimward basin, barely two miles inward from the unseen drop-off where the ancient mountain roof breaks off into clouds and lowland heat.

The floor works harder here. The updrafts from the valleys miles below don't arrive as smooth, warm currents; they tumble over the far lip raw and gusty, dragging ragged fists of mist across the western swale while the eastern side of the same basin is still dry and rasping under full sun. The fifteen-foot mat swells unevenly under that kind of weather. Its braided arches groan; its seams pull and shift; its water-pools tilt. Everything here goes out of tune faster than it does inland.

And that is why I live in the middle-dark of this particular vault, slung in an open-meshed hammock of braided root-fiber halfway between the granite and the moss, with one long wrist looped lightly through a taut watch-cord.

I am past the quick, restless years when you spend half your waking hours beneath the rodent warrens, tapping a chisel-nail in a fake falcon-alarm just for the clean satisfaction of the drop and the catch. I am not yet old enough to have given up the climbs. My limbs are still spare and long enough to take a thirty-foot chimney without burning too much breath, though my jawbone has begun to carry that dense, warm weight that comes after twenty or thirty summers—the kind that turns a faint shudder in a root into a clear picture you can almost see behind your translucent lids.

There is also a small flaw on the left side of my head.

Years back, climbing a storm-split vent-stalk during a sudden downdraft, I took a ragged sliver of sun-baked wood across the cartilage ridge behind my left jaw. It healed cleanly enough, pale as dry sinew, but the crescent fold never clamped quite flush afterward. When I rise toward the crust and lock my ears shut, the right side turns the midday din into a dull, bearable pressure against the skull. The left leaks. Not much—just a hairline crack, enough to let the sharpest, saw-toothed edge of an untended chimney’s shriek slide straight into the hinge of my jaw like a cold needle.

It made me particular. Maybe it made me stubborn.

My kin in the basin share the usual work—propping sagging arches with bleached grazer-ribs, scraping green mold from the pale chestnut-fungi along the wet pillars, mending the crescent peat-dams around the long, dark reservoir where our granite floor tilts downward. I do my share of that, gliding through the low galleries on all fours with my weight spread across four broad, velvet-padded hands so the rodents overhead don't even break stride.

But what I really tend are the *lines* and the *throats*.

This basin is an ear for three wider basins to the east. Every morning, before the sun climbs high enough to drive the surface swarms to full skull-rattling pitch, I drop out of my hammock, breathe slow and deep into the bottom of my barrel ribs, and walk the main horizontal root-trunks that tie our western rim to the interior. I carry a lump of warm, yellow deep-resin in a folded leaf-cup and a couple of notched grazer-knuckles strung on my sinew belt.

A sounding-line goes dull quickly near the rim. Damp seeps down through a strained seam; loose fiber swells; a root sags an inch until its belly kisses a wet shelf of granite, and suddenly the stone drinks half the message before it can travel a hundred paces. So I crawl the length of the great lead-trunks in the warm cedar-scented dimness, stripping away fresh fuzz with my worn horn-bright nails, rubbing yellow resin into the wood until it slides smooth and glassy under my palm, and wedging a clean bone knuckle underneath to lift the string back into empty air.

Then I drop onto my flat palms, lower my chin, and press the hollow underside of my jaw straight onto the wood.

Eyes half-lidded. Breath held for twenty beats.

The whole western edge of the world sorts itself out through my teeth.

Half a mile upwind, the upper fibers of the mat are already slackening by a quarter-tone—soft, elastic, drinking moisture. A mist-ribbon is spilling over the far lip right now, still invisible, still ten minutes from reaching our swale. Closer in, over the southern lip of the basin, there is the rolling, dry-bean scatter of a grazer ribbon drifting east—thirty or forty of them, unhurried, nobody pushing their heels. And far down the eastern trunk, two miles away through leagues of root-vaulted dark, comes the slow, steady heel-of-the-hand thrum of an elder in the next basin checking the line.

I answer with two crisp clicks of a chisel-nail and a long, resin-slick slide of my thumb along the grain: *Mist coming over the lip. Light band. Pools full. Roof holding.*

A pause of three heartbeats while the wood carries it away, then the reply humming back up through my molars—slower, heavier, shaped by hands stiffened with age and lichen: *Heard. Two-beat walkers crossed our south trough at dawn. Four of them. Carrying metal.*

I lift my chin and sit back on my narrow haunches, letting out a thin, soundless sigh.

Humans climb up from the hidden valleys along a steep, broken notch three leagues south of us, which means our rimward swales often see them first—before the plateau has finished teaching them how to move. You can always tell a fresh lowland party from a seasoned band. The seasoned ones spread their weight over their broad woven soles, keep their fires small and shallow, and step around the thin seams above the water-saucers as if they can smell the dark pools underneath. The fresh ones walk like falling stones—*thump, pause, thump, pause*—too blunt for grazers, too heavy for anything born up here, dragging smoke down through the upper lattice until our inner eyelids film over and we have to shift our hammocks two galleries east.

Still, I listen for them with a quiet, practical interest. They carry copper up from the unseen floor of the world.

When the mist-band finally hits our swale overhead, I don't need to see the sky turn milky to know it has arrived. The surf-roar above the fifteen-foot ceiling drops abruptly in half, losing its dry, tearing rasp, and in the sudden hollow behind it the frogs surge up from the stalk-bases and the whole roof begins to tick—a thousand tiny silver beads gathering on the surface hairs and trickling down through the braided lattice into our tea-pools.

That is my hour.

I check the belt around my narrow waist: two curved bone scrapers, three plugs of resin-hardened peat, a dry gourd-baffle pierced along its rim, and—wrapped carefully in a scrap of bright red dyed wool traded two summers ago—a curved tongue of green-tarnished copper, thinned along one edge with a flat river-stone until it trembles when you breathe on it.

I flow up through the angled root-ribs toward the base of our rimward cluster.

Unlike the calm inland basins, where a single lone vent-stalk can stand like a mast and hold one clean note all afternoon, a lone chimney here near the edge goes mad in the cross-drafts. So over generations my kin encouraged four stalks to grow close along the western lip of our saucer—two tall, sun-bleached flutes thirty feet high, and two shorter, thicker ones standing shoulder to shoulder on their lee side. Left to themselves, the raw wind off the cliff tears through them in four competing howls that grind against each other like splitting rock. Even down in the vaults you feel that discord as a sour flutter in the ribs.

Up under the cushion-shrubs, where the damp green twilight smells of crushed thyme and wet bark, I clamp my ear-slits shut. The right side closes tight as a diving seal. The left seals most of the way, leaving that thin, aching wire of sound.

I press one broad palm against the underside of a gnarled moss-seam, feel no sharp footsteps, no looming shadow of a tufted-eared pack, and ease the seam apart.

The world opens white and warm.

Everything is drowned in ten minutes of moving milk. The horizon is gone; the indigo zenith is gone; the vultures have already slid down-wind to wait out the fog. Around my splayed hands the ochre-green turf steams faintly, silver drops clinging to every curled leaf and already beading along the fine down of my shoulders and forearms. I lick a cool streak cleanly off my wrist, keep low on all fours so I barely dent the springy skin, and flow across twenty paces of mossy swell to the base of the tallest stalk.

Its lower throat is crammed with bright-throated frogs, singing their wet, brassy booms straight up the wooden flue. They barely scatter as I hook my toes and long, double-jointed fingers into the oval pores and climb. Halfway up, a flurry of bristle-winged swifts bursts out of a shaded vent-hole around my knees, furious and chattering, vanishing into the white cloud. Higher still, the mist begins to thin; the sky above my back hints at a hard, pale blue.

I cling twenty-five feet up the fluted, bone-pale column, ribs expanding slowly against the thin air, while the raw rim-wind rushes past my flanks and screams straight through a split upper pore just above my left shoulder.

The sound saws right through my bad ear-slit, hot and jagged, making my teeth ache.

I don't hurry. Haste burns breath, and breath is dear up here. Hooking one ankle firmly into a lower pore, I pull a curved grazer-rib from my belt and begin to shave the splintered wooden lip smooth, rounding the wood the way a flute-maker bevels a mouth-hole, letting the pale shavings whirl away into the thinning mist. The shriek softens immediately—still wild, still too high, bending sharp whenever a gust tumbles over the western cliff, but no longer tearing.

Next, I press a thumb-sized plug of dark resin-peat into the port directly opposite, choking back the cross-draft that was parching the cool condenser-chamber below. I lean my chest close to the bleached wood, feeling the vibration run straight through my sternum.

Better. A low, hollow moan now, settling somewhere near a steady root-note, though the rim-gusts still yank it up a half-step every few seconds.

Before the mist rips completely apart, I slide down the shaft, flow across six paces of damp moss to the shorter leeward stalk, and climb again. This is the real trick of a rimward cluster—you never fight the gust head-on. You give it somewhere to go. I widen a shaded mid-height pore with my bone knife until the second chimney catches the overflow whenever the wind surges, dragging the wild interval down into a wide, open fifth.

Then, just inside the leeward lip where the swifts won't foul it and the noon glare won't warp the sinew, I tie in the curved tongue of scavenged copper, hanging a tiny polished rodent-bone clapper half a finger's width beside its edge on a strand of dry gut.

As I finish the knot, the mist tears open like wet cloth.

Hard, clean sunlight slams back down onto the rolling green-ochre expanse. Steam lifts off the cushion-shrubs in pale ribbons. All around the base of the stalks, three leagues wide in every direction, the stridulating insects wake all at once—a rising, dry, saw-toothed wall of noise that climbs back up to full skull-rattling pitch in ten heartbeats.

I stay clinging to the stalk for five seconds longer than I strictly need to, arms hooked tight, translucent inner lids slid across my eyes against the white glare, letting the first clear afternoon gust hit the cluster.

The tall chimney catches the draft and settles into its deep, wooden drone. The gust swells, bending the pitch upward—and immediately the leeward stalk answers a clean fifth beneath it, holding the chord wide and steady against the push. Inside the shaded lip, the bone clapper nudges the green-tarnished copper.

*Tinnnnng.*

Not loud. Not trying to out-shout the billions of rubbing legs or the screeching kites overhead. Just a pure, liquid thread of metal ringing cleanly through the middle of the storm, right into the gap where my left ear-slit leaks, easing the ache behind my jaw until my whole ribcage drops half an inch in relief.

I slide down the bleached shaft, drop onto my flat palms, and slip back through the cushion-shrubs before the high thermal gyres of vultures have even finished widening back over the basin. The pale moss knits smoothly shut behind my heels. No ground-birds flush. The insects don't miss a beat.

Down in the cool cedar-smelling shadow, the copper note follows me faintly down the hollow throat of the stalk, softened by fifteen feet of living roof until it feels less like something heard and more like a quiet pulse in the dark water.

Evening comes slowly up here. You feel it first as a slackening in the air—the warm updrafts easing off, the taut surface fibers cooling by a few degrees as the sun drops low and lays brassy light across the swells. Overhead, the pitch of the plateau turns from dry rasp to deep wet chorus: booming frogs, rattling night-wings, the long, deliberate chords of our rimward cluster humming out across the empty miles like harbor bells over an unseen sea.

Two hours after dark, when the sharp cold stars are out above the roof, I feel a faint, clumsy rhythm travel down our southern lead-line.

*Thump. Pause. Thump. Pause.*

Not a full band—just one pair of blunt, heavy boots, moving erratically thirty paces off the compressed hunting trough. Lost, probably, or lingered too late after a chase when a stray ribbon of night-mist crept over the rim and swallowed the bone-markers. I press my jaw hard to the resin-slicked root and read their weight. They are drifting too far west, straight toward the thin, rotten arch above our northern condensation-pocket. Another twenty paces and their flat woven soles will punch straight through the crust, dropping them waist-deep into sour peat and dumping dry turf straight into our clearest tea-pool.

I don't wake the rest of the kin. There's no malice in this step—no axe-blows against a stalk, no deep pit dug into a reservoir, just the blind stumble of a creature trying to navigate a drumhead with wax stuffed in its ears.

I glide back up through the warm woody ribs, ease my head and one long pale arm into the dry throat of the leeward chimney—not even emerging into the open starlight—and take the hanging bone clapper lightly between two chisel-nailed fingers.

Through the palm of my other hand, pressed flat against the inner wall of the stalk, I can still feel the lost hunter’s hesitant steps pausing, turning, casting blindly across the springy moss.

I tap the copper rim once. Count ten slow heartbeats in my deep chest. Tap it again.

*Tinnnnng.*

Down through the wood, three hundred paces south, the blunt two-beat cadence stops dead.

Ten heartbeats.

*Tinnnnng.*

They turn. Slowly at first, testing the give of the turf beneath their heels, angling away from the thin arch above our water and back toward the hard-packed trough where the bleached leg-bones stand wrapped in wool. I keep striking the copper every ten beats—patient, steady, no warmer and no colder than a buoy bell—until I feel their heavy soles find the compressed path and straighten out toward the faint, sweet root-smoke of their camp half a league east.

Then I let the clapper go, withdraw my pale arm into the dark, and slide back down to the junction of the eastern vaults.

The elder two miles inland is already awake on the line, sending a quiet, inquiring thrum through the glassy wood.

I drop onto my broad palms, rest my chin against the smooth root, and tap back that the night is clear, the pools are unbroken, and the stray walker has found their path.

The elder answers with a slow, amused figure—three crisp clicks and a damped slide—and then opens a five-beat rhythm that carries no news at all.

I catch it in my teeth, let it settle into the hollow of my jaw, and send a counter-rhythm braided straight back through two miles of living wood. Above us, the night-feeders pick across the mossy roof, the grazers shift in their sleep, and the rimward chimneys hold their clean, wide fifth against the dark wind—while down here, stretched out along the strings of the world, we keep the chord alive and keep the roof from falling in.
