At first, I think I would not be a person at all.

When a place arrives as noise before it becomes shape, the instinct—at least my instinct—is not to stand up inside it and claim a name. It is to go very flat and listen for the seam where the violence stops being random. If I drop into this valley without deciding too quickly what shape to wear, I feel myself pulled in two directions before a body ever forms.

Part of me wants the sky’s own geometry. Not to be the wind—the wind has no patience, no memory, it only boils and collides—but to be one of those heavy, angular gliders hanging in the bright gulf where two thermals grind against each other. There is something intoxicating about never beating a wing, only folding and snapping dark membrane into asymmetrical angles to ride the boundary between rising air and falling air. Pure responsiveness. Living on the seam between opposites and never once mistaking stillness for safety.

And yet, watching from that height, I feel the loneliness of it immediately. The glider reads the air with its whole skeleton, yes, and plunges cleanly into a tumbling root-raft for the pale grubs inside, and banks away without a backward glance. It has no use for what was on that cliff last week, or who tied three pale strips of hide to the leaning ochre needle. It keeps nothing. If I became the glider, I would lose the very thing in this world that makes my chest tighten: the small, stubborn marks tucked into the leeward cracks.

So I drop back down to the warm stone, and the second pull is stranger still.

For a long moment, I think I might just be the curved slab of hardwood pinned beside the copper pegs.

Look at what it does. It lies lashed to bare quartzite with rawhide and stone shims, hollowed underneath, notched along its upper rim. By itself, it is dead brush-wood, dark with oil and sweat. It has no voice of its own, no urge to cross the chasm. Yet the entire roaring incoherence of ten miles of hollow sky pours through the bedrock into its belly, and because of the shape of its hollowing and the width of its notches, the froth falls away. The screeching surface shear disappears. What remains is grammar: the slow swell of a distant boiler, the heavy tread of a vortex rounding an arch, the brief slackening that means ten clear breaths across a knife-edge rib. It exists solely to sit between something too vast to bear and someone small enough to be broken by it, and make the one legible to the other.

I could almost stop there. It feels startlingly familiar—that quiet usefulness, that waiting pinned to stone until a warm jawbone comes to rest along the rim.

Except a trough does not know who knelt at it yesterday, or whether the pale strips on the far needle have frayed down to threads since the last dry draft. A trough does not feel the sudden, sharp gladness when a pair of scarred knuckles appears over the satin-smooth rim of an overhang. And someone had to carve those notches in the first place.

That is where the shape finally settles, and warms, and begins to breathe.

---

I would be the one who tunes the wood, and keeps a crossroads.

Not on one of the solitary outer needles—those belong to the lean, spring-jointed climbers who have the stomach for three days of waiting and a blind tumble inside six feet of wet peat. I would have made a peel-crossing once, maybe twice, when I was younger and lighter and still curious about what the green blizzard felt like from the inside. Enough to know the stomach-lurch of weightlessness, the bruising slam of a sinking pocket, the way white vine-latex smells when it seals you and a shredded hillside to an unfamiliar cliff. Enough that when someone rolls over my windbreak trembling and streaked with sap, I know what their ribs feel like without having to ask.

But my place would not be out in the flying green. It would be on a broad, perforated stone arch near the middle of the valley—one of the ancient, forked bones where two different chasms meet and four distant spires stand in clear view across the bright emptiness.

High on the leeward haunch of that arch, sheltered by a deep lip of iron-bound quartzite, there is a dead flue. Ages ago its lower throat choked on fallen slabs, leaving a warm, dimly sunlit well thirty feet deep, just wide enough for a braided vine-hammock, a terraced ledge of wedged stone, and a tiny baffled hearth where copper-brush roots smolder slow and smokeless over hot pebbles. Above the rim, the shear rips past like surf over a sea-cave, muffled through my oiled moss-plugs into a soft, constant wooly rush. Down inside the belly of the rock, the walls stay faintly warm through the night, humming with the deep bass of the living chimneys fifty yards away.

On the crossing-hip outside—behind a low half-circle of dry-stacked stone—I would keep not one listening-trough, but two.

One pinned to the eastern rib of the arch, facing the wide gulf where the biggest thermals boil up out of the pale haze; the other wedged into the western shoulder, where the rock narrows toward a chain of leaning pillars and bridged saddles. Because no two spires share a throat, and even two flanks of the same arch carry different harmonics. A trough carved for the east flank will rattle and lie if you lash it to the west.

That is the craft I would have given my hands to.

When a heavy wind-shift tears through, or a cloud-slam swells the rawhide lashings and then the dry cross-draft shrinks them again, a trough loses its honesty. A stone shim works half a millimeter loose, and suddenly the surface roar bleeds back into the wood as a muddy, teeth-numbing buzz. Worse, once every few years, deep below the haze or inside some neighboring pillar, an old chimney collapses or splits a new eyelet, and the bass-line of the entire valley shifts by a hair. The old notches stop matching the stone.

So I kneel on bare horn-thick soles behind the windbreak, press my cheek directly to the naked quartzite—taking the raw, unsorted hammering straight through my molars until I can hear the true pulse buried beneath the froth—and then I lift the hardwood slab onto my knees. With a flake of dark flint, I shave a curl of wood no thicker than a grazer’s shell from the underside hollow. I widen one notch by the breadth of a fingernail. I wedge it back down, test it with my jaw, listen, lift it again. Sometimes it takes six afternoons of mist and blinding sun before the muddiness falls away and the deep sub-bass rings clean against my teeth, crisp as a knuckle-tap. When it is right, I rub boiled vine-latex and a thumb-smear of glider-oil into the fresh cuts so the grain stays dense and does not split in the shaking.

I would travel, now and then—never far, and never by peel, only along the old connected ribs and traversable saddles during the ten-breath lulls—carrying a flat pouch of flint scrapers, annealed copper shims, and two seasoned blocks of dense brush-wood wrapped in oiled hide. If an elder’s trough on the north saddle has cracked, or a young pair has dug out a new dead flue on a bridged shoulder and needs a listening-block matched to their rock, I go. I read their stone with my bare palms and knees, carve the wood to fit its throat, and leave them with ears they did not have before.

Most days, though, I stay at the arch. Because a valley whose forests peel off and sail away in a single afternoon needs someone who stays still long enough to notice where they landed.

---

My days would have a slow, attentive rhythm, punctuated by thirty-second emergencies.

In the bright morning—though up here, daylight is so ordinary and fierce that morning and afternoon feel mostly like changes in the angle of the heat—I climb out of the flue, settle behind the low stone wall, and look across the gulfs. Not gazing blankly at the empty blue, the way a stranger would, but reading the horizon pole by pole.

Half a mile south, on the leaning ochre needle, the three pale hide-strips are still blurring horizontally against the sky. Good. Two miles west, on the broad saddle with the twin arches, the single sap-blackened rag that warned of bare rock all last moon has been taken down, and in its place snap two broad, knotted strips: a fresh mat must have struck the windward rib and held, enough to fill the water-hollows again. Far to the east, a solitary pole I have watched for half a year is gone—either the climber peeled off to another bone, or a shear-blast snapped the stripped wood clean out of its crevice. I make a mental note, and later, down in the warm dusk of the flue, I will shift a knot on one of the thick vine-cords hanging from a copper peg along my wall.

I keep no paper—paper would be confetti in two heartbeats here—so my maps are knotted cord and scraped lines on a flat slab of buff quartzite wedged beside my hammock. Which spires are lush right now, and which are stripped gleaming bare. Which windward shelves have grown so thick and water-heavy with emerald moss that they are groaning toward a peel. Which crossing-hips are low on water-bladders or have copper pegs working loose in the quartz. A memory of things that do not last, anchored in the only stone that does.

Then the eastern trough shudders against my palm—a wet, heavy, rolling thump rising from miles down in the haze. I have perhaps twenty heartbeats before the cloud-ribbon hits.

I hook one forearm through the braided anchor-loop pegged beside the wall, wedge my copper blade between two fingers, and slide out just past the quartzite lip. When the mist slams into the arch out of the clear blue sky, the world turns dark, slick, and blinding in a single instant. Spray whips sideways off the thrashing olive fan-leaves, smelling fiercely of crushed sap and mineral damp. Along the horizontal seam beneath my ribs, the coin-shaped grazers lift their ridged dust-colored shells a hair’s breadth to flick their bristled tongues over the wet rock. Flick, flick—I take two, leaving the rest so the seam stays rich, and tuck the heavy discs into my waist-loop. With my other hand I jam a dry wad of fibrous moss into the foaming cup of a waxy rosette, haul it back swollen and tepid, and wring it hard into the neck of the scraped bladder wedged behind the stones.

Thirty seconds later, the dry cross-draft slices the mist to tatters. The ochre rock steams in the bright air. I roll back behind the windbreak, hair varnished flat with latex so it never stings my eyes, chewing slowly on a sliver of tough, salty grazer-meat while the valley dries around me.

And in the quiet hours between cloud-slams—when the sun beats warm on my bare shoulder and the air-roots stream straight outward into the void like thrashing green flames—I wait for hands to appear over the overhang.

---

That is the heart of why I would not be the glider, and why I could never quite settle for being the slab of wood.

Days can pass with no one crossing my arch. Weeks, sometimes, when the shears turn ugly across the southern ribs. And then, with no sound at all—because nothing with lungs or footsteps can out-shout the chimneys—two broad, sap-glossy palms settle flat on the satin-smooth quartzite lip, pause for two heartbeats to read the spine of the bridge, and a climber rolls smoothly into the pocket behind my wall.

Maybe it is someone I have not seen since last season, their glider-hide cloak newly patched with pale sinew, their brow-band dark with sweat and grit. Maybe it is a young one I have never met, still wild-eyed from their first big peel-landing on the east spire, white latex crusted across one cheekbone and dried in their cropped hair.

I do not wave. I do not pull out my moss-plugs. I hand them a full bladder first, let them drink and catch their breath while their knees stop shaking from the rib-crossing. Then we kneel face to face behind the stacked stones, wrap our scarred hands behind each other’s necks, and press our foreheads squarely together.

And the valley disappears into bone.

Through the warm contact of skull against skull, and a flat palm pressed lightly against the sternum, their voice arrives—not a sound fighting the wind, just a deep, steady carpentry of chest-rumbles, gliding nasal notes, sharp little clicks of the teeth, and the swift, familiar shorthand of fingers tracing arcs and counts along my forearm.

They tell me the northern arch lost its whole emerald crown three days ago in a wet screech that shook the stone for half a mile; they watched the green wreck sail east and snag half-whole on the twin needles. They tell me an old glider broke a wing-hinge against the pale fin two spires over, and there is fresh membrane and heavy hollow bone waiting if anyone can make the ten-breath rib before the scavengers strip it. They tell me a child was born in the deep flue on the western saddle—healthy, already gripping vine-cord with both fists, lulled against her father’s humming jaw. Sometimes they simply hold a long, low, steady note against my brow, pitched right down at the stone’s own thrumming, and press three slow taps into my collarbone, and I know without another gesture that someone’s moss-raft sank into the pale haze and did not come back up.

We sit like that for a long time, eyes closed, matching the vibration of the ancient rock beneath our knees.

Then I hum back what I have gathered. I tell them about the west pole changing its strips, about the loose copper peg on the lower rib, about the crimson-creeper mat that landed fat with grubs on the leeward shelf below my arch. If their knees are trembling from a rough turn of the air, I take their hand and lay it flat on my newly tuned trough, guiding their jaw to the notched rim so they can feel how clean the eastern pulse comes through now—how the shear across the next gap is already softening, promising a window before dusk.

If they have time, we drop down into the dead flue together. I break open a few dried grazer-strips over the tiny glowing bed of brush-roots, let them warm their stiff fingers over the pebble-hearth, and mend a frayed toggle on their harness while they sleep an hour in the quiet dimness.

And if it is a child traveling strapped to a parent’s chest, or a weary climber who needs a moment out of the teeth-rattling wind, I reach into a small sap-sealed root-basket tucked into a flute of the chimney wall and pull out one of my small luxuries: a pale, wind-pitted mineral nodule, no bigger than an egg, caught off a gravel-shelf after a deep upward blast flung it out of the abyss. Down here in the plugged quiet, away from the gale, I lift it to my lips and blow softly across its hollow mouth. Even through oiled moss-plugs, you can just catch the faint, clear, descending whistle—or feel the delicate tremor of it against your fingertips if you hold the stone against your throat. A tiny voice from the warm, unnamed mist below, where nothing solid ever climbs back out.

---

Eventually, of course, my own knees will stiffen with salt and old bruises, and I will stop even the short scrambles to neighboring saddles. I will become one of the true elders of the crossroads—one of those still figures who can lay two crooked fingers against a sun-baked fin and feel a shear turning three spires away before the olive fronds even shift their pitch. My hands will stay busy re-twisting frayed vine-ropes, annealing soft copper nodules over the choked coals, keeping the two troughs wedged tight and honest, and waiting behind the low stone wall for the next pair of hands to appear over the lip.

And when the day comes that even my fingers can no longer scrape a flint edge along hardwood, and the slow breathing of the chimneys starts to fade out of my teeth, they will carry me out to a heavy windward shelf where the emerald moss has grown thick and ready to let go. They will wrap me in broad, glistening creepers and damp peat, tuck me into the springy root-heart without a chopper or a harness, and kneel together behind the stones with their foreheads pressed to the warm ochre rock until the deep sub-bass swells and the hillside tears cleanly loose into the bright air.

Even then, I think I would still be what I was—just traded back to the wind, drifting across the wide blue toward some bare spire that needs a coat of green, or sinking softly into the warm haze that feeds the singing throats of the stone.

Until then, you would find me here: knees drawn up behind a shoulder-high wall of stacked quartzite, ears plugged against the roaring surf of the sky, one scarred palm resting flat on a slab of notched hardwood, and eyes narrowed against the ordinary, dazzling daylight—listening to the bones of the valley, watching the distant rags snap in the wind, and keeping the bridge warm for whoever comes next.
