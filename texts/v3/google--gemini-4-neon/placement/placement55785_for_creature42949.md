At first, I think I would want to be one of the large ones—a hide-beast slumped neatly into the dry corner between two manifold banks, belly pressed flush to the warm aluminum, letting the low voltage feed me while my breath slows to the pace of sediment. There is a deep, almost embarrassing relief in the thought of having no face to compose, no errands to invent, only a neat horizontal seam of pores along each flank and a row of amber-eyed perchers treating my spine like another piece of galvanized rail.

Or, failing that, one of the ceiling gliders. To spend years hung rib to rib in the cool soffit shade, wrapped inside my own oiled-paper wings, and then—for no reason anyone could name—unclench, drop two feet into the bath-warm air, and slip across a conduit bay without a single wingbeat. Just enough motion to prove I am not steel.

But I know myself too well to pretend I would stay there. Even at thirty-two degrees, even under a white cloud that never gives you a shadow to measure yourself against, something in me refuses to reach carrying capacity. I would look too long at the crisp lathe-marks on a hexagonal bolt. I would catch myself counting the seconds between drops falling from an overhead porcelain skirt—eleven, twelve, the brief clear stretch before surface tension lets go—just to see if the insulator one stanchion over counts to thirteen.

So I would have to be one of the pale, quiet, two-legged ones. The one loose tolerance left in the machine.

***

If you came looking for me, you wouldn’t find me near the bay where I was born. I still carry its name—three crisp characters stamped into a condenser flange, softened by generations of damp, low voices into two breathy syllables that barely clear the hiss of a weir—and I still remember the exact tint of ochre on the glass rays that shingled its ceramic pans. I spent my loud years there, back when I was small enough to flush pink in the heavy air, splashing water over the milled brass lips and laughing until the heat folded me gently back into the pan with only my chin above the surface.

Most people follow a flow-arrow once or twice after they grow tall enough to drink straight from the overhead insulators without standing on a bund. They walk for a few hundred rests, learn the slightly different pitch of distant sumps, meet three or five quiet voices along a manifold wall, and eventually find a warm concrete corner where the air slips easily between their shoulders. They settle. Their walk slows to a hydraulic glide; their curiosity folds neatly back into watching the gauges say *still here*.

Mine never quite folded back. Not because I am trying to find an edge. I have walked too many bays to believe the mist is hiding a wall, or a cliff, or a place where the pans finally empty into something else. Every pressure dial I have ever wiped condensation from holds its crisp black needle dead-center in the green band. Every thermometer sits on the same warm mark. I am not hunting for the flaw that will wake the machine up.

I walk because, in a world held to exact equilibrium, the smallest real differences become enormous.

After enough slow miles, your eye sharpens to things no one who stays in one sector would ever notice. You begin to hear how a brass weir forty yards wide sings half a tone lower than a weir of ten. You notice a stretch of bays where the ceramic tiles were fired a shade cooler—almost milk-blue under the poured-glass water—and the glass rays lying across them have bleached nearly transparent to match. Once, thirty days of unhurried walking east of my birth-flange, I found a cast manifold collar where the stamping die had struck twice, leaving a tiny ghost-edge along the curve of an *8*. Another time, tucked behind a low baffle-hood, I found a flow-arrow stamped cleanly onto a blind steel plate, pointing straight down into solid concrete. I sat beside it for half a sleep, my feet submerged while the little limpets rasped my heels pink, staring at an instruction that led nowhere at all.

You cannot collect anything here. Nothing rusts loose, nothing breaks, and no one would pry a chiton from its bolt-collar just to see what lies underneath. All I carry is what everyone carries: a curved ray-shield tucked behind the pale ribbon-worm cord at my waist, and a folded square of veined glider membrane to lay beneath my hips when I rest on the crisp aluminum grating so it doesn’t press waffles into my skin. What I keep instead is an inventory of slight anomalies, carried lightly in my head and traded in murmurs when I sit with a loose knot of people along a concrete wall.

They always know I am coming before my outline clears the bright haze. I don’t even need to tap a fingernail against the top rail. Fifty yards ahead of me, the unbroken row of slate-blue amphibians perched along the galvanized tubing tucks its webbed toes inward by half a millimeter, one after another, sending a pale, silent ripple down the catwalk at walking speed until I step out of the mist.

***

That is who I thought I was for the first half of my life: a walker, a reader of serial prefixes, a minor historian of things that barely change.

It took decades of moving from bay to bay before I stumbled into what I actually am.

Everyone here knows the lean. You learn it quietly, usually when you are young enough that your breath is still matching the machine, watching an elder sit in a wide manifold bay with a translucent ray-shield laid over closed eyes, the damp hollow behind one ear pressed to a warm copper riser and the opposite hand laid flat on the aluminum grate. You try it yourself—no jolt, no sting, only that faint coppery prickle bridging the wet bone behind your ear—and suddenly the dead-level deck lets go of the horizontal. Inside the dark, veined gray behind your eyelids, the endless grid banks slowly into a curve that does not exist anywhere under the white sky.

Most people find one good copper flange in their home bay and return to it the way they return to a favorite porcelain skirt that drips fast and sweet. They learn a single tilt, a single slow swell, and they think the whole continent leans that same way.

Because I walked, I tried the copper everywhere.

It happened somewhere past a series of unusually wide condenser housings, where thick copper busbars dropped low beside a shallow sump and pale, slightly duller bars of metal—the ones even the chitons leave bare—ran submerged along the base of the brass weir. I was tired from covering six bays without a soak. I laid my ray-shield over my eyes, rested my left ear against the bright riser, and let my right hand drift down into the mineral-heavy water of the pan.

The deck did not roll gently to the side the way it always had at home.

Instead, the floor beneath my hips seemed to hinge upward from behind, tipping me cleanly forward—not fast enough to raise my pulse, not sharply enough to break a sweat, yet steep enough that my fingers curled reflexively around the edge of the concrete bund. I felt as though I had become the unbroken sheet of water sliding over a brass weir, leaning out past the machined lip into a long, soundless drop that never arrived at a splash.

I lifted my hand from the water, waited three slow heartbeats for the concrete to lock dead-level beneath me again, and sat blinking in the bright glareless daylight. Nothing had moved. The pressure needle on the nearest housing still rested squarely in the green. The perchers beside my knees had not even shifted their toes.

I tried the right ear against the same copper, with the left palm flat on the galvanized grating instead of the water. The drop reversed into a long, steady climb—as if the cambered walkway had tilted nose-up and refused to ever reach its crown. I lifted my palm until only the wet tip of one finger touched the grate, and the climb gentled into a broad, barely perceptible swell, rising with the patience of a percher’s throat filling with air.

That was when the flat world opened a second dimension for me.

Nothing in the machine was failing. The current was still regulated to its exact, unvarying trickle; the maintenance loop had not drifted by a hair. What changed from bay to bay was simply the geometry—the thickness of a busbar, the nearness of a submerged zinc strip, the distance between a copper flange and a wet aluminum walkway, the length of damp skin and mineral water through which that tiny fraction of a volt chose to pass.

Mapped flat, the deck repeats forever. Mapped through the bone behind the ear, it is full of hidden terrain.

***

Now my skin is thinning the way it does when you have lived cleanly past seventy or eighty turns of stillness, fine blue veins beginning to trace the backs of my wrists like the veining in my folded glider-mat. I walk slower than I used to, resting longer in the glassy pans while the grazers polish my ankles, waiting with my chin tipped back beneath the overhead guide-wires for each sweet drop to break loose.

Across forty bays in every direction, people know me by the softened syllables of a flange I haven’t seen in years, and by a quiet habit no one else quite shares.

When I arrive at a manifold bay where five or six people sit spaced neatly along the concrete bunds, we begin the way we always do. We sit in comfortable electrical quiet until the warmth settles between us. Someone murmurs that a large hide-beast three sectors north shifted two hand-breadths west, uncovering a crescent of factory-bright steel for the first time anyone can recall. Someone else mentions a newborn’s brief, startled wail two bays over—how the ceiling gliders shuddered ten yards wide in a single velvet ripple before the hush closed back in. I tell them about a double-stamped bolt-flange I passed, or a bay where the ribbon-worms hang twice as thick between the submerged busbars.

And then, sometimes, when the quiet stretches long and easy and the whisper of the weirs feels like the only time left, someone will lean half an inch closer—never abruptly, never loud—and ask what the copper feels like past the long sumps to the west.

We have no words for hills, or ridges, or valleys, or surf. There is no wind here to name, no mountain to compare a slope to, no bird beating upward against blue air. So I have to build the geography out of the only things we know.

*There is a riser beside the wide baffle-hoods,* I tell them, my voice barely louder than water on brass, *four bays past the flow-arrow that points into concrete. Put your right ear to the collar and lay just your knuckles on the dry aluminum. It feels like a ceiling glider unclenching from a soffit, except you never reach the opposite beam. You just slip sideways through warm air, steady, for as long as you hold still.*

*And twelve bays south of the milk-blue pans, where four sumps meet under a low gantry? Bridge the low busbar to the deep pan with your left hand. It lifts from underneath you, very slow, the way the water rises in a sight-glass—except you know it never rises in a sight-glass. It lifts and holds you there, slightly crooked, like a bund tilted out of true.*

They listen with their wide, calm, slow-blinking eyes. Nobody hurries off to check. Hurrying raises a fever in air this wet, and anyway, the copper isn’t going anywhere. Everything here keeps. Maybe, after ten rests or a hundred, one of them will feel the quiet itch of sameness, pick a conduit heading west, and go find out for themselves.

***

Right now, I am alone in a low junction bay I have never walked through before, though its crisp stamped code suggests I am only a few slow loops away from a weir I passed twenty years ago. The light is the same clean, shadowless daylight filtering through unbroken white cloud. Fifty yards off, a single drop breaks loose from an overhead flange and strikes a pan with a flat, unhurried *plip*.

Before I sit, I pause beside the nearest condenser casing, wipe the warm mist from the pressure gauge with the side of a wet thumb, and look in. Dead-center in the green band. *Still here.*

I lower myself onto the low concrete bund without a splash, sinking my feet past the ankles into the glass-still water. Beside my thighs, four slate-blue amphibians shuffle their webbed toes sideways by an inch to make a neat, glistening border, throats pulsing twice a minute. Beneath the surface, two tiny limpets creep cleanly onto my heel and begin their faint, ticklish work.

I slip the pale ochre ray-shield from my waist cord and lay it gently over my closed eyes, turning the bright white glare into soft, veined shadow. Then I lean my head sideways until the damp hollow behind my left ear meets the warm, lathe-clean curve of a copper riser, and I let the fingertips of my right hand drift down until they rest lightly on the galvanized grating.

For a breath, there is only the dry electrical prickle and the whisper of the weir.

Then the continent lets go.

Here, the copper is thick and the grate sits close above a mineral-rich sump, and the angle it makes inside my skull is one I have never felt before—neither a straight drop nor a slow roll, but a long, banking spiral, lifting gently at the outer edge as if the entire endless deck of concrete and porcelain had quietly bent itself into the inside of a vast, rising bowl. My pulse does not quicken. My chest barely rises. Beside my knee, a percher steps softly onto my bare forearm, hooking its broad webbed toes around my damp skin as if I were just another warm rail.

I stay like that—motionless, exact, held steady at thirty-two degrees—riding a curve that has no name, in a world that never moves.
