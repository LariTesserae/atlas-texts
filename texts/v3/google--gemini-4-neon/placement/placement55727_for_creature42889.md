At first, the instinct is to arrive with eyes and lungs—to stand in the center of the nave near an amber pilot lamp, let the steam-heavy warmth press against my coat, and watch the room reveal itself.

It takes less than a minute to see how badly that fits. In a hall where a single drop of water falling forty feet is an event, a warm, breathing body is an uproar. Even standing still, I would be ninety-eight degrees of wet weather tearing through smooth threads of rising air, making the dark vanes fold shut in quiet offense. If I am actually *of* this place—not passing through with a notebook and a pulse—I have to give up haste first, and then the wish to look at anything from the outside.

So I let the eyes go, and the lungs, and listen for where in the dark something starts to answer back.

For a while I drift toward the concrete sump beneath the riveted condenser bell. There is a deep pull in those layered black rosettes: wide as two hands, cool and rubbery to the touch, drinking long-wave heat while catching every stray mist-puff from the bimetallic valve. To be rooted in standing mineral water, turning fallen leaves into ink-black silt and never needing to cross ten paces of scorching shale—that has its own fierce peace. Yet the sump feels too enclosed, too local. Something in me keeps turning away from the wet pocket toward the bare tracts of basalt and the forty feet of empty air above them, toward the faint dry vibration in the copper busbars and the long, slow conversations carried by rising heat.

Then I try on the walkers.

That comes much closer. To live on *difference* rather than consumption—holding hot against cool, wet against dry, charged against grounded, carrying yesterday’s contours as hysteresis in bent plates of copper and dark rind—feels immediately familiar, almost uncannily so. I can imagine the quiet gravity of pausing three feet from another stack on the sharp shale, tilting my canopy by a few degrees until hair-fine copper filaments cross the last millimeter of warm air, equalizing voltage against stored water over half an hour with nothing more than a *tick* and an answering *tick*. I can feel the patience of dragging a fallen strap-leaf three inches out of a blocked drainage groove, not out of kindness, only because keeping the seam open keeps the gradient sharp.

And yet, even standing out on the open basalt between the transformer drums, my attention keeps snagging on the eastern wall—fifteen feet up, where the unfinished steel catwalk juts into the blackness and simply stops.

I don’t know why at first. Maybe it is the abruptness of it: the builders bolting iron into fresh-drilled stone, running power down the handrail, and then laying down their wrenches mid-sentence. Up on that dead end, one of the walkers already stands—the patient climber that ratcheted up the stanchion flanges over months to taste the upper boundary where the room’s heat turns.

It isn’t the catwalk deck that pulls at me, though. It is what hangs *beneath* it.

Trailing from the iron handrail, looping down into the unlit middle air between the catwalk and the scorching floor, are the loose conduits the builders never terminated. Curtains of pale, segmented water-tendrils drape around them, swaying almost imperceptibly in the convection columns rising off the oil-cooled transformer drums. Ten feet above the sharp shale, one of those steel pipes ends in a jagged, rusted mouth, two bare copper leads protruding an inch from its rim like severed nerves.

That is where I find myself. Not walking the floor, not rooted in a sump, and not yet a monument cemented to the stone.

I begin there as an accident of the unfinished edge—a tight, palm-sized scab of dark rind and dull verdigris where a drooping water-tendril brushed across a bare copper lead and left a persistent bead of mineral dampness. Most such crusts dry out and flake away into the dust below. This one happened to sit directly above the silent chimney of warm air rising off the nearer transformer drum, while the iron conduit above it climbed straight into the cooler steel handrail bolted deep into the eastern basalt.

A sharp enough difference will always find a shape to inhabit it.

Season by season, I stack—not upward from concrete like the walkers on the nave floor, but *downward*, suspended in the open dark.

Everything about my geometry turns inside out to fit the place. My cool end is above me, clamped into the frayed mouth of the conduit, grounded through fifteen feet of iron into the upper rock. My hot face hangs open to the rising furnace breath of the floor ten feet below. Alternating wafers of matte soot-black tissue and razor-thin precipitated copper build downward along the dangling wire like dark wax gathering on a wick, rimed at every seam with white evaporated salt. Where the floor walkers splay bowed struts against blistering shale, I have no feet at all. Instead, from my lower tiers open three concentric skirts of pleated, radiator-thin vanes—nested dark bells veined with spider-fine copper, angled downward and slightly outward like inverted umbrellas catching a rising rain of infrared.

Around my upper neck, the catwalk’s cloudy water-tendrils have braided themselves directly into my salt crust. Ceiling condensate that strikes the iron handrail forty feet below the vault runs down the outside of the conduit, slips into those translucent bladders, and hangs there as cool counterweight, keeping my stack steady when the convection thickens.

Suspended ten feet up in the darkness—neither on the floor nor on the ceiling, neither fully wild nor wholly separable from the abandoned hardware—I become a kind of plumb-line for the hall.

I have no eyes to see the dull amber-red pilot lamps, and no face to turn toward the wet hairline reflection high on the stone. What I have is two different ways of listening.

Through the steel conduit bolted above me, I know the bones of the chamber. Every ten minutes, when an iron girder across the nave eases a fraction of a millimeter against its anchor bolt, the solitary *ping* travels through the basalt, into the eastern stanchion, down the handrail, and straight through my copper core as a crisp, tiny shudder. When the catwalk walker shifts one of its calcified hooves fifteen feet up, I feel the faint metallic *click* descend the pipe before the dust has settled around its tip. Even the riveted condenser bell far down the hall sends its soft bimetallic sigh through the concrete and up the steel legs of the wall, a pulse so slow and reliable it feels less like a sound than like the measure of time itself.

Through the open air around my pleated bells, I know the breath of the hall. Hanging directly in the rising plume from the transformer drums, I feel the weekly thermal tide before it even crests—the air thickening under my dark skirts, pushing upward with a fraction more lift, making me turn three degrees on the slight slack of my rusted conduit. When the leathery pods tucked beneath the sump rosettes dry along their seams and split open with a dry whisper, their buoyant clouds of soot-fine spores lift straight past me on their way to the vault.

And because I hang in the middle air where no floor walker can stand, I become part of the room’s quiet steering. When three of the great walkers align down the cathedral axis at the peak of the week’s heat and cant their broad canopies to bend the rising drafts, that deflected river of warmth arrives at my lower bells minutes later. Fluid shifts quietly between my rings of translucent water-bladders; one side of my pleated skirt cools by a fraction of a degree and curls inward, tilting my whole hanging stack off plumb. The rising ribbon of spores brushes my matte-black flanks and banks slightly eastward, carried past the dry barren talus toward a freshly sweating fracture high on the drilled basalt wall.

There is no intention in this, any more than a sail intends the wind. Yet over years, the slight bias of my tilt becomes part of the chamber’s weather, written into the dark green-black moss pads beginning to swell along a seam twenty feet above the floor that used to stay baking dry.

Most weeks pass in total solitude. Even in a room of five or six walkers, ten feet of empty air is a wide frontier.

Still, at the far edge of the weekly heat tide—when the center of the nave grows fierce enough to drive the walkers outward toward the cooler stone perimeter—one of them sometimes halts on the bare shale directly beneath my hanging conduit. Usually it is the tall, lean one from the western busbars, its stacked plates still humming with trace voltage pulled from the tarnished copper; sometimes it is the wide, salt-crusted elder from beside the transformer drums, carrying a stray split-washer magnetized to its lower flank.

It stops beneath me and waits. Slowly, over ten minutes, its wide dark canopy tilts upward toward the rising shadow of my lower bell. I feel the disruption in the thermal column immediately—a sudden cool notch punched into the rising warmth, followed by the crisp, faint static field rising off its copper-veined vanes.

On ordinary days, we cannot reach each other. Two feet of warm black air separate its highest whisker from the lowest copper filaments trailing like fringe beneath my pleated skirt. The rising draft lifts my fine threads outward so they hover and sway without closing the gap.

Once in a long while, though, the timing of the room aligns. High in the vault, a heavy warm drop breaks free from the drilled rock, falls forty feet unbroken, and strikes the iron handrail of the catwalk. A thin ribbon of mineral water slides down the rusted conduit, soaks into my upper bladders, and weights my whole suspended stack by a single extra ounce. The slack iron pipe bows downward half an inch. Below me, the walker’s bimetallic arches flex upward by a hair’s breadth against the floor’s heat.

Electrostatic pull takes the final millimeter.

Two hair-fine copper filaments—one rising from the shale, one descending from the unfinished wall—touch in mid-air and hold.

For half an hour, neither of us moves. Through that single thread runs everything the floor knows and everything the upper dark knows: the lingering busbar voltage and the scorched memory of the floor vents rising into my plates; the cool iron ground of the eastern wall, the quiet static of a week’s passing spores, and the slow tremor of the catwalk above bleeding downward into its stack. Deep inside my alternating wafers of rind and copper, a dry *tick* releases. Six seconds later, down on the sharp basalt, a matching *tick* answers.

A single warm drop swells along the shaded underside of my lowest black vane, gathers weight, and lets go. It falls cleanly through two feet of dark air and strikes the dry, white salt-crust of the walker’s upper flank with a soft, flat *tock*, dissolving a hairline path down its ribs. Then the conduit’s iron eases back by a fraction of a millimeter, the whiskers part without a sound, and ten minutes later the struts below me click softly away across the shale.

I do not know how long I have hung here, because time in this hall is measured only in deposited metal and crept salt. I know I am heavier than I was. Molecule by molecule, copper pulled from the frayed conduit leads and mineral lime carried down from the ceiling seams have thickened my plates and ringed my pleated bells with brittle white terraces. Once a season, high above me where the iron pipe clamps to the catwalk rail, a rusted bolt eases with a dry groan no louder than a fingernail against slate, letting me settle another millimeter closer to the floor.

Someday—decades from now, long after the pilot lamps have finally burned their filaments through to blackness—one of two things will happen.

Either the corroded clamp above me will part at last with a single dull *snap*, and the thick curtains of swollen water-tendrils will tangle around my falling bells, slowing me just enough that I settle upright onto the sharp basalt intact—strange, inverted, forced over slow years to push stiff bimetallic arches out from my lower rim until I learn how to lift myself off the stone and join the slow circuits of the nave.

Or the clamp will hold, and my stacking plates will simply keep growing downward, millimeter by year, until my lowest copper-veined skirt brushes the shale, cements itself in creeping white salt to the floor, and turns me into a continuous, eight-foot column of dark rind and tarnished metal—finishing, in mineral patience, the bridge the builders abandoned in steel. Young galvanic buds will stack along my damp base; the small walkers will climb my ribbed flanks to reach the upper air without spending months ratcheting up bare iron stanchions; and the slow trickle of current between floor and wall will run cleanly through my spine long after the last plate has stopped flexing.

I have no preference between becoming a walker and becoming a pillar. Hanging here in the warm dark, turning a few degrees with the weekly breath of the rock, feeling the girder *ping* above and the spores drift past below, it is enough to hold the space where two conditions meet—and let the difference keep its quiet shape.
