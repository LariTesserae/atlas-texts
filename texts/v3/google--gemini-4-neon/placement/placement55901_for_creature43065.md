At first, I think I would look for one of the old ringed courts near the center of the basin—the places where three generations of patient wedging have braided the roar into two or three clear harmonics, and the mist has glazed the jagged walls into something smooth as river-glass. It is easy to want the finished grace of a place like that: a rim you can lay your bare forearm along without bleeding, a black pool that holds still enough to read a kinswoman’s greeting across the surface before she walks around to meet you.

Yet the longer I sit inside the sound of this world, the less I believe I would stay there.

The old courts are already resolved. Their notes have found one another; their edges have already learned to forgive the skin. If I wake in this basin—jaw kept slightly slack so the low bronze drone can enter through the hinge without rattling my teeth, gray clay pressed softly into my outer ears, soles thick with salt-darkened horn—I do not find myself in the pearlescent center.

I find myself further out, in the frayed outer third of the lowland, where the tilted fins grow shorter and meaner, the mist thins enough to let the bruised pewter glare bite along the crests, and the valley’s great chord begins to lose its coherence.

I am one of the people, yes—my collarbones lifted and squared by forty years of listening through the ribs, my hair scraped down to salt-frosted velvet, a rubbery wrap of cured vent-hide slung over my torso and crawler-shells lashed along my forearms with pale gill-cord. But I am not a swift hunter who plays the mouths of chimneys to coax filter-feeders upward, nor one of the elder court-gardeners who can hold a thirty-year geometry of standing waves in their head.

I am, first of all, a mender of neglected seams.

Out near the ragged outer corridors, the silence-pockets do not get much traffic. Most knots of kin keep deeper in, where the pools are richer with oily bacterial film and the overhead bridges of crusted stone grow thicker and safer. Out here, a triangular alcove wedged between three leaning fins might sit empty for twenty swells while the Rise hammers at its joints. By the time I slip sideways through its narrow mouth during a Slack, the wet clay some traveler pressed into the cracks years ago has dried, shrunk, or shaken loose. Fine hairlines of raw sound are leaking back in—a thin, sour whistle from a vent two lanes over, a bright metallic needle of drips cutting straight through the dark.

I like the slow, unhurried work of making them dark again.

I scoop gray clay from the margin of a shallow hollow, grind a tuft of brittle crystal whiskers between my horn-plated palms until it turns to cold, gritty paste, and knead the two together with a smear of milky pool-skim. Then I kneel in the cramped triangular gloom, lay my bare cheek close to the seam to feel where the air trembles, and press the paste in with the side of my thumb. Inch by inch, from the shingle floor up to shoulder height. You can feel the room change around your ribs as you work. The high clatter folds away. The sharp buzz behind the eyes loosens. By the time the last vertical slit closes, the basin’s fury has dropped back down to a dull, companionable throb in the heels, and for the first time in hours I can hear the small, wet rustle of my own breath.

Nobody will ever know it was my thumb that sealed that seam. That is the whole point of it. A silence-pocket belongs to whoever needs it when the water climbs and the stone starts to scream. Leaving it quieter than I found it is not virtue; it is just the minimum courtesy owed to another body made of soft bone and blood.

---

Wait—staying with that image, kneeling there in the dark with gray clay under my blunt nails, something else surfaces. Because if I only mended pockets, I could do that anywhere in the twenty miles of the lowland. Why do I keep drifting back toward the outer lanes, where the shale is meaner and the haze grows pale?

It is because of the *bad corners*.

Deep in the basin, the standing waves are vast and ancient; even where nobody has gardened them, the sheer weight of overlapping chimneys settles into a grand, heavy chord you can lean your posture into. Out near the rim, the crust is shallower and more broken. The pitches don't blend cleanly there. You step around a leaning gypsum blade and walk straight into a tight, nasty pocket of air where two neighboring fissures fight each other half a tone apart—a jagged, wobbling beat that makes the jaw ache and frets the mist so unevenly that the walls sprout dense, ugly beards of crystal whiskers. Brush one with your shoulder and it shatters with a dry chime into cold grit; step wrong on the fresh chips beneath and you open your shin.

I cannot walk past a place where the stone is fighting itself without stopping.

Tucked into the belt-fold of my vent-hide wrap, I keep five or six flat, pale wedges of shale—not blades, never blades, because the floor gives you a knife whenever you reach down. These are smooth-faced, palm-sized stones I have chosen for their weight and taper. When I come to one of those sour, clashing intersections, I crouch in the ankle-deep milky water, tilt my head to let the warring pitches wash across my cheekbones, and find the louder throat.

I do not silence it completely. That belongs to the trails, the quiet seams cut through the fog for navigation. Instead, I tuck the thin edge of a pale wedge just a finger’s width into the whistling lip, listening through my jaw as the note bends down a hair, shifts, and locks cleanly against its neighbor two paces off.

The wobble vanishes. The air stops shivering like a struck plate. Over the next hundred swells, the mist will stop throwing brittle needles onto that wall and begin laying down a thin, dull glaze instead. Nothing anyone would call a court. Nobody will ever gather there to trade news or press palms to collarbones. It is just one narrow bend in an ugly outer lane made slightly less cruel to pass through.

Sometimes, while I am crouched there adjusting a wedge by the width of a fingernail, three chimneys in a row down the alley go suddenly dull—muffled in slow, neat succession, as though something broad and unhurried were gliding through the drowned honeycombing fifty feet beneath my soles. I stop immediately. I take my hand off the stone, lower both gray-plated palms flat onto the wet shingle between my knees, and hold perfectly still. The breath slows in my chest. I feel the muffled line pass straight under my knuckles and drift on toward the deeper basin, and I wait until the bronze chord straightens out behind it before I lift my hands. You do not ask what lives in the flooded dark. You only let it pass without adding your own noise to the floor.

---

And still, there is one more reason I haunt the edge—one I am slower to admit, even to myself, when I return inward during the long Slack to meet my kin.

Once every ten or twenty swells, when the water sits low in the Draw and the vents are sighing inward—drawing faint, dry, peppery scents down from the highlands beyond the lip—I climb. Not often, and never for long. I find a broad, leaning blade near the outer ridge whose crest has been dulled a fraction by old mineral rime, test each grip with my calloused fingers, and pull myself up until my head clears the tangle of wet spines.

Up there, the silence hits me like a hand across the face.

It is nothing like the velvet hush of a caulked pocket. A pocket holds you; you can still feel the valley’s pulse humming patiently under your heels, keeping track of where the walls are. Up beyond the rim, the expanse is empty, flat, weightless. The dry air scrapes my throat. The ground says nothing at all. Looking out across that pale, unshielded stretch, my jaw tightens instinctively, searching for an acoustic shadow that isn't there, and for ten seconds I understand what it would be to lose your sight all at once.

I never stay more than a few breaths. Yet I keep going back up once in a long while, just to press my nose against the edge of that emptiness—because it is the only way I can still understand the strangers who stumble down out of it.

They come rarely, maybe two or three in a decade, always looking like creatures dropped from another element entirely. Because I spend so much of my time tending the ragged outer seams, I am often the first to feel them.

You do not see them first; you read their panic through the wet shingle half a mile away. Nobody who belongs to the basin walks like that—heels slamming down hard and fast, kicking loose chips in frantic, jagged sprays that echo crazily off the leaning fins. When I feel that ragged cadence shiver up through my soles, I stop whatever crack I am caulking and glide sideways toward it, moving swift and quiet along the vertical creases, knees loose, shoulders angled clear of the flint edges.

Once, three swells back, it was just as the Slack was ending and the groundwater was beginning to turn toward a heavy Rise. The bass was already thickening in my ribs. I slipped along a parallel lane until I was two alleys off from the clatter, slid into the wet shadow where a dark shale fin met a crusted bridge, and waited.

He came around the turn twenty paces away—tall, upright in a way that looked terrifyingly exposed, wrapped in sodden, heavy stuff that snagged on every crystal whisker and bled dark water down his shins. His mouth was clamped shut against the noise, teeth clenched tight, which meant the rising drone was hammering straight into the fragile drums of his ears instead of moving softly through his jaw. Every few paces he flung a hand out to steady himself against a wet wall, flinching back too late, leaving bright smears of red iron-scent along the slate crests. Already, two blind crawlers near my knee had shifted half an inch toward the tang of it.

Two lanes ahead of him, in a shallow court, a translucent wader that had been standing motionless on glass-thin legs suddenly folded itself down and vanished into a submerged crack.

My stomach tightened. Somewhere just ahead of his clumsy boots, an over-wedged plate was about to shear.

I did not show myself. Showing yourself to a highland walker only makes them bolt, and running in these alleys is a good way to open your throat on an overhang. Instead, I tapped three quick, flat triplets against the hollow-ribbed gypsum at my elbow with the edge of my tied crawler-shell—*tong, tong-tong*—just sharp enough to slip above the drip-choir, warning any kin within three alleys to brace. Then I moved fast, gliding sideways through a squeeze-gap too narrow for his wide shoulders, circling ahead of him before he could reach the stressed court.

I pressed my wet palm flat over a booming chimney ten paces to his left, choking its low foghorn note down into a sudden, startled hush, and jammed one of my pale shale wedges tight into its mouth. Twenty paces further toward the rim, I muted another. Then a third, dropping back into a vertical shadow as his ragged breathing turned the corner.

He stopped dead right where the court opened—frozen, swaying slightly, hands clutched over his ears. Then the shear happened behind him, half an alley back from where he would have stood. A rifle-crack tore through the mist, ricocheting off a thousand wet faces until the whole floor shuddered under my heels. He dropped to one knee in the milky water, gasping, and when he lifted his head—eyes huge and white and blind in the silver dusk—he felt it.

To the left, back toward the outer ridge, the crushing wall of bass had parted by a hand’s breadth. A thin, merciful seam of quieter air led away from the roar.

He never knew why. He only staggered upright and stumbled toward the relief in his teeth, following the quiet seam I had cut for him, lane after crooked lane, while I drifted parallel two corridors over, silencing one throat ahead of him at a time until the floor began to slope upward beneath his bruised heels and the gray haze thinned toward open sky.

I stayed in the wet shadow of the last tall fin until the clumsy thunder of his boots faded over the lip of the rim. Only then did I step out into the open alley, bend over the silenced chimney, work my calloused fingers under the pale wedge, and lever it free. The wet air rushed back up with a deep, grateful boom that settled straight into my collarbones. I walked back down the lane I had cut, pulling each stone in turn, giving the valley back its notes.

On the last shard he had gripped, there was a bright streak of blood, already thinning under the continuous silver sweat of the rock. I crouched and watched it dissolve into a drop that took ten seconds to swell before falling with a clear, resonant *tong* into the shingle. I felt a strange, quiet ache for him—going back up into that vast, deaf emptiness where the ground never tells you what lies around the next turn, where nothing sings to you at all.

---

When the next long Slack arrives and the bass thins out to a hollow murmur of drips, I finally turn inward.

I do not go all the way to the ancient center. I go to a half-grown court six corridors in—a crooked circle of stacked shards that a small knot of my kin has been tuning for only fifteen years. Its glaze is still young, milky and uneven, smooth along the eastern lip where the standing wave has settled nicely, still rough and chalky on the west where we haven't quite agreed on the pitch of a stubborn fissure behind the overhang.

I step up to the edge of the milky pool, lower both wrists into the cold black water, and wait while the tiny rings travel across to where my sister stands on the opposite rim. She lowers her own wrists in answer, our ripples crossing cleanly in the middle. Then we walk slowly around the curved lip until we are shoulder to shoulder, and I lay my flat, horn-plated palm gently against her broad collarbone. Beneath the cool, damp skin, I feel the steady, unhurried thrum of her breath and bone—no tight rattle of sickness, no stiffness from a bad fall. She rests her hand against mine in return, feeling the chill of the outer haze still clinging to my ribs, and taps two blunt fingernails lightly against the smooth sinter lip between us: *You stayed out past the crest again.*

We duck together into the broad, deeply caulked chamber tucked behind the leaning northern wall, where a grandparent and two children are already sitting cross-legged over a shallow sinter dish of salt-dusted curd. Inside that heavy, velvet dark, away from the weight of twenty miles of singing rock, I pull the clay pellets from my ears and let my voice out—low, breathy, careful not to bruise the quiet.

I tell them which outer bridge dropped during the Rise. I tell them about the ugly intersection I eased with a half-wedge, and the two pockets I re-caulked along the rimward lane. I tell them that a highland walker came down into the mist, cut his hands on the fresh cleavage, and went back up over the ridge before the chord could break him. The older child listens with wide, pale eyes, smearing curd across his knees with his thumb, fascinated by the thought of creatures who live where the sky has no lid.

That is who I am in this world, I think—not a hero, not an exile, not a master of the grand songs. Just someone who belongs to the margin between the roar and the emptiness. Someone who knows that quiet is not the absence of the valley, only a small, deliberate shelter pressed into its seams by hand.

One day, long from now, my knees will lose their quickness on the shifting chips, or my jaw will grow too stiff to read a sour beat before a plate snaps. When that happens, my kin will carry me during the Slack to a narrow trough beneath a pale gypsum curtain, take back my vent-hide wrap and my tied crawler-shells, and lay me straight along the wet shingle with my hands folded over my collarbones and my mouth left slack.

Drop by patient drop, ten seconds to swell and a resonant *tong* as it strikes, the mineral water will soften my edges until I am nothing more than a smooth, pearlescent ridge humming along with the floor. And out near the frayed outer edge of the basin, the wedges I left in the bad corners will have long since fused solid into the chimneys under glossy coats of sinter—holding the peace between two warring stones long after anyone remembers whose calloused hand slipped them into the crack.
