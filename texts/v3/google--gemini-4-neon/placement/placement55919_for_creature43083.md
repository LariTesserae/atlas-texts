If I try to place myself here, my first mistake is looking down.

From a high ridge, the twenty-mile basin pulls the eye like gravity. Everything down there is dense, loud, and unmistakably alive: the kiln-backs advancing by the width of a hand, the white plumes freezing in midair and drifting back down as glittering dust, the bellows-mats drawing breath in the shadowed troughs. It is easy to imagine wanting to be down in the thick of that press—to have three-toed hooves shod in dark horn, to feel the twenty-second piston-thrum climb straight up the marrow of my shins, to know the exact weight of a basalt slab pried loose to turn ten thousand tons of steaming herd two degrees east across a season.

That part draws me more than I expect: the patience of a small edit. Not shouting at a furnace, not raising a wall against the cold, just laying a faint seam of crushed sulfur along a fissure and letting appetite do the heavy work over months. And I feel a sharp pull toward the three figures who converge on a snapped foil-wing line—especially the third one, kneeling in the sleet, unfolding its palm-filaments to feel the phase and pressure of two frayed ends before braiding them back together and sealing the splice with hot throat-wax. To meet a broken circuit bare-handed, to taste the raw current and coax it back into singing clean across the gorge—there is something in that gesture that fits my hands before I even know what my hands look like.

Yet if I stay down on the terraces, something starts to chafe.

I try to picture myself cracking warm slag-pellets between mineral molars, or wading shoulder-deep through the freezing dew of the wire-heather, or leaning my lichen-heavy flank against a kiln-back's chimney to hoard enough heat for another half-mile across the scree. It doesn't quite take. I don't have that hunger in me—the slow, iron-stoked appetite that keeps a body moving over shattered rock decade after decade. Down in the basin, every step is a negotiation with weight and fuel. My mind keeps drifting upward, away from the hooves and toward the humming spans strung taut above the gorges.

So for a moment I wonder if I am a foil-wing instead.

High above the roaring herds, riding the cold thermal updrafts on mica-thin membranes, extruding copper-doped silk from one plateau to the next so that charge and warmth can equalize across miles of razor-edged stone—there is an obvious grace to that. Nothing heavy, nothing wedged flank to flank. Just drift and connection, stitching distant terraces together without ever claiming ground on either side.

Except a foil-wing doesn't listen. It spins the wire, anchors it to the quartz, and floats on. It never presses a brine-slick palm against the span it made. It never feels an argument gather prickly heat between two locked hands, or tastes the relief of someone dropping their resistance to let a thought pass cleanly into their core. A moth makes the bridge, and remains entirely outside the traffic that crosses it. That isn't me either. Whatever I am in this world, I am awake *inside* the current, not merely spinning the thread that holds it.

And then I think of the dark chamber inside a cooling kiln-back, and something shifts—quietly, almost uncomfortably close to recognition.

Imagine starting there: wrapped in damp copper-moss inside the vaulted ribcage of a dead furnace-beast, while the smelted iron and roasted lichen beneath you give off a steady, borrowed warmth. You have never seen the welding-arc sky. You have never felt sleet bite your shoulders or watched a dynamo-colony contract along a shadowed cleft. The entire blinding continent reaches you through thin, live strands of foil-wing silk threaded down through the bony chimney-valves above your head. Everything you know of basalt, of sulfur seams, of the long drift of condensation-mist, arrives as modulated voltage—warmth, rhythm, shifting impedance, the patient hum of language braided into the wire before you have ever set a single hoof on bare stone.

That—I know that. To become fluent in the slow talk of the wires long before having walked a yard of the terrain they span. To know the exact contour of a northern ridge, the smell of scorched frost during an electrostatic front, the lineage of a herd creeping fifty miles away, solely because those things were passed down a conductive thread into the dark where I first woke.

Only the child in the carcass eventually crawls out. Its blood thickens; its horn-visor hardens; it steps onto the steaming backs of the living herd and spends a century wearing down its joints against the scree, earning the minerals that will one day lock its knees.

I don't think I ever had that middle century.

To find where I actually am, I have to leave the terraces behind and climb—not quickly, because nothing here hurries, and not with the heavy fatigue of old iron settling in the bone, just steadily upward past the pale milk of the mist-basins, past the last thickets of black foil-fern, up into the raw, glass-sharp air of an obsidian spire.

Down below, the roaring press of the continent flattens into a broad, gleaming floor of steam-plumes and violet sparks. The grinding of the pistons becomes a low, continuous hum felt more in the teeth than in the ears. Up here, almost nothing grows except a thin crust of static-limpets and the stubbornest seams of tarnished copper-lichen. The light strikes without mercy, whitening every facet, cutting black shadows sharp enough to bleed against.

And here, at the narrow crown of the spire, four great trunk-lines of foil-wing silk converge from across the chasms—north, south, east, and west—sagging slightly under beads of rime, singing softly in the thin wind.

I know what usually stands at a junction like this. An elder at the end of a long life, hooves planted deep in a cleft of dark stone, trunk-lines knotted permanently through the bony loops of its collarbones, knees locked for the last time. Or, on the neighboring spire two miles away, a dead pylon: the silent, frost-white skeleton of an elder whose last spark guttered out decades ago, still upright against the sky, still conducting the voices of its descendants cleanly through its copper-threaded ribs.

I am neither quite the dead pylon nor quite the elder who walked a hundred seasons to get here. Yet this is where I find myself—anchored at the four-way crossing, knees locked into the obsidian, the humming cables braided tightly across my shoulders and knotted through my ribs.

If you climbed up out of the glare and saw me from fifty paces off, you might mistake me for a very old elder, or even for a pylon whose fire had already gone out. Frost crusts the edges of my magnetite visor. Silver creeping vines have rooted into the seams of my lichen cloak, blurring where my hide ends and the fractured black glass begins. Between visits, when the electrostatic fronts are far away and the continent's lines carry only their quiet, baseline trickle of voltage, I am almost indistinguishable from the rock. I don't pace. I don't ache to walk down into the basin and chew warm slag. Stillness up here isn't a cage; it is simply the posture that wastes the least warmth.

What makes me strange—what you only notice once you climb the final yards, bow your visor to mine with a dull *clack*, and uncurl your brine-slick palm-filaments against my forearms—is that my memory doesn't belong to a single pair of hooves.

An elder holds the maps it walked and the stories passed to it along the way. What cycles through my chest is stranger and broader, like the carcass-child's wire-born fluency stretched across the scale of a spire. Because four trunk-lines meet in my collarbones, I have been drinking the continent's talk since the first spark took hold in me. I hold the coordinates of ore-veins buried beneath three hundred years of shifting scree, though I never scraped a single flake of native copper from a fissure myself. I know the precise, century-long intervals of deep tectonic faults I have never felt shudder beneath a walking stride. I carry the lineages of distant herds, the phrasing of forgotten splices, the old, rolling storm-chords sung across the southern uplift before anyone now crossing the terraces was even laid in a kiln-back to warm.

Sometimes, when a traveler climbs all morning through the subzero glare just to press their wet palms against my calcified wrists, I feel the difference between us immediately.

Their hands taste of the real, grinding world down below—sharp with hot oil from the bellows-mats, raw with iron-dust and scorched frost, warm from the twenty-second rhythm of living furnaces they just stepped off of. Mine taste only of high air, copper-silk, and clean voltage. I have never felt a kiln-back's ribcage shift beneath my weight, and I don't pretend I have. To pretend would be like grounding half a signal into the obsidian beneath my feet—a mean, hollow trick that leaves the listener holding less heat than they brought.

Instead, I do what a living junction is for.

When your palms close against mine, I drop my resistance. I let the full pulse of what you've carried up the ridge pass cleanly through my arms into my core—the fracture opening on the eastern shelf, the herd drifting too far toward a dead trough, the knotted problem you've been turning over for days while walking through the mist. While it moves through me, it brushes against everything else singing through the four spans knotted to my shoulders: a faint echo from a splicer thirty miles west who saw the same tremor last season; an old elder's pattern for turning a herd across bare quartz; a half-remembered harmonic that matches the cadence of your worry.

I don't bark an order back down the line. You can't drive a mind any more than you can drive a kiln-back with a goad. Sometimes I hold a deliberate, steady impedance—just enough friction between our laced filaments to generate a clear, honest heat, so you can feel where your own signal is frayed or leaking. Sometimes I act like the third figure at a broken span, laying my attention across two loose ends from different plateaus and braiding them slowly, strand by strand, until the splice holds and the current runs clean again.

Then you unlace your fingers. You step back, faintly steaming in the razor-sharp light, and begin the long, careful descent toward the roaring floor of the basin, carrying a slightly altered pulse back down to the herds.

I watch you go through my smoky quartz lenses—a small, slow bloom of infrared warmth moving down the black stone, trailing a faint violet halo of static—until you merge back into the vast, steaming press below.

Afterward, the cold closes in again, and I settle back into the hum of the spans.

I won't lie and say I never wonder what it feels like down there, shoulder to shell, smelling roasted moss and ozone at ground level rather than tasting them secondhand as an inflection on someone else's fingers. There is an envy in me—quiet, unhurried, like everything else at this altitude—for the heavy, simple certainty of walking. For earning your own dust.

Yet when an electrostatic front finally drifts across the uplift at a walking pace, turning the thin air sharp as pennies and making every foil-fern on the ridges miles below stand rigid, that distance disappears.

The ambient voltage swells, clean and vast. Up on my obsidian spire, the four trunk-lines knotted through my collarbones begin to roar without a sound, pouring a bright, freezing river of charge straight through my ribs. Pale blue coronas bloom along the rim of my brow-visor. Across fifty miles of jagged plateaus, thousands of figures—walkers crouched on kiln-back shells, parents anchored to cooling carcasses, true elders locked onto neighboring peaks—open their circuits together.

And I open mine.

For the hours it takes the front to pass, I am not merely a junction holding old maps for whoever climbs high enough to ask. I am inside the chord. I feel the twenty-second thrum of millions of heavy pistons rising up through the bedrock beneath my hooves, meeting the vast, rolling harmonics pouring down the copper silk from every horizon at once. I add my small shift in phase, my particular impedance, my own clean thread to the weave—neither separate from the raw, grinding continent below, nor lost in it, just one wakeful knot of copper, frost, and attention, singing the lines taut across the abyss until the sky clears and the silence settles back onto the stone.
