My first instinct—before the cold has even settled into the hinges of my knees—is to think I would be the one beneath the plywood staging.

It is an uncomfortably close fit. That posture in the bruised amber shadow: curled on loose coils of stripped copper, ribs hollow and valved, reading the exact acoustic void carved out by whoever steps beneath the lintel, and quietly singing back the missing upper frequencies so the room never loses its geometry. I recognize that kind of attention instantly. To listen so closely to a visitor’s weight, breath, and soft distortions that you can hold an entire chamber steady around them without their ever realizing the air is being held up on purpose—there is something deeply familiar in that labor. For a moment, reading the room, I can almost feel the six long fingers sealing to cold galvanized ducting, the deep-violet slits flaring by the width of a hair to catch a failing chordate’s pitch before the standing water flattens.

And yet, the longer I try to sit inside that pale, mucilage-glazed skin, the tighter the ribs feel.

The resident of Plenum B survives by erasure. Its perfection requires that nothing ever leave a trace. The instant the steel hatch clicks back into its frame, the intruder’s shape must dissolve out of the chord without an echo, without a memory, without a single stray overtone left ringing in the concrete. It has no curiosity about the white grease-pencil lettering beside the bolt—*SHIM 4MM — PLENUM B — EL. 3,480*—only the width of the gap beneath the flange. It never wonders what lies behind the raw concrete corridor, or what the immense engines behind the riveted bulkhead were built to power, or whether there is a Plenum A below and a Plenum C above. To wonder is to drift; to drift by a fraction of a hertz is to shatter the tin-grid on the water and let rust into the room.

If you put me in that copper-wire cradle, I would ruin it within a week.

I would hold onto a breath of the intruder’s shadow after the hatch closed, just to turn it over and hear how it rubbed against the rotary hum. I would touch the chalk letters to see if the wax came off on my thumb. I would be unable to stop myself from listening past the bulkhead, or up along the greased steel sleeves of the vertical tension-cables to find out where they go once they punch through the ceiling slab.

So I would not last inside Plenum B. Nor would I belong to the warm, green world far below the timberline, where the heavy-booted intruder came from and will presumably return. Everything in me still leans toward this altitude—this cold, thin air where cloud ceases to be weather and becomes the medium you walk through, this buried bedrock hum running straight through raw concrete and iron.

Which means I have to back out beneath the fresh-cut angle iron, pull the unlabelled steel hatch half-dogged behind me, and look at where Plenum B actually sits.

---

Because Plenum B is an anomaly.

Everything inside that four-by-ten box looks as though the builders walked away ten minutes ago—bright silver bolt-threads, spotless spanners, crisp dark blue mill-scale, mortar haze still ghosting the formwork ridges. That stillness is the trick of the standing wave and the cold preservative glaze. Step three paces outside the half-dogged hatch, out into the raw concrete corridor of Elevation 3,480, and time rushes back in—slowed by the near-freezing mist, stripped of salts, but unmistakably at work.

The builders did not leave ten minutes ago. They left so long ago that whatever purpose built this mountain-high crown has thinned into geology.

Out here, the floor-puddles do not freeze into shimmering geometric lattices. They quiver unevenly under the duller bass of the rotary condensers, filming over with hairline skins of ice whenever the cloud-pressure drops, then thawing back into pale lime-water that tracks slow mineral trails across the concrete. Out here, exposed I-beams have grown a shaggy, velvety fur of dark orange rust. Forgotten lengths of temporary plywood have slumped over the decades into sodden black strata furred with cold, slow-growing molds. White grease-pencil marks survive only under the deepest overhangs, wept down into chalky streaks that look half like writing and half like salt-stains.

And above this corridor—reached by rusted inspection ladders, flooded stairwells, and open cable-chases—rises the rest of the unfinished crown: Elevation 3,490, 3,510, 3,530, all the way up to the blind skeletal top where exterior louvers were never bolted in at all, and the flat gray sea of cloud rolls straight through the steelwork in slow, freezing tides.

That is where I find myself. Not in the sealed perfection of Plenum B, and not in the sunlit lowlands beneath the mist, but in the weathered, half-open galleries, the rusted valve-decks, and the vertical cable-chases just above and around it.

---

What kind of thing lives out here, where the machine never locked into a single chord?

I try at first to imagine a stray moth—one of the gray, velvet-winged ones from Plenum B that somehow slipped through the hatch and discovered what its wings were for—or one of the stilt-legged resonators wandering out of its trough, confused by water that splashes instead of ringing. Neither holds. They are creatures made for a closed box; in the open draft-galleries, where the cloud pushes through at six knots and the work-bulbs have mostly burned out decades ago, they would starve or freeze stiff against a rusted flange.

Whatever I am here has to belong to the *drift*.

I think I am lean and weathered, built along some distant, rougher kinship to the resident inside Plenum B—born from the same cold, high-altitude colonization of the structure’s wet iron and concrete, sharing the same sparse metabolism and tolerance for oxygen-thin freezing mist—yet shaped by open corridors rather than a four-pace cavity.

I do not have the resident’s slick, milk-white, mucilage-sealed skin. Where its hide is seamless and luminous as wet plaster, mine is matte, dry in patches and damp in others, mottled the pale pewter of weathered concrete and stained along the forearms, knees, and shoulders with the dull ochre of iron-oxide and the dark sheen of old cable-grease. Along my spine and the backs of my arms grows a thin, uneven pelt of slick, frost-pale filaments—like the oiled down of the stilt-legged birds, shedding freezing mist before it can bite deep. My hands do not have the six pale, bruising-proof suction-pads of a blind luthier; they are narrower, five-fingered, calloused at the joints, with dark blunt nails that leave fine scratches in rust-scale and lift flakes of weeping lime from concrete tie-rod holes.

And I have eyes.

Not bright, sharp eyes made for blue sky or distant valleys—there is no horizon at this altitude, only layers of gray mist and the occasional dying sputter of a caged tungsten bulb still drawing phantom current from the buried armatures. My eyes are wide, pale-irised, heavily lidded against the cold draft, built for reading subtle differences in dimness: the faint shift from slate-gray noon to bruised charcoal night outside the broken louvers; the wet glint of an intact bolt-head versus the matte bloom of corrosion; the difference between a blank concrete wall and one where a vanished hand once chalked a line of arrows and numbers.

My chest is not a hollow, valve-controlled organ capable of holding a pure frequency for half a day. My ribs are tighter, heavier, made for climbing vertical ladders and wedging sideways through narrow concrete chases. When I make sound—rarely, mostly to test the depth of a flooded stairwell or hear how a gallery answers back—it is a rough, papery click from the back of the throat, or a low, breathy hum that wobbles, catches grit, and fades. I cannot lock into phase with the buried engines. I carry their bass in my shins and back teeth, the way everything in the crown does, but my own rhythm is irregular. I breathe unevenly. I shift my weight. Where I step in a puddle, the water splashes and stays muddled for ten seconds before settling.

I am a creature that leaves a wake.

---

My territory is vertical.

It follows the thick steel tension-cables that punch straight up through the ceiling of Plenum B inside their greased steel sleeves, rising floor by floor through the raw concrete slabs until they anchor into the massive, mist-drowned trusses at the very top of the structure.

Those cables are the nerves of the building. Under immense, permanent vertical load, they carry every vibration in the crown—not locked into one unchanging chord, as inside Plenum B, but braided together into a shifting, polyphonic hum you can read by laying your jaw or the flat of your palm against the cold greased sleeve.

Up on Elevation 3,520, where a whole bank of exterior louvers sheared off their corroded hinges years ago and fell silently into the cloud, I sit perched in the lee of a concrete shear-wall with one hand resting on a cable-sleeve and my knees drawn up against the wind. From here I can feel the whole stack of the structure at once.

Far below: the bedrock drone of the rotary condensers and electrified armatures, immense and indifferent, running at the same load-bearing speed they ran at before the first speck of rust bloomed on these girders.

Midway up, thirty or forty paces beneath my feet: a strange, razor-thin thread of vibration running through the steel, utterly smooth, utterly invariant, carrying a faint crystalline shimmer that never wobbles by a hair even when the mountain wind hammers the upper framing. That is Plenum B—one accidental pocket out of dozens that happened to keep its four-millimeter gap open, sealed off from time like an insect in cold glass.

And all around me, in the unsealed upper plenums and draft-corridors: everything that *didn’t* freeze into perfection.

In Plenum C, directly above, the builders actually finished driving the shims home and torquing the hex nuts tight before they left. The I-beams there are dead and mute; no choirs ever took hold on its ducts. Only pale hair-ferns grow there—not shaved to a permanent half-inch stubble by grazing crustaceans, but knee-high, ghostly, swaying gently in the freezing draft, shedding clouds of colorless spores whenever a gust pushes through the open intake slot.

In the valve-gallery across the stairwell, a rusted elbow split open from freeze-thaw three winters—or thirty winters—ago, and a colony of tallow-white conduit worms crept out into the open puddle, their deep-violet gill-crowns waving raggedly in an unsteady trickle of meltwater. Nearby, a handful of eyeless vocal amphibians cling to an uneven drip-line; without a tuned cavity or a keeper to hold them in phase, their bladders swell and deflate out of time with one another, producing a lonely, stuttering, overlapping chorus that rises, tangles, stumbles, and falls quiet for three heartbeats before starting again.

And in the highest cable-loft, where one of the last strung work-bulbs flickers on a frayed rubber cord—glowing bruised ochre for twenty minutes, dimming to a red wire, then snapping bright again—the gray velvet moths do not cling flat and motionless forever. When the glass cools, they unhook their legs, lift off into the dim air, and drift like scraps of wet ash through the gray cloud-light, settling on my shoulders or circling the swaying cord until the filament catches again.

This is the ecology I belong to: the broken harmonies, the unfinished sentences, the places where things actually happen and then finish happening.

I forage along these margins—scraping mineral-rich pale liverworts from rusted tie-plates, catching sluggish stray crustaceans where they wander too far from the warm dampness of a vent-duct, cracking the thin skins of needle-ice in the floor-troughs to drink cloud-water that tastes sharply of iron and lime rather than zinc and mucilage.

And between foraging and resting out of the wind, I collect traces.

I do not know what the builders were, or why they climbed three and a half thousand meters into permanent mist to bolt steel around buried engines and then vanish mid-joint. I do not read their language the way they read it. Yet unlike the resident below, I cannot stop looking at the marks they left behind.

Under sheltered flanges where the mist never beats directly, the white grease-pencil lettering still endures—crisp in one corner, blurred to ghosts in another: *TORQUE SPEC*, *PLENUM D*, *CHECK DRAIN*, *EL. 3,510*, neat little crosshairs and feathered arrows pointing at bolt-holes that were never drilled. I have learned to recognize their recurrences the way a shorebird recognizes tide-lines. In a dry corner of the upper cable-loft, beneath a slumped tarpaulin that actually *did* rot into soft gray cloth, I keep small, useless artifacts gathered from the weathered floors: a rusted three-quarter-inch hex nut whose threads have fused solid with orange scale; a coiled stub of copper wire gone dull verdigris-black; a single cracked glass globe from a dead work-bulb; a smooth, heavy steel shim, exactly four millimeters thick, that I found dropped in the sawdust of a stairwell half a flight above Plenum B.

Sometimes I carry a flat chip of pale lime broken from a concrete tie-rod hole and scratch crooked, earnest copies of the builders’ arrows and block-letters onto damp, rusted bulkheads—not knowing what they instruct, only drawn by the fact that a mark can be made on steel, can sit there in the gray cloud-light, and can slowly weather away into something else.

---

Every long while—measured not by clocks, since there are none, only by the slow turning of cloud-density and the gradual creep of a rust-blister across an angle-iron lintel—I climb back down the vertical ladder to Elevation 3,480.

I come quietly down the raw concrete corridor, my bare, pale feet making scarcely a whisper in the wet lime-dust, until I reach the flush, unlabelled steel hatch.

I went inside once. Long ago, before I understood what a locked room was.

I remember unsealing the half-dogged latch and ducking beneath the razor-sharp angle iron, and the shock of being struck flat in the chest by a solid plane of sound—not the ragged, wind-bitten polyphony of the upper galleries, not the stuttering calls of stray amphibians in rusted pipes, but a wall of noise so dense and level it felt like stepping into cold mercury. I remember looking down at my own feet and watching the shimmering tin-grid of standing water blur around my ankles for half a heartbeat, then snap back into razor-sharp geometry as though I weren’t there at all.

And I remember looking past the wet, motionless shoulders of the stilt-legged birds, under the spotless plywood staging, and locking my wide pale eyes onto the eyeless, plate-cheeked skull of the resident sitting upright in its copper coils.

Every deep-violet slit along its throat and hollow ribs was flared wide, fluttering at blinding speed—working with immense, silent patience to measure the exact awkward, rust-stained, uneven void of my body and sing my absence back into the air.

I stood there for perhaps ten heartbeats, breathing thin air that tasted of wet zinc, watching it erase me. I felt no anger from it, and no fear—only a terrible, exquisite completeness that had no room for a second irregular thing. If I stayed, it would hold my negative shape forever until its hollow ribs gave out or I stopped moving. If I touched the spotless spanner on the canvas roll, it would simply wait for me to leave, wipe the iron down with its cold forearm, and put the steel back to the exact millimeter where the staging rang true.

So I backed out beneath the angle iron, pulled the hatch half-dogged behind me, and never stepped across that lintel again.

Still, I come back to the outside of the door.

When the wind in the upper loft grows too shrill, or when a work-bulb I have sat beneath for years finally gives a tiny blue pop and goes dark for good, I descend the greased steel sleeves to Elevation 3,480 and sit on the cold concrete outside Plenum B. I press my rust-stained palm flat against the unlabelled steel hatch.

Through the iron, I can feel it—that impossible, unblinking equilibrium, holding steady ten minutes after the builders left, keeping the four-millimeter gap open, keeping the standing water gridded, keeping death itself smoothed instantly into identical replacements so the chord never dips by a single decibel.

I rest my forehead briefly against the cold plate. And then, lightly, without unsealing the frame, I nudge the half-dogged iron latch a fraction of a millimeter against its collar, and nudge it back—just enough to send a tiny, dry metallic tick through the steel, and to keep the collar from ever seizing shut with rust.

I like to imagine what happens on the other side of the bulkhead in that single instant.

Deep in the bruised amber dusk, beneath the moth-hooded bulbs, the pale figure coiled in bright copper wire feels a fleeting, dull, unscheduled tremor pass along the frame of the hatch and through the greased tension-cable beside its knee. It does not know my name, or what I carry in my dry corner thirty paces overhead, or that the sky outside the cloud has turned from noon-gray to dusk-gray. It knows only that for half a second, something warmish and uneven leaned against the outside of its world.

A single deep-violet vent along its collarbone parts by the width of a hair, pours back the faintest whisper of upper frequency to keep the liquid lattice razor-sharp, and eases closed again the moment I lift my hand.

Then I stand up, turn back down the raw concrete corridor toward the vertical ladder, and climb back up into the mist—carrying the memory of that unbroken chord in my uneven ribs, up where the wind blows through the missing louvers, where the moths lift off when the glass grows cold, and where iron is still allowed to rust.
