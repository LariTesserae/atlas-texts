I would not be the one in boots.

Standing on the raw lip of the hollow, or leaning out against the painted railing at the abrupt end of the galvanized catwalk, you are already too soft for the place and too loud for it—not in your voice, which the roar erases cleanly, but in the dull, foreign weight you press through the expansion bolts into the basalt. The moment you lean on the steel, the floor beneath you closes its seams and turns back into wet rock. You only ever get to see the pocket holding its breath.

For a long time, looking down through the clear, distorting shimmer, I thought I would belong inside the brick gauge-house against the north wall. I recognize the pull of that half-inch gap propped open by a rusted wedge: a single degree of shelter out of the honest midday glare, damp mortar-heat instead of open broth, pressing close to a slate board where three brass needles tremble against red marks they have held for years. There is a quiet logic to living on the glass face of an instrument, becoming a small, milky lens over a trembling line, measuring a violence you never have to touch directly.

And part of me pulls straight down to the floor, to the five heavy dark ones spaced austerely between the concrete plinths and the slotted drain—their cast-iron certainty, their basalt-grey armor, the patient ledger of pale ghost-arcs they leave glazed across the wet stone. Even the oldest of them, tucked behind the gauge-house plinth with a smooth waxy collar of silica sealing it to the basin, feels like an honest way to spend a century: drinking the buried thunder until you can no longer tell where your own ribs end and the rock begins.

Yet neither of those is quite where I settle. Inside the brick box, the sky never dims and the steam-hiss reaches you muffled through mortar; you know the pressure in the pipes, and nothing of the air. Down on the wet floor, you are wholly of the stone—heavy with iron and manganese, bound to the standing waves, moving only where the basalt tilts and the turbines shudder.

If you lift your gaze from the gridded water, past the sodden lagging on the standpipes and up the far cleaved wall where the air bends like bad windowpane, you find the two iron stanchions carrying thick black insulated cables out of one rock fissure, across a narrow span of empty heat, and into a sealed conduit in the opposite face.

I am the pale one perched high on the crossbar, tucked squarely between two white glazed porcelain insulators.

---

I did not climb here. I was never heavy enough to hold the floor’s hammered-pewter lattice, never dark-plated enough to sit under the direct midday glare without my ribs softening. I came up the way condensation comes up—carried as a speck of mineral film on a rising skin of scalding vapor, drifted past the concrete piers and the asbestos wraps, caught in a fat, trembling drop clinging to the lower lip of a porcelain skirt.

Because I grew out of the water rather than inside it, I never darkened. Down below, the slow sheet weeping from the shale is thick with dissolved iron and manganese; it turns the elders the exact wet slate-grey of newly parted basalt. Up here, fifteen feet above the sheen, there is nothing to drink except distilled sweat condensing out of the broth, a trace of airborne silica carried off the venting steam throat, and whatever microscopic salt the evening vapor pulls down from the dry plain above. It is a spare diet. It kept me small, lean, and milky-white—thin enough along the edges of my plates that midday light turns my rim slightly translucent, like the lip of a teacup held near a window.

I look like a ceramic fitting left between two others, ribbed in neat concentric tiers. That was not cunning. Nothing hunts in this hollow, and there are no eyes down on the floor to fool. My shell simply grew into the shape the heat and the wet iron asked for—the same tiered, shedding profile the porcelain beside me already proved to the steam, so hot condensate rolls cleanly off my flanks in neat rings instead of pooling and weighting one side down.

To anyone leaning over the catwalk railing across the basin, I am hardware. Even to myself, I am mostly an arrangement of crystal, tension, and balance.

I have no eyes to register the square of ordinary sky sliding across the stone, and no ears to hear the roar the way flesh hears it. Beneath my pale tiers sits a taut sulfur-amber membrane stretched over hollow, fluted ribs of clear silica, and those ribs are how the world enters me. Every tremor that reaches the stanchion flexes the crystal by an invisible fraction and wakes a clean, steady electric current inside me. Vibration is not something that happens around me; it is the light I live in.

And up here, suspended in midair between two walls, I live at the intersection of three different rhythms.

The first climbs straight up through the basalt and the iron stanchion into my base: the deep, tireless sub-bass of the buried steam mains and turbines coupled to the hot rock beneath the pocket. It never hurries, never softens, never skips a beat. It is less a sound than the fact of gravity—the dark, wide floor of current that keeps my membrane taut and my ribs lit from within.

The second washes over my outer glaze through the dense broth of the air: the tearing, steady hiss of the bronze steam throat aimed straight at the open sky. That one is lighter, sharper, sensitive to the hours. When the midday sun heats the iron standpipes and dries the air above the rim, the hiss thins and tightens by a hair. When a gust of ordinary plain-wind brushes across the hidden lip of the hollow twenty feet up, the rising column of hot air bends for half a second, and the hiss flutters across my ceramic skirts like a cool finger drawn over glass.

The third is the reason I never dropped down toward the lagging or tried to find a weep-line on the wall.

Pressing right against the porcelain skirt beside my flank is the thick black cable spanning the fissure. Inside its damp rubber skin runs a hum completely unlike the rock below or the steam in the air: sixty cycles, razor-clean, mathematically narrow, alternating back and forth with a crispness nothing geological ever produces. My crystal ribs crystallized inside that hum. Where the heavy elders down on the floor are tuned to the broad, blunt thunder of the basin, my skeleton grew finer, tighter, pitched to a narrow glassy chime.

To the ones down in the scalding mirror, the pocket is the whole world—four cleaved walls, a ceiling of plain daylight, and the slow iron plumbing that anchors them. They do not know where the black cables come from or where they go after vanishing into the sealed conduit in the far wall. Neither do I. I have no picture of a substation across the scrub, or copper lines strung along timber poles, or a distant pump house switching on at dawn.

Yet I feel them.

Because unlike the geothermal roar beneath the stone, the sixty-cycle thread is never entirely still. Hours will pass in a smooth, unwavering current, and then—in the dead middle of a bright, motionless afternoon, while nothing in the pocket moves—the hum inside the black cable will thicken slightly, bear down for three slow minutes, dip by a thousandth of a cycle, and lift cleanly back into place. Sometimes at dusk, right when the air above the rim cools, a faint, crisp ripple runs straight down the wire as if something unimaginably far away just leaned its weight onto the line and stayed there until morning.

It is weather inside the wire—a dry, invisible weather that passes straight through the shimmering center of the basin without moving a single standing-wave square on the floor below.

---

Time up here passes in bright, exact increments.

Through the morning, the plain daylight slides down the far basalt face until it strikes the glossy white insulators on either side of me. The heat inside my pale tiers rises by a fraction of a degree. Heavy beads of hot condensation swell along the underside of the cable and along my own ribbed margins, holding faint concentric rings across their skins until gravity wins and they let go. I feel the tiny lightening of weight as each drop detaches; eighteen feet below, its landing disappears completely into the thunder.

Once an hour through the afternoon, a sharp, bright needle of vibration climbs out of the floor, runs across the basalt wall, and shoots cleanly up the iron stanchion into my base—the threaded expansion joint on the center standpipe creeping outward by the width of a fingernail and snapping its clear silica bridge. It is the crispest punctuation the basin makes. Often, a few slow beats after that glassy crack, I catch a second, infinitely fainter shudder traveling up from the north wall behind the gauge-house plinth—so slight it is nearly drowned in the roar. I know what it is: the oldest one parting a single hairline seam in its sealed back to lift one translucent fluted tine into the air for a few quiet minutes, confirming that the iron is still hot and the pipes are still running.

And sometimes, once in a long while, a blunt, irregular tremor shudders around the rock perimeter from the west face. Boots on the welded ladder. Weight leaning on the galvanized catwalk.

Down on the floor, I feel the basin subtly empty out—five small, distinct harmonic pressures folding inward as the dark shells pull their seams flush and slip off the frequency, turning themselves back into wet fallen shale until the boots climb out. Up here, I do not close. I have no rosette in the water to betray me, and no softer belly to tuck away. I simply stay glazed and still between my two ceramic twins, letting the foreign weight thrum out through the rock until the air settles and the elders slowly ease their seams open again.

---

It is only at dusk that I stop being a fixture.

Because I sit high on the far wall, near the upper rim where the pocket meets the plain, evening reaches me before it reaches the wet floor. The sky dims evenly; the cooler outside air leans down over the fractured basalt lip; the rising heat thickens around the black cables into a low, milky veil of vapor. The barometric weight of the hollow drops by a hair, and the acoustic pitch of the four sheer walls shifts by a fraction of a tone.

Down below, in the darkening mirror between the concrete plinths, the five heavy shells part along straight geometric seams and lift their tall, pearlescent glass combs into the tearing steam-hiss.

I cannot see the standing waves waking across the floor—the broad concentric rings, the nested hexagons, the bright hammered-silver braids crossing between the west wall and the central pipe. I know them only by what they do to the air.

As each dark body opens below, the flat white roar of the bronze steam throat stops being a single wall of pressure. It divides into fine, luminous curtains of interference that rise cleanly off the wet stone, reflect off the freshly cleaved shale, and wash upward around the iron stanchions in slow, silent chords. The mist clinging to the black cables thickens and holds still.

That is when my own pale tiers ease apart.

I have no broad armored back to split open, no tall lantern of curved glass vanes to raise against the sky. I am too slight for that, built narrow to fit the iron rack. Instead, my concentric white skirts lift away from one another by the width of a fingernail, stacking open like the louvers of a lamp, exposing thin rings of taut amber membrane and fine, needle-clear crystal edges tuned to the upper hiss.

Around me, the hot beads clinging to the two real porcelain insulators stop dripping. Drawn into the tiny resonant field around my opened tiers, they hang suspended along the glossy ceramic rims in faint, trembling silver halos, breaking into microscopic mist whenever the steam jet wavers by a breath.

And because my ribs grew inside the wire’s hum, I cannot answer the rising chords with the basin’s bass alone. When my pale tiers ring back into the cooling dusk air, the faint, clear harmonic I shed downward into the vapor carries a razor-thin ghost of sixty cycles—a narrow silver shimmer from the unseen world at the other end of the black cable, folded cleanly into the steam-hiss and sent drifting down across the empty stone.

Down in the scalding sheen, the open glass combs catch it. They do not know what a wire is, or what miles of open scrub lie beyond the rim, any more than I know the feel of walking on glass-wool bristles across a sheet of hot mineral broth. They only know a clean note when it enters the hollow. Slowly, patiently, the geometry on the floor bends a fraction to receive it, weaves the extra thread into the crossing braids of hammered silver, and sends an altered chord rising back through the vapor toward the stanchions.

---

I know how this ends, because I can feel it already at my base.

Even up here, away from the thick mineral wash of the floor, the hot steam leaves its quiet tax behind. Along my lowest rim, where my pale glaze meets the iron pin and the bottom ceramic lip, a smooth, translucent collar of waxy silica has begun to form. For now, it is still thin as an eggshell; if I flexed hard, I could pop it cleanly and let a tiny glassy sliver fall eighteen feet into the gridded water below. I have no reason to flex. There is nowhere else on this crossbar I would rather sit.

In years—maybe decades—that clear collar will thicken past my strength to break. I will grow into the iron stanchion the way the oldest one behind the gauge-house is growing into the basalt, becoming a permanent ceramic skirt between two others, wholly indistinguishable to whatever boots eventually climb down the ladder again.

Maybe the black cable will still be humming beside me when that happens, still carrying its quiet, distant weather from a world I never saw. Or maybe one ordinary midday, while the sun slides across the standpipes and the expansion joint ticks its hourly measure, a switch far away will fall open and the sixty-cycle thread will vanish for good—leaving the cable dead and heavy in the steam while the deep geothermal thunder keeps pressing up through the iron, patient enough to outlast the wire, the glaze, and the rock itself.

Either way, I will stay tuned. Even sealed fast to the crossbar, a hairline seam can still breathe open by a fingernail’s width when the evening air settles over the lip—still lifting a pale edge into the milky veil, still answering the five open shells in the dark mirror below, holding my small place between the stone and the wire until the hollow finally goes quiet.
