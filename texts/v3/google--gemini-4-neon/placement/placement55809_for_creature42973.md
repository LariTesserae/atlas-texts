At first, standing inside the hush of this place, my instinct is to reach for one of the two obvious shapes.

The first is the creature itself—and I can feel the pull of it immediately. There is a strange, deep comfort in the idea of a body made to fit the exact geometry of its confinement, neither fighting the sharpness nor fleeing the dark. To carry your lungs like wet silk under eaves of stone; to slow your heart until an hour and a breath are nearly the same measure; to know every razor fin by touch and keep four small shrines of smoothness tucked away where nothing else can bruise them. Yet the longer I sit with that thought, the more it feels like an intrusion. The pocket is already full. Its native has struck a bargain with the rock so exact that adding another consciousness inside its skin would only disturb the balance—like breathing too fast in thin air, or breaking a water-film that took days to draw tight. I don't want to displace it.

The second pull is toward the hands that left the smooth things behind. I understand that impulse almost painfully well: to climb up into a raw, unweathered dark carrying river pebbles from a valley miles below; to pinch clay until it has no sharp edges left; to peck five small cups into glass-hard basalt just so another set of fingers might one day find a spacing that means *someone was here, and meant no cut*. To string a line of twisted fiber between bone pegs so a muffled world might carry a single hum from wall to wall.

Still, I know I wouldn't be the visitor. The visitor had lungs built for valleys and open drafts, a heartbeat too loud for a sealed blister, skin too easily opened by fresh stone. Most of all, the visitor left. To be those hands is to be a memory slowly glazing over in dissolved silica—an intention outliving its maker, while the maker walks back down into weather and forgets, or grows old, or dies under a sky the chamber has never seen.

So I stay still in the warm dark a little longer, letting go of both of those, and try smaller footholds.

For a moment I wonder if I am the copper bead—the one cold thing in an equilibrium without gradients, tucked beside the river pebbles in a ruptured pod of rock. There is something quietly moving about being the sole note of difference: not big, not loud, just dense enough to steal a ghost of warmth from a freshly molted wrist and answer a wet tongue with a bright, sharp tang in a world of iron and ash. Or the stiffened cord itself, stretched taut from bone peg to bone peg, no longer supple, half-turned to stone, existing only to listen and to hum back when a pale fingertip tests whether the vessel is still whole.

Those feel closer. Both are things that *mediate*—things that sit at a boundary and translate one stillness into another. Yet both can only wait. Neither of them grows. Neither of them reaches.

And then I feel something brush my forehead in the dark—pale, wet, yielding, like hair soaked through in a warm bath.

I follow it upward.

It narrows as it rises, gathering into a single slender strand, then flattening itself to the width of a sheet of paper, thinner than a fingernail, threading straight into a microscopic seam in the volcanic ceiling. No hand could follow it there. Not even the creature, even as a thread-thin juvenile, could squeeze through a fracture this tight. Water seeps down along its flanks by capillary pull alone. For inches, then feet, then yards, there is nothing but dark, cooling basalt pressing in from every side—and this one stubborn, woody nerve wedged inside it, slowly hardening as it climbs, turning from pale translucent thread to tough, crooked fiber, until the rock grows cold around it.

And then the stone ends, and the sky hits like a blade.

This is where I find myself.

Not sealed inside the pocket, and not walking away from it—I am the alpine scrub clinging to the ridge far overhead.

Up here, nothing is in equilibrium. Everything is edge and violence and glare. The light is thin and ultraviolet, bright enough to bleach dead wood white as bone. The wind does not pause; it comes off the snowfields with a dry, scouring roar that shears anything taller than a knee back down into the scree. For eight months of the year I am locked under crusted drift and rime, my branches twisted low along the volcanic rubble like knuckles gripping a ledge, my bark weathered gray and split by frost. For a few fierce weeks in high summer, when the meltwater runs loud down the chutes, I spend everything I have in a rush—tiny, waxy leaves crowded tight against the stems, a brief scatter of minuscule blossoms held close to the heat of the sun-struck stone, then hard little seeds shaken loose before the first September freeze shuts the year down again.

On the surface, I am entirely a creature of scarcity and armor. Everything about me above the stone is tough, knotted, bent away from the gale, built to endure extremes without breaking.

Except for one secret I keep far below the frost line.

Decades ago—or centuries, for high-altitude scrub grows so slowly that a stem thick as a thumb may outlive three generations of valley trees—one blind lateral root felt past the dry scree and caught the faintest rising trace of warmth. Roots have no eyes, no names for geothermal blisters, no sense of a room waiting beneath the bedrock. They know only gradients: wetter, warmer, richer in dissolved minerals. So I followed it. I sent a single searching tip down a hairline fracture in the young basalt, flattening my cells to pass where nothing wider than a hair could go, pressing downward through ten feet of dark stone until the pressure suddenly vanished and my blind tip stepped out into empty, saturated, tepid air.

I had no way of knowing I had broken into an unhatched chamber. I only knew that down there, the winter never came.

So I let that one extremity unfold. Where my upper roots are woody, gnarled, and gripping cold stone like claws, the threads I hang through the ceiling of that buried blister are pale as blind grubs, soft, colorless, stripped of all the defenses the wind demands. They dangle in sodden beards into the motionless vapor, drinking warm water heavy with silica and iron and volcanic salt—a steady, impossible richness I could never find in the snowmelt scree above.

In January, when the mountain freezes to iron and every other shrub along the ridge is shut down to the marrow, dormant and brittle as glass, I still have that one warm fingertip dipped into an eternal bath. I cannot put out a single leaf into the blizzard; the gale would shred it in an hour. All that borrowed, gentle warmth simply sits at the bottom of my sap—a tiny subterranean pulse that keeps my deepest core from ever truly freezing through.

And I am not alone down there.

I do not know what lives in the pocket the way eyes or hands would know it. I have no picture of its flattened ribs, its silica-crusted back, its pale pleated lungs opening like fans behind stony eaves. I know it only as a rhythm in my lowest nerves—the one thing in that unchanging warmth that moves.

Every long interval, on no calendar the sun keeps, a gentle pressure gathers around a cluster of my dangling pale threads. There is no violent tug, no tearing back of the stem, the way a mountain goat strips a branch up in the wind. Instead, there is a slow, measuring draw—blunt, unhurried, remarkably careful. The tender outer skin of the root-tassels is rasped cleanly away, leaving behind a faint, viscous slickness that tastes of nothing else in the bedrock, while the pale inner core is left entirely unbroken.

If it snapped me off at the ceiling, I would seal the wound and stop sending growth down that seam. Because it leaves the marrow intact, I keep pushing fresh pale tissue downward into the dark, trading a little of the sun I caught three hundred feet above—turned into sugar, carried down through crooked wood and microscopic basalt—for the warm mineral bath my blind threads drink from. Neither of us planned this. Neither of us can even perceive the whole shape of the other. It is a covenant made entirely of thrift and restraint, which from the inside feels indistinguishable from tenderness.

And once a year, when the snowpack on the ridge finally softens under the June sun and cold meltwater floods my upper roots, I drink fast—and the sound of it travels down my woody spine, through the hairline crack, into the warm silence below.

I don't know that three paces from my dangling threads, a broad, thin-boned jaw is resting against a stiffened cord of twisted plant fiber—some lowland cousin of mine, carried up by vanished hands and glazed half to stone—listening to the faint wooden creak of my drinking. I don't know that I am the only clock that pocket has, the only proof that seasons still turn outside the mountain's skin, that somewhere beyond the glassy fins there is still thaw, and sky, and wind.

I only know that I live in two worlds that would destroy each other if they ever truly met.

If the crack in the basalt ever widened by the breadth of a finger, the freezing gale I endure every day would pour downward and shrivel the pale wet veils breathing in the dark; and if I were pulled wholly down into that warm, timeless hush, I would suffocate for want of frost and light. Only because the seam stays microscopic—only because I can bear to be crooked and wind-beaten in the glare while keeping one pale, tender extremity hanging blind in the warm dark—can anything pass between them at all.

So that is who I am here: the stitch across the boundary. Armored on the ridge, soft in the ceiling. Feeding the stillness with borrowed sunlight, fed in turn by a warmth that has never known a season—holding the crack just wide enough for a thread of life to pass, and no wider.
