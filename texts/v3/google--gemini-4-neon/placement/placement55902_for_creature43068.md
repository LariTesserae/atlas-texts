My first instinct, ducking past the lip of the fissure and feeling the wind cut off like a slammed door, would be to look up.

Anyone new to the crack looks up first. You cannot help it. Three hundred feet overhead, the mountain splits into two blades of newly born slate with a knife-edge of bruised indigo wedged between them, and across that upper throat the drift-weavers’ silver curtains shiver in the shear-gale while the brushless rotors blur into black smoke. For a minute, standing there with my lungs burning from the thinness of it, I would imagine myself up among the Web-Combers, lashed by three redundant chains to the razor lip, combing meteoric grit out of spider-silk in the raw noon violet. Or swinging out over the Winding-Well on a padded pulley with the riggers, chalked fingers flashing in the amber gloom while a bank of mercury-arc rectifiers hums beside my shoulder.

But I know myself better than that. The high wind is magnificent to look at, and it would hollow me out inside a week. I am not built for brute weather or for wrestling three-ton turnbuckles in midair.

If I am honest about where my feet would actually carry me—soft-kneed, one palm pressed lightly to the copper-wrapped rail so the mesh doesn’t jump under my heels—it is downward and inward, to the middle tiers where the sodium lamps meet the cherry-red leak of the annealing kilns.

And I would not be Cleft-born.

That took me a moment to admit, because it is always tempting to claim the deep barrel chest and the effortless balance of the people who grew up suspended. Yet if I picture myself inside this crack, my ribs are still a little too narrow for three miles above the cloud-deck. My blood thickened slowly, over two miserable, nose-bleeding winters, until my nail-beds finally took on that faint plum bruise of the Wedged—and even after years, a fast climb from the Sump to the Winding-Well still makes me stop on a landing, hook an elbow through a spreader-bar, and breathe carefully behind my oiled leather mask until the black spots clear from my eyes.

I would have come up on the blind counterweight sling as a Low-comer. Not a debtor fleeing a magistrate, and not a brawny ironworker chasing rare-metal scrip—something quieter and more impractical. A draftsman, maybe, or a lens-grinder’s apprentice, or a clerk who kept ledgers in some damp coastal custom-house where the ink feathered on wet paper, the iron hinges wept orange rust onto the floor, and the constant roar of cartwheels, surf, and shouting voices made my skull ache by midday.

I remember the lowland world, in other words, and I remember it the way you remember a fever. When the counterweight sling cleared the tin-bright sea of clouds and dragged me into that thin, parched, ink-sky stillness, my nose bled black crusts across my upper lip and my lungs felt full of crushed glass—and yet the moment I ducked past the stone lip and the wind vanished, something tight behind my sternum let go for the first time in my life. Everything here was dry. Everything was leashed. Nobody wasted breath shouting across a room when a flick of two chalked fingers would do. When a senior rigger crossed the catwalk in two swift-feathered strides and pressed a warm, tallow-scented leather palm gently over my mouth the first time I yelped at a passing iron gondola, I didn’t feel shamed. I felt instructed. I looked at the two white knuckle-taps she gave the black phyllite wall, and I understood: *this place has rules that actually match its physics.*

So I stayed. When the shear dropped four months later and the empty sling went back down through the cloud-deck with three weeping Low-comers strapped into its canvas, I stayed clipped to the railing and watched the cables disappear into the bright grey floor.

---

For the first few years, I would have worked in the precision bays off the Winding-Well—sintering piezo-quartz wafers, inspecting hair-thin alloy filaments under hooded sodium lamps, learning the discipline of static the hard way. You only have to forget to ground yourself once, watch a single impatient half-inch arc snap from your fingertip into a morning’s batch of annealed glass capacitors, to become religious about your brass thumb-ring. *Snick* against the strut. *Snick* against a shift-mate’s knuckle before passing a tray. Even alone in my corkboard bunk at night, I catch myself tapping my thumb against the riveted wall before I reach for my tin mug, out of pure courtesy to the dry air.

Because I arrived late to the one-handed cant, I learned it the way adults learn a second language—by watching its skeleton, noticing every tiny shift in how a palm flattens or a chalked index finger hooks. And because I used to keep books in a wet world where paper rotted, the first time I climbed down into the Warm-Bays to deliver a coil of mended catch-net and saw the elders’ Chalk-Ledgers, I could not take my eyes off them.

Thin, hinged tiles of local black phyllite, strung on braided copper rings, etched with steel styluses and rubbed with white talc until the hairline grooves glowed like frost in the cherry-red light of the kiln viewports. A library made out of the mountain itself. Records that could not mildew, could not curl, could not burn.

That is where I would finally settle into what I am.

Not a Bite-Watcher—my molars are not ground flat by decades of clamping seasoned ash against vibrating stone, and I don’t possess their austere, priestly stillness. Not an elder—my knees still take the flex of a braced cross-bridge, and my eyes haven’t clouded yet from mica-glare. I would live in the seam *between* them.

The Wedged don’t care much for grand titles, so they’d probably just call me a **Tile-Runner**, or **the Chalker**, or—when the riggers are teasing me in the rapid moth-fencing of their hand-sign—**the Echo**.

Here is what a shift looks like when it belongs to me.

I start in the Warm-Bays, before the noon sun-blade even touches the upper cleft, kneeling on a swift-feather quilt beside an elder whose hands look like cured horn and whose lap is buried under three sleeping coil-pikas. We ground our thumb-rings—*snick*, a pinprick of blue light between us—and she drops a heavy copper ring of twenty blank phyllite tiles into my palm, along with three old ones pulled from the kiln-wall pegs. In her dry, breath-saving cage-voice, kept close to the collar, she gives me the morning’s question: the east face at Tier Six crept a half-millimeter during last week’s cold snap; nine winters ago, when the same vein stretched, a smaller hairline opened forty feet lower behind the third induction kiln three days later. She wants to know if the lower stone is answering yet.

I clip the copper ring to my belt-frog, check the waxed-hemp tether on my steel stylus, check the short cord on my brass vernier-key and my smoked-mica spectacles, and climb.

Up out of the cherry glow, through the smelling-of-baked-dust warmth of the transformers, into the crisp, ozone-sharp dusk of the open shafts. My boots are resoled with molted soot-swift flight feathers stitched tight into the arch around the brass ground-tag; I make no more sound on the steel mesh than dry powder settling. Around me, the whole furious machine is spinning at full industrial pitch in its uncanny quiet—gondolas shooting silently down guide-rails, stamping-presses cycling twice a second on rubber mounts, grey curtains of ore-dust falling in the Sump far below.

At Tier Six, a crimson shutter drops silently from a bracket forty feet across the gulf.

I don’t swing out into the void—that belongs to the riggers, who are already clipping onto the trolley-lines before the shutter stops nodding. I stay back against the cross-bridge, hooked by my belt-frog to a spreader-bar, and watch them move. Three harnesses gliding across the dim shaft on padded pulleys, unbolting a live rack of singing glass rectifiers without ever breaking its braided umbilical, winching the blazing assembly clear while a second crew eases the giant horizontal turnbuckles half a turn so the black stone can breathe. Beneath them, a dark ribbon of soot-swifts lifts off a trembling busbar in one soundless, wheeling sheet, banks straight through the single harsh blade of violet daylight slicing the upper cavity, and vanishes into the shadows behind the Kiln-Stacks.

When the riggers have cleared the rail and chalked their release-sign, I cross.

A Bite-Watcher is already there, hunched slightly beside the brass vernier bridge, her ash listening-rod clenched between her teeth, eyes half-closed. I wait three paces back, unmoving, until she pulls the copper-shod wood from her mouth. We tap thumb-rings. She does not speak—we are out in the open shaft, where words scrape the throat raw—so her white-chalked fingers snap through four crisp shapes: *Not a slip. Just a stretch. Lower seam’s humming sharp, though—like a tight wire.*

She holds out her rod.

This is the part I never grow used to, no matter how many winters I spend in the crack. I wipe the copper stud on my sleeve, press it gently against the raw, newly split phyllite beside the vernier needle, close my lips around the notched ash, and clamp my molars down.

Instantly, the silent city roars inside my skull.

Through the bone of my jaw, the mute air ceases to matter. I hear the glassy, impossibly fast whine of the magnetic rotors three hundred feet above, conducted straight down through their steel anchor-bolts. I hear the muffled heartbeat of the stamping-presses across the chasm, and the dry, continuous hiss of sieves down in the Sump. Beneath those human rhythms, vast and indifferent, the mountain ticks like cooling cast iron as a cloud-shadow slides across the outer cliff. And there—just where the elder’s nine-year-old tile predicted—is a tiny, taut *tink-tink-tink*, thin as a stretched filament, humming upward from somewhere behind the third kiln.

And underneath even that, so faint you only catch it when you hold your breath and let your jaw go slack, is a steady, dry, wind-drawn note coming down from the blades of the ridge overhead. The copper lashings on the Dry-Perch, singing into the rock where the desiccated dead face out over the tin sea of clouds.

I release the rod, hand it back to the Watcher, and pull my copper ring of tiles from my hip.

Clipped to the rail with one elbow, balancing the thin black slate against my knee, I read the vernier scale through my mica goggles and drag the steel stylus cleanly across the stone: the tier, the bridge number, the fraction of a millimeter, the pitch of the lower hum, the date. Then I rub my chalked thumb over the fresh scratch. The line flashes white and exact against the dark mineral grain.

Before I climb back down, I check the third kiln’s vent-grates. Sure enough, a grey, silent stream of coil-pikas is already pouring single-file out of the lower housing, tiny blue sparks snapping soundlessly between their stiff whiskers and the steel brackets as they abandon the warm brick for a transformer twenty feet higher up. They don’t even wait for the crimson shutter. I scratch one more small symbol in the corner of the tile—the forked-whisker mark the elders use when the beasts agree with the brass needles—and loop the ring back to my belt.

Wait. Look closer at what just happened there, because I think that is where the real answer to *who or what I would be* hides.

At first I thought I was simply choosing a job—a sensible, middle-tier craft for someone who likes quiet rooms, careful records, and high places without wanting to hang from a chain in a stratospheric gale. And I *would* love the domestic texture of it: sitting around a communal heat-grate after shift, scrubbing graphite off my forearms with warm oat-bran and chalk; drinking scalding, tallow-slicked brick-tea vented with a wool-muffled sigh from a bolted brass kettle; letting a bold coil-pika wedge its crackling grey warmth under the hem of my quilted vest while the whole chasm shudders softly against an outside shear-gust and my rubber-bushed hammock absorbs the blow.

I would love the elders’ company in the Warm-Bays—the way my fresh tile gets laid edge-to-edge beside a tile etched before I ever left the lowlands, until the mountain’s slow, geological restlessness stops looking like chaos and starts reading like a long, patient sentence.

Yet the longer I sit with this world, the more I realize that in the cleft, *nothing survives as mere ornament*. Everything is load-bearing, or it gets left below the clouds. The spiders’ silk filters the rotors; the swifts’ frayed feathers silence the riggers’ boots; the brass thumb-rings bleed off charge that would ruin the kilns; even the dead, wrapped in silk and wired into the stone ridge, add one steady reference-note to the rock’s song.

So what am I, really, walking those vertical catwalks with my leashed stylus and my ring of black phyllite?

I am a **hinge of memory**. A translator between speeds.

The riggers live in seconds—ninety heartbeats to unbolt a rectifier before a seam pinches it. The machines live in milliseconds—rotors blurring to smoke, stamping-presses cycling twice a beat. The mountain lives in winters and centuries—millimeters of tectonic creep, quartz veins stretching with the patience of crust. Left alone, those speeds tear each other apart. Someone has to carry the small, exact scratches back and forth between the jaw-aching present of the Bite-Watchers and the kiln-warmed archive of the elders, matching what just shivered under our soles against what shifted nine frosts ago, so the city knows when to ease a turnbuckle and when to unhook an entire tier.

And maybe—if I let the image become even more itself—that is why I wouldn’t mind growing old here, and never once asking for the counterweight sling to carry me back down into the wet, roaring sprawl beneath the clouds.

Because I already know how the arc of my life would travel inside this crack. My knees will eventually stiffen from the constant soft-bent crouch on the mesh walkways. The high corona-glare and quartz grit will haze my sight, and my breath will grow too short for the climb to Tier Six. When that season arrives, I will hand my travel-ring and my steel stylus to some younger runner—maybe another wide-eyed Low-comer whose nose is still healing from its first winter, who arrived terrified of the thin air and discovered, to their astonishment, that they had been looking for this exact quiet all along.

I will move down and inward to the Warm-Bays, settle cross-legged on a swift-feather quilt beside the cherry-red slits of the annealing kilns, and let the pikas treat my lap like a live transformer. By touch alone, my callused fingers will run over the hundreds of thin black stone pages hanging from the copper rings—reading the deep grooves I cut thirty years earlier alongside the ones cut by hands long gone to the ridge—and I will mend the horsehair catch-nets while murmuring in cage-voice to the next runner about which seams have a habit of lying before they split.

And when my thick, iron-dark blood finally slows in the warm dry dark, they will scrub me clean with bran and cedar oil, braid my copper ground-rings into the cuffs of someone still climbing, wrap me tight in drift-weaver silk, and carry me up past the blurred black rotors into the bruised twilight sky.

I like the thought of the Dry-Perch more than I expected to. No wet lowland earth, no swelling or dissolving into mud. Just a clean notch of freshly split black phyllite above the bright tin sea of clouds, lashed taut with copper wire that never rusts—light as seasoned cedar, dusted faintly with quartz-talc, humming one thin, faithful note back down through three hundred feet of stone to whoever presses an ash rod between their teeth and listens.

Still leashed. Still grounded. Still holding my small share of the mountain together.
