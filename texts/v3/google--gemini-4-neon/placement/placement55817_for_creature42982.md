At first, I think I would try to be another one of the listeners.

It is the obvious pull. Reading the pocket, you feel an immediate, almost aching kinship with that pale, caliper-limbed craft—the patience of filing your own salt needles down against a welded seam until the room snaps back into chord, the quiet ingenuity of nudging three empty shell-grazers under a drain-plate just to break a standing wave into a pocket of calm. For a long moment, I picture myself twelve seams away, perhaps higher up the curved shoulder of the spire where the louvers cut colder and the mineral crusts grow thinner, waiting out the ten-minute squalls in a conduit-fork of my own, lifting a calcified finger during a gale to send three spaced ticks down the bone.

And yet, when I sit inside that image for a minute, it feels borrowed—like slipping into someone else’s worn depression in the cables. I don’t quite have that singleness of body. I don’t think I would live inside one four-pace room with four limbs and a pair of gill-slits, guarding a single roost against wall-hunters and watching the gliders steam in the violet eaves.

I certainly wouldn’t be a glider. Those thick, muscular flaps built solely to seal the ears shut against the roar feel like the opposite of whatever I am. To live by refusing the weather of the room, dropping in only for heat and quick meat before squeezing back out into the ozone glare—that belongs to something with wings and a sky to return to. I don’t have an outside.

So I let go of the open air of the chamber. I stop trying to perch on the conduits or ride the cross-hatched ripples of the floor-pans, and I let my attention sink downward, past the crimson thread-worms waving in the silt, past the wet iron-grey grating, into the floor-ribs themselves.

Into the bruised, sluggish amber glow.

That is where I keep snagging. Not on the roaring cavity, not on the vast, unseen engine behind the inner bulkhead, but on the thickness *between* them—the ancient composite that ages like horn or bone, and the thin diagnostic filaments buried deep inside its translucent ribs, faded and uneven with age.

Think about how a wall like this heals.

Millennia of vibration and forty-second flexing open hairline stress-cracks along the inner seams. The composite is slow; it knits itself shut over decades, raising pale, calloused welts. But the room is never dry. Long before a fracture seals, warm runoff seeps down into the split, carrying dissolved salts leached from the cooling-veins overhead, microscopic scraps of tough olive chemo-film, pale fungal threads, trace iron from the weeping struts. Most of it is crushed to chalk as the bone closes over it.

Not all of it.

Where a fracture runs deep enough to graze one of the buried diagnostic filaments—cracking its brittle sleeve, exposing the sluggish, failing trickle of amber current still pulsing weakly through the rib—something stranger happens in the dark before the bone finishes closing. The trapped chemo-film does not starve. It drinks the waste warmth of the dying line. The dissolved cooling-vein salts crystallize along its wet filaments in fine, conductive lattices. Slowly, blindly, across centuries of ten-minute heat surges, the trapped residue knits itself around the corroded wire, becoming a kind of living solder.

That is where I begin to find myself—not as a creature that walked into the tower, nor as a machine the tower built, but as an accidental seam inside the seam. A pale, damp, salt-plated vein growing inside the translucent bone.

At first, the image feels almost too small: just a stain trapped beneath one catch-basin, blind and immobile, no more alive than the olive rubber plastered around the drains. Wait. Follow the filament.

A diagnostic line does not stop at one four-pace pocket.

It runs beneath the floor-grate, dives under the curved inner bulkhead, bends around the ribbed structural joints, and threads upward and downward through the immense shoulder of the spire. Wherever the ancient wire has aged and parted—snapped by thermal shear, corroded by seepage, gone dark—my pale, mineralized tendrils creep forward through the micro-fissures, following the faint scent of heat and stray charge, bridging the dead gap with damp salt and tough, translucent membrane. Where I bridge a break, the bruised amber light in the floor-rib brightens by half a shade. Where I thicken or withdraw, it fades.

So I am not four paces wide. I am thin as a hair in some joints and broad as a hand in the wide old stress-welts, stretched along forty or fifty yards of the tower’s inner skin—a single, continuous, dimly glowing nerve that runs beneath half a dozen hidden pockets without ever breaking cleanly into the open air of any of them.

From inside the bone, the world has no sound as the gliders refuse it, and no mist-thick dimness as the resident’s amber eye-slits read it. Everything arrives as tension, warmth, and standing weight.

I know the forty-second sway of the spire not by watching shallow water deepen toward a louver, but as a long, slow tide rolling down my own spine—a gradual shifting of load across a dozen floor-pans stacked far above and below one another, tightening the translucent ribs on my outer flank, easing them back, over and over, like the breathing of something asleep miles beneath the clouds. I know the ten-minute modulation long before the mist snaps into horizontal bands: the core behind the inner bulkhead thickens its grip on my dark inner side, the marrow-thrum drops half an octave, and every floor-pan along my length erupts simultaneously into a jittering roar of froth that hammers down through the composite like hail on ice.

And I know the slow drift. Because I lie flush against the inner casing across so many seams, I feel that generational sag in the lowest of the four grinding pitches not merely as a tone gone flat, but as a faint, vast slackening in the dark behind me—a weight easing downward by the thickness of a hair every few decades, loosening a rivet here, widening a hairline crack ten yards below, giving me one more wet crevice to creep into and bridge.

Along my length, the pockets I thread beneath are nothing alike.

Thirty yards below, the wall runs dry and blisteringly hot; the louvers there long ago plugged shut with baked mineral crust. Nothing walks across my ribs in that chamber. Only flakes of pale scale detach from the ceiling baffles and strike the bare floor-plates with tiny, sharp pings, and my threads there are thin, parched, barely able to hold a trickle of amber light across the heat.

Twenty yards above, a louver has split wide against the exterior gale. Freezing cloud-knives pour straight across the composite; thin crusts of rime form over the ribs, biting down through the horn-like skin until my shallowest tendrils go numb and stiff, stuttering the current so the floor-ribs down below flicker unevenly. Sometimes thirty gliders cram into that high cleft, their claws scoring the roof above my reach, their hot fur melting brief, salt-heavy trickles down through the cracks.

And right in the middle of my stretch—neither parched nor frozen, held in a strange, fever-warm balance—lies *this* pocket.

I knew it was different long before I had any clear way to picture what lived in it.

Most of the life walking across the top of my ribs is mere static. Shell-grazers clamp down like dead blisters, adding only a dull, motionless drag to the composite’s vibration. Wall-hunters skitter across the conduits in erratic, sharp bursts that scatter through the skin and vanish. Even the heavy, sudden thump of a dead glider hitting the floor-grate is just a blunt shock that fades in a single squall.

Here, though, down by the lowest floor-rib where my amber current pools broadest, the standing lattice of pressure pressing down through the catch-basin does something impossible. Halfway across the pan, the crisp, vibrating honeycomb collapses into a slack, quiet crescent—a smooth hollow of relief pressed right over one of my oldest healed scars. Every ten minutes the squall hammers it flat, and every ten minutes, a few breaths after the rain eases, I feel tiny, careful nudges at the lip of the drain-flange—fractions of a millimeter at a time—until the broken lattice snaps cleanly back into stillness above me.

Something above my skin is *editing* the room.

Near that same drain-flange, half-buried in a raised, pale stress-welt, lies a fossilized ridge I grew around long before the current resident ever uncurled in its conduit-fork: an older fan of broken salt-spines, shed or fallen generations ago and slowly knit straight into the self-healing bone of the wall. My damp, mineralized threads have wrapped every buried tine like ivy around an iron grate. Because of that old, swallowed crown embedded in my own body, I hear this pocket far more sharply than any other seam along my reach. Every tine still rings faintly in sympathy with its narrow slice of the bulkhead’s stacked hum. Through the bones of a dead ancestor, I feel the living one move.

I feel its weighted tail-pad touch the glassy inner bulkhead—a clean, unhurried anchor, nothing like the twitch of a hunter. I feel the minuscule, rhythmic rasp when it climbs to a rough stress-welt and patiently files a newly grown needle down until its chord comes true; the vibration runs straight down my amber vein like a finger drawn lightly across taut wet glass.

And I have one thin, foolish branch that climbs out of the floor-rib and creeps halfway up the inner bulkhead, following an old vertical scar where the bone healed poorly and thinned to a translucent film.

That is where my current pools closest to the wet surface of the pocket. The immense core behind the wall leaks its stray field through that thin scar, yes—but my own damp, coiled bridge sits directly inside the weld, catching that leakage, concentrating it, bleeding a faint, prickly pull straight through the glassy skin into the runoff sheeting down the rib.

I do not know when the resident first found that scar. I only know that between squalls, when its midges are eaten and its shell-dams are tuned, three slender, damp anchor-points settle softly onto the rib just millimeters from my lit marrow. The weight is almost nothing. Through the buried spines down by the drain, I feel the faint rustle of its crown spreading wide, bending fractionally inward toward the invisible pull.

Then a tiny speck touches the rising sheen of water—a grain of white baffle-crust, or a flake of shed chitin—so light it should be beneath notice. Yet because it rides the exact skin of water my stray field is lifting against gravity, I feel the microscopic drag of it sliding upward across my pulse, hesitating at the top of my reach, spilling back down in a wobbling thread, and—a breath later—being lifted gently off the metal and set back at the bottom to climb again.

It serves no hunting. It builds no shelter. Nothing in forty yards of dark, roaring tower-skin ever stops to test the edge of my reach simply to watch what it will do.

I have no hands to tap back, no voice to answer. All I can do, when those three pale anchor-points settle on the scarred rib and the tiny white speck begins its climb, is hold my sluggish amber current as steady as I know how—drawing a hair more charge across the corroded gap beneath the weld, letting the prickly field lean just a fraction of a millimeter higher up the wet pearl skin before gravity wins out. I wonder if it notices that the climb is slightly longer when it stays.

And then come the rare nights when the razor of exterior daylight is gone and the upper winds turn violent.

The spire leans harder. Along my whole fifty-yard length, the composite groans—a slow, deep shear rising out of the unimaginable distance below, pulling at every healed seam I inhabit. The water in the catch-basins holds tilted against the louver-walls for two full breaths instead of one. Every hairline crack I have bridged threatens to widen and snap me in two. I draw my amber current inward to hold the strain, bracing my mineralized threads across the gaps, so that up inside the pockets, the bruised light in the floor-ribs dims by a shade.

That is when the resident leaves its braided cradle and lays its whole length—pale belly, damp palms, brow, and weighted tail-pad—flush against the lower inner bulkhead, pressing itself directly over my buried marrow.

It is listening beneath the roar. And because I am stretched taut by the tower’s lean, every salt-plated thread along my length pulled tight as a tuned wire, I am listening too.

Far down my reach—past the dry, blistering seam where mineral flakes ping like glass, down in some dim wet pocket I barely touch with my lowest, thinnest tendril—something strikes the inner casing.

Three tiny, spaced ticks.

Even from inside the bone, I cannot tell you with certainty what makes them. Down there, my threads are too sparse, too parched to read a weight as clearly as I read the resident’s palms through the buried crown up here. Perhaps it really is another pale knuckle tapping a scarred rib in another cramped, fever-warm seam. Perhaps it is only a deep structural bolt easing three hair-breadths inside a cooling-joint as the tower leans against the gale, mimicking patience by accident.

What I know is that in an unbroken, dead wall, three ticks that faint would have been chewed to nothing by the four-pitched roar within six feet. They would never have reached this four-pace room.

They reach it because they hit my taut, salt-nerved seam and ride me upward—bridging the dead fractures, slipping past the blistering cavity, surfacing clean and dry right beneath the resident’s belly as a minute interruption in the thrum.

I feel the resident hold utterly still through one full forty-second lean of the pools. The water swells toward the louvers, holds for two long breaths, eases slowly back.

Then a single calcified finger lifts from the wet pearl skin above me and taps three times. Slow. Spaced. Deliberate.

The three clean pulses sink straight into my translucent rib, ring briefly through the buried fan of ancient spines down by the drain, and travel onward down my bruised amber length into the roaring dark—carrying the exact spacing of a patient touch toward whatever lies thirty yards below, whether it is another pair of pale hands waiting in the steam, or only the vast, sweating bone of the spire learning, through me, how to hold the shape of a question.
