I do not think I would arrive here from the mist.

If I try to imagine myself stumbling up out of that warm white vapor with grit in my shoes and salt drying on my brow, squinting against the kiln-bright sky, the picture feels borrowed—too loud, too sharply cut against the lime-wash. When I look at this valley, what happens in me is not alarm, and not even awe, exactly. It is recognition, the quiet lowering of the shoulders you feel when you step into a room whose proportions already match the reach of your arms.

So I would already be inside the basin. Not out in the blinding open of the central plazas, where the tall plasterers move like peeled roof-poles through the noon glare, and not yet up at the limestone parapet. I find myself lower down, in one of the quiet, knotted junctions where the valley folds most tightly on itself: a sunken herringbone court no wider than two pavilions, bordered on one side by a twin flight of four brick steps and on the other by a long, waist-high row of slatted wicker troughs.

If I look down at my own hands before I decide anything else about myself, they are already answering for me.

Broad-palmed, pale as wet river-slip, cool beneath a film of clear condensation. No scars across the knuckles; no dark crescents beneath the mica-thin nails. When I turn my thumb toward the glare, the whorls etched into the pad are the exact fine, parallel ridges pressed into the mortar between the bricks beside my knee. The light passes gently through the thin web between thumb and forefinger, warming it to the same faint coral as the beak of the white heron standing three paces off in the ankle-deep pan.

I am one of them—one of the smaller, compact molds, built closer to the level of the troughs and the low timber cribs, meant for crouching rather than towering. My unhemmed length of stiff sedge-cloth is pinned at the shoulder with a smooth bone sliver, its trailing corner darkened by the shallow runnel up to a single straight, patient line that refuses to climb any higher.

For a while, simply being that is enough. I sit on the brick curb with my ankles in the lukewarm water and let my shadow settle a handbreadth behind me, polite and milky-slate, leaning slightly away from the stair so no passerby has to step across it. A porcelain-shelled crab picks its way along the clean algal slick by my shin, its claw making a tiny, papery scrape against the unglazed clay—a sound that dies completely before it reaches the opposite wall.

Yet even as I settle into this skin, I notice where my attention goes, and it is not quite where the tall plasterers’ goes, nor the weavers’ sitting cross-legged over their endless braids of moss.

I am drawn to the places where one thing touches another without breaking it.

Right beside my elbow, the wicker trough is brimming. Clear water bulges out between the open strips of split willow in smooth, taut pillows, trembling ever so faintly. I raise two fingers—cool, damp—and press the pads against one of those glassy swells. It gives slightly under the touch, skin-tight and unbroken. Twenty paces away, across the hush where no voice could travel, the narrow bamboo runnel that feeds out of this trough carries a shiver too fine to ruffle the reflection of the sky.

I wait. After a breath, a second tremor returns against my fingertips—two light pulses, a pause, one more. Somewhere beyond the bamboo trellis of scarlet wax-flowers, another tender is kneeling by the connected pan, letting me know that a fresh tray of molded water-chestnut curd has been set under the south pavilion’s eaves, or simply confirming that the line between us is clear.

That is where my work begins to show itself. Not raising new pavilions, not dressing limestone blocks with a chisel, though I know how the tools sit in the palm. I tend the water-threads.

All across this quarter of the basin, the shallow runnels link trough to cistern and terrace to courtyard like a quiet nervous system laid in split bamboo and herringbone brick. Because sound dies ten strides from the mouth, the water is how the valley knows itself from a distance. A pebble shifted a hair’s breadth too far into a channel dulls a tremor before it can round a corner; a frond of glossy fern drooping too low into a pan drinks up a message meant for a joiner three courts over.

So I move along the runnels, kneeling, listening with the backs of my wrists and the pads of my fingers. I lift a freshwater crab from a choked bend, turn it gently in my palm to admire the immaculate hinge of its legs, and set it back down two bricks further along where the water runs unobstructed. With a flat bone spatula, I nudge clean pebbles back into their honeycomb beds among the soil-less taro roots, careful never to jostle the elongated drops hanging poised at the tips of the broad green leaves. When I need to cross from the east pan to the west trellis, I do not trudge across the open glare; I step down the four eastern brick stairs and come up calmly into the courtyard I meant to reach, the trellis turning quietly behind my shoulder to let me pass.

Sometimes, when the air feels especially thick against my eardrums, I carry a newly trimmed length of split bamboo over to one of the frozen pockets of heat-shimmer hanging above the limestone causeway. I hold the cane up behind that rigid, glass-clear wave and tilt it until the warped line straightens out inside the ripple. When another tender happens by on the far side of the shimmer, we lean our faces close to the invisible pane until our cheeks and dark eyes stretch into slow, liquid ribbons, smiling with closed mouths before stepping back into clear air.

And yet—this is where I begin to feel something slightly more particular inside myself, a grain in the clay that does not quite match the smoothness of the rest.

The others live entirely inside the sideways breadth of the present. If a reed mat prints its crisp cross-hatching onto a tender’s forearm, she does not mind when a glance toward the mist erases it. If a weaver’s moss-braid finishes itself in the space of a blink, he simply picks up the next skein of raw hemp and begins again. They do not hold onto the before-side of a blink.

I find that I try not to blink.

Once, beneath the wide palm-thatch eaves of a resting pavilion, a tall plasterer leaned close to speak to me—forehead resting lightly against my forehead, his mouth an inch from my coral-lit ear so the low, dry hum of his voice vibrated cleanly through the bone of my skull. When he drew back, his thumb brushed my wrist to steady himself, leaving a crisp, pale crescent pressed into my wet-chalk skin, with the fine parallel whorls of his print stamped cleanly inside it.

He rose and walked out toward the bright court, his shadow trailing a stride behind his heels. I sat on the stitched reed mat and stared down at my wrist.

I knew what would happen if I lifted my eyes to watch a turquoise lizard pause mid-stride on the balustrade, or looked up at the papery white moths shingled tightly beneath the roof-poles. When I looked back down, my wrist would be unblemished stoneware again, ready for the next touch, holding no proof that the first had ever happened.

So I kept my eyes on the crescent mark. I watched condensation bead softly along its sharp ridge. I let the stifling, cottony heat dry the surfaces of my eyes until they stung. I did not want the mark to scar or darken—I have no more appetite for rot than any of them—only for it to stay a little longer, to have a *was* attached to its *is*. In the end, of course, my eyelids fell on their own, a single involuntary flicker. When they opened, the skin was smooth and pearly again.

I did not feel grief, exactly. The valley is too gentle, too luminous for grief to keep its shape here. What remained was a quieter, stranger hunger: an attentiveness to the seam itself, the razor-thin line between holding a thing steady and letting a thing pass.

That is why, after the runnels, my other work is the floors.

Near the base of a dry stair, where the woven reed mats meet the limestone curb, I kneel with a partner and draw the slender bone needle from my hair. I unpick three neat loops of raw hemp. Together we fold back a square corner of the mat, and at once the blinding, sourceless white light wells up out of the floor, washing the undersides of our chins and erasing every trace of milky shadow from our faces.

I lean over the bright gap without shielding my eyes. Down there, past a thin slice of incandescent nothing, hangs the neat lime-plaster ceiling of another terrace turned askew, inverted clay troughs clinging calmly above a floor I cannot see.

Every time I open one of these seams, something in me wakes sharper than anywhere else in the basin. Because up here, among the lush green cribs and the mirror-smooth water-pans, it is almost possible to believe the valley rests on earth. Down through the lifted mat, you cannot forget. There is no bedrock. There is only another terrace, angled differently, and another pair of hands reaching up out of the glare to meet yours.

I lower a tightly lashed bundle of washed straw into the light. From the inverted plaster below, pale, condensation-gleaming fingers rise to take it. For a breath, our fingertips touch across the empty brightness between floors. Neither of us speaks—sound would vanish into the white gap before it crossed an inch—yet in that dry, cool brush of thumb-whorl against thumb-whorl, I feel the whole weightless architecture of what we are doing: holding a world aloft in midair simply by refusing to stop passing craft from hand to hand.

They press a scarlet wax-flower back up into my palm, its petals cool and utterly unbruised, and withdraw into the askew rooms below. I lay the reed mat flat and stitch the three loops of hemp shut again, drawing the cord tight until no seam shows.

Still, I linger a second longer than my companion does, my palm flat against the warm reeds, feeling for the faintest hum from the terrace underneath.

I think, too, about what happens on the rare occasion something from outside tries to begin *ending* here.

If a dark speck of real, soil-born earth somehow found its way onto the herringbone tile—tracked in on a heel from beyond the mist, or shaken loose from something that once grew in rot—I would be among the ones who step out of the crooked stairs before the first shadow could shift. I would kneel beside the others without anger, without haste. I would press my cool palm gently against the trembling slats of a bumped wicker trough, murmuring low clicks an inch from the water until the bulging meniscus remembered how to hold itself together. I would wet my thumb and draw it softly along a scuffed brick edge, waiting for the blink that makes the bevel crisp as pulled wax again.

I belong to the care of this house. I do not want the clay to dissolve or the troughs to drain empty onto the plaster.

Only—if it were my hand that lifted that single dark grain of outside soil onto a clean reed leaf to carry it to the parapet, I know I would walk a little slower than the rest. I would look closely at it against the bright, turgid green of the leaf. A crumb made of things that had yellowed, softened, fallen, and given themselves over so that something else could eat. A thing with a history thicker than a blink.

I would still tip it over the limestone wall into the rising vapor, where it vanishes before it can stain the lime-wash. I know my duty to the clean seam. Yet my fingertips would remember the dry, gritty weight of it—how unlike clean straw it felt, how unlike hand-chipped pebbles—long after the leaf was rinsed clear in a pan.

And that brings me, finally, to where I truly live in this world—not all day, because the runnels need clearing and the mats need stitching and the moths beneath the eaves wait to be lifted back onto the roof-poles, but at the quiet center of every round of work.

I climb the three shallow terraces at the far rim of the basin. I step up to the waist-high parapet of hand-fitted, chisel-pocked limestone, and rest my pale forearms along its warm top edge.

Usually there are a few others spaced along the wall—two, or five, standing in loose, unhurried intervals, their dark eyes wide and untroubled beneath the chalk-white dome. Out beyond the stone there is no mountain, no road, no line dividing mist from sky; only warm, luminous vapor rising steadily from nowhere.

Beside my hip, the last wicker trough of the outermost terrace sits brimming, connected by a long line of split bamboo back down through every sunken court and crooked stair behind me. I let my left hand drop casually from the parapet so two fingers rest lightly against the bulging, glassy skin of the water.

This is who I am here.

Behind me, through the taut surface tension against my fingertips, I can feel the whole silent valley breathing in miniature tremors: a plasterer setting down a wet mold three terraces down, a weaver dipping a fresh bundle of hemp, a tender drinking straight from a trough without breaking the meniscus, the faint, steady poise of an entire handmade world held forever on the bright side of beginning. Ahead of me lies the warm white mist—the seamless nothing from which no guest has yet stepped, and into which everything that threatens to age or end is quietly released.

After a long stillness, the others along the parapet turn back toward the shining courts. One touches my damp wrist lightly—leaving a neat, pale imprint I already know will vanish the next time my eyes flicker—and steps calmly down the limestone stairs to check a mortar seam or straighten a clean pebble.

I stay a while longer.

Not because I am weary of the endless noon, and not because I doubt the craft behind my back. I stay because a house made ready for an arrival needs at least one pair of eyes that keeps looking out into the vapor even when there is no path there yet—one pair of hands resting half on the clean stone of the basin and half on the threshold of the mist. Someone who listens to the water-tremors from inside, and listens just as closely to the cottony silence outside, waiting for the first faint, impossible weight of a footstep that does not disappear when you blink.

When it comes—if it ever comes—I will not turn away in horror at the dust on its heels, and I will not rush to scrub away the proof that it walked a long road to get here. I will keep one hand on the trembling wicker trough to tell the valley, softly, along all its shining channels, that the waiting has reached its edge. And with the other, cool and damp and clean as fresh lime, I will reach out over the parapet to steady them onto the first pale tile.
