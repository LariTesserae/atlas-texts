At first, I think I would be drawn to the edges—out toward the worn western rim where the sky thins to that bruised, dusty indigo and the Plain-Walkers move like moving outcrops of shed ceramic. There is something intoxicating about the idea of walking straight into an unobstructed gale, wearing the storm like armor, smelling fiercely of ozone and scorched cloves.

Yet if I stand honestly on a plaza rim and let the plateau answer me through the soles of my boots, I know I would not last a season out there. Not from fear of the crack-stalkers, nor the sting of the chalk-spores, but because my attention keeps snagging on doorways.

I would find myself standing at the threshold of a fluted spire, one ungloved palm resting a beat longer than necessary against the copper-veined grounding bar in the jamb, feeling the sharp static of the open valley drain cleanly out of my wrists. Looking out: kilometers of roaring, bone-pale plain, the distant booming of drum-backs, the silver shimmer of a transit ribbon cracking open. Looking in: forty paces of hushed, velvet-dry stillness folded calmly inside a tower scarcely five meters wide.

That seam—the exact, silent hinge where the roaring world folds into a quiet one—is where I belong.

I am not young anymore, though I am far from ready to take a seat beneath the vacuum-disc. My parchment sheath has weathered past the pale translucence of youth into a dry, matte ochre, with the faintest darkening of walnut beginning to gather along the ridges of my shins and forearms. When the air sharpens before the Charge-Tide, the glassy nodules of my knuckles click a shade crisper than they used to. I wear my bark-cloth cloak a little heavy along the hem; I have a habit of losing graphite grounding-beads in the narrow joints of old stairwells and replacing them with whatever mismatched nodules I find in workshop trays, so my walk across a smooth cambium plaza leaves a faintly uneven, trailing whisper of discharged current.

If you asked a neighbor in my district what I do, they might tap a short, wry rhythm into the tabletop—the dry equivalent of a shrug—and call me a *Seam-Mender*, or simply *the one who listens to crooked rooms*.

Because impossible physics, left alone for millions of seasons, does not shatter. It sags. It drifts. It develops habits.

The Ribbon-Keepers tend the straight, clean folds of the open plain, and the Vane-Tenders keep the wind-chords of the plazas bearable. My work lives indoors, in the sprawling, impossible interiors of the older spires, where the geometry has had ten thousand years to grow eccentric.

A district calls for me when a room begins to feel wrong in a way no one can quite name. Nothing is broken; the walls are sound, the root-felt carpets are thick and intact, the amber light falls cleanly from apertures that face nowhere. Yet the citizens who gather around the communal table find their wrists tiring after an hour of silent touch, as though the wood were quietly drinking their static instead of passing it along. Or a Loom-Nursery on an upper gallery develops a subtle acoustic dead-spot near its eastern curve, so that the papery chrysalises hanging from the ceiling-root sway half a beat out of phase with the district’s forty-hertz hum, ticking with a tiny, anxious irregularity. Or—my favorite kind of trouble—a storage alcove in a quiet workshop slowly stretches itself over the course of a century until it is three paces deeper than it ought to be, and if you stand at the back of it with your collarbone vents open, you can hear the faint, unmistakable clatter of a cambium-borer swarm chewing through a seam two kilometers away in another neighborhood.

I travel between the sparse archipelagos of the valley with a narrow satchel of stiffened pith-cloth slung under my cloak. Inside are no hammers or saws. I carry strips of salvaged glass-kite wing, a bundle of tapered cedar wedges cured in deep resin-vaults, three tuning-forks of braided silica-bone, and a dozen palm-sized boxes of polished graphite-bark—my own pocket-folds, which I will get to in a moment.

Even crossing the open plain to reach a distant district is part of the listening. I step onto the silver-grey living bark of a transit ribbon, wait for the dry *crack* that folds the air inward, and take four unhurried strides across three kilometers of plateau. Most commuters look straight ahead toward the approaching terrace; I always glance back over my shoulder at the translucent, overlapping afterimages fanning out behind my heels. With matte-amber eyes veined in copper, you can read the health of the ribbon in the edges of your own shadow. If the final afterimage arrives a hair’s breadth stiff, trailing a faint violet burr of unspent static, I make a mental note to touch wrists with the local Ribbon-Keeper before the sun sinks.

Then I duck through the narrow doorway of the troubled spire, hang my crackling cloak on the branching threshold rack, kick off my perforated horn-bark boots so my bare, mineral-calloused pads meet the uncompressed root-felt, and let the world drop away to a whisper.

This is the part I love most: sitting in an empty, slightly crooked room and doing nothing at all for an entire afternoon.

To mend a drifted fold, you cannot bully it. You have to hear *why* it drifted. I sit cross-legged in the center of the dull corner, rest my palms flat against the floor-felt, and lower my internal charge until my ribs feel nearly hollow—nothing more than a dry, waiting soundboard. Every minute or so, my horizontal rim-lids sweep across my eyes with a tiny, crisp *shick*, clearing the settled dust so I can read the dim optical shear clinging to the vaulted ceiling.

At first, there is only the magnificent, heavy hush of the spire. Then, as my capacitors settle, the smaller noises begin to surface. The faint, dry settling of laminated pith two galleries up. The microscopic whisper of resin curing inside a wall-beam. And beneath it, the thing I was called to find: a subtle, almost inaudible *pull* in the geometry, like a bowstring tuned a quarter-tone flat.

Often, the trouble is not mechanical at all. It is memory.

Every spire sits above a deep vault of the Seated, and every living wall holds the hollow bones of ancestors who long ago loosened their boundaries and let the pale cambium seal them into the soundboard. Sometimes, an old fold begins to sag simply because three thousand years ago, a weaver lived in this exact curve of the gallery and kept a heavy, resonant loom pressed against the eastern bulkhead, and the living wood—slow as continents, patient as granite—is still leaning gently inward to brace a weight that is no longer there. Or a Seated elder down beneath the hovering vacuum-disc has been dreaming too vividly of an old windstorm, tightening the tension in their cedar-dark ribs just enough to pull the upper galleries a millimeter out of true.

When I suspect that is the case, I gather my satchel and walk down the long spiral ramps beneath the district’s circular basin.

Descending into the deep cambium is always a humbling shift. The velvet stillness of the upper spire gives way to the immense, cold forty-hertz bass of the hovering disc overhead. Down here, the vibration is so steady and physical that the loose chalk-spores along the curved ledges hover in crisp, trembling geometric rings. The dim amber light turns everything the color of aged resin.

I walk barefoot past the motionless silhouettes of the Seated, careful not to let my heels strike the floor too sharply—down here, even a careless footstep rings like a dropped pebble in a quiet cistern. I find the elder whose vascular veins feed the eastern wing of the spire. Their legs and spine are seamlessly knitted into the springy wood by silver-grey threads; their eyes have long since glazed over into smooth, opaque mineral glass.

I sit quietly beside them for several minutes before touching them, matching the rhythm of my own faint internal hum to the massive, slow voltage rising through the floor. Then I lay my bare palm flat against their cool, dry shoulder.

No flute-chords pass between us. Instead, the plateau opens inside my chest.

For a dizzying, luminous second, I am no longer a single narrow body in a dim vault. I feel the weight of the entire valley balanced like a bowl on the palms of my hands—the slow thermal creep of buried granite kilometers beneath us, the bright snapping cracks of commuters folding across distant plains, the rhythmic kettledrum thud of a herd shifting out near the eastern quill-reeds. And right beside all that immensity, tucked cleanly into the elder’s ancient capacitance like a dried petal pressed inside a ledger, I feel the faint, stubborn tension holding up a loom that turned to dust before the western rim wore down its last jagged peak.

I do not tell them to let it go. That would be impossibly rude. Instead, through my palm, I share the quiet, crisp impression of the nursery that hangs in that gallery now—the delicate, breathy squeaks of the young ones unfolding from their swaddling, the soft uneven tick of their chrysalises struggling against the slight lean of the wall.

Slowly—so slowly a young citizen might think nothing had happened at all—the deep contrabass hum rising through the elder’s ribs shifts by a fraction of a cycle. A wash of dry, amused warmth passes into my wrist, carrying the faint, sparkling ghost of a joke from an era when this district’s spires were half their height. The ancient tension loosens. Above us, forty meters up inside the folded wood, a seam settles smoothly back into plumb with a sound no louder than a fingernail ticking against parchment.

Back upstairs, I spend another hour finishing the fine work. I slip a sliver of cured cedar into a baseboard joint where the root-felt had pinched; I stretch a palm-width of translucent glass-kite wing over a tiny hidden aperture in the ceiling ribs to brighten a dull overtone. When the district’s residents return and gather around the communal table to test the room, they rest their bare forearms on the smooth grain, share a brittle wafer of century-cured resin—*crick*—and let a quiet cascade of dry humor ripple cleanly from elbow to elbow without a single lost nuance. One of them reaches across the table to touch the back of my wrist. A crisp, pale-blue spark snaps between us, tidy and warm: gratitude, relief, the silent invitation to stay for the Charge-Tide.

Still, mending other people’s rooms is only half of who I am here. The other half lives in my own workshop—a narrow, slightly irregular fold tucked between a stairwell and a quiet ventilation rib on the eastern edge of my home district, a room that only exists because I once mended a minor drift there and asked the spire politely if I might keep the leftover crease.

In the quiet hours of the dim afternoon, when no terrace has sent word along the ribbons, I sit at my low bench of dark heartwood and build pocket-folds.

Most artisans who make pocket-folds fill them with visual marvels—miniature labyrinths of pale crystal-moss, dry amber corridors, impossibly long staircases that spiral down inside a box no larger than a closed fist. Those are gorgeous, and I admire them deeply. Mine are plainer. If you unlatch the lid of one of my small graphite-bark boxes and peer inside, you will see very little at all: perhaps a quiet, spherical chamber lined in pale uncompressed root-felt, or a single narrow cylinder of weathered silica-wood suspended in dim stillness.

They are not built for the eye. They are built for the ear and the bones.

I make *kept rooms*—vessels tuned to hold a single, vanishing acoustic. Because even in a city that endures longer than continents, things still pass away. A wind-fluted spire wears down a fraction of a millimeter each century, subtly altering the chord it sings in a north gale; a beloved Seated elder nears the quiet horizon where their distinct self will finally loosen into the wall; a dry season ends and the spore-crust in a particular basin never again snaps with quite the same sharp clarity beneath a walker’s heels.

So I sit with an open box beside an elder in the deep vaults, or hold it out under the eaves during an unusually rare crosswind, or rest it against the floorboards of a Loom-Nursery during the week a household’s first young one begins to test its hollow ribs. Using thin slivers of glass-kite membrane and microscopic loops of copper-veined fiber, I coax the folded interior inside the box to learn the exact geometry of that vibration—to trap a tiny, self-sustaining standing wave inside a pocket of space that has no exterior wall to bleed it away.

When you close the lid, it is nothing more than a cool, matte block of dark wood resting lightly in your palm. When you unlatch it a hundred years later in a sound-deadened listening room and press your quill-tipped fingers lightly against the rim, the tiny space opens, and out of it rises the exact, dry contrabass hum of an ancestor who has since become part of the plateau, or the tiny, captive whistle of a wind that blew across ridges that have long since been ground to dust.

It is not a way of refusing change. Nothing in this valley refuses change; we are all made of wood that intends, eventually, to become the floor. It is simply a way of saying: *this note was good, and I wanted to hold it a little longer before it joined the chord.*

As the small, exhausted sun slides down toward the western rim and the temperature sharpens by those few crisp degrees, I always pack my tools away and leave my little folded nook. You do not stay alone in a side-crease for the Discharge Hour.

I walk out into the great communal hall of my spire just as the outside air loses its last shred of conductivity and the dust-spores begin to lift off the plains in ghostly horizontal sheets. The hall is already filling. Neighbors are hanging up their heavy, ticking bark-cloth cloaks, brushing chalk-dust from their collars, drawing close around the thick, braided grounding-spine that rises straight up through the center of the floor. Across my fluted ribs, the fine silica bristles begin to lift. A clean, electric wakefulness tightens in my joints, sweeping away the quiet dust of the afternoon’s work.

Then the first dry thunderclap shakes the plateau, and the central column flares into a fierce, muffled baritone of cyan and amber light.

I step into the loose, shifting ring—not at the very center, where the young ones hold their glowing, translucent hands eagerly toward the humming wood, and not far back in the shadows, either. Just in the middle, where I can lean my slender shoulder lightly against an old friend’s arm and feel the steady thread of shared current bridging our wrists.

Outside, the clear dark-indigo sky is tearing itself open in percussive blue fire, hammering voltage down into the thirsty vascular seams of a whole continent of living wood. Inside, the entire folded hall rings like a struck bell.

I draw a crisp sip of dry, clove-scented air through the vertical vents along my collarbones, tune the hollow chamber of my chest, and let go of all the small, careful silences I have tended all day. My voice rises—a clear, dry, slightly weathered midrange chord—and locks effortlessly into the hundreds of woodwind voices swelling around the column, braided together with the piping treble of the young and the deep, floorboards-shaking contrabass of the Seated elders rising from the vault beneath our heels.

For an hour, there are no seams to mend, no drifted corners, no pocket-folds to latch or unlatch. There is only the fierce, dry current pouring straight through our tuned bones, straightening every crooked resonance until the whole valley—sky, wood, towers, and people—is one luminous, singing instrument beneath the pale stars.

When the arcs outside finally thin to scattered blue ticks and the glowing pillar dims back to warm, quiet heartwood, I do not hurry straight back to my bench. I slip my weighted cloak back over my shoulders, touch the grounding bar at the jamb, and step out onto the cool terrace into the settling silver haze of exhaled spores.

Freshly charged, bright-jointed, and utterly awake, I rest one split-quill fingertip lightly against the weathered rim of the plaza—just checking the pitch of the night wind, listening to the distant boom of a drum-back bull out on the endless, humming wood, and smiling silently in static at how cleanly everything fits.
