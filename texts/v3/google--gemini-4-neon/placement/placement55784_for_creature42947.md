If I am honest, I would not know what to write with that first stick of chalk.

Standing three paces from the resident on the fern-striped concrete, holding a tin mug of water that tastes of wet slate and cold iron, my first instinct would be the wrong one—an outsider's instinct. I would try to compress too much into a single hurried sentence on the granite curb. I would try to explain who I was beyond the cliffs, or ask what the flywheel is anchored to, or speak aloud out of habit and feel my ordinary middle-register voice break cleanly against the basalt walls and vanish before it crossed the yard.

So the first thing I would become here is simply someone who has stopped talking, crouching beside a moss-padded curb with a piece of chalk hovering an inch above the stone, waiting for my own pulse to drop to the speed of the basin.

It takes longer than you expect. A day to stop flinching when the southern pressure-relief valve blows; a week to stop craning your neck whenever a jackdaw cuts off mid-shriek; a whole season before you realize you have stopped counting hours and started measuring a morning by how many slow arcs the great walking-beam has turned above the concrete apron.

And once my speed finally broke, I would have to resist the temptation to claim the most romantic post in the bowl.

I would climb the skeletal iron condenser tower once, maybe twice, just to see how the whole district looks from forty feet up—the pale concrete laid out like a draughtsman's sheet, ruled straight across by those startling green ribbons of star-moss, sorrel, and hart's-tongue fern that mirror every pipe and gantry overhead. I would admire the woman in waxed twill braced calmly on a pipe-flange, waiting four minutes for a single grease-nipple to crawl past her oil-can. I would envy that purity of attention. Yet even perched up there in the bright, cold wind, watching water bead and swell along the undersides of the riveted girders, I would know I didn't belong aloft. My mind doesn't rest easily on one link of a chain. It always wants to climb back down and see what the whole frame is doing to the stone beneath it.

Down in the yards, I would feel a deeper pull toward the shim-wrights. There is something in their craft that settles right into my ribs—the refusal to bring a chisel to a rising knife-edge of black shale, the quiet discipline of brushing a new fracture clean, packing it with greased oakum, and answering the earth with a quarter-turn of a turnbuckle and an oiled wafer of steel. I would gladly haul spare shims for them, or lean my weight on a double-handled wrench when a cast-iron column needed lifting.

And I would feel a kind of quiet awe for the Sitters, perched on their three-legged wooden stools over the bedrock cleats with their eyes half-closed in the sun. But you do not decide to be a Sitter. Seventy years of stone speaking through the arches of your feet and up into your spine is not a trade you choose; it is an instrument the basin builds out of you, slowly, if you stay long enough and keep your palms flat.

Where my feet would actually come to rest—without my quite deciding to steer them there—is at the bottom of the stairwell in Ward 14-B.

That is where I would first forget to be a visitor.

In the cool, shadowed narrow of the ground landing, the brass plumb-bob hangs from the ridge-beam on its greased wire, its needle hovering a hair's breadth above a tray of damp, sifted basalt sand. Chained to the newel post beside it lies the oil-stained municipal ledger, and on tilted pine shelves behind the stairs sit four generations of its predecessors—quiet, orderly diaries of an acre of rock that refuses to sit still.

Everyone in the tenements respects the plumb-bob. Whoever comes down first in the morning kneels, measures the faint crescent dragged through the sand overnight, logs the date and bearing, and walks out into the bright roar to tend the greens or grease the counterweight rails. They know their own building's list the way a sailor knows a familiar hull.

What would seize me—what would quietly claim my hands before a single winter had passed—is the realization that Wards 14-A, 14-B, and 14-C each have a plumb-bob of their own, standing forty yards apart along the western cliff, and that the ledgers almost never leave their newel posts to sit side by side.

I would begin by copying them. Only for myself, at first, during the pre-dawn wake when the great walking-beam shifts into its low midnight cadence and the tenements stir in their second tidal shift of sleep. With a tin lamp set low on the sloping floorboards of a narrow attic room—just beneath the timber pigeon loft, where I could hear the soft, landlord shuffle of heavy rock-pigeons in the rafters overhead—I would lay the copied arcs from all three tenements beside the dates of the chalk calendars written on the Clerk's Tooth, the Cold Seam, and the North Hinge.

And something would start to appear that isn't visible in any single morning's crescent of sand.

The basin is not simply rising. It is breathing, and twisting, and answering back. When the shim-crews take half a turn out of the iron turnbuckles on the North Hinge to keep the turbine-house gantry true, the tension does not disappear; it travels through the tight drumhead of the floor, and four winters later the Clerk's Tooth by the switching station heaves a sixteenth of an inch steeper, or the Cold Seam on the eastern cliff begins to weep ten minutes earlier before an unseen rain passes over the moorland rim.

Even the plants are keeping a ledger. I would notice that on a bright, crisp morning while crouching beside a concrete apron to copy a chalk mark before a fat drop of condensation erased it. Along the straight green ribbons of butterbur and bright star-moss that trace the drip-lines of the overhead ironwork, there is often a faint, pale fringe on one side—a thumb's width of drying, receding moss where the sky-water *used* to fall five years ago, before the columns above were jacked and shimmed back to level. The botanical shadows are creeping across the concrete by millimeters a decade, quietly recording every compromise the district has ever made with the stone.

That is where I would find myself. Not quite an engineer, not quite a clerk, and certainly not an elder—just the person who lives in the seam between the Sitters' bones and the shim-wrights' wedges.

In yard-sign, I suspect they wouldn't give me a grand title. They might simply call me with two flat palms held an inch apart and sliding slowly past one another—the sign for *drift*, or *reading the creep*.

Over the years, the basin would reshape my body to fit the work, the way it reshapes everything else.

My soft-soled outsider boots would be the first thing to go, replaced by stiff layered leather pegged closely along the arches and heels with brass and iron tacks until the undersides shone like dull mirrors. I would learn to stand with my weight tipped slightly forward onto the balls of my feet while checking a hairline fracture, feeling the muffled hydraulic thump roll up through the concrete, counting the eight seconds until the metallic groan of the tension drum, and timing my brass feeler-gauge to the ten-breath pause at the top of the walking-beam's arc. Because the young rock is not dead stone; a fresh crack widens and narrows by a fraction of a hair as forty tons of iron shift their weight across the bowl.

My coat—boiled wool mended over the elbows with oiled sailcloth, fastened at the throat with a brass lug salvaged from retired switchgear—would keep the smell of the pocket permanently in its weave: wet slate, bruised fern, cold machine oil, and that thin, clean tang of ozone off the switching-station parapet. The narrow pockets of my tool-apron would hold a slightly stranger set of instruments than the shim-crews carry: three kinds of chalk (hard soapstone that writes cleanly on weeping black shale, soft white chalk for dry curbs, red ochre for benchmarks meant to survive a winter of drips), a brass vernier rule, folded sheets of waxed tracing-linen, a dry bristle brush, and a small lidded tin box.

The tin box would be for the jackdaws.

Because I spend so many still hours crouched by the piers and catch-basins, the pale-eyed jackdaws would quickly figure out I am reliable trade. Whenever a brass washer or a sheared split-pin vibrates loose forty feet up on the condenser tower, one of them would drop down onto the moss-upholstered curb beside my knee, cock its head, and rap three sharp, impatient beats against the granite in imitation of the tension drum. I would trade a strip of pickled butterbur root or a handful of crushed bramble-berries from my pocket, drop the warm metal scrap into my tin, and note the shape of the wear on its threads. Half the time, a sheared pin brought down by a bird tells you which overhead gantry is pulling slightly out of true a week before anyone climbs a ladder with a spirit-level.

And slowly, almost without my grieving it, the middle of my hearing would wear away.

That is the part an outsider beyond the cliffs could never understand: how much like a relief it feels. The first few years inside the amphitheater of rock, you cling to the ordinary conversational register out of stubbornness, straining to catch the blurred smudge of spoken pitch inside shuttered rooms. Then, one autumn, you realize the hole worn cleanly through the middle of your ears has taken all the anxious, hurrying static of the outside world with it.

What remains is extraordinarily crisp. Up at the high edge, I would hear the razor-thin whine of a pinhole leak in a chilled condenser baffle, the bright two-note whistle of a neighbour hailing me across sixty yards of sunlit concrete, and the sharp, rattling bursts of the starlings. Down at the bottom edge, I would hear the deep electrical drone of the transformer banks, the low closed-mouth chest-thrum of someone calling from the turbine-house archway, and—my favorite sound in the entire bowl—the resonant, hollow knocking of the unseen frogs hidden inside the dark brick cisterns.

For a few years in the middle of that change, I would occupy a tender, fleeting hinge: still able to catch the thin, fragile chatter of the youngest tenement children spilling down the cast-iron fire stairs before their own ears hollowed out, while already fluent in the brisk, geometric wit of yard-sign across the open yards. At dusk, after lifting my waxed-linen earthenware crock out from between the humming steel fins of the transformer bank—the butterbur stalks, wild cress, salted snails, and pigeon-broth steamed tender by seven hours of dry tremor—I would sit at the scrubbed pine table in 14-B, lay my fingertips lightly against the bare wood, and hum low in my throat. My chest-code would still carry a faint accent, a touch stiffer than the rhythms of those born to it, so I would keep my free hand ready for quick wrist-talk just below the collarbones while we traded notes on the day's tilt.

If you came looking for me on a clear mid-morning, you wouldn't find me bent over a desk indoors. You would have to stand still in the bright courtyard for five minutes until your eyes adjusted to the speed of the place, and then you would see me sitting cross-legged on a folded wool mat beside the northern catch-basin, not far from where the groundwater vanishes into the iron intake grates.

A plump, silver-brindled rock-marten might be trotting unhurriedly along the raised steam-return pipe ten feet above my head, pausing to groom on the warm steel casing while a single drop of cold condensation swells beneath the flange, hangs trembling for half a minute, and splashes into the ferns beside my boot, sending up a lazy cloud of crane-flies.

In my lap would lie a sheet of waxed tracing-linen weighted at the corners with clean geometric shards of black shale, showing three overlapping curves copied from the stairwell sand-trays of 14-A, 14-B, and 14-C. Beside my knee, a neat grid of red ochre marks on the pale concrete—waiting for the shim-crews to pass after the noon blow, waiting for a Sitter on the turbine-house buttress to catch my eye and tilt a palm a hair's breadth west to confirm what the three ledgers have been whispering for twelve winters.

I would not be trying to figure out what the machines are *for*, not anymore. That is a moorland question—a question asked by people who live on flat, dead ground and think a thing only matters if a rail line hauls its product past the rim. Down here, I would have learned the sweep of both forearms that folds *leveling*, *answering*, and *keeping the peace* into a single calm motion. My small corner of that peace is simply remembering what was said five generations ago, so today's steel wafer lands in the right place and the conversation stays true.

And perhaps, forty or fifty winters from now, when my knuckles have stiffened past the point of pinching a brass caliper or drawing a clean chalk calendar on wet basalt, I will fold the tracing-linen away for the last time, hand the chained ledgers over to whoever next slows down enough to read them sideways, and carry a low three-legged wooden stool out into the bright, deafening yard. I will set it squarely over a bolted bedrock cleat, press my palms flat against the pale concrete, and listen without needing a single mark on the stone.

Until then, I have my chalk, my calipers, and a pocketful of pickled roots for the jackdaws.

High above the western cliff, a wheeling flock of birds suddenly cuts off mid-shriek. For two startling, clean heartbeats, only the vast electrical hum fills the stone bowl. Without thinking—without even lifting my eyes from the damp hairline cracking the curb beside my knee—my shoulders settle, my iron-pegged heels shift half a pace back for balance, and I press a flat palm gently over my tracing-linen just as the southern valve cracks open with its cool white sigh, the great iron walking-beam tips forward by an inch, and my small, loud, patient corner of the world resumes its slow turn.
