At first, I think I would want to be the thing in the stone.

There is an unmistakable pull to it—the relief of surrendering the whole exhausting business of self-propulsion to the mountain. No lungs to ache in four-thousand-meter air, no hands to stiffen, no fragile boundary to defend against sideways mist and razor basalt. Just heavy internal grains jostling to the beat of a diesel block, corrugated pads breathing only when a relief valve screams, black collars thickening or thinning around a chafed cable until lethal voltage becomes plain nourishment. You don't have to decide anything when you are stitched directly into the fault line. You just keep time with the roar, and when the roar stops, you fold back into dry volcanic glass and wait out the centuries.

Yet the moment I try to step inside that skin, something in me refuses to go blind. I would still be listening for the difference between the live skids and the seized one. I would still want to watch the slow red beacon up on the eastern rim turn the boiling cloud-mist crimson and drop it back into pitch black. The mat has no name for that color, and no sorrow for the cold engine on the far wall. If I claimed to be it, I would be smuggling a witness into something whose whole fierce perfection is that it needs none.

Nor could I stay down in the valley where the aerial tramway dumps its crushed rock. Down there, the air is thick and easy, the rooms have roofs, and this hollow is nothing more than a set of numbers on a wall—kilowatts drawn, fuel burned, pressure held down in the unseen boreholes. You can't know a place from its balance sheet. Down there, the silence would feel brittle and suspicious, the way a torn clump of the hollow's dark matting dries to slag the moment you set it on a quiet table.

So I find myself on the line between the two—the one who climbs up into the dark when the numbers stop making sense.

Not an engineer with a clipboard, and not a naturalist hunting for a new Latin name. Just a solitary line-tender from the deep pump-stations or the transfer shack cut into the opposite ridge, sent up into the throat of the cleft whenever the load curves start doing things the manuals say are impossible.

Because from below, the hollow looks haunted by minor miracles. A high-voltage splice down in wet cloud-forest shouldn't hold a steady, low-grade leakage for eight months without either blowing the breaker or burning clean through its insulation; it shouldn't settle into a warm, stable resistance as if something had tuned the leak by hand. A compressor intake choked with wet mountain bamboo in a forty-knot crosswind shouldn't sag for three seconds, drop its load, and howl cleanly back up to pitch without a single hand touching the grate. Down in the lit rooms, they call it sensor drift, ghost current, bad grounding. They hand me a multimeter, a spool of heavy rubber tape, a wire brush, and a harness, and tell me to go up and scrape the hardware clean.

The first few times, I try.

You drop into the bowl through the mist with a bright headlamp, and within thirty seconds you learn to turn it off, or dial it down to its dimmest red setting. White light in driving cloud only builds a blinding wall two feet in front of your face. You have to learn the hollow through the soles of your boots first—the hard, numbing sub-bass of the drills grinding far beneath the fractured floor—and then through your teeth, your ribs, the smell of hot gear oil and crushed green stems forcing its way past your scarf.

With the headlamp off, the hollow stops looking like ruin and starts reading like a circuit.

I kneel in the angular scree beside a taped junction where the rubber jacket has chafed against a knife-edge of young basalt. Wet fern fronds slap my helmet and flick icy droplets across my visor. A pale blue-violet arc spits out of the damp join, sharp enough to leave the smell of ozone on my tongue—and in the half-second after the flash, right before the dark snaps shut again, I see the tiny, dull filament-orange tips of the black glass spines cooling in the mist.

I reach out with a heavy insulated glove, wire brush in my other hand, intending to tear the crust away and re-wrap the line. My fingers touch the leathery collar cupped tight around the wound. It isn't slimy like rot, and it isn't crumbly like rust. It is springy, strangely heavy, warm from the current it is drinking. When I put the meter leads across the damp outer skin, the draw is clean, steady, almost polite—far cleaner than the hasty field-splice underneath it. If I rip this off, raw freezing mist will hit the bare copper before I can get two turns of tape around it, and the whole borehole line will blow before I'm halfway back to the rim.

I put the wire brush back in my pouch.

That is the moment I stop being a repairman sent from below and start becoming whatever I actually am here.

I move over to the roaring compressor skid, bracing one shoulder against a stunted, iron-hard tree to keep the colliding gale and updraft from knocking me off the wet basalt shelf. Wet fern ribbons are plastered thick across the wire intake grate. The turbocharger's high, oxygen-starved whine begins to falter, dropping half a note. I wait, hand hovering near the mesh. Through my palm I feel the change in vibration ripple across the steel housing—not a mechanical clunk, just a sour shudder—and then a sudden, slick sheen of translucent oil and water beads out through the dark, horsehair-thick mat fused to the wire. The next violent eddy peels the entire sodden blanket of leaves off the grate in one torn sheet and flings it back into the bamboo. The turbocharger screams smoothly back up to pitch.

I don't touch it. I just stand there in the stinging dark, breathing thin diesel-tainted cloud, realizing I am standing inside a pact that didn't wait for human permission. The machines were dropped here blindly to rip rock out of the mountain's spine; the forest tried to choke them; and out of the hair-cracks of the volcanic stone rose a third thing, older than either, that stitched them together into a single trembling body.

Where do I actually sit, when my breath runs short at four thousand meters and the noise starts hollowing out the back of my skull?

Near the far wall, on the cramped shelf beside the old generator skid that seized two winters ago.

It is the only quiet shape in the cleft—not silent, because the newer unit ten feet away still shakes the loose scree down the cliff face, and the pneumatic relief valves still crack like rifle shots every minute or so, throwing scalding white fog through the bamboo. But the dead skid itself is cold. Its iron housing doesn't vibrate under my spine when I lean against it. The dark mat has abandoned it, shrinking into dry, iridescent slag that flakes away in the wind, and wet clubmoss has already begun to soften its sharp steel corners. It is a small, cold lee inside the cauldron.

Sitting there with my knees drawn up against the freezing draft, a gust bends a laden cloud-forest branch double overhead and dumps the dark rainwater out of a bromeliad cup across my sleeve. Three or four tiny, oil-slick black spheres—no bigger than peppercorns—roll across the waterproof fabric, jostling even on my arm from the tremor coming up through the stone, before dropping into the angular basalt beside my boot and vanishing into a wet split.

I don't brush them off. I watch them go.

And slowly, over seasons of climbing back into this throat, my real work takes shape. It is nothing anyone down in the valley pays me for, and nothing I ever write in the maintenance log. Down there, I simply sign off *Nominal. Line stable. No fault found*, keeping the engineers from sending up caustic wash or steam-lances to scour the skids bare.

Up here, I become a quiet hinge—the only creature with hands and eyes that passes through the hollow, doing small, almost invisible favors that belong neither to the mine nor to the forest, only to the narrow, ferocious equilibrium between them.

When frost-heave shifts a fresh, razor-edged shard of basalt up against the belly of an armored power cable, I wedge it loose with a pry bar and kick it down into the undergrowth. A slow rub lets the dark skin build a collar and drink; a clean slice through the copper kills the line for the pumps below and starves the mat above. When a bolted anchor ring begins to work loose in the shuddering rock, I lean all my weight into a wrench until the chain bites tight again, keeping another skid from shaking itself to death and going cold like the one at my back. When a relief valve jams halfway open, bleeding pressure into a useless, continuous hiss instead of the sharp minute-by-minute blast that works the corrugated bellows on the opposite cliff face, I thaw the stem and tap the seat free until the gunshot rhythm returns.

And before I climb out, I always crouch in the wet tangle by the scratched brass faces of the pressure gauges. They are the only steady light down near the floor—tiny, jittering amber pinpricks glowing an inch into the dripping moss. Around every cracked bezel, a thin, glassy ring of the hollow's dark growth has crept right up to the edge of the glass and stopped, pressed against the faint warmth leaking around the threads, shivering in exact sympathy with the trembling needle inside.

I take off one glove, letting the cold bite straight into my knuckle, and wipe the greasy soot-condensation from the center of the glass with my thumb, careful never to scrape the dark ring at the rim. I don't really need the reading anymore; I can already tell the borehole pressure from the pitch in my jawbone. I wipe the glass simply because I like knowing the small amber light is clear in the dark, even if nothing here will look at it until I come back.

I know what I am to this place, and I know what I am not. I am not its guardian; it survived centuries as a coal-black film in frozen volcanic cracks before a single steel housing ever touched these ledges, and it does not need my protection. When the ore seam finally plays out, when the last helicopter lifts nothing out because nothing here is worth the fuel to salvage, when the diesels cough their last soot into the bamboo and seize one by one into cold iron, the whole conductive skin will shrink back into brittle black slag and slip down into the hairline fractures of the basalt to sleep. It will not miss the drills, and it will certainly not miss me.

And down in the valley, when that day comes, I suspect I will feel like a piece of it torn off and left in a dry room.

So while the current still runs, you would find me up here in the roaring dark—wedged against the cold flanks of the dead skid, headlamp off, boots humming with the deep work under the stone, looking up through the narrow black throat of the cleft as the lone red beacon blinks overhead and turns the wet black tassels streaming off its strut a sudden, bruised crimson against the cloud. Just listening to the pitch of the steel, checking the warmth of a taped wound, and leaving the hollow to its fierce, borrowed breath.
