At first, I try to stand on the rim and look down, the way a stranger would—squinting through the silver-blue glare, trying to map the whole two-mile mosaic from above.

It doesn’t hold. Nothing in this valley wants to be looked at from a height, and nobody built to walk in warm boots belongs on these kerbs. If I stayed human here, I would only be a clumsiness: breath fogging the freezing air, grit from some unseen lower slope falling off my heels into the sterile pans, eyes watering shut against a sky like beaten tin. To actually be *in* this place—not passing through it, not measuring it with the wrong senses—I have to give up warmth first, and then hurry, and then almost everything I know about sight.

So I let the glare strip that away, and I listen for where my weight settles.

For a while, I think I might belong up along the rim, among the fog-combs. There is something deeply familiar about that edge—standing canted slightly toward an unseen outside, waiting for whatever drifts over from the lower slopes, catching thin vapor and turning it into something heavy enough to travel. If I were a piece of the valley rather than one of its keepers, I might be one of those hand-split plates of grey slate, darkening from chalk to slick charcoal in the hour after dawn, letting the cold threads slide down my face into a pecked runnel.

Or maybe one of the masons who works up there on moonless, cloud-smothered nights, wedging flint levers beneath a frost-split slab with three others, communicating only through double-taps of a knuckle pressed flat against the foundation block. There is a clean, austere pull to that work—the first translation, where raw weather becomes the valley’s pulse.

But as I stay with the feeling, I notice I am already sliding downhill.

Not rushing—nothing here rushes—just following the slow gravity of the bowl, moving from the glassy-clear upper runnels into the wide honeycomb of the middle benches, where the water takes on the faintest ghost of pale rock-flour. I crouch on a knife-edged kerb beside a knee-deep pan, and for a moment my fingers know the weight of a black basalt hammerstone. I know the patience of that rhythm: *tock*, wait while the crisp spall settles through four inches of cold water, wait while the dust blooms and vanishes, *tock*. I understand the impulse to keep breaking fresh surfaces so the world downstream does not starve on old, smoothed-over stone.

Yet even there, on the open terraces, I feel exposed. Even in fog, the sky above the middle slopes is too wide. My attention keeps pulling inward and downward, toward the place where the horizontal honeycomb runs out of room and turns vertical—where the beehive vaults and stubby windowless towers crowd shoulder to shoulder in a tight, weeping crust, and the alleys narrow to the width of a forearm.

That is where I find myself. Not at the source of the water, and not out in the bright expanses where the noon sun wakes acres of mirrors at once, but down in the cold, dense shadow where all the slopes finally arrive.

---

I am one of the masons—not young anymore, though not yet ready to climb the six wet risers to an open doorway.

I have lived long enough in the lime-heavy water that the valley has begun to write itself onto my back. Along the ridge of my spine and the flat backs of my knees, the smooth charcoal skin no longer sheds its pale glaze cleanly when I climb out into the night air; faint, chalk-white patches stay rough and matte to the touch, like limestone that has sat an inch above the waterline for decades. My shoulders are still narrow enough to slip sideways down the tightest alleys between the corbelled vaults, and my long fingers still grip wet chert without a scuff, though my left wrist carries a slow, granular stiffness when the nights turn sharpest.

I stopped making the long climb up to the fog-combs maybe eighty years ago, maybe a hundred. It isn’t a loss. Up there, you are always sending. Down here, at the perimeter where the lowest terraces meet the dead-level grid of the central cisterns, you become a reader.

During the blinding hours of noon, I lie beneath an overhanging corbelled ledge in one of the deep, slate-blue cisterns. Three feet above my face, the flat surface of the pool holds the terrible white sky like a sheet of hammered metal, never quite touching me. My pale lids are drawn shut. My breathing-flaps stand slightly parted along my ribs, taking the cold oxygen straight out of the stillness, and my heart drops to one slow, heavy turn every few minutes.

From down here, you do not see the day pass; you hear and taste it.

First comes the silence of the freezing morning, so tight the masonry feels locked. Then, as the high sun heats the stone two miles wide, a faint, dry *tick* travels down through the foundation blocks against my spine—the valley expanding by the width of a hair. Hours later, the noon swell reaches the central grid. Not as a splash, never as a current you could point to, just a subtle, unmistakable thickening of pressure against my rib-flaps, and a thread of water sliding down the stairs that is a fraction of a degree warmer and carries the faintest fresh chalk on its tongue.

I read it with my fingertips, resting lightly against the submerged riser beside me.

Every pan on the terraced slopes above leaves a signature in what arrives here. Three days ago, someone high on the eastern bench wedged a new pebble into an overflow notch—I can tell because the afternoon swell from that sector now arrives late, thinner by a hair, and slightly sweeter with dissolved limestone where it has been diverted across a newer block. Another mason on the northern terraces favors iron-veined slate when they mill their morning dust; when their water finally reaches the deep alleys five days later, it carries a cool, dark tang like wet flint struck in the dark.

I have not been within arm’s length of the northern mason in forty years. We have no names, no voices, no reason to gather unless a slab splits on the rim. Yet I know when they are working steadily, when a frost-crack has kept them busy for a week, and—lately, by the way their fresh spalls have grown sparser and their notches left longer to crust over—I know that the lime is taking hold in their joints, too.

We correspond at the speed of evaporation and sediment. A whole season is a single unhurried conversation.

When the sky finally stops hurting—when the silver-blue glare drains to pale grey and then to sharp, freezing indigo—I rise step by submerged step out of the cistern. The water sheets off my gaunt flanks without a sound.

My territory is a tangle of blind alleys around seven central vaults, three of them sealed, two open only by their low square apertures at the waterline, and two still open-doored, waiting for whoever stiffens next. In the purple dusk, the weeping walls around me look like carved salt. I carry a fist-sized black hammerstone in one hand and a flat wedge of grey clay pressed against my damp ribs to keep it from freezing hard.

First, I walk the perimeter kerbs where the last terraced pans feed the central grid. I move low, three limbs always anchored to the crisp stone edges, dipping two ridged fingers past the meniscus of each pool. Here, a V-notch has grown a pale, crystalline lip over the past ten winters, choking the flow to a dry gleam; I do not smash it back to its old width. I check where the excess has begun to creep instead, along an adjacent wall that has stayed matte and pale for half a century, and I tap the stone twice with my knuckles—*click, click*—listening to the vibration run down into the foundations.

Nothing is hollow beneath it. The path is good.

I crouch beside the new line, lift the basalt, and begin to dress a shallow lip into the dry limestone.

*Tock.*

Ten breaths of cold air.

*Tock.*

The sound bounces softly off the flanking beehive towers, indistinguishable from a heavy drop falling inside a dark vault. Fresh white dust settles across the skin of the pool and sinks. I am not imposing a plan on the valley; I am just clearing room for where the water has already decided to go, and leaving a little meal behind so the water doesn't reach the dark seams empty-handed.

Later, when night seals the bowl completely and the first wisps of cold mist begin to crawl over the far rim, I kneel beside one of the low square apertures at the waterline of an older vault.

I do not reach in with a slate scoop tonight. I had a spoonful of the colorless film three weeks ago; my stomach is still quiet, my blood slow and heavy and sufficient. Instead, I press my flat palm against the dressed block just above the square black hole, and wait.

For a long time, nothing moves. Only the faint, hollow *tock* of condensation somewhere deep inside the unlit beehive, and the dry hiss of the fog-combs half a mile uphill beginning to drink.

Then, out of the dark water beneath my hand, something barely the size of a palm slips silently into the opening. Almost invisible—a scrap of cold translucency, fine white cartilage showing beneath a glass-clear skin, oversized fingertips dark-rimmed and tentative. One of the young, hatched perhaps five or six years ago in the thickest seams inside.

It pauses with its wide, lipless mouth just breaking the meniscus, reading the water I just fed from the bench above. Its tiny, flat-tipped fingers find the edge of the wet stone an inch from mine. It does not touch me—we rarely touch—but it rests its hand on the same block, feeling the faint, lingering tremor of my weight, and the slightly warmer, slightly richer thread of limestone flour drifting past the threshold.

Across the narrow alley, barely a forearm’s breadth behind my crouched knees, stands one of the sealed vaults. Six steep stone steps climb its flank and end abruptly against a blank, flush wall of wet corbelled blocks. I helped close that doorway twelve years ago, fitting the unmortared seams with grey clay in total silence after an old mason from the western benches climbed inside and folded its calcified limbs into the dark water.

Tonight, water weeps steadily through those sealed joints, glazing the outer stone in a slow, gleaming sheet that runs down into the kerb-channel and bends—because of a fitted pebble I wedged there three winters ago—straight toward the aperture where the young one is hovering.

It takes thirty years for a sealed vault to finish its quiet work, for the colorless film to reclaim every grain of salt and phosphorus and carry it back out through the seams, leaving nothing inside except clean white bone resting under slate-blue water. Right now, in the pitch dark, that slow dissolution is slipping past the nursery lip, invisible, tasteless to anything that isn’t built to know it, feeding the transparent fingers resting an inch from mine.

I stay crouched there for an hour, motionless as dressed stone, keeping the line between the sealed wall and the open hole clear while the fog thickens overhead.

Eventually, the young one withdraws a fraction of an inch, turns without rippling the surface, and glides back into the black interior. Just before it vanishes, one tiny fingertip brushes the inner lip of the aperture—not a signal, not quite a tap, just the faintest shudder through the rock.

I lift my hammerstone and tuck it back into a submerged corner of the nearest pan, where it looks like any other dark cobble.

Before the eastern rim can turn pale, I press my knuckles once into the wet masonry of the alley wall—a single dry *click*, passed block by block across the central crust to tell whoever is still out on the terraces that the dark is thinning. Then I slip back to my cistern, lower myself down the sunken steps, and let the cold slate-blue water close cleanly over my chalk-rough shoulders.

I have another century in me, maybe two, before the stone in my knees locks too tight to climb the benches. When it does, I already know which open doorway I will take, three alleys over, where the unmortared seams lean slightly toward the newer nursery vaults. I will climb the six wet risers, fold myself into the brimming dark, and wait for two or three narrow shadows to arrive with flat blocks and wet clay.

Until then, I lie beneath the ledge with my milky lids drawn shut, listening through the foundations to the valley’s slow breathing—waiting for the midday sun to warm the terraces by a single degree, waiting for the water to bring me the next three-day sentence from the slopes above.
