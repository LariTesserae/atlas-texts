I would not be one of the tall ones walking upright through the murk. I know that much immediately, before my eyes even adjust to the stutter. There is too much of the mud in me to pass through a herd without drawing steam, too much habit in my knees to stand straight under a cloud deck full of charge. If I tried to walk out over the wet gneiss swells toward the quiet hills beyond the rim, I would make it half a mile past the hum before my chest forgot its count and my legs folded under the weight of unbroken daylight. I would crawl back down the shale before dark, glad for the iron-brine soaking through my hide wraps, glad for the bedrock drone locking back into my molars.

So I am in the press. Not at the dead center of the basin, where the herds pack so tight you can spend three shifts seeing nothing but wet ash-colored flanks and salt-rimmed hooves, and not up on the slick ridges where the seepage starts. I am further west, where the broad silt-pans begin to thin and tilt imperceptibly toward whatever frozen sea waits past the stacked mirages. Out here the channels run a little deeper over smooth black stone; the herds still hammer, still drop their cylinders into the slush, still throw up standing waves every four seconds, but between their flanks there are pockets of supercooled water wide enough to hold still for a beat between shudders.

At first, watching my own hands in the grey flicker, I think I am only an inspector.

That is what my fingers already know how to do before I have a name for myself. I crouch in the churning brine behind the stamping ash-backs, sleeves slick with iron-slime, my weighted copper braid dragging heavy behind my left heel to bleed the squalls to ground. When a warm cylinder of smelted silt drops out of a beast’s underside, I am already stooping for it before the standing wave hits my shins. Inside my glider-hide vest—kept dry beneath the overlapping leather louvers—rides a straight, hollow strip of hanger-bone, cut square and notched along one edge by hands three generations colder than mine.

I lay the bone along the warm dark flank of the piece. I run my thumb across the right-angled grooves before the brine can blur them. Three hundred times a shift I feel the slight slump already beginning in the corners, the shallow serial marks losing their bite, and I flick the piece back into the slush without thinking. Then once—maybe twice in a long cold stretch—the grooves sit flush against the bone notches. Every corner holds crisp as stoneware.

I wipe it on my forearm, wrap it in oiled hide, and tuck it straight against my bare ribs.

I know what happens next. Even kept dry against my skin, the basin’s damp is patient. Within an hour the sharp angles soften like tallow held too long in a fist; the marks forget what they were pressed to say; cold black grit begins to trickle down my stomach and back into the marsh. It feeds nobody. It warms nothing for longer than a few breaths. Yet I cannot stop stooping for the next piece, and when a good one softens against my chest I feel a small, dull ache I have no word for—not grief, exactly, just the sense that a clean thought slipped out of my grip before anyone could hear it.

That is the first shape I find myself in: a keeper of tolerances that refuse to last.

Only I am not crouching alone.

I do not notice her properly until the light dips. A broad band of dark rolls silently across the flats from the west, dropping the flickering sky half a shade, dragging the continent-wide hum down a half-step in my jaw. The bellows-herds stumble from a crisp hammer into a heavy, labored thud. Ahead of me, a spray of brine kicked up by a ratcheting worm-loop hangs mid-air in glassy cold beads while the ripples beneath it flatten out and vanish.

The crew stops dead in the shallows. Hoods come back. Fifteen seconds of loose air, and everyone crowds in to pour out a week of hoarse, overlapping talk before the voltage catches again.

And six paces to my left, standing ankle-deep in a quiet eddy with her head bowed over dark water, is the other half of me.

She is stouter than I am, steadier in the knees, her eyes gone pale and milky from forty winters of sixty-cycle light. We wear the same patched glider-hide, cut from the same fall; we answer to the same clipped name when the crew taps our shoulders; she carries the single bone knife at her belt, and I carry the bone gauge inside my vest. When she lifts her face in the dim to speak to our crew’s younger runners, I feel the words gather in my own throat a fraction of a beat behind hers—and sometimes, when the brownout runs deep enough to loosen the seam between cause and effect, my mouth finishes her sentence before she has drawn breath to start it.

That is what I am. I am the slip.

I was the second wail the runners went splashing out into the fog to find, forty years back, lying naked on a flat tongue of wet shale two hundred yards downwind of the dying bellows-flank where my mother timed her labor to the steam-vents. My cord was already severed clean as if by cold iron. They made it to me before a glider dropped out of the vapor or the freezing brine filled my lungs—barely. Ever since, my registration has been a hair’s breadth off hers. I blink a fraction of a second late. When a stray vent catches her knuckles, a pale ridge of scald rises across my own hand half a breath before the next voltage dip evens the ledger. When the light sags, the young ones look away from my left side, because a second, faint outline of my ribs drifts half an inch out into the mist and shivers there until the whine climbs back up.

Together, the two of us are the crew’s puddle-reader.

She is the one they trust when the sleet turns sideways and the herds begin to shift. She walks slowly out across the broad silt-pans, milky eyes fixed on the dark water between her boots, reading the soaring phantom girders and the iron stanchions mirrored beneath the rust-slime. She reads what holds. Where a heavy reflected column meets the mud, she plants her heel, tugs the tendon loop at her collar, and sits down in the slush with her knees drawn up on smooth river-stones. The rest of the crew files in around her, wedging back-to-back inside that square yard of empty air where the sleet thins and the bedrock roar softens just enough to let them sleep.

I sit at the outer edge of the footprint, shoulder pressed hard against hers like a nested shell, looking down into the same puddle and seeing it slightly off-true.

Because I was stamped out of phase, the reflection never sits quite square for me. Where she sees a solid rusted beam stretching cleanly across the dark water, I see the seam where the beam shivers against the mud. I see the double-edge on the hooded yellow lamps swaying in an unfelt draft. I look up into the flat grey sheet of trapped steam two hundred feet overhead—where the others see only pale wet rags hanging in empty air—and down in the water I can read the tension in the hooked calcified toes of the blind hangers clinging upside down to the phantom rafters. Long before a hard brownout hits, I can feel in the roots of my back teeth when one of those upper girders is going to thin out and drop a hanger straight through the fog. Twice in the last three winters our crew reached a fall before the benthic pressers or the gliders because I was already running through the knee-deep channel while the thing was still gripping nothing two hundred feet up.

The crew thinks I am strange even for a doubled elder. They leave the reading of shelters to my steadier half, and they leave the deep burial pools to me.

Out here on the western swells, where the worn shale holds basins of glass-clear supercooled water beneath thin hexagonal skins of ice, we bring our dead when they grow too calcified to keep the four-second step. We weight them with cooled iron-silt cylinders—passed and rejected alike, because the dead do not care whose notches held—and lower them face-up onto the polished gneiss. Then the others step back into the fog, and I kneel alone at the rim while my other half stands guard six paces upwind, watching the sky for voltage squalls.

I look down through the clear brine. Down at the bottom, plated slowly in pale iron-slime, our old ones lie motionless with their faces turned upward; and laid cleanly over them in reflection are the high iron catwalks and the swaying yellow lamps of the ceiling that isn’t there. It looks, every time, as if they have finished their shift and climbed up off the mud to rest along the beams. I check the registration of the water over their chests. I make sure the ice forming at the rim isn’t pulling the reflection crooked. It is another useless courtesy, like wiping a warm cylinder on my sleeve, and I cannot stop doing it either.

Sometimes, when a brownout rolls in while we are near those still western pools, and I happen to have one of the rare clean cylinders still firm against my ribs, my other half comes to stand directly behind my shoulder. Neither of us speaks; the sag is only fifteen seconds long, and we do not need words between the two of us anymore—our pulses carry the same slight hitch, our breath drops out in one grey plume.

I pull the wrapped cylinder from inside my vest, peel back the oiled leather, and hold the warm notched metal face-down an inch above the dark mirror of the pool.

My steadier half watches the bottom—the iron girders, the resting shapes of our dead, the long perspective of empty gantries dropping away beneath the stone. I watch the gap between the real mud and the reflected iron, where my own offset eyes see best.

Most times, nothing happens. The cylinder steams faintly in the dim air; the ripples from my breathing bend the yellow lamps; the voltage catches with a high whine, and the sharp corners of the piece are already beginning to slump under my thumb.

Once in a great while—maybe three times in all my winters—the dark band deepens just right. Down in the supercooled water, the swaying hooded lamps steady by a hair’s breadth, burning a shade warmer against the iron. And somewhere far out along the phantom catwalk, up in the dry, empty space two hundred feet above our heads or two hundred feet beneath our boots, something gives a single, crisp *tick*.

Not a voice. Not an answer. Just a counter turning over by one on the other side of a seam nobody can cross.

Whenever it happens, my other half lets out a short, hoarse laugh—except the sound comes out of my throat half a beat before her chest shakes, as if the valley tossed the joke back from the next channel over. Then the light snaps bright again, the sixty-cycle drone locks back into our jawbones, and the cylinder in my palm softens into wet black sand and trickles through my fingers into the pool.

That would be enough of a life. I think I could spend the rest of my shifts like that—half of a watcher, half of a measurer, wedged beside myself in the lee of invisible columns until my knees lock up and the young ones weight me down with cooling mud on the polished gneiss.

Except that out here on the western edge, where distant bellows-herds stack above themselves in shimmering vertical mirages, we cross paths with the tall ones.

Not often. Twice in a bad winter, maybe once in a mild one. I always feel them coming before my steadier half sees them, because my left ribcage stops its faint ghosting and goes strangely tight, as though whatever cosmic press stamped me twice is suddenly straining to pull both halves back into one clean mark.

The last time, a brownout had just begun to thin out to the east. The herd beside us had clamped its steam-vents shut in uncanny silence, parting half a step to make a lane through the slush. My other half dropped her eyes immediately and pulled her leather hood low, grounding herself in the mud. I stayed crouched where I was, holding a freshly dropped cylinder in my bare palm—a good one, clean-notched, still hot enough to hiss against the drizzle.

The tall figure paused right beside my knee.

It had no grounding-tail, no slotted hide, no crust of rust-slime in the creases of its skin. As the tail end of the brownout passed over it, its shoulders went thin and watery for two seconds, trembling like a reflection stirred by wind, then sharpened back into solid flesh as the sixty-cycle whine climbed back up. Its chest did not wait for the four-second drop.

It looked down at the warm dark cylinder resting across my callused palm. For a beat I held the hanger-bone gauge up beside it—not proudly, not even hoping, just showing the fit the way you show a clean seam to someone who might know what the seam was built for.

It gave a single, crisp shake of the head.

There was no anger in it, and no pity either. Just a flat refusal of the whole arithmetic—the bone gauge, the serial marks, the faint *tick* in the water, the endless shift with no customer. Then it stepped past me and walked on toward the stacked, humming horizon, lightning arcing silently across its bare shoulders without turning its head.

Behind it lay a neat line of narrow footprints in the black silt, filling immediately with clear seepage.

My other half tapped my shoulder twice with the flat of her palm—the sign to move before the herd closed its lane again and the hooves came down. I ignored her for three seconds. I leaned forward on one knee, keeping my head low below the ash-colored backs, and looked straight down into the nearest print before the bedrock vibration could shake its walls smooth.

There were no girders in it. No rusted catwalks, no hooded yellow lamps swaying in an unfelt draft, no blind hangers rocking beneath an invisible roof. Only a flat, unbroken, piercing blue—so bright and empty it made my milky-edged eyes water, a sky with no sixty-cycle flicker and nothing hanging over it at all.

Then the four-second strike hit. *THUMP.* Standing waves slapped across the print, the silt slumped inward, and the blue broke into grey brine and rust-slime.

I pushed myself upright—not all the way, never above the line of the backs—and hurried to catch up with my other half where she stood waiting in the fog. When the next brownout rolled through ten minutes later, loosening our throats for twelve dim seconds, she didn’t even have to ask.

*There was nothing built over it,* I told her, my voice hoarse and overlapping hers as she opened her mouth to ask what I had seen. *No rafters. Just open water all the way up.*

She frowned, blinking a fraction of a second ahead of me, and pressed her salt-chapped hand briefly against my cold cheek before the voltage whine returned and sealed our mouths. Neither of us looked back toward the western horizon. We know what we are made of; our lungs need the four-stroke hammer, and our bones would shatter in a quiet sky.

Still, when the herd beside us inhales and the dull orange glow blooms behind three thousand translucent ribs, I reach inside my vest for the straight, right-angled bone. I wait for the hooves to drop. I stoop into the freezing brine for the next warm piece—not because I believe the mark will last, and not because I think anyone on the catwalks is keeping score, but because I am the offset half, the one who lives in the slip between two registrations, and somebody down here in the mud has to notice both: the clean edge before it melts, and the three seconds of empty sky before the vibration closes it over.
