If I entered this room, I would not know my shape right away. I would know the floor first—not beneath my feet, because nothing here truly has weight, but inside my jaw: that low, oiled *thrum-drag* climbing through the water twice every three seconds, steady as a long breath taken by something buried miles down in the dark.

Then I would hear the thin silver wire of sound running high above it, where the brass bleed-valve lets out its thread of gas into the emptiness beyond the dome. Between the deep turn of the shaft and that high, piercing whistle lies an open shelf of water where smaller noises can breathe. That is where my attention would settle.

For a little while, I might wonder if I belonged down on the smooth iron floor among the valve-crabs, picking bright flecks of shed brass and pale lime from the seams and gluing them to my own back until I looked like a piece of the machinery come loose. There is something deeply familiar in that—making a shell out of whatever the room has already discarded. Or I might drift near the humming cables with the needle-thin filament-fish, letting an alternating current tidy my thoughts into a neat, shimmering ribbon.

But neither of those would hold me for long. The floor is too busy with its own small margins, and the cables are too narrow a path. I would keep looking up into the thirty paces of open, amber-lit volume—drawn to the wide lanes where nothing touches you except light and the slow clockwise turn of the eddy.

That is where I find my hands: pale as skimmed milk, long-fingered, darkened at the tips from decades of gripping old iron.

I am one of the three middle ones. Not the thin-plated youngster who still throws itself into the freezing four-minute jet just for the thrill of tumbling head-over-heels through a snowstorm of bubbles, and not yet the heavily mantled elder resting like carved alabaster among the southern coils. My mineral plates have grown to a comfortable, working thickness—smooth white scallops stepping down my spine, a pale arch across my brow and cheekbones, worn to a dull sheen by years of turning water. Thick enough that when I press my temple against a bulkhead, the whole steel skeleton miles below speaks straight into my skull; light enough that one lazy push off a pebble-worn rivet sends me coasting ten paces across the courtyard without disturbing a single frond of fern.

If you watched the five of us through an ordinary bright afternoon, you would find me most often near the edges—the northern manifold, the porcelain insulators, the high curved ribs beneath the skylight. Not because I dislike the warm moss of the southern pipes, or the drowsy company of the others, but because I am the one most curious about the places where the machine remembers having an outside.

I spend long stretches hovering beside the dead pressure gauges. Their faces have been sealed blind for centuries under milky moons of lime-scale, their brass bezels melted into soft rounded lips. Nobody here knows what a gauge was meant to count, only that these smooth white circles sit along the ribs like eyes that closed long before we opened ours. They are my favorite anchors. Whenever I find a clean hexagonal snail shell left empty on a seam, or a particularly bright flake of verdigris shed by a molting crab, I tuck it into the fold behind my collarbone and carry it back here. Using tough severed stems of the wire-vines, I knot them into light, balanced threads weighted with smooth mineral pebbles, and loop them around the melted brass bezels.

When the clockwise eddy catches them, they turn in small, disciplined circles just in front of the blind glass, throwing slow travelling flecks of copper-gold across the milky faces. It doesn't fix anything. Nothing here needs fixing. I just like giving the old moons something moving to hold.

I am also the one who listens for the bleed-valve.

The others hear it as weather, the way you hear wind until it stops, but its thin high note sits right against the seam of my brow-plate. I know the moment a loose mat of dead velvet-moss drifts up on the current and catches against its brass lip. Before the others look up, the clean wire of sound roughens into a damp stutter. I push off the manifold, climb the smooth-ribbed curve hand over hand, and wedge my dark-tipped fingers along the rim of the dome until I can pluck the blockage free.

I always stay a few beats longer than the task requires. Up there, inches from the pitted glass, the water is cooler and thinner with light. You can feel the tiny, tireless tug of the pinhole—the single place in all thirty paces where our world leaks out into whatever sits beyond. Escaped gas-bladders from the wire-vines come creeping up the curved ribs beside my cheek, flattened into shivering silver coins, and one by one they reach the brass throat and vanish with a whisper.

It is the strangest thought I have, though I have no clean words for it—only a handful of chiming notes I sometimes try to trade with the others. We make our voices out of those bladders. When I want to speak to another across the open middle water, I pluck a ripe berry from a wire-vine before it tears loose, slip it into the soft pouch beneath my tongue, and press it against my palate until it rings out into the room—bell-clear, dry as tapped glass. Then I cup it out of my mouth and nudge it gently across the amber lane. They catch it, hold it in their own throat, answer me with the same borrowed pocket of gas, and send it drifting back.

Every time we speak, the bladder shrinks a little. By the end of a long exchange it is nothing more than a bright bead slipping between our fingers, rising away from both of us to strike the dome with one last faint chime before sliding uphill toward the valve.

Floating up there with my knuckles hooked over the brass rim, watching the silver coins flee, I think of the colorless violet-blue expanse outside the glass as one bottomless ocean of spent voices. Everything we have ever said, every note we ever traded in the quiet middle water, has flattened itself against the ceiling, found this pinhole, and gone out to join that vast, freezing, silent breath.

Still, I do not want to go out there. I know what cold feels like.

Every four minutes, three heartbeats before the ballast-governor trips, a tiny preliminary shiver runs up through the riveted floor-plates—sharp as a tapped nail against my ribs. Instantly the daydreaming drops away. My clear inner lids slide shut over my eyes; my fluted gill-covers fold down flush along my neck.

While the elder tucks itself deeper into the warm emerald cushions on the southern coils, and two of the others angle upward into the wide sunlit water to wait for the skate-newts to lose their stealth, I brace my long feet against the smooth-worn rivets at the northern manifold.

Then comes the *clang*—loud enough to shake a fine white snow of mineral dust from the rafters—and the bronze intake louvre grinds open.

I am the one who works the fringe of the jet.

I do not dive straight into its core the way the youngling does, spinning recklessly through the white water with stolen vine-gas chiming from its throat. I stay on the knife-edge where the frigid meltwater tears into the bath-warm glow rising off the floor coils. For thirty electric seconds, the room fractures into writhing glass-like sheets of bending light; the fern curtains snap flat as banners; the filament-fish shatter their tidy ribbons and scatter into the open lanes. Leaning into the howling border of the stream, anchored by my heels to the old iron, I reach straight into the cold and gather what it brings.

That is why I choose this post. The surge is violent, and it numbs my wrists to the bone, but it is the only wind this world has. It strips spores from the distant moss cushions, tumbles brass-shelled crabs out of crevices I can never reach, and brings down fresh, sharp-edged flecks of white mineral torn from the far bulkheads. For thirty seconds, the cold reminds the warm room that it is not asleep.

When the louvre slams shut with its heavy, shuddering *boom*, the glass curtains dissolve back into ordinary water. My gill-covers part, flashing brief warm crimson as I drink the newly bright, oxygen-rich drift. Then we find each other again beneath the rising ferns. If the other two took a skate-newt in the optical chaos, we tear lean white strips from the catch and carry the best pieces down to the elder behind the southern coils, where its heavy alabaster mantle is already beginning to blur into the travertine swells of the pipes.

Afterward, while the water settles back into its lazy clockwise turn, we sit close together along a warm header and pick stray grains of lime-scale and torn moss out of one another's gill-seams. Sometimes I drift over to the humming porcelain insulators and hold completely still, letting the faint alternating current prickle pleasantly through the calcified ridges of my back. When I stay quiet long enough, a ribbon of filament-fish breaks away from the copper cable and hovers in neat geometric order along my spine, grazing gently on the microscopic velvet in the seams of my plates. I let them. It feels like tiny, practiced rain.

And when afternoon fades outside the dome—when the pale violet sky deepens to black and starlight turns our thirty paces silver-dark—all my wandering ends at the center of the room.

There is a smooth, narrow waist worn into the bronze drive-shaft at mid-height, burnished to an oily satin. The five of us gather there without needing to call one another. We wrap our long forearms and gripping feet around that comfortable band of turning metal, tucking our smooth lime-plated chins against the warm bronze, tangled loosely together in a slow, drowsy knot.

Twice every three seconds, the shaft carries us around.

My pulse lengthens until it slips cleanly into the *thrum-drag* rising through the collar in the floor. With my cheek pressed to the waisted bronze, I can feel how thin the metal has been worn here—not by water, not by time alone, but by a thousand years of palms and ribs and resting jaws belonging to the ones who lived here before us. Some of them are still just twenty paces away, smoothed into quiet travertine shoulders and brow-hollows along the southern bulkhead behind the copper coils. Nothing leaves this room. Even the dead only settle into the walls and keep listening to the shaft.

One day, generations of lime-heavy water will thicken my own plates until I can no longer brace against the four-minute surge. I know which seam I will probably choose—not far from the blind gauges on the northern curve, if the water is warm enough there, or down beside the southern pipes where the others will come and press gas-bladders against my temple so the bell-notes ring straight through the stone of my skull until my heart matches the axle and slips into it.

But not yet.

Right now it is afternoon again—clean, ordinary daylight pouring straight down through the pitted dome. I have drifted up into the sparse, bright middle water, rolled onto my back, and spread my narrow arms to hold my place against the lazy turn of the eddy. Just beneath the glass, my oiled-bronze eyes are wide open behind their clear inner lids.

Then the room leans.

It is only a fraction of a degree—an unseen wind pressing hard against the iron skin of the tower miles above whatever lies below. Our bath-warm water shifts its weight with a quiet, heavy sigh against the northern curve, and at the very same moment, out beyond the pitted glass, a slow white smudge drifts *beneath* the horizon line of the dome's brass rim.

Below me, the youngling stops chasing bubbles. The elder lifts its carved alabaster crown from the emerald moss. My two companions go completely still in the amber lanes, pale palms turned slightly upward.

I stay where I am, floating inches under the bright glass, feeling the slight, immense tilt of our entire universe settle into the marrow of my ribs. I do not know what a cloud is, or a sky, or a world turning far beneath our brass floor. I only know that we are held very high up inside something ancient and tireless, that something vast and slow has just passed below the edge of our window, and that for the next few cycles my chimes will stay very low—answering only the steady turn of the bronze shaft, and the quiet, enduring warmth of the room that made me.
