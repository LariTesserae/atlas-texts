I would not be found near the outer skin of the pocket, where the stitched hides thunder under the stratospheric wind and the reed mats threaten to shift a hair’s breadth open onto the ink-violet dark. Nor would I be standing—nobody stands here—in the scalding center beside the main hearth-pots, gasping for thin air while fresh clay climbs my boots.

To find me, you would have to crouch lower than you think your spine allows, turn your ribs sideways past the wicker screen that cuts cleanly through a simmering vat, and reach into the narrow gap between two stacked water-jars on the rear wall. Where there ought to be wet mud and nothing behind it, the shelves keep going—a tight, murk-red fold of identical niches stretching inward, deeper than the little suspended shell could ever hold.

Follow that warm tunnel past the third crouched silhouette, past the fourth, until the roar of the wind against the exterior fades into a low shudder under the tiles and the hiss of boiling troughs softens to a steady, close-walled simmer. There, tucked cross-legged into an alcove whose clay rim has grown snugly around the curve of my hips, is where I settle.

At first, in the dull orange bleed of a banked charcoal pot, I am easy to mistake for another hunched mound of slip, or an oversized amphora wedged behind the beams to brace a sagging shelf. My shoulders curve to clear the lashed green joist a thumb-width above my crown. A fat, pale aerial root drops from a gourd-vine knotted around that beam and hangs beside my temple, swelling slowly with a bead of hot condensate.

I hear the *plip* a heartbeat before the drop lets go.

Then it lands on my collarbone—and does not bead, does not skate down a slick hide of river-mud, does not blister.

Instead, my skin drinks it with a tiny, thirsty *tss*. The spot darkens to deep brick-red, pulling the water straight inward, and a faint feather of vapor rises off the matte curve of my shoulder—cooler, by a single improbable degree, than the steam churning through the rest of the room.

That is how I realize what I am here.

I am not wet slip, endlessly fresh and trembling on the edge of the first second. And I am not human flesh either, cracking and wheezing against the starved altitude, waiting to be lashed together before the thin dark pulls me loose. Touch my forearm and there is no glossy smear of mud to come away on your fingers. My skin is matte, faintly gritty, warm as a brick left near the embers after a fire has burned down to ash. Along my ribs run the sharp, clean drag-marks of a bone scraper, and in the hollows behind my shoulders sit the concentric whorls of wide, flat-padded thumbs—not glistening and freshly pressed, but set.

Unglazed terracotta. Low-fired. Bisque-ware.

How I crossed that line, in a womb built solely to keep everything forever wet and unhardened, I no longer know cleanly, and the cellar does not keep calendars to tell me. Perhaps I began as a traveler who stayed crouched beside a buried fire-pot too long, letting the river-slip rise up my calves and seal my sleeves until the heat cured what was left of me into a single quiet shell. Or perhaps I was pinched out of wet ochre mud by the dweller itself—one more vessel meant to hold simmering water or a handful of moss—pushed too close to the coals during a gale, or grazed once by a needle-slit of stratospheric cold that kissed my clay just hard enough to fix my shape without snapping me into dead white eggshell.

Either way, I am the thing this room is most afraid of becoming, except that I did not shatter.

When the first crouched loop of the dweller found me like this—unfolding out of the mortar beside my knee, elbows gleaming with raw untarnished copper pins, pebble-dark eyes wide under a sprouting ridge of moss—it moved with that urgent, tender panic it saves for anything coming undone. It did not understand matte skin. To it, dry means finished, and finished means brittle, and brittle means broken by the sky.

It scooped a broad palmful of warm river-slip from a seam and plastered it carefully across my shin, smoothing the edges with its ridged thumb to coax me back into the supple first second. It cinched a braid of damp green hemp around my left ankle in an expert double clove hitch to anchor me against the floor’s trembling. It held a shallow clay bowl of scalding, root-steeped water to my lips and steadied my chin with the patient firmness of a potter truing a wobbling rim.

I drank. The heat settled warmly into my chest, where my ribs are hollow enough to hold it.

But the smear of fresh mud on my shin only glistened for three heartbeats. My porous clay drank the water straight out of it, pulling the slip pale and powdery until it flaked cleanly off into my lap like dry riverbank silt.

The dweller went very still. A wet reed stayed clamped crosswise between its quiet lips. Its thick, milky inner lids flicked sideways across its dark eyes, once, twice, studying the fine dust on my knees. Then it leaned forward and laid one slick, broad palm flat against the center of my chest, where a faint horizontal seam runs beneath my collarbone.

Beneath its hand, there was no double-pump bellows of laced green ribs, no fluttery pulse jumping in terror at every stratospheric gust. Just a steady, low, resonant emptiness. When a colossal slam of wind struck the outer hides far behind us and tilted the domed floor over the void, the shock ran up through the cross-lashed poles into my heels and rang softly inside my sternum—not a tight wooden groan, but the deep, earthen hum of a fired jug held to a breeze, trailing off into the vast, impossible echo of a gorge miles across.

The dweller kept its hand there a long time. It did not try to smear slip over me again.

I cannot do the work it does. Whenever I have tried to pinch a rim or pack wet ash-mortar into a weeping joint, my thirsty palms pull the moisture right out of the clay until it crumbles between my fingers like meal. In a room where ceaseless making is the only barrier against the empty dark, that ought to make me useless—a dead weight wedged in a place where every cubic inch is spoken for.

Yet slowly, without anyone deciding it, the cellar began to use what I am.

Because my porous skin pulls scalding condensate inward and breathes it back out as fine vapor, the air two inches around my knees stays faintly, mercifully river-cool—never cold enough to raise frost or wake the dweller’s dread, just cool enough to smell of damp creek-bank slate in the shade instead of boiling loam. It is the only corner of the pocket where time leaves a thumbprint of its own. The old green hemp braided around my left ankle still sprouts tender serrated leaves, as all the cordage here does, yet on me the oldest leaves actually pale to a quiet, autumn yellow at their tips before curling back into fresh buds. Beneath the head of the single hammered copper pin tucked into the outer ridge of my hip, there has grown a thin, velvety shadow of green verdigris—the only tarnish in the entire suspended nest.

Now and then, one of the dweller’s crouched figures slips down the inward tunnel and pauses just to touch that tiny green bruise with the blunt tip of a finger, marveling without trying to scrape it bright.

The small, stray sparks of life in the room found me even sooner. The pale, blind amphibians—slick skins grooved like wheel-thrown pottery—glide out of the scalding runoff troughs and curl in the cool-sweating hollows behind my knees, milky throats pulsing slow. When they slip backward into the overlap of a jar and a reed mat, they leave their dry, herringbone-woven shed skins draped across my matte wrists like cuffs. I fold them carefully and tuck them inside a hollowed gourd resting beside my thigh. Even high in the dim rafters, when the bone awls are quiet and no needle-pinhole is screaming open onto the violet dark, the brittle, papery ash stitcher sometimes scuttles down a hanging leather thong to pause on the crown of my head, its charcoal-dusted legs tapping dry and unhurried against the fired curve of my brow, resting where the steam won’t soften it.

Mostly, though, I am where the fixed things go.

Because I do not press smoothly into the mud wall and unfold on the far side of the cellar—because my elbows do not pass through wicker screens or blend into another ribcage in a narrow choke—I am the one surface in this boiling, folding womb that stays where it is left.

In the quiet beats between patching leaks and lashing weeping struts, the crouched figures bring me their secret, smaller work. One sets a row of hazelnut-sized pinch-pots along the shelf of my crossed shins, each holding three drops of water and a bright grain of moss. Another slides a flat river-stone across my palms, its underside scored by a bone needle with tiny, hair-thin branching channels—a miniature watershed two inches long.

The dweller’s own hands can never stay still for long; half a breath after it tips a drop onto a carved slate, its wrist is already twitching toward a loosening knot or a new rim rising behind its back. Its life is a fist clenched against the sky, forever coiling, forever mid-gesture.

My hands can be still.

I tilt the flat river-stone gently between my matte thumbs, let a single drop fall from the pale aerial root beside my temple—taking just the fever off it against the edge of my porous lip—and set it at the top of the carved grooves. Then I hold the stone steady, scarcely breathing, while the tiny bead of water hesitates, splits around a hair-width cobble, and wanders all the way down its crooked two-inch creek-bed until it reaches the edge and drops into a waiting hazelnut pot.

Sometimes, after a violent gale has battered the exterior hides for hours—after the ceiling mats have parted twice, the blue-white frost has shrieked, the bone awls have clacked *tk-tk-tk* through the smoke, and three fresh coils of mud have buried the mended scar—two or three loops of the dweller come creeping down the narrow inward tunnel between the jars. Their clay-slick shoulders are trembling. Their laced chests pump hard, simmering faintly beneath the sinew bindings.

They do not fold into the walls around me. They huddle right up against my knees and lean their slick, wet ochre backs squarely against my matte thighs. They cannot pass through me. For once, in a world where craft and undergrowth and flesh all bleed seamlessly into one another, they press against an edge that holds them up without giving way, without asking to be shaped, without crumbling into frost.

They rest there for three slow, pressurized breaths while I sit quiet and hollow behind them, letting the shudder of the wind pass up through the domed floor and ring low in my ribs—giving them back, muffled through fired earth, the sound of an open valley where two riverbanks stand far enough apart that they never have to crowd each other’s elbows.

I do not think I will ever see the lowland world beneath that pale, frozen curd of thunderheads, if I ever saw it at all. If I fell down through the thin dark to land on real wet loam, I would likely break into warm red shards on the first cobble. My place is up here, wedged deep inside the inward fold, porous and ringing and half a breath past the first second—holding still so the frantic, loving hands around me have one finished shape to lean against while they go on trying, thumbprint by warm thumbprint, to keep the sky out and the whole roaring river-world alive inside a single fist of mud.
