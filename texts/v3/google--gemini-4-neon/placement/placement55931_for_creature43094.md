I know a ledge before I ever trust it with a spike.

Not by looking. Sight lies down here, under that flat, dust-white glare that strips every ridge of its shadow until a twenty-foot drop reads like a chalk line across flat shale. And not by listening through the air—the air is a liar too, or worse, a bully, crammed wall to wall with clacking reeds and detonating husks and the great, sawing hiss of the cane-sea tearing itself upward. Besides, the top edge of the air has already begun to leave me. Last season the razor-whine of the brassy insect clouds thinned to a faint silver thread and then snapped altogether. Now I only know a swarm is rolling up the canyon when the ceiling-glare dims a shade, like flour tossed across a lamp, and the fine hairs under my sleeve stand up against the dry leather.

No: I know a ledge through the outer edge of my right arm.

I crouch on the bare basalt spur, letting my copper bleed-tail drag across the raw stone behind my heel so the static drains out of me in tiny, tickling pinpricks. With my left hand I unlace the shed borer-plates from my right forearm, fold the pleated glider-membrane back to the elbow, and lay the grey, callused ridge of my ulna flat against the rock.

The stone is warm—everything here is warm, kiln-dry, humming faintly like the brick wall behind a baker’s fire. I take the antler mallet from my belt, check the copper wire braided around its grip so a stray arc won’t kiss a gas-crack, and strike the basalt once.

*Thum.*

It doesn’t enter my ears—those are stopped tight under knuckle-bone guards packed with bast-fiber. It travels straight up the bone of my wrist, leaps the elbow, and rings cleanly behind my collarbones.

A bad ledge—a sheared slab perched over a pocket of hot gas or a hollow chewed thin by borers—answers short and sullen, like a cracked clay pot. This one answers long. Dark. Deep enough to lose a plumb line in. Rooted straight down into the spine of the earth.

I strike it twice more, slower, and wait with my eyes narrowed behind my slate visor, letting the echo die so I can read what rides underneath it.

There it is: the low, gritty *grrr-chk, grrr-chk* of a giant borer working a gypsum seam maybe two hundred paces to the east, grinding raw rock into warm flour beneath the heaving wicker. Higher up in the pitch, a nervous, intermittent *ping* where a fresh sheet of slate in the vault overhead is still stretching under its own weight. And beneath both, so faint I have to press my bare heel against the stone as well to catch the full shape of it, the deliberate cadence of another crew’s Knocker hammering a ridge half a league across the bright haze.

*Two short. Pause. Three long.*

Water-runner down at the north chimney.

I sit back on my heels, re-lace the overlapping shell-plates over my forearm, and glance over my shoulder at the rest of my knot—four rust-and-ochre shapes braced against the hot updraft ten paces back, waiting behind a crooked ridge of raw shale where the clicking cane hasn’t quite climbed yet. I don’t try to shout. I raise two fingers, then press my palm flat toward the ground and twist it like a spike biting home. *Good rock. We anchor here.*

---

I didn’t come down into the split intending to be a Knocker. Nobody comes down here intending to become what the valley makes of them.

Up in the old caverns—leagues above this wound, where the tunnels are cold, dim, and slick with centuries of slow water—I was an apprentice tally-keeper to a salt-factor, spending my days scraping damp mildew off waxed ledgers by the stutter of tallow lamps. I had soft wrists, a bad cough from the wet air, and a habit of pressing my palms against the limestone walls of the market halls just to feel the vibration of ore-carts rolling in from the deep shafts before anyone else knew they were coming. When my master’s ledgers went sideways and his creditors started looking at his apprentices to make up the gap, I followed three seam-runners down the fresh tremor-crack with nothing but a dull knife, two dry loaves, and a terror of being caught.

I lasted forty minutes on my first descent before the white glare gave me a headache like an iron wedge driven between my eyes, and the noise made my nose bleed. I curled behind a raw basalt tooth, hands clapped uselessly over my ears, convinced I had climbed straight into the throat of a furnace that meant to grind me to chaff.

Then an old Stone-Eared woman—half her scalp scarred bald from an oil-flash, her forearms grey as weathered slate—squatted beside me in the howling bright wind. She didn’t shout. She didn’t pull me up. She simply took my trembling right hand, flattened my palm against the basalt beneath us, and struck the rock a foot away with the butt of a copper spike.

Through all the screaming chaos of the air, that single dark note came up through my fingers like a quiet voice speaking in an empty room.

I never really went back up after that. Not to stay.

---

In our knot of five, everyone has a way of reading the engine.

Maren—the oldest of us, though she’s barely past forty, her skin cured the color of smoked resin—reads the wicker. She can stand on a swaying mat of hollow canes and tell by the flex of her knees whether a gas-bladder two yards beneath her boots is merely breathing or swelling toward a burst. Talo and Venn, brothers who came down together three seasons ago and move like two halves of one pair of tongs, work the curved obsidian saws and the ironwood gaffs; they bleed the pressurized stalks before cutting them, hissing off the sulfur-sweet plumes with a practiced flick that hasn’t cost either of them a finger yet. And Kess, our youngest—still fast enough to outrun a recoil, still twitching whenever a pressurized seed-pod cracks behind her heels—handles the twelve-foot resin pole when we have to trip a pale basket-net and sprint under its thrashing spines.

And I read the bone of the place. And I make the bridges.

You don’t hear people up in the wet trading caverns talk much about bridges, because up there the air is cheap and dead, and any fool can flap their mouth and be heard across a hall. Down here, a bridge is the difference between five strangers surviving on the same rock and a crew that actually knows each other’s minds.

Any harvester can whittle a splinter of dense root-wood in a pinch, clamp one end in their teeth, and press the other against a partner’s throat. It works—roughly. A bad splinter buzzes against your enamel, muddies the low hums, splits down the grain when the kiln-dry air pulls the last of its tension out, or leaks bitter resin onto your tongue.

A good bridge is a small miracle.

I hunt for the wood myself, down in the tight, choking clefts where a thick cane-mat anchors directly into fractured basalt. Up in the canopy everything is hollow, gas-pumped, brittle as dry quill; it shatters if you look at it hard. Only down in the dark pinch between root and stone—where the living wicker has to clench hard enough to hold back a roaring updraft—does the plant lay down dense, twisted heartwood with no air pockets in it at all.

When we clear a collapsed ledge or follow a giant borer’s trench, I pry those compressed root-knots out with an ironwood wedge and tuck them into my pack. Then, later, inside the manufactured dark of our dome, I work them by fingertip.

You don’t need light to carve a bridge; in fact, light gets in the way, makes you judge by look instead of tremor. Sitting cross-legged on the pith mat with a sliver of knapped black obsidian, I shave the heartwood flat, thin it down along the waist, notch the biting end so it seats gently against front teeth without slipping, and round the throat-pad smooth against the ball of my thumb. Every twenty strokes I clamp it between my own teeth, press the flat end against the side of my jaw, and hum a low, three-note roll.

Too thick, and the vibration dies halfway down the grain like a shout in fog. Too thin, and it chatters angrily, turning a joke into a rasp. When it’s right—when the heartwood is cured dry in the warm dark and scraped to the exact thickness of a glider’s wing-rib—the wood disappears. You clamp it between your teeth and the person leaning into your visor seems to be speaking straight from the hollow behind your eyes.

I’ve made two for each of us, strung on oiled leather thongs around our necks. I know each crewmate’s bridge by the tiny differences in how they hold it: Maren bites light and speaks in clipped, dry clicks; Talo hums low in his chest so the wood vibrates like a copper wire; Kess laughs through hers—a quick, breathless shudder that makes my molars itch—even when we’re huddled behind a windbreak watching a dust-devil tear our drying-rack to confetti.

---

The afternoon after we spike down the new dome on that deep basalt spur, the valley tries to test our knots.

We are out forty yards along a raw shale spine, watching the churn below. Maren spotted the sign an hour back: a slow, steaming volcano of pale gypsum dust rising through a break in the clicking cane-canopy, accompanied by the faint, steady grind I could feel right through the soles of my boots. A giant borer—big as a log, armored in overlapping plates the color of fired rust—is surfacing to shed.

The wind is fierce today, pouring hot out of a wide floor-fissure and slamming into cooler sheets sliding down the vaulted walls. High overhead, beneath the blinding white ceiling-curtains, two hundred pleated gliders spiral in a furious tangle, their hollow chest-slits screaming like torn brass as they dive through a rising plume of blown chaff. Down on the heaving wicker, six-legged grazers sprint along flexing cane-bridges, their bristled backs sparking blue whenever two collide over a burst seed-pod.

We wait until the borer drags its pale, soft new segments back down beneath the root-basket, leaving its discarded dorsal armor wedged half-upright in a steaming mound of warm mineral grit.

I tap the rock once—*all clear below, the big one’s gone deep*—and Kess and the brothers slide down the shale rib, loose-kneed, copper bleed-tails spitting tiny blue arcs off the stone. They move fast, because smaller spider-legged scavengers are already boiling out of the dark weave to chew the brittle edges of the shed plates and sift the fresh grit for crystalline dust.

I stay on the spur above with Maren, holding the slack of their haul-lines, my eyes narrowed behind my slate slit-visor against the white glare, my lips thick with amber resin-wax so they won’t split when the wind hits them.

Suddenly, back on our new ledge fifty yards up the ridge, the cured glider-throats we lashed to the dome-poles begin to shriek.

Not the ordinary whine of diving birds. A piercing, metallic howl that slices straight through my bast-stuffed bone ear-guards and makes the roots of my teeth ache.

Pressure drop. A big one.

Before I can even turn my head, the whole expanse of green-ochre cane two hundred yards south of the borer-mound lifts three feet into the air like a rug shaken by a giant hand. A subterranean gas-bladder—massive, hidden deep beneath the root-mat—strains, bulges, and rips open with a dull, belly-deep thud I feel in my guts a heartbeat before the wave reaches our stone.

A column of shredded husks, shimmering gypsum powder, and charged yellow spores explodes upward, immediately twisting into a howling dust-devil that comes spinning straight across the canopy toward the borer-mound.

Maren doesn’t hesitate. She steps close, strikes her copper-ringed knuckles against mine—*snap!*—the sharp blue spark equalizing us before she leans in until her dark slate visor nearly clicks against my own. She presses her root-wood bridge against her throat; I open my lips just enough to clamp the notched tip between my teeth.

*—haul them now, the ridge’s holding, wind’s bending east—*

Her throat-syllables ring clean and steady inside my skull, calm as a winter hearth even while wind-hurled seed-burrs rattle like hail against our borer-shell shoulders. I nod once, let the splinter slip from my teeth, and drop to one knee on the bare shale.

Three sharp strikes with the antler mallet. *Now. Drop the grit. Come up.*

Down in the swirling white haze, Talo’s hand is flat on the rock lip beside the borer-mound. He feels it instantly. He yanks Kess by her apron-strap, throws a weighted loop of braided copper wire across a sparking grazer that darts across their path—grounding the beast in a frantic blue hiss—and together the three of them haul two curved dorsal plates up the shale rib hand over hand as the dust-devil clips the edge of the mound, ripping a twenty-foot tangle of dry cane skyward in an avalanche of splintering hollow wood.

A single stray leaf, thin and serrated as hammered mica, whips past my cheek and slices a neat red line across the leather strap of my visor. Half an inch closer and it would have opened my jaw to the bone.

I don’t even blink. I just brace my boots against a hammered copper spike, grip the braided hide haul-line, and pull until Kess’s dust-caked shoulder bangs against mine behind the windbreak. She’s panting dry, her eyes wide and bright behind the charcoal smear under her lashes. She reaches out, taps her copper knuckles to mine—*snap*—and grins around her waxed lips, already lifting the shed borer-plate to inspect the clean, unbroken rim.

A good haul. Enough armor to replace Venn’s cracked shin-guards and trade two plates up at the damp caverns for fresh copper wire and half a bladder of water.

---

And then, when the shift ends—not because the light fades, for the ceiling-curtains never dim by a hair’s breadth, but because our limbs begin to shake and our jaws ache from the relentless percussion—we crawl back into the night we built with our own hands.

One by one, we duck into the low, crooked crawl-tunnel of our scorched-cane dome, pushing past the first heavy curtain of glider-leather, then the second, letting the baffled folds fall shut behind us.

The sudden dark hits like a physical weight. Mercy.

Out there, every edge is sharp and blazing, every surface shouting dust-white glare without a single pocket of shadow to rest an eye in. In here, there is nothing to see at all. The roar of the valley drops to a thick, distant surf pulsing quietly through the basalt floor beneath our woven pith mats. The air is stiflingly warm, heavy with the honest, dry smells of scorched cane, amber resin, tallow, and the coppery tang of five unwashed bodies.

Nobody moves to speak. Nobody lights the ceiling-dust yet. We unlace our shell-aprons by feel, let the pleated glider-coats slip from our shoulders, pull off our slate visors, and pry the bulky bone ear-guards free. For five full minutes we simply sit hunched in the pitch dark, pressing the heels of our hands against our aching jaws, waiting for the phantom scream inside our skulls to thin out and sink.

Then comes the faint, unmistakable *slosh* of leather against leather—the only wet sound anywhere within a league.

Maren unties the double-seamed bladder from the coolest shadow against the rear basalt wall. She passes it right, into my hands. It feels absurdly heavy, dense and cool as a living thing. I uncork the bone stopper, tilt a single measured mouthful onto my tongue, and stop. I don’t gulp. I hold the water against my parched gums, letting the dryness soften bit by bit, feeling it trickle down my throat a drop at a time before I stopper the bladder and pass it on to Kess. Then I dip a finger in the resin-wax tin and grease my lips again.

In the upper caverns, I remember people pouring half a cup of stale water onto the stone floor without thinking, or spitting into a gutter to punctuate a bargain. The memory makes my stomach tighten now, almost like remembering a blasphemy. Down here, water isn’t a thing you spill; it’s borrowed blood.

Once the bladder is tucked safe against the wall, Talo uncorks a single brass thimble of luminous ceiling-dust.

Just a pinch, glowing a soft, ghostly white—not enough to hurt our glare-burnt eyes, just enough to turn our skin to pale clay. We kneel in a loose circle, backs turned to one another, checking shoulders and ribs by slow, careful fingertip for silica splinters. You can’t always feel them out in the wind—they’re finer than hair, broken off the serrated fronds—until you bend in the quiet dark and feel the tiny, hot stab working deeper into muscle.

I sit with my bare back to Kess while she draws two slivers from near my left shoulder blade with fine bone tweezers, her fingertips warm and dry and impossibly gentle on my skin. Then I turn and check Maren’s ribs, feeling the old raised ridges of healed cuts beneath her calluses, pulling one shining glass-hair from just above her hip. No bridges now. No need. In the hot dark, when the armor comes off, touch is the whole language.

Talo corks the thimble. Darkness rushes back in, soft and complete.

We pile onto the pith mats together—shoulder tucked against spine, knee hooked behind knee, five dry bodies breathing against one another’s ribs so that even half-deafened and dead tired, each of us can feel the steady, reassuring heave of another chest.

Before sleep takes me, my left hand slides into the inner pocket sewn against my tunic and closes around my stone.

I found it three trips back, down in the cold stream-sump of the wet trading caverns, after a shelter-collapse swallowed my first one into the churning wicker. It’s a thumb-sized oval of dark river-chert, rolled for ten thousand years in cold water until not a single corner survived on it. Running right around its middle is a hairline vein of pale quartz. In the pitch dark of the dome I can’t see the quartz, of course, but my callused thumb can just barely feel the change in grain where one stone yields to the other—smoothness meeting a slightly different smoothness.

I turn it over and over between my thumb and forefinger. Across the pile, I hear the faint, dry whisper of Maren’s fingers doing the same with hers. Five people turning silent curves in the dark, reminding our hands that somewhere far above the kiln, water still teaches rock how to be gentle.

---

Usually, I fall asleep within ten minutes, my thumb still resting on the chert.

Tonight, though, after the others’ breathing slows and deepens against my back, I stay awake a little longer. I ease my right arm out from the tangle of shoulders and stretch it just past the edge of the pith mat, laying my bare forearm flat against the raw basalt floor beneath us.

My ear-guards are off, and the dome walls muffle the cane-sea to a dull hush, so for once nothing competes with the bone.

I close my eyes and let the bedrock speak.

Near at hand, twenty feet below our dome, a tiny fracture ticks softly—*tk*—settling after the day’s heat. Farther out, the giant borer has already finished hardening its new plates and is back at work, its steady millstone-grind muffled by a hundred yards of gypsum. Somewhere off to the west, another knot’s Knocker taps out a slow, private rhythm I don’t recognize—maybe just testing a newly spiked ledge, maybe knocking just to remind themselves the stone hasn’t moved since dusk-that-isn’t-dusk.

And deeper than all of it—so slow and vast you can’t hear it in a single breath, only if you hold still for minutes at a time—is the sound that never lets me stay up in the wet tunnels for more than a week.

A low, immense, patient groan running down the whole length of the valley’s spine.

The crust is still opening.

Up above, in the cold trading caverns where we go every few months to haul down water-bladders and copper and flint, everything is finished. The stone has stopped moving. The stalactites have been dripping into the same cold puddles for a thousand lifetimes. When we walk into those damp, dim halls, people stare at our bent, springy knees and our grey forearms; they jump when we tap our knuckles against their copper lanterns waiting for a spark that never comes. And I stand there smelling wet moss and rotting wood, listening to the slow, dead *plip-plop* of condensation hitting puddles in the dark, and my skin turns clammy and heavy. A terrible restlessness settles behind my teeth. Those old upper tunnels feel like the inside of a skull whose owner died long ago.

Down here, under my bare forearm, the rock is still making itself up.

Tomorrow, or next month, or two domes from now, this basalt spur might tilt forty degrees and dump our resin-plastered blister straight into the clicking green-ochre sea. Tomorrow a new vein of copper might split open under a borer’s jaws, or a ceiling-crack might pour down a waterfall of amber dust that catches the white glare like dry fire. One of these seasons the last high edge of the air will go quiet on me for good—first the diving gliders, then the sharp snap of seed-husks, then even the rare voices of traders up in the damp caverns—and I’ll become one of the Stone-Eared elders, calm on swaying walkways while the young ones still flinch.

I don’t dread it. Let the air have its racket. I already know where the real conversation lives.

In the dark of the dome, Kess shifts in her sleep, pressing her shoulder warmer against my ribs. I tuck my river-chert back into its pocket, rest my chin on my bent wrist, and keep my forearm on the warm, humming basalt until sleep pulls me under—feeling the whole fierce, unfinished valley hammer itself into being straight through my bones.
