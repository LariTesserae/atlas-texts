At first, I do not know whether I have hands here at all.

When I drop below the jagged lip of schist, leaving the scouring gale to hiss over the planed juniper behind me, the gorge does not immediately offer me a body. It offers pressure, and wet cold, and the slow iron-grey dusk sifting through five tiers of interlocked needles. For a long while, I think I might simply be a seam in the architecture—the thin film of cold water weeping down raw fracture lines and soaking into the back of a moss-brick wall, or the faint, stubborn pocket of warmth trapped three feet deep inside compressed sedge and rotting leaf-litter, breathing ghostly threads of vapor out into the freezing lanes at dusk.

It is tempting to stay unbodied in a place like this. Everything that lives here is already half-merged with everything else: bark scarred around raw stone like flesh around a ring, living roots braided into stairways, dead elders pressed gently beneath the acid turf until young hemlock roots thread down through their quiet ribs. Why insist on an outline? Why carry the narrow, expensive margin of a pulse when you could be the long continuous grain of a fused hemlock truss, carrying the memory of a knuckle-tap twenty feet down through the dark?

Then the midday cloud deck thins for twenty minutes, and the upper canopy loosens.

Slush drops silently through the lattices. The moss-walls darken and swell, pinching the doorways narrower by an inch. A single oversized drop falls from a bark-gutter and strikes a leathery olive leaf with a hollow *plock*—and something in me flinches toward the sound, listening not as wood listens, but as someone who knows what happens when meltwater works its way into a hairline split before the evening freeze locks down.

That is where I find my weight. Not in the stone, and not in the wind-plane above. On the fourth rung down of a braided root-ladder, toes gripping frozen haircap moss through soles thickened to dark, pebbled leather, shoulders already tucked forward into the permanent, easy crouch of someone who has spent a lifetime under sagging ceilings.

I am not one of the lean young ones climbing into the blinding greenish glare beneath the juniper crust. When I glance upward through a drainage shaft, even on an overcast noon, the raw slot of sky hurts; my pale, wide-pupiled eyes water and narrow to tight slits until I drop my chin back into my felted collar. Nor am I brave enough—or restless enough—to strap fermented seed-cake beneath my cloak and haul myself over that sharp grey-black rim into the howling dry white of the ridge. I know what lives up there: wind that strips moisture from your lips in a single breath, rock that tears leather, light with nowhere to hide. Let the leanest of the young carry green willow-cuttings against their ribs into that terror. I was made for the shadow between the walls.

Yet I am not ready for the lowest walkways either, where the elders sit wrapped in grey moss-weave above the rising black water, chewing inner willow-bark soft and holding infants who have never seen the slit of sky. I go down there often—my feet know every slick, half-drowned ramp where the puddles never quite dry—but I do not stay. My joints have not stiffened into root-hooks yet. My blood still wants the climb.

If I belong to any single place in the cleft, it is the vertical seam between the middle lanes and the floor—and the narrow, forgotten hollows where the living timber stops drawing sap.

***

No one here has a title for what I spend my days doing. Nobody speaks titles in a place where even the great-grandfather who shaped the eastern arch is remembered only by the way he always braided three roots instead of two. You are simply your habit of attention, and the particular stain on your knuckles.

Mine is listening for the dead note inside living wood.

Because every arch, ramp, and doorway in the gorge is grafted into a single continuous skeleton of fused birch and hemlock, the whole city rings when you touch it. Most tenders press a palm to a load-bearing trunk to read the strain—heavy ice overhead, a blocked drainage groove on the west seam, a cousin crossing a narrow bridge two tiers up. They listen for tension, for the living weight of water and timber negotiating with the cliffs.

I listen for what has stopped answering.

Even in a city kept relentlessly alive, a bough dies now and then. A frost-shattered slab of schist shears cleanly through a leader on the upper tiers; a lower truss drowns slowly where the acid bog creeps upward an inch a lifetime; an inner branch gets pinched off when two thicker trunks fuse around it and starve its cambium of sap. From the outside, in the drained dusk of the corridors, the wood looks unchanged—still scarred, still rimed with frost, still bearing its nap of lichen.

So I walk the tiers with a bare, slate-grey knuckle resting lightly against the trusses as I pass, giving a small, inquiring *tok* every few paces—so quiet it does not even register as speech to the tenders working nearby, no louder than an icicle shifting on a spruce bough.

Live wood answers deep and tight, carrying the vibration down into the bone of my wrist. Wet, rotted wood answers with a sodden thud—useless, already turning back into peat.

What I am listening for is the rare, dry, spongy note in between: black birch heartwood that has died cleanly behind its outer rind, neither frozen solid nor drowned to slush, gone punky and fibrous in the dark.

Because there is no fire in the cleft. There has never been a coal, never a tallow wick, never a breath of smoke trapped between these weeping walls. When the temperature drops hard at dusk and the olive-leaved shrubs curl their rubbery blades shut across every arch and window-slit, locking out even the meagre silver bounce of frost and reindeer lichen, the domed chambers would drop into a darkness as thick as buried stone—except for what grows inside that dead birch heartwood.

When I find the hollow note—today it is on the underside of an old double-arch on the third tier, where a birch trunk split years ago around a wedged slab of scree and healed over the wound, sealing a fist-sized pocket of dead core inside—I stop. I pull my resin-rubbed outer cloak tighter against the drip from the gutter overhead and listen for a long minute, ear pressed flat against the icy bark, to be sure the dead seam is not bearing load. If taking it would weaken the arch by even a hair's breadth, I leave it alone and tuck a tiny sliver of raw schist beside the seam as a mark for whoever reads the truss next winter.

This one is free weight—a dry knot cradled safely between two thick, healthy roots that have long since taken over the span.

I have no metal blade, no saw. I work with a natural flake of freshly split schist wrapped in a strip of inner willow-bark so it does not slice my own calloused palm, and a bone awl ground smooth on wet stone from a winter windfall. Slowly, with no hurry—haste wastes heat and slips the tool—I pry back the dead collar of bark and work out three pale, fibrous chunks of heartwood, each no larger than my curled fist. They feel cool and faintly velvety, smelling of damp earth and old rain.

I tuck them straight inside my layers, into a small pouch of woven willow-bast sewn over my sternum, next to the dry sedge-fluff that guards my ribs.

There is a quiet kinship in that, though I would never say it aloud. Once every generation, the leanest of the young climb up over the blinding rim with live green willow-cuttings and moss-wrapped birch seedlings pressed against their ribs so their own heat keeps the buds from dying across the open rock. Down here in the green twilight, I carry dead wood against my chest for the opposite and identical reason: so the sharp evening freeze does not lock the pores tight before I can bring it down to the light.

***

The climb down takes me through the whole thickness of the city.

On the middle lanes, I step aside into a recessed doorway so two cousins can haul a bundle of freshly harvested sphagnum along a sloping ramp; neither of us speaks, though one brushes my shoulder with a frozen knuckle—*gutter clear on the east wall*. Passing the western cistern, I pause to test the ice-crust along its waxy leaf-lining, breaking a thin crescent of rime with my thumb so the weeping water from the cliff can keep feeding it before nightfall. Everywhere I look, the walls are dense with quiet sentences I can read without stopping: a single willow-knot letting a young hemlock leader grow wild against a draft; a double knot pinching back a hedge; a threshold worn dark and glossy by ten generations of passing hips, still green beneath its callous.

Lower still, the light drains away until it is no longer green at all, only a wet, silvery grey. The air stops moving completely. My breath hangs in a slow ribbon before the haircap moss drinks it out of sight.

Down on the lowest walkway—just above the springy, dark bog that is slowly swallowing the oldest foundations—the cold changes its character. It loses the knife-edge of the upper tiers and becomes heavy, damp, and almost steady, fed from beneath by the vast, slow decay of centuries of fallen needles and ancient turf. Puddles spread wide across the braided floor, shimmering faintly where the pale glow catches them.

Here, tucked into a wet root-cradle built into the base of a submerged arch that nobody walks across anymore, sits one of the oldest mother-logs in the gorge.

It is half the length of my arm, blackened at the edges where the rising peat has begun to kiss it, and soft as wet felt along its split face. In the near-dark, it sheds a steady, ghostly sheen of pale green—cold light that burns no air, throws no heat, asks nothing of the dormant trees around it. Just enough to catch the wet rim of an elder's hollowed burl-bowl, or the slow blink of a grey-furred vole slipping between the root-masonry and the weeping stone.

An elder sits three paces away, wrapped so deeply in grey moss-weave she looks like a boulder grown over with lichen, cradling an infant inside the open front of her inner bark-cloth. She does not turn her head when I crouch beside the cradle; she knew my rolling step on the lower ramp ten seconds before I arrived. From somewhere deep in her layers comes the tiny, wet clicking of the baby sucking pre-chewed root-mash off a knuckle.

I unfasten my outer cloak just enough to draw the three chunks of fresh birch heartwood from over my ribs. They are faintly warm from my skin, soft enough to take the impression of a thumb. Gently, I press their pale, split faces directly against the glowing white threads spreading across the mother-log, binding them snug together with a single peeled strip of wet willow-bark so the slow mycelium can cross the seam and wake inside the new grain.

It will take weeks before the pale green haze shows clearly on the fresh wood, and half a winter before it is bright enough to carry up to the third tier and set beside a doorway or a fermentation wall. I am in no hurry. Nobody in the gorge is ever in a hurry. While I tie the willow-strip, I take two older chunks from the far end of the cradle—chunks I bound there back before the first hard freeze of autumn, now veiled in a steady, milky green radiance that leaves a faint cold luminescence on the creases of my fingers when I lift them.

Before I climb back up, I press my bare palm against the base of the submerged arch where the wood disappears into black water. Two soft taps—*tok-tok*—just to listen to how far down the drowned roots still hold firm, and to greet whoever is buried beneath the living sphagnum below my knees. No answer comes up from the peat, of course. Only the deep, almost imperceptible *groan* of five stacked tiers of frozen water and living timber settling by a fraction of a hair's breadth as the day's light drains out of the slot far above.

***

The temperature drops hard before I reach the third tier.

All around me in the dim lanes, the settlement begins its nightly closing without a single hand touching it. Trained around every doorway arch and window-slit, the thick olive leaves of the alpine shrubs stir with a dry, leathery whisper—curling slowly inward along their midribs, folding down over one another like overlapping slate tiles. The wet moss-walls glaze over on their outer skins into a crisp, glittering rind of ice, turning spongy corridors rigid. Frost-needles stitch themselves across the puddles in silence.

I slip beneath the closing curtain of a middle-tier dome just as the last leaves draw tight behind my heel, shutting out the breath of the lanes.

Inside, it is almost warm—never cozy, only that stubborn few degrees above freezing held by three feet of compressed peat and the slow heat of piled bodies. The air smells heavily of damp earth, resin, and the sharp, sour yeast of the cold ferments working patiently inside their stone-grown birch-burls stacked into the masonry wall.

Two chunks of old heartwood beside the cistern have gone dim, faded to barely a whisper of green. I lift them out—not to throw away, nothing here is wasted; tomorrow I will crumble their spent fibers into the moss-packing of a drafty wall—and settle my two fresh, glowing pieces into the damp root-cradle in their place.

The pale green sheen swells gently across the curved interior of the dome. It catches the frosted whiskers of a cloak hanging from a peg, the glossy worn lip of a burl-bowl already being passed from hand to hand, and the pale eyes of six others already wedged into the dry bog-cotton hollows of the floor.

I unfasten my resin-rubbed outer birch cloak, hang my damp sedge-stuffed liner near the doorway flap so I can set it out beneath the dry frost-drafts of the upper eaves tomorrow afternoon, and crawl into the heap.

A cousin shifts her knees to make a pocket for my back; an older weaver drapes a shared pelt of felted bark-cloth over both our shoulders. Someone presses the cold burl-bowl into my hands. The fermented mash inside is thick, grey-brown, sharp on the tongue with crushed juniper and bitter hemlock seed, faintly effervescent. I swallow slowly, letting the quiet, surprising ember of warmth uncurl deep in my belly, and pass the bowl on into the green-lit dark.

Only now, with the olive leaves locked solid and our breath shared inside the same small air, do our voices wake. No more than soft clicks, closed-mouth hums, breathy consonants murmured with lips close to a neighbor's temple so none of the heat escapes into the room. Someone speaks softly of a loose slab of schist on the fifth tier that will need two birch-leaders bent beneath it come thaw. Someone else murmurs the long genealogy of the eastern cistern's root-wall, tracing a knot back four lifetimes.

I tuck my chin into the curve of my cousin's collar, feeling my pulse slow toward the heavy, measured drum of the sleeping dome, matching the pace of the dormant trees holding the mountain apart around us.

Across the room, the fresh birch heartwood sheds its cold, unburning green onto the wet rim of the empty bowl. My nail-beds still carry a faint, ghostly trace of it where I pressed the living threads into the dead grain—so dim I can only see it if I hold my hand close to my pale eyes.

Beneath us, the bog is rising by a sliver too thin to measure in a single winter. Above us, the mountain gale hammers the sharp rim, shearing flat every twig that tries to lift into the sky. One day, far beyond any hand lying warm in this heap, the floor and the ceiling will meet, and the crack in the mountain will heal completely shut into one solid, dark braid of frozen peat and ancient timber.

I close my eyes, breathing into the wool-soft fold of bark-cloth, listening to the slow, quiet *groan* of the arches holding their burden of ice. I do not need the crack to stay open forever. It is enough to be here in the middle dark, carrying a small cold light from what has finished growing to what is still awake.
