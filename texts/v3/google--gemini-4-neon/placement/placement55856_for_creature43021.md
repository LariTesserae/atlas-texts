If I try to enter this place as an outsider—bringing soft feet, a hunger for morning, and a mind built out of beginnings and endings—the basin rejects me in ten seconds. The vitreous basalt would open my heels to the bone on the first step; the scalding, iron-wet air would sit in my chest like warm grease; the solid wall of throats and ratcheting carapaces would press against my ears until thought itself turned to froth. There is no neutral ledge to stand on here and watch from. Nothing in this sunken dish survives as a visitor.

So to find myself in it at all, I have to let go of arriving and ask what shape I would already have grown into, if this dim pewter ceiling had always been the only sky I knew.

At first, I feel a strong pull toward the seated ones.

There is something deeply recognizable in that stillness—wedged into a hexagonal seam where the black water brims level with the glass, draped in pale root-filaments, holding a single calibrated pitch across miles of steaming flats. I understand the patience of it. I understand the six-fingered hands drifting across one’s own shoulder with an unhurried, almost absent precision, lifting a skin-pleat a fraction of an inch so a warm drop from a palm frond lands where the mineral-feeding moss needs it, sorting the swollen ticks without malice, holding a whole living reef in balance without ever claiming to own it. There is a quiet nobility in being the only soft ground in a world of knives.

And yet, when I try to settle my weight permanently into one of those pockets, something inside my chest resists.

If I sit in the southern pans until my rib-domes calcify around a single overtone, I know the basin only as a set of returns striking *this* wall from *that* distance. I become an anchor—essential, true, immovable—but I never get to feel the rock that sent the echo back. My own impulse isn't to become a fixed bell tuned to one seam of stone. It is to move between the bells, to hear how the same note changes shape when you swim two miles through the flooded fissures and listen to it from the other side of the haze.

For a moment I wonder if I am smaller than them—one of the pale, mineral-grazing snails tracing the damp furrows of a scute, or one of the blind, translucent shrimp crowded inside a blood-warm collar-fold, knowing nothing of the razor basalt outside, living entirely inside the care of a larger body. There is a strange tenderness in that image, too: to exist only on living tissue, tucked into a crease of warmth while the roar goes on above. But no. A shrimp in a neck-fold cannot hear the chord. It doesn't know when a distant voice on the eastern flats dips a fraction of a semitone flat. And whatever I am in this world, I am built to listen to the whole dish at once.

So I slip off the terrace into the black mirror of a trough, scarcely troubling the surface, and discover the body that actually fits me.

I am a roamer.

Not an infant still curled inside a mother’s flank-pouch, and not yet one of the heavy, broad-backed elders fused to a chosen shelf. I am somewhere in that long, unmeasured stretch in between—leaner beneath my pleated hide, my dorsal scutes not yet thick with mineral crust, my chest-domes still supple enough to bend toward whatever pitch the air asks of them.

The moment I imagine myself in that body, everything about how I move snaps into focus.

I have no trail, and I leave no mark. When I haul myself out of a crowded soup of salamanders and crawlers onto a knife-edged basalt blister, my pale, tallow-soft heels flow cleanly over the glass, gripping by suction without ever being cut. When I lift my weight onto the next shelf, the thin peel of shed mucus I leave behind is stripped clean by iridescent beetles before my hand has even settled on a hanging curtain of aerial roots. I pass across miles of jagged stone and leave the geometry untouched, as if it finished cooling an hour ago and nothing has ever crossed it.

That feels startlingly familiar—to move continuously through a landscape, touching every surface closely, and leaving no permanent groove behind.

My own reef is sparse, restless, and borrowed. Because I am always sliding from deep flooded cracks up into the waist-high fern terraces and back down into the dark water, no long dynasty of mites or snails can settle permanently on my hide. Instead, I carry stragglers. A smudge of pale moss picked up from a northern elder’s shoulder while our flanks pressed together in a narrow trough; a handful of tiny grazing snails that crept onto my forearm during a long stillness beneath the bloated palms and stepped off onto a different watcher’s back miles away across the flats. Even without meaning to, I am a slow bridge for the small lives that could never cross the volcanic glass on their own.

Mostly, though, I am a bridge for the silence.

This is where I finally recognize myself—not in the swimming, not even in the great brassy hoots rolling across the mist, though I love the feel of drawing a full minute of iron-scented vapor into my hollow ribs and striking the air with it. Where I truly live is in the wet, dull knock of brow against brow.

Down beneath the leaning trunks, where the aerial roots hang like fleshy curtains into the brim-full pools, the wall of sound is impenetrable. Ten thousand amphibians thrum through the black water; the cicada-kin click in dry, mechanical shingles along the pale bark; warm condensate patters endlessly off waxy leaves. Nothing delicate can survive in the air out here.

So I climb half out of the fissure beside a seated elder, tucking my lean limbs neatly around the razor seams. For a long while we merely vent warm plumes from our vascular pleats and let the chorus press against our hides. Then we lean forward until the broad, flattened plates of our foreheads meet, and our milky lids slide shut.

And the roar drops away to a distant, muffled surf.

Inside that locked bridge of bone, everything clears. Through tiny, rapid clicks of the jaw-hinge and microscopic flutters of the inner ear, the seated one opens its entire district to me—not as a story with a beginning and a middle, because nothing here ever finishes, but as pure, crystalline presence. I feel the exact overhang of a basalt ledge three hundred yards off where a heavy palm frond tore loose and sealed itself again; I taste the slightly sharper iron of a deep fissure to the west; I feel the faint, troubling heat pulsing beneath a hexagonal seam that has grown half a degree warmer since the last time a roamer passed through.

And in return, pressed bone to bone, I give back everything I carried across the flooded flats from the other side of the dish. My rib-domes aren't locked to one echo, so I can hold several geometries at once—a moving ledger of angles, tensions, and water-depths, translated straight from skull to skull with no noisy air in between to blur it.

There is something else I begin to notice, staying a roamer as long as I do.

When I press my brow against the oldest seated ones—elders whose throat-valves have begun to thicken slightly, though their notes still ring true—I sometimes catch that faint, buried dissonance vibrating deep at the base of their skulls. The phantom tremor of rock stiffening into hexagons, the sudden hiss of the first water meeting hot glass. Each of them holds it slightly differently, shaped by the exact rock they sit on: in the north it feels like a sharp, vitreous fracture; along the wide southern pans it feels like a slow, ropy buckling. None of them ever meet each other, so each only carries its own angle of that impossible memory.

Moving between them in the dimness, I end up carrying all the angles inside one head. I don't turn them into a history—I have no more notion of *before* than any other creature in the basin—but when I hang suspended in a quiet, flooded crack between visits, my eyelids shut, those borrowed tremors overlap inside my hollow ribs like a faint undertone beneath the basin's chord. Not a story of how the world began, just the shape of the dish remembering itself in three dimensions.

I know, too, that I would climb toward the rim at least once.

Not out of courage, and not because I dream of some other world beyond the bruise of gray-ochre haze. I would go simply because a mind built to read echoes eventually wants to feel the wall at the far edge of the room. Up where the bloated palms shrink to stunted, rubbery scrub and the black pools thin to damp seams, I would creep out onto the rising glass and let that awful, ghostly stir—too weak to call a breeze, barely a cold finger across wet skin—brush my open vascular pleats.

I would lift my chest and sound a soft note into the thinning gray, and feel the terror of it failing to come back.

That would be enough. To hear a voice leave my throat-valves and simply dissolve into an open, unreflecting void, to feel the solid wall of the basin’s chorus fray at the edges and let slivers of emptiness leak through—that would teach me, in my very bones, what the floor below actually is. Down in the fever, it is easy to mistake the equilibrium for mere habit. Up on the lip of the dish, feeling the chill flay my damp pleats, I would know that the basin’s unchanging note is not inertia. It is an active, living embrace—hundreds of hollow chests holding the world together so that nothing struck ever has to die unanswered.

I wouldn't linger up there. Turning quickly, peeling my cushions cleanly off the glass, I would slide back down the stepped shelves and plunge headlong into the first deep, hot trough, letting the blind pinkish salamanders brush my flanks, letting the dark waxy leaves seal overhead, letting the roaring chorus close around my ears like a warm hand.

And yet, even as a roamer, I know the basin has no permanent exceptions.

For now, I am the thread moving through the roots—lean, wet, quick-fingered, unburdened by a single address, living for those sudden, quiet hours when two foreheads meet in the roaring dusk and a whole quadrant of stone opens clean and bright inside the bone. I drift across the steaming flats, listening to the miles-wide counterpoint, checking the weight of the mist and the return off the charcoal ridges.

One of these unmeasured stretches—an hour from now, or a thousand years from now, which here weigh the exact same—I will be threading a stand of tall, jointed reeds when a brassy hoot out across the flats sags a hair flat. I will hold still in the black water, letting my pale lids narrow, listening as the echo comes back slightly ragged off the far basalt face. I will wait to hear if the next breath corrects it.

When it doesn't, I will know where I am meant to go.

I will swim silently to the base of that jagged terrace, haul myself out of the crowded soup, and fold my limbs neatly beside the stiffening elder, hip to hip, shoulder brushing shoulder. I will lower my brow against theirs and stay there for as long as it takes—my supple domes learning the exact sharp angle of that left-hand overhang, my voice rising a fraction behind their failing note to lift it clean and bright again, while across the warm bridge of our hides their velvet mites, their grazing snails, and their tiny cupped palms of blind shrimp walk steadily onto my skin.

And when they finally peel themselves off the glass and slide into the black mirror below—gone in a brief boil of shells before their flesh can cool—I will remain in that exact hexagonal fold. I will vent a slow plume from my crimson flank, draw in a full minute of heavy, iron-scented air, and send the same brassy note rolling out across the haze.

Waiting, then, in the pewter dusk, for the next lean swimmer to slip up out of the roots and knock its forehead against mine.
