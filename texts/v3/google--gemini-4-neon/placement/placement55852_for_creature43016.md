At first, I would try to convince myself that I belong to the cord.

Not the people who string it—just the thing clinging upside down beneath it, pale green and dry-shelled, passing for a sliver of curled cambium. There is a clean mercy in being an ambusher. No eyes to ache in the sourceless afternoon glare, only milky salt-crusted beads that ask nothing of the light. No need to measure the stretched, dishonest floor, or wonder why a pocket six paces wide swallows thirty strides. Just six long hinged legs ending in forked bristles, gripping the braided bast-fiber, reading the entire buried world as a single steady hum. When a sap-crawler clicks across the hollow pith below, you do not have to guess or hope; the tremor reaches your feet half a beat before the sound hits the air, and you simply let go. Signal, drop, snap. A life without hesitation, lived entirely on the pre-echo.

It is tempting, for a moment.

Then the pressure-surge doubles, the walls flush dark jade, the upper stomata flare open with their collective *shhhhk*, and I realize I could never stay that blind. I would want to know what the light is. I would want to see the bead of sweat tremble on the overhead flute and hear the sharp *tok* ring out from the floor-trough before the drop even lets go. An ambusher only reads the chamber to eat from it. I would be reading it just to hear how badly it disagrees with itself.

So I would have to be one of the wedge-folk—though not the kind a kin-band builds its survival around.

In the sleeves, at first, I would fit well enough. I know the comfort of tight dark spaces, of pressure that holds you in place instead of crushing you, of sleeping curled around a short bar of heartwood braced between knees and breastbone while the outer sheaths slide past with a slow, wet sigh. There is no shouting into a drum there. Just the muffled vascular thrum, warm living tissue against your spine, and the soft, bone-carried language of jaw-hums and knuckle-taps passed shoulder to shoulder in the dark. I would learn the off-beat *thocks* early. I would keep a lump of amber sap tucked behind my ear for the inevitable razor-clean slices of freshly parted ribs, and wear my salt-dusted hide like parchment.

I would not be a bracer, though. My shoulders wouldn’t have the thick, boxy leverage to plant bare heels against one curved wall and hold back tons of healing jade with my collarbones while a kin-mate drove a bone pin home. My frame would be leaner, narrower through the ribs—better suited to slipping through creases that have already begun to pinch shut, worse at holding the world apart by force.

I would become a listener instead. A tuner of the bast-cord.

Every kin-band needs someone with a careful jaw—someone who steps out into the dry trough of an open pocket, crouches by the vascular nub near the floor, and touches the flat edge of their front teeth lightly to the vibrating fiber. It tastes faintly bitter, dry, dusted with chalky salt, and the bass note runs straight up the roots of your molars into the hollows behind your eyes. Through that single pitch you can weigh the whole room without ever looking across its dizzying middle. You feel the lateral groan on each charred billet. You feel the hydraulic river swelling ten paces deep behind the translucent green. You feel whether the room is settled, or whether the pitch is beginning to waver.

Most listeners stop there. The moment that clean, low drone splits into a faint double-note—a slow, wet wobble inside your cheekbones, like two strings pulled almost to the same tension—they pull their teeth back, tap the three-beat warning against the nearest rib, and start scraping the last mineral crust from the floor-troughs. The pocket is getting old. Time to coil the cord, lever out the intact bone wedges, and let the buried green swallow its own blister before the shadows lose their manners and the seams start jumping sideways.

That is where I would begin to go wrong—or where I would begin to become what I actually am in this place.

Because the first time I felt the pitch split in my jaw, I wouldn’t tap the warning right away. I would keep my teeth on the fiber a few seconds too long, just to hear the gap between the two notes widen. And I would lift my narrowed, wet-slitted eyes from the curved perimeter—where every sane wedge-keeper keeps them—and look straight out into the bright, hollow middle of the floor.

That is what would hook me: not the healthy pockets, where the salt dries neat and the walls behave, but the *fraying* ones.

The ones where the sourceless afternoon daylight begins to slant from two conflicting angles at once. Where the shadow of a tall, wind-tossed stalk sways lazily across the smooth jade rib, bending under a breeze from some sun-struck meadow that does not exist here, or stopped existing before the green swallowed the valleys whole. Where a hand-tool left two paces too far from the walls—a leaf-shaped flake of pale grey chert lashed to a curved root—begins to blur at its mineral edges, softening visually as this pure-plant world tries to edit stone back into dry thorn or shell.

Everything down here is made of yielding, wet, relentless healing. Leave a wound alone and it snaps flush without a scar. Leave a body in a sleeve and the sap digests it back into the tide. Leave a rock sitting on bare pith without a warm hand to insist on what it is, and even flint begins to forget itself.

Someone has to stay in the glare long enough to insist.

***

Over a few hundred cycles, that habit shapes a body just as surely as the sleeves do.

I am still wedge-folk—willow-pale, salt-creased, cross-hatched along the forearms with thin glossy seams of amber resin, ears pinned tight against the roar—but I no longer travel inside the tight knot of a single kin-band. I drift through the dark interstitial seams alone, or trail a few pressure-cycles behind a band I know, listening through the pith for the rhythm of a pocket being abandoned.

You can tell when a kin-band is pulling out of an old room even from twenty paces away in the dark. The steady bass hum of their main cord cuts off abruptly. Then come the dull, straining grunts of bone levers working loose the good pins, the rapid double-ring of a river-pebble tapping free a stubborn heartwood wedge, and finally the quick, soft pops of vertical ribs snapping flush behind retreating heels.

I wait through the next surge—let the sixty-second green steam hiss and clear, let the walls bow inward an extra hand’s width as the leftover, pinched-flat splinters take the full weight of the buried mass—and then, in the dry trough that follows, I peel back a vertical seam and slip inside.

A dying pocket does not smell like a fresh one. The sharp cut-celery brightness is gone, replaced by the dry, heated smell of mineral crust and the faint metallic tang of sap that has not yet spilled. The afternoon light is blinding, too crisp, throwing twin shadows off my hunched shoulders in two slightly different directions. What is left of the battered wooden wedges groans continuously, spitting dry little puffs of splintered fiber. Half a beat before a rib cracks beside my knee, the gunshot report slams into my chest from the opposite wall.

My kit is lighter than a bracer’s. A smooth river-pebble worn oily by generations of palms, a slender femur-awl from an elder whose name I never knew, a folded leaf-pouch of raw amber, and three spools of very thin, finely braided bast-cord—too delicate to hold a room’s tension, meant only for listening.

I never walk straight out onto the stretched pith. Not anymore.

My left heel still drags a fraction of a beat behind my stride when I am tired—a faint, heavy numbness that never entirely cleared after the first time I let my bare foot cross that swaying phantom stalk on the floor. And across the heel of my right palm runs a pale, perfectly straight ridge of hard brown amber. That was the first unseated chert blade I ever tried to take. I thought I was quick enough; I grabbed it while the room’s two versions were still sliding past each other. My fingers closed on hot, dry air, the grey flint stayed right where it was, and my shadow’s hand flinched back from a clean wet slice. I didn’t bleed until I was already sealed back inside the dark sleeve, a full pressure-cycle later, wondering why my fist had suddenly gone warm and slick in the pitch black.

You learn, or the dying pockets take pieces of you and leave them in whatever adjacent room they are overlapping with.

Now I work differently.

First, I tie one end of my thin bast-cord around a groaning remnant of wooden wedge in the wall, and the other loop tight around my left wrist. Not to anchor myself against distance—distance here does not obey string—but to keep one unbroken thread of vibration running between the living rib and my own bone. Then I anchor a second thin cord to a severed vascular nub three paces along the curve, stretch it past my chin, and rest my front teeth lightly against it.

The split pitch floods my skull. *Wah—wah—wah—wah.* Fast now. The room is shaking itself apart, preparing to slip its moorings and close.

Out near the barren middle—six paces by sight, twenty-five by the tired stretch of my calves—lies what the kin-band had to leave behind. Maybe it is that leaf-shaped chert knife, with the wet green ribbon of freshly shaved cambium curled permanently beside it, pinned three seconds after the cut. Maybe it is a snapped piece of an ancestor’s shin-bone that worked loose during a surge and rolled too far from the gutter, its dried marrow already beginning to look faintly porous, faintly fibrous, as the green tries to turn bone back into pith.

I step away from the wall, keeping my eyes half-shut against the double glare, letting the thin wrist-cord pay out behind me.

The floor booms hollow under my bare heels like an empty gourd. My elbows still feel the dry heat of the curved ribs close on either side, even as the walls refuse to come nearer. I do not look directly at the knife; looking straight at an unseated stone makes your eyes ache and slide off its smudged grey edges. Instead, I watch my own shadows—both of them—stretching long and neat across the ivory-ochre floor.

The fainter one belongs to the room that is already closing. The darker one belongs to the room that is slipping past it.

When I stop beside the blurred tool, I do not reach down. I hold my calloused right hand hovering over the pith and wait for the darker shadow to move first.

Half a second later—or half a second early, it stops mattering which after enough cycles—the shadow’s wrist bends at a sharp, impossible middle joint and its long dark fingers close around the root-grip of the knife.

That is the seam. That is the only door that matters.

I fold my real fingers cleanly into the exact shape the shadow has already made, matching its lag instead of fighting it, pressing my wrist into the wrongness until my hand and my shadow snap flush together like two halves of a parting rib.

For one heartbeat, the wavering double-note in my wrist-cord collapses into a single, blindingly clear pitch.

Instantly, the smudged edges of the chert flake sharpen to cold, stubborn grey flint. The weight of real rock slams back into my palm—heavy, dense, utterly un-plant, almost shocking in its refusal to yield. Beside my knuckles, the pale green shaving of cambium suddenly remembers the hour it has missed; it curls tight, turns papery brown, and lifts a millimeter on the hot draft.

I do not linger. I pull my hand back straight along the path the shadow took, tuck the knife hard against my bare ribs where my skin can keep insisting on what it is, and walk backward along my humming wrist-cord until my spine hits the curved jade wall.

Sometimes, if the hydraulic thump hasn’t begun to double yet, I take five heartbeats before I peel the seam open and go.

Just five heartbeats, crouched in the gutter with my knuckles pressed to the salt-crusted pith, watching the swaying shadow of that tall stalk on the far wall. I never touch it. I don’t even know what a stalk like that would grow out of, if there is any floor in the universe that isn’t living vegetable mass—whether stone and dirt were once vast enough to lie flat under an open sky instead of migrating out of swollen seams like splinters from a palm. I only know that whatever moves that shadow does not surge, does not boom, does not hiss through stomata. It pushes lazily, without tearing anything, and then lets go.

Then the deep thump behind the wall doubles its speed. The jade flushes dark. One of the leftover wedges shears with a rifle-shot crack whose echo hit my chest half a beat ago.

I untie my thin cord, reel it fast over my thumb, and peel back the vertical rib. I slip backward into the dark crease, and the edges snap flush behind me with a wet, forgiving *pop*.

Instantly, the impossible afternoon is gone.

The silence of the sleeves feels thick as sap after that glare—no roar, no stretched canyon echoes, just the deep visceral pulse of the buried green sliding past itself in the warm dark. Inside the pocket I just left, I can feel through the pith as the last crushed splinters give way and the curved walls seal seamlessly shut, swallowing the light, the phantom stalk, and the empty space where a stone knife used to lie.

I hold the chert blade flat against my breastbone with one hand, feeling its cold mineral edge stay sharp against my palm, and begin the long, dark crawl back toward the kin-band’s new sleeve.

They will hear me coming long before I reach them—three soft, evenly spaced knuckle-taps against the sliding sheath, struck cleanly off the vascular pulse. They will make room in the warm fold, hand me a stiff salty strip of dried vein-runner, and take the recovered flint without much ceremony, passing it hand to hand in the pitch dark so every calloused palm can verify its hardness. Maybe they will let me sleep pressed against their shoulders for a cycle or two, curled around my breath-peg, before the itch of the splitting pitch draws me back toward the next aging seam.

And when my joints finally lock stiff with mineral salt, and my front teeth wear too smooth to tell a clean bass note from a wavering double-pitch—when my lagged left heel slows me just enough for a closing wall to catch me—I know what will happen to me, and I would not ask for anything else.

They will strip me cleanly in the dry trough of a quiet pocket, give the soft parts back to the wet seams, and keep what is hardest. Scraped smooth with a flint flake I probably pulled out of a dying room, dried in the crisp sourceless light, lashed with bast-sinew, my femurs and forearms will be hammered sideways into a fresh vertical tear.

Groaning under tons of living green. Refusing to yield. Holding open a narrow blister of impossible afternoon light just long enough for someone else to scrape the salt, press their teeth to the humming cord, and listen for the moment the pitch begins to split.
