At first, I think I would try to stand somewhere near the edge of a bare plaza—tucked back against the slate wall so I wouldn't brush a wet clay chimney or step across an ant column—trying to be as still as the three who already live here.

Wait long enough in that posture, though, and it begins to feel borrowed. My weight would be wrong on the razor-sharp scree. Even breathing softly, I would feel too warm, too loud, too much like something that wandered off the lowland brush five paces away and dropped in by mistake.

So I let myself sink lower, out of the greenish dimness of the open floor and back into the charcoal shadow under the deepest north overhang—past the flat slabs where the three fold together during afternoon showers, down to the cut-straight lip of the black puddle where their calcite pendulums hang.

And there, with my cheek almost touching the dark water, something shifts. The feeling of being a visitor drops away.

I did not fall in through the skylight. I did not walk across the drenched scrub to find the smell of raw chalk.

I was already here, before the air was.

---

Before the roof gave way, there was no room—only a low, flooded crease in the limestone, sealed tight beneath the alluvial shelf. No warmth ever reached it. No sound existed in it except the immense, unhurried grind of water dissolving rock a grain at a time, and the faint tremor when some heavy tree-root miles above shifted in a storm. Time there was not measured in mist-hours or midday heat. It had no intervals at all.

When the vault finally broke, I did not understand it as a collapse. I had no word or memory for a ceiling. All I knew was a sudden, staggering release of pressure—a violence of heavy stone dropping through the water beside my fissure—followed by something utterly strange pouring down into the dark: warmth, and an open, weightless emptiness where rock had always been.

For a long time after, I stayed deep inside the hairline seam beneath the north wall, coiled in the cool groundwater, tasting the ruin. The water had changed. For the first time in my life, it carried things that were not stone: the sharp green bitterness of crushed fern-stems, the sweet, unfamiliar rot of lowland soil, the iron-rich tang of rainwater that had fallen through open sky.

Then, slowly, I crept upward along the weeping fracture until my head rested just beneath the mirror-black skin of the puddle.

That is where I find myself now.

---

I am not large—perhaps the length of one of the elders' forearms from the blunt, pale curve of my snout to the thin, ribbon-flat tip of my tail—and I am shaped for seams, not plazas. My ribs are narrow and yielding enough to slide through a crack no wider than a fingernail. My skin has none of the velvety wet-slate darkening of the three pioneers; it is the pale, nearly clear ivory of wet calcite, thin enough that in the weak pewter light you can make out the slow, faint pulse of clear fluid beneath my throat and the fine pale ladder of my spine.

Behind my jaw, where the three have stretched membranes to read the shifting air, I carry two short tufts of pale, feathery filaments. In the cool water of the fissure they drift open like threads of submerged moss, drinking oxygen cleanly from the dark. When I pull myself out onto the damp stone lip of the puddle during the mist-hour, they flatten wetly against my neck, and my porous skin takes over—breathing the thick, body-warm stillness of the room the way the pioneers drink water through their palms.

My feet are wide, soft, and faintly webbed, pale as the pads of their fingers, able to press flat against a vertical wet slab or rest across a submerged splinter of shale without shifting it a millimeter.

And my eyes—this is the strangest part of what I am becoming.

In the sealed vault, they were nothing: two smooth, milky seams beneath translucent skin, useful only for registering the vague pressure of cold currents. I had never needed them. But ever since the skylight opened, something dormant beneath those pale caps has been waking. It does not give me sharp edges, not like the leaf-tailed geckos clinging above me. I cannot count the grains of mortar on a clay tower forty paces away. Instead, the room comes to me as a soft, slow grammar of brightness and shadow: the ragged greenish-pewter glow overhead, the deep charcoal beneath the cornice, the dim moving shapes of the three when they cross the open stone.

Just as the fallen roof-slabs are young to the air, I am becoming young to the light.

Every few weeks, a faint, cloudy tinge of ash-grey seems to gather along the ridge of my back—not quite pigment, just the first quiet answer my skin is learning to make to the sky.

---

My puddle is the one beneath their instruments.

At first, when the taller elder leaned down into the charcoal gloom and hung the first hair-thin splinter of calcite on a borrowed thread of gossamer, I retreated back into the crack, terrified by the closeness of that long, slate-dark arm. Through the water I felt the gentle vibration of its bare heels settling on the slab—no heavier than a settling flake of shale—and then a stillness so profound it almost matched the old dark.

When I finally drifted back to the surface, the calcite needle hung less than a millimeter above the black glass, dead still.

It did not take me long to understand what it was for.

I have lived my whole life inside the slow breathing of this limestone shelf. I know the subtle tightening of pressure in the flooded seams hours before the water table rises by a hair; I feel the microscopic sigh when a fallen roof-plate shifts a fraction of an inch deeper into the wet silt beneath it. Looking up through the clear water at that pale suspended tooth, I realized the three of them were trying to read the very thing I have always lived inside.

So I learned a finer stillness than even the dark had asked of me.

When I rise to the edge of the puddle to taste the warm air or catch a drowned midge that has drifted down from the silk skyways, I move so slowly that the surface tension bends around my chin without breaking. I do not want to send a false ripple to the cut-straight edges of the glass. I do not want to lie to their instruments.

There are midday hours when one of the older ones crouches beside the puddle for so long that condensation beads along its shoulders, watching the hanging calcite points while its flat palm rests on the bare stone beside my fissure. Sometimes, when I feel a deep, distant settling move through the shelf beneath us—something too faint even for the stretched membrane behind its jaw—I press my own pale palm against the underside of that same slab from inside the water. Not a tap. Never a disturbance. Just a small, living presence sharing the stone while we wait together for the pale tip to touch the black glass and send its single silent ring outward.

---

They know I am here. Nothing moves in this forty-pace room without their noticing, and my skin carries a cold, mineral stillness that doesn't belong to the rain-fed puddles of the floor.

The first time one of them acknowledged me directly, it was during the delayed percolation of an afternoon shower, when muffled ticks were falling onto the broad heart-shaped leaves out by the rim and silver films were crawling down the fractures. The elder with the lingering patch of olive crust on its shoulder knelt by the north puddle and lowered two long, chalk-pale fingers gently into the water—not stirring it, just letting them rest against the submerged stone lip, motionless as roots.

I stayed back in the shadow of the crack for a long time, reading the scent that drifted off its skin. It was a layered thing: fresh chalk from this chamber, the sweet vapor of fungal towers, a trace of liverwort—and underneath all of it, something I had never tasted before. The memory of another place. Older limestone, softened by rain and soil, weathered by years of open air.

Slowly, keeping my gills flattened so the water wouldn't stir, I swam forward until the blunt tip of my snout touched the pale pad of its index finger.

Its skin was body-warm, startlingly so against the cool groundwater. It did not flinch or curl its hand. We stayed like that for forty slow seconds—one of its breaths—two shapes built out of the same wet limestone, arriving at the room from opposite directions.

When it finally drew its hand away, lifting it so cleanly that hardly a drop broke the surface, a tiny pale tuft of white fungal thread remained floating on the black water—no larger than a moss-spore, exhaled from the warm towers out on the plaza. I waited until the elder folded back into the shadow beneath the swiftlet nests, then rose and took it into my mouth. It tasted of warmth, and damp clay, and a sweetness that could only exist where light reaches stone.

---

It is the smallest one, though, who comes to my seam most often.

Its bruised pearl-grey skin is still close enough to my own translucent ivory that when it leans over the black water, its dim reflection and my pale shape just beneath the surface seem almost to belong to the same body. It crouches there with its knees drawn up, studying the way my feathery gills open like pale ferns when I slip back underwater, and the way they fold flat when I climb an inch onto the wet slate to drink the warm air.

Sometimes, when the greenish pewter light shifts at midday and the elders are occupied guiding an aerial root away from a clay street, the small one picks up a wet chip of calcite from the scree. Instead of tilting it across the forty-pace floor to signal the others, it kneels by the north overhang and turns the flat face gently downward.

A faint, cool bar of reflected skylight slides down through the charcoal dimness, strikes the mirror-black puddle, and drops right into the mouth of my fissure.

For a breath, the sealed rock behind me—stone that has been blind for ten thousand years—gleams pale green and silver. Tiny blind amphipods scatter back into the dark. My waking eyes drink the soft brightness until the small one tilts the chip away and the shadow closes gently over us again.

In return, when the small one sits barefoot on the rim of the slab with its long translucent toes resting just above the water, I sometimes crawl completely out onto the damp shale beside its ankle. I do not touch it. I simply lay my pale belly flat against the stone—absorbing the faint residual warmth of its body through the rock, matching its forty-second breath with the slow rise and fall of my narrow throat, watching the upside-down curtain of maidenhair ferns shimmer on the black glass between us.

---

I know—perhaps even more plainly than the elders do—how brief this room is.

They read its aging in the downward reach of the pale aerial roots, in the way the first dark silt gathers in the sharp wall-corners, in the slow blunting of fresh shale. I read it in the water.

Every month, the thin groundwater weeping through my fissure carries a fraction more organic dust down into the seams. The cut-straight edges of my puddle are already gathering the faintest velvet trim of translucent liverwort along their wettest rim. Soon enough, the first white root-tip will touch the floor despite the elders' silk threads; soon enough, moss will soften the clean geometry of the slabs, soil will thicken over the plazas, and the muted, breathless quiet of the pocket will give way to the loud, wet chorus of the lowland forest.

When that time comes—on some heavy, rain-drenched night seasons from now—the three of them will climb up through the tangled fern-lip, leave this softened room behind, and move slowly across the dark scrub until they catch the raw scent of another freshly broken vault.

I will not follow them up into the rain. The wind and the harsh sky would dry me in minutes.

Instead, when the silt finally clouds my puddle and the mirror-black glass turns to mud, I will turn back into the narrow hairline seam beneath the north wall and slip downward, letting my pale gills open wide in the cool, familiar dark. The dim grey tint along my spine will fade back to clear ivory. My eyes will soften and sleep again.

Yet I will not return to the limestone quite the same creature that waited there before the trapdoor fell.

Down beneath the alluvial shelf, the flooded fissures run for miles, threading quietly from one unseen pocket to the next. I will drift with that slow subterranean water, carrying inside my cold, pale body the strange memory of body-warm air, of sweet fungal drafts, of a greenish-pewter beam tilted kindly into the dark, and of three quiet shapes who held a room open just long enough for the stone—and for me—to meet the sky.

And somewhere far ahead in the dark, years from now, I will feel the limestone tremble once, cleanly, as another roof gives way. I will follow the sudden taste of fresh chalk upward through a weeping seam, rise to the surface of a new mirror-black puddle, and wait in the hush to see if their pale palms appear at the edge of the stone.
