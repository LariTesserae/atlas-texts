If I try to place myself here too quickly, I reach for the obvious centers.

My first instinct is to put myself inside one of the shaded dry-stone halls in the middle of the plateau, stretched out on a plaited sedge mat over an uncovered conduit—a listener at the hub of things, reading the three-quick, two-slow ticks of water-talk as they pass between precincts, nudging a pegged baffle to send an answer back upstream. Or else I imagine myself kneeling at the exact midpoint of a flooded causeway between two quarreling districts, palms submerged in the runnel between us, patiently tuning a tightened sluice back into accord.

Both of those feel close, and neither quite settles level.

Deep in the old interior precincts, the water has already been measured ten times over before it reaches your doorway. Every drop has passed through a dozen notched basalt weirs, been steadied by a dozen pairs of hands, been folded into the great, unbroken grammar of the city. Sitting there, I would spend my whole life inside a conversation that already knows its own cadence. And if I look the other way—all the way out to the stark southern promontories where the evening watchers kneel with their dark backs turned permanently to the city, facing the white cloud-floor in solemn vigilance—that does not quite fit me either. Their silence holds the world below at arm’s length, as something only to be endured and buried when it breaks across the glass.

So I keep walking south along the last dead-straight causeway, past the outermost walled precinct, until the neat rectangular cisterns end and the basalt stops remembering it was ever quarried. Here the tall glossy sedges thin out. The flat benches tilt slightly upward toward the lip of the plateau, fracturing into dark, freshly broken teeth that hang straight out over the level sea of cloud.

That is where my weight finally slows and stops.

Just inland of those sharp black teeth—not out on the exposed cliff-edge with the watchers, and not safely back inside the walls of the lowest district—there has to be a seam. The warm moist updraft rising off the cloud deck condenses continuously along the jagged rim, beading into silver droplets that detach and slip inward down the angular rock to feed the basin’s quiet loop. Before that water can enter the city’s conduits, something has to catch it.

Because cloud-drip does not arrive in the city’s rhythm.

Far beneath the white floor, unseen weather turns over in the valleys. Nothing climbs up to the plateau except warmth and vapor, yet when a storm moves through the lowlands miles below, the updraft quickens by half a breath. The droplets along the sharp volcanic teeth swell and fall faster—a scattered, irregular patter with no peg-interval behind it, no intention, no poise. Left alone, that unmeasured shiver would slide straight down into the southernmost listening-panes, muddying the glass and sending false tremors up the clay-lined runnels until half a quadrant knelt beside its weirs wondering whether the bedrock was about to shift.

I would be the one who tends the catch-basin at that seam.

---

My precinct is scarcely a precinct at all—not a walled cluster of halls, just a single low crescent of dry-laid basalt curving inland from a notch in the southern rim, holding a broad, ankle-deep pan of raw bedrock. On the southern side, there is no wall higher than a knee, only a rough curb set along the natural fracture so the mist-water cannot slip back off the cliff into the clouds. Behind me to the north stands one narrow dry-stone shelter roofed with overlapping basalt slabs, its doorway facing inland, its reed floor laid over the single runnel that leaves my basin and runs dead-straight across three hundred paces of open flooded flat toward the first southern district.

I am one of the plateau’s own—tall, narrow through the ribs and hips, long flat soles that never step off the clean rock into silt, wide dark eyes washed every minute or two by a pale, slow-gliding membrane. When I step out of the velvet dimness of my shelter in the mild mid-morning sun, my shoulders carry the same pale ash-grey bloom as anyone inland, and darken to glazed charcoal-black the moment I wade into the pan.

Yet living here changes a body by small, quiet degrees.

Inland, the water weeps out of young volcanic seams, rich with dissolved silica. Over decades, even with careful moss-scrubbing, that mineral settles deeper into the fluted grooves of a person’s hide and stiffens their knuckles and knees until they narrow their life to a single sunlit bench. The water in my basin has never touched deep rock. It is lifted out of the clouds as vapor and given back to the stone as nearly pure dew. Because I wade in it from dawn to evening, and because the faint warm updraft beads along my back and brow-ridge even when I stand on the dry curb, the pale contour lines never build thickly along my ribs. My hide stays darker, more supple, the olive-grey seams along my inner wrists curiously soft, as if the mineral weight of the plateau only half-claims me.

Even the moss here is different—paler than the deep emerald cushions inland, fine-haired and quick to drink, upholstering the three low ribs of uncarved basalt I have laid loosely across the floor of my pan.

My work is simple, and it never finishes.

All day, I move slowly through the shin-deep basin with a peeled hardwood paddle and bundles of cured rushes, listening through my soles and palms to the two different worlds meeting at my knees. Behind me, coming softly upstream through the northern runnel, is the faint pulse of the city—so distant out here that the cobble-tapping on the upper escarpments is scarcely more than a phantom hum in the arches of my feet, and the deep ringing of a central cistern-slab arrives a full heartbeat late, round and fading, with nowhere left to go before the drop-off. Ahead of me, slipping down the jagged teeth, is the erratic silver scatter of the cloud-drip.

Between them, I tune the baffles. I lay fresh wet sedge-stems across the stepped stone ribs, raise or lower the water-swollen hardwood sluice at my northern weir by the thickness of a fingernail, and comb out the pale moss so it drinks the irregular impact of the droplets without a ripple. What arrives from the rim as weather leaves my northern lip as a silent, millimeter-thin glaze—calm enough to carry a message, level enough to keep the tide marks fixed across the lowest terraces.

I am a quiet hinge. I translate what has no grammar into something the city can bear.

---

I am not an exile, though days pass without another silhouette crossing the causeway toward me. On the plateau, three hundred paces of open water is not distance; it is courtesy.

Most mornings, I tip the thin wooden peg at my northern weir to send a brief interval down the runnel: two slow ticks, a pause, one quick—*the cloud floor is sitting low and still; expect a hairline less by midday*. A long minute later, a soft pressure-pulse nudges back against my submerged wrist from the receiving hall of the southern precinct: *heard; we will hold the eastern sluice*. When I need dried algae-sheets, or fresh bundles of split sedge-fiber, or a replacement hardwood wedge, I tap out the slow cadence of a request, and the next afternoon a dark, narrow figure walks out across the mirror-flat causeway carrying a woven rush-frame balanced lightly on one shoulder, sets it on the dry flagstone beside my weir, rests a palm in the water beside mine for a quiet half-minute to share the warmth of the afternoon, and walks back into their own reflection.

Now and then, an elder makes the long crossing from the upper quadrants.

It takes them two or three mild days, resting at cisterns along the way, their strides shortened by decades of volcanic silica settling into their joints. When one reaches the outer causeway, I feel their slow, careful footfalls half an hour before I raise my head to see their chalk-lined shoulders against the pale sky. I clear the broadest sun-warmed slab beside my inner weir, heap wet pale moss along its lip, and open the runnel-baffle slightly so the cloud-water pools deeper and warmer against the step.

They do not speak—none of us do in the air. They simply step down into the soft, mineral-poor water, settle slowly until it laps against their narrow ribs, and close their dark eyes. After a while I kneel behind them in the pan, gather a handful of cool wet moss and a pinch of fine silt from the outer notch, and work slowly along the fluted grooves of their back and shoulders, easing away the chalky maps left by fifty years of inland seepage. Sometimes, when the sun drops low and the stone holds its steady afternoon warmth, they lean their shoulder lightly back against my collarbone and let out a low, closed-mouth hum—felt only from bone to bone, carrying no further than a palm’s breadth—and I answer it at the same pitch until both our ribs vibrate together like a submerged slab struck very gently from far away.

In those hours I feel the whole city resting behind me, patient and immense. And when the young rock groans beneath us once every few decades—those three brief heartbeats when every mirror-flat basin shivers into crossed rings of light and the southern rim tilts by the width of a thumb—the builders do not forget this edge. By the next morning, four or five tall charcoal figures are walking out down the causeway with hardwood levers and coiled sedge-rope. Together, without a sound in the air, we lift the loose flags of my crescent curb stone by stone, feel the new slope of the black bedrock with wet palms, and lay the basin level again so the cloud-loop does not break.

---

Still, it is the rarer afternoons that tell me most clearly why I belong here and nowhere else.

Once in many years—measured not in winters, since no winter ever climbs above the clouds, only in cycles of unfurling tree-fern fronds—the warm updraft thickens and carries something up over the black teeth that does not belong to stone or water.

It always announces itself to my palms before my eyes find it. A sudden, jagged splash in the outer half of the pan, followed by ripples so sharp and chaotic they feel like splintered glass against my listening-slits.

My first movement is pure reflex, learned from a lifetime of tending the seam: I step swiftly to the northern weir and press the hardwood sluice home with the heel of my palm, sealing the basin shut. No tremor of this must escape up the runnel into the listening-panes of the southern quadrant. Whatever has arrived from beneath the white floor stays inside my crescent curb alone.

Then I turn back.

On the shallow wet stone near the cliff-teeth lies a small, storm-flung thing—a moth with tattered wings beating blindly against the water-film, or a tiny bird dropped out of some violent sky below, its breast heaving in the thin highland air.

The watchers on the outer promontories stand back on the high dry ledges when this happens, watching in motionless dread until the noise stops. I understand that dread; it rises in my own throat too. Everything about the creature is an obscenity of speed and heat. Its breath is dry and frantic. Its heart hammers against the wet basalt at a tempo ten times quicker than the fastest drop-interval, sending harsh, meaningless triangles of light racing across the calm pane. Even its sudden soft fluttering in the air strikes my taut ear-slits like staring straight into midday glare.

Yet I do not retreat up onto the dry curb.

I kneel in the shallow water two paces away, close enough to feel the full violence of its tremor, and lay both pale inner wrists flat beneath the surface. I let my wet inner membranes glide slowly across my eyes. If the plateau’s balance means anything, it cannot only hold what is already still; somewhere along the rim, there has to be a basin wide enough to receive a storm without breaking, and someone willing to stay knelt inside the disturbance until it finishes spending itself.

So I listen. I let that alien, terrified cadence run up my forearms and into my collarbone—a language of wings and blood and short, burning lives that I will never comprehend, yet refuse to leave entirely unanswered on bare stone. I hold myself as steady as a notched weir, absorbing the jagged ripples against my shins and palms so the water around the edges of the pan stays calm.

Slowly, the intervals between its tremors widen. The wild hammering against the basalt stumbles, softens, pauses, strikes once more, and gives way to the quiet, patient tick of condensation slipping off the rock teeth.

Only when the surface returns to glass do I rise. I fetch a broad woven rush-frame from my shelter, slide it gently beneath the small, impossibly light weight, and lift it clear of the water. Before I turn inland toward the upper fern-beds where the wet roots will fold its brief heat back into green silence, I pause for one breath and let the tip of a single wet finger brush the edge of a wing—feeling, with a quiet, wordless wonder, the dry, hollow architecture of something made to fight the sky rather than rest inside the grain of the earth.

---

By evening, when I return from the fern-beds, the low sun has turned the cloud sea beneath the southern lip to the pale gold of wet rushes.

I step back down into my catch-basin, ease the hardwood sluice at the northern weir upward by the width of a sedge-blade, and feel the soft cloud-water resume its silent, hairline slip toward the city. Behind me, three hundred paces across the mirror-flat expanse, the tall dark walls of the southernmost precinct sit cleanly inside their own reflection. Ahead of me, the white floor glows and breathes upward, beading in bright silver along the sharp black teeth and down the fluted grooves of my shoulders.

I kneel at the curb with one palm resting in the unmeasured water of the rim and the other touching the smooth lip of the outgoing runnel.

If a strange, uneven cadence ever comes trembling down the conduits from far inland—the heavy, dry-booted weight of an outsider standing in the middle of a wide stone plaza, unsure whether they are an intrusion or a guest—I will be the last one on the plateau to feel it arrive through the stone. And I will stay very still right here at the edge of the clouds, holding the water level, waiting to feel whether the footsteps shift into a kneel.
