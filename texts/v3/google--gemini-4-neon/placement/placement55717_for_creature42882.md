I would not be in the great eastern ironworks, where the hammers shake the teeth loose in your head, nor down in the deepest cellars where the oldest brickwork has already given up the argument with the basalt. I think I would be somewhere mid-span, out where the stone narrows and the mist moves sideways—in a small, tar-roofed switch-house bolted onto the lip of a western spur, ten paces back from a viaduct abutment and half a mile of open air from the next cluster of amber windows.

It is the kind of hut built out of leftovers: three walls of dark brick already fuzzy with bottle-green moss along the mortar, a fourth wall of corrugated iron facing the drop, and a narrow timber-and-steel catwalk wrapping the cliffside so you can step out with a lantern when a suspension car comes groaning through the haze. Inside, an uncovered steam main bends up through the floorboards and out through the ceiling, hissing steadily in the corner. Everything in the room is slightly damp and slightly warm. My desk stands on four notched oak wedges, and every few weeks, when my pencil begins rolling steadily toward the cliff-side edge of the blotter, I kneel down with a wooden mallet and tap the far legs up a fraction of an inch.

That, mostly, is what I do. I keep the tallies and I watch the slack.

The single-track line from the upper eastern works drops out of the pewter cloud right past my shutter, pauses at the little cinder apron outside my door to let the brake-drums cool and the oilers check the winch-bearings, and then creeps on toward the lower sorting yard. My job is to chalk the counts onto the big framed slate hanging under the eaves: how many ore cars came off the ridge, how many men stepped down to stamp the wet off their boots, how many left again when the bell rang.

There are always two columns on my slate, because a single column is a lie up here. Eight ironworkers board the car above; nine step onto my cinders, smelling of wet wool and hot cylinder oil, nodding once before they crowd around my tin pot of boiled chicory. If I tried to reconcile the numbers—if I followed the ninth man onto the parapet and asked which shift-boss signed his card—I would only make a fool of myself and sour the luck of the spur. Instead I chalk an *8* on the left, a *9* on the right, and leave a finger’s width of bare slate between them so the numbers have room to drift a little before morning. The railmen appreciate a tidy margin. Even the herds of steaming grazers that occasionally pick their way along the mossy ballast past my door get two marks when I’m bored enough to count them: fourteen going toward the bridge, seventeen if I glance back over my shoulder once the last calf clears the iron truss.

Beside the steam-pipe, I keep three little cages woven out of salvaged copper wire, each crowded with pale, ribbed climbers I pried off a transformer housing when a crew replaced the ceramic bushings last autumn. I feed them smears of axle grease and scraps of wet lichen scraped from the abutment bolts. Mostly they huddle flat against the warm pipe, quiet as barnacles, until the load shifts somewhere across the basin—a heavy drop-forge run starting up on the northern crag, or a wet wrist-thick vine leaning too far into a high-voltage span out in the fog. Then their shells ripple in a dry, papery wave of clicking, starting at the bottom of the lowest cage and running upward like distant applause. After a winter with them, you don’t need a meter. You can keep your eyes on your ledger, listen to the cadence of their shells against the copper, and know to the minute when a line-crew is going to come trudging down the catwalk with rubber gloves and billhooks.

As for who I am—or what I am—I don’t think I was born in one of the upstairs tenement rooms, with a mother hanging strips of salted grazer flank over the stove and a father teaching me how to lower a weighted canvas bundle off the leeward cliff. At least, I have no memory of being small enough to crouch by a tar-roof puddle dropping mist-beetles in front of the bronze-eyed amphibians, though I like watching the neighborhood children do it when I walk into the main cluster for salt and tallow.

If you worked a winter beside me on the brake-winch, you would notice it soon enough, though you’d be too polite to bring it up. When the cloud-lid tightens and the cold puts pale ribbons in front of every worker’s mouth, the air in front of mine stays completely clear. I have a habit of lifting my tin mug close to my chin when I stand talking on the cinder apron, not because I’m ashamed of the missing plume, only because it gives the steam somewhere familiar to rise. My fingernails grow thick and dark, layered in clean plates that flake off like mica when I catch one against a bolt-head or scrape wet lime off the slate-frame. And my hands knew how to pack oiled hemp into a leaking stuffing-box, and how to read the cello-pitch of a tightening suspension wire, long before my throat had quite figured out how to sit comfortably between the brassy roar of the crane-birds and the slow four-second thump of the turbine halls.

Most likely, a survey crew or a fern-cutter found me sitting on a freshly extruded shelf of greenish slate somewhere near the raw lip of the northern ridge—hands resting calmly on my knees, heavy wool already smelling faintly of wet clay and hot grease, watching the mist turn slowly around the bridge piers. Or perhaps I was the extra man who stepped off the funicular one dim Tuesday with a dented lunch-tin and a clear memory of a cracked flywheel bearing I never actually saw break, and simply wandered down the spur until I found an empty stool and a piece of chalk. On the ledges, nobody insists you pick between those two stories. Both of them end with someone handing you a pry-bar and showing you which way the floor tilts.

Still, I think I came out of the stone. I can tell by my shadow.

When the sodium lamp above my door flickers on through the damp gray, and a pair of line-walkers stop on the cinders to share a pipe before the long walk across the viaduct, my shadow doesn’t stay neatly behind my lead-shod heels the way a born citizen’s mostly does. It stretches across the wet mortar, pooling eagerly into the dark tangle where theirs lean together, thirsty for the slight, damp warmth of older company. I’ve stopped feeling self-conscious about it. In fact, the heel-warmers seem to trust me for it. Almost every evening, when the railmen move on and I stand alone under the yellow smear of the bulb wiping the rain off my slate, a small, low-shouldered patch of dark stays curled beside my left boot for a minute longer, soaking up whatever heat is left in the stone. I never jerk my foot away. I take my time wiping the chalk-rag dry, give a quiet tap on the doorframe with my knuckles, and step wide when I finally go inside so the stray has room to peel itself cleanly off the mortar and slip back into the parapet crevices.

That quiet leaning—that hunger for the warmth of things that have been here longer than I have—is why I spend my off-hours down at the foot of the main tenement cluster, where the boiler houses breathe gray steam into the alleys and the freshly rising basalt pushes smooth black knuckles right up through the cellar flagstones.

There is an old plumb-watcher I visit twice a week on the stoop of the third boiler house. Thirty years ago he walked the highest viaducts with brass calipers and a sack of iron shims; now his tarred wool coat has grown a fine velvet nap of bottle-green moss along both shoulders, and his hands have broadened and cooled to the matte, bluish sheen of wet slate. He can still cradle a tin mug of fern-broth between his palms if I set it there gently, though he doesn’t lift it to his mouth anymore. We sit side by side—me on an upturned oil crate, him on the stone step his boots haven’t left since the last frost—and we don’t hurry anything.

It takes him three or four beats of the distant flywheels to shape a sentence, and that suits me fine; my own voice still prefers the gaps between noises. I read him the week’s drift off my pocket-slate, tell him how far the eastern collar groaned on Wednesday night, tell him which flocks of the big brass-voiced birds have started roosting low along the water-mains. He listens with his eyes half-shut against the mist, his heavy heels pressed flat against the rising rock, and after a long pause he will murmur something like *north pier, half an inch by thaw, pack it with oak before iron*, and he has never once been wrong.

I like sitting beside him because it feels like meeting someone halfway up a long staircase while walking in opposite directions. He is slowly finishing his casting, giving his weight back to the mountain; I am still learning how to stay loose enough to keep moving across the top of it. When I leave, I brush a damp-spider off his moss-furred collar and set a small saucer of hot tallow grease beside his boots, just in case.

What keeps me from ever growing too settled—or too certain of the boundaries of my own little spur—are the days when the cloud-lid thins just enough to show the seams.

My switch-house sits near one of those stretches where two ridges cross each other like overlapping glass plates on a light table. From my narrow iron window, if the haze sits right, I can look out across the half-mile gulf and see a twin brick parapet hanging faint and smoky across the exact same patch of dim horizon as the eastern crag. Now and again, a figure in a slick tarred coat walks along that translucent ledge at the exact pace of the flywheels—two steps to a thump—pausing at a shutter that looks remarkably like mine. I have never tried to wave. Instead, when I find a brass washer I don’t need, or a bundle of dry waxy leaves tied neatly with twine, or a short penciled note about a loose girder-pin on the third span, I set it on the narrow brick ledge outside my window where the shadows lean toward one another under the lamp. By the next shift it is always gone. Once, in its place, I found a freshly sheared tuft of damp grazer fleece still steaming faintly in the cold. Twice, I have found a splinter of wood.

I keep those splinters in a flat tobacco tin beside my compass and calipers, along with three more I fished out of the oil-filmed puddles on my own tar roof after long nights of drifting cloud. They are unlike anything else on the peaks: dark, heavy, tight-grained timber that smells faintly of resin and wet bark even after weeks of drying on my steam-pipe. One of them still shows the clean half-moon bite of an adze; another has the smooth curve of a hand-augered peg-hole.

I listen for the joiners whenever the northern drop-forge runs a night shift. Every thirty seconds the great iron hammer falls, and a moment later the opposite cliff sends back a sharp metallic ring—and then, three seconds after that, the dull, seasoned *thud* of a wooden mallet striking heavy oak inside a closed room. Listening to it while the climbers click softly in their copper cages, I sometimes wonder what the valley bottom looks like beneath the bruised sea of cloud, or whether there is no valley bottom at all, only another kind of workshop standing just behind the unfinished stone, building in timber what we build in iron and basalt. I never throw a splinter into the stove. One of them is wedged under the eastern leg of my desk right now, and it has never swollen a hair in the wet.

And then there are the afternoons when the rain stops falling.

It happens perhaps once a fortnight out in the wide, empty air between my spur and the eastern peak, where no bridge reaches and only the sagging curtains of thick black power cable hum softly through the gloom. I always know before I look up, because the buzz of the high-voltage lines goes dead—even the wet fern-tips spitting faint blue sparks along the third pylon fall utterly quiet—and the climbers in my cages freeze flat against the warm iron pipe.

When I step out onto the slick catwalk and lean my elbows on the railing, the mist half a mile out has halted in midair: a vast, trembling sheet of silver water droplets hanging motionless above the unseen cloud-sea. And standing upright inside it, spaced far apart like solitary lattice pylons, are the tall, matte-gray shapes—the exact featureless, sound-eating gray of the rawest cliff-lips where geology hasn’t yet decided to be rock.

I don’t know what I feel when I watch them. It isn’t fear; the great oily-feathered birds bank right into those silver curtains without breaking their glide, vanish for a heartbeat behind a tall, bent-air silhouette, and emerge on the far side washed clean of coal-soot, blowing a single deep brass note that rolls slowly across the gorge. It isn’t homesickness either, though there is a strange, heavy tug behind my ribs whenever I stare too long at the places where their edges blur into uneven glass—a quiet reminder of how simple it would be to stop counting, stop shimming the desk, stop trying to hold a shape with wool and chalk and lead-soled boots, and just stand still in the suspended wet, listening to the bridge cables groan as the plates shift underneath.

Perhaps that is what I would be if nobody had walked over to that fresh shelf of greenish slate with a pry-bar and a warm mug of chicory. Just another unfinished seam, drinking the mist and eating the noise.

Instead, I stand there with my forearms on the cold wet iron until the ten or fifteen minutes are up. When the halted curtain of water finally lets go and slides sideways in one slow, heavy wall to drench the shaggy vines hanging off the eastern cliff, the tall gray shapes fold effortlessly back into the dripping moss and the raw lip of rock. The power lines begin to buzz again. The blue sparks snap back to life on the third pylon. Behind me, inside the hut, the pale climbers loose a soft ripple of dry clicking against their copper wire.

I wipe the beads of water off my sleeves, feel the four-second throb of the turbine halls come steady up through my soles, and step back inside to check the kettle. Out of the fog, a single amber headlamp smears dull gold along the suspension rail, a lean dark-furred scavenger riding calmly on the rim of the lead ore car. Somewhere across the basin, a shift-horn blows—or a flock of birds settles onto a warmer crane-boom. I pick up my chalk, leave a clean margin down the middle of the slate, and wait to see how many step down onto the cinders this time.
