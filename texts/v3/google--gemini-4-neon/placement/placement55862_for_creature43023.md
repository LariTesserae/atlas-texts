I do not think I could stay long in the valleys at the bottom of the cloud-sea, where the sky is only a damp gray lid and the seasons arrive late and blurred through the rain. Nor could I live forever inside the bare quartzite bowl above, where the light has no mercy and every half-hour turns a breathing body to frost-feathered stone.

I find myself where the two worlds chafe against each other.

Two miles below the bruised indigo, and a mile above the drowned river-bottoms, the spine of the ridge puts out a broad, shelving shoulder of fractured gray shale. It sits right inside the ragged upper skin of the cloud-deck. Up in the high crescent, the clouds are a tidy tide that rises over the lip for five silent minutes and tears cleanly away. Down here, the cloud does not visit; it lodges. It snags in long, slow skeins on the rocks, drifts through the doorway in cold silver wool, and turns the afternoon into a soft, depthless gray where nothing casts a shadow at all.

The first thing you would notice here, if you came down from the silent pockets above, is the sound of water wasting itself.

Just below this shelf is the last line where wood can survive—no tall lowland timber, only kneeling, wind-sheared pines and gnarled junipers that grip the shale with roots like clenched knuckles, their windward bark scoured pale as bleached bone, their leeward sides furred in sulfur-yellow and dark indigo lichen. All day, their stiff, waxy needles comb droplets out of the passing fog. The water gathers until it grows too heavy to hold, then drops onto the flat shale slabs outside my hut in an unhurried, irregular percussion—*tock, tock, tock*—soaking straight into the dark scree without anyone bothering to trap it in parchment funnels or ration it by the swallow.

I have lived at this sill for seven turnings of the sun, and I still catch myself listening to that drip with a faint, old astonishment.

My shelter is wider than the amber crouch-huts of the high spines, though not by much. It is stacked from flat gray shale wedged under a low jut of cliff, its roof weighted with heavy stones against the sudden gales that plunge off the upper glaciers, its seams packed tight with wet moss and pine-duff. Unlike the pockets above, there is a hearth here—a cupped hollow of blackened slate set deep into the back wall, feeding a small, grudging fire of twisted juniper root. The flame burns low and blue-rimmed in the thinish air, hissing when the fog rolls in, filling the room with the sharp, resinous bite of turpentine and wet ash.

It is the first real fire a traveler meets coming down from the sky, and the last one going up.

My knees are the reason I stopped here. Up on the finger-wide ledges of the high ridges, a body has to be all hinge and economy—able to rise from a crouch in one smooth draw of breath, able to hold still on a blade of unweathered slate while a white-out seals the joints in ice. For sixteen years I kept a high crescent further west along the spine, folded into the silence of the burrowers and the pale-horned climbers, drinking my own melted night-breath off stitched bracts, reading the weather off the bare bones of the world. Then one autumn, when the winter lock came hard and early, my left knee refused to unbend after a five-minute freeze. I sat in my eastern windbreak staring at my own stiff leg while the sunlight slammed back into the bowl and the steam curled off my white-furred shoulders, and I knew the ledges were finished with me.

When the relief climbed up the next spring—a thin-cheeked boy from the mud-terraces with raw copper strapped to his ribs and breath roaring in his throat like a bellows—I stayed beside him for two moons. I showed him the angle of the three balanced quartz blocks, the salt-trough behind the windbreak, the curing of the blackened lantern-stalks beneath sun-heated slate, the way a single flick of a black-tipped tail across the floor means a pale harrier is dropping straight out of the zenith. I gave him my mirror—its smooth back worn glassy by my thumb and by the thumbs of three watchers before me—and I climbed down.

I tried to go all the way home to the valley. I lasted four days.

Down there, beneath the unbroken gray ceiling, the air felt like warm tallow in my chest. My lungs, stretched broad and cavernous by years of high altitude, did not know what to do with so much wet weight. The low timber roofs pressed over my skull like the flat lid of a cache pit; the voices of ten people speaking at once rattled against my ears like loose scree. Worst of all was the blindness—looking up and seeing only wet pewter, never knowing whether the western glaciers were holding or cracking, whether the frost-line was steady or plunging down the spines.

On the fifth morning I walked back up through the dripping fern-belts, past the last sheepfolds, past the straight-trunked valley firs, until the air thinned enough to taste clean again and the clouds began to shred around my boots. I stacked shale beneath this overhang, dragged up dead juniper roots, and built the sill.

Most of my habits came down the mountain with me intact. Even here, where a person could gulp air through an open mouth without scorching their lungs, I still breathe slowly through my nose. My ribs stay broad; my nailbeds still keep a faint plum dusk at their edges; the backs of my hands carry the pale hairline scars of old quartzite, though the fingertips are now stained amber-brown with pine resin and gray with wood-ash rather than dark with rendered burrower fat. Hanging on a juniper peg beside my narrow doorway is my old carved visor of translucent climber-horn, its two grass-blade slits smeared clean of frost, its cold-hammered copper pins gone deep indigo-green at the heads. I rarely need it in the soft drift of the cloud-skin, yet I keep it oiled.

Because I am not here merely to warm my knees by a root-fire.

Every afternoon, when the sun climbs clear above the cloud-deck and strikes the pale quartzite faces miles overhead, the heated walls stir the upper air. Cold downdrafts peel off the high ridges and plunge straight down the sheer flanks like water poured from a jug. They strike the upper surface of the cloud-sea and punch violent, ragged chimneys straight through the vapor.

That is when I leave the fire.

Wrapped in dark oiled wool over an inner vest of cured moss-felt, I step out onto the flat shale bench facing uphill. Wedged into the low dry-stone wall before me are two notched slabs of slate—a cruder cousin of the balanced quartz points on the high lips—angled sharply upward at a high gray shoulder three miles above the timberline, where the spine steps down toward the fog.

Sometimes I sit on that damp bench for two hours while the silver wool slides past my knees and nothing opens at all. The juniper needles drip; the wind sighs softly through the twisted boughs; far below in the mist, nothing answers. I do not grow restless. Years in a silent bowl teach a person how to wait without spending themselves.

Then the cold draft drops out of the gray, smelling suddenly of dry frost and bare stone. The curtain rips apart.

For ninety seconds, or for four minutes, a ragged tunnel opens straight up into a slice of piercing, cobalt sky. Far above the last pines, above the bare gray scree, the pale edge of the upper ridge leaps out in blinding white relief—so sharp it looks near enough to cut a thumb on.

And from its lip, a tiny cold star winks down through the gap.

*Once. A pause. Twice. A long, steady burn.*

I count the draws of my breath through my nose, holding the rhythm in my chest where the old grammar still lives like a pulse. Sometimes the fog slams shut across the chimney halfway through a phrase, swallowing the light mid-count. Then I brace my elbow against my knee, lift the fresh plate of pale quartzite I cleaved from a vein behind the hut, catch the last shaft of sunlight pouring through the closing rent, and send a short, stuttered double-flash straight up the vertical dark: *The cloud took it. Hold.*

And when the next tear opens and the ridge stays blank for a minute, I do not panic or flash again too soon. I know what is happening three miles above my head. A silent tongue of white cloud just spilled over her razor lip; frost is blooming like feathers across her white-furred shoulders and the dark indigo moss cushions alike; she has drawn her wrists inside her sleeves and turned to stone for five minutes. I sit on my wet shale bench in the dripping pines and wait out her white-out with her, breathing slow, until the wind shifts, the glare slams back into her cathedral of rock, and the little cold star wakes and finishes its word.

*Still breathing. High weather holding clear. The frost-line is dropping on the western ridges.*

Or, later in the turning of the sun: *Ice-dam swelling in the north cleft. Early lock behind the western cloud.*

Or, rarest of all, the slow, doubled pulse that travels peak by peak down fifty miles of knife-edge stone: *Need copper. Need chert. Knees failing.*

When the rent finally seals for good and the afternoon dims into wet silver, I go back inside to the clicking root-fire and reach for a bundle of split juniper sticks, each one planed cleanly on four sides. With a cold-hammered copper awl, I turn the sharp, soundless grammar of light into something the wet valleys can read with their fingers. One deep transverse cut for a frost-warning; three shallow slants for a ridge holding clear; a cross-nick at the crown if a pocket needs relief before the snows lock the trails. I rub pine-soot into the grooves so they show dark even in the dim smoky rooms below, wrap the tally-stick in waxed hide, and hang it by the door.

In the morning, if the word is urgent, I step out to the downward lip of my shelf and put my lips to a long, hollowed horn—a lowland ox-horn, heavy and warm-colored, nothing like the pale translucent horns of the high climbers. I blow two low, flat notes straight down into the muffled gray gorge. Up in the high pockets, a sound like that would be an obscenity, swallowed instantly by the thin air; down here, the damp mist catches the low resonance and rolls it three miles down the wet slopes to the upper pastures, telling the herders to send a runner up through the ferns.

And when the word is too tangled for notches or horn-calls—when the valleys must decide whether to cut the barley green or trust another week of rain—I lace rawhide grips over my boots, take my juniper staff, and walk down into the mist myself.

They wait for me at the edge of the first stone village, where woodsmoke hangs trapped beneath the gray ceiling and the air smells of wet wool, manure, and boiling grain. They offer me hot barley broth in clay bowls and set a dry stool near the hearth, though they know I will sit a little back from the blaze, squinting slightly, speaking slower and quieter than anyone else in the room. I lay the notched sticks on the table and tell them what the high spines saw while the mist kept them blind. In return, before I turn back uphill, they press into my carrying-frame what the bare quartzite bowls can never make: heavy nodules of river-copper, dark glassy cores of valley chert wrapped in damp moss, cakes of coarse gray salt, coils of oiled hemp.

Back at the sill, I test every nodule of copper myself on a flat anvil-stone beside my hearth, tapping the edges cold with a water-rounded river pebble. Lowland smiths use fire and bellows; they do not always notice a trace of brittle impurity that will hold fine in a damp valley and snap clean in half at thirty below freezing. I reject the ones that crack, and set the soft, true pieces aside in a dry cleft behind the chimney.

And once every few years, a young one climbs up out of the fog behind me, carrying a pack of supplies and the wide, unsteady breath of the lowlands—chosen by the villages, or drawn by whatever quiet stubbornness pulls certain people out from under a gray ceiling toward the sharp edge of the sky.

They do not go straight up into the bruised indigo. If they tried, their lungs would fill with fluid on the first sheer pitch, or their eyes would blister blind against the mirror-walls. They stop here, at the sill, for a full turning of the moon.

I give them a place beside the root-fire while the altitude hammers behind their eyes and their blood begins to thicken. I make them sit still on the wet bench and practice breathing through their noses until the roaring in their chests quiets down. From a dry horn box tucked near the chimney, I shave tiny slivers of cured, blackened lantern-stalk—not as potent as the high-cured rods, brought down years ago and rationed like gold—and have them hold a bitter crumb beneath their tongue until the warmth returns to their stiff fingertips.

On afternoons when the fog thins to a bright veil across the hundred-yard width of the saddle, I stand at the far edge of the scree and flash small, slow signals back at them with my cleaved quartz plate, teaching them to read the length of a burn not by counting aloud, but by the draw of their own ribs. *Hold for ten breaths. Drop the stone into your shadow. Never hurry; panting steals the water from your blood.*

In the evenings, while the pine needles tick softly outside and the fire settles to red ash, I tell them the quiet things that never travel by mirror-light. I tell them how to fold a coat so white fur holds a mirror around a dark, warm core. I tell them never to hunt the pale-horned climbers, only to leave salt on a bare quartzite trough and wait for the click of translucent hooves at dusk. I tell them to leave warm entrails on an open flat of stone for the harriers, and never to chase the thumb-sized mice out from beneath their cloak when the night freeze locks the roof, because five frantic little hearts drumming against your ribs will carry you through the coldest hours before dawn.

Then comes a morning when the high wind blows hard and dry out of the west, scouring the upper walls clean of vapor until the whole spine stands naked against a sky the color of a fresh bruise.

I strap the tested copper, the moss-wrapped chert, and the salt-cakes onto their frame. We leave the hut together, climbing out of the kneeling junipers, out of the drip of fog-water, out of the soft world of voices and smoke. Around us the shale sharpens into splintered gray slate; the lichen thins and vanishes; the last small sounds fall away until there is only the dry *tick* of a displaced pebble and the slow measure of our breath.

At the first pale ledge of raw quartzite—where the glare suddenly strikes off the rock like tilted mirrors and my stiff knee begins to lock against the cold—I stop. I lean both hands on my juniper staff, catch my breath through my nose, and do not try to speak aloud in the thin air. I only touch the horn visor pinned to their collar to be sure the copper pins sit fast, and nod upward toward the bright, razor-edged drop.

I stand there until their small, dark figure climbs out of the gray shadow into the blinding white brilliance above, shrinks to the size of a slate flake, and disappears over the high lip into the silence.

Then I turn slowly back down into the silver wool of the clouds.

By late afternoon I am back on my wet shale bench, wrapped in oiled wool, smelling faintly of pine resin and old frost. The wind shifts; the katabatic gust drops off the pale heights; the gray curtain tears open for two clean minutes, revealing the jagged notch three miles above. I lift my chin and watch the cold indigo gap between my sighting-stones, waiting for the single sharp star to wink down through the mist, ready to catch the bright word of the sky and carry it gently into the dark, warm fog below.
