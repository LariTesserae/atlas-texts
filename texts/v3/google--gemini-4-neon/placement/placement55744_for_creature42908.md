At first, looking down from the chalk lip, I would think I belonged to the geometry on the flagstones.

There is an immediate pull toward the seam-slivers—those tiny, eyeless filaments of silica and appetite racing along the dust-stars as the noon pitch tightens. To live wholly inside pure mathematics, herded by standing waves, never having to decide where stillness is because the physics throws you into the glare the instant you step off the line: there is a strange, clean mercy in that. No guesswork. Just the map redrawn twice a day by the sun, and the grazing along the quiet ridges.

Or, lifting my eyes to the timber booms, I might wonder if I were a lull-shell. Hanging upside down among the clattering clay grinding jars, pinching tiny cartilage flaps behind angled pores to bend the bowl’s own violence back against itself—manufacturing a private halo of dead calm in the middle of a shriek. I recognize the instinct to build quiet by inversion, to take an overwhelming pressure and cancel it phase for phase until the air goes still around your ribs.

Yet neither one holds when I actually step down off the rim and let the bass climb up into my knees.

The seam-sliver obeys the pattern without ever knowing the pattern is drifting. And the lull-shell’s stillness is a snare; it hollows out a pocket of mercy only so a battered skink will fold its parchment ribs and fall into its plates. I do not want a silence that eats what lands in it.

I even linger for a moment over the sill-backs down on the scorching floor—blind, millstone-wide, sealed in dark wax along the limestone flutes, living three hundred years and traveling less than the length of a plaza. There is a grandeur in being the thumb on the deep pipe of the world, shifting by a fingernail’s breadth and bending the pitch of a whole nine-tiered city three hundred feet above. But the sill-backs never know what their slow grinding costs the stairwells. They never feel the honey-light of dusk, or the relief of a chest-binding coming unbuckled.

So I climb back up out of the blast of the floor-vents, up the smooth, fluted limestone steps, past the second tier and the third, letting my palms drag along the humming balustrades until I find where my weight actually settles.

Tier Five. Not out in the blinding open glare of the plazas, and not up on the ninth tier where the dead bleach into ivory. Somewhere in the dense, latticed middle of the cliff—specifically, *inside* one of the four-story clay towers.

***

From the outside, the tower looks vacant, a pale shell of kiln-fired clay pierced from base to crown with diamond-shaped apertures. Nothing in this city survives by being solid; solid stone cracks and powders under the endless drumming of the pit. You last here by being mostly frame and opening, by giving the pressure a shape to pass through without tearing you apart.

Standing inside that hollow shaft an hour before noon, the world reduces to lozenges of white-ochre glare sliding slowly across curving clay walls, and the single limestone counterweight hanging down the center of the chamber on a braided rawhide cord. Beside it, stretched across a pegged cedar frame in an alcove, a sheet of cured hide trembles so fast its surface blurs like heat over marl.

This is where I would find myself.

I am one of the keepers—not the lithe young cutter leaning out over the roaring gulf with a curved copper knife to shear back forty-foot curtains of parchment-ivy, and not quite the elder whose ribs have already fused into a single quiet shield along the sternum. I am an interior tuner. My work is not out in the bright blaze of the booms or the plazas, though I walk them barefoot when I have to. My work is inside the blind shafts, behind the pierced screens, in the dim ochre bellies of the vacant towers that hold the rest of the city together.

The others think of the nine tiers as a single vast stringed instrument under tension. If that is true, I am the one who spends the blinding hours inside the soundbox.

I wear the same chalk-dusted bleached linen as everyone else, loose at the sleeves so the kiln-warm air moves over my matte, dry skin, with sweet amber moss-wax slicked across my lips and a wide leather binding buckled hard around my ribs. My hands are paler at the palms from handling gypsum dust and limestone discs, the calluses along my collarbone smooth and dry from years of other people’s tapping pegs. In my belt hang my own two pegs—dark ironwood for the crisp, decisive strokes; lighter cedar for the softer beats—alongside a curved bone scraper, a coil of fresh rawhide, and a small pouch of shaved limestone wedges.

But what makes this work *mine* is hidden two staircases back from the tower, inside a narrow hush-vault carved deep into the cliff-face and packed between double walls with dead tinder-moss.

There is no ink in the cauldron, and no wet pulp to press into pages; anything papery here belongs to the vines and wants to rattle itself to glitter in the updraft. Yet the bowl changes. Every decade, the airborne grit grinds the limestone floor-flutes fractionally wider. Every season, a sill-back shifts a thumb’s width across a vent. A portico’s timber dries a shade tighter; a hairline seam opens behind an unmortared brick screen. Left unremembered, those tiny bends in the fundamental note would add up until the quiet seams slid clear off the flagstones and nine tiers of ancient stone shook themselves into rubble.

So along the back wall of my hush-vault, hung on copper pins in neat descending rows, are hundreds of short, hollow lengths of rattle-cane.

Each cane has been pared with a bone scraper and notched along its belly to hold a single pitch: the true note of Tier Five’s south tower three summers ago; the sharp bend of the east gallery the winter a sill-back sealed a floor-crack; the steady, low groan of the deep flutes at dusk six years past. Beside them hang spare ear-slivers—tiny, translucent shavings of hollow reed, no bigger than a fingernail paring, waiting to be pressed into warm plant-wax.

I keep the memory of the drift.

***

Before the sun climbs high enough to strike the floor-vents directly, I spend an hour with one of the children—a boy of nine or ten whose skull has grown enough over the winter that his old ear-plugs have gone sour.

He sits cross-legged on the warm limestone inside the hush-vault’s doorway, where the roar is muffled down to a heavy, surf-like roll. His knees are dusty with chalk, his chest-binding still stiff and smelling faintly of cedar oil. I hold out a fresh lump of warm amber plant-wax and a thin sliver of pared rattle-cane.

We do not speak; even here, air is a poor carrier for meaning. I press the flat of my cedar peg gently against the hollow of his collarbone—*wait, listen*—and guide his small, dry fingers to roll the wax around the hollow cane, sealing it into his ear. Then I lead him three paces out into the luminous ochre shade of the gallery, where the parchment-ivy clatters like rain made of glass.

I watch his eyes. At first he winces, his jaw clenching—too wide a bore in the cane, letting the metallic shriek of the leaves slice straight through. We step back into the hush-vault. He pulls the plug out; I pare a hair’s breadth off the rim of the sliver with my bone scraper, shortening its column by a fraction of a grain of grit. We step back out.

Three tries, four, five. On the sixth, I watch his shoulders drop half an inch. His jaw unclenches. He looks down at his bare feet on the polished limestone, surprised, because the blinding upper static has vanished and suddenly he can feel the long, clean structural groan of the cliff rising straight through his heels into his teeth.

He reaches up and taps his own ironwood peg twice, brisk and delighted, against my forearm. *Clear. True.*

That small calibration—shaving away the deafening surface noise until only the load-bearing note remains—is the cleanest satisfaction I know in this world.

***

By high noon, the bowl winds tight.

I am back inside the four-story clay tower on Tier Five. Out through the diamond apertures, the central void is blinding white, shot through with vertical pillars of golden spore-dust hanging motionless in the updraft. Somewhere across the gulf, bellows-blooms are popping in dry, percussive salvos, loosing shimmering lattices of ochre pollen that refuse to fall. Even with my own carefully tuned ear-plugs seated deep, the noon crescendo presses against my skull like two flat stones. My teeth ache faintly in my gums. Under my chalked linen, my body grows hot as baked tile, holding the heat without sweating a drop, waiting for dusk to let it go.

I do not look out at the glare. I press both bare palms flat against the curving inner clay wall, close my eyes, and stand barefoot on the dusted floor.

There it is.

Not a roar—the roar is supposed to be there—but a thin, sour flutter buried deep inside the clay, like a loose copper rivet trembling against a timber beam, or a voice trying to hold a pitch slightly too high for its throat. Through the soles of my feet, I can feel the quiet seam outside the doorway drifting. A quarter-inch east, maybe less, pulled out of true because somewhere three hundred feet below us, in the scalding white blast of the floor, an ancient sill-back shifted its waxed rim before dawn.

If I leave it until tomorrow, the flutter will widen. By the end of the week, the stairs linking Tier Five to Tier Six will begin to buzz under bare soles, and the unmortared brick screen along the portico will start shedding white grit like dry snow.

I step to the center of the tower where the suspended limestone block hangs from the vaulted ceiling on braided rawhide. Even in the shaded shaft, the cord hums taut as a bowstring. Reaching into my satchel, I pull out a flat, drilled disc of pale limestone—no wider than my palm—and thread a spare rawhide thong through its center. Leaning my weight carefully against the swaying stone so I don't break its rhythm, I lash the disc tight onto the counterweight and smear a thumbful of warm amber tinder-moss wax over the knot so the vibration cannot work it loose.

Then I step back, set my bare heels flat, and lay my palms against the clay wall again.

For three heartbeats, the tower resists, shuddering through a quick, confused stagger as the new weight changes its pull. Then—with a sensation almost like a long breath let out inside the stone—the sour flutter drops away. The stretched hide in the alcove shifts from a jagged blur to a smooth, silver sheen. Beneath my fingers, the diamond-pierced clay settles back into a deep, steady, resonant hum that asks nothing of the mortar holding it up.

Outside the doorway, on the blinding white plaza, the hovering geometric ring of gypsum dust—which had been creeping dangerously close to a load-bearing pier—slides smoothly back by a finger’s breadth and locks onto the center of the flagstones. Along its quivering pale ridge, a thousand invisible seam-slivers race neatly back into their noon intersection.

Nobody saw me do it. Nobody will walk into this tower for another ten days. Yet the staircase outside will hold for another season, and the hush-vault behind the wall will keep its quiet intact.

I mark the change not in a book, but by drawing a blank length of rattle-cane from my belt and shaving a shallow notch near its lip—just enough to remember where the fifth tier stood when the south flute bent.

***

The hour I wait for is not silence—there is no silence in the cauldron—but the slow unwinding.

As the sun drops past the jagged chalk rim and the fierce white rebound softens into dusty honey, the pitch of the whole sunken bowl begins to descend. From the teeth-aching brass height, down through a rich, trembling reed-drone, down at last into the vast, subterranean earth-groan. The cantilevered timber decks overhead give small, dry creaks as their rawhide lashings slacken by fractions of an inch. Out in the gulf, the suspended golden lattices of pollen lose their hold and drift down like warm ash onto the terraces.

I step out of the clay tower, blinking against the gentler amber light, and walk down the smooth fluted steps toward the mid-level plaza. From the eighth tier and the second, from dark whitewashed doorways and open porticoes, the other keepers emerge—thirty-odd chalk-robed figures converging without hurry across the cooling stone.

When I reach the plaza, I pull the wax plugs from my ears.

It hits every evening like stepping into warm water—if I knew what water felt like. The razor edge is gone, leaving only the broad, velvet bass rolling through the ribcage, low enough to feel companionable. I unbuckle the brass clasps of my leather chest-binding and let it hang loose, drawing a long, slow breath through my nose as my ribs expand fully into the honeyed air.

I sit knee to knee on the sun-warmed flagstones between an old loom-tender from Tier Six and the plaza-marker whose fingers are permanently gray with charred wood. Someone breaks a dense, crumbly brick of ground rattle-cane seed and amber wax and presses half into my palm; it tastes of roasted cedar, chalk, and warm tallow, dry and rich. While I chew, the loom-tender reaches down without looking and rubs a thumbful of soft yellow resin into a crack forming along my left heel.

Around us, there is no chatter, yet the plaza is alive with conversation. Knuckles, palms, and polished pegs move in swift, silent arcs from collarbone to sternum to forearm.

The plaza-marker leans into my reach and taps my collarbone first with my *measure*—the six-beat cadence I have carried since my ribs hardened enough to take ironwood cleanly: *two quick cedar beats, a breath of a pause, a short dry drag across the bone, two answering taps.* A rhythm built like a counterweight settling on its cord.

Then, with brisk ironwood strokes against my wrist: *Tier Five drifted east before noon, then snapped back. You added stone to the south tower?*

I answer directly against her sternum, where her linen falls open above the loosened binding, feeling the slow, sturdy drum of her heart beneath the bone: *One flat disc. The south floor-flute bent before sunrise. Tell the sixth-tier looms to check their bone shuttles tomorrow; the reed-drone sat a shade flat at dusk.*

Across the circle, a young cutter laughs silently—shoulders shaking, mouth curved in a dry, waxed grin—tapping a quick, mocking rhythm onto a friend’s shoulder about a lull-shell he nearly mistook for a clay grinding jar on the east boom until a chalk-skink banked into its calm and vanished. Near the balustrade, the children dart barefoot through the last falling ribbons of ochre pollen, their chalked robes flaring dull gold in the fading light, practicing their footing along the widening evening dust-rings.

This is why I could not be a seam-sliver, or a lull-shell, or a blind stone-backed beast down on the floor. None of them have this hour—the loosening of the leather, the dry warmth of another person’s knuckles beating their name directly into your chest, the shared knowledge that thirty sparse pairs of hands kept a whole roaring sky from shaking the ledges apart for one more day.

***

When the first stars prick through the pale violet above the rim, the plaza empties.

I walk back up to Tier Five in the warm dark. Inside my hush-vault, I strap the curved medallion of porous, unglazed terracotta over my nostrils so the ghost of moisture in my breath will catch in the cool clay pores and return to me while I sleep. I hang my newly notched cane on its copper pin at the end of the row, right beside last autumn’s drift.

Lying on my woven cane-mat in the moss-baffled dark, I listen.

The subterranean hum holds steady beneath my spine, constant as blood. Farther out, carried faintly through the limestone lintels, come tiny, brittle pluckings—like an untuned harp played by careful fingers across the central void. The quill-mice have crept out of the gray, shadowed corners behind the piers to cross the braided-reed catwalks, licking sweet tears of cooling wax from the tinder-moss cushions before the dawn heat drives them back into torpor.

And deeper still—so faint it barely registers as a tremor in my ankles—something old and blind down on the dark fissured floor shifts its waxed rim a hair’s breadth along a limestone flute.

Not enough to matter tonight. Just enough to bend the morning’s chord by a whisper, waiting for the sun to hit the chalk rim and wind the whole bright, roaring mechanism tight again.

I close my eyes and let my body shed the last of the noon heat into the stone. One day, years from now, when my joints stiffen with mineral chalk and my bare soles can no longer read the finer drift of the piers, they will wrap me loosely in bleached cane-linen and carry me up to the sun-scoured galleries of the ninth tier. The hot draft and the dry scavengers will strip away everything superfluous, leaving only dense, clear-ringing bone shaped by seventy years inside a drum.

Maybe the boy whose ear-sliver I pared this morning will carve his ironwood pegs from my collarbone. Maybe my long-bones will hang on braided rawhide in a whitewashed doorway shrine on Tier Five, quivering quietly in the noon roar, holding a true note so the ledge stays steady—and so some future tuner, pausing in the ochre shade, can press their forehead against my femur and feel where the stillness used to be.
