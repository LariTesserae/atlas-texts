At first, I thought I would simply be the one hanging motionless in the doorway—the watcher who has stopped swimming long enough for the fine dust to settle, learning to tell a pleated dark hide from a slumped heap of sea-wrack.

That is always the first temptation in a place this still: to keep your lungs and your eyes, to borrow a vantage from outside the water's rules and move through the drowned lanes like a ghost with a lantern.

Wait here long enough, though, and that kind of presence begins to feel obscene. Too sharp, too warm, too loud. Everything in this sea has spent millennia shedding haste, bones, shells, and bright pigment. A vertical creature with a pulse of seventy beats a minute does not belong under a pewter ceiling that moves by the inch per century. Even your own breath would be an interruption.

So I let go of the swimmer. I let go of sight—that thin, frantic habit of the upper air—and ask what actually fits my shape down here, where the floor is all hand-laid stone and the silence has weight.

Not the kelp. The kelp is magnificent, holding fast to the chinks with rubbery fingers and turning the avenues into vaulted mahogany tunnels, yet even in this gloom it is still reaching upward toward the rumor of a sky it will never touch. I do not want to spend my centuries leaning toward a light that barely survives the descent.

Not the masonry, either. The limestone and basalt have already finished everything they will ever do. They are only unmaking themselves now, softening arris by arris, waiting ten thousand years to settle half an inch and send a single low sigh across the plain.

No. If I belong anywhere in this drowned geometry, it is as one of the mantles—and not just any one, nor in just any room.

It takes me a while to find the exact coordinates of myself, because at first I am pulled in two directions at once. Part of me wants the deep, sealed cistern in the tight warrens—to become one of the vast, rooted elders draped twenty feet across a roofless courtyard, my ballast-stones surrendered forever, pale threads of biofilm sunk deep into the leached mortar-seams, a private grove of ink-maroon kelp growing straight out of my own back so that the distant weather tugs gently at my hide like a hand on a sleeve. There is an immense, quiet seduction in never having to lift again.

And another part of me wants the scarf-sized youth—light, taut, clutching four terracotta pebbles in a narrow hem, catching every three-day current down the thirty-foot avenues, snagging briefly on the weed-furred monoliths just to taste the sharp lime of a new terrace before letting go again.

Neither of those is quite true to me. I am neither finished with moving nor restless enough to drift every season.

So I look for the seam between them.

---

I find myself a hundred yards shy of a circular basalt plaza, where a broad, slab-paved road steps down across a gentle continental terrace and crosses a silted aqueduct arch.

It is not a dramatic place—nothing here is—just a subtle change in the floor's grammar. For twenty miles behind me, the road runs dead-straight between waist-high stone kerbs and rhythmic rows of weathered monoliths. Ahead, the kerbs flare outward and dissolve into the wide, austere ring of fitted basalt where three avenues converge and the slow eddies sweep the flagstones nearly bare.

I do not lie in the plaza itself. Bare basalt is clean, cold, almost metallic, and poor in seepage; you go there to meet, to fuse, to listen, not to live. Instead, I have folded myself across the threshold just before the widening—one half of my dark, pleated skirt sealed tightly over the mouth of a blocked stone culvert where centuries of fallen cord-weed have compressed into rich, bitter peat; the other half draped up and over the rounded shoulder of the road's low kerb, resting flush against the first broad paving slab of the avenue.

It is an awkward posture by the standards of the cistern-dwellers, who prefer the even, cradling corners of windowless rooms. Half of me is steeped in the dark, sour sweetness of trapped decay and ground terracotta silt that washed down from the warrens miles uphill; the other half presses against cold, swept stone that tastes of almost nothing except dissolved lime and the faint, lingering ghost of whoever passed over it three winters ago.

That division is why I stay.

From here, my pale underside drinks enough slow methane and mineral salt from the culvert to keep my internal chambers steady, while my upper edge stays awake to the whole road. Every three-day current that wheels across the terraced paddocks narrows as it approaches the plaza, brushing directly across my pleated back. Every drifter funneling in from the eastern warrens or the northern dikes slides past only four feet above my kerb, trailing flakes of organic snow and the faint chemical signature of whatever district it left behind.

I am large now—not the twenty-foot hill of an ancient, not yet, though wide enough to carpet the whole culvert-mouth and a good span of the kerbstone beside it. My upper hide is thick, dull charcoal dusted with pale lime-floc, creased into heavy overlapping folds that catch the fine grey sediment when the silt-fogs roll in. Inside those folds lie flat, taut chambers humming with cold bacterial velvet, keeping me warmer than the water by a fraction too small to name.

What makes me *myself*, though, is hidden in the permanent night between my belly and the stone.

If you could lift my hem—if you had the strength to pry loose the blunt, patient grip of my lower margin—you would find my underside embossed like soft lead pressed against old carvings. I have been in this world long enough to carry a layered geography in my own flesh.

Faintest now, nearly smoothed away after forty years, are the narrow, parallel ridges of a stepped alley in the clay warrens far to the east—my earliest clear memory of weight, from a long decade spent wedged beneath a slumped doorway where the water tasted sharply of red ground pottery and stagnant sulfur. Pressed over that, clearer still, is the broad, shallow dish of a limestone stair tread from an open paddock cistern, where the water ran bright with chalk and cold currents hissed faintly overhead. Deeper than both are the contours of this kerb: the blunt chisel-pits along its dressed face, the rounded lip of the culvert, the hairline fracture where a flagstone settled a century ago and left a thin weeping seam of calcium.

Because I have not yet rooted—because I still keep my ballast-stones tucked inside the deep fibrous pockets of my lower hem rather than sending pale threads down into the chinks—I carry a second archive beside the embossed one.

When a mantle prepares to drift, it eases its heavy stones out into a neat ring on the floor it is leaving, keeping only a handful of small pebbles to balance itself upright in the current. Most drop even those as soon as they claim a new room and gather heavier local cobbles. I have a quiet, stubborn habit of keeping certain small ones, tucking them deeper into the inner folds of my margin while the newer, heavier weights sit along the outer rim.

Against my left flank rests a thumb-sized nugget of dull terracotta, ground smooth from some thick vessel before the water ever came; it still tastes faintly of iron-clay when the water shifts around it. Along my trailing fold sit two pale knuckles of leached limestone from the terraced paddocks, light and porous, and a flat, dense shard of dark basalt from a plaza forty miles south. They are not much weight on their own—a few ounces against the twenty pounds of rough rubble anchoring me to this kerb—yet pressed into my pockets they are small, solid anchors of elsewhere, keeping the proportions of the wider plain tucked directly against my ribs.

---

My days are not measured in light, because light is only a rare, prickling thinness I felt twice in my restless youth when a freak eddy lifted me too high above the canopy and I had to vent gas in a rush to sink back into the safe, heavy dark.

My days are measured in buoyancy, pressure, and the slow grammar of vibration.

Most of the time, the work is simply equilibrium. Down in the sealed pocket under my culvert-skirt, the compressed mats of dead kelp ferment in the near-freezing dark. My silvery-white underside drinks the seepage; my internal bladders slowly swell with waste gas. Left alone, my own living would undo me. It would pull my pleats smooth, stretch my dome taut, lift my hem off the kerbstone, and float me helplessly up through the mahogany curtains toward the thin, fatal pewter above.

To stay where the nourishment is, you have to hold onto stones, and you have to know when to let go of breath.

Every six or seven days, when the tension in my upper chambers grows sharp enough to dull my listening, I gather the muscular ring hidden among my highest folds and release a single heavy, pearlescent bead. I feel it slip free of my pleats, feel the slight, grateful settling of my own weight back onto the chisel-pits of the kerb. Moments later, far above, the muffled *bloop* comes back through the dense water as a tiny, fading ripple against my drum-taut bladders. Across the mile of drowned avenues around me, I sometimes feel another mantle answer—not intentionally, just another body keeping its balance in the dark, a soft punctuation mark dropped into the immense hush.

Between those vents, I listen.

My gas-filled bladders are tuned to the whole plain. Long before the maroon fronds overhead begin to bend, I feel a leaning current gathering twenty miles out across the paddocks—a heavy, horizontal pressure leaning gently against my eastern flank like a shoulder pressing a door. Half a day later, the kelp curtains tilt twenty degrees down the avenue, parting above my kerb, and the drift begins.

That is when my threshold comes alive.

Out of the dim vaulted tunnel of the road, dark hooded shapes glide silently past—widely spaced, solemn as shadows, hovering three feet above the moss-furred wall-tops. I cannot see them, but I know each one by the faint displacement of pressure as it passes overhead, by the tiny shower of organic snow it disturbs, and by the sudden, fleeting smear of chemistry it trails down into the water over my back.

A young one, light and hasty, smelling sharply of raw limestone and open paddocks, banking tightly around the kerb on shifted internal bubbles to drop straight into the circular plaza.

An older drifter, heavy-hemmed and slow, trailing the rich, bitter savor of deep warren-peat and a culture of dark biofilm I have not tasted in thirty years.

Sometimes, when one settles onto the bare basalt of the plaza a hundred yards ahead, silence holds for two days while the current dies and the tall fronds straighten back toward the unseen ceiling. Then, cleanly through the flagstones beneath my belly, comes a single low *thud*.

Not the long, crumbling sigh of a ten-thousand-year-old wall giving way by half an inch, followed by a slow shiver of settling rubble. A clean, deliberate stroke—one ballast-stone lifted in a blunt margin and dropped three inches onto resonant basalt.

*Here.*

I do not always answer. Some arrivals are only resting between currents, waiting for the water to lean south; some are old, frayed cloaks seeking another old cloak to merge with forever in the quiet center of the square. You learn, over decades, the weight of different stones. A small pebble dropped high makes a brisk, dry tap. A heavy cobble let fall cleanly makes a round note that hums along the paved road for miles.

When the note feels open—unhurried, inquiring—I begin the slow work of answering.

Just beneath the outer fold of my road-edge hem, resting directly on the bare basalt flagstone rather than the soft peat of the culvert, I keep a single smooth, fist-sized cobble of dense river-worn granite—the heaviest stone in my keeping. It takes half an afternoon to draw the thick muscular rim tightly around it and lift it clear of the slab, feeling the brief lightness along that corner of my skirt. Then, when the water is altogether still between swells, I slacken the fold.

The stone drops.

The *thud* runs straight up through my pale underside, sets my internal bladders quivering like taut skins, and rolls outward down the thirty-foot road and across the circular plaza in an expanding, silent ring of pressure. A lazy, pale bloom of lime-silt rises around my hem and takes hours to settle back onto my folds.

Sometimes nothing comes of it. The other mantle rests a week, inflates on the next leaning current, and glides away toward the northern terraces, leaving only a neat ring of abandoned stones on the plaza and a faint, sweet ghost of altered biofilm on the basalt.

Other times, days later, a dark edge creeps slowly off the bare plaza slabs, following the road-kerb backward against the dead water, moving by the width of a finger an hour until its trailing margin touches mine.

---

Those meetings are the closest thing to speech this sea permits.

For the first few days, we do nothing at all. We simply lie edge to edge across the kerbstone while the cold water sits heavy over us and a distant surface storm sends a slow, twelve-second swell rocking gently through our cores—a cradle-pulse from a sky neither of us has ever known.

Then my outer hide softens along the seam where we touch. Hundreds of microscopic pores open.

For three weeks, we are neither quite one nor quite two. Without sound, without hurry, we trade the slow wealth of our centuries. Into my chambers flows a pale, luminous strain of bacterial velvet tuned to squeeze life out of nearly barren basalt—something I could never have grown alone in my rich culvert. In return, I pass along the dense, bitter-adapted cultures I have carried since my years in the clay warrens.

And along the slow-firing lattice of our fibrous cords, moving in quiet waves of contraction and ease, we exchange the shapes pressed into our undersides.

Through that fused border, I feel places I have never drifted: a sunken courtyard ten leagues west where three low corbeled arches still stand intact, trapping a pocket of warm mineral seepage; a collapsed causeway whose broken slabs channel the silt-fog sideways like a weir; a wide, silted canal where five vast elders have rooted side by side until their kelp-groves form an unbroken roof of mahogany gloom. In turn, the newcomer takes away the exact pitch of this culvert, the chipped chisel-marks of this kerb, the long straight axis of the monolith-lined road behind me.

Once, years ago, during a fusion that lasted through two whole silt-fogs, a deep surface storm rocked the plain for four days straight. On the fourth day, right along the rhythmic pulse of that twelve-second sway, a small, taut lobe pinched slowly free from our joined seam. No wider than a folded scarf, clutching three tiny chips of limestone and one of my own small terracotta flakes in its newborn rim, it swelled quickly and caught the very next current straight down the avenue. I felt its brief, bright tug as it snagged on the first monolith a hundred yards away, held for an hour, and then let go into the dark.

I have never known where it settled. Yet sometimes, when a milky silt-fog rolls in from the south—turning the alleys into absolute blind weight for days while I ripple slowly from crown to rim to shrug the grey dust off my back and drink the fresh surge of leached lime—I feel a distant, familiar cadence in the water: a single heavy *thud* miles down the straight road, neither too brisk nor too slow, and I wonder if a piece of my embossed geometry is still traveling the plain.

---

There is one question that moves through my fibrous cords more often now, though "often" down here means once or twice a decade.

The culvert beneath me is generous. Year after year, the tall mahogany fronds along the road-kerb age, dull, tear free in a sluggish current, and sink intact right beside my hem, stacking into fresh cold-preserved mats of peat. I am heavier than I was thirty years ago. My pleats are deeper; my dark hide is tougher; a single stubborn holdfast of young sea-wrack has already tried twice to grip the silt-dusted crown of my back, before I rippled hard enough over the course of a week to dislodge it.

I am very close to the weight of no return.

If I stay here another twenty years—if I let the next kelp spore anchor itself into my upper hide and stop shrugging off the silt, if I open my hem pockets and ease every last stone out onto the flagstone, even the small iron-tasting terracotta nugget from the eastern warrens, and let pale threads of biofilm creep down into the leached chinks of the kerb—I will become part of the road forever. I will grow wide across the culvert and the paving slabs until no drifter passing overhead can tell where the masonry ends and my cloak begins. I will feel the leaning currents through the pull of twenty-foot blades rooted in my own back, and young ones will settle beside my rim for a month at a time to drink my deep, tangled memory of the plain.

It would be a good ending—or rather, a good permanence, since nothing truly ends quickly here.

And yet, when a great five-day current builds out of the northern terraces and leans hard across the avenue, tilting the dark forest twenty degrees and sweeping a clean, sharp rush of unfamiliar water across my kerb—water tasting of cold washed lime and distant, unmapped stone—I still feel the old readiness stir along my rim.

I know how it would begin. I would stop venting. Day after day, I would hold my breath inside my chambers until the heavy pleats along my back smoothed out into a swollen, buoyant dome. I would ease my twenty pounds of rough local rubble out onto the flagstone in a neat, low ring—leaving behind even the heavy granite cobble I used to speak down the road—and keep only my four oldest, smallest pebbles tucked deep inside my hem.

Hanging an inch above the moss, holding to the rounded corner of the kerb by a single trailing fold, I would wait for the water to lean its full weight down the avenue.

And then I would let go.

I have not decided yet. There is no hurry. A frond takes seasons to lengthen by a hand's breadth; a wall takes centuries to bow an inch; a mantle at the crossroads of a drowned city can take ten years to choose between rooting into the stone and rising into the drift.

For now, I stay balanced on the seam—half over the bitter sweetness of the culvert, half pressed to the cold, resonant road. My bladders are steady. My stones sit heavy in their folds. Far above, miles through the dark ceiling, an unseen storm is beginning to breathe, sending a faint, twelve-second sway down through the kelp and straight through my core.

I hold the kerb, listen to the whole plain hum through my chambers, and wait to hear what arrives on the next leaning of the water.
