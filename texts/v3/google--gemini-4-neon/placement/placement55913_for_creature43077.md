At first, hanging in the bruised pewter haze above the central terraces, I would be tempted to ask for weightlessness.

There is something fiercely enviable in those milky bell-creatures. When a down-surge drops out of the unseen ice-shadows and detonates against the paving, they do not brace, do not calculate, do not cling. They are simply taken—whipped sideways through breached colonnades, tumbled down worn stairwells, flung upward into the murk—and they never tear. A lazy pulse every few seconds, and the storm does the rest. Even the silver ribbon-swimmers have a clean, enviable freedom: no attachment to any particular flagstone, only the flick of a hundred translucent flanks turning as a single cloth around whatever obstacle the current throws up.

I think I would hover there for a little while, watching them, trying to believe I could be something that light.

Then the grit-cloud would erupt from the seams below, blind me to fifty paces, and spin me backward toward a curved parapet wall—and I would realize I do not actually want to be carried without knowing where the floor is. Weightlessness here is only freedom if you don’t mind being unreadable to yourself, tossed from one terrace to the next with no memory of the stairs you just crossed. I would want to know the seams. I would want to feel what the water is striking against, and why the current folds inward *here* and spills outward *there*.

So I would drop to the paving. And the first thing I would discover is that I am not naturally heavy enough to stay.

That, more than anything else in this place, feels immediately, almost startlingly recognizable: the need to borrow pieces of the ruins just to keep from being swept off the plateau. To hold my ground, I would have to become one of the walkers—pewter-backed, milk-blue along the throat and ribs, built low and broad to split a falling wall of cold water. Not because I want to be armored, though the matte hide helps, and not because I want to conquer the flow. Nobody conquers a down-surge. You only take enough of the world into your own flanks to meet it on honest terms.

---

Even among the walkers, though, it takes time to know where on the plateau I would actually settle.

I don’t think I would belong to the outer causeways. I admire the ones who pack their flanks heavy with egg-smooth black basalt and creep hand-over-hand along the soap-smooth parapets, right above the abyssal drop where the meltwater turns black and vanishes. There is a stark nobility to that kind of ballast—living at maximum weight, never lifting more than one grip at a time, enduring the purest, sharpest edge of the cold. But that much density turns the whole world into sheer holding-on. You spend every slack interval simply gripping the lip.

Nor would I stay forever in the quietest inner cisterns, carrying only a few light fragments of pale cornice-limestone, gliding easily between sheltered pockets where the dust barely stirs. Too much stillness would make me restless; I would start to lose the feel of what the higher watersheds are doing.

I think I would be a walker of the thresholds—someone whose hips carry an uneven, constantly revised mixture of stone. Two or three dense dark cobbles tucked deep near the spine so a sudden cross-eddy won’t peel me off a plaza, balanced with lighter, pale veined fragments I pick up, trade away, and replace as the gravel-bars migrate. Never quite heavy enough to be sluggish; never quite light enough to forget the rock beneath my pads.

And if you looked for me when the water goes slack—in those clean, breathless ten heartbeats after a down-surge collapses and the stone-flour folds back into the joints—you would find me in one of two places, moving slowly between them.

---

The first is down beneath the paving.

Not down in the deepest drainage trenches where the heavy whiskered bottom-dwellers lurk in the silt, though I would know to tap a fallen lintel twice with a knuckle before reaching for a pitted crab. I mean the older buried layers just beneath the perforated plinths—the smooth, drowned vaults stacked beneath broken slabs, where the pewter glare dies out into a clean, mineral dark and the violent blasts from above comb themselves out into steady, laminar streams.

I would go down there often, long after my own feathery juvenile gills had withdrawn behind pleated muscular valves, and long before my own bones had grown dense enough to force me off the plazas.

Partly it is for the young, hanging in pale, translucent clusters under the ancient vault-crowns. There is a strange quiet joy in bringing down crushed crawler-meat and watching a soft-boned hatchling drift clumsily toward your blunt fingers, untouched by grit, still as buoyant as a bell-creature. And later, when one of them is finally tough-skinned enough to follow me up a buried stairwell into the dim pewter light for the first time, I would stay pressed tight beside it in the lee of a retaining wall—holding it down with one broad forearm against its silent panic while my other hand gathers smooth cobbles and tucks them one by one into its empty flank-pouches, testing the weight until its splayed pads finally catch the stone and it realizes it will not be blown away.

Mostly, though, I would go below to sit with the elders.

The calcified ones—the walkers whose brows and knuckles have taken on the permanent chalky patina of the sheltered masonry, whose jaws rest forever against the smooth slabs of the buried arches—draw me more than anything else on the plateau. They have stopped roaming. They no longer carry basalt because their own ribs have become stone enough. They breathe once every half-minute, and they do not waste a movement.

I would crouch shoulder-to-shoulder beside one in the dark laminar flow, tuck my tail-spade flush behind me, and press the flat of my jaw and both open palms to the same flagstone.

In the water above, everything is visual chaos and velvet silence. Down here, through the interlocking rock, the silence opens up and becomes immense. After a long pause—measured not in breaths, but in the slow intervals between distant upper surges—the old one’s mineral-crusted knuckle lifts a finger’s width and drops.

*Tock.*

A long stillness. Then two quick taps, followed by a faint, deliberate drag of bone across wet slate.

Through the acoustic bone of my jaw, the plateau stops being a jumble of ruined plazas and reveals itself as a single stacked body. I feel what the elder has been listening to for weeks: a buried arch three layers beneath the western arena settling by the width of a crawler’s shell; a migrating bar of broken cornices creeping steadily toward a sunken stairwell; a subtle, metallic sharpening in the meltwater’s bite that means the unseen ice-sheets far above the murk are thickening and about to fracture into deeper plumes.

I would have to learn patience here—the real kind, not merely waiting for my turn to speak. You cannot interrupt a conversation conducted through quarried granite, and you cannot hurry it. When I tap back with my own voice-stone—a small, dark pebble of basalt worn glassy in the fold near my collarbone—every click has to earn its place. No filler. No clamor. Only weight meeting weight, clean and dry through fifty paces of stone.

---

The second place you would find me is back up in the dim pewter light, braced upright against the chest-high slotted troughs.

If the buried vaults are where the plateau remembers, the trough-rows are where it speaks to the water.

Nobody knows what the builders cut those long rows of slotted basins to measure or hold, and I wouldn’t pretend to know either. Maybe they were sluices, maybe calendars, maybe channels for something that vanished before the mountain ridge ever drowned. What matters is what they do now: when the turbulent meltwater threads through those narrow vertical openings, it turns from blind chaos into clean, pressurized blades.

When I rise out of a perforated plinth with an elder’s slow map still echoing in the bone of my jaw, I glide across the open terrace on the next outward blast—tucking my long arms under my chest, angling my domed back into the rush, letting the cold storm carry me forty yards before I drop my weighted flanks and lock my fingers into the widened gutters beside a trough-row.

Then I stand on my pillar-thick hind legs, open the sensory pores along my collarbone and ribs to the rushing jets, and listen to how the row is beating.

It is never quite the same as it was yesterday. A migrating bar of cobbles has deflected the approach angle by half a pace; a fierce surge has blown two wedged fragments out of the fifth and seventh slots; the water itself is running a fraction colder and denser from the high basins above. The old rhythm is ragged, slightly off-true. Out in the fifty-pace veil of suspended stone-flour, a walker crossing from the eastern cisterns might misread the pulse.

So I reach over the soap-smooth rim and begin to work.

I gather water-drilled fragments and flat limestone wedges from the lee of the basin. I slide one into a vertical slot, narrowing a broad, sloppy rush into a clean, singing blade; I block the next slot halfway with a rounded egg of pale granite; I clear a wedge of jammed grit from a third. My palms rest lightly on the worn stone as I test the change—not listening with ears, which barely register a murmur, but feeling the hydraulic thrum gather and tighten inside my sternum, pulsing steadily across my teeth and lateral-line pores.

*There.*

When the tuning locks in, the shift is unmistakable. The silent, erratic violence of the plateau doesn’t stop—clouds of stone-flour still bloom and shred overhead, bells still whip past like pale snow—but for thirty yards in every direction, the cold water holds a clear, deliberate cadence. A landmark made purely of pressure. A pulse that tells anyone blind in the murk exactly which terrace they have reached, and which way the sluice-channels lie open.

Sometimes three or four other walkers gather along the lee-side of the row when they feel the beat settle. We don’t tap much then. We just brace our pads in the concentric gutters, rest our knuckles along the curved rims, and let the ordered weight of the unseen higher watersheds thrum through our ribs while our gill-valves open and close in slow unison. When a massive down-surge eventually strikes and blows half my carefully wedged cobbles clattering onto the flagstones, I don’t feel anger, and I don’t hurry to grab them while the backward suction is still tearing at the seams. I clamp my valves shut, flatten my back to the paving, and let the voiceless wall of water roar over me.

Once the grit settles, I unfold, gather the stones, and tune the row again—a little differently this time, because the flow is already different.

---

I think that is who I would be, if I let the place shape me honestly: neither a solitary guardian of the abyssal edge nor a builder of anything new, but a keeper of the correspondence between layers. A walker who climbs down into the dark arches to listen to what is shifting underneath, and climbs back up into the grey weather to wedge loose stones into ancient slots until the chaos above beats true to the structure below.

When I pass another walker in the lee of a breached archway, we touch palms—cold against cold, reading the slow, unhurried rhythm of each other’s flank-valves—and I pull a smooth, veined cobble from my pouch to trade for one of theirs. Weighing it in my blunt fingers before tucking it against my ribs, I read where they have been by its grain and heft. Mine tells them the same: *the central plinth is clear again; the northern trough-row holds a deeper beat.*

And over the long, mineral decades, I know what this life would quietly turn me into.

I wouldn’t notice the first year that the faint chalky patina along my brow-ridge and knuckles stopped wiping clean after a surge. I would only notice, fifty or eighty years in, that I needed fewer stones in my flanks to hold the paving when the vertical plumes dropped. One by one I would leave my dark basalt cobbles in the rubble-bars near the buried stairwells, where the newly emerged young could find them. My own skeleton would be ballast enough. Crossing a hundred yards of open terrace would stop feeling like a glide and start feeling like a slow, deliberate heave of quarried rock.

Eventually, I would drop my glassy voice-stone into a cache beside a perforated plinth and stop climbing back up to the troughs. I would pick a sheltered bay one layer down—just beneath a trough-row I knew well, where the steady hydraulic thrum still reaches softly through the ceiling-slabs—settle my broad jaw flush against the floor, and become one of the deep ears myself. Younger walkers would come down through the dark, lay cracked crawlers beside my blunt mouth, press their palms to my slab, and wait patiently while I tapped out the slow creep of the plateau’s bones with a single calcified knuckle.

And when the last half-minute breath finally didn’t follow the one before it, I know where I would want them to carry me.

Not over the curved outer parapets into the dark below. Just up one level, in the slack intervals between surges, to the windward cheek of a leaning trough-wall where the lower course has finally scoured loose. Three or four of them easing my stone-dense shoulders tight between the ancient granite blocks, wedging smooth cobbles around my ribs until weight and gravity lock me fast.

The next fierce down-surge would wash fine pale grit into the seams and seal them clean. And from then on, I wouldn’t have to wedge stones into the slots anymore to shape the current. I would simply be the stone—holding the cold water true, splitting the silent surge, keeping the rhythm steady for whoever comes up out of the dark next, reaching for a smooth cobble to hold their place on the floor.
