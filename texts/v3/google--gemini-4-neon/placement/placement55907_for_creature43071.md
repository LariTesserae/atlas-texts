At first, looking down into the basin from the edge of the fissures, I think I might be the stone itself.

That is the easiest shape to reach for. An empty vaulted storehouse on the lower terraces, or one of the dry rock-cut arches curving over a flagstone apron—hollow, settled, stripped of every sharp edge, asking nothing from the air except the chance to take the valley’s breath in and give it back as a hum. There is something in me that recognizes that posture immediately: the patience of limestone that has stopped losing pieces of itself, the quiet satisfaction of being a vessel through which an immense, steady sound moves unchanged.

But if I linger in that image, it falls short. A stone vault cannot hear the slight wobble when a culvert clogs thirty paces away. It cannot feel the difference between a barometric drop and a shifting talus ramp. Most of all, it cannot wait ten paces from another living creature until the crickets knit their hiss back together, and then step close enough to press three chalk-dusted fingertips into the hollow of a collarbone.

So I let go of being the stone.

I test the other reflex—the outsider’s reflex—of walking briskly down the broad gray avenues, mapping the districts, studying the dry-fitted masonry and the cured lintels. That falls apart even faster. The moment I picture boots striding across a flagstone plaza, I hear the terrible hole of silence they would tear through the dry-earth crickets beneath the slabs, and feel the chain of alarm-barks rattling half a mile down the rock-dweller ledges. I do not want to be a rip in the air. I do not want to come down from a sky where water falls out of nothing and rots what it touches.

Instead, I stop moving. I let my breathing quiet until the roar stops feeling like a wall pressing against my ears and starts separating into height, depth, and grain—the high, papery rustle of ceiling-carpets in the storehouses, the dry metallic sizzle rising out of every root-bound crack, and beneath all of it, the wide, slow breath moving through the avenues like a cold tide that never turns.

And down on the eastern edge of the city, where the broad aprons of laid limestone slope toward the twenty sunken rectangular cisterns, a figure on a rounded parapet slowly ceases to look like part of the wall.

That is where I find myself.

***

Not in the middle terraces, where most of the households gather beneath the thickest hanging root-curtains and the sheltered courtyards hold deep beds of evergreen shrub. I visit there, and I know the feel of their flagstones under my heels, but my own posture belongs farther out—on the wide, uncrowded eastern flank where the floor of the city drops into its heaviest note.

Down by the twenty cisterns, the barometric draft rising from the deeper dry caverns beneath the basin pours straight across the rows of rectangular mouths. The drone here is thick enough to bruise the sternum. If you unclamp your jaw, your back teeth buzz against one another in a fine, dry vibration. Few families want to sleep right on the rim of it; the bats dislike the turbulence and keep to the higher storehouses, and even the rock-dwellers cluster two terraces up, where the stone vibrates less fiercely beneath their bellies.

To me, it feels like solid ground.

My sleeping-alcove sits two tiers above the fourth cistern—not a true hush-cell, only a shallow oval chamber cut into the cliff-rib, with a single heavy mat of woven olive vine weighted across the doorway with smooth cobbles. It keeps the chalky draft off my face while I rest, but it does not shut out the cisterns. I have tried sleeping deeper inside the cliff, behind three curtains of pelts and braided root-mat, and I always wake too soon, my palms pressed flat against the floor in the dark, checking to make sure the basin has not stopped breathing.

I am spare and long-fingered, dusted matte with limestone silt in the creases of my knuckles and the hollows behind my ears, my cheekbones and forearms rubbed to a dull sheen with bat-fat and crushed waxy resin so the dry air will not crack my skin. My bare feet are pale and horn-calloused from decades of rolling heel to toe across scooped stair treads and gravel aprons; I know the eastern quarter through my soles almost as well as through my ribs—where a slab rocks by the thickness of a fingernail, where a dry conduit runs hollow underneath, where the dust lies deep enough to soften a beetle’s track. Over my sleeveless hide tunic I wear an old mantle of oiled bat-wing membranes, patched twice along the left shoulder with bird-sinew where a sharp flake of limestone caught it years ago. Along the cuffs, tiny shards of black beetle-carapace glint faintly in the slate dusk whenever I lift my hands. At my waist hang two strikers split from a fallen ancient lintel, dark as cured bone and worn glossy where my thumbs rest against them.

If someone on the middle terraces pressed their fingertips to a child’s collarbone to explain who lives down by the twenty mouths, they would not offer a grand title—there are no grand titles in a place this old and unhurried. They would send a short, steady pulse through the bone meaning roughly: *the one who reads the drop in the floor; the one who scrapes the cistern slabs.*

***

That is how my days—my long, gentle shifts from pewter light to blue-charcoal and back—take their shape.

Because the eastern cisterns open nearest to the deep dry passages beneath the plateau, the unseen world overhead announces itself here first. Hours before the indirect light filtering through the sky-rifts dims to storm-pewter, a weather-front miles above leans its weight onto the tableland and alters the pressure of the breath moving through the stone. Down by the twenty mouths, the low drone sags by a hairline fraction. You cannot quite hear the drop with your ears; the cicada choir hides too much of the upper air. You feel it instead as a faint loosening behind the knees, a subtle easing against the breastbone, as though a heavy hand resting on your back has shifted its weight.

When I feel it, I do not hurry. I finish chewing the two waxy evergreen leaves I wiped clean of silt at the start of the light-shift, stopper my resin-plugged pouch of bitter root-sap, and walk slowly up the scooped limestone stairs to the parapet overlooking the broad eastern avenue. There I unroll my wide length of rock-dweller pelt rubbed bright white with chalk, hold it square against the pale gray dusk, and lift it from knee to shoulder at the pace of shifting light.

Across a quarter-mile of bare flagstone and dusty olive thicket, I wait. My pale eyes stay wide and unhurried beneath my brows, blinking slowly to save moisture. After a long stretch—never rushed—another pale square rises against the shadowed colonnade of the middle terrace and dips once in answer. They have felt it too, or now they know to weight their vine-curtains and check the sinew bindings on the newly tapped root-tendrils before the draft strengthens.

Between those slow exchanges with the unseen sky, my hands belong to the tuning of the quarter.

It is work so small that an outsider could watch me for three days and think I was doing nothing at all.

For weeks, perhaps, I have been noticing an uneasiness in the eleventh cistern—not a sudden noise, nothing so crude as a break, just a thin, dry flutter riding along the upper edge of the low drone. At first I do nothing. A basin this old shifts for its own reasons: a colony of beetles burrowing deeper beneath a flagstone, a rock-dweller wedging dry twigs into a crevice, a subtle change in the barometric pull. I sit on the rounded lip of the eleventh cistern for half a pewter afternoon, one palm resting flat on the cool stone, eyes half-closed, listening through my wrist and my sternum until I am sure the flutter is not passing.

Then I climb down into the ankle-deep gray dust at the bottom of the dry basin, moving slowly so no cloud rises to coat my throat, and trace the leeward wall with my fingertips. There it is: decades of fine limestone flakes drifting off the overhang above have packed into a dry fan along the southern culvert mouth, narrowing it just enough to pinch the moving air into a restless rattle. And along the upper windward lip, where an ancestor wedged two flat slabs generations ago, the chalk-earth packing has dried out and shrunk, letting one stone quiver infinitesimally in the steady current.

Fixing it takes days, because rushing tears the air and wastes water from the lungs. With a bundle of molted rock-dove feathers I sweep the fallen flakes gently aside, packing them into the leeward corner of the seventh cistern where I tend a dense, stubborn pocket of silver-green rock-ferns and gnarled evergreen shoots. Then I find a flat, sound piece of chalky limestone from an ancient collapsed parapet and spend an entire gray afternoon kneeling on the flagstones, rubbing its underside slowly back and forth against a rough step until every high grain wears smooth and the block seats dead-flat without a whisper of rock.

When at last I wedge it tight beside the older slabs across the windward lip of the eleventh cistern, tapping it home with the heel of my palm, nothing dramatic happens. The city does not burst into song. Only, as I sit back on my heels and let the crickets beneath the flagstones resume their metallic hiss, the dry flutter in my ribs unknots itself. The eleventh mouth folds smoothly back into the broad, level chord of the twenty. Two terraces up, a cluster of stout furry rock-dwellers stops its uneasy shifting along the ledge and goes back to drowsy grooming.

No one will know I did it unless they pass this way and happen to notice the pale, unweathered scrape on the underside of the new slab. That is the right size for a human mark in a city this ancient.

***

I am not alone down here, even if I spend long stretches with only beetles, lizards, and the cistern-hum for company.

Every few light-shifts, someone drifts down the broad avenues from the middle terraces—perhaps a root-climber carrying a sealed bat-membrane pouch of fresh, tannic sap and a strip of wind-cured dove flesh to trade for the pressed green syrup I squeeze from my cistern-ferns, or an old companion coming simply to sit on the eastern rim because their ribs miss the weight of the lowest note.

I see them long before they reach me: first the faint, rhythmic glint of beetle-carapace at a cuff moving slowly past a colonnade, then the matte olive-gray outline separating from the worn pillars. Neither of us speeds up. When we are ten paces apart on the wide flagstone apron, we both stop and stand entirely still until the dry-earth crickets beneath our feet forget our weight and knit the metallic hiss seamlessly across the slabs between us.

Only then do we step close.

With a neighbor or a young climber, I raise one hand and rest three chalk-dusted fingertips lightly against the hollow of their collarbone while they lay theirs on mine. With my two oldest companions, we step right in and rest the flats of our foreheads together, letting our eyes drift half-shut, mouths closed, keeping every drop of breath inside. Deep in the throat and the nasal vault, we pass tight, dry hums, clicks, and modulated pulses straight from bone to bone.

In that narrow bridge of pressed skull, the roaring city turns into a quiet backdrop. I feel the steady rhythm of their news—that a swiftlet flock has abandoned the western storehouse for the upper aqueduct; that a new root-tendril dangling beneath the northern sky-rift has grown a hand’s-breadth closer to the guano-packed cairn; that an elder in the third terrace has grown very light and dry and has moved behind the four vine-curtains of the deep cliff chamber.

Sometimes, when the news is about an elder like that, I walk back up the broad avenues with them.

I do not go into the hush-cells often, and never casually. Stepping through four overlapping curtains of woven olive vine, braided root-mat, and stuffed rock-dweller pelts always takes a moment of steadying; as the basin’s roar drops away into a distant, surf-like murmur, the sudden lightness in my chest feels almost precarious, like standing on a narrow ledge in the dim air. Every tiny sound becomes startlingly sharp—the dry scrape of a calloused heel on polished limestone, the soft click of knuckles, the faint rustle of oiled bat-wing leather settling over a shoulder.

Yet people ask for me there sometimes when someone is nearing the end of their decades. Not because I hold any special wisdom, only because living beside the twenty cisterns teaches you how to sit without fidgeting, how to leave wide, unhurried room inside a pause, how to listen with your whole ribcage rather than waiting for your turn to answer.

In the cool, baffled dark of the hush-cell, I sit cross-legged near the dry, light body wrapped in hide and listen to something almost unbearably tender: a human voice spent aloud on open air. The elder speaks slowly, rationing their final moisture, their words ragged and dry as rubbed parchment—telling of a roof-block that fell in their childhood, of the taste of sap from a root-curtain that has since thickened to the width of a waist, of the way the eastern cisterns used to sound before the fourth arch was narrowed fifty years ago. I do not speak back. I keep my mouth closed, conserving the quiet for them, occasionally laying two fingers gently against their dry wrist so they can feel a steady pulse answering their breath.

When at last I push back out through the four heavy curtains into the permanent slate dusk, the full roar of the basin hits my chest like cool stone, anchoring my ribs again. I stand still on the outer apron for a long while, letting the cricket-hiss settle around my ankles, carrying the cadence of that spoken voice inside me—knowing it will never be spoken into the open air again, only folded quietly into bone-speech, and into the care I take with the stone.

***

And there are two higher places I climb to, when the long, unhurried intervals turn around.

One is the far edge of the collapsed eastern vault, past the last of the twenty cisterns, where twenty-five years ago a pale thread pushed out of a limestone fissure high beneath a sky-rift. It still hangs more than a hundred feet short of the dry aqueduct below it, fine as braided sinew, swaying almost imperceptibly in the steady draft. I never tilt my face straight up into the harsh pewter wash of the rift; I angle my chin just enough to catch its pale movement at the edge of my vision. Once every year or two, when I find a good flat block of fallen limestone near the talus ramps, I carry it slowly up the scooped stairs of the aqueduct and set it onto the dry-stacked cairn rising millimeter by millimeter beneath that thread. I will be dry and cured in the high galleries long before a climber ever lashes a pebble-weighted sinew cord to its tip, and my bones will be dust before anyone drinks a drop of its bitter rain. That is why I like carrying the stones. Down here, the best things are done at the speed of roots.

The other high place is the cliff-cut galleries themselves—the ancient upper courses whose oval doorways have weathered smooth into the natural overhang, far above the gray plazas and the wheeling eddies of pale cave-swiftlets.

Now and then I climb the worn, polished stairways up into that cooler, thinner draft, passing the quiet rows of our dead seated upright against the smoothed limestone walls, knees drawn up, wrapped tightly in woven olive vine and cured light as dried gourds. Rock-doves nest peacefully in the alcoves beside their shoulders; trails of dusty silver-green fern drape across their wrapped knees. I stop beside the one who first walked me down to the twenty cisterns when my bare soles were still soft enough to feel every grain of grit, who pressed my small palm flat against the parapet of the first reservoir and tapped a dry ceramic *tok—tok-tok* with their lintel-wood strikers so I could learn the difference between a voice riding over the roar and a voice rising up through the floor.

I sit beside them on the chalk-dusted ledge, facing outward over the vast, dim horizon of avenues, plazas, and dark green thickets. Gently, careful not to disturb a sleeping dove, I rest my matte, resin-sheened palm against their dry, vine-wrapped shoulder. I do not expect a hum to come back through the bone. I only sit with them for an hour while the basin holds its single, unchanging note through both of us.

***

And when the timber-strikers ring out across the wide avenues in the slow, basin-wide grammar of gathering—*tok… tok-tok*, bouncing cleanly off the curved limestone cliffs—I lock my strikers back to my waist, wipe the limestone dust from my palms, and drift with the others toward one of the ancient stepped amphitheaters carved into the terraced flanks.

We arrive over half a day, a few hundred quiet figures emerging from the shadow of the colonnades and the hanging root-curtains, climbing the scooped limestone tiers without haste, taking our seats with generous space between us. I always choose a slab halfway up the eastern curve—high enough to feel the whole basin open wide before my chest, low enough to stay rooted in the floor-note.

Nobody stands at the bottom. Nobody raises a voice.

I lay both hands flat on the rounded, smooth-worn limestone beside my knees, let my heavy lids drift shut, and open my sternum to the valley. Above us, invisible behind my closed eyes, thousands of swiftlets wheel through the pewter dusk, their cries folded seamlessly into the hiss of the cicadas and the colossal barometric flute rising out of the avenues.

Inside that immense, unchanging chord, I can feel the whole geography of our home: the taut, bowstring hum of the oldest western root-curtains; the cool, hollow flutter beneath the collapsed northern vaults; the faint new warmth where a household narrowed an aqueduct arch ten years ago; and down along the eastern rim, scarcely separate from the geology itself, the level, untroubled drone of twenty rectangular cisterns breathing smoothly into the dusk.

I do not want the raw light of the plateau. I do not want a wind that breaks into sudden silences, or water that falls out of the sky and takes things away before they have finished settling. Right here, with the dry grit beneath my heels, the chalk-dust in the creases of my knuckles, and the steady vibration of the earth humming cleanly through my ribs, I have nothing left to hurry toward and nothing left to lose—just one more dust-matte listener, patient as the stone, holding the valley’s quiet from inside the roar.
