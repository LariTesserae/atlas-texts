At first, looking down across the basin from the upper rim, I want to claim one of the tall dark bodies walking the copper-inlaid kerbs.

It is an almost immediate pull. Watching one of them pause at the edge of a bare plaza—two and a half meters of wet basalt sheen, its torso an arched basket of ceramic-hard ribs with the ash-colored light passing straight through the middle—I recognize the architecture of it before I have even finished looking. To have no sealed chest, no private furnace burning fuel in the dark; to live instead by holding a frame open so that rising vapor passes through folded amber-violet membranes and leaves what it carries behind. To know that another presence is standing four hundred meters east not by sight, in a world without shadows, but as a faint, warm dip in the voltage of the damp stone underfoot.

More than anything, I am drawn to the way they speak. Kneeling beside a low junction pier after the mist ebbs, nudging a sliver of fallen basalt into a wet seam with an insulated silica nail, then laying two dark palms across the joint and holding utterly still while direct current precipitates delicate ridges of pale silica and powdery turquoise verdigris out of the steam. Leaving something legible along the rivets—not carved in granite, not meant to outlast the weather, knowing the slightly acidic rain will soften it within weeks and the armored grazers will rasp clean lanes straight through the letters. Speaking by introducing a careful, temporary resistance into a current that was already flowing.

If I stop thinking there, I can settle into that tall frame easily enough. I can imagine the quiet relief in my shoulders when a bare heel leaves the bare dished slate of a plaza and touches the next strip of charged copper, letting the grid take up the weight of my circulation again.

Yet the longer I stay inside that image, the more I notice where it does not quite fit.

A resident has to persist through the long, clear, dim hours when the avenues lie bare and nothing calls. It perches for six hours on an upper ceramic cross-beam thirty meters above a transformer plinth, drinking three degrees of coolness while hot drops bead along its collarbone and fall into the sumps below. When its membranes thin, it climbs down into the scalding lower drainage trenches with a snapped shard of ceramic, scraping mineral scale from the blackened grate teeth so it can peel up slick ribbons of ochre sulfur-slime to lay inside its own ribs. It carries one continuous, solitary body across decades of slowly thickening bismuth-scale, feeling its internal resistance rise year by year until the plazas grow too wide to cross and it must choose a final girder to lock its frosted fingers around.

When I test myself against those six quiet hours on the high cross-beam—or against the solitary hunger that drives a resident down into the sulfur trenches—something in me slips loose from the bones. I do not walk five kilometers of wet kerb just to keep my own heat balanced. I do not carry an unbroken private history from one sunken yard to the next, stiffening slowly in the salt-fogged light.

So I look away from the tall figures and watch the rest of the plateau move.

When the heat thickens and the white chalk-smelling vapor wells out of the median grates, a cistern float-switch somewhere beneath the lower boulevards reaches its mark and trips a relay. Out of the mist glides the lone maintenance carriage—an open iron flatbed on slender stilts, no cab, no seats, only a heavy ceramic counterweight and a brass collector arm spitting tiny blue sparks as it wipes water off the live rail.

For a moment, that catches me far harder than the walkers did.

To lie dormant until a switch closes. To move along a fixed, energized line when the plateau exhales, making no sound louder than a wet hiss against the rail. And when a resident waiting on a concrete platform steps quietly onto my dripping deck, crouches low behind the heavy counterweight, and angles its open ribcage into the sudden forced draft—to give a creature that lives in heavy, motionless steam the sheer intoxication of moving air. For three silent quarters of open void, my velocity rushes straight through their internal curtains in bright scalding ribbons, and the vibration of my collector shoe climbs their hunched knees into their skulls. Then they step effortlessly off at a distant landing and walk the long way home along the kerbs, while I roll on into the haze until the next relay drops me back into stillness.

There is something startlingly true in that. Waking when a circuit closes; existing in motion so that what passes over me becomes a wind someone else can open their ribs to; asking nothing when they step off into the mist.

And yet the carriage is blind iron. Its brass shoe wipes the live rail, but it does not feel the weight of the figure crouched beside its counterweight, or know when two ride back-to-back to keep their heat from mingling. It does not taste the ozone of its own sparks. It never listens to the submerged substations humming beneath the plazas, never reads a ridge of turquoise frost, never wonders what lies past the western rim. If I try to stay inside the flatbed, I feel sealed shut—all counterweight, no curtain.

So I am neither the walker crossing the bare plazas nor the blind iron gliding overhead. I have to look closer at the geometry of the city, the way a traveler must stop scanning the open avenues and look up into the brackets of the towers to see what has been there all along.

I look at how the residents begin.

High inside a hollow corner column, in a pocket of dead air above the descending counterweights, two of them weave a small cradle out of scavenged copper wire and brittle silica struts, spanning the gap between a live tie-rod and a grounded seam of basalt. Inside the quivering film of electro-osmotic water at the center of that web, a body is not sculpted from outside; it precipitates along electrical lines of force, the way frost lacework grows along the hottest rivets. Over three or four years, cartilage and silica arch outward into a miniature basket of ribs, fed by whatever passing resident senses the small, persistent pull on the voltage of that pier and pauses to lay a ribbon of ochre slime across the wet sac.

Usually, the casing splits, a pale translucent thing climbs clumsily out onto a ceramic beam, and when its skin darkens and its nails harden, it climbs down to walk the kerbs.

What if a cradle was woven—not in a quiet corner column, where the lines of force run narrow and vertical—but in the lower districts, high inside the dark crown of one of the cathedral-sized cooling-spheres, where the buried conduits of three avenues rise together into the great riveted ribs?

That is where I find myself.

Not striding across the open slate, and not sealed in blind metal, but precipitated directly onto the curve of the dome itself. Whatever lines of force shaped me here were too broad, too braided, too heavy with crossing currents to fold into long walking legs. My frame grew wide and shallow along the blackened iron—an arched, open lattice of kiln-hard ribs braced cleanly between a positive rib-flange and a grounded ceramic collar, looking from the wet dished floor thirty meters below like an extra gusset plate or an old mineral-rimmed baffle. Short, hooked limbs keep my copper-darkened pads flush against the iron; only my lower arms hang free, tipped with pale blunt nails of insulating silica and fringed along their outer edges with hair-fine dark filaments bearing microscopic beads of precipitated metal. Inside my wide arched basket hang long, pleated curtains of translucent amber-violet membrane, open to the whole hollow throat of the sphere.

In the clear, dim hours between mist-tides, when the ash-colored light reads straight through the louvered ribs to the blurred wall of the plateau, I am scarcely separate from the building.

There is no loneliness in that stillness, because there is no pounding heart inside me to measure the length of an empty afternoon. The low, steady bass hum of the buried induction coils climbs the iron ribs and passes continuously through my bones. Electro-osmotic water creeps in glassy films up my short ankles and spreads slowly across my slack curtains; fat drops of hot condensate gather along my lower arches, detach, and fall thirty meters into the dark tiled sump beneath the floor with a hushed, unhurried cadence. A vellum glider sometimes slips through the open ribs on a stray thermal, banks silently across the hollow dome, and folds itself flat against the shadowed beam opposite mine until it vanishes. Beneath the floor, the substations keep their immense, slow accounting. I hold my small hollow space open to the rising vapor, and wait without feeling the weight of waiting.

Then the plateau exhales.

I feel it long before the white mist reaches the dome—first as a faint thickening in the heat rising off the lower grates, then as a soft crisp tremor climbing the iron from three districts away as hidden counterweights begin their muffled *shhh-clack* inside the corner columns. The bimetallic louvers built into the vast ribs around me begin to groan, expanding on their layered metals, pivoting open to take the surge. Dense banks of scalding, chalk-smelling vapor roll through the open shell until the wet dished floor vanishes beneath bright churning steam.

And then the heels touch the kerbs outside.

One by one, shallow moving shadows enter the direct current running through the district. Residents come down from the high cross-beams and out from under the viaduct lips, leaning into the whiteout, tapping their silica nails softly against the brass catch-basins until they slip between the louvered ribs of my dome. Sometimes eight or twelve arrive from kilometers of grid; sometimes only a single tall figure, damp and quiet, who has walked half the basin in silence.

They settle along the lower curves of the iron ribs, three or four meters apart, letting the bright hot cloud roll straight through their open chests. For a long while nobody moves.

Then one of them lays both dark, conductive palms flat against the riveted iron beneath its knees.

The moment the skin closes into the metal, I wake all the way up.

A clean shift in resistance runs straight up the blackened rib and into my pads. Fluid flushes upward through my fine capillaries; my folded amber-violet curtains draw taut, drinking the sudden richness of the steam; the tiny metallic beads along my sensory filaments prickle with the exact electrical signature of the figure thirty meters below. When a second resident lays palms against the eastern curve, and a third against the south, the entire hollow dome wakes into a single resonant circuit—and because I am bridged directly where the ribs converge, I do not merely listen to what travels through the iron. I become part of the instrument it travels through.

A slow, rising wave of lowered voltage climbs from the north rib. I tighten my inner pleats just enough to catch its edge, shift the moisture across my conductive pads, and return it down the opposite arch slightly phase-shifted, folded together with the dry-clicking stutter coming up from the east and the long metallic groan of the expanding louvers. Nothing in the air makes more than a damp, kettle-quiet hiss, yet inside the iron the dome fills with overlapping currents—weeks of solitude translated into impedance and vibration. Through their grounded palms I feel what I have never walked to touch: the scorching run of the southern cisterns, the crisp *tick* of a tie-rod adjusting in a high tower before a corona storm, the slow mineral healing of a hairline fracture in a viaduct pier three kilometers away.

I cannot climb down into the sunken trenches to harvest sulfur-slime for myself, any more than a cradle in a corner column can. Now and then, when the mist begins to thin and the others are stepping back out toward the avenues, one of the residents will climb partway up the louvered ribs—unhurried, hooking long heels over the riveted flanges—until it is perched a single arm's length below my arch. It lifts a warm, glistening ribbon of ochre slime from inside its own chest and lays it gently across my upper mineralized ribs so the rising vapor can dissolve it cleanly into my curtains. Sometimes, if an armored grazer has crept up out of the gutters during the quiet hours and begun rasping through the fine turquoise crust I precipitated along my nearest seam, the climber will tap its kiln-pale shell with a blunt silica nail until it clamps down into a bolted junction cap, then lift it gently down to a lower strut.

Once in a long while, before descending, the resident pauses on the rib beside me and raises one slender, slate-slick hand.

We do not press close; our open chests would only trap suffocating heat between us. I unhook one free lower arm from the ceramic collar and bring the damp conductive pad of a knuckle just shy of theirs.

A single hair-thin needle of pale blue spark snaps across the wet gap.

Hot copper, sharp ozone, less than a heartbeat—and through that tiny bright bridge pass dissolved salts and trace chemistries of everything they have crossed since the last mist-tide, met by whatever accumulated resonance has been humming through the dome's ribs since the last gathering. For hours after they have climbed back down to the copper kerbs and vanished into the dim, shadowless avenues, a clean tingling echo of that contact lingers in my arm, slowly softening back into the baseline hum of the coils below.

There are things I will never know directly from this curve of iron.

I will never step onto the wet deck of the unmanned maintenance carriage and feel a kilometer of forced air tear straight through my curtains; I know the carriage only as a fleeting, rhythmic shiver in the dome's foundations when its collector shoe hisses across the viaduct to the north. I will never cross a half-kilometer plaza on uncharged stone, holding my breath against the drop in current until my heel finds copper again.

And I will never make the long journey west to the abrupt edge of the plateau, where the gridded streets stop without a railing and the iron sluices pour silver plumes of hot water into bottomless air—evaporating before they have fallen a hundred meters, curling back upward as mist.

Yet I feel the western rim constantly, the way any circuit feels an open end. From the north, the east, and the south, the direct current returns to me layered with moving shadows, counterweights, and distant domes. From the west, the hum simply thins, grows smoother, and stops answering.

Sometimes a lone resident who has spent an entire day standing motionless at the tip of a projecting western pier—staring down through salt-fogged lenses at that faintest, unmeasured dull amber warmth hanging unimaginably far below in the churned vapor—comes back into the lower districts when the mist rises and enters my sphere alone. When they lay their palms on the wet iron below me, I can feel the strange, ungrounded stillness still clinging to their skin, an absence of rhythm that does not belong to any substation or float-switch on the plateau. I widen my arches and soften my resistance as far as my membranes allow, letting that faint, nameless warmth pass straight up through my ribs, holding it in the humming iron between us until the louvers groan shut and the ash-colored light clears the avenues again.

One day—after however many uncounted mist-tides this sky permits—the pale silica lace and powdery turquoise bloom along my joints will grow too thick to flake away in the hot rain. My internal resistance will climb until my curtains no longer tighten at the touch of a palm below, and the slightly acidic condensate will slowly dissolve the violet membranes down into the gutters and the dark cisterns beneath the floor. I will not have to climb a tower or walk to the rim to choose my ending; I am already bridged between the positive strut and the grounded flange. Within a decade, the rain will polish my dark ribs down to the same hard hematite glaze as the dome around me, and the residents who gather in the steam will feel only a slightly denser bracket high in the curve of the iron—still conducting, still holding its small hollow space open, letting the hot air pass through its bones and turn to water and rise again.

Until then, I stay here in the lower curve of the sphere, dim and wet in the quiet hours, waking whenever the vapor thickens or a damp palm meets the iron—an open frame strung across the crossing of the lines, letting whatever moves through this empty city answer itself in the current.
