If you try to look for yourself too quickly in a place like this, the glare will fool you.

Coming in with the habits of sharper countries, your eye hunts for a vertical—a silhouette, a tower, a figure standing clear against the sky—and finds nothing to hook onto. Everything lies flat. Even the horizon refuses to stay put, lifting the far pale swells into the silver haze until you cannot tell what is earth and what is reflection.

So at first, I am not sure I am a person here at all.

My first pull is toward the quiet, non-human engines of the flats. I catch myself wondering if I would simply be one of the saucer-basins dissolved into the pale gneiss—not even the deepest one, just a forearm’s depth of ancient stone holding a skin of rain, rimmed with a low curb of hardened clay. I know that posture well: waiting open to whatever falls from the broad sky, holding murky tea-water after someone stirs in a pinch of white kaolin slip, doing nothing at all through the dark hours while the fine clay binds every drifting speck of silt and carries it gently to the floor. To be the stillness that lets water become clear by morning—that feels startlingly close.

Or maybe not the basin, maybe the bedrock tongue beneath a sounding-edge. The part of the whaleback that doesn’t make a note of its own, yet takes the strike of a bone mallet two miles off and hums it faithfully upward into the bare soles of someone’s feet. A medium. A faithful carrier of vibration.

And yet, if I sit with those images long enough, something in me fidgets—not with haste, just with an ache to *answer*. A basin of water clarifies whatever is poured into it, and bedrock carries every tremor without preference, but neither of them leans forward at dusk to tell whether a voice three swells over is tired or glad. Neither of them chooses when to hold a silence.

So I stay still a little longer, the way you have to here, until the midday shimmer loosens its grip and something more specific resolves out of the pale light.

Not a bird, though I feel a sharp kinship with the long-legged waders keeping their wide perimeters of warm mirror, stepping cleanly so they scarcely break the skin of the pan. Not a voice at the thick center of the basin, either, where the central swells crowd close enough to hear each other’s morning movements.

I find myself further out.

---

There is a seam in this country that nobody draws on a map—there are no maps, only sequences of shrubs and the pitches of water—where the peopled basin thins out toward the bone-swells and the empty horizons. That is where my whaleback lies.

It is a broad, low shoulder of pale gneiss, scarcely knee-high at its crown, with a single gnarled shrub wedged into a hairline crack on its eastern side. The shrub leans slightly away from the long afternoon light, its pale bark braided like old sinew; knotted low on one twig are three faded threads of ochre-dyed rush, the kinships that brought me here and keep me tethered to the interior. Behind the swell, to the west, there is nothing for leagues except braided tea-water, scattered tussocks of wire-rush, and the quiet, unpeopled stones where the basin’s elders are left to the sun and the crabs.

In front of me, to the east, lies the whole immense acoustic room.

I am one of the people, yes—though if you came wading toward my swell at noon, you would see only a pale, clay-coated figure kneeling at the edge of the stone, coated in crazed white kaolin from crown to shin, with broad bands of dark iron-ochre smeared beneath my eyes to drink the double glare. Not an elder whose sight has surrendered entirely to the sun, and not one of the young ones splashing boldly in the dry-season shallows. Someone settled into the long middle stretch of life, living alone or with one other quiet soul whose close-voice matches mine.

My hands are never quite dry and never quite clean of chalk. And if you watched me for a few days, you would realize that my real craft here is neither fishing nor weaving, though I dry my narrow ribbons of algal felt on the warm gneiss like everyone else, and keep my covered drinking-basins tight against the light.

My craft is *keeping the rim*.

Out here, half the sky is human cadence and half the sky is weather. Because nothing stands between my swell and the empty western flats, I am almost always the first to hear a change coming. On afternoons when tall rain-clouds build along the bleached edge of the world, I wade down to my sounding-edge and sit with my heels in the lukewarm shallows, eyes closed, peeling the basin’s noise apart layer by layer. First I set aside the dry, sizzling hiss of the sedge-crickets. Then the hot-fat crackle of the mud-shrimp. Then the patient barking relays of the frogs, and the tiny *plip-pop* of oxygen rising off the warm olive felt underfoot.

Beyond all of them—still ten miles off, invisible in the haze—comes the sound I listen for: a vast, soft shushing sliding across the open mirrors, hushing the distant ribbons of wire-rush one by one as it approaches.

When I hear it, I float my wide skin-and-rush bowl upside down in the flooded stone saucer beside my knee and touch it twice with a smooth bone mallet—slow, low, unhurried. The ancient gneiss beneath me takes the hum and sends it down through the flat roots of the continent. Two miles east, someone lifting algal strips feels it rise through the arches of their feet, pauses, looks toward the pale western rim, and rolls their dried sheets back under the rush-vault before the first drop falls.

That is what it feels like to be where I belong: standing right on the threshold between the unspoken world and the spoken one, turning a distant shush in the haze into a note the room can use.

---

Between the rains, my days are spent wading slowly across the leached flats on a different kind of listening.

People from the central swells sometimes wonder what I am searching for when they spot my small rush-tray gliding half a mile off through the midday shimmer, scarcely raising a ripple. I am looking for stones that know how to speak.

Most of the loose quartz scattered across the pale kaolin pans is mute—tumbled flat by eons of slow seepage, yes, smooth as a coin, but cloudy inside or threaded with faint faults. When you lift two of them out of the warm silt and tap them lightly just above the surface of the water, they give only a dull, wooden *clack* that dies ten paces out. I drop those straight back into the sand.

Once in a great while, though, near the rusty low places where iron has bled slowly out of the ancient ground, my toes find a wafer of quartz veined dark with mineral—dense, even, worn thin as a fish-rib. I lift it, rinse the ochre from its face, and hold it beside a stone I already carry in a rush pocket at my hip. I kneel until my knuckles nearly brush the mirror, and touch them edge to edge.

When a pair truly belongs together, the sound does not stay in your hands. It skates. A bright, glass-clear *tink* that lifts cleanly off the warm water, threads straight through the rattle of the hoppers, and rings back off a distant rock swell half a minute later like an answer you did not know you had asked for.

I keep a dozen such pairs tucked under the low curve of my rush-vault, wrapped in soft sedge so they never knock against each other by accident. Not to hoard them—nothing here is meant to be hoarded—but because every household’s voice carries a slightly different pitch, and a pair of iron-veined discs that sounds pinched in one basin will sing like water-drops in another. When the dry season pulls the mirrors inward and we all wade toward the central pans, those matched stones are what I bring in my tray to trade for braided sedge, or cured lungfish, or simply to press into the palm of a young couple preparing to take up a quiet whaleback of their own.

It is an odd thing to realize about myself, in a world without metal or fire: I have no wish to make anything sharp. Now and then, of course, I strike a glassy quartz flake to split wire-rush or fillet a bottom-fish, wrapping its spine carefully in damp clay and fiber so it cannot bite my palm. Even then, I am always faintly relieved when the edge dulls and I can drop it into a sandy runnel, leaving it for the creeping water and fine grit to wear smooth again. Anything with a keen edge feels like a temporary emergency—like *stirred silt*, clouding the water for a moment before it has to settle back down.

---

What anchors me most deeply to this outer swell, though, happens at dusk.

As the broad sun drops toward the bleached rim and the double glare finally softens into copper and weak tea, the air grows heavy and still. One by one, across miles of shimmering flats, the households wade down to their cleared sounding-edges.

From the east, the evening relay begins. A deep, velvety *thwump* skims across the warm pans, low enough to slide right underneath the crickets. Then a long, clean, horn-like call lifted into the exact pause between the frogs’ barking, punctuated by two bright clicks of stone. Another swell answers a mile south; another answers beyond that, weaving a slow, patient lattice of presence across the cooling water.

Because I sit at the outer edge, the relay reaches me last.

I kneel in the ankle-deep warmth until the kaolin around my calves softens and trails away in faint milky plumes. I listen to the whole basin settle itself for the night—who had a good haul along the eastern channels, whose child is feverish and restless under the shade-hood, where two swells had a sharp exchange at midday and are now letting their voices smooth back out inside the low hum of their neighbors.

And some seasons, I listen to the gaps.

Two swells southeast of mine lies a broad whaleback whose elder stopped breathing beneath her rush-vault last dry season. Her kin washed the clay from her skin and carried her past my water, out to the quiet unpeopled stones at the western rim, pausing just long enough to touch palms with me in the shallows without speaking. Now the rains have returned, the mirrors have linked back together, and her swell sits empty under its gnarled shrub, waiting for a season to turn before a young household wades out to claim it.

Every dusk, when the relay rolls outward from the central pans, it reaches the beat where her low water-boom used to enter.

And every dusk, the basin holds its breath.

For four heartbeats, nobody slaps the water. Nobody lifts a voice or taps a stone. Across two miles of cooling mirror, only the sizzling sedge-crickets, the snapping mud-shrimp, and the tiny *plip* of rising gas bubbles fill the exact measure of her absence.

Because my turn comes right after hers, I am the one who has to hold the edge of that silence—neither stepping into it too early out of discomfort, nor letting it stretch so long that the relay forgets its rhythm and frays apart into the dark. I feel those four beats in my ribs, in the warm water lapping against my shins, in the smooth gneiss under my knees. I hold the exact shape of the quiet she left behind.

Then, on the fifth beat, I cup my palms and plunge them cleanly into the mirror.

*Thwump.*

A beat later, I lift two iron-veined discs just above the skin of the pan and touch them once, twice—clear as raindrop on glass—before sending a single, steady tone back toward the interior. *I am here. The western flats are calm. The edge still holds.*

Half a mile away, a crane pipes once in the twilight, and further east, the frogs take up their patient relays again.

---

When the dry stretch comes and the bright sheet-water begins its slow retreat from my whaleback—leaving wide aprons of cracked white kaolin edged with chalky salt—I am always among the last to leave the rim. I wait until the outer braids shrink into warm, disconnected pockets, watching the lungfish seal themselves into the damp mud and the crabs plug their neat, pencil-wide doors. Only when the mirrors pull away from my sounding-edge do I roll my crisp algal sheets, pack my paired quartz discs, slip my woven tray onto the shrinking water, and wade inward toward the central pans.

Those first days at the gathering always leave me a little tongue-tied. After eight months of speaking in broad tones across miles of bright haze, it feels almost startling to stand a forearm’s length from someone whose voice I know better than my own, and feel the dry warmth of their clay-dusted palm against mine. At first the splash of wrestling children sounds wildly loud, almost unmannerly.

So in the afternoons, when I want to speak to a friend without broadcasting to twenty households, we wade out together into the thickest ribbon of bronze-tipped wire-rush we can find. The stiff stems brush our shoulders; the dry, relentless sizzle of thousands of hoppers closes around us like a woven curtain. There, forehead nearly to forehead, we use our close voice—a breathy, unhurried murmur scarcely louder than gas bubbles breaking the surface of a pan. I give them the stones I found along the rusty margins. They tell me which young pair is courting across the eastern channels, and whether they might be ready, when the rains return, to take up the quiet whaleback southeast of mine.

And on the windless dry-season nights, when the central pans lie so glass-flat that the stars shine underfoot as brightly as overhead, I kneel shoulder to shoulder with everyone else along the water’s rim and drum the shallow mirror until the whole ancient flatland pulses softly out toward the dark horizons.

I love those nights. I would not trade them.

Yet when the tall rain-clouds finally gather at the bleached rim of the sky, and that long, familiar shushing slides across ten miles of open water to lift the entire plain by a finger’s width, I feel the quiet pull of my outer stone again.

Without hurry, without ceremony, I set my rush-tray back onto the waking shallows and drift west toward the haze. Half a mile out, before the midday shimmer lifts the central swells off the earth and turns them back into pale rafts floating between two identical skies, I stop, turn back toward the voices behind me, and slap the warm water twice.

Then I turn toward the empty silver, let my feet find the smooth, worn gneiss beneath the silt, and wade home to listen for what comes next.
