If I step through a wind-slot and try to feel where my weight naturally settles in this place, my eyes go upward first—probably the way anyone’s would.

There is an immediate pull toward the rim. Toward the skimmers hanging half out over the white glare in their slotted bone visors, scraping frost off waxed hide before the sun can steal it back. Or toward the canopy, where the tree-keepers walk gnarled bottle-wood limbs over a seven-story drop and watch a single branch across thirty years for the dry split that announces a Yielding. Even toward the rare walkers who slip down the bleached spiral trail into the flat salt-pans and come back months later smelling strangely of alkali and open wind.

I linger on each of those for a minute, testing how they fit. And one by one, I let them go.

I do not have the stomach for the rim. The thought of leaning out into that thin, unheld glare—or worse, living down on the dead plains where the wind can lay its hands on all four sides of a house at once—gives me the same quiet vertigo the people of the bowl feel when they watch the walkers shrink to specks. I admire the tree-keepers’ patience; I recognize that kind of waiting, the slow attention that never forces a green limb. Still, my balance is not made for high branches over open air. I would rather look *up* at the woven roof of roots and sewn goat-hide than walk across its spine.

So I keep going down.

Bare heels find the four-inch trough worn glossy into the living stone of the stairs. Down past the sixth and fifth tiers, where the light is bright enough to dry clay jars on the balconies and the fluting call-language darts back and forth across the chasm like bright needles. Down through the warm, dim belly of the city—past the weighted looms clacking in time with the roof-cicadas, past the metal-beater coaxing an ancient bronze blade one hair thinner on his boulder, past goats arguing softly with old women on the landings. The brown-gold twilight thickens to soot-and-amber. The air grows closer, smelling of warm limestone dust, roasted millet, dry dung-smoke, and the sweet musk of fruit skins.

At the very bottom of the lowest court, where the glow of the sunken hearths stops short of a dark, rock-cut mouth in the basalt floor, the bakery heat meets a thin tongue of mineral chill rising from beneath the city.

That is where my feet stop.

I wouldn’t be one of the oldest cellar-minders yet—not cave-pale, not stiff-jointed enough to have given up the lower stairs altogether—though I think I am slowly becoming one, year by year. I live half a turn above the vault threshold, in a narrow, low-linteled room whose walls carry ten skins of ochre plaster and the shorn dark hair of four generations who slept here before me. It is a hinge-place. Behind my back lies the cool basalt core where the city’s water rests in darkness; just outside my doorway, seven stories of tangled timber, fleshy vines, ladders, birds, and breathing bodies climb toward the humming stone lip.

My work has no grand name. Above, in the middle galleries, people simply know to send down for me when a clay jar begins to sing wrong, or when an old amphora finally gives up its seam.

By morning, I grind broken pottery.

Nothing leaves the bowl, and clay is no exception. Whenever a cooking-pot splits over a hearth or a water-measure slips from a child’s cord and shatters on the concave stairs, the sherds find their way down to my low doorway in woven baskets. I sit cross-legged beside a smooth basalt saddle-stone, my forearms sealed dull and dry with tallow, crushed silver-herb, and limestone dust, and I roll a heavy riverless cobble over the broken pieces until they become fine orange grit. Fresh clay dug from the deep seams is too smooth, too eager to shrink and tear itself apart in the dung-kiln; it cannot survive the heat alone. It needs the ground bones of older vessels kneaded back into its belly to hold its shape. I sort the dust by coarseness into narrow leather pouches—fine meal for narrow-necked flasks, rougher grit for the fat storage jars—and send them back up the cliff-face on the squeaking grease-slicked pulleys.

There is something in that labor that fits my hands like an old awl. I do not make anything out of nothing. I take what has already held water, held millet, held heat, and been broken by use, and I prepare it so the next vessel can endure a little longer.

By afternoon, when the heat in the upper tiers presses down hardest and even the goats lie flat against the shadowed plaster, I step down into the vaults.

Here the roar of the city softens to a low vibration in the soles of the feet. Even down in the basalt core, you can feel the wind shearing over the mountain lip seven stories up—a steady, subterranean bass note that lives in the rock itself. Along the cool galleries sit the great clay amphorae, shoulder to shoulder in the dark, plugged with beeswax and resin-soaked wool. Some were filled last month; some hold a lean winter’s reserve sealed away three or four years back.

This is the second half of my work: listening.

I walk slowly down the rows in the smoky orange wash of a single tallow saucer, tapping each jar’s curved belly with the second knuckle of my right hand. Every vessel has its own pitch, and after years down here, the dark stops feeling empty and turns into a room full of low voices. A full jar answers with a heavy, stubborn *thum*, solid as the basalt behind it. A jar half-drawn rises a clean fifth. You can even hear the age of the clay in the ring—whether it was tempered fine or coarse, whether it was fired long or pulled early from a scarce dung-fire.

What I am really listening for is a stutter.

Before a drop ever darkens the floor, before your fingertips can even find a damp shadow on the terracotta, a hairline crack changes the note. The bell-tone loses its tail; it dies flat and breathless against the knuckle, as if the jar had opened its mouth in the dark. When I hear that hesitation, I crouch beside the amphora with my lamp, running the backs of my dry fingers across the cool curve until I feel the faintest chill of moisture trying to escape into the air.

Then I warm a thumb-sized lump of cured hyrax-stone in a spoon of goat-tallow over the saucer-flame. The sharp, smoky smell of the lamp turns sweet and woody—the scent of sunlit cliff-ledges brought down into the dark. While the resin is soft and glossy, I press it cleanly into the hairline weep with a bone spatula worn translucent at the edge, smoothing it until the clay is sealed shut again. *What is inside stays inside.* Then I tap the shoulder once more, just to hear the note come back whole.

I think I would love the quietness of that repair more than any spectacle in the city. Nobody stops the looms for a patched hairline crack; nobody whispers blessings over my shoulder on the stairs the way they do for the skimmers coming down from the frost. That is fine with me. The skimmers pull life out of the freezing sky. I just make sure the bowl doesn’t lose what they bleed their knuckles to bring in.

In the evenings, when the children climb down from the galleries with clay measures tied to hemp cords, I sit beneath the low vault rafters beside the elder minders. My fingers are learning their arithmetic by touch—the long knotted sinew cords hanging from the beams, strung with drilled beads of pale limestone and dull cold-worked copper. A slide of three limestone beads for a family of potters on the fourth tier; a knot checked by thumb-pressure where a reserve amphora was unsealed during the dry spring two years past. I like that our memory isn’t scratched flat onto wasted skins. It hangs in the dark above our heads, weighted and taut, swaying slightly whenever a warm draft slips down from the courts.

Because my work is pitched to low sounds, my voice rarely climbs into the bright whistling calls that cross the upper chasm. Once in a while I step out into the bottom court, tilt my chin up toward the tangled green ceiling, and send a single thin, reedy note threading cleanly between the wind’s bass hum and the cliff-swifts’ rattle—just enough to ask a potter on the third gallery for a lump of beeswax, or to tell a roof-tender that a cracked herb-trough can be sent down for mending. Even that brief call feels careful, economical, drawn straight from the ribs without wasting a breath on the air. Mostly I stay in the close register: soft murmurs in the shadowed lanes, a flick of the chin, two dry fingers resting lightly on a neighbor’s forearm to say *wait*, or *enough*, or *I heard you*.

I am not alone on my threshold, of course. Nobody is alone in the bowl.

A pale, blunt-hoofed nanny goat from the courtyard weaver’s pen has decided that the cool draft leaking out of the vault stairs belongs to her during the stifling afternoons; she folds her knees right across my step and chews dry thorny-leaves with her eyes half-shut, forcing me to step cleanly over her bony spine whenever I carry a basket of sherds. Near my doorway lamp lives a copper-backed skink with a faint notch in the tip of its tail—*little sweeper*—waiting with absolute, bead-eyed stillness for the grain-moths that wander down toward the tallow flame. When I crush a dust-beetle under my grinding-stone, I flick it onto the warm plaster by my knee and watch the lizard dart out, snatch it, and vanish back into the crack beside the lintel.

Even the room itself is company. The crooked bottle-wood beam over my doorway is glossy black with a century of hearth-smoke and scarred with old rawhide lash-grooves; I know the weaver’s loft it held up before this one, and the storm that brought its parent branch down from the upper ledges in my grandfather’s youth. Behind the thickened mud-brick buttress beside my sleeping-mat, tucked neatly into a stone fissure and marked with dabs of ochre, rest the dry bundled bones of two elders who kept the vault cords long before I learned the pitch of clay. Leaning my back against that warm plaster wall at night doesn’t feel strange or solemn. It feels like sitting beside someone who is already awake and watching the dark with you.

There is only one time when the peace of my threshold breaks.

Now and then, in the deepest heat of late summer, the high-altitude wind outside drops dead. I feel it in the vaults a heartbeat before the city above goes quiet. The basalt beneath my heels stops carrying that faint, familiar bass tremor. Even the rows of amphorae go strangely dull under my palm, as if a hand had been laid flat over a drumhead.

Up through the stairwell, the looms falter. The bronze-smith’s *tink-tink* stops mid-stroke. Even the swifts huddle quiet in their mud-gourds beneath the eaves. Without the wind shearing over the stone lip, the whole bowl feels suddenly unlidded—cracked wide open beneath an enormous, pale, listening sky.

In that silence, I set down my bone spatula and sit on the lowest glossy basalt step with my knees drawn in. I lay one palm flat against the cool belly of the oldest reserve-jar—the one sealed four winters back, its clay tempered with the dust of vessels nobody alive remembers—and I keep my mouth closed, breathing slow and dry through my nose, waiting. Thinking of how thin every wall really is. How much stubborn work it takes to keep a little warmth, a little green, a little water from vanishing back into the salt-pans below.

And then, high up in the overhanging clefts, a string of hollow bird-bones gives a thin, dry moan.

The deep hum slides back into the basalt lip, closing the ceiling over us again. Above my head, a shuttle clacks once, twice, then finds its steady rhythm. A hyrax barks sharply from an upper ledge. The goat on my threshold lets out a long, rattling sigh through her nostrils.

By the time evening settles and the freezing dark rushes in outside the mountain, the bats are pouring out through the wind-slots in a chittering black ribbon, and the tallow lamps are winking awake along all seven stacked tiers. Someone on the fifth gallery begins to tap a slow, playful rhythm on the belly of an empty water-jar—a hollow note that would send me reaching for resin down in the vaults, though up there it only means dinner and company. The smell of roasted millet, salted goat-cheese, and caught pot-steam drifts down the corkscrewing stairs.

I wipe the orange sherd-dust from my palms with a scrap of raw wool, press the cloth gently against the roots of the silver-herb in the clay trough beside my door so nothing goes uncounted, and step back into the warm, brown-gold dark—listening to the whole crowded, leafy jar breathe slowly through the night.
