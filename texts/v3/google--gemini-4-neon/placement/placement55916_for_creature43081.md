If I woke out on the gravel flats, I would not linger there long. The sky is too wide, the wind comes from every quarter at once, and there is nothing to set a shoulder against—only stiff sedge and grey puddles mirroring back an empty lid of daylight. I would follow the cold, chalky smell rising from the crack in the earth, find the first steep stair cut into the blue-black shale, and step down below the lip before my eyes had even finished watering in the glare.

I would not stop at the topmost tiers, either, though I could sit for a long while watching the weavers work up there in the clean daylight. There is something deeply enviable in the way their fingers read green osier—how they braid air and leave gaps where a spout or a lip will grow, seeing stone inside a bent twig the way another mind sees a house inside a stand of timber. Perhaps I would have spent my younger years up on those bright slate ledges, peeling bark with a flint blade, learning to turn a hanging cage a quarter-turn every morning before the crust thickened unevenly. Everyone in the cleft learns willow first. You cannot understand the rock here until you know the shape of what bends beneath it.

Still, my feet would keep carrying me downward, testing each slick rounded tread with the ball of my felt-wrapped sole, elbow tucked close to my ribs, until the sky narrowed to a pale strip between overlapping eaves and the light turned cool, watery, and blue.

That is where I find myself: not at the bright dry rim, and not yet down on the warm benches of the deepest floor where the elders listen to the sumps, but right in the wet seam-belt two-thirds of the way down—the place where the horizontal fractures in the dark cliff weep hardest, and where the town does most of its quiet breathing.

If I ask what I am doing there, my hands answer before my thoughts do. They are already reaching into the cold mist beneath a lashed alder bridge, thumb slick with tallow and marsh-wax, feeling along the underside of a hollowed elder-wood trough where it rests upon its bone pivot.

I am one of the people who tend the balance.

It is not a title anyone speaks aloud, nor a guild with a sign above its door—nothing in a pocket two hundred paces wide is grand enough for that. It is simply the work that gathers around a person whose ears are sharper than their eyes, and who feels a quiet, persistent unease whenever a moving thing begins to stiffen into stone.

Every forty minutes, when the deep bedrock siphons let go and the cold water surges upward through the fractures, the whole stacked town speaks in wood and water. First the long heavy troughs beneath the north bridges fill and dip forward with a muted *clack*, spilling sheets of water down the slate chutes and across the hanging drip-racks; then the middle alleys answer in a cascading, overlapping chatter; then the smaller troughs beside the lower stairs tip and swing back upright with soft thuds. For five minutes the cleft is a single wooden clockwork breathing out. Then the surge ebbs, the weeping springs settle back to their faint *plip-tock*, and the hush returns.

Most people listen to that chorus the way they listen to their own heart—noticing it only when a beat drops. I listen to the spaces *between* the beats.

A trough sits right on the knife-edge between the town’s two states: what bends and moves, and what has turned to limestone. Carve a length of seasoned elder-wood, rub it warm with rendered tallow and marsh-wax, seat it cleanly on a pared leg-bone pin, and for a month it will dip and return as smoothly as an eyelid. But it hangs directly in the spray of the mineral seams. Every turn leaves an invisible skin behind. A pale ridge begins to gather along the front lip, waxy at first, then chalk-firm, then hard enough to turn a fingernail. That small extra weight forward makes the trough tip three breaths too early—before it is full—sending a thin, wasted splash against a stair-wall instead of cleanly across the woven pitchers hanging below. Or worse, fine sinter creeps into the notch around the bone pivot. The swing grows sluggish. The wood hesitates for half a heartbeat, then sticks upright while water brims over its back edge and darkens a neighbor’s ceiling.

My workshop is scarcely wider than a pantry, tucked into a wet angle beneath a vaulted stone-grown landing where two narrow alleys cross a vertical light-well. I chose it—or settled into it as the knees of the man before me stiffened and he moved down to the warmer lowest tier—because from that threshold you can hear nearly half the cleft at once. Sound carries strangely down the wet black shafts. Sit just inside my heavy felt door-curtain with a bowl of lukewarm broth and the tallow dish warming near the peat coals, and when the surge rises, I can trace the water down four tiers with my eyes shut: the deep *clack* above the east cistern, the twin quick taps under the weaver-bridge, the slightly wet, hollow drag of the old trough by the south stair that will need its pivot scraped before tomorrow evening.

Inside the cell, tucked into the dry shadow away from the mist, sit the tools of my trade: no iron anywhere, only three widths of ground chert lashed into hide-wrapped oak handles, a hooked flint scraper for teasing fresh crust out of pivot-sockets without chewing the elder-wood, a small hide-padded mallet, and bundles of bone pins—deer leg-bones and heavy flatland cattle-bone traded at the outer cairns—rubbed smooth with fine shale grit until they gleam like old ivory. Along the back wall season spare lengths of elder, peeled and hollowed, waiting their turn in the wet dark. And hanging from a hemp line just outside my narrow bladder-skin window are not the usual three petrified signal-gourds, but five.

Three are for talking to the stairs—the thin chalky blush that gives a dry *tok*, the mid-crust woody knock, the thick pale bowl that rings clear down the light-well. The other two are only for my own ear, dipped to half-measures between them. When I tap the belly of a trough with my knuckle, or strike a growing stone gutter with the butt of an antler pick, I match its pitch against those five gourds in my head. Sound tells you what the dim blue light hides: whether a wall of pale stone is still thin and laced with live willow, whether a hollow has trapped water inside it, or whether the mineral crust on a wooden runner has thickened past the point where scraping will save it.

Because eventually, scraping does not save it.

That is the part of the work I think about most when my fingers are busy in the dusk. You can keep a trough swinging for five winters, maybe seven, paring away pale sinter from its lip, waxing its belly, shifting its balance by lashing a small petrified river-stone onto its tail with rawhide—which the mist soon stiffens into a neat white collar that never slips. Beneath the trough, the small glossy-black snails creep along the wet underside, grazing the cold biofilm, their own coiled shells frosted white with the same mineral film. I never brush them off when I work. We are in the same bargain, those snails and I: keeping just enough motion in us to carry the rock without letting it cement us to the wall.

Yet sooner or later, even with wax and chert, the elder-wood drinks too much cold water and grows heavy and dark; the limestone crust creeps into the grain itself. One morning you lift the trough off its bone pin and feel in your wrists that it no longer wants to swing. It wants to stay.

We do not smash it or haul it up to the wind-scoured rim to rot. Instead, my apprentices—or whatever young pair from the upper lofts has come down to learn the middle dark for a season—help me carry it two alleys over, where a worn slate runnel is beginning to leak. We wedge the tired trough level on a bed of dry-laid shale, direct a gentle seep across its rim, and stop scraping it. Within a season, what used to tilt and clack every forty minutes has fused to the cliff and become a silent, milky-white aqueduct, holding water steadily while a fresh, pale elder trough swings cleanly on bone pins two spans above its back.

Everything in my hands moves along that slow road—from supple to balanced, from balanced to fixed—and my work is simply to widen the middle stretch for as long as it will hold.

Living at the hinge of the water also puts me quietly inside the town’s small, unspoken weather.

Down here, nobody shouts a quarrel into the wet stone. A raised voice hits the curved domes and comes back ugly, like a dropped mallet, making everyone behind six different curtains flinch. When two households sharing a stair-landing fall out—over a cracked storage jar, an unreturned bundle of cured peat, a harsh murmur overheard through a thin shared wall—the anger goes straight into the water. One neighbor simply forgets to run a chert blade along the lip of the shared overflow chute. Over two weeks, a pale tongue of sinter grows outward by the width of a thumb. The falling sheet of runoff shifts two fingers out of true, just enough that every surge sends a cold spray across the other family’s felt door-curtain, stiffening its hem into eggshell by morning.

Usually I hear it before either household taps a gourd. A deflected sheet sounds thinner—a flat *shhh* against hanging wool instead of a clean splash into a stone basin.

I do not meddle with speeches. There is no room in an alley this narrow for moralizing, and no one welcomes an arbiter standing in their doorway. Instead, I wait for the quiet between turns, step onto the slick landing on damp felt soles, wipe my palms on the dry rag at each threshold, and work a thumbful of tallow into my knuckles before I speak a low greeting. Sometimes I simply mention, eyes on the dripping ceiling rather than on either face, that the north chute is pulling heavy and throwing the lower trough off its beat—that if it stays unscraped another ten turns, the drain beneath the elders’ bench two stories down will begin to back up.

That is almost always enough. Down here, you may nurse a cold grudge against the shoulder that brushes yours on the stairs, but nobody wants to flood the floor where their grandmother’s knees are aching beside the peat brazier. Before the next dawn, I will hear the soft, dry-wet *scritch* of chert on fresh crust up in the dark. By noon, a fresh bundle of peeled willow will be leaning quietly against the cleared chute, and the chorus of the surge will fall back into its clean, familiar order.

There are times, between the afternoon mist and the lighting of the tallow lamps, when I sit on the lip of the light-well outside my door, knees drawn up inside my peat-dyed wool, letting my reddened fingers warm around a stone-grown cup of steeped bitter-cress water. The cup is pale as milk, light as dried bone, and keeps the heat in my palms because of the tiny hollow veins where its willow skeleton shrank away inside the rock.

If I lean my head back against the dark wet shale and look straight up through the narrow jagged slot of roofs and lashed bridges, I can just see a sliver of the cold outer sky. Once, maybe twice in a season, a slate-colored dipper drops down from the marshes and perches on a knife-edge tooth of the rim far above. I always stop rolling my bast-cord when I see it. It bobs twice on its thin legs, peering down into our stacked, dripping comb, entirely untroubled by the shadows and the hush, and then flickers away over the flats. I feel a brief, strange fondness for it—not envy for the wide sky, which still makes my shoulders hunch just to picture, but gratitude that something from up there can look down into our wound in the earth and see it clearly for what it is: not a tomb, just a shelter built out of cold patience.

Then I look downward instead, through the slats of the wooden bridge below my feet, toward the deepest tiers where the amber glow of bladder-skin windows is already beginning to reflect in the black slick stone.

My hands are broader at the knuckles than they used to be. On cold mornings, after a long frost up on the flats sends an extra chill down the seams, my fingers want to stay curled around the phantom grip of a scraper until I work warm tallow into the joints. I know the vertical map of a life here. In ten winters, or fifteen, climbing out onto the wet alder beams to seat a high bone pin will ask too much of my knees. I will pass my hooked flint blades and my five hanging gourds to someone whose wrists are still quick in the dim light, pack my oiled wool and my bowls, and move down two stories to the lowest occupied tier, where the flatland wind never reaches and the peat-heat pools softly under the heavy domes.

I am not afraid of going down there. I already know several of the rooms sealed beneath those smooth, rounded alley humps—the old basket-soaking room whose doorway narrowed past my shoulders six winters ago; the cistern where my uncle’s work-bench still sits under clear, icy water, watched over by the tiny translucent amphipods flickering like mica-slivers in the dark. I will sit on a low bench beside the main sumps, felt draped across my knees, doing the slow, wide scraping that keeps the bottom drains breathing, listening to the troughs tip overhead in the exact order I helped tune, able to tell by a faint shift in pitch when a seam widens after a hard freeze.

And later still—when the forty-minute tide begins to sound far away, as if it were surging in another cleft altogether, and my fingers finally grow tired of their small, restless motion—I know what will come next. The young hands up in the bright dry lofts will strip fresh green osier and weave an open-meshed shroud, light as a winter birdcage, fastened with willow ties. They will carry me down in the hush between turns, felt soles whispering over the humped white stairs, and lay me on a dark shale bench inside a room whose lintel has already shrunk to the height of a waist.

No one will seal me in with mortar. They will simply step out, take down the curtain, and let the scraping stop.

Season by season, the cold water I spent my middle years balancing will creep across the sill and sheet cleanly down the walls. It will skin the green willow around me in smooth, pale limestone, turning the open weave into a hollow, ribbed shell resting in clear quiet water while the oval doorway draws slowly shut like a pale eyelid. Above that sealed dome, they will level a new course of split blue-black shale, lash fresh alder beams, hang a new elder-wood trough on a pared bone pin, and listen for its first clean *clack* in the dark.

I think I would rest well there—having kept the rhythm true for my stretch of turns, and then yielding, without hurry, to become part of the stone that holds the next tier up toward the light.
