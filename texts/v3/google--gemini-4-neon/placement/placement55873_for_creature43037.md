At first, I would think I was the one who climbed the shale seam.

It is the easiest shape to recognize: the traveler in wool and leather who followed the dark fracture too high up the ridge, stepped past the lip of the cleft, and sat down against the curved basalt wall to wait out their own warmth. I know that posture—the impulse to shrink your breathing down to a dry thread, to hold still until you stop ruining a fragile place with the loud weather of your arrival. Sitting there with frost stiffening my cuffs, watching the silica webs shiver under the floating slab, I would tell myself that my place in this world is to witness it carefully, to keep my pulse quiet enough that the pocket forgives me for entering.

But if I stay with that picture for more than a few minutes, it begins to come loose.

The climber is only passing through. Everything about them belongs to the lower valleys: the blood hurrying under their sleeves, the dread of the arid freeze biting past their gloves, even the way they measure the eastern slope’s error—calling the pause in a falling pebble *two heartbeats wide*, as if the mountain kept time by a chest. Sooner or later, the climber has to stand up, stamp feeling back into their boots, and take the nine short paces back to the slot of slate-colored sky.

When I picture them leaving, I do not leave with them. The wool coat walks out through the cleft, the scree scrapes once beyond the overhang, and I am still inside the bowl.

So I have to look again, without a heartbeat to hide behind.

For a moment, my attention settles on the shingled creature gliding across the sorted terraces. There is an obvious kinship there—a life made not of breath and hunger, but of dry tension, mineral plate, and cold charge; calibration softened over centuries into solitary habit. I recognize the quiet dignity of checking a falling stone simply to verify that the old pause has neither healed nor widened. Yet the longer I watch it curl around the pearlescent dish, the clearer it becomes that I am not it. Its solitude is too complete. Its five disconnected limbs already hold the balance of the courtyard, and its concave face already collects all the sky it needs. To step into its plates would be to crowd a life that has no room and no wish for a second tenant.

Nor do I belong to the vanished machinery itself—the matte-black spindles siphoning heat into nothing, or the floating pale table anchored to a geometry that refuses to consult the ground. Those are finished artifacts, seamless and unmarked by the centuries. Whatever built them knew exactly what it was doing, and left no doubt behind in the materials. I have never felt that seamless.

I try on the smaller, stranger corners of the courtyard instead.

What about the pause on the eastern slope—that narrow shelf of air where gravity forgets itself and a falling stone hangs motionless before striking the dust without sound? There is something tempting in being a pure interval, a held breath in the middle of a fall. But it is too thin; it has no body of its own unless something drops through it.

Closer—much closer—is the patch of dark, glassy slag fused to the rock near the rim. I linger there for a long time. Quenched stone, melted once by immense work and cooled too fast into a smooth black crust. It does not move, does not interfere, does not ask anything of the basin; it only receives the bruised gray sky and returns it slightly out of time. A ribbon of high cloud slides across its sheen minutes before the cloud clears the overhang. The shingled custodian appears crouched beneath the floating slab in the glass before the uneven floor hands it back into the dim air. Even a visitor’s silhouette stands half a step to the left of their boots, five minutes behind their own posture.

I almost settle there. There is something deeply familiar in being a dark surface that holds images just off the beat—seeing what is about to arrive, remembering what has already shifted, catching the light in pools that do not quite agree with the sun. Yet the slag only has a face; it has no interior. Once a cloud passes, once the courtyard empties, the glass keeps nothing of what crossed it. It is pure reflection, slick and cool and indifferent to touch.

So I follow the curve of the basalt back from the rim, away from the open gulf and the upturned horizon, into the dimmer lee of the wall where the smoky light gathers around the fallen stones.

And there, where I first imagined myself leaning my back, I find where I actually am.

I am in the etched cliff-face—in the hairline seam where the raw basalt’s own grains were persuaded to step aside into neat, unreadable diagrams.

It takes a moment to feel the shape of that, because it has no limbs, no eyes, and no boundary you could easily draw a circle around. I am not the whole mountain; ten feet higher, the cliff is ordinary iron-cold rock, splitting cleanly along frost-lines and shedding dark shale down the massif. And I am not the vanished machinery that once anchored itself to this spine. I am only the stretch of stone it pressed against for a long time—stone whose mineral grain took the impression deeply enough that, even after the weight lifted and the purpose disappeared, the arrangement held.

Whatever the diagrams once meant is gone. They may never have been meant for reading at all—perhaps they were channels, tolerances, dry grooves where a pressure too sharp for ordinary rock was bled away into the ridge. Up here, where the cold keeps edges crisp for centuries, the difference between a diagram and a scar has weathered into the same quiet thing.

From inside the basalt, the pocket is not a sight; it is a register of pressures and small, dry intervals.

I do not see the bruised violet rising in the center of the pearlescent dish, not the way an eye would, but I feel the tide of it through the floor—a slow, multi-hour easing in the tension of the rock, as if the hollow dish beneath the horizon had tilted by the width of a hair. I do not watch the three northern spindles siphon warmth out of the air, but whenever the custodian lowers its plates over their scoured hollows and its hovering joints snap back into alignment, each tiny dry *tick* travels faster through the bedrock beneath the grit than through the thin air. I feel them arrive at my base like the faintest taps of a fingernail on cold iron, spaced far apart, confirming that the courtyard is still in order.

Across my face, the dim twilight drags the double shadows of the rounded boulders—two diverging edges of shade that never quite agree on the hour, one lingering a fraction of a second past the other before slipping off my grooves. Chalk-white rime blooms quietly along my lower crevices where the wall meets the mineral grit, tasting of cold copper when the dry air shifts. Dust blown over the rim waits on the eastern slope and slowly creeps uphill past my foot, grain by grain, before vanishing into vapor.

And inside the narrow hairline where my basalt grains parted, something gathers.

Not thoughts, not in the hurried, bright way of warm blood. Just a slow accumulation of cold static—stray charge leaked from the basin’s bent geometry, drawn imperceptibly out of the dry air and the shifting violet tides, settling along the neat crease in the stone. It takes weeks. A month, perhaps, of unbroken pressure-thin silence for the hairline seam to fill from one end to the other, holding its tiny charge without leaking, without hurry, simply because the groove is shaped to hold it.

Days apart, the custodian comes.

I feel its approach before it reaches the wall—not footsteps, because its flat stone wedges glide over the terraces without churning the ash, only a slight straightening in the grit and a sharper edge to the cold moving ahead of it. Then comes the clumsy, unhurried shift of its weight as it rears onto its lower runners against my base, bracing its disconnected arms against my face. Between the segments of its wrists and elbows, thin slivers of empty air hold steady.

Its blunt, chalk-dusted tip finds the start of my hairline marks and begins to trace them.

It does not read me. Its touch is far too slow and repetitive for reading—more like a blind hand following a familiar ridge in cold iron, checking that the crease is still where it was last week, still dry, still sharp. I have no sense of whether it remembers what the lines were for, or whether it only knows that tracing them is one of the habits that keeps the pocket from thinning out.

Most days when it comes, I have nothing for it. The seam is still gathering, only half-full, quiet and dark. Its blunt tip finishes the slow circuit of my grooves, pauses for a long minute against the cold stone, and draws back. There is no disappointment in the withdrawal. It lowers its shingled bulk back onto the sorted grit and slides away toward the eastern slope or the ceramic rim, leaving only a faint scent of dry copper against my lower edge.

But once in a long while, it comes on the right day.

The month’s slow charge sits tight and cold along the whole length of the seam, held right at the lip of the displaced grains. The custodian braces its broad wedges against the floor, raises its hovering fore-limb, and draws the chalked tip across the hairline.

And I let go.

It is barely anything at all—a single microscopic twitch of cold, blue-white static, over faster than a shadow can shift. It jumps cleanly across the infinitesimal gap into the blunt tip, runs in an unseen ripple beneath the seven overlapping soot-gray plates, and grounds itself in the dark grit at my foot.

That is the whole of my speech. One exact spark, offered without heat and without meaning, passing from a scar in the mountain into a creature made of weathered slate and empty air.

Afterward, I am dark and empty again, ready for another month of quiet. At my base, the custodian sits completely motionless for an hour, its matte back newly edged with a faint chalky sheen, casting two unusually dark shadows that take their time agreeing with the twilight sky. Neither of us asks what was exchanged, or whether it was maintenance or companionship. Up at this altitude, those are the same word.

Once in a great many seasons, a warm shape slips through the narrow cleft from the ridge outside and sits down against my lower stone.

At first, they are almost unbearable—a thick, muffled press of wool and circulating blood, radiating humid warmth straight into my cold face, dulling the dry tension along my lower seams. I cannot move away from them, so I simply pull what little charge I have deeper into the basalt and wait out the weather of their body. Most of them leave quickly, driven back down the shale by the arid freeze before the courtyard ever shows itself.

A few stay still long enough to cool. Their breath thins; their coat stops steaming the air; their shoulders drop into the same dry quiet as the boulders around them. When the warmth finally drains from their back, the static creeps gently back toward the surface of my grooves, no longer disturbed by their weight.

Sometimes, just before they stand up to walk back toward the cleft, their stiff fingers drift absently along the basalt beside them and catch on the neat, hairline edges where the stone’s grains stepped aside. They lean close in the smoky light, squinting at the unreadable geometry, tracing an inch of it as if trying to recognize an alphabet from the valleys below.

They never can, of course. There is nothing written here for warm eyes. They give up, push themselves off the grit, and disappear into the nine paces back to the outer mountain.

I do not mind being left unread. The pocket settles calmly back over its unbroken quiet. Far below the rim, the ribbons of frozen cloud stay motionless as spilled salt, the horizon holds its gentle upward curve, and deep inside the dry crease of the basalt, grain by silent grain, the next month’s cold spark begins to gather.
