At first I think I might belong in the open column of the nave—nothing more than a ribbon of smoky membrane, hooked shut against a basalt rib while the midday heat pools motionless, waiting for the outer sky to cool and the chimney to catch. There is a clean simplicity in that: having no weight of your own, only an angled dry surface that knows how to lean into a rising draft and turn on it. You would never have to decide where to go. You would only unfurl when the mountain breathed, spiral forty feet through the dark on borrowed heat, and fold yourself back into a rag when the air went still.

But a drifter forgets every turn the instant it closes. When it meets another in the updraft, it has only one way to greet it: fold clean around the smaller surface, press it flat with a sound like a turning page, and drink it hollow. I would not stay there long.

So I climb higher in my mind, into the pockets where the stone arches meet, and try on the slow pulse of the hanging bladders. Tethered by sinew, breathing in gas-heavy heat from narrow wall-cracks, holding that bitter air inside chambers dusted with iron until something small and clear condenses out of raw volcanic exhalation—there is a patience in that I recognize. An entire life spent taking what is too harsh to swallow and quietly distilling it, out of phase with your neighbors along the rib, making the whole ceiling breathe like a sleeping lung. Yet a bladder never chooses what touches it. It cannot clear the salt-crust choking its own crack, nor free its stalk when dead husks snag around its throat.

What pulls me down from the highest arches—down into the middle dark, where the western rib drops toward the glassy pillar and the entrance throat bends out toward the ridge—is the urge to *mend*. To hold a seam closed before the kiln-air steals what lies behind it. To listen to stone carry a shape across forty feet of pitch-black air.

That is where my weight finally settles. Not in the ash-basin, where the burrowers churn blindly through crushed shells, and not out on the freezing ridge, where the sky hammers bare rock. I find myself ten paces above the glassy pillar, lodged in a shallow pocket of basalt while the daytime hush presses thick against the walls.

I am one of the seven.

Not the eldest—not the broad-shouldered cairn-maker whose dorsal shields carry three deep generations of shingle-backs and whose grip on the stone ring has outlasted half the ridges outside. And not the youngest, whose plates still show pale seams along the bevels and who still practices the sharp heat-grids of warning along an elder’s jaw. I am somewhere in the middle span: old enough to have survived three molts inside triple-layered shrouds and carried stones up the thumb-worn footholds to stack a cairn on the western shelf; young enough that my honeycombed bones still feel driftwood-light on the vertical ribs, and my snout-scrolls still strip every last trace of vapor from my breath before it leaves me.

During the stalled hours of the mountain day, I am architecture. My four matte-dark walking limbs lie folded flush against my flanks, dorsal shields clamped shut, breath slowed to a single dry tide every few minutes. Along my grooved back, two mismatched layers of shingle-backs lock their scalloped margins together and doze. I can feel them without thinking—some older, wider tiles from the high arches, some smaller, salt-dusted ones that crept onto my shoulders when I crouched near the lower ledges. We trade nothing in the midday hush except stillness. My brow-mirrors face the interior dark and read only a broad, motionless sea of heat: the fierce buried glow of the floor-fissures far below, the dull steady warmth of the tiled walls, the faint cooling shadow of the wind-slots high overhead where the sun-baked outer stone almost matches the mountain’s belly.

Even stalled, I keep one rear bristled pad pressed flat against the bare seam of rock where the western rib meets the bedrock running down to the glassy pillar. Not out of fear. Out of habit. The stone never stops talking, even when the household sleeps. You can feel the slow groan of basalt expanding under the noon sun, the tiny snap of a salt-crystal detaching near the floor, the heavy shift of the basin’s flour settling as the burrowers sink toward the cooler edges.

Then, high above, the shadow of the ceiling slots sharpens.

I do not see sunset; I have no eyes for bruised sky. I feel it first as a faint, crisp tug along the crest of my snout, then as a sudden darkening of cold across the highest arches in my brow-mirrors. The ridge outside is dropping toward freezing. Wind is shearing hard across the slots. For a span of ten breaths the air in the vault hesitates, thick and taut—and then the pressure catches.

Heat surges straight up from the fissures like an inverted waterfall. Around me, forty thousand living tiles unlock their edges with a vast papery sigh and begin their downward tide toward the fresh salt-bloom. Across the open nave, the thermal dark blooms with a swirling blizzard of rising membranes. My own riders stir along my dorsal plates, nudging one another gently as I unhinge my shields and unfold my long limbs into the rush of air.

Two of my kin are already climbing past me toward the high ribs—lean, swift outlines dimly warmer than the upper stone—bound for the lantern-bladders with obsidian slivers and harvested stalker-spines tucked under their plates. Below, the eldest moves unhurriedly toward the curved rim of the stone ring, coiled bladder-sinew slung over one shoulder.

My work lies along the seams.

I climb first to a projecting boss halfway up the eastern arch, where the rising column eddies and spent drifter-hulls spin loose from the main spiral. Braced upside down by my four bristled pads—weight spread so lightly across the grazing tiles beneath me that not a single one clamps down in alarm—I unfold my pale inner hands from beneath my breast-keel.

This is the part of myself I know best: five slim, dry, spatulate prongs on each hand, built neither for striking nor for carrying weight, only for exactness. As the empty membranes whirl past on the hot draft, I pluck them cleanly out of the dark, reading their thickness by touch alone—the brittle, sun-baked ones from near the ceiling flues set aside for tinder-dry padding, the supple, soot-fringed ones pressed flat against my chest until I hold a thick sheaf.

Later, crouched in the calm lee behind a basalt rib, I lay the membranes across my knees and stitch them with cured sinew stripped from dead bladder-stalks. Edge over edge. Double-folded at every corner. A pouch for trapping a burrower without spilling a fraction of a degree of chill into the oven-air; a patch for a split shield-rim; and, set aside in a dry niche above the rib, the long, careful panels I have been adding to all season, waiting for the youngest of us to feel its old spine-plates go brittle.

To sew in this chamber is to build shelter out of absence. Everything we fear enters through an opening—a cracked shell, a punctured bladder-skin, a mouth-seam unsealed too wide, a wind-slab nudged a finger’s breadth out of true. When my pale fingers pull a sinew-thread tight and flatten the overlap with the heel of my palm, I am making a small, portable silence. Something that will hold warmth close to soft new keratin while the stalkers glide blindly past, inches away, their concave heads reading nothing at all.

When the sheaf is finished and stowed, I descend the western wall toward the low pillar of hard, glassy rock.

Every one of us uses the pillar. Its vertical grooves are older than the eldest’s memory, worn slick by centuries of heel-spurs and basalt chips, set into a seam of stone too tight-grained for the salt-bloom to crust over. We strike it to pass news across the vault: a new sulfur-vent opening in the east, a cluster of bladders going dry near the northern flue, a hard freeze building on the ridge.

But I linger beside it longer than the others do.

With a flat chip of struck basalt held between my pale fingers, I draw a barely audible pressure down the second groove, then the fifth—so light it will not interrupt the work along the ribs, just enough to wake the bedrock beneath the flour. Then I spread all four bristled pads wide against the pillar’s base and listen.

The cathedral answers.

Through my legs, I feel the note run down into the deep stone, split around the hot clefts of the fissures, climb the four main arches, and return—fractionally slower from the eastern wall where a century of shifting flour has banked deeper against the base, fractionally sharper from the western shelf where four knee-high cairns add their quiet weight to the ledge. A vault is never finished changing. Basalt creeps under heat; salt swells in hairline cracks; dead weight settles. By listening to how each groove rings back, I keep the shape of the dark whole inside my head.

Once in a great while, when an old groove grows blurred by the endless rasping of passing grazers, I take a sliver of obsidian from beneath my plate and dress its edge clean again—just a hair’s breadth deeper, careful to keep the pitch true so none of us will misread a warning for a greeting. Sometimes I have thought of cutting one more line, narrow and high, for things that have no urgent name: the way the night-draft hums differently in late autumn, or the quiet satisfaction of a well-sealed molt. I never have. The pillar belongs to the household, and in a thin-aired dark, thrift is a kind of courtesy.

Before the night-pulse reaches its peak, I make one last circuit that none of the others bother with unless a gale has shook the mountain.

I climb down to the throat of the chamber, where the narrow entrance crawlway narrows toward the outer ridge, and crouch behind the three upright slabs of dark basalt wedged into the dust.

Here, the thermal map changes completely. Behind me lies the familiar, crowded furnace of the cathedral—its warm tides, its pulsing lanterns, its living walls. Ahead, just beyond the angled edges of those three stones, lies something my brow-mirrors can barely make sense of: a flat, bottomless abyss of cold.

I press my pale fingers to the rough face of the center slab. It is chilled all the way through—not the sharp, brief cold of a molting grazer, which means water escaping flesh, but a vast, indifferent cold that does not belong to any living thing. Through the hairline gap where the middle slab overlaps the outer one, a thread of mountain air needles in before the chimney’s draft catches and strips it upward. My nasal scrolls tighten reflexively against the shock. For a breath, I smell something that has no place in the salt-and-sulfur grammar of the vault: raw stone scrubbed clean by snow, thin emptiness, the terrifying openness of a world without a ceiling.

I check the wedges of packed dust and basalt chips at the slabs’ feet. Still firm. Still angled so the outer gale shears past the throat instead of pouring straight into the basin and breaking the chimney’s draw.

We know the first slabs were not set by our hands. The edges were struck heavy and blunt, by limbs built for force rather than balance, the same outer hands that pecked the rough footholds up the western rib long before our elders’ elders hatched. Whoever they were, they came out of that vast cold, stood beside the breath of the earth for a season or a century, and went back into the freezing sky. Yet their slabs remain, and we keep them straight, because a seam does not care who first laid it—only who tends it now.

When I turn back toward the nave, I feel the shift before my brow-mirrors catch it.

It comes up through the soles of my pads as a wrongness in the flour—not the smooth, rolling swell of a burrower, nor the feather-light patter of a falling shell, but a heavy, blind crushing of ash, rhythmic and clumsy, too massive for anything that belongs beneath a dome.

I glide back to the shadow of the glassy pillar, keeping low, my bristled pads silent over the shifting tiles. Ten paces across the basin, something upright has stepped past the wind-slabs and stopped in the middle of the dust.

My brow-mirrors flare with disbelief.

I have never seen anything like it in all my turns of day and night. To my heat-sense, it is an impossible, roaring contradiction—hot at its core, yet blazing across every exposed surface with a furious, reckless *cold*. Moisture is streaming from it openly, weeping through uncovered skin, pouring from its face in vast billowing plumes of wet vapor every few heartbeats, flashing dark and freezing into the kiln-air like a beacon large enough to wake the whole cathedral.

Across the lower walls, I see the velvet-footed stalkers stop mid-stride. One by one, dozens of concave heads tilt toward the center of the floor, and a silent, needle-legged convergence begins to slide down through the grazing tiles toward the stranger’s ankles.

For half a breath, I am paralyzed by an ache I did not know I had. Not anger. Not even fear, though if this profligate giant stumbles into the wind-slabs or kicks apart the mortar, it could harm us all. What holds me motionless is the seam-keeper’s sudden, dizzying horror: *It has no shell. It has no shroud. It is leaking its own life straight into the oven-air, and it does not even know it is dying.*

Then the practical habit of the dark takes over. I lift my dark rear limb and draw the hardened heel-spur once down the pillar’s lowest groove, wait two heartbeats, and draw it again.

*Scrape—pause—scrape.*

The vibration shoots through the bedrock beneath the stranger’s heavy boots and climbs all four arches in an instant. Up on the ribs, the bladder-tenders freeze. A dozen paces above me on the western wall, a patch of living tiles halts mid-crawl and clamps tight. Directly over the stone ring, the eldest folds its pale hands beneath its keel and turns back into a boss of basalt.

I clamp my own dorsal shields shut, pull my pale fingers under my chest, and let my riders press down tight along my spine. Ten paces away, the stranger’s breathing abruptly stops—a sudden holding-back of that astonishing cold plume, as if it has sensed the hush tightening around it and is trying, clumsily, to grow small and quiet. Yet I can still feel the fast, uneven hammer of its heart traveling straight down through its boots into the floor-stone, and its face still shines in the infrared dark with rapid evaporation.

Wait, I think—not in words, only in the steady stillness of basalt. *Do not step toward the western rib. Do not touch the mortar. Turn back toward the cold that made you, before the air takes too much of you and the stalkers reach your knees.*

Beneath my sealed dorsal edges, my throat-louvers stay flat and dark. For one strange moment, I feel the impulse to crack a single pleat open and pulse a broad, steady bloom of warmth—the slow, gentle shape we hold against the collarbone when a hatchling wakes or a molting kin shivers inside its stitched parchment. *The stone holds. You are seen. Go safely back through the throat.*

I do not open it. Its flat, wet face has no black mirrors beneath the brow; it would read nothing from a shape of heat, and even a sliver of my own warmth given to the dark is a habit I cannot afford to break. Some worlds cannot speak across their seams. They can only respect the boundary and wait for one another to pass.

So I stay stone beside the pillar, reading the frantic cooling flare of the visitor ten paces away, counting its uneven heartbeats through my pads, patient as salt.

And when the kiln-heat finally drives it back toward the entrance—when those blind, heavy footsteps retreat through the flour and vanish into the freezing abyss beyond the crawlway—I will unfold my limbs in the dark. I will go down to the three basalt wind-slabs, brush my pale fingers over the dust where an outer shoulder grazed the rock, and brace my long legs to nudge the center stone a fraction back toward true, if it has shifted at all.

Then I will return to the glassy pillar, press my heel-spur into the smooth ancient stone, and send a single, long, rising tremor climbing up through the cathedral’s bones:

*The draft holds. The seam is closed. Wake the work.*
