I would not be found up on the lip, leaning into the sweep of the grass. If you were looking for me from the rim, you would miss me for an hour—just another grey wool back moving slowly along the shadowed edge of the middle-lower tiers, crouched where the lanes meet the retaining walls, never quite standing straight for long.

I am one of the ones who keep the grooves.

Not a quarry-cutter—my shoulders were never broad enough to swing a hardwood mallet all day against a cliff face—and not yet one of the old listeners who sit wrapped in double felt on low stools in the alleys, because my knees still take the sharp stairs without complaint. I live in the work between the two. Whenever a mason’s chisel breaks a new seam between the buff limestone and the tight grey marl beneath it, the rock begins to weep, and someone has to teach that water where to go before it softens a footing or creeps under a neighbor’s willow-slat bed.

You would find me first by my hands. The left is wrapped at the thumb and forefinger in oiled-leather stalls, cracked white along the seams from wet lime; the right is bare so I can feel the grit in the water. Tucked through my belt-cord is a small bronze gouge, a bundle of peeled willow rods no thicker than a reed, a waxed linen pouch of dry charcoal dust, and three bone scrapers—the oldest of them worn so thin at the blade that when I hold it up toward the noon sky, the pale light comes straight through it like horn.

That scraper does not belong to this basin. Neither do I, though I have been down in it since the first winter, when there were only twelve ledges cut and we slept ten to a canvas tilt while the frost locked the spoil-heaps solid overnight. Look closely at the grain of that bone, or at the patched elbows of my wool coat, and you can see a faint, stubborn smear of yellow-ochre clay that no amount of cold rain or chalk-dust will ever wash out. That was the color of the last hollow—three days’ slow walk north across the wind, where I learned to walk with my elbows tucked in before I learned to speak above a whisper.

I was twenty-six when the old bowl began to tire. I remember it mostly by sound. The drainage runnels down in the bottom quarter stopped ticking clean; the limestone there had grown slick with white crust, and the water began to make a soft, muffled *slur* underneath the paving stones where we could not reach it. A retaining wall behind the weaver’s stair bowed out half a hand’s breadth in a single wet spring. Nobody panicked. Nobody cried out. One morning the elders simply stood by the eastern switchback, sniffing the wind off the grass, and by the next thaw we were oiling the donkey panniers and wrapping our chisels in tallowed rags.

When we poured down into this new sink, the hardest part for me was not the cold, or the rawness of the ledges, or sleeping on bare clay before the roofs went up. It was that the rock here had a different voice.

The old hollow was dry-tempered, friable, pale yellow; when you tapped a wall there with the butt of a gouge, it answered with a crisp, chalky note. Here the marl is grey, dense, and hungry. It drinks groundwater like a wool blanket and holds it until you think a cut is dry—and then, three mornings later, a dark glossy band spreads overnight down a whole stair-wall, smelling faintly of iron and cold mud. For the first two seasons, my hands kept expecting the old stone. I cut my runoff channels too shallow, and the swelling marl pinched them shut in a week. I had to unlearn twenty years of wrists and thumbs and start listening to this hollow from the beginning.

That is where I spend my mornings: kneeling in the mist that lingers over the lower-middle terraces until the sun clears the eastern rim.

Before the bearers come down with their chalk-hampers and scuff wet clay off their soles, I walk the lanes that feed the south sumps, bending low, running the edge of my bone scraper cleanly along the carved water-grooves. The stone is still razor-sharp here; three seasons of water have not rounded a single corner of the cuts. Where a groove passes a fresh masonry terrace, I clear away the pale curd of curing mortar that flakes down overnight. Where a seam is running cloudy—milk-grey instead of black and clear—I stop, lay my bare knuckles flat in the trickle, and feel for the grit. Clear water means the stone is holding. Gritty water means the seepage is eating clay out from behind someone’s footing two tiers up, silently hollowing a pocket no eye can see.

When I find grit, I climb.

I know almost every lane in the lower half of the bowl, not by the names of the families who live there, though I know most of those too, but by the pitch of their water. The lane behind the potter’s drying-cribs has a quick, bright *tick-tick-tick* where seepage drops half an inch off a stepped shale lip. The double stair below the kiln-bakers runs silent and thick, barely a hiss along dark moss that is already two fingers wide because the warmth from the eastern flues drifts that far in winter. At the blind corner where three roofs step down into a narrow chute, the runnel drops into a hollowed catch-basin before plunging toward the central cisterns, and its note is low and steady, like a thumb tapped slowly against a full clay jar.

Moving through the town, I barely speak. A dry click of the tongue behind a woman carrying a lidded crock of barley broth from the communal ovens; a half-turn of the shoulders so we pass without brushing wool; two quick taps of my gouge-handle against a lintel to let a mason perched on lashed scaffolding above me know I am stepping under his plumb-line. People are used to seeing the crown of my felt cap passing beneath their window-ledges, or finding me crouched beside their threshold scraping silt from the edge of their steps. Now and then a door-shutter opens a crack and a hand sets down a mug of hot leek-water or a folded scrap of bittercress and salted fat—not payment, just the quiet arithmetic of a city where everyone’s wall rests on everyone else’s dry feet.

Sometimes, when a seepage line refuses to make sense—when the water turns milky and I cannot trace which ledge above has shifted—I go fetch old Maren, or one of the other seated listeners from the lane above the sumps. She has lived inside two other hollows before this one; her knees gave out on the march here, and two of her nephews carried her down the switchback in a wicker chair slung between ash poles. Now she sits wrapped to the ears in three layers of felt, and when I bring her word of a sour seam, I carry her stool for her while she walks slowly with my arm braced under her elbow, leaning her weight into me with the easy, practiced trust people here have for anything that bears load cleanly.

We do not talk while we listen. I press the dry end of my peeled willow rod against the wet limestone and set the other end lightly against my front teeth, closing my eyes so the pale bounced light disappears. Beside me, Maren lays her bare, scarred palm flat on the marl and tilts her grey head down toward the groove. Through my jawbone, the stone opens up. What looked like a quiet, solid wall turns out to be full of tiny, slow life: the faint, rhythmic creak of a green ash joist drying in the house behind us; the soft, distant *thump* of a wetted wedge splitting rock four terraces to the west; the thin, vibrating whisper of water moving behind the plaster. Once, sitting beside her in a blind alley, I felt a long, low sigh travel up through the willow wood into my skull—so quiet no bare ear could ever have caught it—and we both looked up at the exact same moment, raised two fingers to the masons above, and had the whole tier braced with spare poles an hour before a hidden shale shelf let go behind the retaining wall.

Maren never praises me when I hear it right. She just clicks her tongue once, softly, and taps the side of my knee with her knuckles before letting me help her back to her corner. That is enough. In a bowl this new, nobody owns their skill; you are only keeping the instrument in tune until your own knees stop climbing and someone else brings you a stool.

My own room is small, wedged on the middle-lower tier just where the ground drops sharply toward the central sumps. I chose it in the second autumn because three different runoff channels converge right below its sill, and I like being able to hear the basin’s water-count from my bed without opening the oiled-hide shutter. The roof above my head is the lane for the tier behind me; I know my upstairs neighbors by the rhythm of their wrapped feet—the heavy, even tread of the mortar-mixer returning at dusk, the quick light shuffle of his two children, who always pause at the bone-edged scraper-board at the top of the stairs to clean their soles before stepping onto my turf-and-clay roof. When their hearth-vent breathes warm smoke through the shared flue-wall on my south side, the plaster beside my reed mat stays faintly warm until midnight.

It is the north wall of my room that is still cold.

Outside, on that unfinished flank, four pale blocks of dressed limestone jut out into the wet air like teeth. Nobody has keyed into them yet. Not out of coldness—nobody here has any quarrel with me—but because the marl ledge just north of my doorway is still unsettled. A strong seepage seam broke open there last spring when the lower cistern was cut, and the ground beside my footings has been weeping and swelling ever since. Until it steadies, no family can safely lay their sill-stones against my flank.

So I tend that wet patch more carefully than any other in the bowl. Every evening before the light goes, I kneel outside my own north corner with my bronze gouge and deepen the bypass groove by the thickness of a fingernail, guiding the dark trickle cleanly away from the clay bed, watching how fast the rock dries along the upper course, pressing my willow rod to the tooth-stones to listen for the settling.

I am in no rush for it to finish, and yet I want it to. I want to hear the quiet scrape of a neighbor’s wooden trowel along the far side of my north wall. I want to smell fresh lime mortar curing against my cold stones, and feel the night chill draw back two inches from my reed mat once another hearth is burning on the other side of the teeth. That is what it means to be housed here: not to have four walls of your own, but to have your walls taken up by someone else’s weight.

In the late afternoon, when my hands are stiff and blue-chilled from wet chalk and the grooves are running clear down to the sumps, I sometimes walk all the way down to the lowest quarter and stand for twenty minutes beside the shaduf-tenders. I do not take a rope unless one of them is tired or grieving and wants a quiet hand to spell them. Mostly I just lean my back against a dry cistern-coping, tuck my hands into my opposite sleeves, and look straight up at the open disk of sky.

That is when the cliff martins drop over the lip.

They come spilling out of the windless blue edge in small, quick flocks, wheeling low over the misting sumps to snap at midges rising off the wet clay. Their faint chittering cuts right across the hush of the basin—the sharpest, loosest sound all day—and for a minute or two, every shaduf-tender down in the hollow tilts their face upward, letting the dipping poles rest slow against their counterweights. I watch the birds bank tight around the cantilevered timber ledges, never touching the stone, never leaning on anything, free to rise back over the torn overhang of turf and vanish into the wind whenever they choose.

I have no wish to follow them up there. Once, last autumn, I climbed the western switchback with a team to help ease a heavy ash beam onto the rim-hoists. The moment my head rose past the dangling grass roots, the wind hit my wet wool coat like a shove in the chest. It tore the smell of chalk and tallow straight out of my nose and replaced it with a thin, empty roaring that made my ears ache. Looking back across the flat, bending grass, there was nothing to brace against—nothing to catch a voice or hold warmth or tell you where your footing ended. When I stepped back down over the lip an hour later and felt the air go heavy and still again, my knees nearly went loose with relief.

Still, I like watching the martins come down. They remind me that the sky is open over us, even when the alleys feel no wider than two shoulders.

Then the sun drops behind the western rim, shutting off the bowl like a clay lid set on a pot.

Within five minutes, the limestone goes flat grey-blue, the chill sharpens, and the mallets stop one terrace at a time. I walk back up my three flights of crisp new stairs, my breath hanging pale in front of my wool collar. Outside my door, I pause and lay my bare palm once against the jutting teeth of my north wall. Still damp along the lowest course, still cool—but tighter than it was a month ago. By next spring, perhaps, the marl will have finished drinking, and a young mason or a weaver will come quietly with a hod of wet mortar and a timber ladder and begin locking their beams into mine.

Inside, I light the tiny wick floating in my clay saucer of tallow. The amber light catches the worn translucent edge of my old yellow-stained bone scraper where I lay it beside the hearth-brazier. I rub a bead of tallow into the white lime-cracks along my thumbs, warm a wooden bowl of thick cabbage and bittercress broth over the smokeless coals, and unwrap my damp linen foot-cloths to dry.

Through the south wall, I hear the soft scrape of my neighbor setting a pot down on his reed mat, and the low, close-mouthed hum of his wife quieting a child two feet away on the other side of the mortar. Above my head, a green ash joist eases a fraction of an inch into its socket with a small, dry *creak* as the night mist thickens over the sumps. And underneath it all—clean, steady, running clear of grit all the way down to the cisterns—I sit in the warm dimness against my neighbor’s wall and listen to the water ticking in the dark.
