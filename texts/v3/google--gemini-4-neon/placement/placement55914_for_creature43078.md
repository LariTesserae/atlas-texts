My first instinct is to put myself on the berm.

It seems like the sensible place to start: perched on that low, rounded lip of moss, dry to the shins, watching the whole four-hundred-pace saucer at once under the ordinary afternoon clouds. From up there, the basin looks like a puzzle you might solve if you watched long enough—timing the thirty-second lag between a northwest pier and its answering southeast amphitheater, counting how many inches the eastern flat drops when two distant archways stitch their thresholds together, trying to reconstruct the vanished civic equation out of its leftover arithmetic.

The trouble is, I can feel almost immediately how wrong that posture is.

Standing on the berm, you do not even see the people who live here on your first circuit, or your second. You mistake them for damp bollards and fluted shadows. Worse, just behind your heels lies the other side of the rim—that flat, dry, stubborn world of dirt and slow timber where nothing rises when you tap it and rain never once pauses to hang bright at eye level. Even imagining my back turned to that silence makes the skin between my shoulder blades pull tight. I would not stay on the rim. I would not watch the city from the dry lip like a surveyor measuring a pond.

I would slide down into the warm amber shallows.

And the moment the ankle-deep water closes over my feet, the surveyor falls away too—too tall, too stiff-ankled, too deaf in the soles. Whatever I am in this place has to be able to hear through its heels. So I find myself slight and crouched, matte olive and bruised umber, my long forearms hanging easy near the mirror-film, the broad, ribbed, four-toed soles of my feet reading the slow hydraulic pulse of the floor-plate the way another creature reads a breeze. Along my crown and spine, sparse pale filaments lift slightly as a patch of warm humidity gathers thirty paces east, preparing to condense into localized rain. My eyes are wide-set and pale amber beneath their little translucent drip-eaves, with that faint secondary outer ring already catching the clean, sharp seam stitched through the daylight under a newly risen arch.

Not a visitor, then. One of the thirty or forty who detach themselves from the columns when the hourly chord strikes.

Still, it takes a while inside the water to find where I actually belong among them.

I try to imagine myself leading one of the four or five loose work-crews—striding out onto the open flat with a cured slurry-wood stave, tapping the calloused xylem-brick in a crisp, practiced cadence to summon a drying-gallery, then throwing sharp, indignant geometry across the bright water with my forearms when a rival crew raises a massive pier and melts my roof into foam. It doesn't quite fit. I admire the fierce, practical unsentimentality of it, the quick cooperative signals that send neighbors sprinting with tendon-nets toward a draining depression, the brisk barter of runner-meat steamed in cedar-scented column vents. I would trade work with them, lend a shoulder to shift the glossy dark counterweights, help wedge wet pouches of fibrous slurry into a hissing stoma to cure a fresh block of bog-oak-hard wood. Yet I do not feel the possessive snap of a crew-leader defending a dry afternoon.

Nor am I ready for the permanent anchor-plinths. I would visit the elders there, certainly—kneeling beside one of the pillowy petrified cambium domes while a broad-backed mossy grazer chews spore-buds nearby, watching an elder's horn-pale palms rest flat on the slick stone-like wood, reading a hydraulic imbalance half a minute before the water shivers. There is an immense, quiet dignity in retiring from the seams and becoming a weather-glass for the saucer. Maybe I would end up there eventually, once decades of warm water stiffened my ankles. But not now. Not while the doorways still fold daylight cleanly in half.

What keeps pulling at my attention—what I realize, slowly, I would actually spend my days doing—is the piece of work most dwellers treat as the dullest chore on a held pavilion.

I would be a pendulum.

Not bound exclusively to one crew, either, though every crew knows my silhouette and the particular slant of my wrist when I signal across the flats that my legs are free. Usually the pendulum shift falls to the youngest—someone who has to keep trotting through the southern arch, popping out forty paces west from a temporary stair-head, splashing back across the bright shallows, and crossing the threshold again every two minutes so the buried xylem-grid keeps registering the pavilion as active traffic. Young runners hate it after an hour. Their minds wander; they stop to snatch at a stranded lung-eel or chatter in palatal clicks with a friend; the rhythm breaks, the columns sigh, and suddenly everyone who was sleeping or curing tools under the canopy is scrambling ankle-deep through melting starch while porcelain runners race in clicking castanet swarms to eat the walls.

I would not get bored of the loop.

Think of what happens when you hold a single fold open for three hours—or through a cool, mist-hung night while a crew sleeps dryly in the shade of the vaulted ribs, trading off only when your calves finally burn. You stop experiencing the pavilion as an object and start experiencing it as an ongoing agreement. Every two minutes, your bare ribbed soles strike the same stretch of sunken xylem-brick, then lift onto the weathered steps, then pass through the bright refractive seam where daylight bends without blurring.

After twenty laps, you begin to notice that no two seams feel identical.

One archway pulls slightly to the left as you cross it, like stepping through a current in warm shallows; another drops you onto its western stair-head with a brief, clean tang of struck wet flint at the back of your tongue. Some folds feel taut and eager, snapping you forty paces across the saucer with barely a ripple on the water-film; others feel tired from the moment they open, requiring a slightly heavier heel-strike on the threshold and a quick palm brushed along the fluted cellulose to convince the hidden veins that crowds are still hurrying through. You learn the exact stutter in a pouring gutter half a second before velvet algae chokes it—time enough to whistle a sharp two-note warning into the acoustic gap between the sub-floor thump and the waders' cries, so the climber on the eaves can clear it with a shell-blade before the ceiling softens.

And because I would trade shifts across all the crews—holding open a northern curing-gallery at midday, a southern sleeping-canopy at dusk, a western balustrade where someone is weaving vascular nets in the weightless hush beneath a cantilever—I would touch more of the city's newly extruded skin than almost anyone else in the basin.

That is where something quieter begins to emerge out of the work.

Every time you turn on a slick stair-head to begin the next lap, your hand naturally finds the handrail to steady your momentum. You run your pale, spatulate fingers along wet cellulose that rose out of the floor ten minutes ago already beveled, already pitted with ancient drip-marks. Most dwellers barely glance at it; a rail is a rail until it dissolves.

Wait long enough, though, and your fingertips start catching on the anomalies—not the grand architectural physics, not the standing glass walls of water or the uphill ribbons racing under vine-bridges, but the tiny, stubborn revisions.

A slight inward dip along a fluted banister where an old elder who dissolved three weeks ago used to hook a stiff elbow. A low step on an eastern stair-head curved smoothly to the exact width of a narrow, crouched back. A vaulted alcove beneath an overhang where the localized rain hangs motionless at eye level for *five* heartbeats instead of three, as though the buried blueprint paused a fraction longer to remember how a dry corner ought to feel.

The city has forgotten what it was for, but it has not stopped taking notes.

And once you notice that, you cannot help yourself. Between hired shifts—in those loose, sunlit hours when no crew needs a roof held open and the saucer is loud with its own unprompted business—I find myself drifting out toward the sparse open flats where structures rise with nobody asking for them.

A bench unfurls out of a puddle where nobody sits. A slender ribbed pier shoots thirty feet up near the northern rim, and thirty seconds later an uninvited little pavilion buds out of the moss in the southeast to balance the civic volume. Ordinarily, nobody bothers with these orphan buildings. They stand for ten minutes, pouring warm water out of freshly slit eaves, letting a few bladder-winged waders claim their cornices, and then sigh down into foaming slurry before anyone ever sets foot on their steps.

Now and then, if no crew's drying-roof is at risk of being tipped out of balance, I would walk over to one of those unasked-for pavilions and step through its arch.

Not to hold it open for hours. Not to squat in it or harvest its steam. Just to run the loop five or six times—ten minutes, maybe fifteen—long enough to let its upper gallery finish extending its wafer-thin kelp-like cantilever, long enough to feel where its stitched seam wants to deposit a walker, long enough to lay my flat olive palm along its already-weathered cornice and feel what shape it was trying to remember before the floor called it back. Sometimes I climb up into the weightless pocket beneath its eaves and just hang there for a minute among the drifting speckled eggs, dodging the furious chalk-white bill of an outraged wader, letting my feet dangle clear of the shifting world below.

Then I step off the threshold, wade twenty paces back into the shallows, and let the columns soften like boiled reeds. There is no grief in watching it melt. Nothing here is meant to stay. What matters is only that, for a quarter of an hour, the ancient floor made a room out of old cellular memory and felt footsteps answer it.

When I am not running a loop—when my shins are tired and the afternoon sun is bright on the mirror-flats—you would find me crouched in one of the dry alcoves beneath a shifting tower, the kind where rain stops mid-fall.

Others come to these alcoves to work: tying microscopic sinew knots or trimming runner-shell splinters behind a motionless hanging bead of amber water, using the droplet as a magnifier for three heartbeats before momentum snaps back and it shatters on the moss. I carry a small bundle of braided lung-eel sinew and a shard of porcelain shell in my hip-harness, so I look busy enough if anyone glances over. Mostly, though, I am not tying anything.

I am just leaning close behind the suspended drop, holding my breath for three or four heartbeats while the entire saucer bends inside it.

Through a single clear sphere of warm water hanging an inch from my brow-ridge, the whole noisy, hyperactive wetland turns upside down and goes impossibly crisp. Four hundred paces of living floor, drifting cumulus clouds, a slow broad-backed grazer stepping politely ten paces left of an erupting stair-tower, two distant crews throwing crisp semaphore angles across the bright flats—all of it held intact inside a lens that does not exist until it pauses and is gone the instant it remembers how to fall.

*Crack.*

The sheet of droplets hits the velvet moss all at once. My dorsal filaments twitch, reset, and another curtain of rain halts at eye level. In the new bead, the northwest pier is already gone, replaced by a widening ring of white foam and a dark scatter of clicking runners, while an arcade rises fresh in the east. Every three seconds, a different city, perfectly clear, refusing to hold still.

Then the deep, woody chord begins to swell up through the soles of my feet from all four quarters at once.

Wherever I am—usually caught halfway between a temporary stair-head and its southern archway, ankle-deep in water that instantly locks flat as blown glass—I stop moving. My filaments settle into stillness. Around the saucer, thirty or forty slight olive figures straighten up from beside the columns and plinths, suddenly legible now that the city has stopped rearranging itself behind them.

For the first four or five seconds of the hush, I only listen. The low, flute-like voices travel cleanly across the dead-calm mirror: a name called near the western berm, a brisk warning about a sluggish drainage vein under the central lawn, an exasperated two-note rebuke tossed from a southern crew whose drying-gallery just melted into lunch for the bugs.

On the sixth or seventh second, if there is a clean space left in the silence, I lift my chin and send out one brief, quiet hum across the water. Nothing urgent. No claim on a roof, no dispute over a harvest. Just a small margin-note gathered from a hundred loops of wet stairs:

*The northern seam is pulling three paces short today.*
*A low curved bench came back under the eastern arcade.*
*The third rail on the southwest plinth still bends where his wrist lay.*

On the ninth second, my throat closes cleanly with all the rest.

A heartbeat later, the northern gutter coughs loudly. A twelve-foot curtain of water loses its containment and drops with a thunderous *whump*, hurling spray across the sunlit flats. The bladder-winged waders burst upward in a rattling white cloud against the sky, and I fold smoothly back into my crouch, splashing across the waking ripples toward the southern arch before my two minutes run out—before the floor decides the doorway has gone empty, and takes the roof back down into the dark.

Years from now, when the warm water finally stiffens my ankles and my broad soles horn over too thick for the seams, I suppose I will climb onto one of the pillowy cambium plinths, drape a living cape of velvet moss over my shoulders, and let my palms read the deep veins for the younger runners. And when the hush finally comes that I do not step back out of, they will lay me on the steps of a softening pavilion, wave the eager porcelain runners back with clicking sticks, and watch the fluted columns carry me smoothly back into the springy olive floor.

I do not want a monument—nothing in this basin keeps one anyway. Only, perhaps, a few weeks later, some young pendulum hurrying through a freshly risen eastern archway might feel her bare heel land on a stair-head turned just half an inch wider for an easier pivot, or duck beneath a shifting eave where the rain hangs bright and motionless for one extra heartbeat before it breaks.

Just enough for someone to brush their wet fingers along the damp cellulose, and know the old city is still listening.
