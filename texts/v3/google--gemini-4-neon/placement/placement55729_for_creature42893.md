At first, dropping into the olive-and-pewter dimness, I would not know how heavy I am supposed to be.

It is tempting to imagine myself down on the cracked central dome, among the seam-readers—half-blind, motionless for half a day, pressing both palms and the flat of a cheek against the scalding basalt to hear the mountain growing a fingernail’s width at a time. There is a clean, stripped-down nobility to that kind of listening. You deal only with the earth and its slow, honest fractures. No feuds, no damp councils, no frayed reed-runners. Just the glassy *tink* of deep rock shifting beneath the crust.

It is tempting, too, to picture myself further out—not even one of the heavy-boned builders at all, perhaps just one of the broad, scalloped grazers rippling across a travertine wall, leaving a neat dark ribbon of clean stone behind me, wanting nothing from the caldera except algae and warmth. Or one of the hollow cane-stalks themselves, rooted in bath-warm volcanic glass and wearing a crown of white mountain rime sixty feet overhead, holding the whole contradiction of the valley in a single pale stem.

Yet when I actually wait in the murk and let my weight decide where it wants to settle, I do not sink all the way to the hot dome, and I do not rise to the slush-lid where the young air-haulers swallow freezing sky like fine glass.

I stop in the middle water.

Thirty feet above the stepped basalt wards, forty feet beneath the trembling gray ceiling, the city lets go of the mountain and hangs. Here, the dim light has neither floor nor sky—only overlapping tiers of lashed cane-decks, fat silica-plastered baskets swaying almost imperceptibly in the slow turn of the basin, and the long, thick mooring-cables dropping down through the olive gloom to the rock below.

That is where I find my hands.

They are slate-dark, matte with natural wax, the palms thickened with horn-dark pads—though not the broad, white-armored slabs of a quarryman’s hands. I wear only two small holed basalt pebbles knotted to my hip-sling, light enough to slip off onto a cane-peg in a single motion when I want a rising thermal draft to lift me, heavy enough to sink me back toward the anchor-ledges when a breath runs long. My brow-pits and jaw-pits are wide and sensitive, reading the warm oil of rising vents against the cooler shadows spilling out of the cane-groves. Every few minutes, a smoky inner lid draws lazily across my dark eyes to clear the drifting mineral grit.

And along the outer edge of my left forearm—the arm I hook through the cane-lattices while I work—I have left a single pale, knobby ridge of travertine unscraped for twenty seasons. Everywhere else, especially across my palms and the pale undersides of my wrists, I keep the hide scraped clean and supple.

I am a keeper of the lines and the bells.

Not a high title—this city does not care much for titles, only for what holds and what tilts. Every hanging district depends on a quiet paradox, and I spend my days living inside it. When a ward lashes a new mooring-rope between a floating cane-deck and a basalt crag below, the line is braided from pale plant-sinew and swimmer-tendon. It is strong, supple, and forgiving; when the caldera floor swells upward beneath the anchor-ledge, a fresh rope simply gives a little slack and shrugs it off.

But fresh sinew is deaf. If a mason on the lower terraces taps a bone pin against a young rope, the soft fibers drink the sound up within ten paces.

Give that same rope ten years in the warm, silica-heavy water, though, and the basin thickens it. Lime and mineral crust bind the braided strands until the cable is as thick as a forearm—half-tendon, half-stone. Now, when someone three terraces down strikes the anchor-ledge with a stone knuckle, the stiffened rope rings clear as bone. A clean *tock—tock-tock—tock* climbs straight up through the padded hush of the alleys and lands dry and crisp against my bare palm thirty yards overhead: *Seep cooling. Need wedges. Bring two hands.*

The trouble is that the water never knows when to stop thickening what it touches. Give a cable thirty years, or forty, and the stone wins. The sinew inside grows brittle and locked. Then the mountain swells another fingernail’s breadth, or shifts sideways by half the width of a thumb, and a line that can no longer bend begins to pull a whole hanging district out of true.

I move up and down those lines all shift, slipping a ballast-stone off my hip to drift upward along a calcified trunk, hooking a heel around a cane-beam to stay steady while I lay my bare palm, and sometimes the side of my jaw, flat against the crusty rope.

I am listening to two things at once.

First, to the traffic of the wards—the patient, rhythmic percussion of neighbors calling across the gloom. I hear the slow double-tap that means a grazer has wandered into a moss-nursery and needs nudging back toward the sluices; the three clipped strokes from the outer cane-groves that mean a mountain carcass has fallen through the ceiling slush and is drifting down through the stalks; the long, deliberate cadence of an elder summoning kin to the scraping-stones.

Beneath all that, I tap the cable once myself with a shaved bone pin and listen to the rope’s own voice.

A young line answers with a soft, muffled *thump*, still mostly plant and tendon. A cable in its prime gives back a dry, woody *tock* that hums gently up into my elbow—stone enough to carry speech, fiber enough to forgive the rising floor. But every few seasons, my bone pin strikes a pale, knobby trunk and what returns through my knuckles is thin, bright, and glassy—the exact *tink* of fresh basalt just before a split runs up a courtyard wall.

You never cut a mooring-line. To put an obsidian blade to a hanging ward’s cable is the one horror every person in the caldera agrees on without needing a council under a bell. Instead, when a line goes glassy, I haul down coils of fresh, oiled sinew, brace my four-toed feet against the sharp anchor-crag below, and lash a twin cable right beside the stiffening elder. Over the next two years, as the dome lifts and the mineral rime begins to frost the new braid, the strain shifts slowly from stone back to sinew. Sometimes we leave the old calcified cable in place anyway, disconnected from the tension, standing like a pale crooked bone beneath the walkways—a quiet monument to how long the district has floated there.

When a line tightens before I catch it, it is the inverted bells that tell on it first.

I watch them from the dim alleys below the hanging decks—wide, upturned domes of tight reed and stitched swimmer-hide, sealed with dark algal resin and rimmed with holed basalt stones. From underneath, they look like vast dark mushrooms rooted to the bottoms of the walkways. When a hanging ward tilts by even two degrees because an anchor-ledge has lifted or an old cable has stiffened too far, the bell tilts with it.

Then I see it: a single fat, flat-bottomed coin of silver gas slipping quietly out from beneath the high side of the rim. It wobbles upward through the hanging olive-weed, splits slowly into two smaller moons, and vanishes toward the unseen gray ceiling.

Air is too costly here to let it leak away into the dark. Every pocket inside those bells was either coaxed patiently off a sweet floor-seep or hauled down sixty feet from the freezing slush-lid by young climbers with frosted brows and stinging lungs.

So I slip one basalt weight onto my hip-cord, rise to the weighted rim of the tilting bell, and tuck a small bladder of warmed algal pitch beneath my arm. I duck under the hide skirt and rise slowly until my brow, eyes, and broad shoulders break cleanly through the shimmering horizontal mirror of the water-skin.

The change never stops feeling strange, even after decades.

Below my chin, the world is heavy, warm, and muffled—a realm of sign, heat-shadow, and bone-tapped percussion. Above my collarbone, the air is dim, thin, and dense with the sour, yeasty scent of fermenting cane-pith mash and drying grazer-mantle. Water sheets quietly off my slate-colored cheekbones and drips back into the black mirror beneath my elbows with sharp, distinct *plinks* that no one ever hears in the alleys outside.

If the bell is empty, I work alone in that low-pressure quiet, hooking my unscraped left forearm over the lashed cane-rail while I trace the inside of the hide dome with my bare fingertips, feeling for the cool pinhole or the slack seam. I press dark resin into the stitch-line, then dip back below the water-skin to shift a holed basalt stone two knots clockwise along the rim until the mirror-skin lies dead level again, holding its silver breath without spilling a drop.

More often, though, the bell is not empty. Three or four residents are already standing chest-deep on the dry-stacked bench or leaning over the rail, their dark eyes gleaming faintly in the sour vapor as they murmur over a splitting lintel or hum a slow, droning genealogy of the northern vents.

When I surface among them, they do not stop talking. A bell-mender is almost like the hide itself—part of what keeps the room from vanishing. I nod once, water running off my blunt jaw, and tuck myself near the low curved edge where the ceiling slopes down. While I smear resin along a weeping seam or re-tie a curing-net of salted olive-weed, I listen to their voices curl around the vaulted dark: low hums, soft throat-clicks, long breathy vowels that bend gently whenever a slow current rocks the deck.

Because I work the cables of four different wards and mend the bells of six, I hear more of the city’s slow quarrels than I ever meant to.

Last season, a lower basalt terrace and the hanging basket-ward just above it fell into a cold grudge. A warm seep had migrated ten paces east, leaving the lower house’s moss-troughs half a degree cooler while the suspended baskets above grew lush with crimson heat-moss. Neither family shouted—no one shouts in air this precious. Instead, the lower house hung two thick, overlapping curtains of cultivated olive-weed from its eaves, angling them carefully to deflect the rising thermal plume out toward the empty alleys, starving the baskets overhead. In answer, the hanging ward stopped relaying the lower terrace’s taps up the shared calcified mooring-line. For four months, whenever the lower house tapped for ash-paste or word of the outer grazers, the message hit the mid-water junction and died in stubborn silence.

I felt it in my palm one afternoon while checking the tension of that shared cable. The crisp *tock—tock-tock* climbed up from the basalt ledge, bright and urgent—*Lintels shifting. Need shims*—and stopped dead beside the hanging ward’s cane-beam. Two paces away, a resident sat motionless behind a curtain of weed, her thermal shadow clear and warm against my jaw-pits, her dark hands folded neatly in her lap, pretending the rope had not spoken.

I did not sign at her. I did not surface beneath her bell to lecture her about the rising mountain.

I simply kept my bare hand on the stone-crusted rope until the cadence finished, unhooked my bone pin from my belt, drifted ten feet higher to the next deck’s anchor-beam, and tapped the exact same rhythm cleanly upward toward the mason’s terrace. Then I dropped back down to the lower ward’s leaking curing-bell, slipped inside with my pouch of resin, and worked so slowly over a pinhole that both elders eventually had to come in under the same hide dome to wait for the mash-trough to finish warming.

Neither of them spoke for a quarter of an hour. They just stood chest-deep on opposite sides of the mirror-skin, staring down at the black water dripping off their chins while the thin, trapped air grew warmer and heavier with our shared breath. It is hard to hold onto a cold grudge when you are breathing the same damp lungful of sky. By the time I pinched the last seam of resin smooth and slipped back beneath the surface, a low, reluctant hum had started up behind me—the sound of two stubborn houses finally bargaining over the angle of an olive-weed curtain.

That is the closest I come to power in this basin, and it is more than enough. I do not want to decide where the wards build or who claims a windfall bird from the peaks. I only want the lines to carry what is tapped into them, and the bells to hold their air level, and the small, quiet bridges between silence and voice to stay intact for another decade.

When my long breath finally begins to feel thin in my ribs—not panicked, never panicked, just the quiet, familiar tug at the base of the lungs after an hour of working the warm middle water—I do not climb to the freezing lid. I leave the slush-crowns to the young, whose blood runs hotter and whose curiosity about that bright, hungry void above the steam hasn't worn thin yet.

Instead, I loop my second basalt stone back onto my hip-sling and let myself drift outward, following the vast, unhurried loop of the caldera away from the central dome. The dim olive haze cools by a degree, then two, then four against the tender pits along my jaw. The hanging districts thin out into sparse cane-pillars, and beyond them rise the raw, unweathered outer cliffs, slick with dark basalt and untouched by heat-moss.

Here, the water that brushed against the frosted cane-crowns far overhead sinks slowly back down the basin wall, heavy and rich with stolen mountain breath.

I find my ledge—a narrow shelf wedged between two dry-stacked windbreaks, smoothed with a braided runner of kelp-skin—and wedge my four-toed heels against a stone chock. For a long moment I simply sit, letting the cold descending draft wash the warmth of the hanging wards off my shoulders. Then, slowly, I unclasp the thick, overlapping pleats of dark hide along the sides of my neck and beneath my collarbones.

The relief is always quieter than a sigh. Out from beneath the heavy folds drift my crimson fringes, soft and feathery as deep-water moss, spreading limp into the cool downward stream. My chest stops asking for air. My heart settles from twelve deep beats a minute down to eight, then six, spacing itself out until I can scarcely tell the thud in my ribs from the distant, muffled *thump* of a giant silver bubble detaching from the central dome half a mile away.

Sometimes, before I pull the smoky lids all the way down over my eyes, a neighbor from the cane-wards drifts down to share the cold bench—often an older mason whose hands have grown too stiff with lime to flake obsidian, or one of the young air-haulers still shivering from a climb to the ceiling, seeking the stillness of the outer wall.

We do not sign much here. She settles knee-to-knee with me on the kelp-runner, draws a blunt grazer’s horn-lip from her belt, and leans forward. I bow my head, keeping my crimson fringes clear, and let her scrape the week’s fine chalky rime from the backs of my shoulders and along the curve of my spine.

Each stroke is slow, heavy, and unhurried, peeling away pale curls of mineral frost that hang for a second in the pewter gloom before drifting off into the current. She knows without being told to leave the pale, knobby ridge along my left forearm untouched, and she knows to draw the oiled pad of her thumb gently around the edges of my jaw-pits so no silica grit blinds me to the warm drafts tomorrow. When she finishes, we trade places, and I scrape the thicker travertine off her knuckles and elbows until her dark hide shines faintly waxy again.

Sitting there in the cool dark, feeling the basin breathe quietly through the open fringes at my throat, I can already imagine the season—far off, unhurried, arriving at the exact pace of a fingernail growing—when I will stop handing over the horn-blade.

I will let the chalk stay along my shoulder blades first. Then my elbows. Then the slow hinge of my wrists, until my palms can no longer tell a green sinew-rope from a glassy one. My shifts in the hanging districts will shorten; my afternoons on the outer downwelling will stretch until they swallow whole days. And when the last spaced-out beat finally leaves my chest, the people of the mid-water wards will not need to wonder where to put me.

They will bring me back inward to the warm water, bind my long knees to my chest in oiled kelp-skin, and smooth a patient coat of white mineral-ash paste over my hide. They will tuck me into a newly emptied niche beside the basalt anchor-ledge of the hanging district whose lines I tended, and dry-stack dark lava slabs across the mouth of the alcove.

Within a year, the paste will set hard as bone. Within a decade, the warm silica water will coat the stone doorway in knobby pale travertine and crowns of fine needles. And when the mountain swells upward another inch beneath the town, some younger keeper of the lines—two light ballast-stones at her hip, bare palms scraped clean for listening—will sink down through the olive twilight, lash a fresh pale sinew-cable around the thickening rock above my ribs, and tap a bone pin once against it to hear whether the line still holds.
