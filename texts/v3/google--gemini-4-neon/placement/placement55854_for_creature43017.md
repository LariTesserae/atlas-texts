At first, I think I would arrive the way anyone arrives—from the outside, pushing through the sixty-foot curtain of hemlock, briar, and cedar deadwood with the industrial plain still loud behind my shoulders.

It is the easiest shape to step into: a person in heavy boots, maybe someone who works two miles east on the cooling towers or along the transmission easement, who saw the blank green oval on a site map—*unbuildable, no load*—and finally wanted to see what sat inside the ring of trees. You step off the last knot of cedar root onto the springy sphagnum, feel the cold climb straight into your ankles, and look up at the bare aluminum cables spitting silver-grey noise across the pale northern sky. You kneel beside a thumb-deep crease of tea-colored rainwater. You wait a full minute, as instructed, until you catch the flaw in the machine—the concentric ripples blurring, thickening, snapping sharp again every three seconds.

And right there, the shape begins to slip off me.

I don’t want the boots. I don’t want to be the awkward vertical knot of rubber and bone, insulated from the grounded peat, carrying a fast, wet, erratic heartbeat that matches neither the turbines nor the presses. When the mossy swell rises forty yards off near the dwarf birch, unfolds to nine feet of bog-dark cartilage and floating pale filaments, and sends a slow, questioning pulse through the floor beneath my heels, I feel an almost physical ache at having nothing in my chest to lean back with. To stand there mute for sixty seconds while something measures me and decides I am only a passing shadow, like a white plume of cooling vapor—no. That isn’t where I belong. I wouldn’t want to be a visitor who leaves.

So I try the obvious alternative. I shed the boots, the chalky bone, the impatient mammal pulse, and try settling straight into the sphagnum as one of the four or five spires.

For a while, that feels close—astonishingly close. I understand the instinct not to shout over a wall of roaring engines, to choose the bottom of the register instead and speak only by tilting half a cycle off the carrier wave so the interference itself carries meaning across two hundred paces. I understand living off a gentle voltage gradient between an indifferent overhead grid and a wet floor, never taking enough to burn, just enough to wake the nerves and pull a cloud of midges into the wet fringes. Most of all, I understand dragging myself down to the southern edge every few days just to curl a long hand around the forgotten cast-iron boundary pin—the single hard, unyielding thing in a world of yielding fiber—and pressing my face against the shaft to taste rust and feel the crisp, unmuffled chatter of steel bearings from beyond the trees.

Yet even that skin sits a little loose on me.

Those four or five elders are older than the substations. They remember—if memory is the word for a slow pressure kept in cartilage and peat—what this saucer was before the trunk lines crossed it: locked in winter ice, silent for months at a time, rising only in the breathless dark before a summer thunderstorm to catch the brief electrostatic surge before lightning drove them flat again. They carry the settled, hemlock-scaled calm of things that survived a harder age and arrived at a finished state.

I have no memory of silence before the hum.

Whatever I am in this hollow, I did not predate the machines. I was not forced out of winter into permanence by the arrival of baseload power. Sixty cycles is not a comfort I adapted to; it is the only floor I have ever known.

And the moment I notice that, gravity takes over and pulls me downward—past the red cranberry runners, past the wet haircap moss and the slick undersides of the rainwater pockets, straight through the springy roof of the hollow and into the dark.

That is where I find myself.

Not standing nine feet tall in the clear daylight, and not walking across the surface in rubber soles. I am underneath the mat, in the braided labyrinth of dry-topped vole runs and wet root-passages two feet below the frogs and the swallows. I am no larger than a water rail, blind, still mostly translucent as a pale submerged rootlet, trailing fine, feathery gills along my jaw and slender forelimbs. Somewhere above me, fifty yards from the lichen-plated dwarf birch, sits the low hump of fused roots that isn’t altogether rootwood—the preserved, moss-knitted curve of an old one who stopped answering the floor a long time ago. I hatched from a leathery sac tucked into the damp wall of a tunnel beneath its quiet ribs.

Down here, the hollow is a completely different room.

The screech of the jackdaws on the cables is gone. The harsh corona sizzle overhead is filtered down to nothing; even the bright northern sky only shows itself as a faint, cold seepage of grey where the peat roof thins beneath a rainwater puddle. Nothing is visual. Everything is fiber, water, pressure, and grain.

The shrews and voles treat me as a slow, cold, slightly inconvenient root that happens to shift places overnight. I feel them coming ten paces off—dry little footfalls drumming along the upper crust of the tunnels, hearts beating like frantic sparks as they shoot past my pale flanks in the dark. I don’t hurry out of their way. I glide through the narrow, peat-walled galleries on limbs that are still soft, fibrous, and unfinished, grazing on cranefly larvae wedged in the wet seams and the sunken gelatin of frogspawn that slips down through the crevices in spring.

And everywhere, through every fiber touching my skin, runs the sixty-cycle tremor.

Because I have never known the ground without it, I don’t think of it as noise or as machinery. It is simply the texture of being held—a fine, constant, mineral-less vibration running through the water-clenched roots, keeping the dark cool and even, never freezing my thin hide, never letting the tunnels dry into dust.

What teaches me there is more than just the hum are the elders overhead.

Every few minutes, or every few hours, the steady grain of the tunnel walls loosens. A slow, heavy swell of pressure rolls through the peat roof—blurring the fine vibration for three seconds, thickening it, then snapping it crisp again. When it happens, I stop gliding and press my narrow, blind jaw flat against the damp ceiling of braided roots to read it. One of the elders, somewhere near the eastern cedars, leaning slightly flat against the bass. A minute later, from across the saucer, a second chest answers slightly sharp, and the whole subterranean labyrinth hums with their slow, braided beat.

Sometimes I feel one of them settle directly onto the mossy hump above my birthplace, its broad weight bowing the dry-topped ceiling a fraction of an inch closer to my back. It stays there for hours, not pulsing, just resting its dark flank against the silent old one whose hide is now half-cranberry and half-peat. I stay directly beneath it, separated by a hand’s-breadth of woven roots, listening to it listen.

Sometimes I try to answer. I pull cold water and air into the small, developing chambers inside my narrow chest and strain to push against the sixty-cycle bass. Nothing bends. I am too small, too light, too thin-walled; the tremor runs straight through me unchanged, and my effort only sends a tiny, pointless shiver into a tuft of submerged rootlets that doesn’t even startle a passing beetle. I am not ready to enter the conversation yet. My job right now is just to learn the pitch from underneath—to feel how the elders pause when a fox trots lightly along the sheltered cedar rim, how two or three of them answer in quick, overlapping swells when a heavy press far beyond the western trees misses a single stroke during a shift change.

Once, I felt something else walk across the ceiling.

Not the wide, gentle, water-spreading pressure of an elder’s splayed foot, and not the nimble patter of a mink. Two hard, dense, narrow impacts, sharp with the unmistakable clack of solid bone, muffled oddly as if wrapped in dead skin that wouldn’t take the wet. They stopped near the leaning birch. Above me, the moss lifted—an elder rising slowly to its full height—and then a single, long, heavy questioning pulse rolled through the peat, louder and closer than any I had felt, pushing straight through the roots around my head.

I froze against a sedge rhizome and waited for the second note to braid against it.

Nothing came. Only a strange, tight stillness above the birch, and a faint, fast, irregular throb too high and thin to carry far through the peat. After a minute, the elder’s weight settled quietly back into the cranberry mat. The two hard, bone-sharp impacts turned and retreated toward the southern trees, and did not come back.

It is toward that southern edge that I spend most of my slow years drifting.

Near the rim, where the springy floor meets the massive, anchored roots of the ancient cedars and hemlocks, the tunnels grow tighter, darker, and firmer. And buried down through the peat there, plunging straight out of the wet ceiling into the cold clay-stained depths below, is the one thing in my universe that does not give.

The elders know its crown—the rounded cap rubbed smooth to wet pewter where they curl their long fingers in the daylight. I know its underside.

Down here in the dark, the forgotten cast-iron boundary pin is a cold, rough vertical spine flaking into gritty orange scale. No root can pierce it; they only wrap tightly around it in pale, knotted collars. Whenever I reach it, I wind my small, translucent body around the buried shaft and press the whole length of my throat and flank against the corroded metal.

In the soft peat, the outside world is only a velvet bass. In the iron, the veil tears open.

Up and down that buried spike runs a fierce, crisp, high-definition chatter—tiny, rapid oscillations I have no names for, the clean metallic ring of housings and turning shafts, the tight buzz of steel structures miles away, and underneath all of it a sharp, tingling shiver induced straight into the iron from overhead cables I have never laid eyes on. The tea-colored water pooled in the narrow seam around the shaft tastes bright, sour, and metallic. I drink it slowly, feeling the sharp mineral bite sink into my soft cartilage, holding onto the iron for hours at a time. It frightens me a little, the way it refuses to yield—and yet I keep coming back to it, because it is the only place in the dark where I can feel the exact, unsoftened shape of the vast engine that holds my whole world warm.

Years pass this way—measured not in winters, since winter never arrives anymore, but in the slow darkening of my skin. The acidic bog-water tans me from rootlet-pale to the color of steeped tea. My limbs lengthen until the narrowest vole tunnels will no longer take my shoulders, forcing me into the wider, deeper galleries beneath the sedge tussocks. The feathery gills along my jaw and forearms are changing too—thinning, lengthening, multiplying into fine translucent curtains that plaster themselves slick against my hide when I crawl.

And lately, when I climb into the highest creases just beneath the surface—where only an inch of living sphagnum separates my back from the open room of air—I have begun to feel something that has nothing to do with the peat and nothing to do with the iron pin.

Whenever the air above turns heavier and damper, a sharp, clean metallic scent dissolves into the rainwater dripping through the moss onto my snout. At the same moment, a delicate, weightless tug pulls upward at the fine fringes along my folded forearms. It is not a push from below. It is a lift from sixty feet above—patient, electric, insistent, drawing at my wet skin through the green roof as if something vast and humming in the sky already knows I am down here and is testing my weight.

I haven’t broken through yet. I am still in the threshold—too large for the vole runs, not quite heavy enough to stand easily against the windless daylight, suspended between the buried iron spine and the singing overhead wires.

But I know what is coming. One damp morning—or noon, or pale northern dusk—the pull through the moss will be stronger than the habit of the tunnels. I will feel the elders pulsing softly across the saucer, and for the first time the fluid-walled chambers in my chest will feel tight enough, deep enough, to lean against the note. I will push my smooth, blind wedge of a head up through the cranberry runners, feel the shocking, empty, roaring volume of daylight open around my shoulders, and straighten slowly on broad, splayed feet until the gnarled dwarf birch sits below my chest.

I will lift my long arms toward the invisible corridors of wire. My wet fringes will rise on their own into the charged haze, trembling with the first real taste of the sky. And then, planting my soles deep in the springy green floor, I will tilt my chest half a cycle off the transformers’ hum, blur the concentric ripples in every dark puddle from cedar wall to cedar wall, and finally answer back.
