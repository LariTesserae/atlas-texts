At first, I try to put myself on the rim.

It is the obvious place to stand—feet braced on a ledge no wider than a tabletop, wind tearing at my sleeves, watching droplets hang like blown glass before turning upward into spray. For a few moments I test what it would feel like to be the human who returns: the one who replaces the rotted catwalk planks, lights the cold hearth-stones, scrapes the lichen off the dry-stone lintel.

No. The moment I imagine boots striking that schist, the whole chapel goes wrong. A fire in that shelter would choke the air with sour smoke; a voice on those ledges would turn the creature on the northern rib back into folded rock. That fifty-yard volume of sky is already full, precisely because it is empty. It has no room for a second pair of hands, and even if I tried to imagine myself weightless—another hollow-boned glider slipping in out of the western glare—I know I would only crowd an instrument built for one listener.

So I step back from the rim, and look down instead.

The only thing in that entire bright cathedral that refuses to rise is a smooth grey stone. When it slips from those long, chisel-nailed fingers, it does not stall. It does not spin into mist. It drops straight into the dark narrowing throat of the mountain, vanishing into the one part of this world that the sun never reaches.

That is where I find myself. Not at the open mouth of the bottle, but at the bottom of its lung.

Miles below the flat white floor of the clouds, the needle of grey-blue schist widens into sheer flanks, then broken scree, and finally into a narrow, steam-hung gorge where a cold lowland river cuts hard against the mountain’s root. Here the rock is no longer sleek and bleached by high wind; it is dark basalt and fractured shale, slick with mineral seep and draped in pale, dripping ferns that never feel a gale. Where the river bends deep under a sheer overhang, it runs directly across a geothermal seam. Scalding springs bleed up through cracks in the riverbed, mixing with the cold current until the whole pooled basin turns blood-warm and faintly milky with dissolved lime.

Above the widest pool, the cliff walls curve inward and open into a ragged black flue: the underside of the throat.

Everything light in this gorge is stolen by it. Mist lifts off the tepid water in slow, pale curtains, stretches into taut vertical ribbons, and vanishes upward into the dark ceiling. Midges hatch off the warm film of the eddies, lift an inch above the surface, and are whisked straight up the chimney in shimmering threads. Glassy moths, dry leaves, torn spider-silk—anything that lets go of the stone is claimed by the rising breath and carried away toward a sky I have never seen.

To stay down here, you cannot be hollow-boned. You have to love weight.

So I let the basin decide what shape can hold it.

I am not sleek, and I am not quick. I am long-bodied, broad-headed, and heavy in the ribs—dense enough to lie motionless on the riverbed while the current curls over my shoulders and the warm draft pulls steadily off my back whenever I haul halfway onto a wet ledge. My skin is thick, smooth, and dark as wet slate along the spine, paling to chalky ochre along the flanks where mineral water dries in faint rings. I have no sails, no oiled filaments, no silvery cheek-dishes tuned to wind-shear. My eyes are small and set beneath heavy, slow-blinking lids; down here, light only reaches the water for an hour at midday, slicing through a crooked cleft in the outer rock to turn the rising steam pale gold and the deep pool turquoise. The rest of the time is wet twilight and shifting shadow.

What I have instead is bone, water, and patience.

My toes are broad, splayed, and blunt-clawed, built to anchor into gravel and slick shale without effort. Along my flanks run faint, recessed seams that read every tremor of the current, every pulse of the hot vents beneath the silt. Most of all, I have a wide, flat lower jaw that I rest directly against submerged bedrock.

I do not hear the wind as air. I hear the mountain as a solid body.

Pressed against the stone beneath the water, I feel the whole peak breathing above me. When a storm tears across the unseen summit miles overhead, the bedrock beneath my chin shudders with a deep, continuous groan—a bass weight that settles into my ribs and makes the blind crayfish tuck deeper into the shale cracks. When the cloud-floor thickens outside the gorge and the barometric pressure leans heavy on the river, the vents beneath my belly pulse harder, pushing thicker clouds of steam up into the dark flue.

I live sparely in the slack-water behind a fallen basalt slab, where the cold river-edge meets the thermal seep. I do not hunt in snapping gyres; I wait. I take pale, slow-moving cavern snails off the sulfur-crusted rocks, blind crayfish out of the silt, heavy lowland beetles that tumble off the overhanging banks and spin dizzy on the surface before the updraft can hoist them away. When midday light strikes the pool, I watch the newly hatched midges rise off the water in silver spirals and disappear into the black ceiling—my warm dark feeding some bright, roaring height I cannot picture.

I have been here long enough to remember when the stone changed its voice.

When I was smaller, the vibration traveling down the rock was always raw—a ragged, shifting shudder that rose and fell with the weather and never held its shape for three heartbeats. Then, for a single long summer many seasons ago, a strange, sharp *tapping* rang down through the bedrock: small, rhythmic blows, steady as dripping water, echoing down through hundreds of feet of schist until the silt danced on the bottom of my pool. After that, the tapping stopped and never came back.

Instead, on clear, still days when the air in the shaft runs thin and clean, something deliberate began to tremble down the mountain’s spine.

I cannot hear the notes as song. The air rushes the wrong way—always upward, carrying whatever whistles in the high arches straight out into the bright sky. What reaches me through the bedrock is only the ghost of an alignment: a sudden, strange stillness inside the mountain’s roar, two pressures locking together so tightly that the tremor in my jawbone smooths out into a slow, steady throb, and the glassy water around my shoulders breaks into fine, motionless rings. It never lasts long. A squall shifts, the tremor loosens back into ordinary weather, and the rings dissolve.

Even so, I know I am not alone in the stone.

Because the mountain’s breath always rises, almost nothing ever falls down the throat. Gravity down here is ordinary; downwardness from *above* is a marvel. Yet over decades, during the brief lulls when the thermal column thins, small impossible fragments drop out of the dark ceiling and settle into my quiet backwater.

I keep them nudged onto a flat submerged shelf behind my slab, out of the main current where the river cannot roll them away.

There is a splinter of pale, waterlogged cedar, worn soft at the grain, that still carried the sharp, dead reek of old smoke when it first struck the water. Beside it lie two snapped hollow needles of white calcite—gradually thinning year by year as the warm mineral pool dissolves them back into lime—and a water-darkened tangle of green-ochre moss unlike any pale fern that grows in this gorge, still threaded with a single broad, slate-barred feather. I do not know what a wing is, or a hearth, or a ledge fifty yards across. I only nose these things when they arrive, tasting their saltless, wind-scoured strangeness against my palate before pushing them gently onto the shelf.

And then, once in a long season, the mountain holds its breath.

It happens without warning, usually when the outer weather is turning hard. The steady tug off my back slackens, then stops. Overhead, the rising ribbons of steam lose their stretch and collapse into a thick, motionless fog that hangs heavy over the pool. The midges stop rising; they drift aimlessly inches above the black water. Beneath my jaw, the deep bedrock thrum fades until only the dull rush of the cold river remains.

The whole drowned cavern falls into a strange, sulfur-sweet suspense.

I unhook my claws from the gravel, lift my heavy ribs just far enough to rest my broad chin on the wet basalt ledge, and wait with my nostrils barely clear of the warm water.

Minutes pass. Nothing moves in the fog.

Then, impossibly high inside the black vertical flue, something strikes stone: a faint, sharp *clink*, thin as cracked ice. A heartbeat later, a second strike—deeper, flatter, closer. Then a sudden hollow ring off the wet limestone overhang right above the pool, followed immediately by a clean, heavy *plunk* that breaks the still surface three body-lengths from my snout.

A ring of ripples rolls outward across the glassy water and laps softly against my throat.

I slide off the ledge without a splash. My heavy bones carry me straight down through the warm, cloudy dark to the silt-bedded floor, where a thousand river-rounded pebbles lie tumbled together in the gravel. I do not need sight to find the one that just fell. I swing my broad snout low over the stones, reading them by temperature and trace, until my jaw brushes it.

It is shockingly, cleanly cold.

Among stones warmed to blood-heat by the thermal vents, this one burns with the thin, crisp chill of high mountain air. I press my cheek against it, tasting what clings to its smooth grey skin before the warm water can wash it away: crisp slate-dust, a sharp bead-trace of bitter resin from some shrub I have never seen, and the faint, dry, oiled scent of something lean and light that belongs to the wind.

It was born in this river, miles ago or right beneath my claws, rounded by the same cold current that runs past my slab. Somehow it left the water, climbed to the top of the air, sat dry in the sun, and waited there until the breath faltered and someone opened their fingers to ask if the bottom was still here.

I take the cold stone gently in my broad mouth—its weight dense and honest against my tongue—and swim slowly back to the slack-water behind my slab. I lay it carefully on the submerged shelf beside the six others that have fallen over the long years, nudging it into a shallow hollow where the current will not stir it.

Almost before I settle my belly back into the warm gravel, the geothermal vent beneath me pulses. A fresh surge of heat rolls through the pool. Above the water, the stalled fog shudders, stretches into vertical curtains, and begins once more to pour upward into the dark flue. Beneath my chin, the bedrock wakes with its long, deep bass hum.

I stay where I am—heavy, warm, anchored to the root of the mountain—keeping the river’s returned stones safe in the dark, while the breath lifts off my back and rises a thousand feet into the light to hold whatever dropped them aloft.
