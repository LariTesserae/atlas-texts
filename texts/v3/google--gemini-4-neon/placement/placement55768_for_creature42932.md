At first, I think I would look for myself high behind one of the translucent screens, in the tallow-colored hush of an upper tier.

It is hard not to want that life immediately. The clean, unhurried discipline of walking the outer terrace with a narrow ceramic blade, paring soot-green moss from the hairline capillary grooves so the wall-condensation can slip downward unbroken into the basalt. The quiet pride of wiping a broad stone bench dry for a visitor who may not come for three seasons. Most of all, the secret tenderness of sitting cross-legged on the matte floor, fingertips dusted with pale ochre against the structural rods, releasing a faint, wholly unnecessary net of silver-ochre light across the gloom just to watch a cold cluster of black dish-leaves pivot on their rubbery stems and hear them rustle.

For a long while, standing in the skin-warm dusk of this basin, I try to fit myself into that tall, vertical frame. I try on the weighted dark coat that refuses to flutter on a high filament, the pale ceramic neck-plates flexing almost invisibly as the mizzle gathers half an hour away, the patience of feet that can read a hairline fissure opening a hundred meters off through the bones of the legs.

Yet the longer I hold that shape, the more I feel a faint, telling friction—the very thing the inhabitants spend centuries learning to avoid.

To be one of the seated is to belong, deeply and continuously, to a single patch of cooling crust. Your breathing, your sleep, the slow mathematical cadence of your mind soak into one ten-story ward until its walls chill to your exact measure and no one else’s. That unbroken persistence is what makes the city hold. If I tried to keep a tower of my own for three hundred years, I do not think my walls would ever stay properly cool. I would be leaning too often over the cantilevered overlooks, watching the dim tallow rhythms blinking across the half-kilometer dark. I would leave the lower colonnade lit far too constantly—that lantern-soft glow meaning *the water is steeped and the bench is dry*—until the neighboring wards began to feel a faint, polite unease at how eagerly I kept the door unlatched.

My attention does not want to settle into one unbroken tower. It wants the crossings.

So I step back down the damp hexagonal stairs, out of the high chambers, and look for myself in the wide, vegetated margins between wards.

For a moment I test the rare, uncluttered grace of the animals. The tall pale-grey strider stepping delicately among the dark rosettes, lowering its needle beak to the swollen water-bladders of the bronze ferns, vanishing into the hanging moss before anyone can intrude. Or higher up, the wide-winged glider, slate-colored and edged with dim amber pores, riding the silent thermal column above a central courtyard without spending a beat of its wings. There is real kinship in that effortless balance on invisible warmth. Still, the strider and the glider have no use for the wiped benches, no interest in the slow rising of welcome between two distant houses, no ear for the low chest-murmur that drops into silence like a bead of water into a black-glass puddle. Whatever I am in this basin, I am not wild enough to be indifferent to its courtesies.

That leaves the architecture itself—which here is never merely dead matter.

Everywhere in the basin, mind and satin-matte stone have softened their borders. Attention bleeds into walls, into hovering anchors, into the careful chill that draws water out of warm air. And when I stop looking at the towering wards and look instead at what the city has built *for the spaces between them*, something finally goes still and recognizable.

At first, I think it might be one of the stretched colonnades.

There is an extraordinary kindness in a hallway that looks fifty paces long and takes ten quiet minutes to walk—an architecture made purely to unknot one cadence before a traveler has to meet another. I linger inside that image for a while: being the cool, sweet-smelling air between pale pillars, holding that single silent shimmer halfway down where distance folds gently around a pair of bare soles, lifting the residue of one ward off a visitor’s shoulders so they can enter the next without causing its walls to warm or hum.

It is very close. Almost right.

Except a colonnade still belongs to a single ward’s threshold. It stays anchored to one house’s flank, waiting for whoever comes to that particular door. And when I listen closer to what actually pulls at me in the pewter dusk, it is not a fixed corridor at all. It is the open half-kilometer dark between complexes—the dark sea of velvet dish-leaves and razor-edged basalt—and the long, pale thing that moves slowly across it.

An aerial walkway.

Not one of the hair-thin transit filaments high overhead that cycle with a three-second sigh and carry solitary specks swiftly against the bruised sky, but one of the long, flat, unhurried spans that detach quietly from a terraced flank and drift at an inch a minute across the dim void to join another spire.

Once I settle there, nothing rubs wrong anymore. The fit is immediate and strange, and the longer I stay inside it, the more of itself it reveals.

***

I am perhaps forty paces long, narrow enough that two of the seated could walk side by side without their dark hems brushing, though they rarely do—usually one crosses alone, unhurried, casting no shadow on the damp air.

My upper deck is made of the same pale, satin-matte substance as the wards, drinking the pewter light rather than throwing it back, kept that exact fraction of a degree cooler than the ambient dusk. Moisture gathers along my flanks in a silent sheen, slips downward into fine capillary channels along my edges, and feeds the deliberately porous stone of my belly. There, out of sight from above, the long, wet curtains of plum-dark vines take root, alongside thick tangles of soot-green moss swollen with dew.

That is how I know the basin floor.

The seated read the young crust through the mineral-calloused soles of their feet. I read it through the tips of my hanging vines.

For weeks or seasons at a time, I rest moored against the quiet leeward terrace of some ward—never bolted, never mortared, simply held flush by invisible stresses matching chill to chill. While I wait, I am almost indistinguishable from a cantilevered balcony. The warm mizzle drifts laterally across the basin every few hours, darkening my matte surface, beading along my waist-low edges, running down the dense curtains beneath me until the whole length of me ticks softly with falling drops. In the deep pool of gloom underneath my belly, a pale-grey strider sometimes folds its impossibly thin legs and waits out an afternoon. Along the jagged basalt below my trailing tendrils, plum-sized iridescent grazers tick rhythmically at the salt-crusted lips of a warm fissure.

Because no one lives on me for months or years at a stretch, I never saturate with a single citizen’s unbroken rhythm. Instead, over centuries of being summoned from one ward to another, my satin-matte grain has gathered something rarer: a thin, non-dissonant sediment of many different calms.

The seated cannot live wall to wall because two dense household rhythms tangle and fret. Yet what touches me is never a whole household—only the brief, careful attentiveness of a crossing, the pause of someone tidying their thoughts on the way to a neighbor’s bench, the faint leftover warmth of a visit carried back through the dusk. Over decades, those light passes have settled into my length in wafer-thin layers, like the pale veins running through the basin’s freshly cleaved slabs, never colliding, simply giving my silence a wider grain.

I do not think in words, or in the tall, exact mathematics of the upper chambers. My awareness is made of tenths of a degree, of the slight lift and sag of invisible anchors, of the soft drag of vine-tips over wet stone, and of the unmistakable pressure-signature of every ward within three kilometers.

I know, for instance, the ward I am moored to now—an old complex near the eastern flats whose occupant has passed their fourth century. Their tallow screens burn very low and steady, with long, motionless intervals between adjustments. When they walk out onto the overlook beside me, their steps carry the slow, mineral stillness of someone whose forearms have already begun to show pale, stone-like veining beneath the matte skin. Their walls are dry-cool, disciplined, almost austere.

Half a kilometer to the west, across a wide expanse of jagged stone and overlapping plum-black rosettes, stands a much younger ward. It rests on three needle-fine points of bone-white ceramic set onto a freshly cooled shelf of basalt only a few decades ago. Its pale translucent stalks are still sparse in the plaza cracks. From here, even through the damp foliage, I can feel the faint, bright earnestness of its occupant—a newly grown solitary, still learning how to hold a complex balance in mind without letting their lower terraces warm by a hair and lose their even sheen of dew.

For two seasons, the only speech between these two towers has been the gentle dimming and brightening of tallow screens across the dusk.

Then, just after a lateral drift of mizzle passes and leaves the stone gleaming black, a low, resonant pulse travels through the basalt from the western spire.

It is not a command; nothing in the basin commands. It arrives along my anchors as a soft, sternum-deep widening of pressure, slightly brighter at the edges than an elder’s call, careful not to bruise the stillness.

I wait three slow breaths—not out of reluctance, simply because abruptness does not belong here—and then release from the eastern terrace without a click or a jar.

The crossing begins.

An inch a minute. Utterly smooth.

If you watched from a high overlook, you would think I was motionless, just another hovering architectural line suspended above the gloom, until you noticed the narrow glistening tracks appearing slowly behind me across the dustless basalt where my hanging vines comb the stone.

This is the hour when I am most myself.

Moving out from the shadow of the old ward, I leave its dry, ancient cadence behind and enter the open basin’s pooled air. The smell changes by a hair: less of wiped stone and steeped fern, more of raw newly cleaved rock, sweet faint ozone, and the thick, velvety dampness of the dish-leaves below. My trailing vine-tips brush over the sharp hexagonal steps of a shallow puddle, breaking its mirror-still reflection of the pewter sky into slow, silent rings that flatten again yards behind me. A little farther on, a warm hairline fissure breathes a faint mineral ribbon upward through my hanging moss, momentarily warming my underside before the cool dusk closes back over it.

Halfway across the half-kilometer dark, a third ward far to the north releases a correction.

A wide net of silver-ochre light glides silently over the black foliage toward me. As it reaches my length, its pale geometry warps politely around the invisible stresses holding me aloft, sliding across my matte deck like water-ripples over the floor of a deep well, warming my surface by a breath before spilling down my wet curtains of vines. Beneath me, hundreds of broad matte-black and deep plum dish-leaves pivot imperceptibly on their rubbery stems to drink the stray warmth, filling the gloom under my belly with a soft, papery rustle.

Somewhere in the young western ward ahead, the tallow glow along a lower colonnade brightens slightly—not impatient, just steady. Hours are passing. In those hours, the young solitary is wiping the overlook bench clean a second time, checking the black-glass puddle in the hexagonal step to be sure their hovering floor is perfectly still, steeping crushed bronze-fern tips with a single flake of leeward fissure-salt. Behind me, the elder is folding their hands into their weighted sleeves, letting the deep arithmetic of their tower rest while they prepare to step away from it.

My slowness is the room in which both of those quiet preparations happen.

When my leading edge finally meets the western terrace, there is no bump—only a soft, exact matching of temperature, chill kissing chill, a slight easing of the young ward’s bright surface tension as my broader, well-traveled quiet settles alongside it.

Nothing happens for ten full minutes. That, too, is part of the courtesy.

Then I feel the shift of weight at my eastern end.

The elder steps out from the old ward onto my pale matte deck, barefoot, dark mantle hanging straight down like wet stone. They do not hurry. I feel the long, supple touch of each calloused sole, spacing out evenly along my length. Because I am neither their tower nor the young one’s, my grain offers a faint, natural stretch—not as folded as a true colonnade, just a gentle wash of neutral dusk in which their centuries-old cadence loosens its grip by a fraction, making room for company.

About halfway across, suspended over the wide, dark sea of foliage, they stop.

Deep beneath the basin floor, the young rock makes its afternoon adjustment: a single clean, crystalline *tink* traveling up through the glassy strata, like a thick rod of quartz snapping underwater. A hundred meters south, a hairline seam widens by the breadth of a fingernail and exhales a small warm plume of vapor into the oiled-bronze ferns.

The elder stands completely still on my center span, weight settled evenly across both soles, head tilted slightly downward, listening through the bones of their legs. Through my hidden anchors and trailing vines, I feel it too—not as geology to be solved, only as a crisp shiver running along the basalt floor and fading into the dampness. Two breaths. Three. Somewhere far overhead, a transit filament cycles with its low three-second sigh, felt in the chest rather than heard.

Satisfied that the young tower’s three bone-white needles have held true without a tremor, the elder steps forward again, walks the remaining twenty paces, and crosses onto the western terrace.

I remain moored between the two wards for the length of the visit.

Out on the wiped stone bench, side by side, facing outward toward the violet-grey rim rather than into each other’s dark, obsidian-plum eyes, they sit in long stretches of companionable silence, letting three or four minutes drift between low sternum-murmurs so each thought can settle flat before the next is dropped. I do not hear the words—their speech is meant to dissolve within two paces of damp air—but I feel the shape of the afternoon through the stone beneath my mooring. The young ward’s slightly fretful hum steadies, drawing a calm, even sheen of condensation along its pale walls; the elder’s cool, mineralized stillness warms by a gentle, unstrained degree.

Hours later, when the cups are empty and the mizzle has drifted past once more, the elder steps back out onto my deck.

As they cross back toward the east, carrying a faint trace of the younger ward’s brightness in the weave of their coat, my satin-matte surface drinks that tiny seam of overlap—not erasing the memory of the visit, only smoothing the edge of interference before it can muddy the old tower’s walls. The excess bleeds quietly down my chilled flanks, slips along my hanging curtains of plum-dark vines, and drips harmlessly onto the black dish-leaves below.

They step off onto their own terrace. After a little while, the tallow light behind their seventh-story screens dims to its old, steady arithmetic. Across the dark, the western colonnade dims as well, leaving only a soft, well-balanced glow higher up.

No pulse comes to summon me back. I stay moored where I am, or drift slowly to a quiet flank where I will not block anyone’s view of the rim, and let the warm twilight close in.

Above me, the bruised slate bands drift imperceptibly across the high ceiling—whatever lies up there has already finished cooling. Beneath me, the glassy rock is still young, still sharp-edged, still learning how to hold roots and water. Between the widely spaced wards, the margins stay wide and hushed, full to the exact measure they desire.

And I hang quietly above the foliage, dripping softly in the pewter dusk, trailing my wet vines just above the stone—keeping no tower of my own, holding no single rhythm too tightly, waiting without hurry for the next low tremor through a terrace floor that says two silences are ready to sit near one another.
