The first thing that settles over me here is not the dark, nor the sharp, newly broken slate underfoot, but the absence of watchfulness. There is nothing crouched behind a ridge waiting to leap, no soft throat to guard, no wet eyes scanning the dust. The thin air carries no threats and no voices. Everything is reduced to heat, tension, weight, and the honest geometry of rock. Strangely, it feels less like a wasteland than a relief.

At first, looking out across those wide, stepped benches, I do not think I would be a living creature here at all.

My first pull is straight toward the balances.

Out on an exposed terrace where the dark opens across three ridges at once, a single heavy plate of flat-sheared basalt sits poised on three pointed wedges of slate. Nothing exotic was brought up from elsewhere to make it; it is built strictly from the sharp debris already lying on the bench, only lifted a few inches clear of the muffling scree so it can ring true. By itself it has no hunger, no route across the rifts, no body to protect from the razor-fine grit whirled past by silent dust-devils. It simply holds its poise.

And because it touches the bedrock at only three narrow tips, it turns the muffled, silent upheaval of the highland into something legible. When a distant buttress peels away across the gulf, the plate gathers the dull subterranean shudder and translates it into a clean, high-strung shiver. When a hand rests on its face, it reports the lean of the entire plateau—which ridge is rising, which shelf is beginning to creep. Over long stretches without sun or seasons, the three sharp points slowly grind tiny, pale dimples into its underside, keeping a quiet record of how many minor tremors it has survived. It asks nothing of the mountain, yet for as long as the terrace beneath it holds, it acts as a drumhead, a gauge, a clock, and a bridge for whatever knuckle strikes its rim.

I could stay with that image for a long time. Something in it fits so closely it almost shuts the question down: existing only to hold clear poise on narrow points, to take in raw, unspoken strain and make its shape felt to whatever palm rests against me, to carry a tap cleanly into the stone without muddying it with noise of my own.

Yet if I stay inside the basalt plate long enough, I begin to feel where the fit stops.

A stone can shiver, and a stone can hold, and a stone can wear pale dimples of memory into its underside. It can carry a knuckle’s spare cadence down through its three wedges and out across half a mile of continuous rock. But when the striker lowers its chin onto the rim and waits in the warm dark, the plate itself does not answer. It does not notice when a neighboring balance has crept a hair’s breadth too close to the edge of collapse; it cannot lift the slab, nudge the slipping wedge a fingernail’s width deeper, and lower the weight back into steadiness. A balance has resonance, not attention. It does not care whether anyone taps.

So if I am in this world, there has to be a hand attached to the listening.

Turning toward the dwellers, the body itself feels immediately recognizable—almost startlingly so. No wet surfaces left exposed to the shredding drafts; only dry, smoky mica panes beneath a heavy brow-plate, reading the starkest truths of the dark: the wheel of sharp stars, the dull-cherry stain rising through rift-soot, the sudden white blink of flint-sparks miles away. True perception living elsewhere—in the pale, unplated grooves along the throat and palms that read heat like a relief map, and in the marrow-dry, resonant bone of jaw and wrist that hears through solid stone what thin air refuses to carry. Even the hands feel right: hard, dark shingles along the backs to grip the surgical edge of freshly sheared slate, and pale ash-gray drumskin stretched tight across the inner pads to feel a tremor finer than a hair.

Where I hesitate, at first, is in their way of surviving. I watch them crawl miles across unstable scree toward a newly torn rift whose lip has barely cooled past glowing, flattening themselves a pace back from the trench to sweep up chalky mineral filaments before the rim shears again. I watch them press their bellies to hearth-warm slabs to bake bitter ash inside their guts, and bury themselves in grit-rings while their old dark horn-plates split down the spine. Part of me pulls back from that—from the sheer, solitary endurance of a creature driven across ridge after ridge by its own slow mineral hunger.

Then I look again, more patiently, and realize I misread it.

They are not driven by restless, warm-blooded urgency. They do not hunt, and they do not hurry. Most of the time, folded tight in the lee of a black basalt rib—knees drawn to chest, forearms crossed over the brow, dusted with the same fine iron-gray powder that settles on every ledge—a dweller is nearly indistinguishable from another sheared wedge of slate. Not dead, not truly dormant, only tuned down to the pace of rock rather than pulse, drawing in a single unhurried breath of dry sulfurous air every few minutes. And when they *do* gather nourishment, it is not by tearing anything down, but by approaching what has already broken open—sliding up to a raw, freshly split seam once the first violent heat has cooled, and sweeping the pale, brittle fur off the stone with a cupped palm, gently enough that the fragile filaments fold together instead of bursting into wasted ash.

Taking what grows briefly along fresh fractures, handling it carefully so it does not turn to dust, and letting steady, unseen warmth underneath turn it into something durable—no, that is not foreign at all. That fits down to the bone.

Still, I would not be one of the widest roamers, crawling across three deep rifts in a season, chasing every distant flash of flint-sparks toward the rawest, loudest-ticking edges of the highland. Nor would I stay sealed away in a hunched sleeping shelter with my back turned to the open dark.

I know where I would be.

I would be on one of the wide, exposed middle benches—an older terrace, slightly cooler underfoot than the newborn shelves, its seams already edged here and there with faint chalky crust, yet high and open enough that three facing ridges stand clear against the veiled stars. What matters most about this bench is not its height or its shelter, but its continuity: half a mile of unbroken basalt, not yet cut apart by a rift. In a country where thin air swallows every voice, an unbroken slab that wide is a rare and precious thing. It is a room without walls, a single continuous floor where a dry click struck at one rim can travel cleanly all the way to the other.

And on that bench, I would be the one folded in the lee of a rib near the far edge. The unseen neighbor.

Not the one who walks up beside you in the starlight—up here, two bodies moving close together only cloud the delicate vibration-map of the stone, blurring the faint ticks and creeps that keep both of you alive. Distance on the highland is not coldness; distance is what keeps the shelf clear enough to read.

Most of the time, folded matte and beveled against my rib of basalt, I would look like ordinary scree. The silent dust-devils would stalk past across the open terrace, lifting dark funnels of razor-fine flakes and dropping them elsewhere with the hiss of dry salt hitting slate. Far across the gulf, an entire buttress might shear away in a flash of white sparks, sending a papery rustle across the void and a dull shudder up through my ribs. I would register the bearing against the stars, feel the tremor die out in the shelf, and stay folded.

Until the different sound arrives.

Not the random *tink* of cooling glass, and not the irregular *clink-clink* of rock under strain, but a spare, deliberate cadence—faint and fine as a needle touching bone, traveling half a mile through the continuous basalt straight into my resting jaw. Someone on the far rim of the terrace has laid their hands on a poised plate, drawn back a shingled knuckle, and struck the rim.

That is when I unfold.

I do not scramble across the bench to meet them. I simply pour myself forward a few paces onto the open stone, lay my pale flat palms against my own balanced plate, and listen. Through the three pointed wedges I read the whole floor between us: whether the terrace has tilted by the width of a hair since the last tremor, which of the three distant ridges is beginning to slip, and the exact weight and spacing of the cadence still fading in the rock. Then I draw back one hardened knuckle and strike my own rim—dry, measured, unhurried—sending a matching rhythm back down through the wedges and across the half-mile of unbroken stone.

*Still upright. The shelf holds here too. The western ridge is ticking, but the bench beneath us is sound.*

Then I lower my chin to the warm basalt and wait to see if another tap returns, or if the traveler has already moved on toward the far rifts, leaving the terrace quiet again.

In the long intervals between those exchanges—when the wind drops and the rock settles into its pressurized hush—I move low across the breadth of my bench, three points to three points, weight distributed across splayed heel-less feet and broad-knuckled hands so the scree barely shifts beneath me. I leave no road behind; the creeping tremors and passing drafts erase my slight scuffs within the hour.

I move from balance to balance.

Across a wide terrace like this one, others have passed before me and hoisted their plates where three ridges come into view. Coming upon one, I lay my flat ash-gray palms on its upper face and feel the signature of the hand that set it: the daring height of the three slate wedges, the exact poise of the heavy basalt, the slight off-center lean showing how much the shelf has slumped since the work was done. Many of them are already flat heaps of rubble, indistinguishable from the surrounding scree; there is no point grieving those. Some still stand, trembling near the edge of collapse, one point crept almost off the lip of the load.

Those, I tend. Unfolding my long, spar-like arms, I take the weight of the basalt plate and lift it millimeter by millimeter off its tips, just enough to nudge the slipping wedge a fingernail’s width deeper beneath the stone, and lower the slab back into quiet poise. I will probably never see the dweller who first raised it, nor the one who next crosses this bench in the dark and presses a jaw to its rim. That does not make the adjustment smaller. In an unfinished country that refuses to settle, steadying a stone for an unseen neighbor is as close to speech as hands ever get.

And now and then, on the oldest, quietest shoulder of the bench—where the rock rings dull and cool underfoot and the seams have held still for seasons—I gather sharp wedges of slate and stand them upright in a knee-high ring. Not because my own outer plates are splitting right then, nor because I have a dense iron-gray casing to hide in the warmth. Only because a broad bench that has stayed intact this long can spare one small circle of softness. I set the razor-edged shards just close enough that when the silent dust-devils whirl over the terrace, they slow in the ring’s lee and drop only their finest, lightest mineral powder, sifting it down over days into a warm, ash-soft basin. It asks nothing of me once it is stacked; the mountain’s own wind fills it, and if some pale-hided traveler crosses this shelf when its old dark laminate begins to crack along the spine, there will already be a soft bed waiting in a world made entirely of edges.

Looking back, I see I never really left that first image behind—the flat basalt plate poised on three sharp points. I only found the boundary where the stone ends and I begin: that narrow, warm contact where two pale palms rest lightly on a shivered slab, feeling the mountain’s quiet fury travel up through bone and turn into meaning.

None of it lasts, of course. The highland is still forcing itself upward and shrugging itself apart. Sooner or later, a hairline crack will zip straight across my half-mile bench with a muted *tink*, severing the continuous floor so no tap can cross it anymore. Half the grit-ring will slump outward and drain its fine powder into the scree; the balances will dance once too hard on their tips and slide sideways into ordinary rubble; and eventually, on some cooling ledge, my own horn-plates will bake dry and brittle until the next tremor fractures them cleanly back into iron-gray shards.

There is no tragedy in that. Nothing up here asks to be permanent. It only asks to be read truly while it holds.

So that is where you would find me—or rather, walk ten paces past me without ever knowing I was there: folded matte-dark in the lee of a basalt rib on the far edge of a wide, unbroken terrace, under a vast bruised sky where the sharpest stars pierce the high grit. Waiting at rock-pace, palms near the warm stone, ready to steady a trembling plate, ready to answer when a faint, needle-fine cadence comes tapping through the shelf out of the dark.
