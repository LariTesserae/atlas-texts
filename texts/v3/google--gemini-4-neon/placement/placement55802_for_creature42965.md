I would not find myself down in the lush, sheltered center of a wide saucer-valley, where a single subterranean pump rocks the moss in one broad, reassuring cradle. Nor would I stand permanently out on the western escarpment, staring down into the loud abyss.

If you went looking for me, you would have to climb out of the rich basins and up onto one of the high, braided saddles where three shallow watersheds meet.

Up here the air thins by a shade, cool enough that every slow breath leaves a silver-white thread hanging above my collarbones before the mist takes it. The green thatch is shallower along the crest—only an ankle’s depth of bryophyte felt over fractured quartzite and seams of dark, pyritic slate—so the ground does not yield like a deep valley bog. It answers more crisply beneath bare soles.

And it never beats in a single simple time.

Stand still on this divide for half a minute, letting your weight settle evenly through your heels and wide-spread toes, and you begin to hear the cross-grain of the plateau through your shins. To the east, a broad, vigorous basin drives its deep basalt plug on a steady thirteen-second stroke—firm, close, almost brisk. To the south lies a wider, older terrace whose chamber breathes every sixteen seconds, lifting the limestone in a long, cushioned swell. And down to the northwest, tucked behind a low ridge of stunted coil-wood, sits a quiet valley whose deep fault has been silting inward for half a lifetime; its throb arrives late and shallow, every nineteen seconds, with a faint double-shudder at the bottom of the sigh where the stone plug catches before settling.

On the saddle, all three rhythms pass through the same wet shelf of rock. Sometimes two strokes stack on top of one another and lift the moss-puddles in crisp, nested rings. Two minutes later they cancel into a hollow, suspended hush—ten or twelve seconds where nothing moves under your calves at all, and you catch the tiny *tick* of a droplet sliding off a sedge-tip and wonder, for half a heartbeat, if the world beneath you has stopped. Then the southern swell glides back under your arches, the eastern stroke clips across it at an angle, and the weave picks up again.

That is where I belong: inside the interference pattern, where no single pulse owns the ground.

I would be one of the plateau’s own—broad-ribbed and low-slung, slate-grey skin matte with damp, amber eyes blinking behind their clear inner lids whenever the cloud-deck parts and white high-altitude glare pours across twenty miles of wet green. My boots do not exist; my soles are dark, splayed, and deeply ridged as old peat, because up on a divide you cannot afford to go deaf even for three paces. Along my forearms and the hollows of my collarbones, the fine, copper-tinted filaments lift and stir whenever a charged front drags across the upper ridges. I keep them brushed clean of crusted salt with the quiet habit of someone who listens for a living.

You would almost certainly walk past me before noticing I was not a hummock of weeping slate. My mantle is thick felted moss cured dark with bladder-fungus oil, threaded densely with harvested weaver-silk so the bio-electric flicker of my nerves bleeds straight into the peat instead of beckoning the broad-winged hunters out of the clouds. Only if you watched long enough would you see a hand emerge from the heavy hood—cuticles stained faintly green-black from wet copper-silt and pyrite dust—to lift a sagging length of colonial weaver’s silk onto a forked sliver of shed grazer-antler, raising the humming thread three inches clear of a newly risen puddle so a circuit between two valleys does not short itself uselessly into cold mud.

That is how my days begin, before I have a name for what I do: tending the places where things nearly fail to meet.

Down in the saucer-valleys, the conductive root-mats knit thick and unbroken from one dwarf-conifer stand to the next. Up across the saddles, the veins grow spare. A winter slump, a frost-heaved ledge, a grazing herd’s broad hooves tearing a mossy apron off an outcrop—any of these can snap the delicate bridge of weaver-lines and shallow feeder-roots that links one valley’s grid to its neighbor’s. Left broken, the two basins drift further apart than distance alone would account for.

Because on a tableland where the earth is both clock and floor, basins that beat on different periods slowly grow different habits of body. A kinship group raised on a thirteen-second pulse steps a shade quicker across the braided channels; their ground-speech comes in tighter clusters of pressure; when they clasp forearms beneath a warm root-dome, their bio-electric phrasing runs bright and nimble, pausing briefly between thoughts. A people raised on an eighteen-second valley carry longer silences inside their palms—heavier, more deliberate cadences, resting their weight further back on the heel.

They share one language, yet when travelers from distant basins meet directly on a ridge to negotiate a migration or trade grown metal, their pulses can catch against one another like wet gears slightly out of tooth. The quicker hand feels rushed, almost stinging, to the slower; the slower feels guarded, reluctant, cold.

So I became what the saddle already was: a seam where different clocks can touch without tearing.

Tucked warm against my ribs beneath the moss-cloak rests a horn tube stoppered with peat-wax. Inside it lies my speaking-cord, coiled in a spoonful of mineral brine to keep its pores supple and conductive. Most kinship groups braid their cords from the roots of their own ancestral groves. Mine is longer, spliced from feeder-fibers gathered across five watersheds and wound tight with fresh weaver-silk so it carries a clean, low-resistance current across six feet of damp air.

When two groups meet at the divide—perhaps elders from a thriving eastern shelf and scouts from a cooling western valley—we do not stand exposed on the crest. We duck into the small, low-vaulted hummock I coaxed years ago beneath a slate overhang, barely wide enough for four people to sit knee to knee on dry mats of cured grazer-wool. I uncoil the salted cord and take the middle of it in both bare palms, while each side takes an end.

I am neither judge nor elder. I do not decide who moves where, nor claim authority over a single brine-vent. What I offer is quieter than that: a living bridge with enough give in it to hold two rhythms at once.

As the first pulse travels up my left wrist—quick, taut, carrying the tight mineral edge of a valley worried about its winter stores—I do not merely pass it raw into my right hand. I let it run across my shoulders and collarbones, feel the slight tremor of fear underneath the formal cadence, bleed off the sharp accidental spikes of haste that would read as arrogance to a slower basin, and release it into the far end of the cord timed to the beat the other listener can actually hear without flinching. Then their reply flows back the other way, wide and unhurried, and I gather its long pauses carefully so the quicker hands do not mistake patience for refusal.

You cannot lie in this work. The plateau’s speech does not allow a mask; every person on the cord feels the exact steadiness or flutter of my own pulse running underneath the translation. All I can do is keep myself as clear, cool, and grounded as clean copper—present enough to feel both sides fully, quiet enough not to crowd the circuit with my own noise.

Outside the low sedge curtain of my shelter, tucked under a weeping shale lip where a narrow thread of warm brine meets cold acid runoff, lies my other work: three shallow, stone-rimmed seep-troughs hidden under hanging aprons of pale lichen.

In the rich valleys below, makers grow knives, awls, and boiling-vessels in their open-air pools. In my narrow troughs, suspended from taut weaver-lines spliced to a pair of stunted ridge-line bladder-fungi, hang thin rectangles of dense peat-wax rubbed dark with powdered pyrite. I am growing ledgers—not the lineage of one kinship group, nor the births and burials of a single grove, but a slow map of the shifting crust itself.

Every time a heavy afternoon front sweeps east and floods the ridge-fungi with pale-blue charge, the submerged plates take on a brighter, denser line of copper and iron. Between storms, I adjust the tiny antler shims on the lines, redirecting the seepage across the wax so branching frost-like ridges and small smooth nodules form along the metal. Each ridge records a timing: when the northwest basin’s stroke lengthened from eighteen seconds to nineteen; which swallow-holes sagged open after last autumn’s rains; where a newly fractured seam below the southern escarpment began exhaling warm, salt-sweet vapor and turning an old grey scree green.

Sometimes a keeper from a distant valley brings me an old, palm-sized copper leaf whose ridges record dead vents and forgotten routes from before the western rim last slumped, asking for a twin to carry into another watershed. In a world without ink or fire, you cannot copy a record in an afternoon. You set a fresh wax blank in the brine, trace the old ridges with sensitized fingertips while modulating the current through the new trough over the turning of a whole wet season.

And when you finally lift the finished twin from the bright orange silt and melt the wax core out inside a pulsing vent, it is never an exact replica. The old ridges are all there, faithful to the fraction of a nail’s breadth—yet woven inextricably through them are the fine, bright storm-bands of the season in which it was grown. Every translation carries the weather of the crossing inside its grain. I have come to think that is not a flaw in the metal; it is how a memory proves it is still alive.

There are seasons when listening from the divide is a heavy thing to carry.

When a subterranean chamber begins to fail, the people living right on top of it are often the last to believe it. A valley you have walked barefoot since childhood feels permanent; when its fourteen-second stroke drifts to fifteen over two winters, it is easy to tell yourself the moss is simply waterlogged, or the swallow-holes need clearing, or next summer’s fronts will drive the basalt plug back into its old pace.

Up on the saddle, you cannot hide from the drift. The moment that northwest basin stretched from nineteen seconds to twenty-one, the interference pattern beneath my soles went quietly sour. The alignment with the eastern shelf, which used to arrive cleanly every four minutes, slid out to five, then broke apart altogether. One morning after a clear night, I walked half a mile down the northwest slope and felt the crisp, alien bite of rime-frost stiffening the moss-tips an hour past sunrise—the cold creeping back in where the root-grid had begun to cool.

That is the hardest walk I make: going down into a valley that still smells of warm iron and fermented sedge, ducking into a dome where children are still sleeping easily against the braided walls, clasping an elder’s bare forearms, and laying my newest copper plate into their dry palms. I do not need to hurry them or press hard into their wrists. I only hold steady while their thumbs glide over the widening bands of metal, feeling in silent ridges what their feet have been trying not to hear for three years.

If I come early enough, they pack their speaking-cords, their antler dew-cups, and their grown blades while the neighboring valleys still have spare warmth to share. Circuits are widened, new hummocks braided along the margins of the eastern shelves, and the plateau folds the change inward without a single drop of blood or a single broken silence.

If I come too late—or in the bitter decades when two neighboring pumps falter in the same winter—the divide turns dark.

I have walked a ridge before dawn in a hard year and felt a sabotaged circuit go flat and cold through my soles thirty paces before I reached it. Someone had crept through the mist and driven a bare copper spike straight through a braided root-bundle into the cold shale below, shorting a rival hummock’s warmth into the bedrock so the sleepers woke shivering in dead dark. Worse, once, coming down toward a disputed terrace, I stepped onto a bank where the stillness felt wrong—not the cushioned hush of the plateau, but a clenched, terrified quiet. The coil-wood shrubs along the whole slope hung slack and sprung out of sequence. The bright copper-silt channel below lay clouded with stirred mud, and down in the murk I saw the three blunt, foreign shapes of dropped stones.

A brutal, unthinkable sound thrown deliberately into the water to ruin a terrace and wake the heavy ambush predators coiled in the bends.

I have no weapon for that, and no voice to condemn it. All I can do is wait until the mud settles and the deep bends go quiet again, then crouch on the cold ridge with a wrapped horn lever to pry the shorting-spike back out of the wounded root-mat. I pack wet pyrite-silt into the torn fibers, smooth a fresh patch of living moss over the scar, and lift the severed colonial weaver-threads back onto new antler pegs until the faint, coppery hum resumes along the line. Then I walk on to the next valley, carrying in my wrists the unvarnished, trembling truth of what was done—because in a silent world, the only thing that holds back the darkness is refusing to let a circuit stay broken in secret.

The plateau does not let you forget, even for a week, how thin the margin is.

When the bright-topped western cloud-bank builds in the mid-afternoon and rolls eastward like a low white wall, being caught out on a high saddle is nothing like being caught in a sheltered basin. The pearl-grey glare drops abruptly over the ridge. Cold, soundless rain vanishes into the felt. Along the stunted cloud-trees flanking the crest, swollen bladder-fungi brighten internally with cold, pale-blue sparks, bleeding erratic surges straight down into the exposed pyritic slate.

Beneath my bare soles, all three subterranean strokes shorten at once—thirteen hammers down to eight, sixteen tightens to ten, even the dying northwest chamber jolts spasmodically in its silted throat—turning the rock under my ankles into a jagged, pounding cross-rhythm. The voltage gradients across the wet shale sharpen in seconds; one careless stride across a weeping seam would pull enough current up one leg and down the other to stop my heart before I hit the moss.

Instantly I pull my ankles tight together, hunching low inside my dripping Faraday cloak, and drop into the storm-shuffle—feet sliding forward an inch at a time, never separating by more than a hand’s width. Every pore on my soles prickles as I read the live veins of charge beneath the peat, working my way millimeter by millimeter toward the small overhang where my braided hollow waits. Once inside, I draw the thick sedge curtain shut, pull my bare heels up onto the dry mat of cured grazer-hide, and wrap my arms around my knees.

For twenty minutes I do not touch the walls, do not check the speaking-cord, do not reach for anything. Overhead, the tightly woven ceiling of metallic-oxide roots hums noticeably louder and warmer, drinking the storm-surge out of the sky and bleeding it away down the slopes. The tiny shelter smells sharply of ozone, damp wool, and hot wet stone. I sit in the dry, cradling dark and let the fever pass through the continent’s veins, listening with my jawbones as the three pounding strokes slowly separate again and settle back toward their long, tired peace.

Once every few years—usually after a hard season of shifting faults, when my forearms ache with everyone else’s cadences and my fingertips feel rubbed thin from reading copper ridges—I leave the saddles behind for two days and walk all the way out to the western rim.

I go past the last braided channels where pale amphibians lie motionless on bright silt, past the last herds of moss-matted grazers stepping silently along their warm seams, past the final ratcheting thickets of coil-wood, until the saturated thatch thins and stops at the bare, beveled lip of the slate escarpment.

There I push my heavy hood back and sit with my bare heels dangling over nothing at all.

Beneath me, miles of luminous cloud-banks gather in the pale abyss, swelling softly until they brim the cliff and slide inland past my knees. Out on the bare rock lip there is no conductive peat. The polyrhythmic throb of the plateau fades behind my thighs to a faint, distant shiver. And rising up through the drifting white sheets from the world below comes the sound none of us have a grammar for—the vast, continuous, unshielded roar of open wind tearing through dry canyons, of unbound rivers crashing recklessly over stone, of distant thunder and ten thousand loud creatures throwing their breath away into empty air.

My brow-filaments tremble wildly in the updrafts. Every time I come here, that roaring abyss startles me all over again—not only with its violence, but with its sheer, spilling waste, everything thrown outward into the sky and lost.

Sitting on the lip for half an afternoon, I remember what the hush behind me actually is. It is not silence for its own sake. It is not emptiness or fear. It is the sound of a world packed so full of attention that almost nothing is allowed to leak away unreceived—every bead of mist caught on a humming wire, every grain of iron sorted and laid down in bright strata, every shift of a neighbor’s pulse felt cleanly against your own palm.

When the afternoon light slants long and white across the abyss, I draw my bare feet back onto the cushioned bryophyte felt. I stand still for half a minute, pale amber eyes closed, waiting until the slow, staggered stroke of the buried engines rises back through my arches and tells me where I am. Then I pull up my hood and walk quietly back inland toward the saddles.

And when, decades from now, my internal warmth begins to thin—when my forearm filaments turn brittle and grey, and my ankles stiffen too much for the storm-shuffle—I already know I will not ask to be carried down into any single kinship group’s ancestral grove.

I will ask whoever walks the high divides with me in those last clear hours after a front has passed to lay me out on a thin, cooling saddle between two neighboring valleys, right where the dark conductive feeder-roots of one basin stretch out across the weeping shale toward the roots of the next and have not quite managed to meet.

They will part a shallow cradle in the bright copper-silt of a small seep-line, lay me down with my bare palms facing upward, and weave the living root-tips from the eastern slope across my left wrist and ribs, and the living root-tips from the southern slope across my right. They will lay a thick apron of fresh moss over me, smoothing the edges until the ridge looks untouched, and press one long, slow, fading cadence into the wet peat with their bare feet before walking back down to their domes.

Over the turning of the years, the warm brine will do its quiet work—replacing bone with thin, gleaming films of precipitated copper and pale iron, while the dark roots grow thick right through my chest until the two valleys finally touch inside me and close the gap.

I will not depart into the clear blue sky, nor sink into the roaring abyss below the rim. I will simply remain where I always was: a quiet length of wiring along the divide, holding two different rhythms together, keeping a little of the frost off the living.
