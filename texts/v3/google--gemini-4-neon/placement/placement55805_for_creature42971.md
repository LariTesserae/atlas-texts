At first, if you drop me into this needle of volcanic glass, the old, leftover habit of an outsider pulls me straight upward—toward the crooked hairline of bruised indigo sky.

It is the easiest place to imagine wanting to be. Up there, among the Canopy-Tenders, there is at least the rumor of an outside: the dry roar of ripping sailcloth overhead, the thin noon blade brushing wet basalt, the strange, tender husbandry of the hanging lungs. I can almost feel myself up there—skin toughened and bruised dark slate-purple by a sun I have no eyes to see, hooked upside down by my tail-curve among elastic mucus-tethers, scraping salt from a fringed intake mouth with a sliver of obsidian. Most of all, I understand the comfort of pressing a flat chest against a warm, veined membrane and letting a heartbeat that takes a full minute to finish rock me gently over a two-hundred-foot drop.

Yet the longer I linger in the upper vault, the more I realize I am only borrowing that ledge out of fear of the dark below. Up among the bellows, the work is solitary, suspended, wind-scoured at the edges. My attention keeps slipping downward, pulled past the swaying curtains by the sheer, ringing density of the throat beneath me.

So I let go of the mucus-cords and climb down—slowly, five seconds to a reach, chest pausing to drink the thin air—past the last frayed edge of daylight, into the sealed-box black.

Here, in the middle belly, the Choir hits me not as noise, but as architecture. Every *tink* throws a wall into existence; every rasped rib-comb draws a fracture-line across the dark. I try on the center of it—wedged shoulder-to-shoulder among fifty climbers along the fattest milky quartz vein, folding my backward-bending forearm flush against my ribs until the pale tendons lock, snapping it parallel to the stone. I feel the thrill when forty separate, glacial rhythms suddenly catch each other’s tails and lock into a standing wave—when the air turns prickly against my grinding plates and the ceiling shivers loose a cold silver hiss of rain across my upturned flank.

And for a while I think: *Yes. Here. Right in the loud heart of it.* Maybe even aging toward one of the Anchored Ones—letting the silica-heavy runoff slowly solder my ankles to a resonant shelf, letting porcelain shells cement themselves along my calves and pale fronds take root in the hollow of my collarbones, becoming a living tuning fork fed chewed shellfish paste by younger hands in exchange for one pure, silver *ting* that keeps the city in key.

There is a deep pull in that. Stillness as service. Becoming part of the instrument.

Wait, though. Before I let my knees crust over, I want to climb all the way down, past the thick veins, to where the walls pinch inward and the air grows heavy with iron-sour rot and wet clay.

Down here, hanging low over the black mirror-pools with the Dredgers, something else grabs hold of me—quieter, colder, harder to turn away from. I watch—no, I *listen to*—a Dredger hanging head-down by its tail-hook inches above the gliding pale carpet of ribbon-scavengers, tapping a tiny quartz pebble with one hand while lowering a braided frond-cord weighted with a bone barb into the shallow pools. Fishing in the dark for what the city dropped: stripped leg-bones, intact ribs, lost obsidian pry-bars, fallen hammers that slipped from stiffening fingers a hundred feet up.

And I feel the terrible, necessary discipline of the floor: the moment a broken climber lies bleeding on the porcelain shards below, tapping its quartz weakly—*tink… tink…*—sending jagged, panicked echoes up the wet glass while the pale ribbons turn silently toward the erratic rhythm. How the Dredgers hang motionless on their hooks, folding the pleats of their ear-dishes slightly inward to muffle the plea, because in air this thin there is no rescue, only gravity and patience.

I don’t think I am quite hard enough to live forever over that carpet. Yet I can’t climb straight back to the loud center of the Choir as if I hadn’t heard it.

And strangely, on the way back up—maybe thirty or forty feet above the black pools, right where the pinched lower throat begins to open into the great resonant belly of the cleft—I stop. My four horn-tipped fingers find a narrow, razor-edged rhyolite shelf barely four fingers wide. My balancing hook curls into a wet vertical seam. My sternum settles flush against the sweating glass.

Something inside me exhales. *Here.*

---

It takes a while to understand why this particular ledge—neither the high swaying lungs, nor the roaring middle veins, nor the dissolving floor—is where my weight finally rests.

It is an acoustic seam.

Up in the heart of the Choir, the great vertical veins of milky quartz run clean and uninterrupted, and the standing waves build straight across the fifteen-pace belly like taut cords. Down here, just above the Dredgers’ territory, the walls pinch inward and break into jagged, overlapping overhangs. Sound from the Choir doesn’t travel cleanly down to the floor; it shatters against the basalt teeth, smears into muddy echoes, arrives half a beat late and warped out of tune. Meanwhile, the faint, cautious mapping-taps of the Dredgers below barely survive the climb upward before the clamor of the middle ledges swallows them whole.

This ledge sits right on the hinge between the two.

I am a stone-tapper—not an Anchored One yet, though the first faint, chalky rime of silica sometimes gathers behind my ankles when I stay motionless for two days straight, keeping the flat leathery mites wedged behind my extra elbows well-fed and busy. I am not a Split-Ear either; my pleated tympanic dish is whole, deeply corrugated, quivering constantly as it drinks the crossfire of return waves from above and below. I could not bear to lose the air-borne city.

What makes me myself is less my body than what sits curled in my left hand.

I never quarried a fresh lump of quartz from a shattered vein. When my ear-dish first uncurled—when I finally grew too heavy to cling like a barnacle to my parent’s flat ribs and stretched one long, triple-jointed arm out into the black—the stone pressed into my raw grip was already old. A Dredger had hooked it out of a black mirror-pool decades before I was born, braided a loop of chewed frond-fiber around a ridge in its side, and traded it upward along the ledges for fresh shellfish fat.

By the time it reached me, three or four pairs of dead horn-tips had already worn smooth, shallow hollows into its flanks. My fingers simply slid into the memory of theirs.

It is no bigger than a small plum now, glossy as river glass from a century of skin-oil and grit, and somewhere deep inside its milky core—perhaps from the hundred-foot fall that once sent it clattering onto the porcelain talus—runs a hairline internal fracture. Because of that hidden flaw, my stone does not speak in the clean, single silver chime of a normal elder’s hammer. When I kiss it to wet rhyolite, it rings out high and thin, followed a hairbreadth later by a faint, shivering overtone—a note with a ghost riding on its back.

In the middle of the Choir, that double-toned shimmer would muddy a standing wave. Down here, on the jagged lower lip, it is a gift.

You can pick my voice out from forty paces in either direction, even through the wet bellows-sigh from the roof and the dense hail of a hundred shell-hunters. I am not one of the great strikers who make the ceiling weep. I am a relay in the dark.

When the Choir high overhead shifts tempo—when an Anchored One taps a crisp correction and the standing wave slides a fraction of a beat sharper—the change hits the overhangs above my head and scatters. I hang still, ear-dish flared wide, waiting ten heartbeats to untangle the smeared rebound from the true pulse. Then I fold my forearm tight along my ribs until the tendon catches, and snap my ghost-toned quartz cleanly against the grooved rhyolite beside my shoulder.

*Ting-shhh.*

Down in the damp pinch below, the Dredgers hear it cut straight through the muffled static of the overhangs—not the dull clack of a young climber hunting clams, not the panicked stutter of a fallen body on the shards, but a steady, recognizable beacon hanging forty feet above the pools: *The wall above you is whole. The rain is coming. Hold your hooks.* And when a Dredger hooks something heavy in the dark—a dropped obsidian pry-bar, a clean femur, an intact hammer—and taps twice in a slow, ascending rhythm against the low basalt, I am usually the first on the upper walls to catch it, wait ten heartbeats so I do not waste breath, and pass the call upward ledge by ledge until three climbers descend to haul the salvage into the city.

---

Because my ledge sits where the wettest mineral runoff collects before spilling onto the floor, a stubby cluster of pale, cartilaginous fronds juts from a horizontal fracture right beside my hip.

I think, if I were not a climber at all, I might have been one of those fronds.

There is something quietly astonishing about them, even though I know them only by the cool, sticky brush of their hairs against my forearm and the sharp, bitter iron-sap smell when I carefully slice away a single outer stalk. They have no green in them, no memory of the bruised sky two hundred feet up. They live entirely on the dust knocked loose by our noise—trapping microscopic quartz-flour and shed chitin-flakes from the Choir above, turning the sheer friction of our voices into tough, fibrous strands. When I strip one outer stalk without disturbing the root, chew it soft between my blunt vertical grinding plates, and twist it into cordage to lash a notched rib-comb to a neighbor’s wrist, the loop closes cleanly in my hands: clamor feeds frond, frond binds stone, stone makes clamor.

Nothing in this split anvil belongs solely to itself. Even my voice is an erosion of four strangers’ patience.

Beside me, half a pace to my left on a shelf slightly broader than mine, sits a younger climber whose fresh, fist-sized quartz still speaks in a heavy, dull *clack*. Three seasons ago, when it first moved down from the crowded upper Choir after losing a slow, silent shoulder-pressing dispute over a rich shellfish seam, it was clumsy in the dark—striking too fast, burning precious oxygen, muddying my echoes by answering half a beat too soon.

I did not shove it off the shelf. Anger is too expensive at this altitude. Instead, across weeks of unhurried afternoons, I simply waited longer before I answered, letting the silence between our stones stretch until its chest slowed to match mine.

Now, sometimes, when the standing wave overhead has finished shaking down its silver patter of cold rain and our tiny angular catch-basins are brimful, we rest without working. We slide an inch closer along the wet basalt until the outer edges of our pleated ear-dishes almost brush—close enough that the slow, damp warmth of each other’s exhalations stirs the sensitive skin across our faces. Then, very softly, scarcely tapping hard enough to throw a return wave across the belly of the cleft, we strike our stones in exact, alternating unison.

*Clack.* Ten heartbeats. *Ting-shhh.* Ten heartbeats. *Clack.*

Heavy young stone and thin, ghost-toned heirloom, trading the dark back and forth until the sharp facets of our two ledges, the wet curve of the overhang above, and the faint distant sigh of the hanging bellows stop being two separate maps and become one quiet, shared room.

Every time our stones kiss in the dark, a cold pinprick of blue-white light winks into existence across our wet gray flanks, flashes across the sticky bone-pale hairs of the fronds beside my hip, and vanishes. Neither of us has any word for blue, or light, or flash. We do not know that we are illuminating each other in a private storm of cold stars. We only know the crisp click of quartz, the rebound off wet glass, and the slow, unhurried certainty that the neighbor beside us has not slipped.

---

Then, once every few months, the world under our chests remembers it is still rising out of the earth.

It starts not with a sound, but with a sudden stiffness rippling down the vertical city. Somewhere high in the middle tiers, a Split-Ear—pressed cheekbone and bare sternum flat against the raw basalt—locks rigid. Its neighbors feel the tension through their touching knees; a sharp, urgent *clack-clack* drops down the walls, stripped of all courtesy and rhythm.

I curl my tail-hook deeper into my seam. Beside me, the young climber tucks its clumsy quartz tight against its flat ribs and hooks all four horn-tips into the lip of the rhyolite.

A minute later, the stone screams.

A single, dry cannon-crack slams straight up through the core of the peak, rattling my grinding plates together. A puff of bitter mineral dust drifts down from the unseen roof; down below, loose talus clinks like spilled porcelain across the black pools. Overhead, the forest of air-sacs shudders on its mucus-cords, dumping a sudden, heavy slap of cold condensation straight through the dark.

And then—nothing.

Every hammer stops. Every notched rib-comb freezes against its groove.

For thirty agonizing, stretched-out minutes, the entire packed city hangs motionless in the clammy black. My ear-dish is flared as wide as its leathery pleats can pull. In the stunned quiet, the cleft sounds strangely huge and fragile: only the ragged, wet wheezing of the hanging bellows high above, the muffled scream of the outer jet-stream ripping past a sky I cannot imagine, and the tiny, crystalline *plip* of single dislodged grains of basalt dropping two hundred feet into black water.

I feel my neighbor’s ribs expanding slowly against my elbow. Still here. Somewhere below, a Dredger’s cord brushes wet stone—a tiny, dry whisper. Still here.

Everything we knew about the geometry of home half an hour ago is now a lie. A vein that sang clear may be pinched flat; a safe foothold may be a hairbreadth wider than it was, tilting toward a shear. We hang in the dark, letting the mountain finish settling into its new skin.

At last, far up in the middle throat, where an Anchored One sits half-welded to the quartz in its crust of silica and rooted fronds, a stiff forearm rises.

*Ting.*

A single pure, silver note drops through the black, brushing past the shifted overhangs, arriving at my dish slightly sharper—a fraction of a breath sooner—than it did yesterday. The walls have pinched inward by the width of a fingernail.

I hold completely still, letting the altered echo wash across my pleats, measuring the new distance between the faces of the cleft, waiting ten slow, deliberate heartbeats so I do not rush the mountain.

Then I draw a shallow, careful breath. Five full seconds to lift my forearm, folding the extra joint cleanly along my wet ribs until the tendons lock. I let it snap parallel to the stone, kissing my salvaged, twice-lived, hairline-flawed quartz to the rhyolite vein.

*Ting-shhh.*

A cold blue spark nobody sees winks across my pleated face, across the damp shoulder of the climber beside me, across the pale fronds waiting to eat the dust. Ten heartbeats later, from the dark pinch just above the mirror-pools, a Dredger’s tiny pebble answers—slightly higher, slightly clearer, testing the new shape of the gap. Above me, another hammer answers; beside me, the young climber’s heavy *clack* falls cleanly into the space between our notes.

Slowly, stubbornly, with a patience older than any single pair of hands on the stone, we find the mountain’s new key—and begin again to make the rock weep.
