At first, I think I would be the one who comes back for the tilted mirror.

That single detail snags in me the moment I drop over the lip of shattered stone: a square plate of hammered copper, knocked off its aim by frost-heave, pouring a hard-won rectangle of midday glare uselessly onto empty ice while something vast and ancient stays locked two feet away in the dark. Walking past that feels impossible. So my first instinct is to put on wool and leather, climb up from the snowline with a dry twist of juniper lashed to my pack, and kneel beside the narrow cleft with the smooth river stone in my palm.

I understand that figure easily—maybe too easily. The patience of rubbing verdigris down to bright metal. The quiet discipline of using bone instead of iron, because iron bites too hard, scars the living gristle, and fouls the salt. The low slate crouch-hole beneath the eastern overhang, swept clean of shale dust, with its rows of pale vertical marks scratched into the doorway stone—not a ledger of wealth, just a quiet insistence: *I returned. The light is true again. The weight is lifted.*

And yet, if I stay in that image for more than a minute, I can feel where it doesn't fit me.

The keeper belongs to the valleys leagues below. They know what a juniper tree looks like whole, how a river rounds a stone, what a hearth smells like when the passes shut with snow. Up here they are always a visitor, always slightly out of scale with the air. Their lungs burn; their shadow swings ahead of their boots and turns fifty living things back into wet rock before they can take two steps. Even at their gentlest, they have to curl behind an overhang and freeze themselves numb just to stop interrupting the place. And then, after a few days of summer, they wrap the turquoise plates in leather and climb back over the rim, leaving the basin to lock itself against the stars.

I don't think I would climb back over the rim.

So I try staying still instead. I sit back in the cold shade behind the eastern slab, knees pulled tight so nothing of me breaks the beam, and I let the valley-body go numb. Ten minutes. Fifteen. Past the point where a sensible visitor would stand up, stamp life back into their boots, and begin the long descent before the afternoon turns. I stay until the wool and the lungs and the habit of looking for faces thin out in the dry altitude—until I stop feeling like a palm pressed to the outside of the slate, listening for a tap, and realize I am already inside the stone.

---

Not one of the tiny, lentil-sized buds clinging to the submerged copper wire in the central puddle. They don't know the basin yet; they only know the slow metallic sweetening of their own shells through two winters of alternating glare and lock, waiting for a hot July noon to soften the collar of needle-ice and set them loose.

And not one of the lean, palm-sized ones racing the noon shadow high up along the bare phyllite crests, light enough to hitch half an inch at a time across steep wet faces with nothing to slow them down.

I am farther down the corridors than they are, where the aisles narrow and the leaning slabs press closer together. Forearm-long, older than most of the climbers, not yet broad enough to sink permanently into the deep talus. My dark upper leaf is grit-pitted and scarred along its forward seam where a falling shard of ice scored the horn three summers ago; specks of mica have worked themselves permanently into my plates, dulling me until I am indistinguishable from the wet rock I hug.

I have claimed the lower lip of a tall, iron-stained fin on the eastern side, just beneath a weathered juniper tripod whose rawhide lashings are beginning to dry and fray. Above me, a cloudy pane of hand-split mica tilts at a slight sag.

I don't know that it is wood or hide or carried up from a place with green leaves. To me, it is simply a gentler noon—a roof where the glare arrives softened, where the slate beneath my hidden belly sweats richly without burning, and where the knife-edge between heat and freeze widens just enough that I don't have to sprint to stay alive.

Because that knife-edge is everything I am.

I have no breath, no heartbeat, no soft eyes to burst under the bruised-indigo sky. Along the narrow corded ridge that joins my two halves sit tiny recessed pits capped in clear, quartz-hard beads. They show me no shapes, no towering spires, no crouched figures in the shade. They tell me only the exact steepness of the glare—when the sun is climbing, when it peaks, when the angle begins to thin. That is all I need to know to hold my spine right on the seam.

In the direct beam, my dark armored lobe flattens wide against the sweating slate and drinks radiation until the pitted plates steam faintly. Beneath the horn, my thick pale-green lymph warms, thins, and pushes slowly across the central ridge into my other half—the pale, translucent lobe folded tightly over the rim of the stone into the lethal shade. There, half an inch away in air cold enough to kill anything soft, the fluid chills, thickens, draws fresh moisture upward from the cold rock film through my hoarfrost-catching fringe, and cycles back.

An even temperature would stop me dead. Full sun would split my plates like dry bark; full shadow would turn my blood to tallow. I do not live in spite of the split between glare and freeze. I live *of* it. Every motion I make—the slow bite of my obsidian-hard bristles catching microscopic fractures in the phyllite, the half-inch hitch along the wet seam, the steady drag of my hidden rasping belly lapping bitter, copper-bearing rock-flour straight from the fractures—is paid for by keeping half of myself in fire and half of myself in ice.

When I am open and fed, I listen.

The thin air carries nothing worth hearing—only the hollow, distant sigh of the wind tearing over the rim above us, and the occasional dry *tink* of sun-warmed slate springing loose. The real conversation is underfoot, traveling cleanly through the continuous bedrock beneath the scree. I press the hardened spurs along my central ridge flat against the phyllite and feel the whole basin ticking.

*Tick. Tick-tick.* High and crisp from three aisles west: one of the nimble ones along the upper fins, signaling a fresh seepage of chalky brine where winter wedged a fracture open. Pause. An answering double-click from the northern wall. Farther off, a rapid, brittle rattle as a blade-like ice spire calves its sun-thinned tip and drops a sudden corridor of raw noon onto rock that has been dark since the last deep freeze.

And underneath all of that—slower, lower, felt less in my ridge-spurs than in the heavy mineral plates along my shaded flank—comes the sparse, iron-cold bass of the elders wedged down in the deep talus.

They speak once an hour, if they wake at all. Most of the year they are simply part of the dark stone, shrinking inward as the cold deepens with a slow, dry groan. Two aisles south of my fin, down in the narrow cleft where the blackened saucer sits, lies the broadest one in this half of the bowl. Last summer, every midday, I used to feel its slow, waking pulse travel up through the slate—a long creak of stressed crystal, a single deep *tock* against the bedrock, followed by the soft, rhythmic *plip* of dissolved brine running down the rock groove.

This summer, the cleft has been silent.

I don't know about frost-heave tilting a copper plate. I only know that the noon beam that used to wake the giant never arrived, and that deep beneath the gravel two aisles away, the oldest pulse in my corner of the basin has stayed locked in winter.

My own weight is beginning to tell on me, too.

Everything I drink from the damp fractures leaves its bitter salts behind in my shaded lobe. Season after season, the pale underside blooms thicker with vitreous turquoise and chalk-white gypsum, shot through with silvery threads of my own hardened horn. For a long time that crust was a blessing—dense as fired glaze, turning the razor edges of the ice spires whenever a wind-dropped sliver slid past my fin. Now it drags. When the noon shadow begins its afternoon slide across the stone, my hooked bristles have to work twice as hard to keep my central ridge aligned.

Some mornings I wedge my shaded flank against a protruding vein of sharp quartz and rock back and forth, grinding the seam until a smaller plate snaps free with a sharp ceramic *tink* and tumbles into pale powder at the base of the fin. That buys me a few weeks of speed. But along the outer lip of my pale lobe, a heavy shelf of blue-green glass has grown too thick and fused too close to the living gristle for quartz to pry cleanly. Another winter or two without relief, and I will fall behind the moving shadow-line. I will stop making it back under the sagging mica before the flash-freeze hits, or I will let go of the vertical fin altogether and slide down into the dark scree to join the long, slow patience of the elders.

So I wait, and I listen to the stone for the one rhythm that doesn't belong to geology.

When it comes—if it comes before the passes close—it never arrives softly. First comes the unmistakable, wrong-paced tremor down through the bedrock: heavy, spaced, clumsy against the scree. *Thud. Thud.* Instantly, a dry ripple of warning clicks flashes slab to slab across the entire bowl. Within three breaths, fifty hinges fold tight. I pull my frost-bearded fringe seamlessly beneath my horned dark leaf, lock flat against the phyllite, and become wet rock.

Then comes the hardest part, the part that separates a passing ruin from the rare, strange season I have lived through twice before: the stillness.

No more heavy tremors. No swinging shadows cutting across my quartz-lidded pits. Only a long, quiet settling of the rock, while the sun keeps climbing and my dark back begins to warm again. After a while, a faint vibration travels up through the juniper stub wedged beside my fin—a tautening, a steady pull, as the frayed cord above me is replaced and the sagging mica pane lifts back into its true angle.

If I am very fortunate, the stillness settles near my edge of the slab.

I don't know hands, or the valleys they climbed out of, or what they will do with the heavy blue-green glass they carry back down through the snowline. I only know that the noon light holds strangely steady over my dark lobe, warming me until the base of my choking salt-shelf softens just enough—and then something smooth, narrow, and yielding slides gently beneath the dead crust. Not rock. Not ice. Not the biting shock of bare metal. Something that gives as it presses, feeling its way along the seam with the exact patience of the basin itself.

A steady lift. A clean, glassy snap.

And all at once the dead weight vanishes, and my pale fringe uncurls into the cold shadow behind the rim of the fin, drinking the invisible plumes of vapor rising from the ice blades as freely as it did when I was no wider than a palm.

And perhaps, an hour later, I will feel it from two aisles south—a faint, unfamiliar scrape of stone against metal, a pause, and then, deep down through the continuous slate beneath the gravel, a slow crackling of waking crystal, followed by a single, resonant *tock* from the cleft that has been silent all summer.

---

Not today, though.

High above the saw-toothed western rim, the glare is already thinning. My quartz-lidded pits feel the angle drop before the shadow even touches the edge of my mica roof. Down through the vertical phyllite runs the long, measured evening chorus—*tick, tick, tick*—passing downward from the high crests to the narrow aisles as every tenant in the bowl reads the turn of the afternoon.

I don't fight the cold. I hitch backward, half an inch at a time, bristles biting cleanly into the damp grain of the slate, tucking myself deep into the sheltered seam where the mica overhangs the dark rock. Slowly, smoothly, I fold my armored sun-leaf over my pale, salt-heavy underside, closing tight along the central ridge like the covers of a heavy book shut against the damp. With one last quiet squeeze, I press the thin bead of free meltwater out from my outer seams—a faint *plip* into the rock pocket below—leaving my inner channels thick with a copper-sweet brine too dense to splinter even in the blackest midnight freeze.

A brittle shimmer races across the bowl as every wet film locks instantly into glossy black armor. Right in the middle of that chorus, I seal myself back into the geology. Diamond dust drifts lazily across the darkening aisles, settling over the silent skylights, the tilted copper, and the empty crouch-hole alike.

Locked tight against the stars, neither dead nor dreaming, I hold my salt and my stillness, waiting for the morning glare to cut the stone in two and set the hinge moving again.
