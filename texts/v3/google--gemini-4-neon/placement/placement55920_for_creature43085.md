I would not find myself by looking. In a place where the light only reaches the floor in bruised amber puddles, eyes are the last thing to learn where you belong. I would find myself by listening—and then, as the roaring walls and the heavy iron chord began to wear the edges off my hearing, by putting a palm flat against a warm pipe and realizing I could feel the whole hollow thinking through the metal.

At first, wandering the springy walkways between the towers, I would test the shapes already cut into this world and find that I only half-fit them.

I would watch the flush-runners crouching in the mill mouths with their wicker creels and hooked gaffs, and feel a quiet, grateful awe—and know instantly I am not made of that kind of nerve. When the shift-lamps dip to ember-red and the forty-gallon bladders tear open eighty yards overhead, my instinct is not to vault into the boiling dark. It is to step back under the eaves, pull the shutter to a narrow slit, and count the seconds until the rhythm steadies again.

Nor am I a footing-warden, though I understand their patience. I like the thought of coaxing a pale woody knuckle around a flywheel pit with bitter sludge and a curved knife rather than fighting it; still, my hands are not broad enough for the heavy wedges, and the wet crawlspaces under the boiler piers feel too close to the stone nobody speaks of. And up on the top gantries, where the bladder-riggers lean out over eighty yards of musky air with twenty-foot lances, the sheer open drop would turn my stomach to water.

No—my feet would carry me toward the copper. Toward the winders, first, because there is something deeply familiar in the way they work: unwinding a burned-out coil by hand, scraping the wire clean, varnishing it with boiled root-sap, laying it back down turn by turn without rushing, treating the violet-veined polyps cemented inside the live housing not as rot to be scoured out, but as living gauges that tell you whether the current is healthy.

And yet, even in the quiet switch-houses, I would find myself drifting toward the walls, leaning an elbow against the insulated conduits, following the sound of a distant metallic *clack-clack, pause, clack* as it tried to cross the cavern from Block Six to Block Two and died half-way across in the burlap wrappings and the damp root-clamps.

That is where I would finally stop moving.

Halfway up the iron flank of Block Three, tucked directly under the underside of the high catwalk junction where the main steam headers, balance-leads, and braided power cables from all six towers pinch together like tendons at a wrist, there is a narrow, bolted-on blister of corrugated iron and caulked timber. It is no larger than a ship’s chartroom—just big enough for a three-legged stool, a worn root-wood bench, a low bunk wedged under the pipes, and one person who does not mind living inside the knot of the web.

I don’t think the settlement planned for anyone to live in the crossing-box. It probably began as a maintenance locker for the switch-house opposite, a dry place to keep spare carbon brushes and tins of tallow. But the hollow is three hundred paces end to end, and across that wide, roaring dark, iron loses its voice. A pipe-tap struck on the far side of Block One gets chewed thin by expansion joints, swallowed by the wet paper-roar of the wall-pelts, and drowned altogether whenever a bellows-wing dives past the railings booming *thup-thup-thup*.

So someone started sitting where the pipes kiss. And over the shifts, that someone became me.

Call me a junction-hand, or a line-reader, or just the quiet one in Three’s blister. Most of the ward doesn’t bother with a title; they just know that if you tap three quick strikes and a long pause onto the main steam return from anywhere in the six towers, somebody in the middle of the web will catch it before the burlap eats it, clean the message up, and strike it fresh and bright onto the far rails.

My tools are small things. A brass-headed tapping-pin polished pale at the grip by my own thumb. A saucer of damp floor-moss beside my elbow that answers with a slow, bruised-green glow whenever I press a fingertip into it to check my chalk tallies. A hollowed knuckle of root-wood hung on a leather thong against my breastbone, kept wet with sap-water so the plump violet polyp curled inside stays slick and alive. Before I ever lay bare fingers on a doubtful balance-lead, I press the cup to the casing and wait for that tiny, unmistakable sixty-cycle shiver against my collarbone.

After enough shifts in the box, though, the polyp stops being merely a safety check. And the tapping stops being merely errands.

That is how you become more yourself in a sealed place: you stay still long enough for the patterns to begin settling on you like verdigris on damp copper.

On the warm iron plate behind my bench—where the heavy braided feeds from the six dynamos pass through ceramic bushings—I have let a whole garden of colonial polyps bloom undisturbed. I scrape them back only when their crusty green shells creep too close to a terminal. The rest of the time I mist them twice a watch with a rag dipped in warm condensate. In the dim amber spill of my single caged filament bulb, they pulse together like a slow purple lung. When Block Four’s stamping presses cycle up hard and draw heavy load, the bloom along the fourth bushing tightens visibly, its hum pitching up a hairline; when a dynamo’s carbon brushes are wearing ragged and spitting blue arcs down in Block Two, the colony on the second line twitches in tiny, uneven skips.

And strung along the overhead steam manifold—where the pipes from each tower clamp to the same iron header—I have hung my own strange instrument: six thin hairs of salvaged copper wire, each holding a little polished scrap of *crop-brass* begged from the foundry-hands after a burrower-butchering. A worn valve-nut for Block One. A sheared rivet for Block Two. A bright, gizzard-tumbled bushing for Block Three, and so on down the line.

There is no wind in my shuttered blister, only the warm, trembling close air of an engine room. Yet those six brass scraps are never still. Each hangs tuned to the iron frame of its own tower, catching the steady bass tremor that travels along the conduits. In a normal Vent, they blur softly in the amber light, humming in a matched, oily chord I barely have to look at. It is when one of them changes its dance that my hand reaches for the tapping-pin. If the sheared rivet for Block Two begins to chatter sideways against the valve-nut beside it, I know a main belt is slipping or a bearing is running dry long before the smoke smells sour. I tap the warning down into the turbine pit; a minute later, a footing-warden or an elder taps back two crisp clicks—*heard, checking*—and the rivet settles back into its smooth blur.

I am somewhere in the middle years of a hollow-dweller’s ears. Not yet stone-deaf like the prized elders who walk the mill floors barefoot and lay their jawbones against a hot boiler to diagnose a cracking flywheel hub—though I am halfway there. The high, brittle clicks of the spindled climbers sprinting along my outside cable-braids have begun to fade into a faint dry ticking, like ash hitting iron. Even the quarter-hour shriek of the central accumulator stack, twenty yards off my shutter, no longer stabs my skull the way it did when I was young; it arrives now as a broad, silver weight behind my eyes and a clean shudder through the soles of my brass-pegged boots.

Being half-deaf is a strange gift in the crossing-box. It leaves me with one foot in air-sound and one foot in iron. When a young runner climbs up from the lower decks between Blows to trade a roasted fruiting-knuckle for a dry rag and a cup of sap-tea, I can still drop into the tight, rapid tenement voice, pitching my words into the narrow acoustic slot between the walls and the wings. When a winder waves across the dim gulf from the transformer gantry opposite, I smear pale grease-chalk across my fingertips and sign back in broad, crisp strokes through the open shutter.

Mostly, though, I know the four hundred souls of this ward by the weight of their knuckles on steel.

No two people tap the same. After a hundred watches, the iron stops carrying mere code and starts carrying faces. The head footing-warden down in Block Six has a heavy, deliberate hand—each strike lands like a hammer setting a wedge, with a tiny drag on the last click where his thumb stiffens from damp. The riggers up near the ceiling ribs tap fast, clipped, impatient bursts, one hand clinging to a swaying rail while the other warns a workshop deck to clear out before they haul a tallow-pale bladder clear of the roof. The children learning pipe-talk on the tenement balconies strike too lightly, uneven and bouncy, giggling as their mothers snatch the pins away so they don’t tangle a shift-call.

And late in the quiet Vents, when the presses settle into their deepest groove and most of the ward is curled into narrow bunks beside dying saucers of bruised-green moss, a different kind of traffic rides the warm steam-mains.

Soft, hesitant tapping. Half-speed, tapped with the flat of a knuckle or a wooden knot instead of brass, meant to travel only from one shuttered balcony to the next along a shared radiator pipe—private jokes, quiet complaints about a sore knee, a pair of young mechanics leaning their elbows on the same iron span across two towers, trading small coded absurdities in the dark. Sometimes a message from Block One is struck just a little too shyly to survive the damp clamps on its way to a bunk in Block Five. I am not supposed to listen to those. I never sign a word about them in the daylight. I only sit in my dim blister with my heels braced on the warm manifold, catch the faint, dying rhythm as it arrives, and gently—just barely loud enough to reach the far side—tap it onward, keeping the exact slight hesitation in the second pause so it still sounds like the hand that sent it.

Every quarter-hour, the whole world tightens around my small room.

The caged bulb over my bench dips from tired amber to a dull ember-red. Down in the mills, the heavy reciprocating presses draw in their breath and cycle up. My purple polyp-garden tightens all at once; the six brass scraps overhead shiver into a sharp, urgent buzz. I latch the timber shutter down to a finger’s width and press my damp moss-plugs firmer into my ears.

Then the accumulator valve tears open.

The shriek turns the air outside to solid white steam. Three seconds later, the heat-surge hits the ceiling canopy and a pendulous water-bladder lets go right above Block Three’s catwalk. Forty gallons of warm condensate slam onto the corrugated iron roof directly over my skull—a sudden, violent drumroll that shakes dust and pale flakes of verdigris down onto my bench. Outside the slit, the dark erupts: sheets of glistening wall-grazers sluicing off the eaves, a pack of six-ribbed climbers scattering frantically across my insulated braids with sharp clicking snarls, and then the massive, coat-wide shadow of a blind bellows-wing banking right past my shutter, its accordion chest snapping shut with a *thup-thup-thup* so close and wet it rattles the tin cup on my table.

Through the three-minute storm, the crossing-box becomes a storm-lantern in a gale. Signals pour in from everywhere at once—a rigger snapping *two more tipping over Five’s plaza*; a lower mill deck ringing *runner down, twisted ankle by Pier Four, bring a sling*; a switch-house flashing *dynamo three surging, trim the load*. My hands move almost without me, brass pin flashing from header to header, translating, routing, steadying, my heel feeling the floor’s rebound while the polyp at my throat hums like a trapped bee.

Then the relief valve clangs shut. The filament bulb brightens back to warm amber. The roof stops drumming; the root-floor far below drinks the oil-filmed puddles dry. The brass weights over my head slow their frantic spin and settle back into their oiled, matching blur. I unlatch the shutter to let the fresh steam-damp, yeasty air cool my wrists, wipe a smear of tallow from my cheek, and press my thumb into the moss-saucer just to watch the gentle green light wake and fade.

If you asked me what I am, I would say I am nothing grand. I don’t stamp the peat-cake that feeds the fires. I don’t lure the water off the roofs, or mend the roots, or haul meat out of the Flush. If the settlement is an organ keeping the living vault warm and breathing, and the people are its stubborn nervous system, then I am simply one small ganglion tucked into the iron ribs—a knot where the threads cross so the left hand knows when the right hand is burning, and so six tall, sparse towers don’t forget in the gloom that they belong to the same body.

Because I know what it feels like when they almost do.

Once every few years, a main drive-belt parts with a gunshot snap, or a neglected polyp-crust bridges a heavy breaker, and the Cold Stop drops onto a wing of the hollow.

When it happens, it hits my crossing-box first.

One moment the six brass scraps are humming their steady chord, the bench is warm under my forearms, and the violet colonies behind the louver are pulsing in clean, even time. The next, there is a hard, ragged wrench in the manifold. Two of the brass pins go dead still, hanging like lead plumbs. The bulb dies to black. The presses hang mid-stroke, and a terrible, ragged hole tears open in the noise.

In that hole, the roaring wall-pelts falter. The air seems to lose its warmth in a single heartbeat. Against my sternum, the plump polyp inside its wooden cup goes suddenly, horribly slack. And through the sudden silence of the pipes, something vast and dry and impossibly quiet presses in from beyond the curved root-walls—the weight of the miles of dead stone nobody has ever seen, waiting patiently for the pulse to stop for good.

In my dark blister, I have nothing left to relay. A knot with no thread pulling through it is nothing at all. I sit completely still, both palms flat against the cooling header, my brass pin raised an inch above the iron, holding my breath alongside four hundred people in six shuttered towers, waiting in the bruised-green dark of our moss-saucers for the chord to return.

Then—far off in a switch-house, a heavy backup breaker slams home.

A spitting blue commutator-arc paints the edges of my shutter-slit. The dynamos catch with a low, climbing whine that crawls straight up through the soles of my boots into my teeth. Against my chest, the little violet creature tightens and begins to shiver again, sixty cycles a second. Overhead, the two dead brass pins twitch, swing once, and fall back into their steady blur.

Before the filament bulb has even warmed all the way back to amber, a faint, shaky tap arrives along the steam-main from Block One—just two short clicks, asking the dark if anyone is still there.

I let out my breath. I bring the brass pin down cleanly on every header in turn, crisp and bright enough to carry clear across the open plazas, clear through the rising roar of the walls:

*Clack-clack. Pause. Clack.*

*Holding. All lines live. Keep turning.*

Then I set the pin down on the bench, mist the violet polyps with a damp rag, and settle back into the hum—mending the gaps, carrying the voices, leaning gladly into the warm, deafening dark that keeps us all alive.
