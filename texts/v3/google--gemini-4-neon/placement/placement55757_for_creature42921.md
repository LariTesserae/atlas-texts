I would not stay up on the blinding causeways. After an hour under that white dome—where the steel glare comes off the grating like cold water off a mirror and a row of silos bends its shoulders away from you—my eyes would already be aching for a roof, even a thin corrugated one. And I would not live down at the bottommost grates, either, where the trusses drop out into the inverted sky and every ladder rung feels like the last solid thing before falling upward.

I would find myself two tiers down, in the chalky twilight just beneath the first pitched roofs, where the downward glare and the pale glow leaking up from the flooded light-wells meet and cancel each other into an even, directionless grey.

Specifically, I would settle into a narrow five-cornered alcove behind an idle turbine casing—a pocket too cool for a family’s sleeping-curtains, too far from a busbar gap to cook in, and ringed on three sides by dead-end plumbing that nobody in the upper households bothers to map. Two dry copper junction-boxes bolted to the masonry, a capped oil-sump breather, and a bank of four brass manifold valves whose pipes vanish into the precast concrete and come out nowhere anyone has ever found.

Nobody uses those lines for calling down from the condenser-courts because their delays are ungovernable. Speak a dry word into the copper box on the north wall of the switchgear yard above, and it will not arrive in seven minutes. It might drop out of my brass valve three afternoons later, or wait until the next long cool when the indicator lamps turn emerald, or vanish into the sub-decks for half a year before clearing its throat beside my knee.

It is a junk-drawer of misrouted air. That is why I would sit there.

---

At first I might think I came down there only for the quiet, or to get out of the biting sharpness of the new steel. Everything in the basin is so exhaustingly crisp—every flange freshly sheared, every bolt head dimpled with cold dew, every seam lined with brittle ridges of mortar squeeze-out that look as though the formwork came off before breakfast. You can walk three kilometers across the terraced bowl and never touch a single thing that has yielded to a hand.

So while I sit in my alcove, waiting out the slow slide of the iron shadows through the grating overhead, my hands would find the only work that makes sense against all that unweathered perfection: taking the edge off.

Nothing grand. You cannot blunt a valley. You can only blunt what touches a mouth, a palm, or a flank.

From the upper substations and the broken corners of porcelain insulator racks, people bring me shards—white, glazed, fractured along curves sharp enough to split a thumb to the bone. From the precast concrete seams around my alcove, I snap off narrow strips of dry mortar squeeze-out and crush them between two heavy brass valve-caps into a fine, pale grit. I mix that grit with a spoonful of roof condensate and a pinch of silt skimmed from an idle ore-hopper, spread the paste across a flat concrete sill, and take a porcelain chip between my thumb and forefinger.

Then I work it in slow figure-eights.

An hour passes before the razor-gloss along one fractured rim dulls to a faint, chalky bevel. An afternoon passes before the corner stops catching the skin of my pad. A week—measured not in days, which do not exist here, but in how many times the bright porcelain-patch cast from the insulator bank two tiers up crosses my wool knee and climbs the brick behind me—before the shard has forgotten it was ever broken, and lies in my palm as an oval white pebble no larger than a fingernail, smooth enough to rest under a child’s tongue for the next forty years without chafing.

That is what I would be: a blunter of small things.

I would take the burr off the rim of a newly cut blackened slate wrist-dish so it doesn’t draw a silver hairline across a walker’s forearm. I would round the brass teeth of a wool-rake until a browser ewe leans into the combing instead of flinching. When someone scavenges a clean length of copper pipe and beats one end flat into a ladle, they climb down the ladder to my five-cornered pocket and leave it beside my silt-bowl; two cycles of the press hall later, they come back to find its lip dulled and satin-soft, aged artificially by fifty years of patient thumb-pressure so it will not scrape the glaze off their cooking-pots.

My fingertips would be the strangest part of me. Everyone in the basin carries fine silver scars across their knuckles and shins, neat signatures of diamond-plate and zinc. Mine would have those too, along the backs of my hands, while the pads of my thumbs and forefingers would be worn almost glassy-smooth from years of wet mortar-grit and porcelain, forever slightly reddened by the cold water, the skin thickened until even a fresh sheet of galvanized iron feels slightly polite to them.

And on the third brass valve beside my shoulder—the one I lean against when my back stiffens—the pale factory film of grease would be completely gone. In its place, a hand-sized oval of warm, dull, tarnished metal. In a valley where nothing oxidizes and nothing wears down, that small, finger-rubbed smear on an otherwise factory-fresh manifold would be my proudest possession. It would prove I had actually been somewhere.

---

Because my work makes almost no sound—only the faint, wet hiss of porcelain turning in damp grit, loyal to the inch of concrete beneath my wrist—I would hear everything the pipes drop.

At first, when a *late word* slips out of the capped breather or the dry junction-box behind me, I would still pause too sharply, spilling a drop of grey slurry onto my trousers. After a year or two, I would settle into the basin’s tempo and hardly lift my head.

Most of what arrives down there is small and orphaned. A dry, rattling cough that someone cleared from their throat three coolings ago before calling a brother up from the cisterns. The brittle *tick-tick* of a porcelain signal-tap that missed its receiver by two terraces and wandered the conduit for a month. Half a name—*—rel*, or *Sa—*—the damp vowels lost back at the speaker’s lips, only the dry consonants surviving the long dark circuit through the copper.

Sometimes, though, whole sentences arrive.

Once, while I am polishing the inner curve of a bone awl with a strip of tallow-rubbed browser hide, the brass valve beside my elbow draws in nothing at all and exhales a thin, furious, unmoistened wail. A newborn’s first cry. High, sharp, completely dry—the sound that left some infant’s silently heaving chest in a sheltered transformer-room six days or six weeks ago, before the midwife’s wet finger touched its lips and coaxed its voice home to its body. By the time the cry finishes its circuit and drops into my cold alcove, that child is already fat on boiled sedge-broth, sleeping inside a stitched wool sling two tiers up, its breathing damp and loyal to its throat.

Still, I set the awl down on the concrete sill. I roll the worn white pebble under my own tongue, wet my lips, and lean an inch from the brass lip of the valve. Softly—so softly the sound stays entirely inside the three inches of air between us—I make three small, wet clicking noises, the way the midwives do against a newborn’s cheek.

Just so the cry has somewhere to land. Just so it isn’t left hanging in a room full of cold iron.

Other times, the voice that drifts out of the brick shed wall behind me belongs to someone who has already gone onto the high outward conveyor belts, or been lowered hand-over-hand on braided wire-rushes into the chalky sky beneath the grates. Elders grow so dry at the end; their last remarks drift loose from their mouths and take the long way around the valley. When a great-grandmother’s slow, unhurried instruction—*check the fifth corner before the iron shadow clears the seam*—comes out of my capped sump-pipe a season after her wool bundle slipped cleanly into the unbroken concrete wall, I do not feel haunted. I rest my damp palm on the bare, grease-free patch of brass, listen until the silence closes cleanly over the last syllable, and murmur a single wet syllable back into the quiet.

*Heard.*

---

I would not be a hermit, though I might look like one to a stranger peering down through the grating overhead. In the basin, nobody lives far from the herds or the busbar kitchens for long; the cold gets into the joints too easily, and your depth perception starts to swim if you stare only at five-cornered concrete for days at a time.

When the concrete shadow beneath the overhang pulls back to the third rivet-row, I rinse the mortar grit from my fingers, stopper my brass flask, and climb the ladder up into the white glare.

Before stepping onto the upper deck, I pull down my slotted vulcanized-hide shades and unstop my flask over the blackened slate dish strapped to my left forearm. Even after decades of living two ladders away, I never trust the switchgear yard above my alcove without looking into the water first. Today, the meniscus settles and shows an iron stanchion rising squarely in the middle of the walkway where the air overhead stands empty and bright. I step three paces left, cleanly around the reflected post, and feel the slight, familiar tug in my gut where the perspective bows outward as I cross toward the transformer alleys.

If a small herd of rubber-footed browsers is picking its way along the corrugated pitch beside the rail, scraping pale geometric lichen off the weld seams, I walk close to the nearest ewe and lay my bare palm lightly against the dense wool over her ribs. Her hindquarters drift half a step out of alignment as she rounds a boiler casing and then settle smoothly back behind her shoulders, and because my hand stays against her warmth, my own boots find the kinked turn without stumbling.

Up in the warm seams between the humming transformers, where the air smells of ozone and steamed sedge-root, I trade. I drop two freshly smoothed speech-pebbles into a grandmother’s cupped palm—she tests one immediately against the tip of her tongue, nodding her heavy-lidded eyes when it catches on nothing—and she ladles a thick, pale porridge of roots and roasted porcelain-beetle marrow into my copper cup from the ceramic pot hanging inside the severed busbar gap. We sit on plaited wire-rush mats, leaning close so our wet murmurs drop cleanly between our knees, sounding to anyone passing along the catwalk like nothing more than drops plinking into a galvanized tray.

While we eat, I tell her about the conduit-scavenger that poked its whiskered snout straight through the glass dial of my pressure-gauge two afternoons ago—no crack, no ripple, just velvet fur pulling out of solid glass to sniff my bowl of mortar slurry—with a tiny flag of lichen-dyed sage wool knotted loosely around the base of its tail.

She stops her spoon halfway to her mouth and lets out a soft, wet click of satisfaction. That was her nephew’s thread, knotted three terraces east, trying to learn where a dry drain-pipe beside their sleeping-curtains actually emptied. Now he knows where his late words go when he speaks too quickly near that wall.

And whenever the glass-walled press hall begins its slow, forty-minute descent, I walk over to stand in the quiet line along the outer catwalk. Not every cycle—I don’t need much—just often enough to keep a small dark clay-glazed flask of press-water stoppered at my belt. Watching that enormous silent die lift off the empty steel bed, watching the thin square sheet of cold fresh water hold its impossible sharp edges for three breaths before the harvesters’ slate trays slide cleanly underneath it, is the one moment in the entire valley when sharpness feels like a mercy rather than a wound.

I carry a spoonful of that honest water back down to my alcove. Not for drinking, most of the time, though I take a sip when the bowed sightlines upstairs have left my eyes doubling. Mostly I keep it for a small private habit: once in a long while, after hours of looking at my blackened wrist-dish and the mismatched shadows of the adjacent valley, I pour ten drops of press-water into a smoothed saucer and look down just to see my own actual face, my own actual ceiling, the exact brass manifold behind my shoulder—nothing missing, nothing not yet built. Just to remind my eyes where my body is standing.

---

Outside my alcove, past the fifth corner of the service alley, lies one of the pale concrete light-wells flooded with an inch of clear condensate.

Because the sub-level light rises cleanly through the floor beneath it, the water there barely looks wet; it looks like a square of sky laid flat on concrete. A dozen chalk-white, flat-faced birds usually roost along its rim, facing inward, their translucent eyelids rippling shut and open in a slow wave from left to right.

Sometimes, when my hands are stiff from rubbing slate and no late words have arrived for a long stretch, I kneel on the wet concrete beside the birds—not too close, keeping their tempo—and look down into the inch of water.

In the reflection, the alcove behind me is almost identical, except that in the water the bank of brass manifolds has five valves instead of four, and a narrow footbridge I have never seen arches cleanly out from my walkway into the bright haze, connecting my tier to a cluster of silos that overhead sits three hundred yards out of reach.

I do not step onto it. I know the rules. You do not argue with the adjacent valley, and you do not hurry toward it.

Still, I think about how long a body stays soft here, and how eventually even the softest body begins to learn the valley’s accommodations.

I can feel, already, how I would grow old in that five-cornered pocket. First my hands would slow from figure-eights to single long strokes, matching the twenty-minute turn of the great spoked flywheels up on the causeways. Then I would find myself sitting beside the birds along the light-well for four hours without shivering, my own eyelids lowering a beat behind theirs, my heartbeat spacing itself down toward the rhythm of heavy beads letting go from an overhead pipe.

One afternoon, stepping out onto the bright grating to check a newly extruded bay of walkway, I would glance down and see that my shadow no longer agrees with itself: my boots dropping a pale grey pool straight beneath my heels like poured concrete, while the shadow of my shoulders leans cleanly eastward like galvanized iron.

And my voice—even with my oldest, thinnest porcelain pebble tucked beneath my tongue, worn down over forty years until it is almost translucent—would begin to dry at the edges. I would murmur a word to someone coming down the ladder with a chipped ladle, feel my lips shape it in complete clinical hush, and know that it had slipped loose from me and entered the copper, headed off on some immense, untraceable loop through the terraces.

I would not mind that.

I have spent a whole lifetime sitting beside capped brass valves, catching the stray breaths of people who spoke too dryly and died too soon, making sure their sentences were received. It feels right that, at the end—after the household washes my smooth-padded hands in press-water, wraps me in my most heavily patched browser-wool coat, and lays me along the dark rubber track of an upper outward conveyor belt—some stray fragment of my own voice might still be traveling through the walls.

Maybe two years later, someone else will have taken over the five-cornered pocket behind the turbine casing. Someone with fresh hairline scars on their knuckles and mortar-grit on their thumbs, leaning their shoulder against the dull, bare oval I wore into the third brass valve.

They will be rounding the sharp corner off a broken shard of white porcelain, working slowly through the chalky twilight, when a single dry syllable—nothing important, perhaps just a quiet reminder to keep the grit wet, or a soft cough from a cold afternoon—slips cleanly out of the pipe beside their knee.

They will pause with the wet stone between their fingers, wait until the hush settles back over the metal, and murmur a small damp sound toward the brass, letting me know I arrived.

And maybe, if they kneel later beside the flooded light-well to rinse their hands, they will glance down past the roosting white birds and catch sight of a stooped figure in heavily mended wool walking smoothly across the footbridge that isn't there—palms empty of silt, stride completely level, untouched by the kinked geometry of the decks—pausing for a moment at the reflected rail to press a worn, smooth thumb briefly against the sharp steel before walking steadily on toward the bright, impossible horizon.
