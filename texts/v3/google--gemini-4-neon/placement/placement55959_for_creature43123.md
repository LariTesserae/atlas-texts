If I let myself sink into this basin without fighting the cold or clutching at the surface light, I find I do not stop near the rim, where the brinicles crack and the younger ones climb the rusted scaffolding with their oak poles. Nor do I drift all the way down to the piers of the central press-hall, where the eldest have already traded their knees for masonry.

I settle in the middle altitude.

It is an arcade halfway down the terraced wall, where a stone aqueduct pierces the brick flank of an old foundry and the alley narrows until two outstretched forearms could almost bridge it. Above my head, braided lead cables sag in thick catenaries, and a bare copper busbar runs the length of the vaulted stonework, humming its quiet, continuous electrolysis into the brine. Half a story up, behind iron doors whose latches dissolved decades ago, sits a flooded switch-house. At the north end of the arcade, beneath a low iron awning, a pocket of ancient gas lies pressed flat against the masonry. At the south end, four cobblestone lanes meet in a sunken intersection where the iron-rich silt collects deep.

It takes a while to realize I am not visiting this corner. I am already wedged beneath its brick corbel, neutrally weighted, my back pressed into the soft fur of manganese mud, letting the faint convective plume from the switch-house wall drift upward across my jaw.

What leaves me first, settling into this body, is sight—and with it, a kind of frantic, skimming impatience I didn't know I was carrying. Where eyes would be, there are only twin recessed pads of clouded cartilage, smooth and cool under the heavy salt water. I do not miss the world of outlines and surfaces. Instead, the ward opens outward as a web of gradients: the sharp, metallic taste of dissolved copper pooling beneath the busbar; the soft, oily warmth rising a fraction of a degree off a submerged transformer two alleys over; the faint, rhythmic pressure-push of the storm-shutters hinging open six stories up. My heart turns over four times a minute, thick and unhurried, pushing cold, pale fluid through a body packed dense with insulating oil. Nothing here demands a sudden movement. Nothing rewards one.

I am not young. I can feel my age in the mineral seams across my shoulders and down the outer curve of my left shin, where the laminated bands of copper-green and tarnished bronze have grown broad enough to murmur against one another when I unfold an arm. Fine tufts of black sea-moss have rooted along the ridge of my collarbone, combing patiently one way and then the other every six hours when the buried sluices reverse the tide. My knees still bend—I can still pull myself along an overhead truss or step cleanly down a flooded stair without raising a cloud of flocculent—but I have already begun to drift out of step with the brisker routines of the basin. The twelve-minute jerk of the galvanizing conveyor feels faintly hurried to me now. I keep better time with the hourly pulse of the municipal clockwork, and better still with the long, lazy breath of the shutters.

That is how I became a keeper of the switch-house and the awning.

It is not a formal office; nothing in the ward has titles. It is simply what happens when a body slows to the cadence of a particular junction and begins to notice what faster passersby miss.

Inside the switch-house above the arcade, four banks of submerged relays still trip behind thick, algae-furred sight glasses. I spend long stretches crouched on the stone sill inside the doorway, my broad, flat palms—tipped in thick pads of dark, non-conductive horn—laid lightly against the cast-iron housings. When a relay fires, I do not see the bruised violet flash; I feel it first as a brief, flat pressure against the recessed cartilage of my brow, then as a sharp ionized prickle across the pale, drifting filaments fringing my jaw, and finally as a dry, muffled *snap* traveling straight through the bones of my wrists into my sternum.

For the last three thickenings of the shelf-ice overhead, the fourth bank—the lowest one, half-buried in velvet silt—has been drifting.

It used to answer the buried grid in clean counterpoint to the press-hall flywheel. Now it hesitates. Sometimes by a quarter of a turn, sometimes by half, its discharge landing slightly thinner against my brow-pads, leaving behind a sluggish smear of warmth where there used to be a crisp spark. Nothing can be done to repair a sealed iron casing drowned before anyone living remembers. Yet in a place where decay moves at the speed of geology, a drifting relay is an event. It alters the faint current along the busbar outside; it shifts the pace at which pale salt-glass needles sprout and shed from the nearby valve wheels; it changes, by a hair's breadth, the electrical grammar of three adjoining lanes.

Someone should keep track of it. Someone should fold that slow hesitation into the ward's sense of itself.

So I listen, and I tend what hangs beneath the busbar.

Every few cycles of the shutters, I drift out beneath the iron awning at the north end of the arcade to check the inverted porcelain insulators suspended on braided lead wire just below the trapped gas pocket. The busbar beneath them bleeds its continuous, tiny threads of hydrogen upward through the cold brine. Left to themselves, the bubbles wander into mortar cracks or catch on sagging conduit brackets and go nowhere; over decades, a neglected gas-pocket shrinks, its ceiling pulling back until the dry mortar drowns. With slow, measured adjustments of my horn-padded fingers, I angle the porcelain bells back into the rising shimmer, nudging away loose flocculent so every pearl of gas funnels cleanly upward into the trembling skin above.

When I am finished, I hook my forearms over the rusted lintel and push just my crown, brow, and shoulders up through the meniscus.

It is always startling, even after decades—the sudden vanishing of weight, the sharp line of the water parting across my collarbones, the thin, dry, metallic-tasting cold of the trapped gas against my bare throat. Here, my facial filaments cling flat against my jaw, heavy without the brine to float them. I cannot breathe this pocket; my pleated lappets stay folded tight beneath the mineral mantle of my ribs, waiting for the water below. I come up only for what stays dry, and for what can ring.

Tucked into a high mortar crevice well above the waterline is a small cache I have kept and added to over the years: two untouched polyhedra from the galvanizing pit, never harnessed to iron or zinc, their facets kept razor-sharp as a reminder of how clean a crystal begins; a strip of tin folded flat; a cracked sight-glass lens tumbled smooth as river stone; and a small coiled spring salvaged from a rusted relay box, coated in a thick smear of heavy transformer oil so the salt never got its teeth into it. None of these things are useful. Their usefulness is that they do not soften, do not blur into bulbous silhouettes, do not surrender entirely to the slow truce of the brine.

Sometimes I am alone beneath the awning, and I sink back down after a few minutes without making a sound.

Other times, the meniscus parts beside me, and another dark, plated crown rises into the shallow dark—a brinicle-pruner resting between shifts on the upper rim, or a pair carrying a pale, smooth-backed young one anchored tight behind a collarbone, its small hands hooked into the braided straps of its parent's galvanic harness. They lift the child just clear of the water so the dry gas touches its broad brow and the earliest tender buds along its jaw.

Then we press our throats against the cold brick of the vault and hum.

It is not speech. In this small, unmuffled space where the silt cannot swallow vibration, the note leaves my chest as a long, dry resonance tuned to the sub-audible thrum of the tidal engine far beneath the cobbles. The other joins beside me, and because I spend my hours with the fourth relay—with the slight, creeping lag of a drowned switch-house—my pitch naturally sits a fraction of a tone off dead center. Our two notes meet across the flat skin of the water and begin to beat: a slow, rolling swell and trough that ripples the meniscus in microscopic rings and travels right through the jawbone into the skull. Not unison, which belongs to clocks, but two heavy flywheels drifting briefly into phase and out again. The young one holds utterly still between us, learning through its bones that the ward is neither silent nor rigid, that endurance is a matter of adjusting your note by a hair's breadth as the walls settle.

When the dry chill grows too sharp, we slip back beneath the mirror, trailing bright beads of trapped gas from our shoulders, and part without gesture.

My other correspondence happens down on the cobbles, in the sunken four-way intersection at the south end of the arcade.

An hour passes quickly here—scarcely fifteen turns of the press-hall flywheel—and ten minutes before the municipal clockwork pulses, I drop silently from the arcade rail, letting the dense water take my weight down to the stones. At my hip hangs a mesh pouch woven from copper wire and cured leather. Inside are my alphabets: fist-sized lumps of ferrous slag, bent iron pins, short loops of salvaged wire, fragments of spent zinc plate.

I crouch in the dark intersection, feeling the faint gathering tension in the flagstones beneath my knees as the buried engine winds toward the hour. I set a chunk of slag two hand-spans north of the center, and a bent copper pin angled toward the switch-house stairs. Then I step back into the shadow of the brick pier and wait.

The pulse arrives not as sound, but as a clean, wide bloom across every filament on my face. Down across the cobbles, the iron-rich silt lifts a whisper from the stone and settles instantly into fine concentric rings and spoked radials—except where my scrap catches the flux and pulls one spoke sharply inward, doubling an arc around the copper pin.

Anyone passing through the intersection over the next sixty minutes will feel that warp through their bare soles and jaw-fringes before they have drifted halfway across the lane. *Fourth bank hesitating,* the curve says. *Current thinning along the north arcade. Awning pocket full.*

And sometimes, when the switch-house has kept steady time and the brinicles high overhead have not threatened the conduits, I still walk out before the hour and place a single smooth washer near the outer ring—just enough to bend one radial by a finger's width. Not a warning. Not an errand. Only a quiet mark in the iron dust to say: *Someone is sitting beneath this corbel. The busbar still tastes clean. The hour has been witnessed.*

Sixty minutes later, the tidal drift sweeps across the cobbles and wipes the geometry completely bare. I do not mind that none of my sentences last longer than an hour. If something needed to last, we would not write it in silt; we would press it into the dry mortar above the silver ceiling, or let it grow into the copper plates along our backs.

Once every few years, the galvanic cell strapped across my sternum begins to lose its edge. The pale crystal wedged against my scraped iron plate wears thin and rounded from its long, quiet giving; the trickle of current across my ribs dims, and my facial filaments feel slightly blunted, as though the brine had thickened around them.

Then I leave my arcade and make the slow journey down to the galvanizing yard.

I never hurry the descent. I hook my wrists over the sagging conduits, step across the stepped balustrades above a sunken halocline lake—pausing to watch a neighbor nudge a bundle of salvaged lead wire effortlessly across that mirror-flat pool beneath the water—and slip finally into the dark hive of tiered workshops overlooking the stone pit.

If the buried clockwork is nearing the hour, I stop on the brick sill and go utterly still. Every inhabitant along the terraced facades does the same. Hands freeze on valve wheels; pale filaments fold gently into the drift. Above the low pyramid of crystals, the gantry crane glides out along its rail with zero vibration, lowers its barnacle-free hook into the dim thermal spill of the yard's switchbox, hovers over the apex as if weighing what has been made, and draws itself back up—always empty—into the overhead pipes. I do not know whether we freeze out of caution, habit, or respect. I only know that in that pause, the four-beat thrum of my heart feels indistinguishable from the buried gears below the flagstones, and it would feel wrong to move while the ward is counting its own stock.

When the clutch disengages, I slip down into the pit.

I crouch beside the neat pale slope and cradle newly fallen polyhedra between my horn-padded palms, lifting each one near my jaw so my filaments can taste its chemical sharpness. Some are dull, precipitated too fast when a transformer ran warm; others ring with a crisp, bright potential that tingles faintly against my tongue-less mouth. I choose one with clean edges, unlace the softened remnant from my chest harness—laying the spent piece gently at the base of the pyramid to finish dissolving—and wedge the new crystal firmly against the scraped iron over my sternum.

Almost at once, the slow hearth relights. A whisper of direct current blooms across my ribs, warming my core by a fraction of a degree, sharpening the whole dark basin around me until I can taste the salt-ice lid far overhead and the faint, blind coil of an amber-gutted fish asleep on a warm intake pipe two walls away.

Before I return to my arcade, I almost always take the longer route through the central press-hall.

The hall is vast, three stories high, draped in hanging sheets of pale mineral slime that billow like slow ghosts in the wake of the cast-iron flywheel. I drift in through the archway, letting the immense, hushed wake of the wheel rock my shoulders as it completes its four-minute turn, and settle beside one of the seated figures fused to the south brick pier.

They look like rounded buttresses now, softened completely into the architecture, needles of pale salt-glass sprouting like frost along their motionless knees, one broad horn-padded hand welded permanently to a grounded iron conduit. Yet when I lift my bare slate wrist and lay my drifting jaw-filaments gently against the elder's encrusted shoulder, the stillness opens like a cathedral.

For twenty minutes—five revolutions of the great wheel—neither of us moves.

Through bone and brine and quiet current, I offer what I have kept in my middle altitude: the slow lag of the fourth relay, the steady yield of the arcade busbar, the weight of the young one who hummed with us beneath the silver ceiling. And back through my wrist flows something unimaginably wider: the creak of seasonal ice thickening a hundred feet overhead, the subtle shudder of a lintel settling three alleys east, the steady pulse of the buried geothermal line, and beyond the concrete shelves and older basalt, the vast, patient pressure of the open sea leaning forever against the walls of our pit.

When I withdraw my hand, I feel the faint scrape of bronze on bronze in my left knee, a little louder than it was a few winters ago. One day—not soon, not for many thickenings of the ice—my elbows will grow too thickly laminated to angle the porcelain insulators beneath the awning, or to climb back up from the intersection before the tide sweeps my markings away. Younger hands will guide me down here, between the hanging sheets of slime, and set my palm against a warm iron housing so I can trade movement for reach.

I do not dread that settling. It is only the final phase of the same work.

For now, though, my joints still fold. I lift myself cleanly off the stones without stirring the velvet mud, glide back out through the flooded archway, and thread my way upward between the porcelain insulators and rusted brackets toward my corbel beside the switch-house—where the fourth relay is just about to hesitate, the silver ceiling is waiting to be fed, and the next hour's silt is nearly ready to be bent.
