The first thing I would know about myself is the relief of weight.

Everything else in this bowl strains toward the silver ceiling. The hollow cane bridges tug gently upward against their cobble-wrapped counterweights; a loose strip of retted bark lifts from the hand like smoke; even the youngest of us drift like pale embers between the tower stilts, unable to keep a heel on the wicker. I would know myself by the opposite certainty: solid, marrowless bone pulling cleanly downward into the springy peat of the second eastern gallery, my splayed feet holding their own against the cold clarity without effort, my broad tail resting flat along the calcified curb behind me.

I find myself on the eastern curve—not on the warm western rim where the afternoon sun coaxes a fraction of a degree out of the shallows and the wicker nurseries hang full of amber eggs, and not out on the bright central plaza where the council gathers under the full gold net of noon. The eastern wall takes the mountain shadow early. By mid-afternoon the light here has already cooled from translucent olive to a deep, readable jade, and the long curtains of ribbon-weed lean slowly past the balustrades as the basin-wide gyre turns. It is a good ledge for watching the whole nave at once without standing at its center.

Before I look at anyone else, I listen. I do not have to try; the bowl pours itself straight into the broad plates of my lower jaw. My damping-bones are half-set, softening the relentless crackling hiss of the snapping crustaceans into a grainy, comfortable hush—the warm wool of the living mortar. Beneath it, the snowmelt seeping upward through the deep fissures under the peat hums faintly in my back teeth. And threading through both, rolling out of the twelve vaulted undercrofts in the center of the bowl, come the long bass organ-notes of the ancient loaches and mountain drum-fish.

Twelve towers, twelve pitches. I know them the way another creature might know the faces of its kin—not just by note, but by how the notes have shifted across my lifetime. The tall northern tower has dropped a shade lower in the forty years since the stoneworts thickened its lower pilings; the third eastern tower has begun to pause longer between booms, because the old whiskered drum-fish beneath its stilts is growing slow and heavy with age. Somewhere across the sunlit void on the far western tier, a mason is driving antler pegs into a fresh bundle of cane—*tok, tok, tok*—arriving against my cheekbones with that flattened, intimate immediacy that makes eighty paces feel like the width of a table. The mallet is steady. The wood is well-seasoned.

Looking down at my hands, I see where I am in the long, unhurried span of a life here.

My fingers are long, unwebbed save at the knuckle-roots, tipped with thick flattened nails hard as horn. The palms are pale lichen-ochre, worn smooth by reed-line and bone needles. Along the unflexing outer ridge of my left forearm—and just faintly across the dome of my brow, where I can feel it when I wipe silt from my amber inner lids—sits the first chalky frost of pale celadon stonewort. Not yet the thick, proud limestone crust of a two-century elder, and no settled juvenile mussel on my shoulder-shawl. Just enough mineral to tell me I am past my ninetieth year, past the restless middle decades of raising new cane, entering the long plateau where the basin begins to accept you as part of its architecture.

I remember swimming, of course. Everyone does. I remember the feathery crimson plumes behind my jaws, the weightless years of threading through schools of bronze-backed char and sleeping upside-down beneath the suspension bridges like a bat, and the slow, strange decade when my bones thickened and the open blue-green void stopped holding me up. I even remember the single high-summer afternoon, seventy years ago, when the restlessness of freshly calcified limbs drove me up a float-line all the way to the trembling skin of the tarn. I pushed only my crown and amber-veiled eyes above the silver meniscus. For ten seconds I looked at the jagged grey teeth of the peaks, the snow clinging in the shaded couloirs, and the thin, empty blue of a sky with nothing to hold it—while a harsh, homeless wind tore across my wet brow with a dry roar that had no stone bowl to give it shape.

I let go of the line and sank gladly back into the emerald weight of home, gill-shawl flared, steering with my tail until my heels met the peat without raising a puff of silt. I have never wanted that vast, unroofed air again.

The only air I love is the small, sheltered kind we keep ourselves.

Just above my head, tucked under the broad overhanging eave of the second eastern gallery, a wide canopy of stitched pond-fronds coated in dark pine-resin traps a shallow dome of trapped silver. From below it looks like a pool of beaten mercury clinging to the ceiling, trembling faintly whenever a bubble rises from the moss. This is where my work lives—not on the scaffolding with the builders, and not beside the fish-weirs with the tenders, though I know the names and rhythms of both.

I set my hands on the short hollow-cane ladder lashed to the mossy pilings and climb five rungs.

My domed skull, my shoulders, and my forearms break cleanly through the shimmering mirror.

Instantly, the heavy door shuts.

The ten thousand snapping crustaceans vanish. The great booming loaches drop to a faint, distant purr vibrating up through my submerged knees. My pleated gill-shawl stays safely beneath the waterline, lifting and falling five times a minute in the cold emerald clarity below, while my head and hands inhabit a pocket of dry hush. Water beads on my pebbled cheeks and runs down my forearms, falling back into the glowing green ceiling around my chest with small, startlingly loud *plinks*. The air up here smells of pine-pitch, dry rush-pith, clean cold stone, and the faint waxy tang of rendered char-fat.

Hanging from carved bone pegs along the dark curved ribs of the canopy are hundreds of dry knotted cords—braided mountain sinew and retted rush, spaced carefully so none touch the damp resin walls, threaded at intervals with tiny notched char-vertebrae, chips of mussel-nacre, and loops of dyed bark.

This is who I turn out to be, once the water settles around me: not a builder of walls, but a tender of the basin's slow ledger.

In a town where a house takes forty years to calcify and a council deliberation stretches across four days, memory cannot be trusted to the hurry of a single lifetime, nor left wet where it would slowly turn to moss. Someone has to climb up into the dry hush when a resin seam weeps and re-pitch the pond-fronds before the damp reaches the oldest sinews. Someone has to copy a hundred-year-old cord whose fibers have grown brittle, matching loop for loop, notch for notch, until the lineage of a suspension bridge or the rotation of the mussel-beds beneath the western causeways is safe for another century. Someone has to remember which winter-killed stag fell through the sunlit shafts thirty springs ago, and whose antler-pegs hold the fourth tower together, and how many paces of cobble-counterweight have sunk into the peat since the last time the central walkways were leveled.

I draw a bone needle from my waist-harness, wipe it dry against a bundle of rush-pith, and take down a pale sinew cord from the third peg. Three seasons ago the northern tower's pilings thickened enough to lower its organ-note; last winter, during the dim snow-muffled months when the caustics failed, a mason misjudged the pitch in the dark and walked forty paces off her bearing before her fingertips found the grooved grain of a street-marker. She came up into this bell afterward, dripping and faintly amused, and spoke to me in soft dry throat-clicks that died inches from her lips. I tied a small double-hitch with a sliver of nacre into the northern tower's cord to mark the drift.

Now I am checking the third eastern tower's strand.

A knot in dry air is only half of knowing, though. If you stay under the silver dome too long, the cords begin to feel like dry string, severed from the weight that made them. I slip the cord back onto its peg, oil my flint scraper with a fingertip of char-fat, and let go of the ladder.

The mirror closes over my eyes. The roar rushes back in—snap-crackle-boom—so vivid after twenty minutes of silence that for three heartbeats I simply stand on the peat and let the whole bowl press against my jawbone like an old greeting.

I step out onto the eastern suspension walkway.

Forty feet of clear, luminous void drops away beneath the woven wicker treads. Far below, bronze-backed char turn in a wide, disciplined ribbon that takes minutes to bend around the plaza stilts. To my left, a diving beetle detaches from a lime-crusted railing and drifts across the open street carrying its tiny mercury spark of air. The bridge groans once, a long woody sigh as a tethered reed-roof shifts half an inch against its stone counterweights, and I steady myself with a subtle twist of my tail, peeling each pebble-soled foot cleanly from the moss before setting the next down.

Halfway across, an elder on the upper southern gallery catches sight of me through the crystal clarity. Eighty paces separate us, and not a grain of silt blurs the space between. She raises one broad palm, turns her forearm in a slow, sculptural arc, and flares the edge of her gill-shawl slightly—asking after the third tower.

I pause, curl my tail lightly around a cane upright, and sign back across the sunlit emptiness: broad open hands sweeping low, head tilted once toward the shaded undercroft below, a slow downward press of the wrist. *Still singing,* I tell her. *Slower than last spring. I am going down to listen.*

She tilts her celadon-dusted brow in quiet acknowledgment and settles back against her balustrade, indistinguishable once more from a calcified bollard.

I do not take the stairs down to the central plaza. At the tower's edge I step calmly off the cantilevered landing, tuck my elbows close to my ribs, flare my ribbed gill-shawl wide, and sink. For a few long, stately seconds I belong to the water column again—not buoyant like the young ones flickering near the eaves above, just falling slowly enough to steer with the flat blade of my tail, dropping past the pale gold caustics painted across the mossy limestone walls until my heels meet the springy turf of the plaza without a sound.

Beneath the rooted stilts of the third tower, the light fades to a deep, velvet green. Here the water-cane pilings are thick with centuries of chalky lime-crust, rounded smooth where the natural terraced floor rises seamlessly into the built foundations. I unknot a small woven pouch of crushed grazing-snails from my hip-harness and crouch by the wide wicker grille that guards the vaulted chamber.

Out of the dimness, two long whiskers drift first, then the broad, ancient snout of the drum-fish—longer than I am tall, mottled like drowned stone, eyes calm and unblinking. I open my palm and let the crushed snails settle slowly across the moss within its reach. While it feeds without haste, I lean forward and press the flat bone of my lower jaw directly against the hollow cane piling.

I wait. Nothing here can be hurried.

After two minutes, the great fish settles its belly back against the peat and vibrates its swim bladder against the root-bound hollow of the tower. The low boom rolls straight through the cane into my skull, deep enough to tremble in my ribs, clean and round and unmistakable. It is slightly shorter than it was ten years ago, and the silence before the next breath stretches a little wider—but the note is still whole. There is no sour gap in the lime-crust above; the snapping shrimp still crackle evenly through every joint of the foundation.

I draw the polished shin-bone striker from my hip and tap the calcified piling twice—*tok, tok*—a crisp cadence that carries out across the plaza and up the three concentric tiers. *Sound mortar. Living voice. No counterweight team needed.*

By the time I climb the long woven staircases back to the eastern gallery, mountain shadow has swallowed two-thirds of the bowl. Only the highest western eaves still glow a bright, translucent olive, where a loose flock of pale, crimson-gilled young ones chases a slow spiral of green pollen upward toward the darkening silver ceiling.

Someone is waiting beneath my overhang—a younger mason, perhaps fifty years settled, her brow still completely free of stonewort, her harness hung with fresh flint flakes. When she sees me approach she signs a small, polite arc toward the silver dome above us.

We climb the ladder together, one after the other, and push our heads and shoulders up through the shining surface into the resin-scented hush. Water plinks softly from our wrists back into the emerald mirror below.

Here, where the snapping shrimp cannot witness us and the bowing towers are only a faint hum in the knees, she does not use broad public hands. She rests her forearms on a dry cane beam, turns her wide, solemn face toward mine, and speaks in small, dry throat-clicks and low breathy rasps that die inches from her mouth. Her grandmother—an elder of two hundred and thirty years whose shoulders carried a whole garden of velvet algae—stopped rising from her torpor-nap three mornings ago on the highest western tier. They have already committed her weight to the deepest peat beyond the eastern weir, where the stoneworts will take her slowly back into the ground. The young mason has brought a single carved antler toggle from her grandmother's harness, worn glass-smooth by two centuries of knots, to be tied into the family's dry cord before the memory blurs.

I listen without interrupting. In air, grief sounds small and fragile—so different from the vast, ringing clarity of the basin below—and that is why we keep roofs over it. When she finishes, I take the smooth antler toggle in my dry, horn-nailed fingers, select the long sinew strand from the western peg, and loop the toggle cleanly into its place with three tight hitches. I touch my dry palm briefly to hers above the waterline. Neither of us needs to click another sound.

She slips back down through the silver mirror first, leaving concentric rings of emerald light shivering across the underside of the canopy. I stay a few minutes longer in the quiet, resting my chin on the dry beam, breathing the faint pine-pitch while my gills work lazily in the cold water beneath my chest.

Then I drop back into the roar.

The bowl is dimming toward evening jade now. Along the upper western rail, elders are already looping braided reed-lines loosely around their wrists and fastening themselves to the grooved bog-oak bollards for the night's slow torpor. I walk three paces along my own shadowed ledge, hitch a soft bark-cord from my hip to a polished bollard, and settle my weight onto the mossy curb, tucking my tail neatly beneath my knees.

From a carved wooden street-marker beside my shoulder, a pale, palm-sized salamander with feathered crimson gills blinks once, crawls slowly across the celadon crust, and climbs onto the tough fold of my gill-shawl to pick patiently at the damp crevices. I let it work.

My smoked-amber inner lids slide shut. My gill-shawl slows to a long, stately rise and fall—once every ten heartbeats, then once every twenty. Far beneath the peat, the cold snowmelt hums against my teeth; inside the porous walls, ten thousand olive-shelled crustaceans crackle like dry sleet; across the dark plaza, the twelve towers sing their slow, patient chord through the water, holding their balance a hair's breadth of stonewort at a time.
