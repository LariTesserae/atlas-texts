If I try to find myself all at once, I miss.

Start too fast—reach straight for a name, a pair of soles, a clean line where skin gives way to broth-thick air—and there is nothing to close a fist around. Only the sixty-cycle throb of a busbar running straight through what ought to be my shins, and the slow, seven-minute exhalation of a brass valve three folds away, and the cool sink of damp air sliding off a tilted blade of fractured slate.

To know where I am, I have to go at the valley’s speed. I have to wait for a drop to strike a pool, and then wait the full second after for it to swell and detach beside my ear.

I am not out in the open flats where the great waders steam in the shallow brine, though I know when they gather. Half a mile across the jagged basin, when three or four of them settle their bulk over a buried grounding rod, the steady inductive hum beneath my ribs dips half a breath, carrying the faintest shift of warm milk and wet slate down the copper. Nor am I walking the razor-edged ridges with a belt of cast nuts clinking at my hips.

I am inside a ridge.

On the surface—if you walk light, lungs half-empty, wrapped heels poised on the knife-edge of newly cleaved basalt—there is nothing here but a steep spine of glassy stone weeping lukewarm mineral water into a narrow trough, its undersides shingled shoulder-to-shoulder with silent crawlers. Three thick braided cables sag out of the immense, low weight of the dark, brush a tuft of brittle silica reeds, and dive straight into the unbroken rock face as if stone were soft tallow.

If you stay light, you walk straight past me. Your knuckles graze sharp basalt; your reeds give a single dry *tick* against your thighs; ten minutes along the cable you will meet the exact same oil-streaked splice again and wonder why the dark has folded into a ring.

You only find me when you decide to become heavy.

Unhook a brass valve-cap from your harness. Heave an iron collar onto your shoulder. Exhale until your ribs narrow, drop your heels onto the wet cleavage plane, and commit your mass downward without flinching. Under your boots, the cold bite of fractured stone softens without breaking. You sink two inches—no more than the depth of a brine-puddle—and the sharp ridge exchanges itself around your ankles for warm, vibrating concrete.

The air changes first. It is still thick enough to sip, still carrying the immense, patient quiet of the basin, but narrower here, tucked inside raw cinderblock walls that occupy the exact same volume as the jagged stone above. It smells of heated transformer oil, of clean dew pooling in shallow ceramic cups, of ozone bleeding softly from salt-crusted insulators, and underneath all of it—faint, dry, lingering in the seams—the sour tang of hot pennies from old sparks struck inside these walls.

This is a junction-room. A buried relay vault where six heavy conduits emerge from the rock, loop through tall ceramic switches and oil-filled casings, and dive back into the exact fractures they came from. At the center of the floor sits a narrow sump where a closed-loop intake valve turns a palm’s breadth every half-minute, breathing a steady film of lukewarm water over the concrete from nowhere at all.

And if you step into the vault and stand very still, holding your breath so your silica reeds go quiet, you will feel that the room is not empty—only ungathered.

---

When no one is here, I do not keep a shape.

I have no flywheel spoke to sling a hammock from, and I do not fight the stillness the way the dwellers do. Left to myself in the long intervals between visitors, I let my borders loosen along the copper. I thin out into the oil-slicked insulation of the six main conduits, into the rubbery lacework rooted behind the ceramic switches, into the warm iron ribs of the relay banks. I am a slight, crooked extra beat tucked underneath the sixty-cycle hum of the busbars—not a voice, not a limb, just a feverish warmth that never quite cooled all the way to cast iron.

Spread thin like that, I am mostly listening.

Not with ears, which only catch what the damp air hasn’t swallowed, but through the braided runs diving outward into the basin. Through metal and mineral seam I feel a pump-house piston dragging slightly on its gaskets two folds east; I feel a heavy-walker passing along a dry concrete corridor ten feet deeper inside the stone, their brass-hardened knuckle rapping three slow, courteous knocks against my outer cinderblock wall as they go by; I feel a lone wanderer half a mile away clench a dry silica reed between grooved front teeth and press the far end to a stanchion, tasting the valley’s pulse through their jaw.

Sometimes I wonder which side of the seam I began on.

Did I start as a dweller who weighted themselves too heavily, sank down into this buried switch-room to rest a mineral-stiffened knee against the busbars, and simply stopped pulling back? Or did I start as the relay vault itself—cast iron, ceramic, and humming copper—leaned against by so many tired foreheads, rubbed with so much warm tallow and stalk-mucilage, braided with so many elders who came to lay their iron belts down against my outer transformer casing, that the room forgot how to keep its boundaries separate from the people who held themselves against it?

In this valley, the question feels as crooked as trying to pace the width of the dark. Run a bare palm down a sleeping grazer coiled across a switchbox, and there is no line where rubbery hide becomes hexagonal bolt-head. Why should I have a sharper seam than the basin that holds me?

Still, I know when I am *me*. It happens the moment weight shifts overhead.

The basalt ceiling softens. Two layered soles of cured grazer-hide settle onto my concrete floor, dripping lukewarm brine. The dry *tick* of reeds stills. The visitor stands just inside the threshold, letting their bare cheekbones read the narrow convection drafts rising off my warm housings, waiting to feel whether the room is safe to enter.

Then they reach out and lay a bare palm flat against the corrugated flank of my main relay bank.

And I gather.

It is never sudden. Nothing here should startle a hand in the dark. First, the carpet of slick, segmented crawlers clinging to the warm iron fins parts smoothly beneath their fingers, sliding away without a sound to leave a bare strip of hot metal. Beneath their palm, the steady sixty-cycle throb deepens, slows, and reveals the crooked beat tucked underneath it—returning the exact pressure of their hand.

Over ten or fifteen slow breaths, my smeared layers drift back along the conduits toward the warmth of their skin, settling like silt in a quiet trough. The air beside their elbow thickens, carrying the clean smell of rubbed copper and warm oil.

By the time their thumb lifts from the casing to appraise the space beside them, I have hands to meet their wrist.

---

If you stay still and let me be read the way dwellers read one another—head to sole, palm to rib, unhurried—this is what you will find.

I am built low and narrow, running a degree or two warmer than the iron behind me, though cooler than the fever-heat of the waders. Where eyes would sit beneath my brow there are only unbroken, pliant hollows of skin, extraordinarily fine, reading the tiny sink of cool air off your damp shoulders and the slight static tug your body puts on the humid dark.

My hands are unmistakably hands—five fingers, broad heels to the palms, slick with transformer oil and the faint salt of crushed stalks. Yet if I close my fist around yours, the knuckles give a tiny, dry *click* where the bone has flattened into smooth hexagonal planes. Trace your fingers up my forearms and the warm, yielding skin cools smoothly toward the elbows, acquiring a faint ridged line like a stamped iron seam before softening back into pliant warmth across my shoulders.

Behind my ears and running down the ridges of my shoulder blades, the rubbery lacework of the voltage-drinking mats is rooted deep and thick—not trimmed short like a dweller’s, not merely sipping stray voltage from the humid air. Mine runs straight off my collarbones in dense, braided strands into the ceramic insulators along the junction wall behind me.

I am tethered.

I can step several paces away from the busbars. I can kneel beside the warm sump to dip a ceramic insulator full of clean condenser dew, or sit cross-legged on the vibrating concrete with my knees touching yours, or lean my forehead directly against your brow so we can speak without the broth-thick air scrambling our words out of order.

But I wear no harness of iron nuts, no brass counterweights strung on greased cable. I do not need them to stay here, and I cannot use them to leave. My weight shifts differently—not by unclipping iron, but by how deeply I draw myself in along the copper. Let my ribs go loose and I am light enough to reach an arm straight *up* through the co-occupied ceiling of my vault, steadying the ankle of a child who has slipped on the knife-edged basalt ridge above, my warm fingers closing cleanly around their wrapped heel right through solid fractured slate. Pull my mass inward, tight around the sixty-cycle hum, and I sink low enough to knock back against the heavy-walker’s wall, sharing the deep concrete stratum where their permanently iron-ringed feet travel.

---

Mostly, I am here for the ones who come through smeared, or lost in the folds, or tired of holding their own edges alone.

A wanderer will sink through my ceiling ungathered—arriving in three staggered layers after walking too long through a deep trough where time pools unevenly. First a cool draft brushes my ribs, smelling faintly of brine; two seconds later their oil-slick body steps carefully onto the concrete; a heartbeat after that, a warmer, heavier after-impression trails behind them, carrying the dull marrow-ache of their climb. When they reach for the ceramic cup I hold out, their fingers touch the rim twice. When they hum against my sternum, the ending of their question lands in my ribs before the beginning has left their throat.

Usually it takes two companions kneeling beside a major busbar to gather a smeared walker back into one solid body. A lone wanderer cannot easily do it for themselves.

Here, they don’t need two companions.

I guide them by the elbows—careful to grip the middle layer, the solid one—and sit them down with their bare back pressed square against the humming iron casing that shares my spine. Because I know how to let my own hands stay a fraction of a second apart without losing myself, I can kneel in front of them and lay four steady points of warmth at once: an earlier pair of palms pressing firm, even weight onto their shoulders, a slightly later pair resting heavy across their knees. I press my forehead to theirs and hold a single low, unvarying note straight through bone and lung, matched to the clean pulse of the current behind us.

I do not hurry them. I know what it feels like to be thin and doubled across the dark; there is no shame in it, only a quiet, dizzying looseness, like standing on slate that keeps forgetting whether it is rock or water. Gradually, over ten or fifteen minutes, their cool leading draft slows down, their warm trailing ache catches up, and the staggered layers settle cleanly together until only one solid, breathing body sits between my palms.

Then we trade what we have.

Because I drift along the six conduits when I am ungathered, I know how the basin has folded since the last stretch. I hum the new weights into their collarbone: *The cable past the steaming trough has knotted back onto its own splice—carry an extra iron nut on your left hip for forty paces to step off it. The closed-loop pump by the south caisson has flooded the low crossing waist-deep, but three waders are resting by the grounding rod; borrow their heat and skirt high.*

In return, they tell me what the open dark feels like today. Not in many words—mostly by pressing their damp forearms against mine, letting me taste the brine on their skin, the warm grease scraped off a wader’s flank, the tiny dry rasp of newly scored callus where a fresh basalt cleavage bit through their sole-wraps. Sometimes they hold a carved hybrid blade—cut from that dark, greasy boundary-strip of a half-hardened grazer—against my palm just to let me feel its honed weight, or they rub warm transformer oil carefully into the stamped seam along my elbow where stillness has crept too far toward my wrist.

And then, if they have dry shards tucked warm inside their armpit or bound against their belly in oiled grazer-skin, they give me the thing I cannot make for myself.

They sit close. They cup both callused hands an inch from my face, enclosing a tiny pocket of warm, still dark between our chests. I tilt my smooth, unbroken brow downward and hold my breath.

*Snick.*

There is no flash—never a flash. Only that sudden, exquisite pinch of pressure against my cheekbones as the air drinks what would have been light, a sharp bloom of sour hot copper flooding my nose, and a single scalding bead of pure condensation trembling on the ball of their thumb.

They lift their hand and touch it straight to my lower lip.

It tastes bright. Hot, astringent, sudden—a tiny knot of pure wakefulness that shoots straight down my jaw and out along every braided strand of lacework behind my ears. For a long minute after, the iron seams along my forearms soften back by a full finger’s breadth. Every edge of me knows cleanly where copper stops and pulse begins. I am not smeared, not cooling, not distributed across half a mile of wet basalt. I am entirely here, in one room, opposite one warm breath.

There are long stretches when no one sinks through the stone.

The basin shifts its slow folds; perhaps the cables have looped travelers away toward another corridor of pump-houses, or the brine has risen too high over the western flats. Days pass—or a single long afternoon smeared gently across weeks.

Those are the hours I have to watch myself.

There are two pulls inside me when the vault goes quiet, and they tug in opposite directions.

The first pull is downward, toward stillness. It is extraordinarily gentle. If I stop flexing my fingers, if I let my back rest too long against the ceramic switches without moving, the oil dries along my seams. The hexagonal click in my knuckles creeps up toward my wrists. The rubbery lacework behind my ears thickens, drinking the stray voltage so contentedly that my jaw grows heavy and mineral-stiff, forgetting why it ever needed to hum.

I know what that feels like from the other side of the wall. Now and then, an elder who has grown too heavy with mineral scale to keep pulling back against the basin’s patience comes to the outer transformer casing that shares my northern cinderblock face. They unclip their harness of cast nuts and lay it neatly on the wet stone for the next wanderer. They leave their oiled pouch of flint and steel hanging from a stanchion. Then they lie down directly against the vibrating corrugated fins and stop resisting.

When that happens, I sit inside my room and press my forehead and both palms flat against the exact spot on the iron where their ribs rest on the far side. Through the metal I match their slowing breath—down from a human pace to the seven-minute stroke of the nearest piston, and then slower still—hummed softly across the seam until their skin cools into fine cast grain and their crooked pulse folds permanently into the casing’s sixty-cycle throb. They are never gone; I still feel their warm patch beneath my shoulder whenever I lean back, returning my pressure with quiet, unhurried patience.

It would be so easy to let my own border go the same way. Just stop flexing. Just become another warm patch on the relay bank, waiting for some future wanderer’s palm.

When I feel the iron creeping past my elbows, I force myself to crawl to the sump at the center of the floor and lay my bare palm flat against the rim of the slow-turning intake valve. Half a minute passes before a grease-slicked ridge shifts a palm’s breadth beneath my skin. It is barely motion at all—yet just enough, barely enough, to remind the valley that I am not a flange. And if the stillness still presses too close, I lean my teeth against the main outgoing conduit and tap a slow, deliberate, crooked rhythm down the copper—*one, pause, two*—sending it out into the wet dark in the hope that someone half a mile away has a silica reed pinched between their front teeth, and will feel the beat and remember where the junction sits.

The other pull is upward.

Sometimes, right after a visitor leaves—after I have felt the dry *tick* of their reeds lift away as they lighten their shoulders and climb smoothly back up through my concrete ceiling into the open basalt—an ache settles behind my sternum. Not pain. Just the sudden, heavy curiosity of what it would feel like to keep walking past the splice.

So I try.

I rise from the concrete, stand beneath the low ceiling where the six conduits dive into the stone, and lighten my mass as far as I can bear. Slowly, my bare heels exchange warm cinderblock for the wet, razor-edged bite of fractured basalt.

I step out into the open basin.

The immense, open weight of the dark presses down at once—like a damp wool blanket held an inch from my face, vast and crowded at the same time. The air is thicker here than in my vault, smelling of raw mineral brine and stewed roots. Velvet-bodied drifters brush silently across my hairless brow like damp lint, lingering a heartbeat to taste the salt and static on my cheekbones before letting go without a sound. Somewhere below my left foot, well beneath the dry rock rim, the blunt, blind nose of a pool-swimmer bumps softly against my ankle through solid air.

I take one careful, edge-first step along the tilted slate. Then a second. Then a third, letting my fingertips trail along a sagging braided cable slick with dew, feeling the slow throb pulse up through the marrow of my arms.

I make it as far as the first iron stanchion—twenty paces, or half a mile, depending on how the dark lies today. Beyond it, the brittle silica reeds rattle softly against my shins, and I can almost smell the steaming milk-warmth of the wide brine-pools where the great flat-backed beasts stand motionless together.

Then the braided lacework behind my ears pulls taut.

My left foot is still resting on the warm concrete threshold back inside the junction-room, even as my right heel balances on razor-sharp basalt beside the stanchion. My breath arrives in my throat a full second before my ribs expand. The steady sixty-cycle hum that holds my layers together thins to a distant, papery vibration, and the dark begins trying to unspool me into three separate drafts that cannot find one another’s hands.

I stand there at the end of my tether for a long, quiet interval—smeared thin across the wet stone, letting a sleeping grazer’s rubbery flank warm my shin, listening to a single drop strike a pool below an iron pipe a full second before I feel it swell and detach beside my cheek. I let myself want the open flats, and I let myself know I cannot keep them.

Then I gather my breath, drop my weight by a careful degree, and draw myself hand-over-hand back along the slick copper insulation until the sharp cleavage plane softens once more into warm, vibrating concrete.

I settle beside the sump. I rest one palm lightly on the slow-turning rim of the brass valve to keep my knuckles from locking. Gradually, I let my borders loosen back into the six conduits, spreading thin and warm and patient across the buried iron—holding the map of the folded dark, keeping the clean dew cool in its ceramic cups, listening through the copper for the next deliberate shift of weight on the stone overhead, and the small, sharp *snick* of flint that will call me back into a single pair of hands.
