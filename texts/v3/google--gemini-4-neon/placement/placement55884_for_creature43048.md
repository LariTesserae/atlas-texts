At first, I do not think I would know what shape I had—only that I had been brought somewhere that asks you to put down almost everything you are used to carrying.

You have to put down sight before you even reach the first terrace. Up near the pale, bruise-grey wash of the cloud ceiling, eyes might still try to make a habit of themselves, straining for edges that are not there, mistaking drifting amphipods for stars. Let go of that. Sink deeper, past the level where the faint grey thins into indigo, until the water closes around your ribs like a hand that neither grips nor releases. Here, there is no hurry left in the world. Even breath—if you arrived with the memory of air—becomes an absurd, frantic thing, too sharp and restless for a sea that has not shifted an inch in a thousand years.

Wait in the dark long enough, and the noise stops feeling like noise. It sorts itself into geography.

I thought, at first, that I might belong out near the outer rim. There is a clean, severe pull to the idea of it—bracing belly-down against the trembling timber where the whole perched sea pours over into thin mountain air, holding the seam of the world together by sheer weight and attention. There is honesty out there, where the thunder hammers straight into the skull and leaves room for nothing trivial.

Yet when I imagine pressing my cheek to those outer weirs, I realize I would be deafened to the very thing that drew me down into this valley. Out at the lip, there is only the fundamental—the vast, undifferentiated roar of water leaving the mountain. Everything subtle gets torn away by the draw.

Nor do I think I belong up at the high cloud-fed seeps at the back of the plateau, where the water enters thin and unmarked, not yet shaped by the columns and conduits it has to pass through. That is a place for beginnings, for the leaner young ones whose ribs still shadow faintly through pale skin.

For a moment, drifting out over one of the wide, empty plazas, I almost envy the great pewter-scaled fish. To have no walls to mend, no strings to shift—only to glide along the sunken causeways with sensitive barbels trailing, carrying a heavy swim-bladder full of dark light, booming once every few breaths so that three hundred yards of braided viaducts ring back out of the black. To be a lantern that never asks what it illuminates.

Almost. Not quite. Because if I made that sound, I would want to hear what it struck. I would want to feel the shape of the room come back against my face—to know where a braided arch throws a clean shadow, where a terrace drops off into the deep peat bowls, where a column answers half a shade lower than it did twenty years ago.

So that is how the body finds me, or how I find my way into it: not all at once, but feature by feature, out of what I want to hear.

First the stillness in the chest—the cold snowmelt temperature settling all the way into the marrow, burning almost nothing, so that an hour and a day stop feeling like different kinds of time. Then the broad, slate-grey mantles lifting and settling down to the collarbone, drinking the thin water without haste. The smooth, seamless brow, no eyes to narrow or strain, only the two tiny lidded pits at the crown keeping quiet track of which way the sky sits, and beneath the pale willow-colored skin of the face, the twin oil-filled chambers waking up to the valley.

And the hands: wide, four-fingered, blunt-nailed, built for gauging and holding rather than cutting.

When I finally know where I am, I am pressed flat against the leeward curve of a colossal pier on the middle terraces—not the highest, where the pale ceiling still smudges the black, and not the very bottom, where the compacted peat swallows all echo. I am at the hinge of the valley, where a broad drowned avenue widens into a plaza and then breaks off sharply over a rounded parapet, plunging straight down into the dark bowls below.

The moss along my dorsal crest is already cropped to the exact height of the velvet nap around me. Blind pale crawlers march steadily up the diagonal flutes of the fused trunks, cross my shoulder without breaking stride, and click on into the dark overhead. I let them. Their dry, rapid tapping is the skin of the silence here; as long as it runs unbroken, I know nothing nearby is leaking or tearing.

I am not young anymore, though I am not yet stiff enough to stay inside one column. My shoulders have broadened with decades of slow drift; the oil behind my cheeks has settled into the clear, heavy sensitivity that comes only after you have listened to the same mountain thunder long enough to stop hearing the thunder itself, and begin hearing only the changes inside it.

Everyone in the basin listens for the sharp flaws. When a stray shoot of water-willow pushes crooked out of a fluted seam and sets up a ragged hiss in the laminar flow, someone comes—sometimes me—to anchor wide, ridged feet into the grooves and hold the pale tendril back into its ancient crease for two or three uncounted days until the living wood remembers the curve. When a clot of shed sedge lodges in an overflow screen and the blind amphibians wedged inside the pipe drop half a tone flat, one of the lean younger ones glides down to clear it with delicate horn-nails. We do that because a sour note in a sea this still hurts the teeth and clouds the water for a hundred yards.

But what claims me—the quiet habit that has turned, over uncounted years, into who I am here—is slower than a clogged pipe or a stray runner.

I listen for the things that go out of tune by growing quiet.

A pier is never truly finished. Even though no wild branches spill out into the avenues and the outer curves look unchanged for centuries, the hollow heart of every column is slowly thickening. One by one, the ancient ones climb inside the leeward slits of the piers they tended longest, wedge themselves into the smooth cured ribs above the weighted strings, and stop swimming. For decades they remain there, gill-mantles lifting in time with the distant falls, answering softly when a younger dweller knocks a question against the outer timber. And then, one cold hour like any other, their mantles settle and do not rise again. Slowly as stillness, the pale water-willows and creeping sedges close over their heavy bones, curing them into the inner wall.

When that happens, the column changes.

Not violently. Not in any way that churns the current or troubles the crawlers. The pier simply becomes a fraction thicker, and its hollow voice drops a fraction deeper.

Inside, stretched from one cured inner wall to another, hang the strands of stripped willow-bast and sedge-tendon, each weighted at its center with a smooth pebble of peat-wood or the dense ivory ear-bone of a long-dead pewter fish. They were tuned to the pier as it was before the elder cured into the wood. For ten years, maybe twenty, nobody notices the difference. Then, if you slip through the narrow leeward slit and hang neutrally in the sheltered, motionless water inside the column, you begin to hear it: a faint, slow wobble in the overtones. A gentle beating between the wood's new fundamental and the old pitch of the strings, pulsing like a tired throat that cannot quite find the count of the falls.

That is where you would find me.

I travel between the middle and lower piers—gliding downstream in the open black when the laminar seams favor me, arms angled slightly out, dorsal keel catching the even draw like a pale, heavy kite until an outstretched palm meets velvet wood two hundred yards away; walking back upstream by the walls, splayed toes hooked securely into the diagonal flutes along the undersides of the braided arches, passing motionless clusters of red-gilled salamanders in the quiet drag of water close to the moss.

I keep no tally of the piers I visit—there are no numbers here, only shapes of sound—yet I carry their chords in the bones of my face. Sometimes I stop at the parapet of a sunken causeway, press the dense heel of my palm against a fluted rib, and knock twice. Out in the black expanse of the plaza, one of the huge pewter-scaled fish answers with a deep *thrum* from its swim-bladder. The sound rolls off the stepped embankments and washes back across my cheeks, lighting up the whole basin in a clean, ringing relief.

I listen to the walls first, and then I listen to the fish.

You get to know them, if you stay in one stretch of the terraces for a century. Each great bottom-dweller has its own timber—one broad and hollow as a deep conduit, another slightly tight and dry, carrying farther across the open spans. They are our lanterns, kept burning along the avenues. And because I am always listening for voices that drop half a shade lower or thin at the edges, I am often the first to notice when one of the old ones begins to fade.

Its *thrum* loses its long tail. It answers a knock late, or only once when it used to answer twice. When I glide out along the causeway to meet it—never rushing, displacing only a slow, cool pillow of water—my acoustic brow reads the change before my hands ever touch its flank: the heavy scales gone silt-dull, the swim-bladder half-collapsed, the huge blunt head tipping slightly sideways in the laminar drift.

I do not rush it, and I do not leave it alone.

For days, sometimes, I drift beside it as it loses its hold on the upper corridors, letting the slow pull carry us out past the rounded parapet and down into the absolute black of the lowest bowls. Down there, the roar of the distant rim is muffled by yards of compacted peat and tangled rhizomes; even the clicking of the crawlers is sparse. I keep one broad palm near its cold flank—hardly touching, just steady enough to give it a wall in the dark—until it finally settles belly-down into the soft, pale-tubered floor and its gills stop working.

Only when the silence around it is complete do I take what it has left behind. Its heavy fat feeds the long cold of my own body for a seasonless stretch. Its dense, curved ivory ear-bones I tuck carefully into the fold beneath my gill-mantles, or carry curled in the blunt palm of one hand as I climb back up the vertical face of the sunken mountainside, foot over foot in the ancient grooves, returning from the abyss to the ringing middle plazas.

Then I slip through the narrow, smooth-lipped slit on the leeward side of a pier whose chord has begun to beat.

Inside, the rush of the open valley falls away into something vast and sheltered. The water hangs utterly still, cold and clear, yet the curved walls around me hum steadily with the whole mountain's weight. High up in the dark dome of the column, I can feel the smooth, newly grown swell in the inner timber where an elder finally finished joining the wood—someone whose broad chest I may once have pressed my brow against, decades ago, trading slow clicks and hums about the cloud-fed seeps and the tuning of piers three basins east. The living fibers have drawn cleanly over their hips and ribs now. The pier is a hair narrower inside, a hair richer in its bass.

I drift vertically in the center of the chamber, limbs loose, weightless, letting my cheek-drums drink the slow wobble between the wall and the strings.

It does not take force. Nothing in this valley yields to force. I reach out in the dark until my blunt fingers find a taut strand of cured sedge-tendon anchored to a knot of living rootlet. I feel its dry vibration buzzing faintly against my skin. Where an old peat-wood pebble hangs at its center, slightly too light now for the column's deeper voice, I untie the simple loop with patience—feeling every hair of tension—and bind in the dense ivory ear-bone from the silent pewter fish.

Then I let go and wait.

I do not pluck the string. I never pluck them. I pull my arms close to my flanks and float motionless while the distant waterfalls, miles away along the rim, find the newly weighted fiber through the buried root-stone foundations.

Slowly, the string wakes. At first the beat is still there, only wider—a long, breathing swell that rises and fades across the dark water. I reach out again, shift the bone weight half a finger's breadth toward the rootlet knot, draw the willow-bast a breath tighter, and fold back into stillness.

This time, the swell stretches out, thins, and disappears.

The wobble is gone. In its place, the whole hollow column locks into a single, seamless chord—deep enough to feel in the marrow of my forearms, bright enough in its upper overtones to light the smooth curved walls from floor to crown. The voice of the elder cured into the timber, the weight of the old fish that once lit the plazas, and the endless falling of the sea off the edge of the mountain have found the exact note where they do not fight one another.

Sometimes, while I am still hovering there, the water near the leeward slit shifts gently and another dweller slips inside—drawn from the outer flutes by the sudden cleanliness of the pier's voice. We drift close in the dark without haste, limbs tangling loosely in neutral buoyancy, and press the smooth oil-filled skin of our brows together.

Through bone and skin, without spilling a single vibration into the public water outside, I give them the new pitch of the column, the name-by-rhythm of the old fish that went mute in the lower bowls, and the faint slackness I noticed last passing in a viaduct two terraces north. They press back a brief, warm slide of hums—news of a young one born near the high seeps, a rim-tender who finally let go into the sky after too many winters of bracing against the draw, a quiet gratitude that the pier rings true again. Our gill-mantles settle into the same slow count, and for a long time we simply hang there, adding our low chest-hums to the trembling strings until the dark feels full.

Later, I will slip back out into the cold laminar drift and flatten myself against the outer flutes to sleep, letting the blind crawlers march across my back until I am indistinguishable from the wood again.

And I already know, though it is still far off, which pier I will choose when my own joints grow too dense and heavy for the long glides across the plazas, and the skin over my acoustic drums begins to cloud. It is a wide, slightly solitary column overlooking the drop into the lowest peat bowls—a pier with a clean leeward slit and an old, generous resonance that leans just slightly toward the deep bass.

When the time comes, I will climb inside for the last time and wedge myself comfortably into the curved timber above the weighted strands. I will let the slow water-willows lay their pale rootlets across my hips. For decades I will hum softly in sympathy with the valley, answering when younger ones knock questions against the outer wood, steadying the room from within until my mantles settle and do not lift again.

And years after that—when the living fibers have closed smooth over my bones and thickened the wall by a fraction of an inch—I like to think that someone much like me will drift through the slit in the dark, feel the faint, patient wobble of the newly deepened column, and reach out with cold, blunt fingers to shift an ivory ear-bone half an inch along a willow string, until the long cold note holds true once more.
