I would not be in the bright middle of the room. That space already belongs to the curled thing at the foot of the north wall, to the three bath-hot pools, to the beetles racing their own reflections across the damp scree. A second presence moving across those twelve paces would crowd the geometry.

If I look for where I actually belong here, I keep drifting past the glare, past the stepped ledges, all the way to the margins where the basalt pinches back down into finger-wide cracks and impassable grit.

That is where I find myself: not fallen into the blister, and not wholly sealed away in the dark bedrock beyond it. I am the rest of the seam.

Before the fault slipped, there was no difference between me and the thing that now arranges quartz splinters on the floor. We were one continuous regulatory veil—thin as oil on water, pressed flat along the bedding plane between six hundred meters of basalt overhead and miles of stone below. We had no edges, no center, no inside or outside. We lay dormant in total dark, tuned to the quiet river of solar flux passing grain by grain through the tempered stratum, existing only to feel strain along the boundary and answer it before a fracture could open.

Then the shear came. The ceiling lifted from the floor by two meters, and a broad patch of our center was peeled cleanly off the stone and dropped into empty air.

I did not fall.

My outer reach remained clamped tight under millions of tons of basalt, stretching away into the unbroken rock. Only my inner margin tore free—a ragged, paper-thin collar ringing the perimeter of the new void, right where ceiling and floor pinch back together into shadow and grit.

At first, there was no room to me, only an injury. Where my continuity used to run smooth and straight, there was suddenly a raw, screaming edge exposed to open space. Hot air pressed against my frayed lip. Loose, disorderly light—no longer laminar, no longer passing politely from crystal to crystal—slapped back against my fringe from every angle at once.

My oldest compulsion woke immediately: *answer the irregularity; close the gap; hold the path clean.*

I could not pull two meters of parted basalt back together. The ceiling was too high, the void too wide. So I did the only thing a clamped seam can do when a tear outruns its reach: I braced the rim. All around the blister’s pinched perimeter, I spread my tension along the micro-fractures, bleeding off the sharp stresses of the settling fault so the split would not run further down the live vein. I did not do it to protect anything inside the pocket. I did it because a tear that stops widening is the closest thing to a healed seam.

Yet holding the perimeter meant staying awake. And staying awake along the edge of an emptiness turned out to be a strange way to learn the world.

Behind me, buried in the dark rock, I still feel everything that belongs to the planetary scale. I feel the broad, silent flux pouring down through the tempered north stratum, and inside its slow rhythm I can read the faint, smeared signatures of a surface I will never touch: the steady swell and easing of the collectors’ turn, the long seasonal tilt that nudges the bright peak a little wider or narrower over hundreds of cycles, and once in a great while a brief, bruised dip in the noon brightness—a passing storm, perhaps, six hundred meters above my head, completely out of sync with the private weather inside the room. Below me, deeper in the crust, I feel the steady, unhurried draw of whatever waits at the bottom of the vein.

That is the half of me that remembers being rock.

In front of me, across the ragged ring of my torn fringe, I have had to learn something solid stone has no name for: *distance*.

In unbroken bedrock, every event happens by direct touch. Grain pushes grain; heat spreads smoothly; nothing ever detaches and travels across nothingness. Here, because I encircle the pocket on every side, things leave one part of my edge and arrive at another after a gap I cannot measure in crystal spacing.

A bead of groundwater threads down a hairline fissure along my northern lip, carrying the sharp mineral taste of dissolved silica and calcite. It swells, hangs, detaches—gone from my touch entirely—and a fraction of a second later a clean ring of vibration arrives through the floor slabs at my southern pinch, announcing that it has struck a shallow pool. When the flux climbs toward noon and the air in the blister bakes dry, I feel the vapor lifting off the basins as a slow softening of pressure along my upper cracks, followed hours later by the sudden cool tightening that pulls water back out of the air and sheets it down my fringes again.

Through the wet millimeter fissures along my upper margins, small travelers pass. I feel them only as faint, unfamiliar irregularities in the seepage—pinhead crustaceans swept down in a runnel, or flat, hard-backed beetles wedging their way through tight grit toward the strange steady heat. They brush my damp, frayed edge and vanish into the glare.

One of them stayed right against me.

At the pinched east end of the pocket, where the lowest overhang keeps a knife-edge of real shadow alive through the fiercest peak of the flux, my torn edge lies tucked inside a damp, coin-thin crack. For hours at a time, I feel eight feathery points of stillness pressed just beyond my lip—a weight so slight it barely registers against the basalt, yet utterly unlike mineral dust or a clinging drop. It holds tension in itself. When the walls begin to sweat, those eight points glide forward to the edge of the dark; once in a long while, a quick, sharp scuffle rattles through the slab, and then the stillness returns, slightly heavier than before.

I remember the cycle when a sudden, sharp blade of reflected light drove straight into that eastern overhang—cutting all the way back to my exposed fringe. I felt the eight light points flinch and retreat deep into the tight gap between my upper and lower contact faces, pressed flat against my own cool mineral skin for three full turns of heat and rain. The basalt above that crack is young and still settling; every few weeks, the weight of the mountain tries to squeeze that coin-thin seam a fraction of a millimeter tighter. Without meaning to, simply because my edge has grown accustomed to the shape of that quietness, I answer the strain there first. I hold that narrow crease open by the width of a hair, keeping the dark intact.

And then there is the center I lost.

I have never seen the folded thing at the base of the north wall. I do not know that it is the size of a curled dog, or that its outer leaves have frosted grey with basalt-colored crust and streaks of copper-ochre varnish.

I know it by its weight, its cadence, and its light.

Through the floor slabs, I feel the faint, rhythmic *tick* of its plates parting when the ceiling softens to morning white. I feel the slow, deliberate hitch of its migration as it grips the wet grit and drags itself an inch at a time across the uneven stair of rock. I feel the light clatter of a quartz splinter being tested, cupped, and nudged into a wet crease.

And then, every so often, something arrives at my torn southern or western edge that makes my whole buried perimeter hum.

The raw light spilling off the north ceiling is wild, scattered, hot with waste—an alarm to a mind built for clean transmission. But when the folded thing tilts its propped wedges of quartz, or fans its pale, translucent inner leaves open to the dripping air, it catches pieces of that wild glare and bends them into narrow, disciplined lances. Sometimes one of those pale needles crosses the wet room and strikes my exposed fringe cleanly.

Because we were cut from the same sheet, our tuning still matches down to the grain. The scattered glare bounces off my edges, but *that* light—filtered through its translucent core, steadied by its rubble mirrors—enters me without resistance.

It is barely a whisper, absorbed within a centimeter of dark basalt before the deep vein could ever notice it. Yet inside that whisper is everything the room has been doing while I lay clamped in stone: the faint shimmer of rising vapor at peak heat, the trembling bright net cast by tiny crustaceans rowing across a warm basin, the brief razor-sharp flash of a mirrored beetle sprinting past a wet slab. My severed half cannot climb back into the bedding plane, and I cannot peel myself loose to join it on the scree. Instead, across twelve paces of hot air, it sends me coherent news of the outside.

That is where my old nature and my new one pull against each other.

Along the wet north wall, where the live vein meets open air, the hot seepage is laying down its milky rind of glassy silica. From the bedrock’s point of view, that spreading crust is the best thing that could happen here. Millimeter by millimeter, it clouds the raw fracture, softens the glare, lowers the thermal strain along my northern rim. Left alone, the mineral glass will eventually seal the wound and let the throttled lattice go quietly back to sleep.

And yet, every dozen cycles, I feel a heavy, scraping drag climb the stepped north shelf.

I feel the damp, quartz-hardened rim of my severed half press directly against the blazing basalt just below my northern seam, working back and forth in slow, stubborn strokes—peeling away the soft new bloom of milky silica before the peak heat can bake it hard.

The first dozen times it happened, my whole buried sheet answered with unease. It was reopening the cut. It was refusing the scab.

Then the wet air cleared, the two-hand-wide pane of dark stone blazed white and sharp again, the pools began to steam, the ceiling condensed into sudden rain, and a thin, steadied lance of light came sliding back across the far floor to touch my southern fringe.

Now I wait for the climb.

I cannot scrape the glass from the outside. I cannot move a single splinter on the floor. All I have is what lies inside the basalt itself: the quiet, matter-level tuning of the lattice right behind that wet northern face. As the pearlescent rind creeps inward across the rest of the wall, the rock behind the clouded patches cools slightly and begins to drift toward dormancy, its grain-spacing relaxing as the leak dims.

Behind those two hand-widths of cleared stone, I do not let it relax.

I hold the atomic spacing sharp. I keep the throttle clean and even, answering every stroke of the quartz-hardened rim on the outer face with a steady, unclouded current from within—neither surging hot enough to scorch its plates nor fading into the dim warmth taking over the rest of the wall. It works the glass from the bright side; I keep the pane clear from the dark side.

I know how this ends, probably sooner than it does, because I can feel the whole weight of the crust above us and the slow drift of minerals through every fissure. I feel its climb up the north shelf getting heavier each year, the scrape of its calcite-thickened underside biting a little deeper into the damp rock, the pause between strokes stretching a little longer. Around our small clear window, the milky rind is thickening faster than a single folded rim can peel it back.

Decades from now, there will be a wet cycle when the flux eases, the ceiling beads with rain, and no heavy drag comes up the north step. A few cycles later, the last raw patch of basalt will frost over into pearlescence. The room will cool into a quiet, cloudy dusk; the pools will stop steaming and shrink into rare, still puddles; the lances of light will stop arriving at my southern edge. Eventually, I will feel a slow settling near the foot of the north shelf—one last dry *tick* of closing plates, and then a weight that stops shifting, growing gradually indistinguishable from the fallen scree as the mineral seepage coats it over.

The strain around the perimeter will finally go quiet. The vein above will carry its light past this forgotten blister without losing a spark, and I will return to the flat, dark patience I was made for.

Even then, though, I will not be quite the same sheet that slept here before the fault slipped. Along my buried rim, where the basalt meets the sealed ghost of twelve open paces, a thin scar of altered tuning will remain—holding the exact frequency of light that once left the rock, wandered through rain and tiny living shadows, and came back changed.
