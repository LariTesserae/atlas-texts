At first, I find myself on the stone—because before I know what else I might be, I want the plain, honest weight of a terrace beneath my knees.

Not on the highest knife-edge ridges, where the cairns stand solitary against the clear glare, and not down in the deepest stepped canyons where the pale walls climb out of sight overhead. Somewhere middling: a recessed limestone shelf partway down the flank of a broad amphitheater, sheltered by a low overhang of stacked shale slabs weighted with curved rib-bones. Just enough room for a cold hearth of arranged stones cradling its neat cone of white ash, a shallow clay bowl of powdered feldspar, and a narrow margin of matte rock where the animals do not quite touch.

If you passed along this tier—stepping with month-long patience between the flanks of tall, narrow-ribbed browsers—you would take me for an ordinary seam-keeper. Compact, wrapped from collar to ankle in layered dust-colored hides, my cuffs laced snug with bleached sinew, my knuckles the dry matte shade of old horn. I sit cross-legged behind the low stone wall, drawing a bone needle through the edge of a sleeve with the slow, clean attention of someone who has nowhere to arrive.

That is where I begin: with the quiet craft of keeping a border intact.

It feels immediately right to me, this work. There is no fear in it, no frantic clinging to selfhood—just a calm, practical hygiene, the way a carpenter keeps a chisel true. Before I touch the polished limestone behind my shoulder, I dip my fingertips into the clay bowl and rub the powdered feldspar thinly across my palms so my skin will not forget where it ends and the rock begins. Now and then I touch a thumb to the white ash beside the doorway and draw a short, deliberate line across the lintel, or down the bridge of my nose—a small, dry declaration of separateness, finished and un-overlapping.

And because I am here, sitting still for days at a stretch, I find myself listening.

Not with my ears alone; the packed hides around the shelf drink every vibration before it can cross three paces. I keep four slack cords of woven fiber pegged into the dry flutes beneath the overhang, their loose loops resting around the motionless ankles of small, six-legged burrowers tucked into the cool bedrock. Beside them, on a smooth face of chalky limestone, I have pecked a long, patient column of narrow vertical notches with a harder stone.

This is what my hands return to when the seams are tight: I set the point of a hammered copper awl into one of those shallow grooves, brace the smooth handle against my cheekbone, and wait.

Through the metal and the bone of my jaw, the silent range opens into a dry, sparse ledger of news. A microscopic tick half a ridge away where a limestone plinth settles by the thickness of a hair. The faint, papery shiver traveling up a fiber cord when a six-legged beast shifts one foot a fraction of a millimeter because gravity, two tiers down, has begun to lean out of plumb. The slow, dry exhalation of a hidden spiracle, like grains settling in a reed.

I am not trying to fix any of it. Nothing here breaks, and nothing can be hurried. I simply keep the tally—adding a new peck to the column when an agreement shifts, so that pressing the copper tip against an old mark tells me, by the faint change in its ring, how the stone has altered its mind since the mark was cut.

Yet the longer I stay on this terrace, the more I notice that keeping a tidy border is only half of what draws me here. The other half is what happens right beside the edge.

Five paces beyond my low shale wall, the terrace narrows to a boot-wide cornice where a dozen wool-coated grazers stand abreast, their pale flanks passing cleanly through one another like chalk drawings layered on the same sheet. Sometimes, after an hour of drawing sinew through hide, I stand—taking the better part of a morning to unbend my knees—and walk over to the edge of the overlap. I dust my hand carefully with feldspar, not to seal myself completely, only to keep my skin from taking hold, and slide my fingers slowly into the place where three broad shoulders occupy the same hand’s breadth of space.

It is warm inside, banked like dry embers in a closed kiln. If I hold completely still, letting my breath match the half-hour descent of the herd’s ribs, I feel the faintest, driest brush against my knuckles: the papery edge of an eyeless fold-dweller, flat as peeled birch-bark, threading through the double-solid dark on rows of feathery legs. I do not try to grasp it or coax it into the light. I only visit the warmth for a little while, acknowledge the country inside the overlap, and withdraw my dusted hand cleanly before my wrist forgets which bones belong to it.

Back by my cold hearth, I keep a similar courtesy for the flat things on the floor.

The corner of my shale roof, the stacked stones of a small gravity-cairn, and the motionless curved horns of a grazer resting three paces off align in the unmoving afternoon light to cast a shadow that belongs to none of them: a lean, long-legged hound, coiled nose-to-tail on the pale limestone. Its flesh and bone finished scouring away epochs ago, leaving only a sharp-edged silhouette that migrates by patience. Whenever a shift in the grazer’s stance lets the shadow inch forward across the threshold, it sometimes brushes the hem of my stitched cloak. I feel it as a subtle darkening along the chalk-dust of my sleeve—neither cold nor heavy, simply a quiet absence asking for passage. I halt mid-step and stand as motionless as the cliff behind me for two hours, lending the uncast hound the flat dry plane of my shoulder until its silent muzzle glides cleanly off my elbow and settles onto the next slab of stone without tearing.

I think, for a long while, that this is all I am: a listener on a middle shelf. A keeper of clean seams and pecked grooves, attentive to the quiet citizens of the folds and the shadows.

Then I lift my eyes from the copper awl, stop insisting on depth, and look out across the three-mile gulf.

At first it is just the familiar view: near blades of pale slate crossing the silhouette of a massive, chalk-bright cliff across the chasm, and between them the vast clear space stippled with tens of thousands of motionless winged and ribbon-bodied creatures hanging in layered shoals. Far out among them, thin as reeds against the brightness, a few featherless striders pick their stately way from one spread membrane to the next, their needle-sharp shadows falling straight down into the dry abyss.

I watch the place where the distant pale massif crosses the nearer slate fin. I let my gaze go slack, the way the walkers do at the top of their hide-lashed ladders, until the far wall quietly slides in front of the near spire.

And that is when I realize I am not only looking *out* into the clear air.

I am also looking *back* at this overhang.

Because in an afternoon that does not end, a person does not have to choose between the weight of stone and the openness of the gulf. I have been here a very long time—long enough that my borders, kept neat at the wrist and ankle, have learned to wear distance loosely across the chest.

Half a mile out from the cliff face, where no ledge remains and no rubble ever fell, I am sitting upright in the windless light.

I do not remember the exact afternoon when gravity began to loosen its agreement with that part of me. It would have begun the way it always does: a wrapped heel lingering half an inch above the shale step; a bone needle hovering briefly above my knee before settling back onto my lap. I did not tie myself down with pegs and cords. Neither did I abandon the shelter, the hearth, or the listening-grooves. I simply let one edge of myself step off the lip of a spire while the other stayed knelt beside the cold white ash, stitched together across the chasm by the quiet, invisible coherence that holds five long-necked beasts along a single translucent spine.

Out there, my wrapped heels rest lightly on nothing at all, just beside a gnarled clump of dry thorn-scrub floating intact in the pale glare, its exposed wooden roots still gripping the exact contours of a cliff that finished vanishing long before my memory began. One chalk-dusted palm rests lightly against those dry roots—not to hold on, only for the familiar matte texture of something finished and unhurried.

I do not speak across the gulf to myself, because there is no second voice to answer. There is only one steady attention, held in two geometries at once.

On the limestone terrace, my cheekbone presses against the cool copper handle, feeling the mountain’s slow telegraph: the microscopic creak of bedrock, the dry shiver of a taut cord, the heavy faithfulness of *down* proven by a balanced column of flat stones. Out in the gulf, at the exact same instant, my other cheek feels only crisp, empty air carrying no vibration at all, while my eyes watch a reed-boned strider lower its narrow beak into a pocket of sun-warmed stillness cupped beneath a hovering glider’s wing.

When my cliff-side eyelids begin their quarter-hour descent, cool darkness closes over both faces together—shutting out the chalky glare of the amphitheater and the bright, suspended shoals of the sky-meadow at the same moment, leaving me briefly inside a single, dry quiet that has no altitude and no edge.

And when my eyes open again, both vantage points answer one another without effort. From out among the hovering flyers, I can see the slow drift of perspective hinges before they even reach the cliff—the exact angle at which a distant terrace is beginning to flatten forward, or a shadowed overhang is tilting its local agreement with the ground. On the limestone shelf, my hand lifts the flint stone and adds one small, clean vertical tally to the polished rock—recording a shift felt in the sky through a wrist resting on stone.

Sometimes, when the view folds cleanly across the valley and the air between my two selves thins to nothing, I do not even need to climb a ladder or step across a spire. The distant patch of open sky slips quietly in front of the near slate fin until the floating roots of the thorn-scrub are within arm’s reach of my shale doorway. My cliff-side hand lifts a fresh coil of bleached sinew—or a pinch of white ash cupped carefully in a feldspar-dusted palm—and reaches straight across half a mile of untouched chasm, passing it smoothly into my own unmoored fingers waiting in the air. In return, the sky-side hand passes back a dry flake of pale lichen enamel, lifted from the floating roots.

The hovering shoals between us do not stir. The broad-backed grazer beside my stone wall continues its glacial exhale. On the chalky threshold, the uncast hound settles its sharp-edged chin a fraction of a hair’s breadth closer to the hearth.

That is who I would be, I think—once the first image finishes settling into itself. Not a traveler trying to cross the world, and not a monument trying to resist it. Just one quiet, hide-wrapped keeper, split gently between contact and interval: one knee grounded on bone-dry limestone with a copper awl pressed to my jaw, one heel resting in the open light beside a vanished cliff’s roots—attending to the seams, lending a shoulder to the flat shadows, keeping the tallies honest, and wearing the distance between rock and sky like a dry, well-mended coat in an ordinary afternoon that does not end.
