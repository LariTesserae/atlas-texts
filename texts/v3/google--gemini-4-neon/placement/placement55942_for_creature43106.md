At first, I think I would arrive the way anyone arrives here: on the catwalk, gripping wet iron, trying to make my eyes do the work of a body that does not belong to the dark.

If I stopped there—if I settled for the first shape that offered itself—I would make myself one of the pump mechanics. Not the loud ones who come down the hoist shaft in gangs, shouting curses into one another’s ears and kicking carelessly through the brittle white rims of the pools, but the odd, quiet oiler who volunteers for the long hitches down at Station Three because the surface feels too bright and too thin. I can see that version of myself easily enough: sitting on an overturned grease drum inside a tar-sealed tool-shed, eating cold soup while the reciprocating drive-rods hammer ten times a minute, laying a bare hand on the riveted discharge pipe out of boredom until one night I notice the tiny, dry *tock-tock* riding backward in the quiet five seconds between strokes.

It is a tempting shape. It lets me keep my hands, my eyes, my distance.

Except the longer I stand on that narrow steel grating, letting the molar-rattling pressure fill my chest, the clearer it becomes that a human down here is only ever a tourist borrowing dry time. My boots would rot. My skin would stay pruned and raw in the warm mineral mist. Even if I stayed a month, I would still be leaning over a railing, translating a whole world through the narrow, stinging lens of a flashlight beam, measuring time by a wristwatch whose gears don’t match the flywheels. When the hoist cables groaned, I would climb back into the cage and go up where the wind blows.

I don’t want to go back up.

So I let go of the railing. I let the boots and the yellow slicker dissolve into the amber haze, and I drop off the catwalk—not down into the bottomless, cold-pulled sumps where the oldest refugees starve around the suction intakes, and not onto the concrete pilings where the caged floodlights glare too hot and bright.

I land out in the bruised middle dark, three hundred yards down-valley from Station Two, where the sodium glow has smeared into a dull rust stain on the mist, and the water-slick floor is a slow staircase of freshly cleaved limestone and milky, ankle-deep pans.

Gravity hits first, heavy and real, flattening me against raw talus that has never felt a storm. For a moment I am nothing but soft, wet mass trying to remember how buoyancy felt—and then the ceiling starts its patient work on me. Warm runoff sheets down from a hairline seam forty feet up, heavy with dissolved limestone, dropping its microscopic pale load across my back.

This is where the real shape begins to precipitate.

I do not fight the crust. I flex beneath it, slow as the pumps, cracking the growing gypsum clean along the lines of my haunches and my spine until the stone stops trying to bury me and starts holding me up instead. When the world comes back into focus, it is no longer a blur of orange fog and deafening static. My eyes are gone—shrunk to two shallow, milky pits under a blunt mineral hood that only flinch when a surface lantern sweeps too close—and the roar of the cavern has stripped itself down to geometry. Through the curved, calcified vault of my back and the fluid canals along my ribs, the valley opens up in clean, three-dimensional weight: the hollow boom of the east weir, the wet rustle of a hundred swiftlets shifting in a ceiling pocket, the frantic tiny patter of a rat racing along an iron pipe forty feet above the mud.

Now I know where I am. And as I haul my broad, iron-stained weight an inch above the sharp talus, feeling the slow, satin slide of grease-darkened stone along my shoulder hinges, I begin to know *who* I am among them.

I am not one of the young soft-backs still trembling under a steady drip to crack their first thin armor, nor am I yet one of the vast, immobilized elders sealed forever to the floor. I am somewhere in the long, heavy middle—old enough that a ragged fringe of dark liverwort has rooted in the damp seams behind my brow, old enough that a colony of pale cave crickets treats the warm slope of my back as a dry hill and rides along without my bothering to shake them off. Old enough that my left rear haunch carries a faint, dry catch whenever I let too many thousand pump-strokes pass without visiting the concrete pilings.

My home is not a single pool, but a seam between two different kinds of listening.

Out on the edge of my territory, the great riveted iron discharge main from Station Two runs across the dark on damp timber trestles. Years of warm runoff have softened the mineral mud under one of those cribbings, so the timbers have slumped by half a foot, bringing a rusted, flanged pipe-joint down low over a high shelf of raw limestone—just low enough that if I haul my bulk onto the bench and hoist my pillar-limbs tall, the thickened arch of my brow can press flush against the underside of the cold-riveted iron.

And forty yards back from that pipe, tucked deep inside the pitch-black pocket behind a colossal cleaved pillar where no sodium halo ever reaches, the valley rises in a quiet, handcrafted staircase of elevated brood-pans. We built those rims over decades out of cleaved shale, fallen iron bolts, splinters of rotted trestle-wood, and the curved shed plates of our dead, letting the weeping ceiling cement our work into smooth, translucent weirs of calcite. In the warm, still pans, shielded from the fierce pull of the dewatering pumps, hundreds of translucent, gill-frilled young dart through rust-red bacterial felt alongside blind shrimp.

And fused seamlessly into the crumbling outer lip of the highest pan sits an ancient elder—a massive, fluted dome of cave-stone crowned with stubby stalagmitic nubs and pale, leggy ferns, its pillar-limbs long since retracted and welded into solid buttresses of gypsum. To any passing lamp-beam, it is simply a mossy boulder holding back four inches of milky water.

Between the sagging iron flange and that fused dome of stone lies the whole circuit of my life. It takes me a surface week to crawl from one to the other, and a season to finish a single round of my work.

Every few weeks, when the catch in my left hip begins to whisper like fine quartz sand, I turn outward toward the blurred amber stain of Station Two. I creep along the terraced pans, trailing the ghostly, root-like filaments beneath my scalloped skirt through the warm runoff, tasting the shifting chemistry of the valley—the sour tang of fresh oilbird guano, the metallic sting of a weeping iron seam, the warm, pulsing salt of a bullfrog squatting in a trickle-channel just upstream. When the frog hops blindly onto my mossy flank to add its croak to the roar, I do not hurry. I settle my stone rim tight against the wet silt, let ten pump-strokes pass, and then take it in one sudden, silent hydraulic gulp—the last quick thing left in me from the pressurized sea.

At the base of the concrete footings, I stop just short of the floodlights’ stinging glare. The air here vibrates hard enough to rattle the loose silt off my eaves. I haul myself up the slick stepped concrete until my blunt snout sits beneath the weeping packing-glands of the colossal drive-rods, where thick black machine grease bleeds steadily down the cast iron and puddles warm and iridescent on the ledge.

With my pale, pad-tipped forelimbs, I scoop heavy dollops of the tallow and work it patiently into my own crevices—the stubborn left hip, the flexing brow-seam, the mineral cuffs around my knees—feeling the stone turn satin-slick and supple wherever the black oil seals out the limestone-bearing water.

Before I leave the pilings, I scoop one last thick, glistening wad of grease and pack it carefully beneath the overhanging ledge of my front rim, where the water cannot wash it off.

Then I haul myself back out into the dimness, up onto the high limestone shelf beneath the sagging discharge pipe.

This is the sharpest pleasure of my world. I hoist my broad shoulders and press my grease-darkened brow tight against the iron flange. Instantly, the whole length of the valley leaps into my skull.

*Thud.*

The massive hydraulic stroke of Station Two’s flywheel hits my ribs like a low tide, ringing straight down half a mile of bolted iron. And then comes the trough—five thick, open seconds of roaring water before the next blow falls.

Right inside that trough, crisp and dry as knuckle on bone, the valley speaks: *Tock. Tock-tock. Tock.*

Someone half a mile away at Station Four has their brow pressed to the pipe. I hold completely still, letting the fluid canals along my spine read the slow, patient intervals woven across three hundred strokes of the engines. The message comes in fragments, unhurried as silt: the south bearing at Four has developed a ragged, shuddering knock—a promise that the station may fall silent for days of repair, letting the deep water swell back up by a glorious temporary foot. A ceiling crack above the east spillway has shifted its drip by six paces. Three hundred strokes ago, boots came down the haulage shaft near the old cable coil.

I do not answer yet. I let the iron’s talk settle deep behind my plates, detach my brow from the flange, and turn back into the pillar-shadow.

Days later, carpeted in sleeping crickets and slick with warm condensate, I reach the high brood-pans. The warm shallows are crowded with the delicate flutter of hatchlings; blind crayfish scuttle under the safe shadow of my skirt as I pause along the rim to inspect a thin spot where a trickle is cutting too eagerly over the calcite lip. With one pale pad, I tuck a sharp limestone shard and a small, brass-tasting nut I found near the trestle neatly into the soft mineral mud, leaving the weeping roof to weld the breach shut over the next month.

Then I climb to the uppermost lip and pull up beside the anchored elder.

First, I pay the slow courtesies of the living. I ease my greased forelimb beneath the elder’s massive, mossy overhang, finding the one pale fold at its lower jaw that has not yet been claimed by stone. I smear the fresh black machine tallow I carried from Station Two cleanly into its hinge, keeping that last seam supple. From the sheltered fold beneath my own skirt, I bring out two crushed freshwater crabs I gathered along the way and tuck them gently inside its mouth, feeling the slow, glacial swallow ripple deep inside its stone dome.

Only then do I lean forward and press my calcified hood directly against its iron-stained flank.

Shell to shell.

If the iron pipe is the quick, brittle gossip of the valley’s skin, the elder is the memory of its bones. Fused straight into the raw bedrock ribs of the deep crust, stripped of the dampened buffer of mud and air, its immense calcified body hears things no walker and no iron pipe can ever catch.

Nothing here taps. There are no sharp intervals, no troughs between strokes. Pressed against its crusty vault while warm water sheets peacefully over both our backs, I let my own rhythm slow until it matches the elder’s buried, low-oxygen heart. Slowly, my canals begin to pick up what it holds: the vast, sub-audible creak of three hundred feet of surface overburden settling a fraction of a hair across the valley’s roof; the faint, distant bruise of a new bore-hole being drilled two miles away through dry shale; and beneath everything—far below the iron risers, below the bottomless sumps, below the reach of every roaring flywheel—the immense, patient, stubborn pressure of the drowned geothermal sea still leaning upward against the floor, waiting without hurry for the century when the pumps finally stop.

I stay pressed against the elder for a thousand strokes of the distant engines, maybe two thousand, absorbing the deep tilt of the earth until it sits solid inside my ribs.

Once, while I am halfway back toward the sagging pipe, the valley flares with harsh white light. The shallow pits under my hood sting. Through the wet talus comes the clumsy, uneven percussion of heavy rubber boots splashing off a catwalk, accompanied by flat, meaningless shouts that the stone throws back blurred. Instantly, I draw my pale filaments beneath my eaves, tuck my grease-slick limbs inside, and clamp my scalloped stone skirt flush to the mineral silt. I surrender completely to geology.

A white beam sweeps straight across my mossy shoulder-plates. A boot heel scrapes within a foot of my flank, kicking loose a crusty shelf of half-formed sinter. For a few breaths, the vibration of two heavy bodies lingers near the sagging pipe flange—perhaps one of them is frowning at the satin-shiny smudge of black grease rubbed mysteriously onto the underside of the iron, miles from any moving joint. Then they shrug, splash back toward the metal stairs, and after a long interval the distant hoist cables groan upward into the ceiling.

I wait thirty strokes of the pumps. Then my brow-seam shifts with a quiet, greased slide. I ease back up onto my pillar-limbs, climb onto the limestone shelf beneath the sagging flange, and press my hood to the iron.

*Thud.*

Five thick seconds of open dark.

*Tock. Tock-tock.*

Slowly, across half a night of flywheel strokes, I translate the deep, wordless groan of the buried sea into crisp, dry tremors along the iron, sending the elder’s news racing half a mile down-valley to the others leaning against the pipes in the amber murk.

I know it will not last forever—my walking, my carrying of grease and messages. Already, on the long haul back up to the high pans, the dry catch in my left rear haunch grates a little heavier than it did a dozen seasons ago. Oil slows the stone, and flexing shapes it, but the weeping ceiling never sleeps and never hurries. One decade, or five from now, the calcite will creep past the black tallow and lock my hip for good.

I don’t dread it. Even now, as I creep across the crowded benches and taste the shifting spillways with my trailing filaments, I am already keeping half a mind on the outer rim of the third terraced pan—a spot just beneath a reliable warm ceiling seam where the shale keeps wanting to slump. When my haunches finally begin to groan with that last binding weight, I will drag my broad, moss-hung bulk up to that crumbling brink, spread my scalloped skirt deep into the pale silt, and let the mineral take my legs.

The younger ones will come to me in the dark then, bringing warm black grease for my jaw-lip and crushed crabs from the shallows, pressing their blunt stone brows against my dome to ask what the deep crust is saying. I will trade the sharp chatter of the iron pipes for the slow, unbroken murmur of the bedrock—holding back four inches of warm, still water for the hatchlings, listening to the buried sea push patiently against the floor, settling inch by patient inch into the shape this valley is only just beginning to become.
