If you set me down on one of the perforated zinc catwalks, I would not stay upright for long.

Everything about that posture is wrong for this plateau. Up there, three feet above the sponge, the sun strikes straight into the eyes with nothing to break it, and the roar hits the skull as a flat, undifferentiated wall—too loud to think inside, too fast to answer. To stand tall here is to argue with the laminar wind and lose, to turn your skin red under the thin cobalt air while your fingers stiffen on the wet iron rail. You can only visit from the catwalk. You cannot live there.

I would want to get low. I would want to get out of the glare and off the hurried, brittle tempo of boots on grating—that strange, frantic staccato that passes overhead once or twice a season, rattling down through the driven steel pilings and gone before a single drop of water has time to swell and fall from a moss frond.

So if you look for me on the tableland, do not scan the silver threads of the walkways, and do not squint up into the hard blue after the solitary raptor riding the thermals. Look thirty yards off from a sunken concrete collector ring, in the northern third of the basin, where the slow sheet-flow begins to feel the distant pull of the escarpment.

Watch me long enough for the sun to swing a lattice tower’s thin shadow across the mire, and you will realize I am not a frost-heaved wedge of slate. I have shifted a body-length west against the light, leaving behind a broad, shallow gleam of dark water that the rebounding sphagnum is already stitching shut.

---

I would be one of the low, roofed ones—not young anymore, not yet ancient, somewhere in the long, unhurried middle of a century.

To be this is to live in two climates separated by a hand’s breadth of bog-cured keratin and saturated moss. Above my plates lies a world I have never actually looked at. I know the sky only as a dry weight pressing against the crimson sphagnum on my spine, pulling cold vapor up out of the seams so that my shell stays refrigerated and damp underneath. Along my dorsal ridge, pale clubmoss trails over the bevelled edges, and a cluster of fingernail-sized sundews roots in the older grooves, catching wind-blown midges I will never notice. I carry a small, intact acre of the plateau wherever I go, and in return it drinks the ultraviolet fire that would split me open.

Underneath my weeping eaves, it is always wet blue twilight.

Here, my skin is pale, cool, and tender as cartilage, scarcely a degree warmer than the peat water. My heart turns over once every minute and a half—a single, unhurried thud that matches the patient dripping of condensation off a transformer fin. When I lift one of my wide, rubbery pads to move forward, I do it slowly enough that the living mattress beneath me barely bruises. Tucked in the damp, sheltered creases of my underside, a small colony of translucent amphipods rides in my moving shadow, tickling faintly against my belly as we cross from one seepage-pocket to the next.

My glassy amber eyes point downward, hooded by my brow-plate, reading only the few inches of dark water and wet lichen directly beneath my chin. I have no use for horizons. Horizons are for creatures that live by light.

I live by the chord.

---

Where I am on the plateau has no name on any engineer’s map, only a signature in my ribs.

Every basin here sits inside its own exact interference pattern, and mine is one I have known for forty summers. To the southeast, a deep drop-shaft sends a steady subterranean throb straight through the fractured basalt, arriving in my right fluid-chamber a fraction of a millisecond before my left. Two kilometers east, a galvanized air-release stack lays a thin, dry hiss across the upper register. Closer at hand—half a day’s creep across the glowing sponge—sits a low concrete transformer shed whose dense electrical hum anchors the middle of the air like a warm stone. And underneath all of it, faint and wide and utterly unvarying from the north, runs the flat horizon-pressure of water sliding off the un-eroded cliff shelf into open air.

Together, they do not deafen me. They illuminate the terrain in solid, measurable relief.

I do not need to look up to know that a collector ring lies ahead; I feel the subterranean rumble sharpen and separate as I near the sunken concrete lip. I know when a bolt works loose on a lattice tower a mile upwind, introducing a tiny, bright chatter into the wind-song off the guy-wires. When a geared motor inside the windowless shed turns a sluice gate by a quarter-inch over the course of an afternoon, easing the draw on the buried turbines far below, the pitch of the whole basin dips by a hair—and it passes through my cartilaginous ribs the way a passing cloud’s shadow passes over a sunlit field.

This is the work I spend my weeks on: creeping up to the concrete lip of the intake ring, anchoring my broad pads firmly in the sphagnum against the tug of the converging sheet-flow, and unfolding my slender, sheltered inner arms from beneath my belly.

With my amber eyes inches from the rush, I slide my blackened comb-tips into the narrow vertical steel slots.

Everything the miles of sponge have slowly surrendered—wind-drifted valley pollen, drowned midges, torn fibers of sedge, molted husks, and thick orange blooms of iron-bacteria clinging to the rusting collars—packs itself tight against those grates. Over three hours, moving with the calm precision of a sluice gear, I comb a two-foot section clean and draw the mineral-rich paste back into the shade of my eaves. My pale amphipod passengers dart briefly out from my underside to snatch loose flecks from the current, then flick straight back into my shelter.

As the last of the matted silt comes free, the tea-clear water slips over the concrete lip a fraction faster, and the deep subterranean voice inside the shaft brightens by a single, clean hairline. I have eaten for the week; the hidden machine beneath the stone breathes a little easier. Neither of us planned the arrangement, and neither of us rushes it. We simply keep each other tuned.

---

Yet if you asked me *who* I would be here—not merely what body I would wear, or how I would feed—the answer is found late in the afternoon, when the sun drops toward the snow-streaked peaks beyond the escarpment and the steel shadows stretch thin across the bog.

I would be one of the listeners who lingers at the iron.

There is something in me that has always distrusted shouting—the urge to add more noise to a room that is already full, to throw breath against a ceiling of sound that swallows every syllable two feet from the mouth. On this plateau, that instinct becomes anatomy. To speak here, you do not push sound outward. You take a narrow piece of the plateau’s roar into yourself and hold it still.

When I finish at the screens, I make my slow way to the shaded flank of the transformer shed, or to a galvanized pipe piling driven straight through the peat into the sharp young basalt beneath. I pull my waterlogged bulk flush against the concrete, or wrap my wide forepads around the cold zinc collar, and press my wet mass directly into the vibrating structure.

Instantly, inside my ribs, I feel the shrill upper overtone enter my saturated sphagnum and my heavy cartilage—and stop.

I drink that single frequency out of the shed’s voice. I hold that clean, narrow absence steady for twenty minutes, letting the dense hum run thinned and hollowed down through the pilings and out through the unbroken iron web and unweathered stone. Two kilometers across the blinding green-gold expanse, another mound pauses, lowers its heavy chin-plate to a sharp outcrop of frost-heaved slate, and feels the missing harmonic arrive through the bedrock.

I am not telling a long story. I am carving a small, deliberate room of quiet inside an inescapable engine and holding the door open: *Here. Leaning against the east wall. The intake screen runs clear; the northern wind has not shifted; I am still holding the note.*

Then I ease my weight back from the concrete and let the transformer ring full again. I wait, chin resting on a cold, crustose-lichened edge of rock, my heartbeat turning over once, twice, twenty times, until a lower register drops clean out of the distant air-release stack to the west and stays muted for half an hour. An answer. Across miles of empty, blinding glare, two dampened chords crossing in the dark stone beneath the moss.

---

Once every several summers, I leave the open chords altogether and creep into the narrow lee behind the transformer blockhouse.

It is the only true pocket of shade in my basin, a thin wedge where the roar drops from a blow to a heavy, manageable press, and cold silver droplets gather endlessly along the concrete cooling fins. One or two others have already arrived, or arrive over the next few days, drawn by the same slow thinning in the hum.

We back carefully against the warm, humming stone and press our curved, moss-armored flanks together edge-to-edge until our roofs seal the gap against the bare-metal sky and the biting wind. Inside that dark, dripping vault, the world shrinks to three things: the steady vibration radiating out of the concrete behind us, the quiet *tick* of condensation falling into wet sphagnum, and the pale, soft-backed newborn huddled between our sheltered knees.

It has no armor yet, no tuned ribs, nothing to stand between its translucent skin and the brutal sun. So in the dark, while the hum of the shed cradles it, I unfold my slender inner arms and reach up to the wet grooves of my own back. With infinite care, I pluck small live sprigs of crimson sphagnum, pale runners of clubmoss, and flakes of yellow-grey lichen from the channels of my own roof, and press them gently into the soft, sticky seams forming along the young one’s spine.

Over the months, we tend that living skin together in the shadow of the machine, feeding the small one minced iron-paste and waiting until its moss takes root and thickens enough to drink the sun on its own. When we finally part our flanks and let it creep out into the glare—a dark, newly roofed mound scarcely larger than a hunched stone, feeling its way by sound toward the nearest collector ring—something of my own back walks away with it across the plateau.

---

I know how this ends, though I am in no hurry to reach it.

Already, in one of the rear seams of my shell, a tough woody thread of dwarf crowberry has pushed down between two older plates. It barely stiffens my stride yet, and my rib-chambers are still clear; I can still pick out the crisp corona spit of the ceramic insulator bells half a mile away, still catch the subtlest twenty-minute muting from a distant piling. I have decades of listening left.

Still, I know that one summer—forty years from now, or sixty—the high overtones will begin to blur. The fine acoustic relief of the plateau will slowly flatten back into mere weight, the subtle absences of distant kin dissolving into the unbroken bass rumble of the shafts.

Most of us, when that happens, turn inward toward the deepest fault-depressions, where the living sponge lies ten feet thick over raw stone, and simply stop moving until the sphagnum climbs their bevelled edges and closes them quietly into the acidic dark below.

I don’t think I will turn inward.

Even now, whenever I pause on the northern edge of my circuit, I feel that flat, steady horizon-pressure in my forward ribs—the wide curtains of cold water sliding straight off the sharp basalt shelf into thin air. When my stride narrows and the high notes finally fade, I think I will spend two or three slow summers dragging my crowberry-laced weight northward, following that deepening white thunder until the sponge runs out and my pads meet the wet, unrounded lip of the rock.

There, at the edge of the drop nobody can see the bottom of, I will settle flat across the stone shelf and let the sheet-flow pour unbroken across my back. The plateau will weld me to the escarpment with fresh copper moss and pale lichen until I am no longer a traveler at all, only a dark, permanent swell along the rim—splitting the falling water into twin silver sheets, vibrating forever at the pitch of the abyss.

Even at the end, I would still be doing the only thing I ever really learned to do up here: leaning my whole weight into the torrent, and changing its voice by one quiet, lasting note.
