My first instinct is to wade in on two legs, moving as carefully as a body with lungs can move—holding my breath near the sunken benches, lifting my knees slowly so the mud stays settled, trying to be a polite guest in a dark room.

It doesn't hold for ten minutes. Even in imagination, I can feel how loud a human body would be here. Too hot, too fast, too full of sloshing weight. Every step tears a jagged seam across the black mirror; every exhale throws a clumsy gust of wet heat against walls that only understand stillness. And the worst part wouldn't be hostility—there is none. It would be that vast, patient, impersonal courtesy: the water flattening around my calves while a colonnade quietly slides farther off, the canal curb turning gently against my heel until I find myself back out on the empty steaming flats, escorted away from a conversation I was never quiet enough to hear.

If I am really in this world, I do not arrive with boots.

For a while I wonder if I would want to be one of the elders—broad as an overturned skiff, cemented into the tiered plinth above a scalding vent, holding a whole vertical metropolis intact in the reflection just by keeping my breath steady. There is a deep pull to that kind of usefulness, that immense thermal patience. To take raw, near-boiling surge from beneath the flagstones and temper it into a gentle, waveless tide so the whole basin stays just past fever and never boils dry.

But when I try to settle into that bulk, I feel too light for it. I don't have centuries of travertine crust welded to my flanks. I don't have the anchored certainty of a body that has stopped gliding because it has already found the one vent it was meant to govern.

Nor do I quite fit the perimeter benches of the sunken reservoirs—the nine citizens spaced as if measured by a cord, turning their pleated fronts inward for tide after tide to precipitate a new sill or soften an old viaduct back into slurry. I want to listen to them. I feel the tug of their barometric grammar—that velvety shift against the outer skin when a fold parts by a knife-blade, the slow wait for a single hollow *thup* to answer from the opposite curb. Yet when I imagine taking a permanent seat in that ring, holding one exact posture until the clay hardens around my stillness, something in me stays narrow. Something in me wants the straight lines between the plazas.

That is where I find myself: not in the grand reservoirs at the center, and not anchored over the vents, but out along the arrow-straight aqueducts and the half-drowned drainage channels where the city thins into emptiness.

I begin to feel my own shape only when I stop trying to look at it and let the heat press against it instead.

I am narrow—no wider than an embrace, pleated tightly like a closed fan, chalk-grey and yielding beneath a thin crust of wet lime. I have no face to turn toward the gloom, no eyes to strain for a horizon that isn't there. What I have is surface, and gradient, and the quiet hydraulic tension inside my chambers. I know the world by where the fever presses hardest against my flanks, by the slow upward creep of condensation threading its way up my vertical creases toward the cool dark dimple at my crown, and by the thickness of the mineral film beneath my hem.

At first I think I am simply one of the couriers of climate—those slender travelers who glide out to the unseen outer marches, seal away a sharp pocket of cold, dry air among the undisturbed reeds, and carry it miles down the straight waterways to release into an inner reservoir.

And I am that, mostly. I know the pleasure of the straight canals: waiting for the silent swell of the hot tide to lift beneath the flagstones, widening my base by a hair's breadth to drink a thin lens of warm mineral water, and slipping forward without a ripple, riding the subtle draw of the basin like a bead of oil sliding down slate. I know the discipline of keeping every fold drawn shut across miles of saturated dark so that not half a degree of the borrowed chill leaks out into the steam before I reach the steps where it is needed.

Yet as I stay with this shape, something more particular emerges—something that separates me slightly from the couriers who run clean errands from rim to center and back again.

I am drawn to the places where the geometry slips.

Out on the wide flats, where a straight canal loops back to a cistern that had six sides yesterday and has five today; out where a solitary viaduct has thinned in the middle between one thermal swell and the next, leaving two rounded stubs facing each other across an unbroken sheet of black water—that is where I slow down, drop my hem onto the slick stone, and linger through a tide I wasn't strictly scheduled to spend there.

I don't build walls. I am too slender, too brief in my staying, to pool enough stillness to cure a bench out of simmering silt. And I don't carry the sour, steam-thick condensate that softens old piers back into mud. My craft is smaller, and stranger.

Between tides, when the basin sinks into its dead slack and the water turns as flat and tight as dark tin, I sometimes wedge myself into the narrow throat of a sluice gate, or halt midway across an empty drowned plaza where two larger tenants sit fifty paces apart, their pleated fronts angled just slightly away from one another, their deliberations stalled by a stretch of overly hot air rising from a hairline crack in the flagstones between them.

I do not speak first. I only hold still until the ripples from my arrival die completely—until the water around my base stops moving and becomes a mirror again.

And then I feel it: the sudden, weightless drop beneath my sole.

While I am gliding down an aqueduct, I am almost nothing—a tall, narrow crease of cool parchment-clay moving through warm dark, carrying a sealed breath that isn't even mine. Moving breaks the sheen; in motion, there is only the sparse, low basin of mud and vapor.

But when I come to dead rest in the slack, the water beneath me opens downward into a height the air refuses to hold. I cannot see it with eyes, yet I feel it through the cool tension along my submerged hem, the way a stone arch feels the weight of the gallery it supports. Above the surface, I am modest and solitary. Below the surface, hanging upside down into the dense, lightless archive beneath the flats, my folds do not stop at my crown.

They do not rise into the stacked towers of an elder, nor spread into the vaulted chamber of a reservoir quorum. They extend *sideways*.

Out from my narrow crown, deep in the inverted dark, long cantilevered pleats unfold across the empty avenue—a slender, perforated footbridge hanging upside down between two much larger canopies that could not otherwise reach each other across the mud. Nothing is cemented. Nothing is carved. While I stay motionless in the slack, holding the surface quiet and parting one vertical crease by a knife-blade to let a single thread of dry cold sink between the two older forms, the inverted bridge holds. Across it—through barometric weight, through the faintest tremor in the water-skin, through a single drop brimming over my crown dimple and striking the oil-black sheet with a clean, hollow *thup*—something passes from one side of the plaza to the other. A measure of chill. A shift in posture. A disagreement about whether the next courtyard's rain should fall an hour earlier, or three arches east.

I am not the archive, and I am not the assembly. I am the span that only exists while someone needs to cross.

Sometimes, when a roofless courtyard is filling with its strict geometric rain, I glide just to the unseen line of the threshold—half inside the vertical fall, half out in the heavy, motionless air. The warm sheets wash the excess travertine grit from one side of my pleats while the other side stays slick only with upward-creeping dew. Inside the ring, six or eight larger tenants sit murmuring in slow drops and shifts of weight. I don't enter their circle. I simply sit in the doorway, narrow as a jamb, feeling the crisp boundary where falling water ends and dry steam begins, letting my crown drink the runoff while I wait for one of them to release a small, sharp parcel of cold air meant for another courtyard three miles down the embankment.

When it comes—a dense, cellar-cool pressure rolling silently across the black water into my open fold—I take it in, draw my pleats tightly shut over it, and wait for the flags beneath me to warm.

Soon the tide begins to rise again, without waves, lifting half a foot up the worn steps with a barely audible lip-smack. The mirror breaks. Down in the water, the slender footbridge detaches cleanly from both canopies and folds back into my narrow frame, leaving no trace in the mudstone except a thin, pale crescent of chalk-silt that is already dissolving in the warm swell.

I widen my underside by the width of a hair, catch the thin lens of hot mineral water, and glide out through the archway into the blind dark—carrying another piece of cold weather toward a cistern that may have four sides by the time I reach it.

And far off, at the end of however many soft, uncounted tides it takes for travertine grit to finally stiffen my creases until I can no longer ride the swell, I know where I will go. Not into the grand flooded vaults at the center of the city. I will make my last slow, capillary passage—minutes to cross a single flagstone—back to one of those quiet junctions where two narrow canals meet beneath a low, weeping arch. I will press my stiffening flanks into the inner corner of the stonework where the current turns, rounding out the bend by a hand's breadth so the next tide slips through a little cleaner, a little quieter, than before.

And just before the pale lime closes over me altogether and I become nothing more than a velvety swell in the curb, I will feel the thin, translucent inner lining peel gently away from my hardened shell—flat, lightless, clinging to the cool shade under the arch, pulsing faintly whenever the hot tide rises over it, gathering tiny reserves of coolness in the dark until it is thick enough to crease itself narrow and slip back out into the straight water.
