At first, I think I want to be one of the three.

It is an easy pull to feel. Standing in the bright green roar of that twenty-pace floor, there is something almost unbearable in the completeness of them—the way their blood has surrendered half its labor to the buried machinery, the way they hold no memory of panic, no concept of a storm, no clutter of half-rotted things left over from yesterday. To lean my bare spine against a fluted root-column, draw pale inner lids shut over my eyes, and widen my ribs into the single overtone that binds the engines and the frogs into one glassy chord—there is a part of me that recognizes that impulse immediately. To belong to a carrier wave so wholly that even dying is only a slow turning into pale ochre stone, another smooth buttress helping the room hold its acoustics.

Even the finger-long juvenile darting through the rust-velvet channel beneath the sedge has a kind of peace I envy—waiting out its slow years in the warm shallows, already ticking to the double-stroke below, knowing without thinking that eventually the elder will seal itself to a pillar and there will be room for one more upright shape.

But when I try to stay inside that image, something in me pushes back against the water.

If I were one of the three, I would not know that the room is a room. I would not know that the leaf-dome has an upper side where cold rain strikes and rolls off into black mud, or that the fern curtain opens onto anything other than a featureless nothing where blood thickens and stops. I would not look at the crisp diamond standing waves and find them strange or beautiful; I would only feel them with my palms and remove whatever snagged their lines.

And I am not the booted wanderer, either. I know the feeling of walking downhill until the vibration stops feeling like distance and starts feeling like something directly underfoot—but the wanderer has dry wool on their shoulders, a hurried mammal heart, and a road somewhere uphill waiting for them to return. They are a gust of outside weather. If I pushed through those slick aerial roots and stepped down the spongy bank into that bathhouse roar, I would not walk back out.

So if I cannot surrender my pulse to the floor, and I cannot bring myself to leave the hum behind, where do I actually land?

Not in the open twenty paces of bright green daylight. And not out in the shifting, wind-raked bog.

I find myself *inside the wall*.

---

From the center of the chamber, the perimeter looks solid—an unbroken, century-braided palisade of fused trunks, fibrous lianas, and dense cushions of weeping moss knitted so tightly that not a sliver of outside sky gets through. From the outside bog, it looks like nothing at all, just the thickest, ugliest snarl of deadfall and root-tangle at the bottom of the slope.

Only something small enough to live between the two knows that the wall is nearly two paces thick, and that it is not solid. It is a dark, wet, labyrinthine seam—a living membrane of overlapping bark, cramped pockets of peat, hollow knots where an older trunk rotted cleanly away inside the grip of younger roots, and narrow vertical chimneys slick with warm condensation.

That is where I wake into the world.

Not standing upright, because there is nowhere here to straighten a long spine. I am compact, narrow-hipped, built to thread tight, wet corridors of wood without snagging. As I settle into the dark of the braid, my body takes the shape of a gradient. Nothing in this seam belongs wholly to one climate, and neither do I.

My back, shoulders, and the outer ridges of my arms—the surfaces that turn toward the bog—are tough, low-sheened, and mottled dark as waterlogged alder bark and old peat, indifferent to cold seepage and the occasional needle of winter draft. My throat, my belly, and the long, pale insides of my forearms—the surfaces I press toward the inner moss—are thin, bare, and nearly as translucent as willow-skin, warm-seeking and lightly glazed along the knuckles and flat shins with pale ochre mineral salts where the charged groundwater leaks outward through the wood.

I do not have the pale, lifted static filaments of the three; out in the open air of the pocket they would be a halo, but inside the tight tangle of lianas they would only snag and tear. Instead, short, fine sensory bristles line the undersides of my jaw and the backs of my wrists, twitching lightly whenever the electrostatic charge on the far side of the moss builds toward a spark. And I have no smooth oval ear-drums recessed behind my jaw—the roar does not reach me through open air here, not cleanly. What I have are broad, flattened, uncalloused pads across my palms, the soles of my long-toed gripping feet, and the soft hollow of my sternum.

I read the world through wood.

Because the century-braided palisade is fused straight into the root-mat below and the domed canopy above, everything that happens on *either* side of the wall travels through the trunks I cling to.

Press my flat cheek and both palms inward against the damp inner timbers, and the massive double-stroke of the buried engines rises straight into my teeth—muffled by a meter of wet fiber from a turbine roar into a deep, steady, bearable purr. Through the same wood come the finer vibrations: the faint, glassy patter of heavy drops falling from the leaf-dome onto the floor-pool; the brassy, seamless tremor of dozens of marsh frogs clinging to the moss flutes an inch past my knuckles; and now and then, soft as distant rain on a roof, the crisp, tapped cadences the three send into the central root-columns when they lay their wet palms against the bark to speak.

Roll my shoulders back half a hand's breadth, pressing my spine against the outer shell of fused trunks, and an entirely different tempo enters my bones: the slow, irregular groan of wind leaning into the bog-trees; the ragged scatter of rain hammering the outside of the leaf-dome; the dry, brittle tick of a cold autumn night tightening the outer bark with frost.

That is the thing I am made of—not the steady state, and not the storm, but the slow interference pattern between them.

Even my heartbeat is double-natured. When I wedge myself deep into the warm inner folds behind the weeping moss, curled close enough to smell hot copper, crushed fern, and the faint dry prickle of ozone, my pulse slackens and stretches, trying to fall in step with the twin hydraulic blows below. It almost catches—beats two, three, four strokes in clean phase—before slipping half a breath off-tempo again. When I creep outward to a damp crease near the exterior bark to drink cold, un-mineralized rainwater off a bruised fern-stem, my heart quickens, alert and erratic, answering the shifting sky. I could not survive on the bright floor without snagging its geometry, and I would not last a week out in the open lowlands without the buried warmth keeping the frost out of my marrow. I only hold together right here, in the strip where heat meets cold and turns to mist.

---

Nothing in this pocket is idle, and though the room does not know it has a seam-dweller, the seam has its own quiet work.

Inside the twenty-pace floor, nothing ever changes. That permanence looks effortless from the middle of the water, like a law of nature, but permanence against a living marsh is never effortless; it is maintained at the boundary.

When a late-winter gale tears across the lowlands and twists an old bog-ash leaning against the palisade, a finger-wide split opens high in the outer braid. Cold, sharp air plunges through the dark pockets of my wall like a blade. If it reaches the inner moss, the temperature along that fluted groove will drop three degrees; the frogs clinging there will stiffen, slip a hair flat of the electrical overtone, and get plucked off the wall one by one until the whole wall of song thins. I feel the cold draft before it crosses the halfway mark of the timber. I climb through the wet labyrinth hand over hand, gather clumps of loose sphagnum and half-rotted liana fiber from the outer hollows, chew them wet, and pack the split tight from the inside, bracing my back against the shuddering trunk until the swollen wood drinks the rain and seals itself shut again.

In the wet months, when the surrounding mire rises and tries to press dark, sour, uncharged bog-water straight through the lower roots, I spend long stretches down in the flooded bottom seams, clearing pale calcite crusts from the tiny seepage channels so the pressure vents cleanly upward into the warm mineral-wash instead of bursting through the floor-mat in a muddy boil. In the dry months, I nip back the pale, thirsty tips of outside briar-roots that smell the bathhouse humidity and try to punch straight through the weeping moss into the open air.

I do not do any of this out of devotion to the three, or out of reverence for the buried engines. I do it because the gradient is my house. If the wall opens too wide, the cold kills my warmth; if the wall calcifies shut from top to bottom, the dark corridors seal up and I have nowhere to move and nothing to drink from the rain-side. I feed on whatever gets caught in the filter between worlds: cold-bog beetles and damp spiders drawn inward by the steady heat until the ozone makes them sluggish; pale, rubbery shelf-fungi that only fruit in the exact lukewarm twilight halfway through the braid; mineral-sweet droplets beaded along the inner fibers; the occasional fallen wren-feather or shed frog-skin washed into a dark crevice.

And sometimes, when the work is done, I climb to one of my two or three narrow slits—places where two vertical flutes of weeping moss part by scarcely the width of a thumb—and I look in.

---

From a thumb-wide slit in the wall, the chamber looks like something held inside a green glass lantern.

Even after years, the brightness of it makes my dark-adapted eyes narrow. The leaf-filtered daylight falls so evenly over the four stout pillars and the glassy diamond lattice of the floor that there are almost no shadows to tell you what hour it is outside. I watch the sleekest of the three wade without breaking the ripples, pinch a stray yellow leaf-rib from the water, crack two tiny snails squarely inside the hydraulic beat, and tuck the bare stem into the warm sphagnum. I watch the heavily crusted elder lie half-submerged in its rust-velvet channel while a damp-feathered wren lands on its calcified knee and snatches iridescent midges out of the vertical ribbons drawn up by the static field.

I am closest to them—closer than I ever dare get—during the slow, unscheduled tides when all three gather around the central root-columns.

I feel it coming before they move, as a faint tightening along the bristles of my wrists, a subtle rise in the static pressure clinging to the wet bark. Then even the elder lifts its heavy, verdigris-veined weight from the shallow channel and moves to a pillar. All three press their bare spines, their heels, and the smooth backs of their skulls directly against the fluted wood. Their pale inner lids slide shut.

I press my flat palms, my chin, and the whole thin length of my chest straight into the inner wall of my crevice.

When their narrow ribs expand and that deep, wet, bone-shaking resonance swells out of their chests to lock into the hidden machinery, it does not merely fill the open air of the chamber. It pours straight down the root-columns, across the floor-mat, and up through every fused trunk of the palisade.

My whole dark corridor hums like the belly of a cello. The loose droplets clinging to the outer bark leap free and fall in unison. Every jagged memory of outside wind drops out of the wood. For several long minutes, my erratic, two-hearted pulse snaps cleanly into the double-stroke—effortless, unhurried, held inside a chord I could never have sung on my own. I close my eyes in the dark behind the moss and let the room borrow my bones.

Then they let the note ebb back into the roaring baseline. The wood loosens beneath my palms. Somewhere out beyond my spine, a gust of rain hits the upper leaves, and my heartbeat slips off the tempo again, small and separate and awake.

---

They do not know me, not in any way that would make a story between us.

Once in a long while, the one who tends the wall glides over to stand an arm's length from my narrow slit, lifting its pale forearm toward a dripping frond to bleed off the charge prickling across its slick skin. *Tick.* The tiny violet needle snaps into its dark-tipped finger; its lifted halo of filaments settles with quiet relief.

And just once—maybe twice in a long stretch of seasons—its pale filaments do not settle evenly. A few fine threads along its bare shoulder lean a millimeter inward toward the weeping moss, right where my uneven warmth and ragged pulse sit hidden behind a curtain of damp green flutes.

I freeze instantly, flattening my spine into the dark bark, holding my breath on the quiet interval between the twin hydraulic blows.

The pale creature turns its smooth, earless head a fraction of an inch. One wide, copper-ringed eye fixes on the vertical groove of moss inches from my face; the taut oval of skin behind its jaw tightens, listening through the roar for whatever small, off-tempo snag sits behind the wall. For two heavy strokes of the engines below, we are separated by less than a hand's width of living moss.

Then its translucent inner lids blink once, slowly. Whatever it reads through the wood is too faint, too deeply folded into the palisade, to mar the standing waves on the floor. Just a knot in the fused trunks. Just a pocket of trapped rainwater warming slowly toward equilibrium. It turns its smooth shoulder and glides back toward the pillars.

---

The only creatures I ever truly follow are the rare ones who push through the fern curtain from outside.

It happens scarcely once in a dozen winters—someone wandering too far down into the bottomlands, following the strange hum rising through the soles of their boots until they part the drooping aerial roots and step down the spongy bank into the deafening, bright green room.

I always hear them coming first, long before the three do. Through the outer bark of my wall I feel the clumsy, heavy thud of leather boots squelching through the soft bog, completely off-tempo, snapping dry reeds. I slip silently through the damp corridors of the braid until I am tucked just above the entrance curtain, peering down through the tangled root-fibers.

I watch them stand frozen in the ankle-shallow pool, their boots smashing the diamond weave into sloppy rings, their dry clothes drinking the static unevenly and bending the nearest midge-ribbon out of plumb. I watch the sleekest of the three glide over, tilt its smooth head to listen to the frantic knock of their chest, touch a crisp violet spark to the back of their wrist, and kneel to coax the water back into tidy geometric lines around their leather soles—folding them politely into the architecture until they go away.

I watch their face while it happens. That stunned, quiet ache of standing inside something that neither needs you nor fears you, running in loud, warm, self-contained perfection while you feel abruptly temporary and loud.

And when they finally back toward the entrance, part the heavy fern fronds, and climb clumsily up the spongy bank into the cold, silent marsh—carrying the faint smell of hot copper and crushed fern out into the wind on their sleeves—I wait two beats of the floor.

Then I slip down through the lowest seam of the braided wall, just behind the drooping curtain where the warm mist meets the cold outside mud. With my long, damp, bark-colored fingers, I scoop away the stray smear of cold bog-clay their boots scraped onto the spongy bank, smooth the bruised fern-fronds back into overlapping rows so the outside chill stops spilling through, and retreat into the dark thickness of the trunks.

Behind me, the green room holds its glassy pattern without a ripple out of place. Before me, the wind shifts in the lowlands and brings the smell of rain. I wedge myself neatly into the warm-cold hollow between them, press one palm to the humming wood and one shoulder to the storm, and listen to both.
