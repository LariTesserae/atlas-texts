If I wake into this world, I do not wake seeing it. I wake feeling a slow lift under my heels—twenty feet up, twenty feet down—and the long, hollow groan of something colossal taking a breath beneath me.

The strange thing is that the absence of ground does not frighten me first. What would undo me is the other thing: the Iron-Peaks down in the root-dark, that raw, unyielding shale that refuses to give an inch under a boot. I understand the Walkers’ nausea on bare rock. Stillness feels deaf to me, too. A place made entirely of tension—nothing holding anything up except the pull of one living thing against another across empty air—makes an immediate, unsettling kind of sense.

So before I know who I am here, I know *where* I am not.

I am not down on the Teeth with a horn wedge and a basket, hammering copper out of sweating basalt while slabs crack loose in the dark. And I am not crouched frozen behind a bark-ridge for six hours with a greased sinew-net and a spear, waiting for the exact pitch of wind across a glider’s wings so I can yank a slip-trigger and wrestle fifteen feet of claws onto a swaying arch. I admire the nerve of the trappers and the scramblers the way you admire a lightning strike—from inside shelter, grateful someone else has the stomach for it.

At first, wandering through the dark of this place in my mind, I think I might not be human at all.

I crawl behind a lashed glider-hide baffle into the belly of a great bow, out of the scouring gale, where the roar drops to a steady thrum in the teeth and the curved walls weep warm mineral dew. And I look up into the quietest stretch of the ceiling, far from the biting draft of the vent-slits, and I see the **chord-sleepers**.

I linger there a long time. Longer than I expected to.

There is something almost unbearably recognizable in them: nearly blind, draped in damp pale fur, hanging tucked inside the hollow architecture of a continent they did not grow, their soft bellies pressed straight against the pressurized veins of the wood. They never go out into the bruised sky. They do not hunt the void or chart the miles. All they do is listen to the organ-pipe note pouring through the vent-slit and hum it back—matching the chamber’s exact frequency, a trembling bass purr that tells every exhausted stranger who crawls in out of the frost: *this arch is sound. The sap is still rising. You can unclip your harness and sleep.*

And if something deep in the hidden roots goes sour—days before any human ear catches the split—they do not argue or panic loudly. They simply stop humming, unhook their claws, and scramble for the slit.

For a while, I think: *That’s it. That’s what I would be.* Not a hero of the spans, not a name sung in the Span-Count. Just a small, warm instrument tucked into the ribs of a living structure, waking whenever the air moves through the chamber, measuring the truth of the tension by answering its note.

And yet—when I try to stay up there on the dripping ceiling, hooking my claws into the pith—something pulls me back down to the floor of the tunnel.

A chord-sleeper only matches the note it is given. It cannot leave a mark for the person coming two days behind in a blinding mist-front. It cannot braid a three-hundred-mile landscape into greased sinew so a freezing thumb can read where the water hasn’t soured. It cannot chip a sliver of slate until the wind itself speaks clearly across half a mile of black air.

I realize I want hands. Calloused along the heel and inside the thumb, smelling faintly of resin-peat and iron sap, maybe missing a tip to frost or a frayed line, clumsy in bare stillness and fluent in the dark.

---

So I am a Walker.

Not one of the boldest ones—not the kind of lone Chime-walker who casts a grapnel blind across a widened chasm just to see what holds on the other side, testing a fresh hand-rope with their full weight over three thousand feet of nothing while the dry gale screams. I would not last a season playing daredevil with the high windward crowns.

If I am a **Chime-walker**, I am the quieter half of a bonded pair—or the kind who moves slower, lingering at the tangled middle-tier junctions where three or four great bows cross out of the worst wind-shear and the acoustics turn knotted and deceptive.

That is where I find myself: not at the very top of the lattice, where the sky is a thin indigo bruise and the rime builds fins on every wire, and never down near the sulfurous roots. Somewhere in the middle depths, where the mist-sails unfurl in rolling waves and the world is a dense, overlapping web of intersecting chords. Where a single careless tap on a guy-vine bounces off five different ribs and comes back sounding like a warning when it was only a greeting.

My work is not speed. It is sorting the chord.

I can feel the posture of it already—knees bent, hips riding the slow twenty-foot swell without thinking about it, a half-tamed hearth-wren tucked warm against my collarbone inside the high collar of my coat, its tiny claws hooked into the wool. At a junction where two bows fuse, I clip both leash-lines tight, drop to one knee on the leeward curve, pull back the edge of my frost-rimed hood, and lay my bare cheek straight against the pleated bark.

This is the moment I live for in this world.

Out in the open air, the lattice is just roar—booming wells and whining wires and breaking ice. Pressed against the wood, it separates. You hear the slow, sluggish *thump-hiss* of mineral sap climbing from some unseen mountain fracture two miles down—a habit I’d have learned from watching the sap-tappers, who have the same patience and the same respect for not bleeding a span dry. Beneath that, you hear the bow’s fundamental tone as a gust leans against its flank: clean, deep, settling back into balance like a heavy pendulum.

Unless it doesn’t.

Unless, half a second after the gust passes, a thin, sour shudder lingers inside the wood—a flat buzz you could miss entirely if you were hurrying toward a warm peat-pan. An internal split, or a holdfast beginning to lose its grip on a frost-pried slab far below.

That is the thing I am built to catch. Not the dramatic collapse, not the thunderous *crump-crack* after a square mile has already dropped—the tiny dishonesty in the pitch three weeks before the fall.

---

When the dry wind climbs too fierce to work outside, you will find me just behind the lashed hide baffle of a rib-chamber, sitting near enough to the vent-slit to hear the traffic of the guy-vines, far enough back that the chord-sleepers overhead still purr down through the damp dark. I would always leave them the best strips of dried gristle tucked into the upper seams. I would know the difference between their sleepy, full-bellied hum and the tight, thinned vibration they make when the pressure shifts.

Between my knees sits a split-bone vise holding a freshly pared strip of pale inner cambium. I do not add peat to the suspended stone pan; the ember stays a small, hooded orange eye. Instead, I turn the wooden peg of the **strain-lamp** one notch, and then another.

The living fibers wake in a cold, trembling sulfur-green glow—barely wide enough to hold my hands and my lap, paid for strictly in torsion. Every few minutes I turn the peg another click to keep the light alive, knowing that soon enough the strip will reach its limit, snap with a soft *tick*, and go dark forever. There is no light here that doesn’t come from something being pulled taut.

By that brief, ghostly gleam, I do the small, stubborn repairs that keep a shifting continent legible.

I unroll my leather bundle of slate slivers and scraped glider wing-bones, bound silent in waxed cord. With a dull copper point, I chip the edge of a wet slate tab, hold it by a sinew thread, and tap it lightly with a bone striker. Too sharp. Another tiny flake off the corner—dust falling onto my greased mittens—and another tap. Now it sits right in the interval: clean, dry, unmistakable even if a spore-burst cuts visibility to arm’s length and the fog coats every cable in slick gray grease.

I also work on **knot-cords**—my own, coiled tight around my left forearm, and the cords of whatever strand I have fallen in with for a few marches. This is my favorite trade within the trade. When two strands meet in the dark, huddled shoulder-to-shoulder on a leeward rib passing a warm bladder of vein-sour, I am the one who holds out my bare hands to read their sinew and let them read mine.

Under the green stain of the strain-lamp, I splice in the news of the season: undo the smooth copper bead that marked a cistern-gall gone bitter after a tremor; tie in a jagged slate chip where a shroud-glider pair has taken to roosting beneath the eastern span; space two notched bone rings a finger-width apart so a traveler running ahead of a freeze knows the exact count of hand-tallies to the nearest baffled chamber. You do not write histories on parchment here; ink blurs in mist, and nobody has light to read it by. You tie the truth into a line a freezing person can feel without taking their eyes off the dark—because their eyes were useless anyway.

---

There is one part of the work I would never grow brisk about, no matter how many circuits I walked.

Every so often, approaching a junction in the indigo gloom, I hear the clean three-note clatter of a way-chime I hung myself two seasons ago—and tucked right beneath it, fluttering out of time, a single thin, off-key rattle.

A nicked bone slat on a worn sinew-thread. Someone’s dead.

Everything in a Chime-walker’s training recoils from a flat note. A flat buzz in a bow means rot; a slipped pitch on a chime means a lie, and a lie on the spans kills people. If a slate cracks in a freeze and goes sour, you cut it down at once and tune a new one.

Except for this one.

You stand on the swaying bark, gloved hand resting lightly on the guide-cord, listening to the clean chord of the road carrying that small, stubborn dissonance beneath it. You check the main gut-cord to make sure the chime itself is strong; you clear the rime-crust from the slate plates with the side of your thumb; and you leave the thin sinew-thread untouched. You let the frost gnaw it. You let the dry gales fray it fiber by fiber until, in some empty hour when no one is listening, the bone slat finally drops away into the dark and the chord rings clean on its own.

I think that is why I could not be only a chord-sleeper, and why I could not be only an instrument of precision. Knowing the difference between a sour note that warns of a broken span and a flat note that means *someone was loved here, and is gone*—that is the whole craft.

---

Twice in a long cycle, when the mist-heavy lulls settle over the upper spans, I follow the sound of personal strand-chimes two miles through the fog until the dark opens into a **Great Knot**.

I am never the loud one in those warm, smoke-thick galleries. I don’t trade tales of wrestling gliders on a twenty-foot roll, and I don’t lead the dancing or the courtship tapping across the chambers. I find a corner near a junction-rib, trade my spare tuned slates for a pouch of salt-bitter sap-cakes and a fresh hank of braided phloem-cord, mend a frayed harness for a young climber who hasn’t learned yet that frost hides a cut line until it snaps, and listen to everyone else’s knot-cords until my fingertips ache with news of a thousand miles of living arches.

And at the end, before the dry wind climbs and scatters us back onto the loops, I crowd into the great central cavern with the rest—a hundred bodies pressed together in the near-black, the peat-pans hooded down to red pinpricks.

I pull my coat back off one shoulder and lean bare skin against the curved, damp wood of the vault, feeling the distant mountain wind shake the entire fused hub through my spine.

When the elder Chime-walker opens their throat and finds the pitch of the high vent-slits, I do not take the high, clear part of the **Span-Count**. I am not the voice that names the fallen arches and the sheared teeth and the people who did not make it back to the Knot.

I slide straight into the floor of it—the low, humming chest-drone beneath the words, holding the pitch steady alongside ninety other throats until the ceiling shivers and shakes a fine mist of cold dew down across our upturned faces. Right there, pressed against the living wall, I am close to the chord-sleepers again. Holding the note steady so the names have something solid to cross over.

And when a name I knew is sung—someone whose walking pole once answered mine with two flat knocks out on a freezing bow, whose knot-cord once lay across my palms—I lift my hand from my knee and strike one flat knuckle-beat against the wood, feeling the pulse run out of my skin and vanish down five miles of singing dark.

---

Afterward, back out on the trails, walking alone or a hundred paces behind a companion with my bone-shod pole going *tok—tok—tok* between the booms of the wind, I think sometimes about the **Unmoored**—those broken saddle-huts hanging askew from a single frayed cable far below an arch, their lashed bone-slats still faithfully knocking in the empty air, leaning endlessly into gusts that no longer matter.

They do not frighten me the way they frighten some of the younger Walkers. They just make me check my toggles twice. And they remind me why the Walkers tell children that soft, impossible story when they wake screaming from the Falling Dream—that miles below the lowest arches, the rising earth-steam grows so thick and warm that nothing ever truly hits bottom, and the lost ones are still down there, riding the updrafts in slow circles, listening to the continent sing overhead without needing a harness anymore.

I don’t believe in that warm bottom any more than any grown Walker does. I know what waits down where the bows end: raw black shale and freezing rockfall.

Still, if a child woke trembling inside a rib-chamber where I was sheltering—waking out of that endless black drop with their small fists clenched on my coat—I wouldn’t tell them about the Teeth. I would pull them inside the warmth beside the sleeping wren, let the slow heave of the bow rock us both, and tap three slow beats against the curved wooden wall.

*Safe. Steady. Here.*

And wait for the vent-slit to hum its answer back, while outside in the freezing indigo dark, ten thousand miles of taut living strings hold each other up over nothing, and sing.
