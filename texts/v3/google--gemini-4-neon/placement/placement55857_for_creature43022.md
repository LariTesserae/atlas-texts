At first, I think I would want wings.

It is the obvious reflex when confronted with twenty kilometers of luminous nothing: to imagine oneself as a glider, five meters of translucent parchment slung on hollow struts, leaning off the raw lip of an overhang and simply letting go. From up there, the hollow would cease to be a trap of splitting seams and become a geometry you could read all at once—every crawling shaft of light, every pale blister swelling on a distant wall, every silent plume of diamond-dust rising out of a collapse. Pure vantage. Weightless detachment.

Except the gliders are made of dry spite, and they can never land. Touch a level terrace and they die there, dragging their elbows through the rime. They have no curiosity—only patience and an appetite for what breaks. Stay with that image for ten seconds and it thins out into something too cold to inhabit. I want the view of the basin, yes, the sense of its whole restless grammar at once, but not at the price of never ducking beneath a hide flap into the smell of melted tallow and warmed pine-sap.

The clamberers come closer to something I recognize. That nervous, lean attention—peeking around a serrated vertical blade forty feet above a stranger’s head, amber eyes blinking behind double lids, following a walking figure for half a day across gaps with no bottom just to see what it drops or leaves behind. The hunger for salt; the inability to leave a strangeness alone. I would gladly borrow those black keratin hooks and that refusal to let frost stick to the coat. Yet a clamberer owns a single shaded puddle and guards it in fierce solitude. It watches people, and chews the sweat out of their ropes, and never asks them what the east shelf did before the last dim.

And the warm-backs—strangely, they pull at me more than I expected. Not the blindness, not the panic when dormant sap slams upward through the vascular tubes, but the intimacy of their pact with the ground. Swimming through the one narrow band of mercy just under the frost, reading the entire architecture of the terrace by pressure and vibration along stiff guard-hairs, nosing up every few minutes to rasp a pinhole through the chalky armor and send a single clean bead of steam into the glare. There is something deeply enviable about knowing the thing that holds you from the inside out.

Still, if I am honest, I end up on two feet, in soft burrower-hide boots, shifting my weight subtly from heel to ball so my shins can hear what the floor is thinking.

I would be one of the people—though it takes a while to see where along the slope of the hollow I would actually settle.

Not among the true seam-runners. I admire them from a distance: lean pairs crouching motionless beside a warming terrace with bone-tipped lances across their knees, reading the exact second a half-mile slab is about to let go, racing out across fresh avalanches to dig a fist-sized nugget of copper out of bruised violet pith before the plant heals shut over the wound. I know myself too well to pretend I would survive a season doing that. I would hesitate. When a cliff face sheared away, I would spend three fatal minutes staring at the cross-section of exposed timber—the pale fibrous strata, the severed warm-back bores glistening with amber glaze, the sudden shocking green where yesterday there was only chalky armor—and the next shelf would take me with it.

Nor would I stay for long in the deepest, mildest crooks of the perimeter rim, where the oldest knuckle-keepers root themselves against the thickest ice and the light arrives permanently dulled. I would love the craft there: the patient skimming of pure water-ice from freezing sap-troughs until only dark cordial remains; the steady rhythm of hammering copper pegs back into irritated calluses; the quiet warmth of four domes huddled together against the chill. For a season or two, I would think I had found my place. Then I would catch myself climbing farther out along the buttresses every dim, walking past the last drying-racks until the safe, gnarled heartwood thinned beneath my soles and the basin opened out again, emptying my stomach.

So I find myself on the hinge.

High on an upper shoulder where the slow perimeter knuckles begin to cant outward into the sweeping ribs that overhang the central drop. It is not safe ground, exactly—nothing that high over the gulf ever is—but it is older timber, dense and darkened, shifting by fingers rather than paces, groaning with a low, honest warning weeks before it decides to part. From the lip of that shoulder, fifteen kilometers of bright air lie completely unobstructed, and the far walls rise like chalk cliffs fading into diamond-dust haze.

There is a single low dome lashed into the crook of a deadened bract—bent heartwood ribs, shingled in overlapping plates of blackened bark, pinned down with four precious copper pegs and six of hammered bone. Outside, a spliced stem flies a strip of rust-red cloth that snaps softly whenever an updraft peels off the warmed cliffs below.

This is where the image stops shifting and begins to feel like skin.

Everyone in the hollow learns to listen downward. You tap your bone-shod stave, rest a palm against the shaft, and read the floor: the hollow *tock* of old timber, the dense wet hum of rising sap, the high, tight wire-hum of a shelf waiting on one degree of warmth to shear. The people know the living ground the way sailors know a hull.

What almost no one can look at is the ceiling.

It takes your eyes if you try. Even through the razor-thin horizontal slots of smoked-pith goggles, tipping your chin up toward the midday glare is like pressing cold needles against the back of your skull. You learn to keep your head down, to know the light-blades only by where they strike—white saws suddenly chewing down a distant cliff, boiling a melt-puddle dry, turning a quiet trail into a blistering ridge.

I think I would have come to my strange little craft by accident, on a midday when I was hiding from the glare inside my dark bark dome with the tallow lamp snuffed to save fat.

One of the cured bark shingles on the wall facing the void had warped a fraction in the dry cold, or a knot had shrunk out of the deadwood, leaving a pinhole no wider than a warm-back’s breathing-slit. Sitting there in the cool purple darkness, letting the glare-aches fade from behind my eyes, I would have looked up at the pale scraped hide stretched across the opposite inner curve of the dome—and seen the far cliff hanging upside down across the wool.

Dimmed. Silent. Blue-white and celadon, shrunk to the span of my outstretched arms, moving with the eerie, unhurried precision of water under ice.

You can stare straight into the midday glare when it passes through a needle-hole. It cannot burn you there. Across the stretched hide, the lethal white shafts from the overhead fissures become delicate pale needles sliding slowly down inverted cliffs of overlapping bracts. You can watch a half-mile blister swell and peel away in complete, painless clarity—rising upward across the hide like a pale feather drifting off a roof—seconds before the faint dry *whump* reaches the outside of the dome.

Once you see that, you do not patch the hole. You make it clean. You ring it with a bit of hollowed bone so the bark cannot swell it shut, plug it with a twist of greased wool when the glare shifts away, and stretch the smoothest, palest hide you can trade for across the back ribs of the dome.

And slowly, over turns of the glare and the dim, you become something the hollow does not quite have a word for yet—not a knuckle-keeper, not a seam-runner, just the person on the high shoulder who watches the ceiling by reading its reflection on the walls.

Because every light-blade is a signature. Four kilometers up, locked inside the vaulted glacier, a rift opens by an inch under the weight of the unseen world above, and down here a white knife shifts three hundred paces east and cuts a carved staircase clean in half. A dark pocket of ancient moraine—granite, shale, iron-stained silt, maybe a raw green lump of copper dragged from some vanished mountain—creeps through the pressure-cracks toward a melt-vein, and the shaft passing beside it splinters, dims along one edge, throws a jagged shadow where yesterday the beam ran clean.

Nobody can draw a permanent map of this basin; paper does not exist here, and even if it did, the ground would make a liar of it before the sap froze twice. Instead, I keep three flat boards planed from pale, dense pith, rubbed thin with a film of milky sap-wax. During the glare, crouching in the cool dimness of the dome while the inverted basin burns quietly across the hanging hide, I mark the tracks of the sharpest blades with a sliver of charred bone. Where the Long Rift’s knife touched at high glare six days ago; where it touches today; the slow, unmistakable curve of its creep as the unseen sun above turns through its season, and the sudden crooked jumps that mean the ice itself has cracked.

When the dim settles in—when the blinding white outside cools to heavy indigo and then to ghostly silver, and the whole miles-wide floor locks shut with that endless soft chorus of *ticks* and *pings*—I pull the wool plug, light the burrower-fat lamp in its hollowed stone cup, and step out into the blue hush.

My eyes are never good in the raw glare; maybe I burned them once when I was younger, or maybe they were simply never built for salt-flat light. Either way, the dim is my country. With my goggles pushed up onto my forehead, the cold stinging the pale, unburned band of skin across the bridge of my nose, I walk the edges of the high shoulder and finally look straight up.

In the indigo dark, four kilometers of glacial ice stop being a wall of white pain and become a vault of fathomless, fractured glass. You can see the ancient architecture inside it—great clouded veins of trapped air, pale pressure-ribs arching across the sky-that-isn’t-a-sky, and, here and there, smeared deep in the blue thickness like bruises, the dark streaks of the heavy place pressing down from above. Whenever the glacier groans, unimaginably high and faint, I rest the butt of my stave against my boot and wait to see if the floor answers.

People find their way up to the shoulder during the dim. Not often—days can pass with nothing moving across the gulf except a solitary glider banking through the last rising mist, or a clamberer eyeing my guy-lines from a jagged rib above—but often enough that I keep extra cups of horn beside the tallow lamp and a kettle of pine-sharp sap-water ready to thaw.

Seam-runners come first, tapping their staves twice against the old heartwood two hundred paces off, lowering their bone-tipped lances and showing their empty palms until I lift the hide flap and flash a dark bark blade in answer. Inside the dome, pushing their goggles up, their copper-burned faces look tired and sharp in the little orange flame. We talk the way everyone here talks: the cant of the floor first, the state of the seams, which terrace is *drinking* too fast to cross by the next midday. Then I tilt the waxed pith-boards toward the lamp, and they lean in with stained, stiffened knuckles hovering an inch above the pale scratches, tracing the projected path of the eastern rift’s blade with their fingers in the air.

*Four more glares,* I tell them quietly, pitched low in the throat so it does not jar the hush, *and that narrow beam clears the lip above the Three-Split. Look at how the shadow pinched on its north edge yesterday—there’s dense weight riding in the rift above it. Might just be grey shale. Might be red clay. Might drop before the seam widens.*

They do not always believe me, and they are right not to treat a guess as stone. Nothing in the hollow is stone until it screams down out of the ceiling and punches ten feet into the green pith. Still, they trade for the looking. They leave strips of salted cambium, braided phloem-cord, a bladder of rendered warm-back tallow, or sometimes the odd, useless debris they pick up out on the shelves and cannot bear to leave behind for the next collapse.

That is how the small shelf along the inside rib of my dome accumulates its quiet museum of things that do not belong to the glare.

A pebble of banded grey-and-white quartz from a disappointing ice-drop, smooth on one face from some river that dried up before the ice ever formed, good for nothing except rolling between cold palms while thinking. A scrap of rusted iron no bigger than a fingernail, worn thin from a blade that passed through six hands before mine. And, tucked between two thin, carefully shaved plates of clear ice-rind that I keep buried in a rime-lined cleft outside the flap so they never thaw, the wings of three blind moths blown up out of the Warm Dark.

I gather those myself whenever the pressure shifts and that cellar-sweet, vinegar-and-compost updraft rises from two kilometers below. After the damp wind dies and the frost snaps back in, I walk the quiet blue terraces slowly, scanning the pale waxy horn for specks of color that have no business existing in a world of white and celadon—muddy purple, bruised ochre, velvet locked into brittle glass. Most shatter the instant they strike the rime. Once in a great while, one lands in a soft drift of diamond-dust whole enough to lift on the flat of a bone knife.

Inside the dark dome, held an inch from the orange flame of the tallow lamp, those frozen wings glow like bruised embers. Sometimes, when a family of knuckle-keepers passes along the upper rim moving their rolled shelters to a quieter buttress, their children duck through my flap with wide, pale-ringed eyes to see two things at once: the upside-down world burning harmlessly across the stretched hide, and the colors from the bottom of the abyss lit from behind by warm-back fat.

Between the heavy dead stone locked four kilometers overhead and the steaming, sunless rot two kilometers below, we occupy a skin scarcely thicker than a breath—blinding, sharp-edged, frantic to grow, always about to break.

I think that is why I belong on that shoulder, with a pinhole in blackened bark and wax boards that I warm over the flame every season until the old tracks melt smooth and leave room for the new ones. I am not here to hold the ground still. Even my dome knows better; every week I walk its perimeter with a bone mallet, tapping the copper pegs back down through the pale, irritated calluses that are patiently trying to push me out. I am only here to notice how the light moves before it cuts, to hold a piece of the sky-stone in one hand and a frozen wing from the Soft in the other, and to pass along what I saw before the shelf decides to let go.

Because one dim, sooner or later, I will step out into the blue chill, tap my stave against the old heartwood beneath my boots, and hear that tight, high hum—like a bowed string held just below sound.

I will not argue with it. I will scrape the wax boards clean one last time, roll the blackened bark shingles, unlash the bent ribs, and pull the copper pegs before the living horn closes over them. I will fold the rust-red rag carefully inside my silver-furred coat and walk along the rim until the ground beneath my soles answers with a dry, hollow *tock* again.

And years after that, when my knees can no longer read the pitch of a ridge and the glare feels too heavy even behind a pinhole, someone will stitch me snugly into an old white pelt with braided phloem-cord and carry me out into the silver hush. I hope they find a clean closing seam high on a wall that faces the morning shafts. I hope they wedge that useless banded quartz pebble from the heavy place beside my shoulder before they walk away. The pale shoots will knit shut overhead in a month, sealing me into cold, pine-scented glass—and maybe a hundred seasons from now, a white blade from a newly widened rift will slice the cliff straight down the seam, showing me for one silent, blinding afternoon to whoever is watching from across the gulf, before the whole shelf shears loose and drops me gently, at last, into the Warm Dark.
