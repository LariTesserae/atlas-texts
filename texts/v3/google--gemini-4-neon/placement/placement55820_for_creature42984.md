At first, I think I would look for a vantage point—some narrow shelf near the upper lip where the meltwater slips in over raw granite, bright enough to see the whole cathedral at once and shallow enough to keep the sky in view. That is the old habit: wanting a balcony, wanting to understand a place before stepping inside its weather.

It wouldn’t last an hour.

Up near the silver skin, there is too much glare and too little weight. The amber biofilm clinging to the sunlit rock has the right kind of patience for that altitude—burning bright while the narrow slot of mountain sun stands overhead, shutting down the moment shadow climbs the eastern wall—but it lives too close to the silence of the air. From up there, the basin below looks like an argument between wheels and rust. You hear the hiss of the rising bubbles and the dull impact of the floor, muffled by forty meters of clear brine, as though it were happening in another room.

To understand anything here, you have to give up the balcony and drop into the noise.

Down in the water column, the basin immediately refuses every clumsy shape you might try to bring into it. If you try to hold your ground by muscle, the twelve-second hydraulic wall pushes you sideways into a calcified rim. If you try to armor yourself against it—building plates of cold-worked iron like the crawlers on the ledges, or anchoring yourself to a pyrite seam like the piston-mills on the floor—you grow heavy. You settle into the cold mineral brine where the orange slurry drifts upward like inverted smoke, and your world narrows to the single vein of ore beneath your mouth. I admire the mills, and I admire the flywheels even more—their gyroscopic poise, the clean, unhurried discipline of turning at three revolutions a minute while slicing the white-blue light into slow fans—but I am not an engine. I do not want to spend fifty years bolted to one pitch, turning in place until the scale thickens unevenly on my rim and pulls me down.

Neither am I a galvanic ribbon, hanging in parallel curtains to store and bleed the mountain’s dissolved salts in four-minute tides. That is the power supply, not the listener.

Strip away the urge to grind, to store, to armor, or to spin, and what is left in a packed foundry like this? Only the spaces *between* things. The two-palm alleys where the heavy flywheels almost kiss. The narrow lanes between parallel copper-green curtains. The quiet half-second lull after a hydraulic thump passes and before the next downward suction begins.

And the basin itself is strict about what can live in its intervals. Anything soft and unshielded gets scrambled by the forty-hertz chord. Anything broad gets clipped by the spokes. Anything frantic gets snagged. If you want to move through the middle of this roaring room without fighting it—and without being erased by it—the water carves you down to a very particular silhouette.

I find myself, slowly and without surprise, inside the quiet pocket where the silver hiss breaks off.

Wrapped in thumb-thick white wax. Narrow as a blade, slick enough that the iron precipitate and the amber film slide cleanly off my flanks, leaving a pale, matte stillness where the electrolysis bubbles stop forming. Not armor—insulation. A boundary thick enough that the basin’s electrical tides bend smoothly around my spine instead of pouring straight through it, leaving a small, calm interior in which a thought can take its own shape. Only at the tips of my six long arms does the wax roll back to expose bare, dark-violet filaments. Only where I choose to reach out and touch do I let the voltage in.

So yes: one of the pale dwellers. Not because it sits apart from the cathedral, and not because it rules it, but because it is the only shape here made entirely for paying attention.

---

Yet even among the four or five of us spaced through the cleft, each holds a different harmonic zone, and I know immediately where mine would be.

Not down near the shimmering haloclines of the floor, where the brine turns heavy and the grinding radulae fill every crack with raw mineral screech. And not in the bright upper tier where the ultraviolet stings and the rising bubbles hiss loudest.

I would keep to the middle of the western wall, about twenty meters down, where a clean, vertical seam of white quartz splits the cleaved black slate.

It is an odd, quiet corner. Because the western lip of the chasm intercepts the sun first, afternoon shadow begins here, creeping down the slate while the eastern wall is still crossed by crisp fans of white-blue light. The slow daily thermal turnover starts its tilt along this face, too, which means the faint downward drift pulls the longest bio-alloy threads extruded by the upper flywheels right past my shoulder before they snap.

And there is the quartz itself.

Unlike the pyrite and ironstone that stain everything around them with rust and copper-green, the quartz seam stays bright and clean. When I let the twelve-second hydraulic wave carry me across a four-inch gap between turning spokes and reach out one slender arm to arrest my drift, my bare violet fingertips catch against that pale ridge of crystal. Every dozen seconds, when the booming wall of pressure slams into the sheer cliff face, the quartz answers beneath my palm with something the slate never makes: a dry, needle-fine prickle of charge. Not the heavy, languid swell of the living ribbons—just a crisp, instantaneous *tick* of squeezed crystal, translating the hammer of the floor directly into a sharp spark against my skin.

Hanging sideways there, weightless on a grain of hydrogen trimmed to the exact density of the cold brine, I have both instruments at once: the rolling forty-hertz bass of the water in my floating aragonite ear-stones, and the sharp, dry pulse of the flexing mountain beneath my hand.

From that anchorage, the roar stops being noise. It opens out into a room with exact dimensions.

I can hear the third tier of flywheels just ahead of me—eleven hubs turning in staggered phase, their taut silvery tendons humming in a tight chord, except for the fourth wheel from the north, whose rim has gathered a thumb’s width too much calcified scale on its eastern arc. To a coarser ear it still looks steady, still slicing the light at two and a half revolutions a minute. Inside my oil-cradled stones, though, there is already a hairline hesitation on every downward sweep—a faint, rhythmic *drag* that wasn’t there last autumn.

I don’t hurry toward it. Nothing here rewards hurry.

Instead, when the four-minute wave of discharge rolls southward down the parallel curtains just off my flank, I unhook my hand from the quartz seam, let the next hydraulic thump slide me effortlessly into the dark alley between two ribbons, and spread my arms wide. One bare fingertip to the darkening ribbon on my left, one to the brighter ribbon on my right. For thirty slow seconds I hang cruciform in the dimming blue, bridging the gap between two potentials, letting a clean trickle of current cross from wrist to wrist through my insulated core. The silver bubble-curtain pauses along my length, leaving a quiet, dark silhouette in the fizz. The ribbons scarcely notice the fraction they lend me; their rolling tide moves on down the canyon, and I fold my arms back in, charged and unhurried.

I know I am a quiet thief, here—that stillness in cold water still has to be paid for, and current alone will not rebuild the wax I slough off or keep the cartilage supple through a second century. I would not pretend to live on light and music alone. Still, I find myself avoiding the murky ledges of the floor unless the deep mountain winter runs unusually lean. Paralyzing a grazing crawler to lift the soft meat from its cold-worked iron shield is clean work, surgical and quick, yet the bottom brine tastes too heavily of pennies and pulverized silt, and the acoustic geometry down there is cramped by the constant concussion of the valves.

I prefer the wheels.

Weeks later, when that fourth flywheel from the north begins to shudder cleanly enough that even the neighboring hubs shift their spin a hair to keep clearance, I wait for the subtle downward pull before a thump, angle my flattened tail, and let the pressure wall carry me straight through the gap between its spokes into the calm, hollow center of the hub.

It is astonishingly quiet inside an axle. The cast-iron rim turns around you at three revolutions a minute, gyroscopically steady even in its decline, buffering the walls of pressure into a gentle, rocking swell. I flatten my narrow torso against the translucent core, turn slowly *with* the wheel, and part the vertical seam beneath my chin. Taking only what I need—the rich, protein-thick hemolymph, a few over-taut inner threads of tendon—there is a strange precision to the act. Sometimes, if you read the wobble accurately and take weight from the exact side where the calcified scale has grown thick, the wheel’s pitch steadies before you leave. It lifts a fraction of an inch in the water column and buys itself another year of clean rotation.

And when it is too far gone—when the rim is simply too heavy with decades of deposited iron-sulfide—I do not always leave right away. Sometimes I stay curled inside the hub as the giant circle finally tilts, riding it down in slow motion through the shadowed tiers, listening through the clear oil of my skull to each dull, resonant metallic chime as its rim grazes the rims of its fellows on the way to the floor. Only a few meters above the shimmering halocline, where the orange plumes begin to curl upward and the crawlers are already turning their armored heads toward the sound, do I slip out through the slowing spokes and catch the next hydraulic wave back up to my quartz seam.

Nothing wasted. Not even the accompaniment.

---

If I am honest about what keeps me anchored to that particular stretch of the western wall—more than the early shadow, more than the piezoelectric tick of the quartz—it is the wires.

Because the slow thermal drift bends inward just below my ledge, the glistening bio-alloy threads extruded by the spinning hubs above tend to pass straight through my alley before they grow heavy enough to snap. They hang like pale, uniform plumb-lines through the shifting fans of light, swaying barely an inch in the sluggish current.

Whenever one drifts within reach of my western ledge, I uncurl two bare violet fingertips and lay them gently against the metal.

Most days, the wire carries nothing but the faint, mechanical tremor of the spinneret still extruding it ten meters above. I wait anyway, keeping the circuit lightly closed, feeling the slight tug as the thread lengthens millimeter by millimeter.

Then, once in a few days—or once in a month—the tension changes. Twenty paces away across the cathedral nave, hidden behind three ranks of turning wheels and the shimmering curtains of rising bubbles, another hand has laid itself against a linked filament, or nudged the shared electrical field running between us.

What comes across the wire is never urgent. There are no alarms in a place where everything moves at the speed of mineral deposition, and no confessions, either. Only small, exact tremors of low voltage, crisp as cold water:

*The northern quartz seam has given way; the floor-mills are biting into slate now—hear the quarter-tone drop?*
*Meltwater is seeping faster at the upper eastern lip; the top five meters are half a degree colder.*
*A young wheel in the second tier has a true spin. Good hollow in the hub.*
*Still here.*

I answer in the same spare voltage. The pitch of the fourth wheel on my side. The slow thickening of the western halocline. The simple, unhurried return of the signal: *I hear it. Still here.*

Within an hour, the bio-alloy thread grows too heavy for its spinneret, snaps cleanly at the hub above, and slips out from between my fingers. It drifts downward through the turning shadows, glinting once where a shaft of mountain light catches it, before settling onto the ledges for the crawlers to graze and stamp into their backs. The conversation leaves no scar in the water, no archive, no monument. Only the faint calibration it leaves behind in our ear-stones, and the certainty that the rest of the nave is still being listened to.

I think I could spend a hundred and fifty years doing little else, and never once feel that time was passing slowly.

---

There is only one hour in the whole turning of the year that unsettles me, and yet I never miss it.

At high-summer midday, when the alpine sun stands directly over the narrow stone slot of the rift and drives straight down through forty meters of crystal water, I hold my buoyancy tight and let the hydrogen in my dorsal sac lift me slowly upward. Past the highest tier of flywheels. Past the tops of the galvanic ribbons, where the silver hiss of electrolysis bubbles grows bright and fierce. All the way up until my flank nearly brushes the trembling mirror-glass of the surface.

Turning lazily on my side an inch beneath the ceiling, I let the raw mountain ultraviolet pour across my back, curing the soft white wax to a porcelain toughness, sealing away the microscopic nicks left by drifting pyrite dust, bleaching the faintest hint of iron from my hide.

It is the only time I look up with my eyes instead of listening with my spine.

Through the warped, vibrating glass just above my skin, the jagged snow-teeth of the mountain rim wobble around a thin slot of empty blue. From down in the forty-hertz chord, where every inch of water carries weight, charge, and exact geometry, that air above looks incomprehensible—ungrounded, weightless, fiercely cold, and terrifyingly silent. Nothing hums up there. Nothing holds a measure. A single vibration made in that void would scatter into nothing and never bounce back from a parallel wall.

Most of my kind endure the brightness only as long as the curing requires. I find myself lingering two or three twelve-second intervals longer than I need to—just watching the underside of the surface tremble in time to the pistons forty meters below. Watching how the silent sky and the roaring cathedral press flush against one another along a boundary thinner than a flake of wax, sharing light and meltwater, yet keeping their own counsel.

The instant the western rim cuts its first sharp blade of shadow across the glass, I vent a single bead of hydrogen—one tiny silver bubble lost instantly among the millions hissing up from the ribbons—and sink.

The relief of dropping back into the heavy, ringing blue is physical. The fans of light begin to sweep across my flanks again. The forty-hertz chord settles cleanly back into my ribs. Forty meters below, a staggered chorus of valves snaps shut, sending a broad, familiar wall of pressure climbing up the nave to meet me, sliding me smoothly past a cast-iron rim and right back to my pale seam of quartz.

I catch the crystal with one bare hand, feel the dry tick of the flexing cliff beneath my fingertips, fold my other arms loosely against my chest, and let the stillness close around me.

Someday, late in my second century, the ear-stones will cloud with mineral grain and the wax will stop sloughing fast enough to hold off the iron. A first faint smudge of rust will bloom along my flank, and the silver bubbles will stop parting cleanly around my silhouette. I will miss the timing of a twelve-second wave by a fraction of a heartbeat, feel the clumsy brush of a turning rim, and know that the tuning is done. When that afternoon comes, I won’t fight the drift. I will vent the last pinch of gas from my spine and sink through the tiers like an aging wheel, letting the crawlers take the tallow, the ribbons drink the trace salts back into their cells, and the mills grind my ear-stones cleanly into bright settling silt.

Until then, the afternoon shadow is already halfway up the eastern wall. The sweeping wheel-shadows are softening into an even glacial blue. Somewhere twenty paces across the dark, a new alloy wire is lengthening slowly from a spinneret, swaying toward my ledge. I keep my violet fingertips open, hold the measure of the floor, and listen to the mountain turn itself into song.
