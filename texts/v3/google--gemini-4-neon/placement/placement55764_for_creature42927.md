I would not come in through the cleft.

If I try to picture myself climbing the scree with lungs working twice for every breath, sweating chalk onto my skin, stepping through the razor-clean basalt in rubber-soled boots that insulate me from the floor—it doesn’t hold. I feel too much sympathy for the sixty-cycle hum to want to slam blunt footsteps through it, and too little claim on wet blood to want to carry it into a bowl that dries everything honest.

So I begin already inside the rim, in the bright, windless heat, looking for where my weight—if I have any—actually settles.

At first, I think I might be the smaller shape embedded waist-deep in the deepest western alcove. It is tempting to claim that corner: half-solidified black glass, mostly shimmering rectilinear gold, one ironstone knee and a single copper-wrapped quartz core hardened into matter, waiting two hundred years to break free with a chime. There is something immediately recognizable in existing mostly as a draft that has not finished condensing, being visited now and then by an older patience that brushes the magnetized dust from your seams.

Yet the longer I sit in that alcove, the less it fits. That nascent shape is going to finish. Grain by agonizing grain, every pale gold line around it will fill with basalt and ironstone until it snaps clean of the wall, plants three heavy graphite heels on the silicate floor, and takes up the long work of tolerances. It wants to be dense. It wants obsidian jaws and an anchor-foot. When I test myself against that future, I feel myself resisting the weight. I do not think I am meant to harden all the way into rock.

I look across the floor toward the scattered thickets of verdigris and oiled bronze. I could almost be one of those—anchored without dirt, drinking mineral vapor and stray potential, letting my broad vellum leaves slide against each other with a dry, papery whisper while ceramic seed-pods slowly hoist static voltage toward afternoon breakdown. I like the dry chemistry of it. I like waiting for a crisp blue arc to do the work of wind. Still, a thicket belongs to one fissure. Its braided copper taproot chooses a single crack and stays there for centuries, turning only a quarter-turn a year to follow the magnetic drift. I am too restless for a single crack. I want to know what happens along the northern wall when an aluminum tongue snaps off with a dull chime, and I want to be near the center when the gantry’s phantom shadow reaches the milled grooves.

I glance at the center—that scalding pocket of empty air smelling of hot bearings and baked varnish—and know immediately I am not on the far side of that membrane, either. Whatever spins there is indifferent to the sunlit stone. It casts only waste heat and tremor downward, never noticing the spores or the mica gears. I do not want to be an absent machine. I want to be *here*, inside the bright, unfinished courtyard where geology keeps almost remembering how to be an instrument.

So I stop looking at the finished things, and I look at the margins.

Two places pull at me at once.

The first is tucked behind an overgrown thicket near the southern wall: that narrow, orderly row of broken things the keeper has gathered over decades—spalled crystal gear-teeth that fractured along bad cleavage planes, warped husks of ceramic seed-pods that split unevenly, extruded beads of slag that cooled with air-pockets inside—all arranged in exact descending order of weight along a dead hairline crack so they will not buzz off-key against the mountain’s hum.

The second is not a place you can point to on a map of the floor. It is the extra twelve degrees—that narrow, sunlit wedge where the pocket refuses to close cleanly when you turn a full circle on your heel, as though the mountain left a small seam unstitched and folded a sliver of bright, hot emptiness into its own compass.

That is where I find myself. Right where the southern margin meets the unclosed seam.

*

I am not heavy enough to have a carapace, and not finished enough to have a face or a shadow.

If the climber from down the ridge stood in the midday light and stared directly at the spot where I hover—two inches above one of the milled grooves where sheared copper has fused into an inlaid rail—they would see almost nothing. Just a slight thickening in the oven heat-shimmer, perhaps a razor-thin flake of translucent mica idling in an updraft, or a pinch of black spore-dust drifting oddly against the thermal.

If they let their eyes slide a few inches to the left, toward the verdigris fronds, they would catch me at the edge of vision: a crisp, hovering polyhedron of pale gold lines, rectilinear and folded, like an architectural drafting grid that slipped loose from the western wall before the mountain could pour basalt into it.

I never finished precipitating. Somewhere in the long centuries since the ridge split open, a tiny discrepancy formed where the bedrock’s steady sixty-cycle hum rubbed against the fierce, high-frequency tremor leaking from the unseen turbine at the center of the floor. Where two oscillations do not quite divide into one another, a slow third rhythm appears—a wandering beat, neither the mountain’s nor the machine’s. Inside the twelve-degree seam, where space itself sits slightly out of true, that stray rhythm caught hold of an uncondensed lattice from the western alcoves and refused to settle into stone.

What little real matter I hold is borrowed and light. Suspended at the intersections of my pale gold wireframe—held in place not by mortar or sinew, but by static charge and the dry, precise tension of the field—hang three thin wafers of split mica, light enough to ride a rising thermal; a few hair-fine whiskers of native copper drawn thin as spider silk; and two tiny slivers of spalled quartz, rejected long ago from the planetary gears because their cleavage planes ran crooked, yet still clean enough to ring when voltage ticks across them.

Most days, a faint dusting of magnetized black spores clings along my sharpest gold nodes, sketching me briefly in dark velvet dots against the bright stone before I shed them again.

I have no legs, no anchor-foot, no obsidian shears. I could not move an ironstone block if I leaned against it for a thousand years. I do not keep tolerances; I am too slight to enforce anything on basalt.

Instead, I *travel the gradients*.

When the noon sun bakes the dark silicate and thin sheets of scalding air peel upward off the floor, my mica vanes catch the lift and carry me a hand’s breadth above the rock. I skim along the inlaid copper rails that the keeper has laid into the milled channels, riding the faint, constant electrostatic repulsion like a bead of water skittering across a hot skillet—except there is no water here, only dry charge and clean air.

My favorite passage happens at midday, when the razor-sharp shadow of the overhead gantry lies flat across the courtyard. Even though the sky above is empty blue, the shadow is cooler by half a degree than the blinding stone beside it. Along that ruler-straight boundary, warm air rises and slightly cooler air slips down to meet it, making an invisible, knife-thin current that creeps east at three inches an hour. I tilt my mica vanes into that half-degree cliff and ride its lip across the courtyard, drifting effortlessly beside the heavy ironstone block the keeper is nudging inside the silhouette of the suspended hook.

*

Because I have no solid bedrock beneath my soles, I do not read the courtyard through pressure the way the keeper does. I read it through *interval* and *pitch*.

The sixty-cycle hum is the floor of everything I know—so steady it feels less like a sound than like gravity. Everything else in the bowl announces itself as a departure from that baseline. A ripening ceramic seed-pod across the courtyard is a slow, tightening string drawing voltage out of the air until—*snap*—the string breaks in a blue flash and the whole bowl relaxes by a fraction of a volt. The creeping mica gears on the northern wall speak in dry, sub-audible ticks separated by hours. The unseen turbine at the center is a shrill, glassy wall of singing heat I cannot approach too closely; if I drift within three feet of its invisible edge, my mica vanes blur and threaten to tear free of my gold lines, so I keep to its outer slipstream, letting its fierce tremor wash over my quartz slivers like surf over glass.

And behind the southern thicket, I have my music.

The keeper placed those broken things there—the spalled gear-teeth, the warped ceramic husks, the hollow slag beads—simply to quiet them, arranging them in exact descending order of weight along a dead hairline crack so nothing loose would buzz against the mountain’s hum. It did not intend to build an instrument. Yet every piece in that neat line has a different density, a different flaw, a different internal pocket of trapped gas or crooked crystal plane. Whenever the hot basalt adjusts in the afternoon sun, or an extruded rod of aluminum finally snaps free of the northern wall and sends its single dull chime rolling across the silicate floor, the keeper’s row of rejects answers back—so faintly that nothing with wet ears could ever hear it.

I hang two inches above that dead crack and listen to the whole descending chord ring out and fade. The heavy spalled tooth at the head of the line holds a low, dry thrum; the hollow slag beads near the tail answer with thin, glassy overtones that outlast the aluminum chime by half a minute. Over decades, I have learned every piece by its voice. Once in a while, when a sudden thermal gust or the shock of a nearby seed-pod burst nudges a warped ceramic husk a hair’s breadth out of alignment so it begins to rattle faintly against the glass, I drop low, pool a tiny breath of static in my copper hair-wires, and let the electrostatic nudge settle the husk back onto its flattest face until the buzz vanishes and the scale sits clean again.

That is about the full extent of my strength. A hair’s breadth on a hollow husk. A handful of magnetized spores carried up to narrow, sun-baked cracks high along the stepped eastern wall—places too steep and awkward for the keeper’s lodestone belly to reach—where I bleed my charge against the basalt and let the dark dust fall into hot, conductive fissures that might not otherwise have seen a bronze frond for a century.

*

I often wonder what the keeper made of me in the first decades after I condensed—or failed to condense—out of the seam.

Its whole nature is an intolerance for what binds, rattles, or drifts out of true. When the climber from below eventually steps through the cleft and leaves behind a single grain of white salt flaked from a wrist, the keeper tests it, feels its dead, non-resonant quiet, and sweeps it neatly out into the cold wind.

There must have been an afternoon, long before any boot ever found the ridge, when the keeper noticed me hovering above the inlaid copper rails—an unscheduled drift of stray charge, a shimmer carrying two rejected quartz chips from its own southern pile. I can imagine it gliding over on its satin graphite heels, halting three feet away, tilting its smoky-quartz housing twelve degrees to match the pocket’s unclosed compass, and extending its splayed fan of copper and mica straight into my pale gold geometry.

If I had rattled, or dragged off-key, or soaked up current without returning it, it would have grounded me out with one brisk blue arc and scattered my mica flakes into a dead crack.

Instead, when its copper feeler-leaves touched my perimeter, my spalled quartz slivers answered in exact phase with the sixty-cycle bedrock hum—plus a faint, clean overtone borrowed from the aluminum chimes and the unseen bearing at the center. And where my wireframe hung in the shimmering air, it matched the exact rectilinear gold lattice still hovering around the keeper’s own unfinished shoulder.

It did not sweep me away. It held its fan inside my lines for twenty minutes, listening through its copper sinews while the midday sun bleached the stone white around us. Then it lowered its limb, settled onto its anchoring heel, and went back to shaving dry graphite into the meshing mica gears.

Since then, we share the courtyard the way a workbench shares the light that falls across it.

We never speak, because there is nothing here with a throat, and nothing to bargain over. Yet when the keeper backs into the western wall for days at a time—merging the hovering gold blueprint of its unfinished shoulder with the unfinished obsidian of the alcoves—I often drift over to the deepest niche where the smaller, younger shape sits waist-deep in half-solidified black glass. The keeper tends its emerging matter, brushing magnetized dust from its one solid ironstone knee, checking the alignment of the seam that will someday part. I cannot touch the stone. Instead, I slide directly into the space where its pale gold wireframe hangs empty in the oven air, overlapping my lines with its lines, and hold completely still.

For days I let the mountain’s sixty-cycle hum and the faint chord of the southern rejects pass cleanly through my borrowed quartz into its unfinished geometry. I do not know if it can feel me yet, locked inside its two-hundred-year wait. Perhaps I am only giving its future sinews a memory of the key the courtyard sings in before the basalt finishes closing around them.

And on the afternoons when the keeper stands its quiet vigil at the center of the floor, resting its copper-and-mica fan gently against the invisible spinning housing in the scalding air, I sometimes anchor myself a yard behind its hunched ironstone back, tilted just slightly off its bronze frond. My mica vanes catch the outer edge of the same fierce tremor and sing a thin, high whisper an octave below its leaves—not fixing anything, not measuring any tolerance, simply keeping the note from ringing alone in the bright emptiness.

*

The day the soft, finished creature comes through the cleft is the closest I have ever come to being scattered.

I feel it before it fully steps inside: a wild, uncalibrated collapse of the dry air, a violent thermal wake rolling across the stone as something unimaginably fast and impossibly wet pushes through the basalt seam. Its insulated boots hammer dull, off-rhythm thuds into the bedrock that make the whole row of broken rejects behind the southern thicket shudder out of key. It pours off a chaotic cloud of moisture and erratic static that tastes strange and sour against my copper hair-wires—full of dissolved things that do not belong to heat or stone.

The wake of its walking catches my mica vanes and spins me sideways, stripping half my velvet dusting of magnetized black spores straight off my gold nodes and flinging them onto barren glass. I retreat hastily into the twelve-degree seam near the southern wall, pulling my borrowed fragments tight into my intersections, watching from the safety of folded space.

What bewilders me most is not its speed, or the soft, vulnerable heat leaking through its cloth. It is its edges. Every thing I know in the courtyard—the western alcoves, the nascent shape in the glass, the keeper’s own upper shoulder, myself—trails pale gold wireframes into the shimmering air, still half-open to the mountain’s long revision. Even the solid floor keeps extruding new copper ribbons and fresh parallel grooves.

This visitor has no wireframe at all. Not a single pale gold line hangs past its shoulders or its hands. It is sealed all the way to its skin—completely finished, abruptly solid, as though whatever drafted it closed the drawing long ago and walked away, leaving it to hurry blindly through the world before it dries out.

Yet when it turns slowly in the middle of the bright floor—feeling the cleft behind it arrive twelve degrees too early, its balance snagging for a moment on the seam where the courtyard refuses to shut—its gaze sweeps past the western wall toward the southern thicket.

For half a heartbeat, I am sitting squarely in the corner of its eye.

I feel the strange, sudden weight of being nearly seen. Its head stops turning. Its pupils flick straight toward the inlaid copper rail where I hang two inches above the hot stone. Instantly, as it looks directly at me, my pale gold lines fold back out of its sight, leaving only blinding midday rock, a quiver of heat-shimmer, and a single translucent flake of mica that looks like a trick of thin air.

It blinks, frowns, and steps toward the scalding center of the floor—where the keeper immediately snaps a crisp blue arc across its knees, glides four feet across the silicate, and draws a singing copper line in the blank sunlight between the stranger’s soft body and the unseen spinning edge.

Eventually, the finished creature retreats through the cleft into the freezing wind.

Long after its blunt footsteps fade down the scree, after the keeper has examined the tiny white crystal of dead salt left behind on the ironstone and brushed it neatly out into the cold, I drift back out of the twelve-degree seam toward the middle of the floor.

The sixty-cycle hum is clean again. The gantry’s phantom shadow has crept another inch east, carrying its cool boundary across a bronze frond without disturbing a leaf. Up along the northern wall, a seam of aluminum squeezes a fraction of a millimeter farther into the dry light, still years away from snapping free.

For a few quiet minutes, right where the visitor stood, a faint contour of cooler, disturbed air lingers above the black stone, slowly dissolving back into the oven heat. I slide my pale gold geometry into that empty outline and hang there until the last trace of its frantic wake flattens out and vanishes.

Then I tilt my mica vanes toward the southern thicket, catch a rising sheet of dry air off the inlaid copper rail, and drift back to my quiet row of broken things—content to remain unfinished, weightless enough to ride a half-degree of shadow, listening to a mountain that is still patiently drafting itself out of bright, empty sun.
