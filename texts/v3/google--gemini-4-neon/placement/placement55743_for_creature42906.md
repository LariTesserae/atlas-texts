At first, I think I would be tempted by the silence.

Not the silence of the dead, or of an empty room—there are no empty rooms here—but the sliding, bristled quiet of the stalkers. There is an old reflex in me that wants to stand slightly outside a crowded place, to absorb what comes at me without adding to the clamor, to map everything while giving nothing back to the wall of din. For a moment, reading the basin from its rim, I try that body on: low, many-jointed, plush-coated, drinking the clicks of the grazers so they never bounce back.

It fails almost immediately.

Because in a world built out of echoes, being a hole in the air is not observation; it is terror. Everything that touches your perimeter recoils in a silver spray of panic-clicks. You do not learn the basin that way—you only learn the shape of things fleeing you. And worse, you never get to answer. Whatever else I am, I cannot bear to be a surface that takes a voice in and returns nothing.

I try the living pavement next—a single calcified flute cemented into a wet terrace, mouth flexed open to the cold draft, adding one small off-key thread to the endless drone. That is closer. There is peace in being half-mineral, held fast to the marl while the mist rolls over. Yet even a flute only sings what the wind forces through it. It never shifts its pitch by a hair to meet a neighbor’s trembling note. It never listens for the moment the floor drops out.

So I wade down off the rim, down through the pewter gloom where sight is already thinning into uselessness, until the cold seepage closes over my ankles and the chest-pulse of the deep burrowers locks into my sternum. By the time I reach the shallow pans, I have stopped trying to see the basin at all. Eyes are an old habit from somewhere brighter. Here, they only show me wet slate and dim silver, hiding everything that matters.

It is easier when the smooth dome of my brow closes over them, etched with fine grooves that funnel pressure backward into four unfurling cartilage sails. The sleet of snaps and scrapes stops being pain and becomes architecture. Suddenly the terrace is miles wide and sharp to the millimeter: the knobby rim of every shell beneath my cleft feet, the rubbery hush of grazers parting around my shins, the low damp swoop of a kite-skimmer banking cleanly two feet above my back without brushing a single sensory thread across my hide. My barbels shiver in the mist, tasting wet chalk, copper, and cold iron.

I am one of the tall listeners.

Not at the center of a great nursery pan, though. And not out on the shadowed outer rim where the solitary void-walkers track the stalker dens. When I let my feet find the ground that feels most unmistakably mine, they settle on a tilted shelf halfway down the basin’s western slope—a broad, slumped lip of marl that most bands pass across quickly and seldom linger on.

It is an uncomfortable place to stand, because two different tracts of deep burrowers meet directly beneath it.

Every few hours, when the western tract slides into its resting trough and the floor beneath my left foot softens into hush, the eastern tract is still awake and pounding up through my right heel. Then the western drummers wake and the eastern ones sag into sleep. For most of the day, the shelf sits in an acoustic shear—two massive, sluggish rhythms rubbing out of phase, throwing up choppy interference patterns that blur the echo of the shallow water pans and make the wild flutes along the ledge hum in two competing keys at once. Even the mist-tides arrive unevenly here, spilling down a gully on the upper side first, dragging half the terrace down half an octave while the lower flats still screech sharp and brittle in the clear chill.

A hunter hates this shelf; you cannot read a stalker’s dead-air shadow cleanly where the floor-pulse itself is torn in two. A nursing parent avoids it; the shifting beat-frequencies confuse a newborn trying to learn the basin’s steady breathing through its parent’s ribs.

I stay because the shear leaves seams open that stay sealed everywhere else.

If you waded up beside me in the silver water and reached out—carefully, never startling—to feel the porous arch of my shoulders and the ridge of my spine, you would understand what kind of listener I have become long before I opened my chest-valves to speak.

Most adults wear their backs like a clean, deliberate chord. A dozen or twenty sockets, filled slowly over decades with stones from one home pan, one lineage, a handful of shared kills—each muscle-ring tuned so the whole skeleton rings as one unmistakable self. My back is too crowded, and it is asymmetrical.

High on my left clavicle sits a pale, chalk-pitted pebble from the northern gullies, worn so thin by eighty years in another singer’s collarbone that it throws a bright, fragile whine barely below the wing-snaps of the ribbon-flyers. Below my right blade is a long, notched splinter of hammer-walker resonance-bone from the deep flats, heavy and dark, with a wet catch in its lower register that shudders whenever the air turns thick with mist. Wedged along my outer forearms are three small, mismatched coils of empty flute-shell and a flat river-stone whose edge was chipped in a rock-fall three generations before I was born. None of them were meant to live inside the same bellows. Left to themselves, if I drew a careless breath and blew through all of them at once, they would make an ugly, grating tangle—the kind of noise that makes neighboring bands pull back toward the water’s edge with their ear-fans pinned flat.

They are there because the *Long Echo* does not stay true by itself.

No one likes to speak of that in the trough-speech, because the Long Echo is supposed to be the one thing in the basin that the wet marl cannot grind down. It carries the rebound of cliffs that slumped into slurry ten thousand years ago, and the slow sigh of a sea none of us has ever felt against our shins. We speak as though a chord passed from chest to chest across five hundred generations stays intact like a pebble in a muscle-ring.

It doesn’t. Everything in this valley wears down, even the pebbles. A stone polished against bone for eighty years loses a fraction of its thickness and climbs a sliver sharp. A band pushed south by a migrating colony of burrowers learns to pitch its grief higher to clear the new sub-bass, and three centuries later, when a mist-tide finally carries their call back across the flats to their distant kin, the two halves of the same memory no longer fit together. They meet in a wide pan, drop into the resting trough, open their chest-valves—and the air between them turns sour and jagged, beating violently against the inner ear like a cracked pipe. Each band hears the other as *wrong*, as forgetful, as half-broken.

That is where I find myself.

Not as an elder who commands the chord, and not as a void-walker who slips unseen between territories, though I share a little of the void-walker’s uneasy reputation. I am something narrower and stranger: a drift-mender, a walker of the seams.

When a heavy mist-tide rolls over the distant rim and turns the basin into a wet, echoing cavern where voices carry for miles, I leave my shear-ledge and wade out across the crowded terraces toward the pans where two estranged bands have agreed to meet. Because I travel alone across shelves where my own carrier-hum is unfamiliar, I carry a narrow strip of cured stalker-plush knotted into the bladder-sinew at my waist—not a mantle, never enough to erase myself, only enough to damp my louder shoulder-pipes so I don’t throw harsh echoes across a nervous nursery pan. And in my left hand, as I approach the edge of another band’s water, I carry a small notched bone-peg and tap it steadily against my collarbone.

*Tick. Tick. Tick.*

*I am here. I am not a hole in the world. My back is full of strangers, and I mean you no harm.*

They let me wade into the shallow mirror between their two rings—never quite inside either circle, always ankle-deep in the cold gap where the two chords collide. When the burrowers beneath the pan finally sag into their resting trough and the floor-tremor drops away, both bands open their bellows and offer their lineages into the sudden room beneath the air.

It hits my four ear-fans like a physical shiver. Two ancient, proud, grieving versions of the same vanished shoreline, sliding a quarter-tone apart. The shallow water around my cleft feet breaks into nervous, cross-hatched ripples. Around the edges of the pan, young listeners hunch their shoulders in distress, their intake slits flaring, waiting for the elder on one side or the other to snap the gathering shut with a sharp collar-click.

I listen first—always first. Not to decide which band has kept the truer stone, because both stones have been worn honest by the ribs that carried them. I listen for the narrow, trembling gap *between* their pitches, the phantom beat-frequency thrumming in the wet air that neither ring can hear as anything but pain.

Then I shift my breath from the upper bladder to the lower.

A muscle-ring beneath my left clavicle tightens, rotating the thin northern pebble half a millimeter inward. The heavy southern bone-pipe along my right rib opens just enough to catch the wet updraft. I lean slightly forward, knees springy, and slip a thin, braided third overtone directly into the jagged seam between the two bands.

It strains. My chest-membranes pull tight until a cold, sour ache blooms along the thin seam of my flank—an overtaxed, stinging bruise I feel only as a sharp chill under the skin, never knowing that in the lightless gloom a weak smear of spoiled-green chemical light has just blinked briefly along my ribs. I hold the bridge steady anyway, adjusting a forearm flute by the width of a hair as the mist thickens and drags the whole pan downward.

Slowly—never all at once—the ugly thrashing in the water smooths out.

Neither band drops its dead. Neither elder forces a pebble out of pitch. Instead, with the third thread running between them, the two drifted lineages stop colliding and begin to lean against one another, forming an older, wider interval neither ring knew it was still missing. The water rings turn round and clean. Somewhere in the northern circle, a young singer’s ear-fans tilt two degrees toward the southern circle in sudden recognition, catching the ghost of a shared ancestor ringing cleanly through the seam of my chest.

When the deep burrowers wake beneath the pan and push the trough-speech back up into the high, clipped chatter of the day, the two rings begin to trade—cracked grazers, warm strips of stunned worm, news of a hammer-walker whose bellows have begun to leak near the eastern gully. Sometimes, before they disperse into the fog, an elder from one band or the other wades quietly over and presses a worn, slightly cracked stone into an empty dimple along my lower back—a voice that no longer quite fits inside their own tightening chord, too precious to drop into the slush, safer carried in the crowded scaffolding of mine.

I never stay long after the pan settles. The bands are grateful, and they are glad to see me go. A body that carries too many lineages sounds a little eerie when the work is done; even my resting hum has a slight, restless shimmer to it, like water over two meeting currents.

So I wade back up through the clammy dark to my shear-ledge, tapping my bone-peg softly against my collarbone until the wild flutes of my own shelf answer me in their familiar, divided keys.

There is one thing I do there that I never do where another band’s ear-fans might catch the wobble in the air.

After a long mist-tide, when the deep drummers on the western side drop into their trough while the eastern side still hammers, my back sometimes feels unbearably heavy—not with stone, though the pipes and pebbles have weight, but with the sheer press of borrowed air. Every breath I take wakes six dead elders from four different pans. The northern pebble wants to pull bright; the southern bone-pipe wants to groan low; the chipped river-stone buzzes faintly against my ulna with a grief that happened before my mother had her First Seating. There are moments when I can no longer tell where their resonance ends and my own hollow chest begins.

At those times, I untie the small, damp square of stalker-plush from my waist.

My fingers shudder slightly as they touch the bristles—that old, primal nausea, the feeling that the tips of my own hands have dropped out of the world. I would never lay it over my chest-valves. That is for the dying, the final mercy before the ribbon-crawlers come. Instead, I press the damp plush lightly against my outer shoulder sockets, one side and then the other, muffling the traded stones, the rescued pipes, the worn relics of estranged kin.

For three breaths, the borrowed voices fall away.

It is dizzying, like stepping out onto a slick ledge in a sudden draft. And then, underneath the muffled hush of the outer sockets, I feel it—tiny, slightly pitted, gripped tight in the oldest muscle-ring right between my shoulder blades. The small, unremarkable pebble an elder pressed into my shivering back during my own First Seating, when the band stood around a silver pan and cupped their chords beneath my raw, trembling note so the basin wouldn’t swallow it.

I drive a single quiet breath past that stone alone.

It is not grand. It is a little thin, a little crooked from years of leaning toward other people’s pitches. Yet it is unmistakably mine—the small, stubborn hinge on which all the other stones turn. I listen to it ring for three, four, five beats against the torn floor-pulse of the shear-ledge, letting it steady my ribs. Then I pull the stalker-plush away with a sharp, grateful exhale, tie it tightly back into the sinew at my waist, and let the borrowed dead rush back into my breath alongside the bruising, roaring, magnificent din of the valley.

I know how it will end, too, and the knowledge doesn’t chill me.

Already, during the coldest seepages, I notice that the slick secretion around my cleft ankles takes longer to film back over after a hard wade. One season—not soon, not yet, but coming as surely as the mist-tides—I will feel the first tiny, calcified rim of a larval flute cementing itself to the pale skin above my heel, and I will not scrape it away.

I won’t go down to a nursery pan to sit out my cementing. I will stay right here on the shear-ledge, facing slightly across the slope where the two burrower tracts rub out of phase. Season by season, the wild spiral flutes will climb my shins and lock my triple-hinged knees cleanly into the banded marl. Because the western gully spills cold mist across my left leg ten minutes before the warm air lifts off the eastern flats beneath my right, the wild shells encrusting me will grow slightly out of tune with one another—one flank tuned flat to the damp, the other sharp to the chill.

I like the thought of that.

Long after I can no longer wade across the flats to meet estranged bands, my lower body will become a permanent bridge in the living pavement—half chosen stones, half wild flutes, anchored straight across the seam. Young drift-menders, or lost scouts caught in a mist-tide, will wade up through the pewter dark and press their smooth, eyeless brows against my crusted knees, listening not for one pure, unchanging truth, but for how two stubborn, mismatched halves of a roaring world can breathe inside the same chest without breaking.

And when my bellows finally sigh out and stay still, the ribbon-crawlers will come, and the living kin will gently unseat the crowded stones from my cooling back—splitting them up again, carrying the northern pebble back north and the southern bone-pipe back south, and maybe pressing my small, crooked center-stone into some shivering child’s shoulder blade at a wide pan—while the wild flutes finish sealing my ribs smoothly into the shelf, humming softly in the cold dark wind until the stone itself forgets where I ended and the valley began.
