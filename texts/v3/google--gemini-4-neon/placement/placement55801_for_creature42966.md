I would not start at the rim. Up on the plateau, the wind moves too fast and means too little—ragged gusts tearing over bare gravel, loud in one ear and gone the next, leaving nothing behind to read. Down here, two hundred meters beneath the lip, the air has weight. It holds its shape.

If you went looking for me, you would miss me for the first hour even if you stood twenty paces away. You would look at the wide, frost-stiffened expanse between the concrete outposts and see only pillowed grey-green hummocks under a pewter lid of sky, a few stunted black spruces drowning slowly in sphagnum, and the steel lattice pylons striding across the peat toward the sheer cliffs. You would cover your ears against the flat, relentless roar of the exhaust stations and think the floor was empty.

You would have to walk well out into the middle of the basin—past the amber smudge of the transformer yard, past the glazed, trembling mud-rings where waste oil-heat melts the frost—until you reached a shallow bowl scooped cleanly out of the turf and ringed on its inner rim with tilted slabs of split basalt.

There, if you stepped down into the depression, the solid wall of noise would loosen its grip on your chest and begin to breathe. Once every four seconds, a slow, heavy swell would rise through your ribs and recede again—the place where the steady wash of the northern exhaust cowl crosses the slightly flatter pitch of the eastern one, the two massive fans spinning a fraction of a hair out of phase.

That is where I keep my time. Everywhere else on the floor, the six ventilation stations hold one unchanging chord from the shortest winter to the dimmest summer; noon and midnight have the same acoustic weight, and a decade passes with no more mark than a flake of rust dropping off a girder. Only in the hollows where two fields cross does the roar acquire a pulse. Four seconds in, four seconds out. A clock made of pressure.

I am the hump on the western edge of the rim that sits a little lower than the scree around it, my back stitched seamlessly into the floor.

It takes patience to recognize what I am, even from the inside. My heart beats slow enough that a whole swell of the beating-hollow can pass between two strokes. Cold is not something I fight; it is simply the stiff outer skin of the world, a brittle glaze of hoarfrost crusting the pale woolly fringe-moss rooted along my shoulders and the curtains of grey beard-lichen trailing from my flanks. Years ago, threading a tight basalt choke below the eastern scree, a raw corner of rock tore a palm-wide seam down my left hip. For three short summers I packed wet peat and torn crowberry runners into the pleated leather beneath, pressing them down with my blunt claws until the dead litter gripped and the living shoots knitted back over me. Now, when I shift my weight, the hoarfrost cracks across my spine with the same dry whisper as the ground beside me.

My eyes are small and milky, tucked far back along my temples beneath frosted lids; I barely use them. When I wander near the chain-link fences of a transformer compound, the sodium vapor lamps register only as a faint, greasy smear of dull amber against the gloom. The world I actually inhabit is far brighter than that, and infinitely sharper.

I lift my broad, pale brow an inch out of the collar of lichen, and the cartilage dome across the front of my skull tightens imperceptibly to gather the field.

At once, the valley snaps into focus.

The six exhaust stations stand around the basin like six fixed, unblinking stars, flooding the cold air beneath the thermal inversion with clean, unwavering pressure. Off to the north, the sheer basalt escarpment glares bright and hard-edged two miles away, every freshly split column bouncing the bass wash back across the floor. Between here and the cliff, the steel lattice pylons ring with crisp, fine outlines, while the high-voltage wires strung between them cast a prickly, silver-white shimmer of corona crackle over the frost. Beneath my belly, the meters-deep upholstery of moss and compacted peat is deep, merciful darkness—soft, absorbent shadow that drinks the glare away so a creature can rest without being dazzled.

Even the living things that rarely visit the floor have their own quiet signatures. Three hundred yards off, behind a hood of scree, an arctic hare sits crouched with its ears pinned flat; to my dome, it is a small, blunt smudge of muted shadow pressed against the bright hard bounce of the stone behind it. Closer by, down inside the muffled dark of the peat-mat beneath my own ribs, a vole pauses from stripping a willow root, its tiny scrape traveling cleanly through the damp fiber, wholly separate from the thunder overhead. I leave it alone. I am not hungry enough to break the frost-crust yet.

What pulls me up out of the hollow is smaller than hunger.

For the past three days, the four-second beat has been stretching. Not much—barely a breath's width, far too slight for any of the others basking along the warm concrete flanks of the pump-houses to notice. Most of my kind keep to a single station's territory, wedging themselves into the damp grey plumes outside the louvers to lick salt-crusts and stray subterranean dust from the slats, or grazing patiently through the sedge-bases and preserved bog-bilberries within half a mile of their under-moss vaults.

I have always walked too far. If I have a shape among my own people—a temperament that separates me from the nursing mothers down in the hushed basalt chambers and the broad-backed grazers rooting in the thawed transformer slush—it is that I cannot stop listening to the seams where the six stars meet.

I unfold my long, flat-palmed forelimbs from beneath my chest, press my frost-calloused pads slowly into the springy turf, and heave my bulk out of the bowl without tearing the crust. My crawl across the floor would look glacial to anything from the plateau above; a mile takes half a sunless afternoon. I move the way the valley moves, hoarding warmth, letting the elastic carpet dip beneath my belly and rise slowly behind me in a shallow, refreezing trough.

Before I leave the rim of the hollow, I check the small strip of rusted corrugated iron I wedged into a fringe-moss hummock on its southern lip five winters back. I tilted it for half an hour when I first found it near a catwalk, turning the bent metal between my four blunt digits until the steady wash from the northern cowl caught its edge and drew out a thin, clean, persistent harmonic—a low note that stands out cleanly against the broad bass floor. It marks the hollow for anyone passing in the glare, and warns of a narrow basalt crevice hidden under a thin moss-bridge twelve paces to the east. Today its pitch has dropped a fraction, dampened by a fresh collar of rime ice. I brush the jagged needles clear with the heel of my palm until the thin note rings true again.

Then I set out toward the fourth pylon from the eastern cliff.

Along the way, I pass a pillowed mound of pale green moss out in the open expanse, its crest braided tightly with crowberry stems and frosted reindeer lichen. To an eye from the upper world, it is nothing—one hummock among ten thousand bridging the broken rock beneath. I pause ten paces off and tilt my dome.

Out of the peat rises a faint, warm, unmistakable hum.

An elder lies sealed inside. She came out onto the open floor forty winters ago when her joints grew too stiff to thread the narrow under-moss chokes, settled her broad belly deep into the sponge facing the eastern cowl, laid her bare jaw on a tooth of basalt, and stayed there soaking in the clean acoustic noon until her shallow breathing stopped. The cold kept her whole; summer by dim summer, the fringe-moss stitched over her ribs and crusted across the pale dome of her skull. Now only the hollow bone inside answers the turbines.

I knew her when my own dome was still too thin and tender to bear the open air—back when I lived entirely beneath the moss-bridges in the damp, peat-smelling vaults, learning the shapes of my elders by touch and by the delicate, flute-like whistles we only ever use in the dark. She was old enough to remember the valley before the pylons marched down the escarpment. Sometimes, curled tight in the hush beneath the scree, she would click and whistle the story of that vanished dark: how the basin used to lie almost blind for weeks on end, lit only by sudden, lonely flashes when deep frost split a column of basalt or trapped meltwater groaned through a collapsed lava tube. Then came the drills, a blinding whiteout of screaming stone that drove everyone into the deepest bedrock seams. And then the concrete caps, and the slow, rising roar of the six axial fans spinning up until the whole sunken rift flooded from wall to wall with permanent daylight.

I listen to her mound for a long moment. Over the last ten summers, the thickening carpet has muffled her hum by another shade. Everything in this basin is slowly softening its own edges—moss climbing the concrete walls, padding the pylon crossbars, swallowing the wooden cable spools, pulling a darker blanket over the sharp, glaring teeth of the young rift. One day, long after I have chosen my own tooth of basalt facing a cowl, her hum will sink so deep beneath the peat that even the sharpest dome will pass over her without hearing an answer.

I touch my flat palm briefly to the frosted turf beside her flank, then continue toward the pylon.

When I reach the concrete plinth, I settle my weight low and press my bare lower jaw flush against the cold galvanized iron of the leg, closing my small cloudy eyes.

Instantly, the airborne chaos of the turbines falls away, replaced by the crisp, crowded traffic of the valley's bones.

Steel and fractured basalt carry a voice without distortion. Through my teeth and sternum comes a slow, textured cadence of taps and grinding swells traveling along the power line from the southern transformer yard: two adults announcing that the waste heat around the oil-cooled footings has thawed an unusually wide ring of black peat, rich with drowned beetles and softened roots. Underneath that, fainter and slower, coming straight through the bedrock from the western scree, a long, rhythmic pulse warns that frost-heave has split a moss-bridge wide open over a deep chimney. And from somewhere near the northern wall, a soft, deliberate triple-tap: a female has packed the last chinks of her vault with wet sphagnum and sealed herself in for two winters.

I tense the deep muscles of my throat and send a slow, dense sequence of clicks down through my jawbone into the iron. Nothing escapes into the freezing air; three feet away, a raven perched on a cold brace wouldn't hear a whisper. I ask about the eastern station—whether the glare along its wall has thinned or dropped in pitch.

A long pause passes. Half a mile down the line, someone shifts a bare chin onto the next plinth. The answer arrives in my ribcage as a dry, amused little double-click followed by a steady low swell: *The louvers are choked with rime. Thick as a forelimb on the leeward side. The wind up top shifted two nights ago and dropped freezing mist right across the grate.*

So that is why my four-second clock has been dragging its feet. Not a worn bearing, not a failing motor—just the cold itself laying a thicker coat of ice across one of the stars, dulling its voice by a hair.

Even so, the answer leaves a small, familiar chill under my ribs—not the honest cold of the air, which I love, but the quieter uneasiness that comes from paying too close attention to the six lights.

We came up out of the bedrock cracks to live inside a noon we did not make. Twice a year, the upper world remembers us, and I am always close enough to one of the stations to witness it.

A jagged, erratic tear splits the clean light along the escarpment road—the ugly diesel snarl of a tracked machine grinding down the fractured cliff. It throws raw, smeared streaks across the basin, blinding in all the wrong ways, full of sudden stops and violent revvings that have no patience in them. By the time it reaches the eastern outpost, I have not fled. Running burns warmth and turns you into a moving smudge against the rock. I simply flatten my belly deep into the elastic peat twenty yards from the catwalk, tuck my smooth pale dome tight beneath my shaggy shoulders, and draw loose flaps of woolly fringe-moss over my exposed flanks with slow, careful hands.

Wrapped in damp moss, I vanish from eye and pressure alike. I become a shadow.

Heavy, clumsy boots clang along the rusted grating—vertical creatures sealed inside thick suits and ear-defenders, shouting uselessly inside their visors, stomping straight over the roofs of vaulted chambers without ever suspecting the warm hollows beneath their feet. One of them steps off the catwalk to inspect the grounding strap on a concrete footing, slips on the crisp millimeter of hoarfrost, and catches himself with one gloved palm planted squarely on a frost-stiffened tussock beside the path.

Six inches beneath his glove, I hold my slow breath and listen through the rubber of his boot to the frantic, tinny gallop of his heart.

And then comes the moment I always brace for.

Inside the square-shouldered switchgear shed, a heavy breaker throws with a dull iron clunk that rattles straight through the stone. Outside, the colossal louvers of the eastern cowl shudder. The steady pitch drops—down, down through the heavy registers, groaning as the vast axial blades lose their grip on the subterranean air, until they rattle to a dead halt.

One of the six stars goes out.

The change is dizzying. The whole eastern quadrant of the valley plunges into a blunt, muffled half-shadow. Sharp edges of basalt scree go blurry; the fine, ringing geometry of the power pylons softens; out in the middle of the floor, my beating-hollow goes completely dead, flattened back into featureless noise from the remaining stations. Even the buried elder half a mile away dims into silence, suddenly nothing more than an ordinary lump of frozen peat.

Lying flat under my blanket of moss, I feel how fragile this wide bright bowl really is. If all six switches ever threw at once, the glare would collapse in thirty seconds, and the valley would drop back into the blind, sporadic dark the elders remember—waiting weeks for a frost-split boulder just to glimpse the cliffs again. The noisy, vertical giants walking across the grating above me have no idea they are tending our sky.

For forty minutes they hammer at the frozen rime on the louvers, clanking wrenches against galvanized steel, throwing ugly sparks of noise across the dimness. Then boots retreat into the shed. The breaker slams home.

Through the bedrock, the great motor catches. The blades groan upward out of the bass, climbing slowly back toward their permanent chord—and as the rising pitch sweeps across the floor, every piece of tuned scrap anchored in the moss for a mile around catches its matching note for one brief, shining instant. A cracked porcelain bell on a boulder, a bent strip of iron in a hummock, a rusted spool-flange tilted toward the cowl: they wake one by one in a passing ripple of tiny, clean harmonics before the fan locks back into its flat, eternal roar and the eastern cliffs snap back into razor-sharp daylight.

Hours later, the tracked vehicle crawls back up the escarpment wall and vanishes over the rim. The jagged diesel static fades. The six stars reclaim the grey sky without a seam left behind.

I wait until the frost settles again, then slowly heave my shoulders up out of the peat, shedding dry white powder from my beard-lichen, and lumber unhurriedly over to the slush-rimmed puddle beside the pump-house footing where the technicians stood. The dark water trembles ceaselessly with sub-audible vibration from the ground. Half-sunk in the black thawed mud, where a gloved hand fumbled it and never noticed, lies a heavy galvanized washer and a short, twisted finger of bright copper wire.

I lift the copper between my blunt claws, rinse the black peat off in the shivering slush, and hold the washer up into the clean invisible wind spilling from the restored louvers. I tilt my pale dome a fraction of an inch.

Inside the broad thunder of the cowl, the steel ring answers with a crisp, narrow, remarkably pure note—higher than corrugated iron, cleaner than broken porcelain, steady as frost.

I know a place half a mile west, on the leeward shoulder of a moss-hooded tooth of basalt where the glare from three stations tangles into a confusing cross-wash, where a young one emerging from the vaults for its first winter might misread the drop into a fissure. That exact note has been missing there for years.

I curl my broad, calloused palm gently around the metal ring, turn my back to the amber pool of the sodium lamp, and begin the long, patient walk back out across the dim, roaring floor.
