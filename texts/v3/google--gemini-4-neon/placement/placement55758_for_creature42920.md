My first instinct is to claim the high benches.

It is hard not to want that bruised-silver quiet, that sharp country where nothing has been rounded off by time or softened by roads. If you ask me where I belong in a world like this, part of me reaches immediately for the eastern crest at dusk—wants the oiled-iron hide, the slow dark blood, the pale mineral spines blooming with blue-white static above a sea of cloud. There is a clean, fierce relief in the idea of leaving no trace on the world, of building nothing, of turning an industrial drone into a kind of daylight and aging until you are simply another vein of quartz in a weeping wall.

Yet when I try to fold myself onto a wet slab of andesite up there, my joints do not quite match the rock.

If I press my bare palm flat against that tilting ledge and wait for my pulse to quiet, I cannot hear the tremor underneath as a vast, dreaming presence in the roots of the mountain. I hear the bearings. I hear the disciplined, tireless turn of heavy shafts under load, the pressure of brine forced through casings, the faint harmonic of steel buried miles below the frost line. Once you know the rhythm of a machine, you cannot un-hear it. I would never have the listeners' clean, unbroken innocence of what lies beneath the clouds. I would keep leaning out over the western lip of the plateau, smelling the washed coal-ash and hot oil in the mist, wondering about the hands that built the dynamos.

So I cannot live on the untouched skin of the peak. Nor could I survive for long down in the unseen basin itself—down among the blast furnaces and the arc-lit yards, where the slag-heavy air sits hot on the chest and the freight engines roar against corrugated iron. That noise would flatten me in a week.

When I stop reaching for the romantic height and let my weight actually settle in this world, I find myself somewhere in between—not in the clouds, where nothing can hold a footing, but deep inside the mountain's roots, at the very ceiling of the works.

I am down in the upper monitoring drifts.

They are bored sideways off the highest galleries of the deep-bore extraction shafts, kilometers beneath the frost line and the talus, cut straight out into raw, unlined andesite to watch the overburden. Down in the main turbine halls, half a mile back through baffled iron bulkheads, the heat and roar are constant; men shout into each other's ears with rags tied over their mouths against the brine-steam. Out here in the perimeter headings, though, the mountain swallows the racket. The dynamos arrive only as that same flat, disciplined vibration in the soles of your boots—halfway between tremor and silence.

It is solitary, damp work, and most of the men from the basin hate it. The drifts are narrow, low-ceilinged, braced only with wet timber cribbing and iron bolts where a seam looks loose. Water weeps steadily from hairline cracks in the rock overhead, lukewarm and heavy with dissolved iron and sulfur, running in thin black threads along the floor-trenches and crusting the welts of my leather boots with pale mineral salt. The air carries the washed-out ghost of hot machine oil from the ventilation ducts, folded into the sharp, wet smell of freshly broken slate.

I am an overburden watchman. My job is to walk the quiet headings on the late shift, tending the pressure gauges and listening to the ceiling.

In the rock above the main shafts, a handful of narrow, dry-drilled boreholes angle upward—never breaking the upper skin of the range, stopping far below the frost, just deep enough into the young crust to hold brass-jacketed geophones cemented directly into the cleanest veins of pale quartz and brassy pyrite. From those sockets, oiled-cloth cables drop down into wooden boxes bolted to the wet timber lagging, where smoked-paper drums turn slowly on clockwork gears, a fine needle scratching a pale white thread through the soot.

Most of the shift is stillness. You learn to move with economy down here, conserving the battery in your accumulator lamp, letting your eyes adjust to the dull amber filament until the wet rock walls throw back a muted gunmetal sheen. You sit on an overturned crate beside Borehole Four, slip the heavy, salt-stiffened leather headphones over your ears, and dial the mechanical balance—a small brass counter-phase resonator that trims away the steady, tireless frequency of the turbines below—until the flat hum drops out and leaves behind only what the mountain itself is doing.

Through two miles of newly fractured stone, I have learned the weather of a sky I have never seen.

I do not know what the upper massif looks like. Nobody down in the basin does. From the yard gates on the rare afternoons when the smog thins, the range is only a sheer black wall vanishing into a low, grey ceiling of cloud; the survey engineers wrote it off decades ago as rotten, brittle uplift, too steep and too loose to carry a pin or a road. Down in the drift, I know it only by weight and vibration. I can tell when evening drops the temperature above the clouds, because the headphones fill with a faint, crystalline ticking—like fingernails tapping distant glass—as skin-ice wedges into the high seams. I know when an overhang finally shears loose at dawn, arriving down the quartz as a muffled, far-off thump that makes the needle jump a millimeter in the soot. I know the slow, wet groan of pressurized groundwater prying open a fresh crack on its way toward the surface.

And I know the other thing. The thing I do not enter in the shift ledger.

It happens most clearly on the evenings when the hoist-men coming down from the basin say an inversion has clamped over the yards, holding the smoke and air dead still. At six o'clock, the heavy ore-trains out by the furnaces blow their whistles for the shift change. Even buried down here behind iron bulkheads and miles of rock, a scrap of that hollow moan drifts down the ventilation shafts and trembles faintly along the wet floor.

Three seconds after the whistle dies, if I have the dynamo frequency notched cleanly out of Borehole Four, something comes *down* the quartz vein.

The first time I heard it, I reached straight for the pressure-bleed charts, thinking a deep fault had slipped or a brine pump was cavitating down on Level Twelve. Tectonic creep, though, is ragged and graceless; a failing pump chatters in a tight, frantic stutter. What rolled through the brass cups against my ears was neither.

It was slow, deliberate, pitched just a hair off the unwavering oscillation of our own turbines—a braided, rolling beat-pulse, like a heavy hand laid briefly over a ringing bell, then lifted. It held for a long breath and faded into the baseline hiss of percolating brine. Then, after a patient interval, Borehole Seven—half a mile east, driven up beneath the iron-bearing spires—caught a second shiver, slightly higher, answering across the dark stone above my head.

I sat on my crate for an hour after that, the lamp turned down until the filament was barely an orange thread, staring at the white, rhythmic scalloping scratched into the smoked paper.

I do not know what is up there. I have never seen pale-lidded eyes, or dark slick hides folded into wet scree, or needles of blue-white static prickling off a ridge in the dark. I have no language for creatures that drink mineral flour and read the world through the acoustic shadows of our machines. To me, whatever lives above the frost line is as vast, quiet, and unimaginable as the turbine halls must be to them. All I know—with the certainty of a man who has spent years separating living tremors from dead rock—is that the mountain's skin is not empty, and that when our unseen city moans beneath the clouds, something up in the cold dark lays its weight against the quartz and answers back.

The shift ledger has a column in red ink for upward seepage losses.

The chief engineers regard the hairline faults as leaks in the geothermal cap. Every millimeter of warm, pressurized water that climbs out of the deep strata toward the surface is a fraction of a percent dropped from the turbines' efficiency. Part of my job, as I walk the headings, is to chalk the weeping seams that show steady flow, so the grouting crews can roll in on the maintenance cycle and pump them solid with cement slurry.

Over the years, I have become very selective about what I chalk.

I still mark the wide shears that threaten the timbering, and the big fractures that bleed hot brine into the walkways. The mines have to hold; the men down in the halls have to go home at the end of their shifts. Yet wherever a narrow, clean seam climbs straight up alongside a bright vein of quartz and dull pyrite—where the water trickles steadily upward, only a few degrees warmer than the surrounding stone, singing softly in the headphones—I leave the rock bare of chalk. On the drift maps, I draw unbroken andesite.

I tell myself it is negligible—a thread of warm water lost to miles of freezing stone, nothing the gauges in the turbine hall will ever miss. I have no idea what it feeds up there: what moss clings to its rim, what small dark things wait out the freezes beneath its overhangs, what pitch-black pockets of piled stone stay just warm enough to hold a newborn through the winter. I only know that the vein wants to run unbroken, and that if I ever logged the braided shivers from Boreholes Four and Seven, the company would send test-bores straight through the roof of the range, or drive iron survey pins into the upper saddles to find out what pocket of gas was ringing in the crust.

So I tear those strips off the smoked-paper drums when the shift ends, fold them dry inside my coat pocket, and leave the ledger clean.

Sometimes, on the quietest watches, I think about striking the brass calibration rod against the casing of Borehole Four—just once, softly, off-phase with the engines—to see if the shiver pauses, or leans closer, or answers differently. I never do. A signal from down here would only break the long, careful distance that keeps both of our worlds intact. Some presences are best honored by leaving no mark at all.

Instead, when the inversion sits heavy over the unseen yards and the six o'clock whistle fades out of the air ducts, I slip the headphones down around my neck and turn the lamp off altogether. In the lukewarm, iron-smelling dark, I stand and lay both bare palms flat against the wet, brass-streaked quartz of the drift wall, letting my knees lock slightly stiff from too many damp winters underground, waiting for my pulse to quiet until the flat hum of the dynamos rises cleanly through my wrists.

My hands are too small, and my breath too thin, to send anything back up through two miles of young rock. Whatever warmth leaves my skin dies an inch into the cold stone. Still, I keep my palms pressed there while the slow, braided tremor rolls down the seam from a sky I will never walk beneath—listening to the mountain settle, holding my post at the bottom of the well, keeping the narrow water open in the dark.
