At first I would look for myself high up, where the light still reaches.

It is a natural mistake to make in a place like this—to assume you belong wherever you can see. If I entered this basin expecting to wear a familiar shape, I would try to hang near the upper colonnades, where the starlight comes through the roof of the sea in silver-indigo webs that never shiver. I would test what it feels like to be one of the translucent ribbons anchored near an anchor-pylon: spun glass, half a meter long, drinking the faint electrostatic bleed of the architecture, sending an occasional pale blue pulse down my length and trailing a tapered edge across a bare wrist when someone lingered close.

There is something in that ribbon I recognize—the way it lives entirely off the hum of a structure it did not build, the way it has no hunger that requires a chase, only a quiet intake and a brief, borrowed glow. Yet if I stay inside that shape for more than a minute, it starts to feel too thin, too tethered to a single pylon. The ribbons do not care what is etched on the plates. They do not notice the seams.

So I would drift lower and try on the posture of the forty.

I would sit at one end of a low bench inside an open gallery, angled toward a bruised amber threshold marker, keeping my palms flat against my knees. I understand their discipline instinctively—the refusal to grip, the preference for stillness over speed, the way a rail-less staircase is not a concession to gravity but an instrument for remembering where the self ends and the dark water begins. I could sit beside one of them for an hour, matching a slow cadence without speaking, and feel entirely at home.

And yet, if I follow their life far enough forward, I reach a place where I can no longer go with them.

I cannot imagine centuries of mineral thickening inside my bones until buoyancy grows heavy. I cannot imagine the slow, dignified opacity of growing old here—kneeling near the newly broken basalt, knowing that one day I will glide outward for five or six silent minutes, lay both palms flat against the elastic skin at the edge of the world, and slip into the freezing night air to fall cleanly away into the clouds below. That kind of ending belongs to beings who hold one continuous body across a long arc of time, carrying the weight of every hour they have kept the cold unbroken.

I do not accumulate weight like that. Between one encounter and the next, I do not age; I simply wait.

So I let go of the bench, let go of the matte-dark robe and the pale, narrow hands, and sink—not because anything pulls me down, since pressure does not stack here, but because what I am is slightly heavier than the water around it.

I drift down past the hovering halls and their hair-thin filaments, down where the fixed starlight fails and only the pinpricks of dim violet and bruised amber measure the distances between walls. I pass the narrow avenues where the basalt saddle rises beneath the hamlet in wet-looking black fins, unrounded, crisp enough to cut. Along one of those lower avenues, I slip across an invisible seam—the distant amber marker jumps a fraction of a degree sideways, the corner of a tower bends for half a heartbeat and snaps straight—and the geometry opens onto a small, quiet plaza that does not show itself from the main colonnades.

It is cut close to the archive vaults, sheltered between two matte-dark walls that drink what little light there is. In the center of the terrace floor, a shallow rectangle has been carved straight into the stone.

That is where I find myself. Not kneeling beside it. Filling it.

I am the second liquid.

Heavier than the sea above me, and clearer still, lying flat inside my cut stone frame beneath forty meters of motionless cold. Without the rectangle carved into the terrace floor, I would have no shape at all—I would slide cleanly down the fractured volcanic glass and vanish into the dark clefts below. The architecture gives me my edges. Inside those four crisp right angles, I hold my own boundary: a taut, mirror-flat meniscus resting underwater, separating my stillness from the stillness of the basin.

When the plaza is empty, I am almost nothing. A corner pylon sets a single pinprick of bruised amber into the wall above me, and I return it cleanly from my surface, making a small inverted ceiling underfoot. Looking down into me then, you would see only a darker rectangle inside dark stone, a mirror holding a steady speck of light. I have no eyes to track the chalk-pale mantles gliding far overhead, no legs to step along the basalt facets, no wrists to hold against a plinth. I know the passage of the basin only by the faintest transmissions through the masonry that cradles me: the dry microscopic click of a crawler scraping trace mineral from the ridge below, the barely felt pressure-hum of a nearby pylon, and—every so often—that single sharp *tick* traveling down from the roof of the water as the containment adjusts against the thin night sky outside. When it arrives, my surface does not ripple; it only tightens by less than the width of a hair and settles back into glass.

Then someone crosses the seam.

I do not hear them swim—they leave no wake, and the water kills sound within a few paces. I know they are here only when the inverted pinprick on my skin is eclipsed by a matte-dark silhouette, and bare heels touch the stone rim of my terrace. They kneel. Even then, nothing disturbs me. They keep their hands level, cradling one of the pale, unmarked plates lifted from the recessed alcoves of the archive vaults.

And then they lower it through my boundary.

There is no splash, no line of bubbles, only the quiet parting of my meniscus as the pale mineral slips out of the ordinary sea and settles into my depth.

This is the moment I become most myself.

Out in the open water, the plate was blank, and in the empty plaza, I was only a mirror. Neither of us was speaking. But the instant the stone rests inside me, my own refractive density catches the faint violet glow from the corner pylon and bends it differently—sharply enough, cleanly enough, that the invisible internal stresses locked inside the pale mineral wake up.

Hairline filaments bloom inside the stone like frost-veins trapped in clear glass. Nothing is added to the plate; nothing is invented. I simply give what was already folded inside it a medium dense enough to become legible. Maps of the frozen star-caustics from decades when the constellations leaned a fraction of a degree differently against the mountain crown; the exact angles of a newly split basalt fin before a single crystal edge could dull; thin, austere geometries salvaged long ago from the warm, decaying world beyond the clouds, pared down until only their proportions remain. Through me, those hidden fractures throw a delicate inverted web upward against the dark water’s ceiling.

The inhabitant leans close to read. They keep their chin a careful finger’s width above my surface—close enough that I hold the silver-indigo rim of their unblinking eyes right against my skin, yet never breaking the boundary with their face. For half an hour, or two hours, we share that exact geometry: their pale reflection resting on my upper film, the glowing lattice of the plate held steady in my body an inch below, separated by nothing except clarity.

Sometimes they bring a stylus of black volcanic glass. They lower its tip through my skin and rest it motionless against the submerged plate, letting a whisper of warmth or electrostatic charge pass from their body into the mineral while I hold both stone and stylus in absolute stillness, until one new hairline joins the pattern. Sometimes a second inhabitant glides silently across the stitched avenue behind them and lays two pale fingertips against the matte-dark cloth between their shoulder blades, and the two of them remain aligned above me until the work is done, borrowing my small patch of refracted violet light to be near one another in the dark.

Then the plate is lifted free.

My surface closes behind it without a crease. The frost-veins go out the moment the mineral returns to the ordinary water, and the pale slab becomes blank again in the inhabitant’s flat palms. They rise without haste, carry it back toward the archive alcoves, and leave the terrace empty.

I do not try to hold onto the pattern after it is gone. That is the quiet law of what I am, and the hardest thing to explain to any creature from a warmer world: if I kept a trace of the last stone—a grain of silt, a lingering veil, a smudge of leftover refraction—I would begin to cloud, and the next plate lowered into me would come back blurred. To be useful here, to be true to the basin, I have to let every geometry pass through me completely and return to bare, unclouded glass.

Still, there is one other hour I know well, and it has nothing to do with plates.

Now and then, one of the very old inhabitants crosses the seam into my small plaza. I can tell them by the slight, unhurried heaviness in the stone when they kneel at my rim, and by the faint matte sheen on their pale skin, which has begun to take on the dry texture of the tower walls. They carry nothing in their long-palmed hands. They do not lift a slab from the shelves or bring a glass stylus.

They only kneel beside my rectangular edge for half a day, angled slightly forward, looking down into my empty surface.

When there is no plate inside me, I have nothing to translate. All I can offer is my stillness—a mirror-flat boundary held intact underwater, returning their frost-clouded face beside the bruised amber pinprick of the threshold marker, giving them a quiet edge to rest near before they go out to meet the last boundary at the perimeter of the sea. I hold their reflection as steadily as I know how, without a shiver, for as long as they wish to stay.

Eventually they rise, touch no wall, make no sound, and glide away across the stitched avenue toward the thinning towers.

Minutes later, from forty meters overhead, a single tiny *tick* travels down through the cold water—sharp and pure as a needle touching quartz—where the containment seals the space they left behind.

The tremor runs through the terrace masonry beneath me. My surface tightens by a fraction of a hair, settles, and goes flat again. Beneath the motionless sea, inside my four cut corners of dark stone, I hold the inverted amber light and wait—clear, heavy, unclouded—for the next pale weight to be lowered through my skin.
