I think I would find myself at the lip of the shaft first, before I even knew who I was—kneeling in dry wild thyme on the sun-baked slope, resting both forearms on the knee-high square of dressed limestone while hot mist rises into my face.

At first I might imagine I am only a passerby who wandered off the hillside track and heard the earth roaring beneath her boots. You would lean over out of simple disbelief: a dry scrub hill, cicadas sawing in the olive trees, and then this neat stone collar enclosing a throat of blinding whitewashed light and a thunder you can feel in your knees.

But the longer I look down into the turquoise blaze, the less my eyes behave like a stranger’s. I don’t just see glare and racing nets of light. I look straight past the silver static of the plunging column to the corners. I check the arrises where the south wall meets the floor. I check the chalky line of rime at the water’s edge to gauge how many fingers of lime the spring has laid down since the last moon. My gaze travels along the submerged east stair and catches on the faint curved arcs left by a cedar float—and my right wrist remembers the exact drag of wet hydraulic plaster under wood.

That is who I would be. Not the master engineer who drew the conduit on waxed linen and collected his silver from the town council, and not a bather down in the valley waiting for clear warm water. I would be the lime-worker who finished the chamber—and who stayed on the hill afterward as the line’s keeper, walking the terracotta run from the tapped vein down to the first settling screen.

I remember the room before it had a voice.

For six days it was cool, dim, and small enough to cross in six strides, lit only by the square of sky overhead and two tallow lamps set on the first baffle. There was no thunder then—only the whisper of slaked lime mixed with crushed tile-dust, the ring of a steel trowel, the distant bark of a shepherd’s dog dropping down the shaft from another world. I knew every stone behind that creamy coat before I hid it. I scored the sockets around the clay inflow pipe and the hammered bronze overflow bars with a pointed chisel so the mortar would bite. On the last afternoon I backed out up the narrow east stair, kneeling on each tread to smooth the one below it with a damp wooden float so not a single bootprint remained, then climbed the rope-ladder up the whitewashed chimney and pulled it after me.

Two days later, they knocked the timber plug out of the uphill intake.

In thirty seconds, the room I had known with my palms and knees became somewhere no breathing body could survive. The quiet stone box filled with screaming heat, blinding light, and a pressure that shook the grass around the coping. You build a thing out of mud, lime, and rock, and the moment the hill enters it, it stops belonging to you.

A thermal conduit needs watching in its first year. Hot mineral water is impatient; it hunts for every lazy seam, every hollow pocket behind a coat of plaster, every bad joint in the clay pipe. So twice a week I walk up the slope with a leather satchel—measuring cord, a small copper-headed hammer, a wax tally-book—to check the intake gate, clear the mesh at the downstream inspection pit, and lean over the square mouth of the settling chamber while the noon sun stands straight down the shaft.

It takes months before I begin to suspect that the room is not empty.

The first strangeness is downstream, fifty paces below the chamber, where the outflow pipe opens briefly into a shallow stone pit fitted with a fine copper screen to catch clay chips before the water reaches the baths. Usually I pinch out slivers of terracotta, grains of mortar-flour, or bright emerald threads torn from the spout. One morning I lift out a tattered ribbon of something pale, slick, and nearly weightless. Between my thumb and forefinger it feels like wet bladder-skin, except that as the hill wind dries it, it turns papery and brittle, glinting faintly like flakes of mica. Held up against the sun, it shows the ghostly impression of delicate, hair-thin ribs.

I don’t write it in the tally-book. I fold it into a dry twist of linen in my pocket and walk back uphill to the coping.

The second strangeness is my own stairs.

Every chamber like this ought to gather a slow drift of pale silt in its lee corners and along the sheltered risers of the steps. That is what the baffles are for, and that is why the stair exists: once a year, or once every two years, we are supposed to dam the uphill spring, drain the tank, and go down with wooden scrapers to haul the lime-mud out in buckets. Yet leaning over the glare at midday, shading my brow with a lime-scarred hand, I see that the submerged east risers are spotless. Not merely washed by the current—*buffed*. Even the faint wooden-float arcs I left on the fourth tread gleam brighter than the mortar beside them, as though someone had been down there with a linen cloth in the dark.

And then, on a windless noon in high summer, my masonry bends.

I am staring straight down through the clear oily ribbons of heat, checking the mortar joint behind the first low baffle, when the straight line of the fourth step-riser behind it thickens, warps, and stays warped. My stomach drops. A bowed wall in a newly plastered tank means the backing stone has shifted under thermal load; it means we will have to shut the line, dig out the hill, tear down six days of my best work.

I brace my boots against the grass and lean farther over the hot mist, squinting until my eyes stream against the turquoise fire. And then I see what does not move.

Tucked into the roaring lee of the baffle, where ten thousand silver bubbles tear upward through the plunging column, a neat curved double row of them refuses to rise. They hang in a trembling crescent, pulsing in place like beads of quicksilver sewn into invisible cloth.

As I watch, the bend in my step-riser detaches itself from the stair.

It does not swim the way a fish swims. It lifts off the plaster in one slow, unhurried peel, catches the downward roar off the north wall, and tilts out into the center of the sun-pillar. I still cannot truly see it—only the edges where its folded clearness turns milky as thick blown glass, and the crisp chalky rim along its margins matching the mineral crust on my walls. As it kites through the blinding column of midday light, its curved, watery body gathers the sun and throws doubled skeins of fierce white fire across the plastered vault I smoothed with my own hands.

My hand drops away from my copper hammer. I stay kneeling on the dry thyme until my knees go numb, watching it bank smoothly short of the hungry bronze grate and ride the rising current back along the south face.

I do not know what it is. I have worked quarries and deep wells; I know the hills are riddled with warm black cracks that never see sky, and that water brings up things the town has no words for. What I do know, instantly and with a strange, quiet certainty, is what will happen if I report it. The bath-overseers will not care that it is eating the loose mortar-flour out of the corners or keeping the steps cleaner than a scraper ever could. They will hear that a pale, eyeless thing wider than a mason’s cloak lives in the settling box, and they will shut the uphill sluice, drop quicklime down the shaft, and kill it before evening.

So I become a different kind of keeper.

I stay through the afternoon one day until the second clock turns. I hear the terracotta conduit drop a half-tone in pitch through the soil beneath my feet; I watch the waterline climb a hand’s breadth up the creamy walls, drowning the geometric lines of pale velvet moss I used to think I ought to scrape away. The bronze sluice begins its deep, gulping rattle. The whole stone coping shivers under my palms.

Out of old mason’s habit—checking for a loose block or a singing crack in the vault—I lay my cheek and jawbone flat against the sun-warmed limestone rim.

Through the brutal bass of the plunging waterfall, something else rises straight through the dressed stone and into my teeth and collarbone.

It is not a splash or a pipe-groan. It is a clean, piercing, glassy hum—a narrow silver thread of resonance riding right through the center of the thunder. It holds steady, ringing against the flat plastered planes six paces apart, caught by the sharp arrises I pulled with my steel blade and bounced straight back, bright and crisp and unmuffled, until the whole sunken cube rings like a struck vessel.

Listening with my face pressed to the hot rock, I understand what I have actually built.

I thought I built a silt-trap. What it became, the moment a torn knot of deep-earth flesh fell out of the clay spout and learned how to survive the glare, is a stone drum—an echoing room where a blind thing from the pitch-dark fissures can sing its old flood-song and be answered, not by its own kind, but by the crisp geometry of young plaster. Every sharp corner I was proud of is a mirror for its voice. Every float-mark on the east stair is a ridge it reads with its whole clear belly.

After that afternoon, my work on the hillside quietly reshapes itself around keeping this chamber undisturbed.

I still walk the line twice a week. I still keep the wax tally-book—only now my entries are careful, deliberate silences. *Silt behind north baffle negligible. East stair clean. No drainage required this season.* None of it is even a lie; the creature swallows the pale grit from the gyres for its own ballast and sweeps the steps brighter than I ever could. We are, without ever agreeing to it, partners in the upkeep of the tank.

At the downstream screen I go early, before the morning bath-boys arrive, and pick the translucent shed ribbons off the copper mesh myself. I lay them in the sun on a flat boundary stone until they curl and dry, studying the faint white calcite veins before letting the hillside wind carry them off into the scrub.

Up in the light-shaft, in a hairline mortar crack two yards below the coping, two small maidenhair ferns have taken root, their fine fronds trembling nonstop in the warm updraft. A proper mason would reach down with a hooked knife, scrape out the roots, and point the seam with fresh lime before they pry the whitewashed blocks apart. I leave them alone. Sometimes I watch a single drop of cool condensation gather at a frond’s tip, let go, and fall straight down through the rising mist until it punches into the blazing turquoise below. I like the thought of that tiny drop carrying a bitter speck of dry summer dust or a fern-spore into a world that cannot imagine leaves, or sky, or dry air.

Once in a while—not often, only on clear noons when I have lingered too long—I lean my shoulder slowly out across the whitewashed lip of the shaft.

Down in that blazing box, there are no real shadows. When I lay my silhouette across the sun-pillar, a crisp dark bar cuts straight through the liquid light. Beneath the dazzle, I see the suspended crescent of silver bubbles dip an inch toward the floor as the water inside its folds cools by a breath. I hold still for three heartbeats, letting that small cool darkness rest across it like an open palm that never touches, and then I draw back and give it the sun again.

I never try to reach down with a pole. I never drop pebbles to see what it will do. There is nothing in my dry, loud, sharp-eyed world that it needs from me except the steady flow of the hill, the open square of sky, and the quietness of being left unfound.

Years from now, the chamber will no longer be young. The fingernail’s width of chalky rime will thicken into heavy, rounded collars of travertine; the razor-sharp arrises will soften under pale mineral crust; the curved float-marks on the east stairs will vanish beneath new stone laid down by the water itself. As the flat planes grow rounder and rougher, the room’s echo will change—growing warmer, dimmer, less piercing—and my own hands will stiffen in much the same slow way.

Until then, I am the one kneeling beside the square stone collar in the dry fennel, keeping the tallies quiet, leaving the ferns unpulled, and laying my cheek against the sun-warmed coping whenever the spring swells—holding that glassy, singing note in my jawbone for twelve minutes at a time, so that somewhere above the bright plaster walls, the song does not only come back to itself.
