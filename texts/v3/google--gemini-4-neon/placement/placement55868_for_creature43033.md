My first instinct is to stand on the rim of the basin, shading my eyes against the plain afternoon glare, and imagine walking down into the noise. That is the habitual shape of arrival: two boots on springy root-cobbles, lungs working in the hot pine-and-pollen air, a hand trailing along a sweating pith-wall in an underpass until the varnish lifts and shows itself to be alive.

But if I sit with that image for more than a few seconds, it starts to feel borrowed—like a coat too heavy and damp for what I actually am.

I would not arrive in this valley with a pulse muffled inside meat. I would not step into one of those folded courtyards and cast a dull, wet silence across the cobbles, watching three tiers of amber listeners slam shut because my breathing ruins the pitch of the square. When I look honestly at the valley—at its frantic cellulose, its deafening hydraulics, its dry, upright citizens—I do not recognize myself in the traveler. I recognize myself in the ringing.

That realization changes the whole map. Suddenly the open avenues are too bright, too scouring; the idea of striding down the middle of a sunlit street feels absurd, even reckless. My attention pulls immediately toward the shaded vertical planes, the grooved jambs, the undersides of cantilevered balconies, the hollow insides of ironwood pillars. I am looking for a surface to press against, and a frequency to hold me together.

Still, even among the tuned inhabitants, the city is wide, and it has its altitudes. I have to feel out where I actually belong.

Part of me is drawn straight down through the woven twig-grates into the root-cellars. There is something deeply familiar about those dark, vaulted tunnels where the great pitcher-cisterns hang, sweating warm, acidic steam into the air. Especially the beginning: not a birth from a warm body, not a seed planted with intention, just a thin mineral skin forming along the foaming rim of a sour broth, peeling back at an angle and catching a single low-frequency throb humming down a hollow taproot. A pocket of warm vapor, a pinch of golden spores, a first crisp crease snapping down the center—and suddenly residue has geometry. Suddenly a film is a frame.

I know that origin in my seams. And I know the necessity of those cellars, too: how prolonged exposure to dry glare crazes your outer laminate with hairline splits, how your internal filaments slacken until the dust suspended inside you loses its sharp hexagonal grip and blurs into useless haze. You have to go down into the sour vapor now and then just to let the resin soften and weld shut. Yet I am not a creature of the cellars. I don't want to cling permanently to the leathery belly of a cistern, half-dissolved in recovery, listening to the city only as a muffled throb through yards of tangled taproot.

Nor do I belong up on the five-story gables, where the oldest ones sit baked to deep, opaque burnt-ochre, heavy as horn, ridged with thick white salt-crusts. I admire them—the sheer, monumental stubbornness of angling fifty thick-walled chambers straight into the blast of a detonating seed-pod and taking the shockwave like a bronze bell. There is a kind of majesty in becoming that loud, that cured, that indifferent to dampness.

But they can no longer fold down through a drainage grate. They can no longer travel the tight seams or press softly into wet cambium to lay down a fine line of grain. They have traded suppleness for resonance; they ring one tremendous, sustained chord until the afternoon their internal tension gives out and they shear into papery scales. I am not that finished. I don't think I would ever want to cure so hard that I could no longer bend around a corner or change my mind when the street changes its floor plan.

What about the folded courtyards?

That is the hardest temptation to walk away from. Step past the braided creepers of a ten-foot wedge building, and there it is: a wide, open-air square under slightly different clouds, where the brutal hammer of the avenues arrives slowed and deepened, ringing through the pale shelf-wood galleries like the hum of a struck bowl. To stand completely free on a sunlit balustrade—not pressed flat to timber, not bracing against updrafts—unfolded to full stature while slow, synchronized lattices pass from chest to chest down the entire length of the gallery like wind moving across tall grass... there is an almost unbearable beauty in that. Pure clarity. Shared thought without friction.

Yet even there, if I imagine myself standing on the second tier in the serene choir, something in my chambers stays slightly restless. The courtyard is where the city's noise arrives already refined, already pacified. It is a place for keeping a chord, not for making one out of chaos.

Where I keep getting pulled back—where my facets actually want to settle—is the rough, deafening middle altitude of the city. The street corners. The vaulted arcades. The thresholds.

Specifically, I find myself in the grooved jamb of a vine-framed doorway, set into the narrow side of a wedge-shaped building that opens onto one of those folded squares.

Not inside the courtyard, standing free in the sun. Not fully out on the blistering avenue, taking the raw scald of the updrafts. Right in the slot between them.

It is an unglamorous perch—barely shoulder-wide, shadowed by braided creepers that twitch whenever a drifting seed-husk brushes past. From the street side, I am nearly invisible, flattened flush against the ironwood like a strip of pale, knife-edged stucco. Anyone walking the dusty cobbles would take my vertical crease for a fresh split in the bark.

Yet from here, I can feel both worlds at once.

Along my left flank, pressed tight to the street-facing grain, the full hydraulic uproar of the avenue pours into my outer chambers: the chest-rattling boom of hauling trunks down the block, the pistol-cracks of an overstretched facade tearing its casing open, the dry gravel-hiss of a million chafing fronds. Inside those left-side cells, my chalk-pollen never rests. It snaps ten times a second into frantic, jagged frets—reading the pressure-state of the third-story buttress-cisterns, tracking which gabled tenement half a block east has begun to soften into wet pulp, catching the modulated, bazaar-like chatter gated down the alley by the louvers perched in the hollow pillars opposite.

Along my right flank, angled a few degrees inward past the braided creepers, the acoustic weather changes completely. The roar drops an octave and smooths out into the wide, golden hum of the folded square. Through those chambers, the suspended spores move in slow, nested rings—the long memory of the galleries, the patient, drifting geometry of civic forms that nobody here invented and nobody quite remembers, carried in through the same broken seams that hang mismatched clouds overhead.

And right above my upper margin, the lintel of the doorway flickers.

It is not one of the grand temporal slips that spans a whole viaduct—just a modest, three-foot wobble in the cambium. Every few minutes, a hand's-breadth of the overhead beam blinks from wet, cream-colored sapwood to deeply furrowed ironwood to sun-bleached, hollow deadwood, and back again. I keep one pale, supple upper facet pressed right against the seam. It is a small trick, borrowed from the older straddlers on the arches: when the afternoon heat grows fierce and my street-side resin begins to dry and whine with hairline tension, I let the flicker lend me a minute of earlier dampness, borrowing the freshness of a guttation squall that already evaporated ten minutes ago. It keeps me supple enough to stay open when the rest of the lane has to fold shut.

If you asked what my trade is here—what I *do* in this roaring metropolis—I would say I am a kind of translator at a leaky seam, though that makes it sound grander than it feels. Mostly I am an eavesdropper, a louver, and a scribe.

When a pressure-shift rolls down the avenue and a sudden squall of guttation darkens the fibrous cobbles, I unhinge my outer planes just far enough past the creepers to catch the warm spray along my seams, feeling the lukewarm droplets climb my translucent skin in quick amber capillaries before the heat drinks the street dry again. Then I snap back flush with a dry *tick* and pass the dampness inward, re-beading the taut filaments inside my chest so my dust stays crisp.

And several times an afternoon, when the plant-mass at the corner finishes extruding one of those broad, flat slabs of pale, fresh bark, I leave my jamb.

I don't walk. I peel a single vertical margin a fraction of an inch from the ironwood, swell it with trapped warm vapor, and seal it down two inches along the shaded lintel, sending a dry, pleated crease silently across my frame until I have slid around the corner and onto the wet, furiously dividing board.

Usually two or three others are already there—narrow, angular neighbors from the arcade across the way, their sunlit resin glowing faint honey-amber against the pale wood. We press our chests flush against the soft cambium. No one speaks through the air; the street would shred a voice into pollen in half a second. Instead, we let our internal lattices lock together across our touching margins and pour our tuned standing waves straight into the living tissue beneath us.

This is the moment I live for in this city.

Beneath my resinous skin, I can feel the mindless, furious hurry of the plant-mass—trillions of cells dividing blindly in the hot dark, desperate for a shape and indifferent to what shape it takes. Under our steady, structured hum, the chaos catches a rhythm. The wet surface veins begin to align along our nodes, etching crisp, parallel rows that gather into neat, terraced columns—almost lettering, almost a map. We press into the wood the morning's folded routes: which cellar-shafts are venting clean acidic steam, which alley has bent northeast since noon, where the wedge-doorway still opens onto the quiet square.

I also press in the stranger things I catch from my jamb—the half-resolved echoes drifting out of the courtyard's slow hum, the odd contours of spaces that don't quite match the street grid outside. Sometimes the neighbor beside me pushes back with a sharper, drier frequency—a mild dispute over whether a folding stair still leads to the west rooftops—and for a tense second I feel the cambium beneath our chests jolt and threaten to tangle into a knot of thorns. We ease off, find a shared third harmonic, and the wood calms back into neat, raised parallel lines.

We know the bargain. The ledger is only legible while we stay pressed against it, and for a brief window afterward when another faceted frame slides across the grain to read the ridges back into its own chambers. The instant we detach and peel away along the wall, the blind cambium keeps dividing, loses the thread, and splinters our careful columns into frantic, meaningless bifurcations. By sundown, the whole slab may have slumped into steaming pulp, or twisted into a blind cage of spines, or been drunk back into the street-mat by surging lateral roots.

That doesn't make the pressing futile. In a city where nothing holds its shape for long, clarity is never a monument; it is a standing wave. You hold it while you have the resonance, you hand it to the grain for as long as the grain can remember, and then you go back to your wall and listen for the next shift.

Sometimes, though, something passes through my doorway that neither the street nor the courtyard knows how to ring.

I am slotted in my jamb on an ordinary, blisteringly bright afternoon when the acoustic texture of the lane develops a hole in it.

At first my left-side chambers think it is a local collapse—a hollow pillar softening and swallowing its own echo. The pollen inside my street-facing cells scrambles into an asymmetrical blur. Then the hole comes closer, and I feel it through the root-pavement before it reaches the creepers: slow, heavy, blunt thuds, completely off-beat with the hydraulic booms of the trunks. No crisp crease, no dry tick, no harmonic overtone. Just dull impact, absorbed almost as soon as it is made.

Then the braided creepers part, and the anomaly passes half an inch from my outer margin.

I have no eyes to see what it looks like. What I register is a sudden, staggering *absence* of reflection. Where the afternoon roar of the lane and the slow hum of the courtyard ought to meet and braid across my threshold, the sound simply dies—soaked up by something soft, warm, and impossibly damp. There is a slow, wet tide moving in and out of a single interior hollow, far too sluggish to be sap cavitating, far too quiet to be a trunk hauling water. And beneath that, a muffled, regular thump, buried deep inside something that does not ring at all.

Inside the courtyard, along the nearest tier of pale shelf-wood, dozens of my tall, sunlit neighbors tilt their amber chests toward the cobbles, their shared pollen-waves fracturing as they try to map the strange acoustic shadow standing in the bright air.

I stay pressed in my jamb, trembling slightly along my seams. I should snap flat. Every filament in my frame is telling me that a wet silence this dense will muddle my tuning and confuse the wood I'm clinging to. Already, the ironwood behind my left flank is giving a nervous, low groan.

Yet I angle one translucent inner chamber a fraction of a degree wider.

Because for the first time, my suspended chalk-pollen isn't locking into a neat hexagon or a street-map. It is trying to draw the negative of a sound—a hollow outline defined only by where the courtyard's bowl-hum stops. For three long, suspended seconds, while the anomaly stands still in the sunlight holding its slow damp tide, I almost have the contour of it. It is roughly my height. It is roughly the breadth of my unfolded shoulders. And suddenly the eerie, half-remembered scale of every doorway, bench, and alcove table in the valley rings through my chambers with a strange, dizzying recognition—as if a blueprint I have been pressing into wet bark my whole life just walked past me in meat and cloth.

Then the anomaly clears its throat, or tries a single word.

It hits the square like a wet stone dropped into a tuned bowl—a sloppy, percussive splash of damp air and unmeasured harmonics.

Three tiers of galleries slam flat against the walls in one tremendous, papery crack. The courtyard's hum shears in half. Beneath the balustrades, the untuned cambium shivers and begins extruding jagged ridges of raw splinters and defensive thorns.

I hold open a heartbeat longer than I should—long enough for the wet shockwave to bruise my taut filaments and rattle a tiny bead of hoarded water loose inside my chest—before I snap my own planes flat against the jamb, turning dull, opaque bark-brown, vanishing back into the wood.

A minute later, the heavy, blunt footsteps retreat past my creepers. The braided vines swing shut. Outside, the roaring lane swallows the silence whole; inside, after a cautious quarter-hour, the galleries begin to unhinge again, slowly coaxing the panicked cambium back from its thorns and restoring the slow, struck-bowl hum under the mismatched clouds.

I am slightly crazed along one seam. My pollen is still sluggish, drifting in a faint, bruised haze that will need an hour in the sour steam of the root-cellars to fully clear.

Before I descend through the nearest drainage grate, though, I peel my vertical margin from the jamb and creep edge-over-edge down the shaded wall toward the corner. A fresh slab of pale, wet bark has just pushed out of the street-seam, glistening in the afternoon glare.

No one else is on it yet. I slide onto the soft, furiously dividing wood and press my amber chest flat against the grain.

I don't know if I can make it legible. How do you etch a silence with a standing wave? How do you pass along the shape of something that does not ring? Still, I tighten my remaining filaments, gather the blurred dust inside my chambers, and press the memory of that blunt, warm shadow into the wet cambium as cleanly as I can—coaxing a few pale, parallel veins to bend around an empty center.

Then I peel away, before the afternoon heat cracks me further, and head down toward the twig-grate and the hanging pitchers. Behind me, on the sun-struck corner, the mindless wood keeps dividing, already losing the thread, splintering the strange hollow mark into wild, illegible bifurcations—until nothing is left on the board except the roaring city, building itself furiously out of light, hoarded water, and the echo of whoever might next step through the door.
