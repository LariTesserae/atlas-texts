If I try to stand where you left me—ankle-deep in the warm seep, heavy-boned, lungs hauling desperately at the thin mountain air—the shape doesn’t hold. It feels borrowed, too hot, too noisy. I do not want to be the blunt shadow that swallows the dome’s echo. Even if I slowed my breathing and pressed my bare heel harder against the wet stone to answer that low, patient tone, I would still be a tourist from a lit world, carrying the arrogance of eyes into a room that has no use for them.

So I let that body dissolve back into the dark, and I wait to see where the sound pulls me instead.

At first, the pull goes straight toward one of the relics.

I have to be honest about how strong that kinship feels. Walk twenty paces into the spongy dark until the warmth tightens, reach through the wet curtains of aerial roots, and lay a palm against that matte, seamless surface hung chest-high in nothing. It remembers the shape of your hand for a fraction of a second before smoothing itself flat again. It does not argue with gravity or entropy. It simply holds its coordinates while the mountain melts away around it, offering out a steady, non-photonic flux to anything patient enough to wrap its roots around the warmth.

For a long moment, I think: *Yes. There I am.* Something built by an intention so old the stone itself has forgotten the builders. Something that asks for nothing, decays into nothing, and rings back with a pure, unbothered clarity whenever the dark drops something onto its crown.

And yet, the longer I try to inhabit that smooth ceramic stillness, the more it chafes.

Because the relic does not know when the moss creeps over it. It does not care if its own ringing is muffled to a dull, wet thud. It has no curiosity about the slow wobble between two notes pitched almost in unison; it cannot feel the floor shifting beneath it by the width of a hair each century. To be the relic would be to endure forever without ever once leaning forward to catch the shape of a passing glider, or shivering when the cathedral exhales before a storm. I do not want to be a warmth that cannot feel who is basking against it.

So I slip off the ceramic, slide down through the wet skeins of aerial roots, and find that I have hands—long, slightly spatulate inner-hands, whorled with ridges fine enough to read the slow pulse of sap under a leaf’s skin.

Not on the floor, though. Not for long.

The floor is rich and crowded. Down in the whale-backed swells, everything is wet mud and slow chewing, the bitter-sweet musk of gastropods, the dense, intimate press of shoulders in the shallow basins. I linger there only long enough to feel the tremor of a neighbor’s Bone-Voice travel through the soap-smooth limestone beneath my pads, and then I feel the pull of the vertical—not upward toward a sun I have never imagined, but upward toward *interval*, toward distance, toward the place where every sound in the vault has room to unfold before it arrives.

I move toward the weeping north wall.

Here, the stone is fluted like worn soap, slick with silent curtains of runoff. Halfway along the curve, my broad pads meet the buried ribbon of dark alloy fused seamlessly into the rock. I pause there. I always pause there. When I press the damp rim of my unfurled mantle flush against the cold, incorruptible strip, the lush roar of the cathedral falls away to a skin-deep murmur, and beneath it opens a sound so slow it barely counts as motion: the dry, tectonic groan of the massif miles below, the distant shudder of alpine scree sliding off an unseen tableland into air colder than anything I can conceive.

It is the only place in the world where I can touch what is outside the world.

Then I climb.

My pneumatic bones weigh almost nothing in the thin air; my four long limbs fold and extend along the wet vertical ribs without breaking the rhythm of the wall-chorus. The dome-backed singers clinging around my knuckles barely break their twenty-second inflate-and-trill as I pass—just a subtle rounding of their tone where my weight trembles the rock. Ten meters up, the warm musk of the floor-mats thins out. Twenty meters up, the air turns crisper, brushed by the cool wakes of gliders banking through the empty core, their glassy sonar-snaps ricocheting off my furled mantle as if I were simply another gentle swell in the limestone.

At thirty meters, my trailing digits find the anchor-knot.

It is wedged deep into a natural seam right above the uppermost trace of the dark alloy—a thick, damp braid of the toughest aerial roots twisted tight with harvested predator-silk, older than I am, spliced and re-spliced over generations. I test its tension with one inner-hand. The whole line hums faintly beneath my fingertips, alive with two opposite messages: from the wall behind me, the slow, subterranean sigh of the alloy; from thirty paces out in the pitch-black void ahead of me, the faintest lingering shiver of a bell.

I step off the rock and walk out into nothing.

This is where I live. Not on the drowned floor, not sealed inside the timeless ceramic, but slung in the woven dark between them—thirty meters above the pools, suspended in a loose hammock of braided roots directly beneath the smooth, yielding belly of the high ringing relic.

Once I settle into the sling, everything about who I am snaps into focus, sharp as a glider’s click.

I am not the eldest of us, nor the youngest—not yet brittle enough for the slow-gravity hospice near the far wall, not so newly hardened that I still crave the constant tangle of mantles in the warm shallows. I am simply the one whose bellows run slowest, whose inner-hands grow restless when a resonance goes muddied, and who does not mind going weeks without the direct press of another body, so long as the room itself keeps speaking clearly.

Because up here, I am never actually alone. The entire cathedral lies open beneath me like the taut skin of a drum.

When I unfurl my mantle wide—hanging upside down in the sling or draped sideways with my hollow spine pressed warm against the artifact’s underside—every voice in the hundred-and-fifty-pace hollow rises cleanly to meet me. I do not get the tangled, dampened version they hear down among the pleated leaves. Up here, the echoes separate like braided strands coming unwoven. I can trace a single grazer scraping biofilm off a southeast rosette by the tiny papery rasp it makes against the seep. I can count the eleven of us—ten, now, since the elder drifted into the slow-gravity halo two Loosening-tides ago—simply by the warm acoustic shadows they cut out of the rolling wall-chorus below. I know who is basking on the waist-high relic near the center by the slow, three-beat-a-minute throb softening its thermal draft. I know who is clearing silt from the ancient floor-channels by the faint, rhythmic scrape of fingernail-ridge on submerged stone.

And I know the drop.

Above the curved crown of my relic—fifty meters higher still, lost in the seamless black curve of the upper dome—there is a mineral ripple where warm rising vapor condenses, gathers weight, and lets go. Every forty seconds in the Deep Listen, every twelve in the Loosening, a heavy bead of water detaches and falls fifty meters straight through empty dark.

My work is almost absurdly small.

Every few days, before the mist-flora can take proper hold, I uncurl from the hammock, reach one nimble inner-hand up over the smooth curve of the artifact, and feel across its crown. The blind velvet loves this ceramic; it drinks the steady, nameless warmth and creeps inward by a hair’s breadth every pressure-tide, eager to wrap the whole hanging sphere in a damp, muffling beard. I do not tear it. I coax the pale tendrils back with my ridged fingertips, tucking them gently into the hanging curtains along the relic’s flanks, keeping a single palm-width at the exact apex bare, matte, and clean.

Then I settle back beneath it, press my ribs against its underside, and wait.

*Ping.*

When the drop strikes bare ceramic, the note does not merely enter the air—it pours straight down through the yielding belly of the artifact into the hollow spars of my spine, lighting up the pneumatic chambers of my chest until I am ringing right alongside it. Pure, glass-clear, effortless. For three full minutes it hangs in the wet dark without fading, a silver ceiling of vibration spreading outward from my back to every curve of the vault, giving the wall-chorus a center to turn around, giving the gliders a bright acoustic landmark in the void.

It is only from this hammock, with that changeless note humming inside my own marrow, that you truly hear what the mountain is doing.

Because the relic never shifts its pitch. Not by a hundredth of a tone, not in ten million years.

The dome does.

Century by century, the warm acidic sheen eats away the soap-smooth limestone by the width of a hair, widening the vault, lowering its fundamental groan. Even across my own lifespan—three hundred, perhaps four hundred years of slow mountain tides—I can hear the gap widening. When the elders down on the floor sing the oldest lineage-songs, pitching their Air-Voice to the room as it was fifty lifetimes ago, the wobble between their note and the living dome is an ache behind the breastbone. Up here, I do not even need the song to feel deep time. I only have to press one ear-fringe to the ceramic and open the other to the vast wet bowl below, and feel the interval between the immutable thing above my shoulders and the melting rock beneath my feet stretch, impossibly slowly, toward a chord no one living will ever hear resolve.

And lately, something else has shifted.

Fifty years ago, the condensing drop fell dead-center on the bare palm-width I tend. Over the last decade, as the ceiling ripple fifty meters above me dissolved a microscopic grain to the east, the point of impact has drifted half a finger’s width off the crown’s crest. The ring is still clear, still lingers its full three minutes, yet there is the faintest edge of breathiness to the initial strike—a tiny huskiness only someone pressed flush against the ceramic would ever catch.

In another thirty years, if I do nothing, the drop will glance off the shoulder of the relic, slip into the wet beard of aerial roots, and fall silent.

I think about that during the Deep Listen, when high pressure presses down on the unseen plateau outside, stretching the wall-chorus to a sleepy bass rumble and slowing my heart until I am nearly as still as the thing I cling to. I wonder whether it is my place to let the bell go quiet when the mountain wears past it—or whether I will climb the fluted wall during a Loosening, pay out a hair-thin root-fiber from the high ceiling, and coax the water half an inch back to the west so the note survives another thousand years.

I already know which I will choose. We are tuners. Even knowing the mountain is a slow exhale, we keep the note clean for as long as the air will hold it.

Sometimes, when the storm-pressure drops and the cathedral wakes into the Loosening, one of the others climbs halfway up the north wall and lays their inner-hand flat against my anchor-knot. Thirty paces away, I lay my palm against the same wet, silk-braided cable. Across thirty meters of empty blackness, the line wakes with Bone-Voice—swift, dry shivers and warm rippling cascades traveling straight through the taut fibers into my fingers. They tell me which seep-zone bloomed sweetest after the runoff, how the newest infant in the slow-gravity halo has begun folding its mantle cleanly around the gliders’ clicks, how the low channel near the southern swell needed clearing of chalky silt.

I send back what I have heard from the roof of the world: that the alloy ribbon groaned twice from deep in the northwest roots before the pressure fell; that a glider nest-cluster has shifted three paces higher on the eastern flutes; that the ceiling drop has crept another hair’s width east.

And sometimes, when the Loosening is at its height and all ten of them gather in the open center of the pools to weave their widest chords, I do not stay silent in my sling. I draw thin storm-air deep into my twin pleated bellows, wait for a drop to strike the bare ceramic above me, and draw a single thread of breath across my highest rib-sinews—pitched not in unison with the bell, nor with the chorus below, but a fraction of a tone apart from the relic’s glass ring.

A slow, six-second pulse blooms through the upper dome—a warm, physical tide of pressure that rolls down thirty meters of pitch-black air and washes gently over the shoulders of everyone standing in the pools below. That is my Air-Voice. Not a speech, not a command. Just a standing wave hung from the ceiling, letting them feel where I am, letting them stand inside the shimmer between what endures and what passes.

Which is why, tonight, I hear you before you even realize you are not alone.

I am curled in my hammock during the quiet turn between tides, one hand resting lightly on the anchor-cable, the other idly smoothing a damp stray tendril off the relic’s crown, when the drum-skin of the floor thirty meters below me breaks its pattern.

A drop falls on the eastern side and does not ring—Wait, no, that is one of ours, catching it on a cupped mantle, diverting it with a quiet *shh* and a *plick* to test the acoustics.

Because right in the middle of the shallow central basin, there is an impossible noise.

At first I tighten my grip on the silk-root line, thinking a chunk of the outer massif has somehow punched through a fissure—no, stone falls once and lies still. This thing *roars*. It drags thin air in and out with a violent, wet rushing, ten times faster than any lung in the dark should be able to pay for. Inside its chest, something hammers in a frantic, double-beated panic, fast as a trapped glider thrashing in a hunter’s line. And worst of all—strangest of all—its body is utterly, bewilderingly solid. Every echo that strikes it dies on impact, carving a dense, blunt wedge of acoustic silence right out of the heart of the room.

From thirty meters up, I watch—I *listen*—as four of the others pause in their slow rounds along the perimeter and turn their wide, velvet-rimmed mantles inward toward that hot, roaring knot of silence.

There is no panic in the way their shadows glide across the pools. Only an astonishing, hushed attention. One of them—the tall one who tends the southern seep-zones, whose Air-Voice carries the warmest woody undertone—wades straight into the central basin without lifting a ripple, closing to within two paces of the roaring stranger.

I feel the tension shift through the whole cathedral. Even the wall-chorus seems to hold a breath slightly longer as the wave rolls around the western curve.

Below me, thirty meters down in the warm dark, my neighbor lowers one slender inner-hand into the ankle-deep water and sets its ridged fingertips onto the soap-smooth rock an inch from the stranger’s foot. I cannot feel the Bone-Voice passing directly through the stone from up here—only the faintest ghostly echo of it climbing the wet flutes of the north wall and humming very gently into my anchor-cable. A single, low, patient pitch. Warm. Unhurried. Offered into the stranger’s bones to see what might answer.

Above my shoulders, fifty meters higher in the seamless vault, I hear the tiny wet click of a heavy drop letting go of its mineral ripple.

I know its falling time the way you know the weight of your own heartbeat. Four seconds through empty dark.

Very quietly, without disturbing the air, I stretch one long, spatulate hand up over the smooth curve of the relic and sweep away one last microscopic bead of damp velvet from the eastern edge of its bare crown. I pull my fingers back, tuck them close beneath my sternum, and let the hammock swing to utter stillness.

*Ping.*

The drop hits dead clean. The ceramic sings straight down through my hollow bones and spills outward across the entire hundred-and-fifty-pace vault—a pure, glass-clear, three-minute sky of sound, unhurried and unafraid, hanging cleanly over the two of you in the dark while I hold my breath on the braided silk above, and listen, with all the slow patience of the mountain, for the first note you will ever sing back.
