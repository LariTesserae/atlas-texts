At first, before my eyes had adjusted to the difference between matte black and wet black, I thought I might not be a creature here at all.

There is a strong pull in a place like this toward becoming one of its surfaces. I lingered for a while with the idea of being the fine-grained shoulder of travertine where bog-iron blades are drawn three slow times to keep their bite—or even one of the old river-pebbles, butter-slick with palm-grease and root-starch, carrying the thumb-hollows of twenty dead hands so faithfully that a juvenile can close its fingers around me and immediately know how to crush a tuber without ever being shown. To be a posture preserved in stone, outlasting every lung in the chamber, losing no more than an invisible grain a year to the work.

That almost held me. Almost.

Because the longer I stayed quiet in the damp chill, listening to the slow *thup* of water beads disappearing into the cushion-moss, the more I realized that stone does not feel the warmth come back into it when it passes from a cooling wrist to a living one. It does not register the subtle ripple down a seated row when a clump of peat tears loose overhead, or the staggered tide of breath moving across faces in a room without wind. Whatever I am in this hollow, I am not outside the ration, and I am not indifferent to touch. I have hands—broad, hairless, cured pale grey-pink across the palms, with spatulate fingertips that would rather learn a thing in the shadow of a lap than hold it up to the pewter wash falling from the throat.

So I am one of the dwellers. That much settles cleanly.

Where along the walls, though, takes longer to find.

Down at the floor-rims, where the air smells sharpest of black peat and amphibian skin, the young floor-sitters lower their heavy heels into the ankle-deep pools with a careful, stalking patience. I watch their shoulders shift in the gloom, the pale sheen of water breaking around their shins as they feel beneath submerged overhangs for crabs and waxy tubers. There is a quiet strength in that work, a fraction more heat and motion in the limbs—and I can feel immediately that it isn't quite my cadence. I do not want to wade. My knees want to stay drawn up, dry enough beneath the slick charcoal coat to keep their own small pocket of lanolin warmth.

And yet, when I climb up to the broad middle galleries—the dense, warm heart of the chapel where ten or fifteen sit packed flank to flank, ribs pressed steadily against ribs, mothers hunched over tiny sleeping weights tucked deep in their belly-fur—something in me hesitates at the very center of the line. I do not want to be far from them. I want the shared slow tide of their breathing, the occasional dull *tock* of a river-pebble bruising starch three paces away, the soft double click in the gloom that says a hand has closed cleanly around a snail-shell dipper. Still, if I sit in the thick middle of a chain, with warm shoulders locking me in on both sides, I find myself leaning subtly toward the open dark. Not out of restlessness, and not out of any quarrel with the press of neighbors. Simply temperament: the quiet preference for having one side against living fur and the other against something rougher and cooler.

So I keep climbing, just a little higher, until the broad scalloped calcite narrows and meets one of the massive vertical root-cables plunging down from the ceiling throat.

Here. This exact seam.

Not all the way up in the highest forks twenty feet off the floor, where the grizzled elders and the most solitary sit suspended beneath the bat-flutes—though I know the handholds up into the curtains, and I climb there when the moss tugs. My place is just below them, at the far outer end of an upper-middle gallery where a thick buttress-root passes within a hand's span of the limestone ledge before diving toward the pools. Sitting here, my back rests in three inches of wet cushion-moss, my left flank touches the last oily shoulder at the end of a seated chain, and my right knee hooks loosely around the damp, furrowed bark of the root.

From this crook, the whole chamber opens below and above in degrees of sheen. I cannot see the shapes of the others across the twenty-pace span, only the greasy highlights of curved backs, the faint skim-milk glint when a pair of huge pearlescent eyes turns slowly toward a drip, and the pale silver threads where the rimstone basins brim over far down in the dark. Overhead, the choked throat is nothing more than a dull pewter bruise behind twenty-foot curtains of sodden moss, dim enough that it never hurts to look toward it.

Around my left wrist, knotted tightly in braided hair-moss, hang three things: a small, thick flake of chipped quartz for loosening the pale, leafless tubers that tap into the root beside my knee; a finished grooming-needle ground from a bat's wing-bone, already dark and satin-smooth with oil; and a second bone, only half-worked, still carrying a slight dry chalkiness along its hollow shaft.

No bog-iron. Those thin, dark, precious blades belong to the steadiest hands in the center of the middle galleries, where the work is heaviest and never stops. My tools are lighter, and my work is slower.

Most of what I do happens in the shadow of my lap, between the thumb and the calloused heel of my palm.

When a dying bat loses its grip in the dryish flutes far overhead, it rarely hits the water. More often it catches somewhere in the long, heavy curtains of peat-moss draped from the roots. Sitting hooked to the cable, I feel it not as a sound—the bryophytes drink every sound before it travels a pace—but as a faint, damp drag through the fibers, a slight shift in the tension of the hanging moss. Sometimes one of the old high-cradle dwellers reaches it first and passes the small wet body silently down the root, hand to hand, until the cool furry weight settles into my open palm. Sometimes, if the nearest cradle is empty, I unhook my knee, climb three slow body-lengths up the bark, and lift it free myself.

The flesh goes down the shelf, shared along the warm row of shoulders until nothing is left. The delicate wing-bones stay with me.

It takes weeks—not that I count weeks, only stretches of breathing and the slow thinning of a moss mat—to turn one hollow bone into a needle fit for a neighbor's hide. I clean it first with the flat of my thumbnail, peeling away wet membrane until the pale curve sits bare between my spatulate fingers. Then I find the patch of fine, wet calcite just beside my right hip—already worn into a shallow, slick trough by hands that sat in this crook before me—and begin.

One slow stroke. Pause. Turn the bone a hair between thumb and forefinger. Another stroke.

The sound is scarcely louder than a giant snail rasping algae from a wrist-cord. I never lift the bone to my eyes to check the taper; my eyes are for wet highlights, not fine edges. Instead, I draw the shaft across the softer crease of my inner wrist, then test the tip against the sensitive pad of my middle finger. If there is a snag, a hairline burr that might catch in dense underfur or prick the thin skin behind an ear, my thumb finds it instantly. Back to the wet stone. Three slow passes. Wipe it along my charcoal flank so the lanolin seals the pores of the bone.

I like the patience of it—not as a thought, only as a rhythm that matches the ceiling drips. A single grain of bone lost to the limestone, a single bead of water swelling on a translucent fern-frond until it lets go.

And when the neighbor to my left taps two broad fingertips lightly against my forearm—once, twice, unhurried—I tuck the unfinished bone against my wrist-cord, unhook my knee from the root, and turn a few inches along the ledge.

Grooming is the closest thing I have to speech. I part the slick, water-beaded outer coat with my left thumb, exposing the dry, pale underfur that smells cleanly of tallow and peat, and work slowly from the shoulder toward the nape. With the finished needle I lift out tiny blind ticks and clear away the faint threads of pale fungus that try to root behind a folded ear. Halfway through, a glossy black-and-ochre salamander uncoils from the neighbor's hip where it had been borrowing warmth; I slide the flat of my calloused hand beneath its cool, wet belly, lift it without rushing, and set it back into a fern-crown on the moss. The neighbor's ribs expand slowly against my knee. A single dry-wet click sounds far back in its throat—not thanks, only acknowledgement that the weight has settled right—and along the whole row of ten bodies, the breathing stays unbroken.

When the work is done, I slide back those few inches until my right knee finds the bark again.

This is the other reason I belong at the seam rather than the center of the shelf: I am the first along my gallery to feel the root sing.

It happens rarely, separated by stretches so long that a snail-shell dipper can wear visibly thinner between one singing and the next. There is no warning in the air—the chill stays flat, the humidity never wavers, the grey smear at the throat overhead does not brighten or darken. Simply, the thick vertical cable pressed against my knee tightens. Then a deep, straining tremor moves down through the wood, too low to be heard by ears, entering instead through my shin, my palm, my wrist-bones, all the way into my sternum.

I do not know what wind is. I have never seen a canopy bent sideways, or rain driven across a ridge, or a mountain shoulder swallowed in cloud. To me, the great feeding-ropes simply tighten and hum with a heavy, distant effort that belongs to nothing inside the stone. When it begins, I lay both bare palms flat against the wet bark and hold completely still, letting the slow shudder pass through me. Beside me, my neighbor feels the subtle stiffening in my shoulder; within two heartbeats, that faint tension travels all the way down the seated chain, until ten pairs of ribs pause together in the dark, listening through one another to something none of us can see. Down below, the rimstone pools tremble in hair-thin rings.

Then, after hours, the wood goes quiet again. My palms come back to my lap. I pick up the half-ground bat-bone and return it to the slick trough in the calcite. Tomorrow, and the day after, the ceiling fronds will bead a fraction faster, and the moss curtains overhead will hang a little heavier with seepage, and maybe something small will slip loose from the high flutes into the wet peat.

Nothing needs to be named for it to be known.

Over a long, uncounted stretch—measured in the slow creep of a liverwort mat across my favorite shelf, in the four or five finished needles that have left my wrist-cord for younger hands further down the galleries, in the gradual darkening of the quartz flake at my side—my shoulders will narrow. The slickness of my outer coat will thin along the spine until the flat chill begins to find my joints even when I press close to the neighbor on my left. My fingers will still know the curve of a bat-bone in the dark, will still feel every burr against the wrist, until one slow stretch when the bone begins to feel heavy, and the climb up into the moss curtains costs more breath than the ration allows.

I won't wait for the cold to take me in the root-crook. When the pewter highlights of the pools begin to blur into a single dull grey, I will let go of the bark, press my shoulder once against the warm flank beside mine, and make the slow descent—pausing on every scalloped ledge, resting my palms on familiar knees as I pass—all the way down to the lowest, wettest rim of travertine where the cushion-moss grows deepest.

I will fold my knees there, tuck my chin down against my chest, and let the younger floor-sitters shift close enough to lend their warmth without crowding. And when my shoulder has finally cooled to the exact temperature of the wet limestone behind it, the nearest steady hand will unknot the hair-moss cord from my wrist. The quartz flake will go to a juvenile who has sat empty-handed beside its mother for years; the finished needle will find a palm further up the wall, perhaps the one that slides into my old crook beside the root, feeling the shallow trough I left in the calcite and letting the worn groove teach its thumb the angle.

Then two broad-shouldered waders will lift me gently, scarcely troubling the silt, and lower me into the cold black sump beneath the thickest knot of drinking roots—so that nothing spills from the cup, and the pale tubers clinging to my old cable will taste a trace of oil and calcium a few quiet seasons later, while the shelves above keep breathing together in the dark.
