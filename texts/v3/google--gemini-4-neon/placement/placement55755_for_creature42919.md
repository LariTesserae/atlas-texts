I do not think I would be found near the great stacked shale ledges where thirty or forty gather to set a mirror-pool trembling, nor high in the safest canopy forks where the cradles hang above the floor-vapors. If you were walking across the crown of the world and happened to notice me—after a long quarter-hour of looking straight through me—it would be somewhere along the older, neglected loops, three tiers down in the bruised twilight, where an ancient arch has finally begun to bow toward the dark water and the paths have started to tell lies.

That is where I find myself when I let the noise of quicker worlds fall away: kneeling on a firm root-ridge beside a widening basin, dark peat-tea welling briefly around my shins and sinking back into the pale velvet, lifting a drowned vine-line out of a mirror-pool.

The amphibians hanging just beneath the smoked-glass surface do not flee; they only drift two inches deeper, their pale lace gills folding gently as the cord breaks the meniscus. The vine is slick and heavy with algae, slackened by a century of slow settling. Somewhere upstream of this arch—though nothing here streams, only migrates—the living bridge that once held this line taut has slumped a hand’s breadth too far, bowing its moss-thick spine down until it nearly kisses the black water.

I would be one of the people of the crown, somewhere past the middle of a long, uncounted life—old enough to remember when three of the great pillars in this district were still supple shoots braided by hands now sunk into the hearth, old enough that the first harmless flecks of silver-green mottle have begun to appear along the backs of my pale forearms, yet not so old that my broad-jointed fingers have lost their patience with a stubborn knot.

Nothing in this world has a title, and no one is appointed to a task. Still, every circuit bends toward what a person cannot stop noticing. Some of us have a gift for coaxing aerial roots into dry domes without bruising their rind; some know by the tilt of a felt-walker’s pleated muzzle where the cleanest mineral-mud lies. My own attention has always snagged on the intervals—on the quiet places where the plateau’s slow body has outgrown the memory left on its cords.

So I sit back on my broad heels, pinch the furry green velvet from the wet vine between thumb and forefinger, and read what a stranger tied here before my ribcage ever drew a breath.

Two bleached seed-husks, darkened now by the black pool, beside a tight double-knot: an aerial cistern forty paces toward the warm vents, deep and sweet. I already know, without lifting my eyes, that the cistern is gone—its parent fork lies half-swallowed in the moss behind me, blooming with pale ear-fungi. Farther down the slick cord sits a naturally perforated river-pebble, impossibly hard and smooth against the softness of everything else in this boneless country, paired with a loose warning twist for a thin crust that has long since thickened into solid root-mat. And tucked neatly between two swollen bends rests a tiny ring of felt-walker bone.

I hold the bone-ring between my pads for a full ten-second breath. Whoever shaped it against a slab of wet shale held it at a slight slant—one inner edge is beveled wider than the other, the unmistakable signature of a particular hand that worked while thinking of something else. I do not know whose breath returned to the buried hearth when that ring was slipped onto the cord. Perhaps no one still walking the crown remembers the pitch of their chest-hum.

And yet I cannot leave the line drowned here, leading the next traveler straight into sinking water, nor can I simply drop the old knots into the peat to be forgotten. That is the quiet riddle I spend decades answering: how to leave a line honest without erasing the hands that held it before mine.

I stay beside the widening basin for four or five dim cycles, watching the overhead cloud-sea brighten vaguely to milky pewter and fade to ash-violet, brighten and fade, measuring nothing urgent. I walk the soft margin of the pool first, lifting one broad-padded foot, waiting while the dark water clears from its depression, listening through heel and arch for the hollow, drum-like tremble that betrays thin peat over a deep vein. Where the fused roots hold steady around the warmer side of the basin, I choose two healthy pillar-trunks. Climbing is only a change in angle; I tilt forward, lay my damp palms flat against the silver-ciliated hide, and flow upward through a tier of dripping crimson membranes—brushing past a cluster of sheet-slugs whose mantles answer with a faint, cool-green ripple no brighter than wet glass—until I find a dark, rot-resistant runner ready to part cleanly at its joint. No blade ever touches it; everything here is coaxed loose where it is already willing to let go.

When I stretch the new line at chest height between the firmer trunks, I carry the old pebble across from the drowned cord, rubbing the black algae cleanly off it against my smoky-plum mantle. I carry the slanted bone-ring across too, sliding it gently between two fresh knots, right beside the new warning twist that tells whoever passes ten or thirty cycles from now that the pool has grown and the old arch has bowed down to drink. The dead traveler’s breath stays on the living line. The ground changes, and the story bends with it instead of breaking.

Because I wander these quieter side-loops, I go long stretches without feeling another person’s footsteps vibrate through a root-ridge. The silence never feels lonely; it is too full for that. There is the perpetual, irregular *plip* of mercury-bright condensation letting go of the membranes overhead; the long, subterranean sigh of warm vapor escaping a peat-chimney; the faint sweet-chalk scent when a cluster of veil-drifters peels loose all at once from a ribbed trunk and floats silently past my brow like eyelids closing elsewhere. Sometimes I spend half a dim cycle standing motionless beside a herd of felt-walkers, my own pulse slowing to match their ten-second footsteps, offering a pinch of pale burrower-lime from my curled palm to an elder whose coat is heavy with thread-ferns.

And sometimes, beside a warm vent along a loop I know I will not walk again for half a lifetime, I mash fallen gourd-flesh, bitter moss-berries, and sweet stem-pith inside a broad folded leaf-skin, bind it with root-fiber, and tuck it two feet down into the fever-warm peat. I bend a single pale root-tendril around its neighbor and tie one bruised knot above the spot. I will not be the one who draws up that steaming, sour-sweet cake. That is half the warmth of burying it—knowing that some distant, wet traveler will pause beside the bruised tendril, push their long fingers down into the dark hearth, and be fed by hands they will never meet.

When I do encounter another of my kind—first as a faint, muffled thump traveling thirty yards through the peat into my soles, then as a subtle shift in the pitch of dripping water ahead in the mist—the meeting feels vast. We stop several paces apart, letting the slow drift of air carry scent and stillness between us. Neither of us hurries. Then we step close, lean our curved spines together like two arches beginning to fuse, and press forehead to shoulder, palm flat to collarbone.

I am rarely the first to speak. I let their low, vowel-less hum enter my ribs and hold it there for a full breath, feeling the slight clicks at their lips and the shifting pressures of their fingertips along my forearm: who was born in the high cradles this season, which central mirror-pool has narrowed, whose mantle was laid down in the pale moss. Only after their resonance has settled cleanly into my bones do I let my own chest answer—a slow, low floor-note carrying back the quiet news of the forgotten vaults, the arches that have slumped into wells of brighter pewter light, the old crossings that have healed thick and firm again.

Once, long ago, when my deep ribcage was still coming into its full span, I made the long walk outward to the rim of the plateau. I remember how the pillar-trunks stunted and thickened as the warm peat thinned to a ragged hem, until dead, pale limestone bared its cold teeth above a sheer drop into churning white cloud. The wind there felt indecent—pushing rudely against my mantle, drying the sheen of wax-shoot resin from my cheeks, smelling sharply of distance and cold. I did not linger. I pried half a dozen smooth, perforated pebbles from ancient stone channels carved by water that once hurried, tucked them into the warm pocket against my chest, and turned back with deep relief into the shadowless shelter of the vaults.

Yet I think of those stones often when I slide one along a knotted cord in the dim interior. Every pebble is a small, cold piece of velocity brought back from a world with edges, put to quiet work keeping a timeless place legible. Maybe once more, before my fingers stiffen for good, I will make that slow pilgrimage out to the cold hem, simply because the old lines always need honest markers.

More often, and for no practical reason at all—perhaps after a long stretch of mending dead routes, or after leaving one of the rare gatherings where forty chests hummed a black pool into concentric rings—I turn upward. I climb the broad, gently sloped backs of the oldest fused trunks, rib to mossy rib, passing through tier after tier of weeping crimson parchment until my shoulders breach the upper roof of the canopy.

Up there, there are no knots to read, no drowned paths to reroute, no blurred marks of past hands to weigh against the present. Only a rolling sea of velvet-dark domes and translucent sheets dissolving into warm white cloud in every direction. I unfasten my damp stem-skin mantle, lay it beside me, and stretch flat upon my back with my long limbs spread wide over the breathing skin of the continent.

I do not look for a sun I have no word for. I simply let the afternoon vapor rise warm from the buried hearth beneath my spine, watch the cloud-ceiling thicken overhead to heavy pewter, and wait through the ash-violet dusk for the whole vast sky to settle gently back down onto my brow—feeling, in every quiet bone, how small a thing it is to keep one slender vine taut across the dark, and how enough it is to belong to a world that has finished with hurrying, and asks only that we continue with care.
