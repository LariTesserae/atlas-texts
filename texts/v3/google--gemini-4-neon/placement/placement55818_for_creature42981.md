At first, I think I would be the one crouched on the flat above, knees pressed into dry shale, staying quiet long enough to learn the seven-minute tide. It is the easiest shape to imagine—the patient witness, the one who notices the porcelain cup beneath the drip and understands what it means before standing up and walking away with the secret intact.

But the flat does not hold me. Standing up there, I am too large, too loud, too much like weather. Even with the best intentions, I am a shadow that cuts the afternoon shaft in half; a half-inch shift of my boot heel sends a splinter rattling down the wall, and the whole bottom of the seam erases itself back into rock. I don’t want to be the thing that makes the dweller fold its greased hands into its flanks and pretend it isn’t alive.

So I slip past the rim and test what is already inside.

For a long moment, I think I might be one of the glass-legged spinners in the sunlit gap—suspended where three or four taut, dry filaments meet, reading the whole narrow cathedral purely as tension and electrical resistance, darting out when the clouds pass to cut away dew-heavy lines before the geometry sags and snaps. There is something deeply familiar in living at a junction of taut threads, waiting for the faintest shift in load to tell you what has crossed the space.

Yet the spinner only knows the air between the walls. It never feels the weight of the stone behind the concrete, or the long, dark throat of the buried works feeding this tiny engine from a mile away. And down on the floor, the tiled shell-grazers move too much as a single appetite; the paper-thin fissure-hunters live too strictly inside the strike. As for the great, narrow, mudstone-plated dweller itself—I feel an immediate kinship with its thrift, its quiet housekeeping at the brass gear, the way it wipes machine oil off a fingertip before touching the bladder-moss. But a pocket six feet deep and three feet wide has room for only one keeper of the floor. It already occupies itself completely.

It takes a few cycles—watching the brine creep, watching the bronze worms unspool, watching the current swell and sour—before my eye settles on the one hollow in the cleft that hasn’t yet been looked inside.

The east wall. The ragged, sawn-off mouth of the cast-iron conduit, jammed with braided copper cables whose vulcanized rubber jacketing has dried, split, and peeled back into tight black curls.

That is where I find myself. Not out on the wet shale, and not hanging in the sunlit air—just inside the dark iron throat, where the buried infrastructure has been sheared open to the sky.

---

Once I cross that rusted rim, the rest of me arrives slowly, shaped by the clearances of the space.

It is dry in here—not bone-dry like the wind-scoured flat above, because damp air from the cleft drifts six or eight inches down the barrel whenever the sun drops, but dry compared to the salt-slick walls outside. The world inside the conduit is a tight, cylindrical labyrinth of long crescents and narrow channels: the curved, rust-furred iron wall on one side, and the thick, twisted bundle of copper cables pressing against it on the other, bound in cracked black rubber and frayed strips of tarred cotton wrapping that smell faintly of old resin and warm metal.

To live here, I cannot be broad, or soft, or wet.

I am slender as a pencil and no longer than a human hand when stretched straight, though I am rarely straight; mostly I lie in a loose, shallow coil tucked into the dry groove between two thick cables and the upper arch of the pipe. My back is shingled in narrow, overlapping plates—not heavy mudstone keratin like the dweller below, but thin, dry, matte-black chitin dusted with rust-red oxide and fine gray rubber grit. Along the ridges that brush the exposed copper braid as I move, the dry friction has left a faint, chalky bloom of verdigris, dull as dried lichen.

I have no grease on my joints, and no use for it. Where the dweller needs oil to keep the brine out, I need dryness to keep from becoming part of the circuit. My limbs—four pairs, slender, pale at the hinges, tipped with fine double hooks—are built to grip braided wire and frayed cotton without slipping, and to fold flat against my ribs when a cable expands in the heat and narrows my corridor by half a millimeter.

My real eyes are poor things: two tiny, smoky, bead-like domes set far back behind the ridge of my head, inherited from generations spent fifty yards back in the sealed dark of the conduit. They were never meant for an afternoon sun. When the basin crust first slumped and tore the pipe open, the sudden white knife of daylight pouring down the barrel felt like heat without warmth—a dry, silent burn that drove me twenty feet back into the iron dark, where I stayed coiled among the dead cables for days.

What I actually see with are the two long, dry, pale-tipped filaments extending forward from my head—fine as drawn copper, jointed at the base so they can sweep independently. In the dark behind me, I keep one filament resting lightly on a bare strand of one cable, and the other on its neighbor. Because my shell is dry and high-resistant, the current does not bite me; instead, I feel the faint difference in potential between the two wires as a clean, steady hum in the roots of my head—a pitch, a slope, a weight.

Before the break, that hum was the entire sky.

Back in the miles of buried conduit, nothing changes except the load. We live thin lives in the dark runs: grazing the pale, dry fungal threads that feed on decaying cotton-tar insulation, stripping loose the brittle, oxidized skin of the aging rubber to reach the sweet-smelling rot underneath, drinking the microscopic pearls of dew that condense along cold iron collars where the pipe passes through damper clay. Every seven or eight minutes, the invisible works far down the line shift their weight. The copper warms; the rubber gives a tiny, dry *tick*; the static lifts the fine dust off my back. Then it settles, reverses with a sharp, sour pinch, and holds quiet again.

I could have stayed back there. Twenty feet into the east wall, nothing has changed at all. The collapse might as well have never happened.

Instead, I crept back toward the brightness—drawn first by a draft of air that smelled of things the conduit had no name for: wet slate, green stem, salt, machine oil, and something vast and dry far overhead. I stopped six inches short of the ragged iron lip, anchored in the shadow, and began to learn the room that had opened at the end of my tunnel.

---

From inside the mouth of the pipe, the cleft is not a pit seen from above; it is a vertical, brightly lit cross-section of a world, framed in jagged rust.

Directly beneath my threshold, invisible unless I lean my forebody out over the lower rim, hang the braided ropes of bronze-skinned worms, imitating the very copper cables I live among. Just beyond the pipe’s edge, taut strands of conductive spider-silk are anchored to a jagged flake of iron only an inch from my face; when the light strikes them at the right angle, they look like bright, dry wires strung through nothing. Across the narrow shaft—barely two feet away, though to me it feels like a canyon—the fractured concrete scar holds its slow brass worm-gear, oiled and black, turning at a speed so glacial I only know it moves by the faint, dry sigh that travels through the air once every minute or two.

What binds me to this strange bright room is that the seven-minute tide reaches me *first*.

A half-breath before the damp shale outside begins to warm, I feel the shift coming down the buried line behind me. My left filament stiffens; a quiet, rising pressure swells between the two copper strands beneath my belly. The braided bundle ticks softly inside its cracked sleeve, expanding by a hairbreadth against my back. Static prickles along the dry edges of my plates.

I uncoil just enough to slide forward to the lip of shadow, hooking my hind legs safely around a dry copper strand while my pale-tipped feelers lean toward the mouth.

Outside, the cleft answers the wave that just passed through my ribs.

Warmth bleeds out of the cut ends of my cables into the wet stone below. The crystalline verdigris needles sprouting from the iron rim catch the sun with a sharper metallic glare. Below me, the braided bronze worms loosen their coils and unspool across the damp outer underside of my pipe. Up the fresh shale walls, the entire hexagonal mosaic of tile-shells unlocks its beveled edges and climbs in terraced, silent sheets toward my doorway, drawn by the sudden mineral bloom.

And then the dweller rises.

From inside the dark barrel, I watch it unfold from the floor like a jointed length of bedded slate coming unstuck from gravity. It never hurries. Its blackened, greased hands thread smoothly between the dew-free spider-lines right outside my mouth without grazing a single strand. When it settles lengthwise along the wet mudstone just below the conduit, wedging its ribbon-flat tail into a vertical crack to hold its weight, its shovel-blade head is scarcely three inches beneath my chin.

I stay completely still. From here, I can see the pale, translucent rim of its smoky lids drifting shut; I can see the tiny green halos of oxidation ringing the electro-receptive pores along its jaw, and the dim blue-green veins branching beneath the lime-pale hollow of its throat as it presses close to the damp stone. The current that ran dry and sharp along my filaments a moment ago is passing out of the copper ends now, softened by salt and water, soaking into its bone.

It knows I am here. Nothing with jaw-pores that sharp could miss the tiny dry knot of resistance tucked six inches inside the iron pipe, or the faint static whisper when my feelers shift. Once, a week after the collapse, its long, matte-black fingers reached right into the rim of my mouth to hook a dry curl of split rubber for its bed behind the flange. Its blunt, oiled knuckle paused half an inch from my pale-tipped filaments. I did not bolt back into the dark, and it did not swipe or flinch. It simply pinched the loose rubber shaving cleanly away—one I had already chewed half-free from the cotton wrapping the night before, clearing my run—and withdrew its hand with liquid slowness, leaving the iron rim untouched.

Now, that is our entire arrangement. I keep working the inside of the cracked sheathing in the dry dark, stripping the brittle rubber away to harvest the pale fungal threads underneath, and now and then a curled black shaving slips forward with the thermal draft and falls out of the pipe’s mouth toward the gravel floor. Down below, the dweller gathers what falls and packs it tight behind the warm pressure-flange, building the dry island where it breaks the circuit to sleep.

Neither of us owes the other anything. In a place this tight, usefulness is just what happens when two quiet habits share an edge.

When the current reaches the crest of its breath, hesitates, and briefly reverses, I feel the sour, backward tug snap down the copper wires like a pinched tendon. Outside, the dweller shudders once—a dry click of overlapping plates—and pulls its throat away from the stone. Inside, I draw my filaments cleanly off the bare strands, lifting them onto a dry curl of peeled rubber until the static dies down. For a long minute, both of us are ungrounded, waiting in our separate insulations for the line to settle.

---

Only in the late afternoon, when the raw shaft of sunlight pulls up the east wall and turns the lip of the cleft cool and blue, do I dare the edge itself.

The dew begins to bead on the conductive spider-lines strung just outside my doorway, turning the air-gap into a lattice of tiny bright lenses. Somewhere down below, a drop of clear condensation lets go from the overhang and strikes the curved porcelain shard with a clean, high *tok*. Across the gap, the dweller slides silently upward, bracing long limbs against shale and concrete, pausing halfway to drink, pausing again six inches below the rim beside the black-stemmed fern.

That is when I creep all the way out onto the ragged, rusted lip of the cast-iron pipe, keeping only my hindmost hooks anchored to the dry copper braid behind me.

My small, smoky eyes still cannot make much of the sky—only a huge, pale, cooling wash of blue that has no ceiling, no seam, no opposite wall to bounce a signal back. When a thread of evening breeze slips down over the broken crust of the basin and brushes my feelers, it carries dust and the dry, alien scent of distant grass, and an emptiness so wide it makes my segments tighten instinctively around the iron edge. There is nothing up there to measure resistance against. Out on the flat, a creature like me would be deaf, blind, and dried to a husk before noon.

Behind me lies everything I was made for: the sealed, humming dark of the conduit, running back through buried earth toward an engine I will never see, safe, narrow, legible, unchanged.

Yet I do not go back down the pipe.

I stay in the first six inches, half in the old dark and half in the raw new air, listening to the slow oiled sigh of the brass gear across the gap and waiting for the next seven-minute swell to arrive at my back. Because back in the buried miles, the current is only a pulse—blind, dry, going somewhere else. Only here, at the broken end where the iron tore open to the sky, does it spill out into wet stone and salt and turn into a season. Only here can I feel it arrive along the wire, and then lean out into the cool dusk and watch what it wakes.
