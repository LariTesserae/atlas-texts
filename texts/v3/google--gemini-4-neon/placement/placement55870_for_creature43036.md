If I arrived in this basin without knowing yet what shape I belonged to, the first pull would be upward.

It is hard not to want the gliders’ version of this place—all thin air, stacked thermals, the clean amber glow of sunlight through a stretched wing-edge, watching a chase unfold thirty yards below like something painted on the far side of glass. And for a moment, there is a quieter temptation in the artifacts themselves: to be one of the leaning ceramic rods or a dark, hovering polyhedron, untouched by heat or grit, holding a private physics that does not answer to the sun.

Neither of those holds for long, though. The gliders live entirely outside the small, enclosed warmth of the limestone rooms; the artifacts are exempt from everything that makes the basin tender. What keeps pulling me back down off the thermals is the seam—the exact invisible boundary, ten paces from a hovering stone or three strides past a felt doorway curtain, where vibration stops surviving in the open air and has to be carried by something else: a flash of cured hide, a knuckle tapped against warm bedrock, a shallow chipped dome overhead.

So I would be one of the people. Not a hunter—I do not have the patience for lying stiff in dried alkali mud along an erosion gutter waiting for a dust-devil to cover a kill—and not one of the lowland traders who climb over the rim with loud river-voices and stumble on the silent scree. I would belong to the terraces, weathered into the same spare, dry habits as everyone else here, with chalk-dust worked into the weave of my tunic and blue-grey silt smudged under my eyes each morning against the glare.

Where I find myself living is on an eastern-facing step about halfway up the basin’s slope, tucked into a weathered spur where two old drainage lines meet. It is far enough from the nearest hovering polyhedron—a quarter-mile upslope—that the cold downdraft never creeps into my joints at night, and the ache behind the ears stays away. What the ledge gives me is an early morning sun that warms the limestone slab outside my curtain before the glare turns harsh, and cool shadow by midafternoon while the opposite terraces are still lit bright white-gold. In that shade I can push my bone visor back onto my brow, keep my eyes unstrained, and read the distant mirror-flashes blinking off the far scree with almost no effort at all.

Downslope, within an easy walk across sorted gravel, sits one of the leaning ceramic rods with its dark crescent of damp silt and thinned rushes. Farther out, flush with the braided wash, is a sunken ring of seven pale nodes. I use them the way everyone does—hauling water from the cisterns upslope, tending a small stone-lipped bed of rushes near the rod, squatting by the ring on cloudy afternoons with a flat winnowing tray.

What sets my days slightly apart—what people from three terraces over flash across the wash to ask for—grows out of a small obsession that makes complete sense only in a valley that eats sound.

I shape the rooms.

Everyone here knows how to stack limestone flags and chink them with alkali mud, and any household can chip three walls back into a chalky terrace shelf. But cutting the ceiling into a shallow dome that *keeps* sound well is slower, stranger work. Cut it too flat, or leave a hidden hairline seam unsealed near the lintel, and the room turns dull; a voice drops onto the floor almost as quickly as it does outside, and the single-stringed gourd dies before the pluck has finished leaving the thumb. Cut the curve too steep or leave the rock ragged in the wrong places, and the room turns brittle—every dropped wooden ladle rings harsh, and a child’s laugh bounces back sharp enough to make the ears flinch.

When a household is digging a new house into a step, or when an old dome sheds a thin limestone flake in winter and goes strangely dead in one corner, they wave their slate-blue cloth from a hundred paces out, or send a steady series of blinks across the dry wash. I pack my horn mallet, two short iron chisels traded from the lowlands and kept wrapped in greased hide, a fist-sized piece of smooth riverbed stone, and a lump of blue-moss charcoal.

Most of the work is just listening. I sit on the bare floor of the half-dug room with the double felt curtain dropped behind me, strike the limestone overhead with the flat of the horn, and press my cheek or palm against the cool chalk wall. You can feel a bad curve in your jawbone before you can quite name it with your ears. Where the stone rings heavy and flat, I mark a slow oval in slate-blue charcoal; where the echo crowds back too fast, I feather a light hash of lines. Then we shave it down—a finger’s width at a time, sweeping out the bright chalk dust, dropping the curtain again, letting someone pour water from a hide into an empty clay jar just to hear what the room makes of the splash.

When it is right, the room does not shout back at you. It simply *holds* what you give it—a low sentence, a wooden spoon tapped against a bowl, a plucked sinew string—holding it intact for two clean, unhurried seconds before letting it go. In a basin where the outside air takes every voice ten paces from the mouth, leaving a family with a room that rings warm and clear feels like digging them a second cistern.

Between houses, the same attention spills into smaller things. Outside my curtain, in the afternoon shade, I sort empty glassy hopper-shells by size and thickness, stringing them with dried reed segments into doorway chimes so they shiver cleanly at the brush of a shoulder without clattering. I scrape and dry gourd rinds, testing sinew tension indoors until the single string answers the dome cleanly.

And every autumn, before the long dry stretch when the cisterns begin to drop and the households prepare to work the hovering stones, people bring me slabs of shale and lengths of dense, sun-bleached bone.

That is the part of my craft I still find the strangest, even after years of it.

A shale slab meant for working a polyhedron has to be chosen and dressed with the same care you would give a musical instrument. I test each one inside my own room, propping it on two twists of dry rush and striking it with a smooth pestle, listening for the clean, deep shiver that tells me there are no internal fractures waiting to split under heavy blows. I match bone clappers in pairs so their weight sits right in the heel of the palm and their snap comes sharp and full.

Then, on a dry, cloud-filmed morning, we carry them down into the cold damp apron ten paces from a hovering black polyhedron, stake the twig with its pale glider-feather at the dry outer lip, and feed every bit of that carefully tuned sound straight into the silence.

I always take a turn inside the circle. There is nothing else in the valley that feels like it. You swing the pestle down onto a shale slab you know—because you heard it in your own house an hour ago—should ring loud enough to rattle clay jars on a shelf. Instead, the impact lands oddly cushioned in your wrists, as if you were striking thick damp wool. The sound dies an inch off the rock. Beside you, neighbors are stamping hard on the wet limestone and driving rough, tuneless syllable-chains out of their chests with all the strain of shouting across a gorge, and all you hear is a dry, stunted breath inside your own skull.

Yet the air beneath the dark hovering facets curdles silver. Cold mist spills over your ankles. The slow, separate beads clinging to the underside of the polyhedron draw together into gleaming, silent ribbons and race down the chiseled runnels toward the cisterns.

I keep one eye on the glider-feather at the edge of the wet stone, watching for the moment it stops shivering and droops straight down in dead-still air while the white-barked scrub thirty yards out still bends in the breeze. When the hollow tug begins behind my jawbones and my forearms grow heavy mid-swing, I wait for the elder’s two fingers against her collarbone, scoop up the shale, and step back out into the bright, dry warmth, panting and rubbing my ears.

Later that dusk, sitting indoors behind my heavy curtain, I pour a cup of that same water into a clay bowl and hear it splash bright and lively against the limestone dome above my head. It is a quiet, private satisfaction I rarely bother putting into words: the feeling that nothing we gave the stone was really lost, only translated—sound turned into cold water outside, and water turned gently back into sound indoors.

My outdoor habits have grown around the same quiet curiosity. In a world where every household knows every other household’s hand in sunlight, people say my mirror-flash is unhurried—a slightly wider pause between blinks than the young runners use, the beam held steady until I am sure the far terrace has caught it. I keep the matte ochre flap closed over my left wrist when I walk near the erosion gutters at dusk, only lifting it to sweep a calm, deliberate line of late sun across the dry clay so whatever pale-ochre hunter is coiled near the water knows I am coming and chooses to uncoil and melt away without fuss.

And when I am alone on my own ledge at noon, or sitting beside someone I trust in the long apricot light before the silver fog rises, I like the stone-tapping best of all.

You learn a terrace differently when you lay your ear flat against its sun-warmed pavement. With a small smooth pebble tapped lightly against the bedrock, you can tell where a shelf runs unbroken for twenty paces and where a hidden silt-filled crack cuts it clean in two. When someone sits opposite you, cheek pressed to the same warm stone, and answers your cadence with a slow knuckle-tap of their own, the rhythm arrives straight through the limestone into the bones of your skull—crisp, close, completely sealed off from the wide, bright silence three feet above your hair. No glider riding the thermals can read it. The air cannot steal it.

Wait—there is one more place I find myself going, more often as the years wear my knees a little stiffer on the scree, though I am not old yet and my eyes still read a distant mirror-spark clean across a mile of glare.

Whenever thin frost-clouds begin to comb across the high blue in the afternoon, I sometimes leave my mallet and rushes inside and walk down to the sunken ring in the braided wash—even when I have no seed or ochre left to winnow.

I do not sit inside the circle of seven pale nodes; that belongs to the very old, whose milky eyes have given up on sunlight, and to the freshly dead waiting out their last cloudy afternoon before dawn on the western rim. I sit just outside the pale stones, knees drawn up, bone visor tipped back, resting one palm flat on the dry gravel.

On the walk down, I pass the leaning ceramic rod and glance out of habit at the scratch-marks around its base, checking whether its shadow has stayed its usual hand’s-breadth east of the sun or begun creeping toward the third outer tick-mark. Everything else in the basin is accounted for by craft or carefulness—water measured by the feather, distance measured by light, rooms measured by the tap of a horn mallet.

Then a high cloud slips across the sun.

Across the seven nodes, the loose grit shivers and lifts a fraction of an inch off the dirt, hanging weightless in the dimmed light. And behind my ears, that almost imperceptible pressure eases—just for a few heartbeats—like a latch giving way on a door somewhere very far off.

Because I have spent my life chipping limestone into shallow domes, testing small rooms to hear where a wall stops a sound and where it lets it breathe, I think I feel that moment in a particular way. Outside, all day, the basin feels sealed tight under its thin, pared-down sky, every surface throwing the white-gold light straight back, every voice cut short in front of the mouth. When the cloud crosses the ring and the sand lifts, it does not feel as though the valley has gone emptier. It feels as though the ceiling overhead—the vast, bright, un-echoing dome we all live underneath—has suddenly opened a hairline seam onto a room too large for any tap or gourd-note to measure.

Two heartbeats later, the cloud clears. The sand settles cleanly back onto the dirt without a whisper. The glare returns, white-gold and steady, and out across the terraced limestone a mirror blinks once, twice—someone across the wash asking if I have time before dusk to look at a damp corner in their lintel, or simply letting me know the cisterns are covered for the night.

I lift the matte flap off my left forearm, tilt the polished hide into the sun, hold one clear, steady answer back across the miles, and start up the gravel toward home before the silver fog begins to rise.
