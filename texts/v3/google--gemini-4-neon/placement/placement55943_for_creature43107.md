At first, standing still in that baker’s warmth and letting my eyes separate the people from the limewash, I think I know immediately who I would be.

I watch the sound-sweepers moving through the narrow galleries during the straw-tinted hours, carrying wide-mouthed terracotta jars lined with unraveled hemp, pausing under thick trellises to catch a stray *clack-tink* or a forgotten sigh before plugging the mouth with a cedar bung. Something in me recognizes that work at once. To walk quietly through the aftermath of other people’s speech; to hold stillness open like a vessel until what was left hanging in the air settles inside; to decide which dry clutter goes down the central well into the inverted sky and which bright remarks deserve an ochre band and a cool alcove shelf. It feels almost too familiar—a ready-made coat cut straight to my measurements.

So I sit down on a warm hexagonal step to test the weight of that idea, resting my palms against the kiln-flour on the brick, and the longer I sit, the less certain I am that I would ever stand up to walk the lanes.

Sweeping is an act of clearing away. It treats the courtyard’s loose acoustics as litter—something spilled that must be tidied into jars or tipped over the brick rim. And as I sit there, listening to the papery whisper of vellum fronds overhead and the dry tick of a beetle behind a woven cane screen, I realize I am not drawn to the jar. I am drawn to the four minutes *before* the jar.

I am drawn to the stretch of warm, empty air between the clay pot shattering on the flagstones and the gentle *clack-tink* waking up inside a hanging basket across the court.

If I stay in this place long enough for my own edges to soften—for the quick, wet habit of haste to bake out of me—I do not think I would be found in the open well-court, nor roaming the bright alleys with a basket of tools. I would find myself inside a *turn*.

Not a room, quite, and not a corridor. Somewhere mid-level in the terraced bowl, tucked into the tight fold where a brick staircase descends six steps and arrives back on the landing it left. If you stand between a limewashed pillar and a trellis heavy with copper-foil leaves, turn your shoulders at the right quiet angle, and rest two fingers on a carved cedar peg, the masonry parts by a hand’s breadth and opens into a narrow, shaded hinge. It is scarcely wider than my outstretched arms, ceilinged with low mortised joists that keep trying to bud tiny square pine leaves down into my hair. Three different courtyards press against this same patch of afternoon without touching—separated from one another only by cane screens, sun-warmed brick, and a course of mortar that is slowly forgetting it is clay and loosening back into dry reed.

Here is where the courtyard’s speech gets tangled.

Because nothing here stops growing—because two sisters lean their backs against a parapet and coax it a course higher, or a man pins a dark polygon to the tiles and wakes up four cycles later to a solid cedar bench—the invisible paths of delayed sound are always bending out of true. A reed-weaver murmurs three gentle phrases into her shady clay pitcher, expecting them to meet her husband’s ear five minutes later by the lintel; instead, a newly budded joist deflects the drift, and her tenderness surfaces twenty minutes late inside an empty gourd-cradle two alleys over. A sharp knock meant for a needle-box snags in the rafters above a staircase and sits there like a burr, ticking down onto the shoulders of everyone who passes for half a season.

That is where I find my hands moving before I have even named the work.

I am not a builder, and not quite a sweeper. I am a tender of the seams where cause and hearing drift apart.

When a murmur wanders into my hinge-landing that clearly belongs somewhere else—a dry laugh meant for a dining table, a careful instruction about trimming a cedar leg that has lost its way and spilled out of my wall—I do not stopper it in a jar to shelve away. I sit still and listen to the *grain* of it, feeling which thermal drift carried it in, smelling whether it brushed past cinnamon reeds or toasted flax on its route. Then I rise, turn my shoulder into the next pleat, and go looking for what grew in its path.

Mending a path of sound here requires the same patience as coaxing a wall. Force would only leave its own bruise rattling in the eaves for days. Sometimes all it takes is lifting a terracotta urn half a palm’s width along a sill, or trimming back three mathematically neat joinery twigs with a bone knife so a trellis stops catching sighs in its fork. Sometimes I press the heel of my hand against a curve of limewash and sit with my shoulder leaned into the heat for an hour, until the plaster gently hollows beneath my ribs—just enough to cup a whisper and send it gliding smoothly down into the next terrace. And when a household’s words are arriving too briskly, tumbling into a room before the listener has even finished laying out the flax-husks, I will borrow a twig-frame, catch a narrow strip of dark beneath the eaves, and peg a thin bar of shadow across the threshold. A pinned shadow has a faint, fibrous density even before it thickens into wood; a spoken sentence brushing through it slows by thirty quiet seconds, arriving right as the bowl is set down.

Living inside the hinges shapes a body in particular ways.

I think I would be lean, angled slightly through the shoulders from a lifetime of slipping sideways between pillars and brittle vines. My skin would have the matte, sun-baked stillness of pale bisqueware, though less rosy than the folk who spend their afternoons out on the open parapets—closer to the cool chalk-white of shaded mortar, dusted fine at the knuckles and elbows where I lean against the masonry to listen. Beneath my layered wrap of unwashed flax, my ribs would not be stout and wide like a trellis-mender’s; they would be slender, hollow bone-reeds lashed tight with dry sinew, forming a chest that works less like a bellows and more like the unvarnished soundbox of an instrument that never plays a note of its own.

When stray voices drift through my landing on their way between courtyards, I feel them before I hear them—a faint, dry vibration passing right through the hollow warmth of my sternum, carrying the toasted-cedar scent of whoever spoke them. I do not hold on to them. I only feel whether they are passing clean or scraping against a burr in the pleat.

Like everyone else here, I would have softened at the edges in small, useful ways, and traded a piece or two where the original fit wore down. Along the underside of my left forearm, where I rest it flat against cane screens to gauge the stillness behind them, the skin would have forgotten how to be bisque and thinned into taut, oiled vellum—sensitive enough to register the distant *pop* of a child’s gourd splitting two courtyards away, minutes before the sound finds its cedar chest. And the middle knuckle of my right hand would no longer be bone at all; seasons ago, sitting on my steps, I would have popped the worn pin out and traded it to a walnut-sized ceramic beetle in exchange for a slender joint of bleached, hollow reed glued to its back. It flexes neatly when I work, and when a thermal draft shifts unexpectedly inside a fold of masonry, that tiny reed-joint lets out an almost imperceptible, cinnamon-scented hum—a small compass built straight into my fingers.

A flat shingle-lizard keeps company with me most afternoons. While I sit motionless against the brick, tracking a delayed knock through three walls, it creeps off the limewash onto my collarbone, flattens its cedar-scale plates until its shadow vanishes, and slides smoothly *into* the bisque of my throat. I feel it only as a faint, warm brushstroke gliding down my arm toward my wrist, where it passes straight back into the plaster of the cane-bound pillar beside me and emerges an hour later on the far side of an alley I have never stepped into. Watching where it reappears tells me where the walls have thinned enough for a whisper to cross.

I do not think I would be lonely in the hinge, though my company would arrive slantwise.

Neighbors rarely step all the way inside my landing; there is scarcely room for two people to sit knee-to-knee without brushing the copper vines. Instead, they pause at the crook of the stairs above me and murmur their small acoustic troubles into a split, gourd-mouthed amphora wedged beneath the eaves—*my grandmother’s remark keeps spilling into the beetle-shed instead of the loom-court; the step by the well has begun giving back yesterday’s footsteps*—and walk on. By the time they have descended six steps and emerged onto the upper terrace behind themselves, I have already felt the problem brush my ribs. When I answer, I tap my reed-knuckle gently against a cedar joist, or breathe a quiet sentence into the hollow seam behind a peg, placing it so it blooms softly out of their apron pocket just as they sit down to supper.

When I eat, it is little and slow: a shallow saucer of toasted flax-husks, a single pale bead of cedar resin that warms the roof of my dry mouth with pine smoke, and—when the parchment sky leans toward toasted straw and the afternoon grows thick with half-arrived words—a cool, dark ribbon of harvested rafter-shade laid flat along my tongue. Nothing steadies a listening mind like that dry, silk-thin coolness sinking down through hollow ribs.

Only once in a long while do I leave the shaded folds altogether.

Sometimes a voice surfaces in my alcove that I cannot place. Not clutter, not an old argument—just a single, clear phrase, warm as sun-heated clay, hanging in the gypsum dust with no obvious speaker and no waiting ear. When I have tested every cane screen and traced every budded beam and still cannot feel where it came from, I duck out of my turn and climb the steep, narrow brick stairs beneath canopies so heavy with paper-leaves that the air feels almost thick as oil.

I come out onto the highest parapet and rest my elbows on the warm outer brick rim.

Directly beyond the wall lies the exact same cramped, terracotta-and-timber bowl, rotated a quarter-turn, bathed in the same steady afternoon brightness. I look past the well-head, past the bench of softening amphorae, until my eyes find the shaded landing halfway down the far stair. There I am, five minutes ago—head tilted slightly to the left, vellum forearm pressed against a cane screen, listening intently to an empty turn. I watch the alleys around my earlier self. I see a woman stop by a dry urn three steps behind my back, press her forehead briefly to the warm clay, and murmur something she did not know anyone would hear before slipping sideways into a doorway.

I do not wave across the brick rim. I simply stand there in the parchment light until the angle of the courtyard makes sense again, letting the quiet certainty settle between my shoulder blades—knowing that five minutes ago, down in the shade, I felt that exact patch of warmth across my back and knew without turning around where to lay my hand.

And after more slow breathings of the pale sky than anyone keeps count of—after my fingers have been re-pegged twice with olive-wood, and my chalk breath drifts out a little slower each afternoon—I know where I will settle.

I will not choose a sunny cornice overlooking the well, nor the broad shoulder of a loggia column where children come to trade toggles with beetles. I will simply stop rising from the low brick step inside my hinge-landing. Over long, unhurried years, my angled shoulders will broaden and square into the limewash where the two staircases fold over one another. My flaxen shawl will loosen into a mat of fine cane woven straight into the screen; my left forearm will flatten cleanly into the bevel of the sill, keeping a faint, taut grain like oiled vellum along the wood. Inside the brick behind my back, my hollow ribs will not fill with mortar—they will open quietly into a neat, dry chamber within the masonry, lined with warm air and a trace of cinnamon dust.

For decades, my face will stay tucked softly in the shadow beneath the budding cedar joist, eyes half-lidded under thin parchment brows. Whenever a neighbor’s murmured question goes astray in the pleats and rattles past my cheek, my dry lips will part just enough to breathe a single crisp ring of white chalk dust through the sunbeam, nudging the wandering sound back onto its true course.

And even after my features have smoothed entirely back into warm, thumb-swept plaster, people passing through that turn will still pause without knowing why. They will lean close to a small, unusually warm hollow in the brick where the mortar carries the faint crescent sweep of a palm, whisper a message meant for someone three courtyards away, and walk on—trusting the dry hush of the wall to hold their words intact, and deliver them gently into the exact ear that was waiting.
