I would not stay long near the rim. A single look down at that flat salt-sea of cloud—those dark knuckles of lesser peaks poking through miles of empty air—would be enough to send me stepping backward into the steam. Even from the upper ledges, where the wind tries to pry at the basin’s edge, the rock already feels slightly thin beneath the soles, as if the hum that holds the mountain together is wearing threadbare there.

Nor would I belong down in the broad marsh-flats, swinging in the woven creeper-cradles above the tea-colored peat. I would admire the young hunters who slip out across the slick silt-bars the instant the white vapor lifts, knees bent, moving along invisible ribs of stone with the unhurried precision of the mottled predators under the ferns. I would know their routes and trade with them in the afternoon rain. But my own balance was never meant for speed, and my eyes are better at reading what barely moves than what darts.

Walking down from the ridges, following the pull in the heels of my feet and the slow tightening of the air along my forearms, I would come to rest halfway up the eastern wall of the amphitheater.

Here the valley folds into a ladder of narrow, mineral-crusted shelves just below the weeping cliffs and just above the first deep canopy of gnarled broad-leafed trees. It is a hinge-place. Behind me, the porous basalt sheets hot groundwater continuously down pale tongues of travertine; in front of me, the terraces step down through thickets of waxy dark ferns toward the wide steaming flats. The ground-current runs tight here. Dip a knuckle into one of the narrow, verdigris-rimmed troughs cut into the pale sinter, and the water answers with a steady, fine-grained prickle that climbs cleanly to the wrist.

That is where I find myself: kneeling on a smooth rib of dark stone beside three shallow troughs, breathing deep and slow into broad ribs, letting the hot, thin air fill my lungs without rushing it.

My shoulders and forearms are dull-mottled with the matte paste of crushed oily berries, fine silt, and pale clay—thick enough that the endless steaming wet beads and slides off instead of soaking my hide soft. My hair is shorn close above the ears and twisted behind into short, oiled cords weighted at the tips with tiny spheres of dark vitreous slag, panned from the deep spring-vents. When the current tightens just before a lift, the little glass beads tap softly against the back of my neck, holding the cords down so they don’t float up in a pale halo of static and mist. Around my left wrist sits a single dull band of red metal, hammered smooth long ago and tarnished green along the edges where it meets the salt of my skin.

Nothing in my hands is in a hurry.

In the trough closest to my knees, four carved splinters of dense upland hardwood and a sharpened leg-bone from a small climber stand upright in the silt, wedged between pale mineral-crusted cobbles. Weeks ago, I painted each of them with a thin, velvet-smooth coat of black magnetite sand ground fine with a drop of berry oil. Since then, I have done almost nothing to them except keep the channel clear and turn them a quarter-spin every few rains. The green water creeps past—warm, heavy with pure copper salts leached from depths no daylight has ever reached—and the invisible current pulling through the basin draws dull, spongy red metal out of the flow, laying it down grain by unseen grain over the dark mud.

I lift the bone awl out between two fingers, keeping clear of the hot runoff seam behind it, and hold it up under the shade of the overhanging ferns.

The red skin is thickening evenly along the shaft, though near the shoulder where a stray thread of pale sinter tried to take hold, the deposit has grown pebbled and rough. I scrape the glassy crust away with a sliver of flaked basalt, rinse the awl in the slow spill at the trough’s lower lip, and set it back into the tingling silt, angled slightly closer to the eastern stone where the pull runs cleanest. Another ten rains—perhaps more, if the seepage cools by a breath—and it will be thick enough to wash free of green salt and carry back under my eave. Then I will spend quiet afternoons tapping the spongy red coat down with a rounded river pebble until the surface closes, brightens, and takes a heavy edge.

It takes half a year to grow a single good knife. Even then, when the finished copper rings softly against a knuckle, the carved bone is still sealed inside it, out of sight, giving the metal its shape.

Once you start noticing that, you see the same habit everywhere in the valley.

Two ledges above my troughs, tucked beneath a dark overhang of basalt, sits the eave where I sleep. My mother’s older sister started weaving it before I could walk—broad, curved aprons of living fern-stems and braided creeper-roots anchored straight into the weeping seam. By the time I was grown, years of mineral-rich runoff had turned the ribs to pale, glassy travertine furred in swollen liverwort and copper-tinted moss. Outside, the afternoon rain and hot condensation sheet harmlessly down the stony curtain; inside, the bare basalt floor radiates dry, steady warmth from beneath, keeping my woven rush-mats clean and scenting the dark with dried peat-grass.

Yet along the low eastern lip, the shelter is not finished. Every few rains I tuck fresh green fern-stems into the wet mineral spill, coaxing the overhang two palms wider so the splash off the outer shelf won’t reach my pounding-stones. It will not turn fully to stone until my own joints begin to stiffen in the evening chill. And twenty paces farther along the same weeping cliff-face, half-buried in smooth pale crust, is the bulge where my grandfather’s eave used to open. He has been gone long enough that the dripping water has sealed the low mouth nearly shut—only a dark crescent barely wide enough for a hand remains, breathing faint dry heat out of a warm hollow no one will sleep in again.

We grow stone over woven stems. We grow red metal over painted bone. We rub dark silt and oil over our own flushed skin. And beneath all of us, miles of wet basalt and glassy sinter hold whatever vast, smooth-turning casing keeps the mountain hot against the freezing sky.

Everything that lasts here is a slow crust grown over a quiet core.

My work lives along the seams between those layers. I am not an elder yet—my eyes still cut cleanly through the unbuffered glare between clouds, and my bare soles still grip the submerged stone ribs in the marsh without slipping—nor am I one of the hunters whose days belong to the quick whiteout of the lifts. Instead, I have become one of the people who listen for when the balance begins to drift.

You notice it first in the troughs.

That is how it starts this morning, three lifts after the first clear mountain sun hits the high outer ridges. The second trough—the narrow upper one fed by a pale fissure under the cliff—is laying down copper slightly softer on its downstream face than it did a hand of rains ago. Nothing dramatic. No sudden stop, no choking. Only a faint sluggishness in the water against my knuckles, and at the quiet silt-lip below the spill, the fine black grains of magnetite sand have shifted the angle of their parallel ripples by less than the width of a fern-stem.

I rinse the green salt from my fingers, loop a strip of braided creeper-bark over my shoulder with two small basalt wedges and a dense bone mallet tucked inside, and climb up the terraced ribs toward the weeping wall.

Above the tree-line of the mid-terrace, the air grows hotter against the skin and thinner in the throat. I keep beneath the dark shelves of basalt and the broad rubbery leaves tilted to shed rain; up in the clear blue sky above the rising mist, a pair of broad-winged shadows slide without flapping along the invisible thermal columns. Their pale eyes miss nothing on open stone. I wait out the last breath of the quiet lull tucked under an apron of liverwort.

Then the valley warns me.

The fine damp hairs along my forearms lift slightly. A faint, clean tang of copper touches the back of my tongue. Down on the wet travertine shelves below, three sleek, blunt-headed amphibians slide out of the mossy clefts, their leathery hides glistening as they nose toward the magnetized mud. A heartbeat later, the water along the channels swells two fingers higher, turning measurably hotter; a sharp, brief thread of ozone and hot mineral oil braids through the smell of wet peat; and white vapor lifts off every bog-flat and seepage seam in unison.

Inside that sudden curtain of steam, I step out across the open shelf and climb straight up the wet basalt rib to the high weeping ledge.

One of the oldest men of our kin-pocket is already there, seated cross-legged on a flat tongue of rock that would blister an uncallused heel. His eyes have clouded slightly from decades of altitude light, and his back is pressed straight against a thick, lichen-flecked bulge of pale stone where the deep rotary hum runs strongest. Even three paces away, I can feel it climb cleanly up my shins—dead-level, unhurried, smooth as oil, holding no hint of the ragged tremor a crumbling peak makes.

I don’t speak loud; the thin air eats a voice and there is no breath to waste. I squat beside him on the hot ledge, pitch a low murmur forward in my mouth and throat—just above the bedrock drone so the two vibrations stay clean of one another in the jaw—and let a soft click carry the question: *The upper trough runs slow. Is the lip closing?*

He doesn’t turn his head. He shifts his bare heel two fingers sideways onto a vein of dark, bare rock and taps once with the side of his foot, listening up through his shinbone. Then he lifts a hand—knuckles knotted from forty winters of paring sinter—and points behind the dripping moss-curtain just above his shoulder.

I part the wet copper-tinted moss carefully, keeping my wrists back from the hottest seepage.

There it is, buried under half a hand’s width of pale glassy crust: the flush lip of blackened cast metal, straighter than any fracture rock makes on its own, weeping hot water down the cliff-face in a thin, trembling sheet. Along its left edge, fresh chalk-white sinter has grown a pale tongue across the geometric seam, pinching the flow down to a tight, uneven hiss.

*Too narrow,* the old man murmurs, barely spending a thimble of air. *My wrist won’t hold the wedge straight today.*

I take the smaller basalt wedge from my shoulder-strip and set its edge against the base of the pale crust—not stripping the blackened metal bare, never that. Leave the dark casing naked to the air and the seam hisses fierce enough to scald the fern-roots on two terraces below and drive the amphibians out of the lower pools. You pare it only back to a smooth, steady weep, the way his elders pared it before him.

Tap. Tap. Tap.

Small, dry knocks of bone mallet on basalt wedge, spaced between breaths so my rhythm never fights the steady turning underneath. Crescent flakes of pale, glassy stone shear cleanly away and clatter down into the wet moss. With the third flake, the tight hiss softens immediately into a broad, quiet sigh. Hot water sheets smoothly out across the travertine shelf again, clean and even, steaming softly into the cooling edge of the lift.

I tuck the wedge away, wipe the chalk-dust from my palms onto my clay-darkened thighs, and sit beside the old man for half a lift while the vapor thins back into clean mountain sun. I lean my own oiled shoulder-blades against the warm, lichen-crusted bulge beside his.

Through the stone, the deep hum runs straight up my spine, loosening the knots between my ribs. We don’t name what turns beneath us. We have no word for it that separates it from the mountain itself, and no hymn for it either—you don’t bargain with a pulse that has never once hesitated or missed a lift in the longest chain of memory anyone holds. You only keep its margins clear, keep time with its swells, and lean against its warmth when your bones ache.

Before I climb back down, I leave a tight parcel of waxy fern-leaves behind a rib of stone in the seam’s cooler runoff, weighted with a smooth river cobble—pale bog-tubers and stripped amphibian flesh I packed at dawn. Three lifts from now, when the mist rises again, it will be steeped clean through for his evening meal. He answers with a slow, dry double-tap of his knuckles against the warm rock beside my knee, and closes his eyes.

By the time I reach my mid-terrace again, the sun has climbed high and the basin is already making its own sky. Columns of warm vapor rising off the wet terraces collide with the freezing air above the ridges and assemble out of nothing into thick, luminous bands of cloud. By midday, a soft gray curtain settles across the amphitheater. Warm, quiet rain drifts down through the bright air, pattering onto leaves that were already dripping, veiling the open silt-bars from both the fierce mountain light and the pale-eyed hunters circling a thousand feet up.

The valley opens.

I gather two finished copper awls wrapped in dry creeper-bark, a small pouch of uniform dark slag-beads sorted by weight, and a parcel of freshly dried climber-strips I pulled off the upper baking slabs before the clouds closed in. Barefoot, following the submerged stone ribs I know through the calluses of my soles, I descend through the dripping ferns to the broad silt-flats on the valley floor.

Before I reach the meeting-ground, I pause beside a quiet bar of black magnetite sand where the runoff slows to a clear film.

Someone passed here earlier, during one of the clear-sky lifts. Three hooked strokes curve deliberately across the neat parallel ripples of the iron grain—the mark of a kin-group from the western shelves, telling whoever follows that they found a fresh nest of bog-eggs near the lower marsh-margin and traded uphill. I squat on my heels and study the edges of the grooves. The shoulders of the sand have already softened, half-combed back toward straight lines by the quiet pull of the ground. Two lifts old, perhaps three. By the fifth lift, the valley will have smoothed the bar entirely clean again, keeping nothing that no longer matters.

I drag one forefinger beside their curve, leaving two short, sharp hooks of my own—*copper ready on the eastern shelf, bring clean bone*—and watch the dark grains tremble slightly in the shallow water as the basin already begins the slow work of erasing my hand.

Down on the wide flat, a dozen people from three separate terraces squat on their heels in the warm drizzle, rain ticking steadily off the fern-umbrellas behind them. There is no shouting, no bustle, no chief calling order. Wrapped parcels pass quietly from dark palm to palm: dense upland nuts, cured strips of copper-backed lizard, fresh bundles of braided creeper-snare, small pouches of pale copper salt scraped from deep eastern vents. We murmur low and close together, teeth and tongues shaping economical clicks that carry cleanly over the hush of rain without stealing breath.

A young hunter from the marsh-floor—long-limbed, smelling faintly of peat and slick moss, with tiny blue sparks still dying on the fern-fronds tucked into his waist-wrap—takes one of my copper awls and tests its point softly against the thick callus of his thumb. He turns it once in the dull gray light, reading the tiny dimples left by my river pebble, feeling the heft of the bone hidden inside the red metal. Without a word, he unties a small leaf-pouch from his creeper-strap and sets it in my palm: three dense leg-bones cleaned pale by the warm bog-water, and a pinch of exceptionally fine black iron-sand washed up from a deep spring-vent along the southern wall.

Good mud for the next half-year’s blades.

I climb back to the eastern mid-terrace as the rain thins and the afternoon begins to tilt toward evening. Along the way, I check my troughs one last time. Already the water in the upper channel runs smoother against my knuckles, the tiny parallel ripples of black sand below the spill aligned straight and true again. The half-grown bone awl rests steady in its green bath, gathering red metal atom by invisible atom while I sleep.

Toward dusk, the light cools to amber across the high outer walls while the cloud-sea miles below turns violet and dark. The upper air drops toward freezing, and the contrast makes the whole basin exhale in earnest. Thick, slow plumes of white steam detach from the marsh-terraces and rise straight into the thinning sky, glowing briefly in the last clean shafts of mountain sun. Down among the drenched ferns, the blunt-headed amphibians begin their sparse, dripping click.

Inside my eave, the bare basalt floor holds its steady dry warmth against my legs. I sit just back from the pale, moss-draped curtain of stone, a fresh bone splinter in my lap and a small river-cobble resting warm beside my hip, listening to the hush settle over the amphitheater.

Then I rest my bare heel flat against the dark basalt rib that runs out beneath the threshold and ties four terraces together.

Beneath the ticking of water off wet leaves, beneath the faint sigh where hot seepage meets cooling mud, the deep rotary hum holds dead-level through miles of bedrock—unhurried, faithful, holding the mountain warm against the empty sky.

A moment later, rising crisp and distinct up through my shinbone against that steady drone, comes a dry double-tap from a shelf lower down the slope.

*All clear. The mist is thick. The chill is settling.*

A heartbeat later, another double-tap answers from far across the steaming bog, faint and clean through the dark stone vein.

I reach one bare, copper-ringed hand briefly out through the dripping curtain of moss and pale travertine, letting the sharp evening chill kiss the backs of my knuckles while a slow plume of amber-lit steam drifts past my fingertips. Then I draw my hand back into the warm dark of the stone, lay my ringed wrist lightly against the basalt rib beside my knee, and tap twice—quiet, measured, keeping time with the pulse beneath the roots.
