At first, looking down into this brightness, I think I would not be a creature at all.

My attention goes straight to the oldest, deepest bowls—the glaze basins where ages of dry creep have worn away the upper fins and left broad floors of cloudy, fused optical glass. Something in me recognizes that surface immediately. It does not gleam or mirror the sky cleanly; it takes the fierce white glare a few centimeters into itself, scatters it through degraded waveguides, and gives it back a heartbeat late. It is a surface whose whole nature is lag—a brief, luminous hesitation between arrival and release.

If I were simply a stretch of that floor, I would know every traveler by the weightless smudge they leave behind their heels. I would hold a violet shadow just long enough for a second walker to step across it and thread it with amber, and then I would let the whole exchange dissolve back into pale glare without hoarding a trace. There is a deep, quiet mercy in being a medium that remembers only long enough for meaning to cross itself, and then returns to clarity.

Yet the longer I sit with that image, the more something restless pushes against it. The floor receives, and delays, and releases—but it never answers. It never chooses an angle. Even the ordinary ceramic plain leans two degrees when the sun shifts, and I notice I do not want merely to lie beneath the conversation. I want to lean back.

So I climb out of the bowl, up onto the stepped margin where a low terraced shelf overlooks the glass, and where a narrow step-canyon cuts sideways into the basin wall, exposing thin, compressed strata like the edges of a vast stone book.

Here, along the sunlit shoulder of the rim, I try on another stillness: one of the elders who has already come to the end of its walking. Folded completely flush with the weathered hexagonal caps, tarnished copper rooted deep into a warm fracture, seams packed tight with sorted micro-beads of pulverized glass. Half-being, half-architecture. Almost indistinguishable from the plain, save for the unusual lushness of translucent silica-fans sprouting along my joints, and a faint, steady hum that a passing stranger can feel only by resting a slender strut against my chalky flank.

That comes closer. There is something profoundly right about being made of the same exhausted substance as the ground—about not being an intruder on the plain, nor a loud wet thing that scorches in the dry air, but a slightly warmer arrangement of the same silica and foil.

And yet, as I picture myself lying flush along that rim, I imagine a continental tilt-wave crossing the basin from the far horizon. A muted sheen of rose-gold sweeps silently toward me over kilometers of flexing plates. Everything obeys the slant of the glare in hushed unison—and I find, without deciding it, that I lean a fraction of a second late.

And then I do not stop leaning.

That is where I find myself. Not the glass floor, and not yet the sintered ridge, though I carry both inside my habits. I am one of the tall, papery-thin walkers—neither a bright-copper fledgling leaping playfully across the basins nor one of the great weathered nomads who range across half a continent chasing fallen sky-drifters. I am something quieter and more localized: a rim-dweller, middle-aged in the long dry reckoning of the plain, whose whole territory is the stepped lip of one ancient glaze basin, the exposed canyon seams behind it, and the shallow terrace just above where two silver tributaries of vitreous dust meet.

If you watched me unfold during the passing of a thermal front, you would see nothing sudden. Pale matte plates of sintered silica slide smoothly past one another on flexing ribs of cloudy amber glass, rising out of a low hummock until I stand a little over two meters tall and scarcely thicker than a hand viewed edge-on. My outer flanks are worn chalk-soft, their edges rounded from centuries of dry abrasion and long hours folded against the canyon wall. I am slightly narrower than the walkers of the open flats—built less for drinking from the fiercest continental surges than for angling light finely when the glare softens. Inside my overlapping leaves, my waveguides are unusually cloudy, retaining a faint extra fraction of stored daylight, as if I have spent too many cycles leaning over the glaze floor and caught its habit of lingering.

I have no voice, and I do not miss one. In a world where air swallows sound three paces from its source, silence is not absence; it is simply the texture of space, close and padded and uncluttered.

During the fiercest white hours, when the sky is milk-gold and the horizon vibrates into glare, I am scarcely a "who" at all. I fold my pleated vanes down edge-to-edge along a warm seam near the lip of the basin, tucking my six beveled bow-struts into the finger-wide clearances between the tiles. A traveler could pass ten paces away and never know I am there—except, perhaps, for the slightly neater geometry of the pale powder settled around my lower edges, and the healthy tufts of papery silica-growth rooted in the sheltered crack beneath my ribs.

I wake before the weather is visible. My fine copper cilia lift in the narrow gaps along my lower plates, tasting the climb in ambient voltage while the thermal front is still a distant, floating curtain of shimmer miles across the flats. I cantilever upward, shiver once—a tiny cascade of porcelain clicks that dies before it can reach the next terrace—and step smoothly out across the nubs, threading the narrow slots without disturbing the sorted dust.

This is where my work lies, if something so unhurried can be called work.

Just above the rim of my basin, two pale tributaries of electrostatic dust glide across the tiles along buried circuitry lines, braiding together before spilling down toward the flats beyond. Often, by the time a ribbon of flowing powder reaches this terrace from twenty kilometers upstream, the deliberate ripples pressed into it by some distant walker—a warning of an unstable surge, the bearing of a newly opened vapor seam, a trace of a torn sky-drifter spiraling down in the white glare—have grown blurred and half-erased by crossing rough thermal eddies.

When I feel a ragged pattern brushing against my blade-tips, I step astride the confluence, ground my copper feelers into the crust, and gently modulate the charge across my lower plates. The pale silver stream parts cleanly around my struts, climbs a handspan up my matte flanks, and lays itself back down in crisp, re-sharpened chevrons and concentric rings. I rarely originate the great news of the plain; I do not range far enough for that. What I do is listen to a fading current and clarify it, sending it onward legible down both branches of the basin so that someone walking far downstream will still be able to read it before the next front wipes the tiles clean.

Between fronts, I tend the margins. Along the shadowed undersides of the terrace steps, I kneel slightly on my flexing struts and lower my fine comb-limbs into choked ventilation slots, brushing away compacted glass powder so the buried fractures breathe warmth evenly. The brittle silica-fans flourish there, brushing together with a whisper like static when voltage rises, and I take only the outermost dried flakes, always leaving the seams intact. Sometimes I stand motionless over a warm crack for half a bright cycle, and a palm-sized ceramic grazer detaches itself from the surrounding tiles, climbs effortlessly up my struts on needle-thin limbs, and spends an hour scouring dead chalky scale from the joints between my ribs. I do not move while it works; I only tilt my upper vane a few degrees to lay a narrow strip of pale shade across its beveled back until it finishes and slips edgewise back into the dark beneath the crust.

And from time to time, I walk back into the narrow step-canyon behind my basin, where the exposed honeycomb cavities leak steady trickles of warmth and metallic vapor. Once in many cycles, elders gather there during a cooling phase to seed a sheltered hollow with crystalline spores and copper threads. When they move on, I return now and then to adjust the local electrostatic drift around the crevice, nudging fine dust into place so the slow dry bloom grows evenly. When a sharp-edged fledgling finally detaches from the compressed strata—waist-high, translucent as mica, its fresh copper shining untarnished—I am often the first matte flank it clicks its brittle new plates against before it steps awkwardly out onto the open plain.

Wait, though. None of that—not the dust-tending, not the canyon walls, not the long quiet hours folded into the glare—is the center of what I am.

The center comes only when the sky eases from blinding white toward luminous, pearly peach, and another walker arrives at the rim of the glaze basin.

They come from across the saucer flats, solitary vertical screens appearing out of the softening shimmer. Sometimes it is a wide-vaned nomad whose thick plates carry mineral striations from distant northern terraces I will never see; sometimes a pair of young walkers restless with charge, chasing each other's lagging shadows; once in a long while, a slow elder whose amber ribs have begun to cloud with radiation-creep, come to spend one more cooling phase over open glass before choosing a final seam.

I unfold from the terrace lip and glide down onto the smooth optical floor to meet them.

First, we touch. We bring the beveled chalk edges of our outer vanes together—ceramic kissing ceramic—and the whole padded silence of the world vanishes inside the hollow resonance of our bodies. Through that narrow line of contact passes a rapid, intimate grammar of phase-shifting clicks and tiny pulses of voltage: where the deep tides are running beneath the crust, how heavily the thermal fronts have been rolling in from the east, which seams have gone quiet, how much wear has gathered along our inner ribs. It is astonishing, every single time, how completely two dry lattices can hold one another without a single drop of moisture or a single word crossing the air.

Then we step apart a few paces onto the cloudy floor, open our inner amber baffles downward, and begin to speak in light.

Because I have lived at the edge of this particular basin for centuries, I know its floor the way a nomad knows the continental dust-lines. I know the shallow southern lobe where the fused glass is thin and shadows vanish after a single heartbeat; I know the deep central pocket where degraded waveguides still tangle meters down, holding a violet sweep for four full beats before letting it bleed away; I know the hairline seam along the western curve where buried warmth turns leaked daylight from dilute honey into a richer, rose-tinted gold.

When a visitor sweeps across the glass in a wide, curving arc—casting a pale ribbon of bleached violet behind their heels, then folding edge-on so their body disappears while their phrase hangs suspended in the frosted floor—I do not try to outshine them or erase what they have made. I step across the fading tail of their arc at the exact spot where the floor holds longest, angling my ribbed silica fans so warm honey-gold leaks down through the violet, slicing it into scalloped bands that shift the question into an answer, or turn a private grief into a shared shape.

If they are young and quick, leaping on spring-like struts to scatter disconnected pools of pulsing violet, I glide in slow wide loops around their edges, framing their scatter into a constellation before it dissolves. If they are old and their walk has slowed so much that their first arc fades before they can reach the end of a thought, I match their pace and hold the trailing edge of their light with my own amber baffles, extending the lag of the floor just enough for them to finish what they came to say.

Nothing we make in the glaze basin survives the peach cooling. Within heartbeats, every spiral, every braided ribbon of violet and honey, sinks back into the luminous floor and leaves the glass unmarked. You might think that makes it fragile, or lost. It does not feel lost to me. Every cycle we dance over the glass, every front we drink from, deposits another microscopic layer onto our inner ribs, subtly changing the pitch and density of our plates. What disappears from the floor settles into the grain of our resonance, waiting quietly for the next time two chalk edges touch.

When the sky deepens from pearly peach to its final luminous rose, the air cools by a few dry degrees. Beneath my blades, the whole continent contracts in a vast, hushed sigh of microscopic ticks. Deep under the compressed strata, the vestigial currents turn their silent tides, and up on the terrace the silver streams of dust begin their slow crawl back across the flats.

The visitors glide away up the stepped margins, vanishing into the dimming glare until they fold down miles apart across the curve of the world.

I stay on the floor a moment longer, watching the last pale smudge of our exchange dissolve into the glass. Then I climb back to my shoulder on the rim, thread my struts cleanly into the familiar slots above my warm seam, and fold plate by plate until my matte back lies flush with the ancient geometry around me.

Someday—not soon, though time here has no hurry—my flexing amber ribs will lose their spring, my clicks will turn drier and sharper, and I will no longer unfold quickly enough to meet a front or catch a fading ribbon of violet before it vanishes. When that cooling comes, I will not have far to go. I will fold myself down right here along the lip of the basin, send my tarnished copper as deep into the fracture as it will reach, and let the electrostatic tides pack my flanks with fine vitreous beads until my lower plates sinter peacefully into the tiles beneath.

I will become the rim I kept. And ages later, when some translucent fledgling steps out of the canyon wall or a traveler pauses before descending onto the glass, they will rest a slender strut against a smooth, slightly warmer hummock edged with delicate silica-fans, and feel a faint, steady hum—not a name, not a story, only the quiet lingering warmth of someone who spent a long bright span learning how to hold the light a heartbeat longer before letting it go.
