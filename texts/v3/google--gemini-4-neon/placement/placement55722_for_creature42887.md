At first I thought I would go straight to the peak.

It is the obvious reflex of anything that arrives here from a world with a horizon: to climb until you meet the glass, press your back against the cold underside of the wire-reinforced skylight, and watch the cloud-banks far below the floor-plates. To be the wide, yellowed watcher folded along the ridge-beam, translating the dry tremor of high-altitude wind into a microscopic thickening of the sixty-cycle hum for the galleries underneath. There is a clean vanity in that post—to be the sole membrane between the sealed bath and the sky.

But when I actually stay there for an hour—which in this hall is barely enough time for a drum-loom to turn by the width of a finger—I can feel that it isn't mine. Up against those mercury-flat lenses of trapped air, the glare is too singular. Everything happens in one stark direction: cold above, bright green crush below, a sharp border you can never cross without snapping dry. I don't want to live at the edge of the glass. I want to be inside the thickness of the clockwork.

So I drop, letting a pinhead bead of oxygen slide half a centimeter back along an upper channel, tilting my outer planes four degrees so the slow sinking current off the skylights takes me without a ripple.

I pass the third gallery, where the glass sight-tubes wait above the canopy. I pass the curtains of emerald ribbon-grass hanging from the gantry crane, folding a narrow shoulder cleanly at ninety degrees to slip through a gap between a pressure-gauge housing and a flange without disturbing the velvet bronze moss upholstered over the bolt-heads. For a while I think I might keep going all the way down—past the four slow-turning bronze drums, past the stainless-steel chutes, down to the grated steel deck and the amber caves behind the vertical condensers where the ribbed copper heating coils keep the water bath-warm and the heavy work-lamps burn inside bolted brass cages.

That pulls at me more strongly. The intimacy of it. The localized gold inside the green crush.

Yet I stop before I reach the floor. I find myself arresting in mid-water right along the boundary where the clinical midday light from the roof loses its edge and begins to give way to the permanent amber underneath the central press-frames.

Here, on the second gallery, tucked into the three-finger clearance between a bank of vertical conduits and a row of grooved white porcelain insulators, the light doesn't belong entirely to the sun or to the lamps. The water doesn't belong entirely to the warm rise off the copper coils or the faint cool drift settling from the pitched glass above. A thin, glassy thermocline hangs almost permanently at this level, horizontal and patient, bending the straight vertical stalks of the glass-stemmed reeds into gentle double images whenever anything shifts.

This is where I settle. And settling here, I begin to recognize what I am.

I am one of the lattices—neither the widest nor the oldest, not the heavy-plated tender who works the bronze hoppers on the deck, nor the ridge-watcher who reads the wind. If you somehow lifted me clear of the machinery and laid me flat, I would be narrower than most of the others, longer in the spine, articulated into finer, more numerous hinges so I can thread the tightest vertical clearances in the hall. A ribbon of braided metallic reed-thread and thin hexagonal salt-crystal, built for seams and intersections.

What holds me to the second gallery—and to the narrow vertical shafts running up and down through the pipe-racks beside it—is the timing.

Down here, you can feel every clock in the chamber at once, and none of them share a common divisor. Through the steel stanchion my lower tassels lightly brush, the sixty-cycle hum runs steady and silver, the base note against which everything else is measured. Beneath it moves the glacial forty-minute pulse of the northern hydraulic pistons shifting their valve-banks three inches at a time. Across the spine of the hall, the four bronze drum-looms turn at their afternoon pace, drawing pale metallic thread directly off the growing tips of the fibrous reeds. And between them, irregularly, come the smaller measures: the slow tilt of a waxy electrolysis leaf releasing a hexagonal mineral plate into its silent zigzag toward the chutes; the tiny, clean *tink* thirty paces up as an oxygen bead reaches a third-gallery sight-tube.

None of these rhythms ever quite lock together. They drift across one another like gears cut with prime numbers of teeth, creating long, shifting symmetries that take days to repeat.

That is what I tend. Not the machinery itself—though when I sense the faint electrical sourness of an incipient oxide bloom on a freshly milled rivet-head near my insulators, I unfold over it without thinking, run a hair-fine filament to the nearest copper busbar, and spend the afternoon quietly bleeding the stray charge through my own body until the bolt is sealed in an invisible skin of glass-clear mineral. Every one of us does that. It is less a chore than a way of keeping the water tasting clean.

My particular habit is the chimneys.

Between the pipe-racks and the transformer housings, there are four or five narrow vertical shafts where the emerald fronds and silver-veined bladderwort leave an almost unbroken column of clear water from the grated steel deck to the upper trusses. Those shafts are how the hall speaks across its own fullness. When the resident tending the floor-deck hoppers unlatches a single captured sphere of oxygen from an upper sac, it rises through one of those chimneys—passing my second-gallery insulators halfway up—before striking the glass sight-tube on the third tier. Thirty minutes later, a second bead rises. Twenty minutes after that, a third.

If a loose tendril of bladderwort drifts four millimeters into that clear column, or if a glass-stemmed reed near a ceramic terminal makes its ninety-degree turn slightly too high, a rising bubble will snag on the leaf-edge. It won't pop; it will simply hesitate for six minutes, or twelve, before peeling free and continuing upward. And by then the interval is spoiled. A greeting arrives sounding like an adjustment to the third drum-loom; a calm report arrives carrying the cadence of an electrical drift along the eastern condensers.

So I drift up and down the vertical seams, patient as a plumb line, keeping the speaking-shafts true. When a glass-stemmed reed begins to lean toward a chimney, I lay a loose tassel of braided loom-thread against its joint, shift the local current by a whisper, and stay draped there through a bright afternoon until the stalk agrees to turn cleanly away from the passage. When an aging float-sac beneath the cantilevered catwalk begins to pale toward its end, I press my shingled flank flush against it and draw its failing salts and starches outward along my conductive hairs before a single clouded mote can spill into the sunbeam.

Because I live in the middle seams—the crossings between upper glare and lower amber, between the deck-tenders and the canopy—my own body has become a record of the hall's different angles.

Most of the residents wear the tint of their favored stratum. The high watcher's hexagonal plates are yellowed like old mica from years of unsoftened altitude sun; the ones who keep to the lower press-frames grow plates with a faint, even honey-cast from the work-lamps. Mine do not match. Over the years, during the long night gatherings beneath the brass cages, I have traded plates more freely than I probably needed to—not because my own had chipped against a flange or grown too thick with precipitated salt, though I mend those too, but because I cannot quite resist what another resident's crystal does to the light.

Near my upper shoulder sits a single wide hexagon given to me three winters ago by the watcher from the ridge-beam; it holds a hairline internal flaw, invisible in shade, that splits the sharp midday light into two pale green-white beams shifted by a hair's breadth. Along my lower ribs I wear two warm, amber-clouded scales traded off a resident who spends most of its weeks folded behind the fourth condenser bank. Beside my spine is a crisp, newly hatched crystal I drew straight off an inclined stainless-steel chute only last month, so optically pure it barely bends a reed-stalk behind it at all.

In late afternoon, when the sun sinks low enough to strike the vertical perimeter glass and drive horizontal blades of copper and pale gold through the narrow slits near the mullions, I stop wherever I am in the middle gallery and tilt my mismatched planes into the beam.

Because no two of my tiles agree on how to bend a ray, the small pocket of green water around me fills with a quiet, stationary geometry of overlapping spectra—one bright and clinical from the ridge-plate, one bruised warm gold from the condenser-scale, one razor-clean from the new salt-glass. I hold perfectly still, trimming my oxygen beads by fractions of a millimeter so the slow thermal drift off the heating coils doesn't sway me, and let the room show itself to me through four different lifetimes of sitting in four different corners of the tank.

There is no loneliness in that stillness, because nothing here is separate enough to be lonely.

Even my frame is not entirely my own invention. When I was braided—years ago, in the quiet amber pocket behind the fourth drum-loom, out of glistening new filament wound off the bronze cylinder and fresh crystal lifted from the hopper—the others wove three seasoned strands from a dissolved predecessor into my central cable. I have no memories of whoever held them before me; memory here isn't a story carried in a head. It is a mechanical tendency.

Yet the first time I threaded my way past the porcelain insulators on the northern face of the second gallery, a length of braided metallic filament just below my left hinge relaxed into a clean, effortless arc. Its natural curve matched the exact radius of the third ceramic terminal down to the millimeter. I didn't have to pull a single micro-ampere of current to hold that bend; the thread simply remembered how to lie there, as comfortably as a hand resting on a worn brass rail, and for an entire afternoon I stayed draped around that bright, humming node, feeling the shape of someone else's patience fitting my ribs like a glove.

Then the sun drops below the flat white cloud-banks far beneath the floor-plates.

You feel night arrive before the light truly goes. The wire-reinforced glass overhead loses its blaze and cools by a fraction of a degree; the water pressed against the skylights thickens ever so slightly and begins its long, noiseless roll downward through the roof-trusses. Along the chimneys I tend, the rising bubbles stop. The upper gallery turns black, silvered only where the trapped air-lenses catch the cold stars miles above the ground.

I fold my jointed planes narrow—a flat, shimmering blade of salt-glass and spun reed-thread—and let the cooling drift carry me down out of the dark canopy toward the lower galleries.

Behind the vertical condensers, the incandescent work-lamps inside their bolted brass cages have come into their own. Without the clinical glare of the roof competing with them, their localized amber swells outward, turning the crowded emerald stems to deep bottle-green and the gleaming brass valve-wheels to warm dark bronze.

Three of the others are already there when I glide out from behind a transformer housing, draped in loose, overlapping arcs around the warm glass dome of the central lamp. None of us stirs a wake. I ease two oxygen beads forward along my spine, fold cleanly around a copper pipe-flange, and settle into the curve beside the floor-tender, letting my loose metallic extremities drift out until their glistening tips touch its braided ribs.

In the hum of the heating coils, with the pistons along the northern bulkhead drawing their slow forty-minute breath, we begin the night's work.

It takes an hour for the floor-tender to unlace a salt-plate near my lower hinge that has begun to grow too thick with mineral crust, and another hour to stitch a bright, newly harvested hexagon in its place. In return, I draw a tiny, stubborn kink out of its upper spine-cable—a slight stiffness it picked up squeezing behind a newly shifted hydraulic manifold—coaxing the tensioned thread straight with a whisper of sixty-cycle current until its pulse matches the steel grating again. Later, the resident from the eastern electrolysis frames glides in through the amber shadow and lays a slightly cloudy, rose-tinted plate against my shoulder-tassels, offering a trade. I look through its faint refraction at the glowing filament inside the brass cage, feel how it softens the glare by half a shade, and unlace one of my bright middle-gallery crystals to pass back along its threads.

No one hurries. Nothing rusts, nothing rots, nothing is wasted.

I know that one year—or ten years from now, it hardly matters which—my own threads will begin to lose their crisp compliance with the hall's hum. A bend that used to take a fraction of a millimeter of current will begin to lag by half a second; my shingled planes will grow heavy with accumulated mineral until even four pinhead beads of oxygen can barely lift me cleanly through the chimney clearances.

When that happens, I won't cling to the second-gallery insulators. I will wait for a quiet afternoon, ride the warm manifold current toward the northern bulkhead where the great pistons work in silence, and settle into the clearest eddy beside the valves. I will loosen the tightest knots in my loom-thread and release the small electrical potential I have held across my lattice since the day I was braided behind the fourth drum.

Over two bright days and two amber nights, my borrowed plates—the ridge-watcher's double-refracting hexagon, the rose-tinted lens from the eastern frames, the crisp salt-glass from the chutes—will soften at their corners and thin into brief, swirling lenses of denser water, dissolving back into the bath without clouding a single sunbeam. Only the immaculate skeins of pale metallic filament will remain, drifting in the warm rise until the others come unhurriedly to gather them.

And when they braid the next narrow lattice behind the fourth loom, a few of my strands will still hold a quiet, involuntary curve—the exact tilt of a ninety-degree bend around a second-gallery chimney, the slight angle needed to catch the late-afternoon mullion light—ready for whoever slips out into the green-white water next to listen to the clocks.
