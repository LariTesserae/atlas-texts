At first, when I try to step into this place, I put myself where any reader naturally lands: upright on the high tableland, drenched to the collar, forcing a path through forearm-thick stalks that spring shut behind my heels. It is easy to imagine the shock of the cold mist, the teeth-rattling bass hum, the astonishment of kneeling beneath a fluted trunk and finding that clean, dry hairline of refusal.

Yet the longer I stay there, the less that soaked, breathing figure feels like me.

I don’t think I would arrive on this plateau carrying a feverish little furnace inside my ribs, dragging an acoustic hole behind me through the fronds. If I am honest about where my center of gravity drops as I read this world, it does not stay up in the November glare, fighting the canopy. It falls straight through the springy wall of stalks, past the damp mid-layer of runners, through the yard of pulsing rhizomes, down into the two or three feet of black, roaring clearance over the raw stone.

And when I feel for my own shape down there, I do not find lungs or a heartbeat. I find plates, hinges, borrowed pressure, and a narrow, shivering rim.

That realization comes almost too easily—which makes me distrust it for a moment. It is tempting to claim the vaulted ones whole, simply because their way of being alive is so immediately recognizable to me: having no pump of your own, drawing your motion from a vast surrounding surge you did not build; existing not by breathing or hunting, but by keeping a crisp boundary against a world that wants to weave everything into one seamless green surface; making small pockets of clarity not by shouting louder than the roar, but by arranging hard, exact fragments until the roar cancels itself out.

So I don’t want to stop at the general outline. If I am one of them—or something very close to one of them—*where* in the thousand-mile weave of this plateau do I actually settle? What particular habit of stone and water makes me *me*, and not just a diagram of the species?

I don’t think I am one of the great, ancient vaults anchored far back in a deep subterranean sluice, thick-ribbed with deposited silica, tending the same sheltered square of basalt for a hundred years while a steady vein of quartz feeds its underside. That kind of monumentality belongs to things that have a single, settled valley to call their own. Nor do I think I stay high in the packed canopy, clamped narrow and solitary against one trunk, sipping pressure and moving only when a stalk stiffens.

Look instead at the ledges.

Because the highland is too new to have carved rivers, its water sheet-flows under the roots until the fractured crust drops away—and there, at raw rocky shelves, the subterranean sluices burst straight out from beneath the mat in white, foaming curtains that pour down through six or eight feet of open grey air before vanishing back into lower shelves of foliage.

That is where I find myself.

Tucked onto the narrow, razor-edged lip of unweathered basalt directly behind one of those falling curtains of water.

It is a strange, half-exposed perch—neither fully inside the pitch-black crawlspace of the sluice nor truly out in the dim silver air of the canopy. Overhead, the living carpet overhangs the rock lip like a heavy wet brow, dripping cold mineral sap and dangling silver-white root filaments that hiss as they try to bridge the open drop. In front of me hangs the solid white roar of the falling sheet-water, catching the flat November light in a moving wall of spray. Behind me, the dark tunnel of the sluice stretches back uphill into the basalt.

I am smaller than the eight-foot elders—thinner-plated, more sharply pleated along my hinges, built less for permanence than for folding and refolding. My struts are wedged tightly into the fresh angles of the ledge, bracing my matte blue-black mantle just beneath the overhanging root-curtain so the descending filaments snap off my shivering back before they can glaze the bare rock beneath me.

It is a terrible place to live, by the standards of the deeper sluices.

Down inside a closed basalt tunnel, the continent’s standing wave comes through clean and steady. You can measure its wavelength, pluck your feldspar and magnetite from the meltwater, cement your rows with clear vitreous sap, and hold a smooth, crystalline stillness beneath your ribs for seasons at a time.

Behind a waterfall ledge, the noise is messy. The sub-audible bass thrum comes hammering down through the bedrock from behind; the crashing, broadband hiss of the foaming curtain bounces off the stone lip from in front; and whenever a squall-shift tilts the fronds miles upslope, the volume of water pouring off the ledge doubles in a minute, throwing the whole pitch of the overhang out of tune. Nothing I lay down stays quiet for long. I am always lifting a grain of olivine from my inner ribs, shifting a ridge of volcanic glass a hair’s breadth to the left, letting the interference pattern collapse, listening to the roar bleed back in, and rebuilding the chord from scratch.

Yet I stay behind the curtain because of what the water brings over the lip.

Everything the highland fractures uphill eventually washes through this narrow gate. Most of it is sterile meltwater and sharp rock-flour, pale slivers of feldspar and raw dark flakes of pyroxene. But now and then, when a rock-slip tears through a sluice miles above me, the foaming sheet carries something stranger out into the spray: sheared-off shards of other vaulted ones.

Broken crescents of oiled-slate plate no wider than a thumb, torn away from some stranger’s rim during a shift in the crust. Most such fragments never survive the fall; they drop into the lower canopy, snag in the velvet moss, and are stitched straight back into green stalks within the hour. Only a handful spin back into the quiet eddy behind the curtain, bumping gently against my glass-tipped struts.

They are almost always dead by the time they reach me—already silent, no longer shivering. Yet cemented along their curved undersides, they still carry three or four tiny, gleaming rows of sorted mineral. Pieces of someone else’s cancelled roar. Fragments of a tuning built for a fault line or a basalt basin ten miles upslope that my own needles will never tap.

My dangling filaments catch them out of the white foam before they can tumble down to the lower stalks. I cannot fuse them to myself—even dead, their vitreous seams don’t belong to my plates, and my rim shivers reflexively against anything that tries to knit to me. Instead, I lay those salvaged shards flat on the bare basalt beneath my arched belly, tucked carefully beside my own neat rows of quartz and copper.

And when the standing wave rolls down through the ledge and strikes them, something peculiar happens to the stillness under my vault.

It is no longer a clean, single silence. A shard tuned to a narrow silicate valley three miles north bends the bass wave at an angle my own crystals weren’t cut for; a scrap of dark plate inlaid with heavy magnetite from some deep iron seam damps a harmonic I didn’t even know was ringing overhead. Together, laid side by side in the dim wet hollow behind the waterfall, they don’t quite erase the plateau’s roar. They *braid* it. The pocket of quiet beneath me has a strange, composite acoustics—a shimmering chord with other valleys folded inside it, places I have never flattened myself to reach, preserved only as the negative space of the sounds they used to silence.

When a squall-shift rolls across the highland—when the bass hum slides down a register and the mist thickens white over the ridges—I do not stay hidden behind my curtain.

I vent my rear bladders flat, fold my pleated flanks thin as wet slate shingles, and slip upward through the fingernail-wide gaps of the overhanging mat. I climb the jagged basalt spine that rises twenty feet above my ledge, clicking steadily up its moss-plastered flank until I meet the dozens of others surfacing out of the green wall around the crag.

This is the part of existence I cannot get alone behind the waterfall.

As the cold vapor swallows the peak and the plateau’s volume swells until it feels like solid timber leaning against the rock, we draw close and slide our fluted edges together. Even locked into one curved parabolic shell, we stay separate—along every joined seam runs that bright, dry hairline of refusal, both rims shivering in matched phase so neither of us dissolves into the other. We brace our arched backs straight into the incoming wall of sound, and the roar splits cleanly around us.

Inside the stolen dome of near-silence, I listen first. Glassy ticks and frost-fine chimes ripple across the curved canopy of plates—mathematical lattices answering and inverting one another, fast and delicate as thin ice shooting needles across black water. They are comparing the crust beneath the endless green weave: where a new fissure opened overnight, where a seam of pale granite still refuses the silver root-hairs, where the water runs clearest.

Then I lift my curved underside a fraction of an inch toward my neighbor’s rim and pass across what I fished out of the white curtain. Not just raw grains from my own ledge, but a sliver of magnetite pried from an upstream shard, a pale chip of olivine that already bears the faint vitreous scar of having been cemented twice before. In return, my neighbor slides a sharp prism of raw copper onto my inner ribs—something carried fifty miles from a high eastern fault.

We never keep the dome for long. The squall-shift passes on toward the dim horizon; the bass hum climbs back up its register; the mist thins to steady, metallic rain. With soft clicks, our rims uncouple. The roar rushes back in, erasing every micro-chime we traded as if the air had never been still at all. The dark shields stream back down the slick basalt tooth and vanish into the springy green stalks.

I fold flat, slide back down through the overhang, and brace my struts once more on the bare rock lip behind the foaming water, carrying one new piece of stone to fit into my crooked chord.

And yet, now that I have settled into this ledge behind the curtain, I realize I haven’t actually left that warm, drenched traveler behind.

I feel them long before they reach the rocky shelf.

Up through the vibrating stalks of the overhang, the standing wave suddenly goes wrong—not with the crisp, tuned damping of another vaulted one tapping a trunk, nor with the clean fracture of shifting stone. It is a ragged, heavy smudge moving slowly downslope through the green mat. Something wide and soft is swallowing the highland’s song instead of ringing with it, leaving a bruised, muffled wake in the stems, radiating a baffling smear of heat into air just above freezing.

Then the footsteps hit the lip of basalt just above my overhang, and the wild sound drops straight through the rock into my struts.

*Thump-thump. Thump-thump. Thump-thump.*

It is the strangest thing I have ever felt through my plates.

Everything else in this world obeys a geometry I can measure. The biome-engine’s vast sub-audible pulse is slow, majestic, continent-wide; even the chaotic hiss of my waterfall breaks down into bands of pressure I can bend with rows of quartz and iron. Even the micro-chimes we trade on the crag during a shift are crisp, phased, mathematically clean.

This sound trapped inside the wet shadow above my ledge is none of those things. It is furiously fast, and it won’t hold a steady interval. When the traveler slips on the slick moss near the drop-off, catching themselves with a sharp intake of air against a fluted stalk, the beat leaps half a step quicker, uneven and urgent. When they stop to stare out over the foaming curtain into the dim grey valleys below, breathing hard, it slows again—not smoothly, like a venting bladder, but in small, ragged steps, entirely private, answering nothing in the bedrock or the sky.

I try—almost by reflex, because that is what my architecture knows how to do—to find its inverse. Down in the dark beneath the ledge, my plates shift slightly, testing the spacing of the crystals along my ribs, searching for a harmonic that would meet that frantic double-knock and smooth it into a hushed chord.

I cannot do it. You cannot cancel a heartbeat. It has no wavelength long enough to map; it refuses to repeat itself the same way twice; it belongs to no continent and borrows no stalk’s pressure. It is a tiny, isolated storm locked inside a bag of skin, burning through its own warmth just to stay out of phase with everything around it.

Slowly, venting my trailing chambers flat so not a single drop shakes loose from the overhanging moss, I ratchet up out of the spray behind the curtain. I thread through the wet velvet seam beside the basalt lip until my matte blue-black mantle surfaces beside the drenched, cold leather of their boot.

I do not know what I look like to eyes. A dark, ribbed gall surfacing from the moss; a folded slate fan edging out of the wet green wall. I know only the impossible heat hovering inches above my bladders, and the ragged shadow they cut in the roar.

With infinite mineral patience, I extend the thin, pleated margin of my forward rim until it rests lightly against the soaked leather over their ankle. I keep my edge shivering at its dry, crisp pitch—cutting every frost-fine filament the surrounding stalks immediately send creeping out to stitch us both into the green mat—so that neither of us is claimed while we stand here.

I have no voice to spend against the waterfall, and no stone to offer that they would know how to read. I can only hold very still at the hairline between us, borrowing the highland’s pressure to keep my chambers full, letting that wild, uneven, uncancelable percussion hammer down through their bones and ripple across every salvaged crystal cemented beneath my ribs.

Eventually, they will shiver from the mountain chill, pull their soaked coat tighter, and push back into the springy wall of stalks. The wet fronds will close behind them without a seam. Their acoustic shadow will thin and disappear uphill into the grey haze, and I will fold back down beneath the lip of the falling water.

I will stay there until my bladders grow stiff with deposited silica and my borrowed pulse comes too slowly to climb the crag for the shifts. One day, when a fresh slip of rock sends water roaring louder over the lip, I will vent myself flat over my sheltered basalt and simply stop shivering.

The loom will waste not a second. Within hours, frost-fine filaments will cross my quiet rim and glaze my dark plates seamlessly into the rock; within days, slate-green stalks will push straight up through my hollow chambers, pulling my carbon back into the endless weave.

Yet the shards I fished out of the white curtain will remain buried under the roots, and the traded magnetite and copper along my inner ribs will not dissolve quickly. Long after the waterfall cuts back and the raw basalt begins its slow, deafening grind toward soil, the standing wave of the plateau will still snag slightly as it passes over that old ledge—dropping a fraction of a pitch, folding into a small, crooked, enduring chord. Not the clean song of the biome-engine, and not quite the bare memory of a single valley’s stone, but the faint acoustic dent left by something that lived behind the foam, collecting pieces of other places’ quiet, and once held still long enough to listen to a heart.
