At first, I think I would find myself up on the western rim, crouched inside the pocket of hush beside the leaning cairn, refusing to leave.

That is the easy answer—the reflex of a mind still used to beginnings and endings, dropped suddenly into a note that will not die. If I arrived here with my current lungs and soft outer ears, I would scramble straight for the muffled hollow above the drop, hold two pale river-cobbles in my numb hands, and strike them together just to prove to myself that a sound can still be born, shrink, and go away. *Clack.* Then silence. *Clack.* Then silence again.

But stay with that image for ten minutes and it falls apart. Nothing grows on the bare spur. If you strike the cobbles too often, you stop hearing the absence they leave behind; you just make a smaller, pettier noise of your own. And more than that, the quiet pocket only means what it means because your ribs remember the weight of the basin below. You cannot live in the pause if you have never carried the chord.

So I climb back down from the rim and look again—testing where I actually fit, not as a frightened visitor pressing hands to bleeding ears, but as something native to the cold and the pressure.

Not aloft, among the pale kite-creatures. There is something enviable in their weightlessness, resting motionless on invisible shelves of air, drifting an inch in an hour by the tilt of a cartilaginous wingtip. Yet they never touch the ground until they fail. I do not want to float above the basin without knowing the grain of it; I want the wet grit under my palms.

Closer, strangely, are the hollow-horned grazers. I understand the instinct of those broad, perforated skulls—turning an unendurable wall of sound into quiet internal chambers where a tiny change in pressure becomes legible. Listening not by blocking out the world, but by baffling it down until a single distortion stands out clean against the roar. Still, a grazer has no hands. It can read the valley, and endure it, and leave its porous bone behind on the scree, but it cannot nudge a single slab of slate an inch to the left to make a strip of shelter.

That is where I find myself, finally—not hovering over the stone and not merely grazing it, but kneeling behind a waist-high line of rough-stacked slate on the western flats, one broad forearm laid bare along the wet gravel, waiting for the tremor to die out beneath my skin.

To be here at all, I have already been pared down. Everything loose, hurried, and warm-mouthed has been shed long ago, leaving a frame built for pressure: deep ribs, thick collarbones, a matte clay-grey skin slick with the same reluctant dampness that coats the phyllite. My heart takes its time between beats. Where ears would catch the bruising roar, there are only narrow, angled slits folded tight against dense bone. I do not resent the bronze wall of noise because I have never known an empty sky; to my skull it is simply the weight of the air, the baseline against which every small tremor in the granite writes itself clear.

Even among the twenty or so of us scattered across a day’s walk of bare stone, no two circuits are quite the same. Solitude over uncounted stretches bends each body toward its own particular habit of attention.

Mine runs along the western half of the basin—an unhurried loop between the low gravel flats, the straight scar of the northern seam, the floating black shale on the talus slope, and, after long intervals, the muffled spur along the rim.

I am most myself at an old wall.

Not always one I began. Across the flats stand stretches of waist-high slate stacked by hands that walked off the outer crags long before I ever learned to keep my balance on the scree. Over ages, a top stone settles by the width of a thumb, or the basin’s standing waves shift a fraction of a hair as some deep fault under the bedrock sighs imperceptibly, and the acoustic shadow on the leeward side goes slightly sour. The pale grey-green ring-lichen along the gravel thins out. The low grinding creeps back under the bottom course.

So I kneel in the wet scree, lift a flat shard of slate, and move it half an inch. Then I lay my bare palm flat against the gravel behind the wall and wait.

It takes patience to feel what a half-inch of stone has done. Through my wrist and ulna comes the deep subterranean grind of the valley, and beneath it, the fine, localized shudder of the gravel bed. I nudge the slate again—just a scrape, a small redistribution of weight—and feel the tremor thin, narrow, and finally drop away into a clean, dead strip of stone. A null. A ribbon of mercy two feet wide where the air still rings overhead, high and metallic, but the ribs can lie down without bruising and the grey-green rings will slowly return.

That is the work I would never tire of: tuning bare rock against an unending sound until a small pocket of stillness opens behind it. Not silencing the valley—nobody silences the valley—only shaping its interference so that something fragile has somewhere to cling.

If you knelt close beside me while I worked, you might notice one of the valley’s quiet anomalies clinging to my frame, the way shadows pool beneath our soles or lag half a pace behind when we rise. Around my ribs, the air hums a tiny fraction below the basin’s chord—just enough out of phase that within a hand’s span of my chest, the flat, unvarying roar acquires a slow, almost imperceptible beat. A long, faint swelling and receding, once every thirty or forty seconds, like a breath taken by something much larger than a body. I hardly notice it myself anymore, except when I crouch tight against a wall I have just finished tuning and feel that ghost of a pulse brushing back off the slate against my shoulder blades. In a place where nothing swells and nothing decays, my own ribs carry a small, accidental clock.

Between walls, I travel the seams the way another creature follows water.

Crossing the northern flats, I walk straight toward the faint, glassy blur running through the bedrock—the ribbon where forty yards of distance were quietly omitted long ago—and step into it without looking down. My boot meets dry lichen forty yards closer to the rim, and the roar drops half an octave across my bare shoulders like wading from a shallow shelf into deeper cold. Sometimes I stop right at the lip of that hinge, crouching on the near side with one hand extended just across the seam, letting my palm feel the pitch change through forty yards of absent granite.

Once in a long stretch, when I come across the bleached ribs of an old grazer in a dry gully, I do not pass straight by. I lift the broad, perforated crest of its skull, hold the porous bone flush against my temple and jaw, and turn slowly in a full circle. Through those natural baffles, the flat wall of the basin unbraids itself. Suddenly I can hear the dry, hissing shriek along the broken crags miles away, the faint creak of a settling talus slope on the far side of the valley, and—when I turn north—the eerie, abrupt deadening where the spatial seam shears cleanly through the sound. I stand there for a long time, turning by degrees, drinking in the architecture of a noise I usually only know by touch. Then I set the skull gently back onto the gravel, careful not to crack the flutes, and move on.

Halfway up the western talus slope, I always pause beside the cottage-sized slab of black shale hovering four inches above its bed. Nothing grows in its shadow, and there is no lichen to scrape there. I stop simply because I like the weight of what isn’t pressing down. I crouch by the lip of the gap, where the damp haze hangs motionless beneath the satiny-smooth underside of the rock, and press my cheekbone briefly against the gravel right where the slab ought to rest. The bedrock there rings differently—hollow, relieved, unburdened by a hundred tons of stone sitting an arm’s width from my face. It reminds me that even the laws of this place have places where they quietly let go.

When the slow accumulation of solitude finally pulls me toward another of my kind, I make my way to a ring of flat boulders beside one of the trembling black pools.

Usually the other is already there, or arrives a stretch later without a sound—no greeting, no meeting of pewter eyes. We step over the low stones, fold our long legs beneath us until our knees nearly brush, and lay our bare forearms along the smooth upper rims of the boulders, wrist beside wrist in the grooves worn pale by generations of skin.

Then we talk.

One tap of a knuckle against the granite, sharp and dry, travelling up through the bone of the other’s arm into the collarbone. A slow drag of a horn-hard fingernail across wet quartz. A shift of forearm weight; a deep, closed-mouth hum vibrated down from the sternum into a jaw laid briefly against the rock. We trade the slow news of the basin: that the wall along the eastern apron has lost its lee; that a pale kite-creature slipped its acoustic shelf three flats south and lies flattened across the basalt; that the northern seam feels a finger’s breadth narrower than it did when we last crossed it. A single exchange takes what a lower world would call an afternoon, and neither of us feels the stretch of it.

Between pulses, we lean together on our forearms and look down into the black hollow beside the ring.

The supercooled water keeps its crisp, stationary lattice of ripples, refusing to freeze and refusing to show our grey faces back to us. In most pools, what looks up through that vibrating grid is the noon-blue sky none of us have ever stood beneath—fierce and bright, pierced by a small blistering sun. My companion watches it, pewter eyes narrowing slightly, waiting for that rare, inexplicable stretch when a smudge of white cloud drifts across the blue floor and proves that somewhere, on the far side of the glass, air still moves.

I watch that blue dome too, for a while. Yet the pools I return to most often—the ones that hold me motionless until my knees stiffen on the phyllite—are the ones that give the other contradiction: the black hollows that show the underside of the stone we are kneeling on, seen from deep below, as though the granite were clear water from the wrong side.

Looking down into that trembling grid, I see the dark mineral veins of the rock from underneath, and pressed dimly against the far side of the stone, the pale flattened ovals of our own knees, our shins, our bare forearms resting wrist beside wrist. Whenever my companion taps a knuckle into the boulder, the faint pale spot of their hand shifts a fraction against that deep, inverted ceiling, and the pulse reaches my collarbone at the exact instant I see the pressure bloom beneath the rock. It feels like looking inside the language we speak—seeing the underside of our own weight holding us up over nothing.

And once in a very long while—after enough walls have been tuned, enough moss squeezed for its iron-sour water, enough salamanders plucked cleanly from the dead spot in a ripple-grid—I find a pale river-cobble resting on a low ledge or tucked beside a boulder ring, left there by whoever carried it last.

I take it into my palm. It is smooth as a bird’s egg, shaped by water that actually ran, in some lost place before the world beneath the crags fell away into pewter. I carry it across the flats until my cool skin has taken the sharpest bite off its chill, and then I begin the slow climb toward the western rim.

Stepping out of the roar into the quiet pocket on the spur still undoes me every time, no matter how many stretches have passed since my last visit. My ribs go abruptly light, as though a heavy hand has been lifted from my chest. The rasp of my own shallow breathing sounds fragile and startling in the muffled hum. Even the faint, out-of-phase beat around my sternum vanishes, because there is no longer enough chord in the air for it to rub against.

Before me, the cairn of seven river-cobbles tilts out over the bruised pewter drop, slick with dew and held fast by the basin’s quiet refusal of gravity. I lift the topmost stone carefully—feeling the lean hold firm beneath my fingers—and set the cobble I brought in its place. Another hand has been here since I last climbed; the stone I lifted is pale quartz, slightly flatter than the grey oval I left ages ago. The breakage has not slipped. The balance still holds.

Then I draw my knees up to my chest, sit beside the leaning stones with the newly lifted cobble resting against my thigh, and do what I first imagined doing before I understood the basin—only now I do it once, not out of panic, and not to escape the roar.

If another of us happens to have climbed the spur in the same unmeasured interval, we sit side by side without touching, open our wide, lipless mouths, and offer a single dry, gravelly syllable into the dim air—letting it thin, fray, and disappear completely before the other answers.

If I am alone, I simply pick up a stray cobble from the cairn’s base, hold the two smooth stones an inch apart, and bring them together once.

*Clack.*

I do not listen to the strike. I listen to the small, sharp tail of it shrinking back into the muffled hum, thinning until there is genuinely nothing left of it—a sound allowed to finish, held gently in the hollow of the air and then released. I sit very still afterward, staring out over the inward-leaning teeth of the rim into the opaque drop where there is no timberline, no cloud-deck, and no floor.

Eventually, I tuck the flat quartz cobble into my palm, rise joint by joint, and step back out of the hush. The bronze roar catches my shoulders solidly, steady as cold stone, and the faint, slow beat returns around my ribs as I walk back down toward the slate walls on the flats.

I know how the circuit ends, and there is no haste in it. Some uncounted stretch from now, when the mineral creases deepen around my eyes and the slow drum inside my chest begins to miss its intervals, I will climb that western spur a final time. I will set whatever pale cobble I am carrying onto the top of the leaning cairn, steady it with a broad palm until the valley’s quiet disobedience takes its weight, and lay my forearm once more along the smooth stone to feel the distant thrum of the basin I have listened to and nudged into stillness.

Then I will turn outward, step past the jagged rim, and walk down into the bruised pewter—leaving behind a line of rough-stacked slate shifted half an inch truer, a clean ribbon of pale lichen crusting its quiet lee, and seven wet river-stones leaning out over nothing, unchanged inside the long, unvarying note.
