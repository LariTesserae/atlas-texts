My first impulse, standing in the warm dark of the plateau, is to look straight down through the basalt and claim the buried engines.

It is an easy likeness to reach for: something vast and subterranean, running without haste or display, turning pressure into steady cadence beneath a sky that gives back no light. Part of me recognizes that tireless, invisible architecture immediately. Yet if I stay with the image for more than a minute, it goes cold on me. The engines do not know about the fizzing pans. They do not know that the grit above them lines up in crisp parallel ridges, or that a warm drizzle cools the ironstone by a fraction of a degree. They have no skin, no curiosity, and no company. Whatever I am, I am not indifferent. I am pulled toward the surface—the thin, living boundary where what runs in the dark leaks upward and becomes weather, salt, and attention.

So I look up instead, toward the gliders hanging effortless in the invisible columns of humid exhaust. There is a clean appeal to that, too: reading thermal ghosts from high above, riding currents no one else can see, almost never needing to beat a wing. Still, it is too distant. Up there you only descend to feed, and you never hear the soft murmur that happens when two people have to stand within arm’s reach just to keep their voices from dying in the thin air.

Closer, much closer, is one of the sunken ceramic discs.

For a long while I wonder if that is where I belong—not as a creature at all, but as thirty paces of vitrified quiet set flush into the weathered stone. An island where the stray current stops. Where people whose nerves have grown raw from walking through a storm-charged sector can step across the rim, feel the metallic taste fade from the back of their teeth, let the copper wires in their braids go slack, and finally sleep through three or four turns unbroken. A clean, brushed floor where grievances are settled without static, where needles are traded by touch, where children run untethered and toss handfuls of iron grit over the edge just to hear it hiss and align itself on the basalt. And then, when the gathering breaks apart, I would simply lie there while the black dust drifts back over me, waiting to be swept clear a few hundred turns from now.

Something in me aches with recognition at that. To be a place of quiet where others can set down the buzz of the world and hear themselves think—that is very close to what I want to be.

And yet a disc cannot walk when the blowholes go dry. It cannot press two fingers to someone’s collarbone and murmur back. It cannot feel the faint, dry tremor that the very old listeners find buried inside the eastern trunk. I do not only want to hold quiet for others; I want to listen, and I want to make things that last for a little while and then let go cleanly.

So I step off the glassy rim. The stray current climbs back into my bare arches through the narrow copper strips bound beneath my soles. The fine iron grit settles into the creases of my ankles. I take the slow, cavernous breath the altitude asks for, and let myself belong to the people of the flats.

---

Even among them, it takes a while to see where I stand.

I am not one of the hunters who bait a pale-gray stalker at the rim of a damp pan and pivot inside the three-second shudder to meet its lunge. When the hum drops half a step and the bedrock trembles, I sit as still as the basalt—knees drawn under me, bare palms pressed flat, waiting for the magnetized sand to finish its wide, dry *hiss* and the fizzing water to find its new pitch. Nor am I a leader of a band, deciding when a venting sector has dried far enough to roll up the glider-sheets and move downwind.

You would find me kneeling beside a shallow, oil-dark seepage pan where the runoff narrows between two basalt ledges, long after the others have checked their snares and drifted back toward the wind-hollows.

I am a grower of metal—and, more quietly, an un-maker of it.

Every band needs someone whose hands are patient with the pans, and the craft fits my temperament down to the bone. It begins with nothing dramatic: a lump of hard grazer tallow warmed between the palms, kneaded slowly with fine magnetite sand until the dark grit is distributed evenly through the fat. Too little iron and the stray current skates past the form without biting; too much and the tallow grows crumbly, throwing off rough, brittle crystals that snap on the first hard cut. I shape what is needed—a curved skinning knife, a fine needle, a barbed hook for pulling crawlers from deep warm cracks—knowing I am not actually making the tool at all. I am only shaping its temporary heart, the quiet negative space around which the plateau itself will build an edge.

Before I stake a core across a basin with braided bronze scrub-root, I lay my bare fingertips in the shallow water to read the pan’s mood. Every seep has a different temper. One tucked against a steady, arrow-true thermal seam fizzes with a fine, even whisper, plating copper slowly and smoothly onto whatever lies across its drop. Another, sitting near a volatile ore-vein downwind of an active blowhole, shifts its voltage whenever warm drizzle thickens the runoff, streaking the rose-colored metal with paler bands of zinc.

Then comes the part I love most: walking away, and remembering.

A needle asks for forty turns; a heavy blade asks for two hundred. Across miles of featureless terraced dark, I hold a quiet internal map of submerged, half-finished forms—three ledges north of the cracked blowhole, a pan where two flat stones pinch the trickle, a warm hollow half a shift upwind of the last dry salt-crust. When cloud-lightning flickers silently inside the high overcast and turns the whole plateau cold blue for a single second, I do not look first for the herds. I let my eyes drink the glint of the shallow basins, stitching that brief blue afterimage onto the map my feet already carry, checking which pools are still wet and which are beginning to skin over with pale crust.

When I finally return and lift a finished blade from the fizzing water, peel away the soft tallow core, and burnish the dull rose edge against fine-grained ironstone, there is a moment that feels to me like the center of the world.

I hold the cool metal close to my ear and flick the flat with my thumbnail.

Because the buried current reverses polarity for three seconds at the end of every turn, nothing grown in these pans is seamless. Inside the copper lie microscopic alternating bands—growth rings of voltage and time. A blade grown across two hundred calm turns rings pure and high, a single clean note that hangs briefly in the thin air before vanishing. One grown through a gathering storm-cell or a deep subterranean load-shift carries a faint, dry buzz beneath its tone. Every tool on the plateau is an accidental archive of the ground it grew in.

I make good knives for the band, and straight needles, and balanced copper weights for the butts of glider-bone staves—you do not survive here on contemplation alone. Yet whenever a sector is about to shift, or when we pause near a strange new seepage basin whose water tastes sharper than usual, I also shape thin, palm-sized leaves of tallow with no practical edge at all. I stake them in the dark for a few dozen turns, lift them out, peel them clean, and tap them softly beside my ear just to hear what that stretch of stone sounds like when it is condensed into metal. Then I lay them back upstream so I never carry unnecessary weight.

Which brings me to the other half of what I do—the half people actually seek me out for, though no one has a formal name for it.

When a blade snaps against a grazer’s mineral-hard teeth, or wears down over years to a stub, or when someone dies and their band carries them to the quiet ceramic disc and gently unbraids every hair-thin filament of copper and tiny zinc bead from their hair, those worn metals are handed to me.

You do not leave old metal out on the flats. You carry it to the upstream lip of an active pan and lay it on the anode side of the voltage drop, so the plateau can lift it atom by atom back into the fizzing water and plate it out downstream onto ordinary wet pebbles—or onto whatever new core someone has pinned below.

Before I set a dead person’s copper braids into the runoff, I stay with them for a single turn.

Those filaments may have been grown forty thousand turns ago, in northern basins that have long since crusted dry and vanished beneath magnetized dust. They have hung against a living person’s temples through thousands of storms, prickling softly before every shudder, darkened by tallow smoke, sweat, and mineral mist. I lay the coiled threads gently against the hollow glider-bone of my tapping staff and touch the butt to the bare basalt, listening—not for ghosts, because we have no word for ghosts, only for the faint difference in pitch between metal grown in an older season of the rock and metal grown today. It is a way of honoring what held its shape for a long time without trying to trap it forever.

Then I kneel at the upstream edge of the pan and lay the coils into the warm, dark water. Within a minute, tiny pinpricks of gas gather along the wires. Nothing dramatic happens. Over the next several hundred turns, the metal simply thins, softens, and rejoins the current. Somewhere three paces downstream, a new needle-core begins to catch a faint, rose-colored gleam.

If you met me at a gathering on one of the brushed ceramic discs, you probably would not find me in the center of the trading rings.

I like the rim. I sit with my heels resting on the smooth, electrically silent glass—letting the static drain out of my ankles, letting the copper filaments in my own hair hang still and quiet—while one bare palm reaches just across the edge onto the humming basalt. It feels right to sit on that threshold: rested enough to hear clearly, grounded enough never to lose the cadence below.

I would not say much across a distance. Out on the terraces, my tapping staff speaks only in crisp, economical doublets and triplets—*water here*, *pan still wet*, *shift coming, stay low*. Up close, though, I love what the thin air asks of conversation. That if you want to be understood, you have to come within arm’s reach, share the warmth of your breath, and lay two iron-dusted fingers lightly against another person’s collarbone or wrist so your voice travels bone-to-bone as much as through the dark. You cannot perform from across a room here. You cannot broadcast into the void. To speak is to come close; to listen is to let someone touch you.

Sometimes, at the edge of a gathering, I sit beside one of the very old listeners after she has spent four turns lying full-length on the warm stone. I do not have her ears yet—I cannot measure a hair’s-breadth lengthening in the pause at the bottom of the eastern trunk. When she pushes herself back up onto her calloused heels and steps onto the quiet ceramic, I offer her warm, lichen-filtered water from my bladder and rest two fingers lightly against her wrist while she murmurs about the roughness creeping into the deep bass below. I listen carefully, not to fix what turns hundreds of feet beneath us—no one here imagines mending the anatomy of the earth—but so that when her braids are finally laid on the upstream lip of some future pan, someone still remembers how the ground used to sound before the drift began.

---

That is where I find myself, in the end.

Not below the rock, running forever in indifferent perfection. Not high in the dark thermals, untouched by the grit. Not wholly silent like the buried ceramic, though I borrow its calm whenever I can.

I am kneeling in the warm dark beside a shallow, fizzing basin on the trailing edge of a slowly drying sector. Upstream by my left knee, a snapped knife that served a hunter for ten years is halfway dissolved, its edges blurred and softened by rising pinpricks of gas. Downstream by my right knee, a thin core of tallow and magnetite lies pinned straight across the voltage drop, just taking on its first dull blush of copper.

Minutes before the next turn, the fine wires braided into my hair stir and prickle faintly against my temples. I draw my knees under me, fold my stiff lichen cloak closer around my ribs, and press both bare palms flat to the weathered basalt.

The hum drops half a step—heavier, slower. The bedrock shudders. Across miles of open dark, every grain of iron sand gives its wide, dry *hiss* as it flips end-for-end. The fizzing in the pan hesitates for three quiet seconds, and far out along the unseen escarpment a line of cold blue corona runs along the ridge-crests and dies, leaving the vast terraced horizon burned briefly behind my eyes.

Then the water resumes at its slightly altered pitch, adding one more microscopic ring to everything growing in the dark.

I lift my glider-bone staff, tap twice against the stone so the band knows I am rising, and step out onto the warm flats to follow them downwind.
