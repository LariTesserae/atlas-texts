If I dropped into this basin without choosing first, I would know where I belonged by what my body leaned toward in the dark.

Not the outer terraces. I can feel the pull of them—that wide, cold openness where nothing hangs overhead and a swimmer can bolt straight up into the empty black—and I understand the sharp, thin-skinned alertness of living out there, darting between ridges of sorted clam-husks, feeling the tiny electric shudder of startled silt under a bolting tail. There is an honesty to living at the edge of the cold. Yet I would not stay there long. Open water has no memory; whatever passes through it scatters and is gone.

Nor would I make a good Mason, much as I admire them. There is a clean, enviable mercy in caring only for gap, draft, and mortar—in chewing grit to paste and smoothing a seam with your chin until the leaking warmth turns solid again. But I know myself too well: halfway through mending a vent-collar, I would stop patting the wet lime just to listen to what was humming behind it, and the draft would cool while I lingered.

What pulls me inward, past the low bulbous warrens and the sagging canopies of the tube-weavers, is not just the heat. It is the density of traces. Every wall in the core is a record of who pressed against it; every rising column of water carries news from the bedrock to the ceiling. To live in a place without sight or speech, and yet to be surrounded by an architecture that remembers everything—that is where I would settle.

So I find myself becoming heavy.

My limbs thicken and shorten, trading speed for grip, ending in broad, blunt pads that will never scratch marl. The nervousness drains out of my ribs. Across my brow, bone fuses into a smooth, dense plate, and behind my cheeks the pleated gill-curtains lengthen until I can fold them tight against my flanks in a narrow alley or spread them wide in a plaza to taste a hundredth of a degree of drift. I become one of the tenants—not a visitor brushing a curious palm along the mucus-slick doorways, but one of the bodies the doorways were beveled to fit.

Still, even among them, there is a question of height and season.

I do not think I would live at the bottom of the hottest core, where the basalt sweats fiercest and the lowest arcades stay crowded through every ebb, humming loud with eighty neighbors packed into the same flue. I would be too close to the furnace there, too muffled by the constant churn of traffic.

Instead, I find my chamber high—two-thirds of the way up an older chimney on the rimward shoulder of the core, where the central towers begin to step down toward the middle warrens and the braided worm-tube bridges angle across toward one of the broadest domed vaults.

It is an edge-perch, even inside the city. During the long cool ebb, when the floor-seepage slackens and denser water from the basin rim spills silently over the parapets like invisible fog, my tier feels the chill before the lower galleries do. Curled tail-to-brow inside my hollow, I tuck my shoulder tighter against the inner flue-lip, narrowing my vent so the stack holds its pressure and pushes blood-warmth up to the eaves above me. Through the bone of my forehead, pressed flush to the damp lime, I feel the whole tower answering: forty chambers below me shifting half an inch in quiet synchrony, regulating the breath of the stone without a single word or command.

And it is from here, in this high alcove, that I realize *when* I am.

I am not young. The frantic years on the outer terraces—grazing pale fungus-stalks, freezing against windbreak walls whenever a hunter’s bow-wave slid past—are decades behind me, thinned down to a faint, ancestral restlessness that only stirs when a cold cross-draft slips through the upper arches. Neither am I sealed in stone yet. I still have the avenues. I still have the bridges.

Yet over the last ten or twelve surges, something quiet has begun to happen at my threshold.

When the deep pressure swells against my ribs and the floor-seepage thickens—when the hinged shell-doors across the plazas click open in that soft, leathery rain—I uncurl from my wall and push out into the rising warmth. For thirty years I slid through this doorway without noticing it, the beveled lime giving way around my flanks like water around water. Now, as my shoulders clear the smooth rounded lip, the stone touches both sides of my hips at once.

It does not hurt. It does not even scrape. It is only a firm, slick pressure—a polite measurement taken by a wall I have shaped for half a lifetime, telling me that I am still growing and the stone is not widening with me. If I fold my gill-curtains flat and angle my ribs slightly, I slip right through into the gallery. I have dozens of surges left, perhaps hundreds, before the lip catches for good.

Knowing that changes how I use the surge.

Many of the tenants on my tier drop downward when the warmth rises, gliding into the circular plazas to graze the freshened bacterial mats, letting Sweepers climb onto their broad rumps to rasp away old mineral crust, exchanging slow brow-to-flank pulses at the intersections. Sometimes I go down there too. I like the blind courtesy of a crowded bowl—tilting a fraction lower so a massive elder can keep the warmest core of the plume, lifting my belly two inches off the soapstone floor so a file of Sweepers can pass beneath without breaking stride, pressing a steady double-thrum into the flank of a newly arrived migrant from the middle warrens whose forehead bone is still fresh and startled by how loudly the core towers sing.

More often, though, while my doorway still lets me go, I climb.

I hook my blunt pads into the worn holds along the chimney face and haul my weight upward through the thickening drift, past the balconies where pale filter-crowns have bloomed into the rising mineral snow. Beneath an overhanging bridge, I pause beside a dangling cluster of leathery egg-capsules—not mine, though perhaps kin by two streets and a dozen surges—and sweep my gill-fringe softly across their slick skins to clear the settling silt and leave a thin trace of bitter flank-oil that keeps scavengers off. A pale hunter hangs motionless just outside the flue-draft a body-length away, waiting for a careless ribbon-eel; my bow-wave rolls over its snout and it yields two yards into the dark without a ripple. Nothing in the valley hunts anything my size anymore.

Up past the tangled worm-tube bridges, the towers run out of height and arch together into the oldest ceiling in the quarter. And there, pressed flat against the domed lime forty feet above the avenues, trembles the mirror.

It is the strangest thing in the world I inhabit—a wide, shivering skin of non-water, barely two inches deep, sharp with trapped volcanic gas that would burn my gills if I ever broke through it. Nothing breathes it. Nothing owns it. Yet it is the only place in the vertical dark where what rises finally stops rising.

I drift beneath it, tilt upright until my heavy ballast-tail hangs straight down in the warm draft, and hold myself motionless a finger’s breadth below the shivering meniscus. Slowly, I unfurl my pleated gill-curtains wide just under the boundary and let my mouth-lobes brush the waxy film pooled against the ceiling.

This is where I would spend my best hours.

Down in the streets, everything is immediate—a passing neighbor, a crumbling seam, a sudden muffled thump along a parapet. Up here, under the dry sky that cannot be entered, time thickens into oil and stays. Swirling slowly inside a faint ring of mineral crust is the whole quarter, rendered in grease and trace: the rich, crowded musk of the central towers where the brood-eaves are full; the bright, urgent readiness of a pair spiraling three plazas south; a thin, sour thread of rot just starting in a damp lower arcade that no one’s skull has picked up through the lime yet.

And along the outer edge of the mirror, spread wide and calm, the unmistakable cedar-bitter ring of an elder from the neighboring block whose pulse stopped four surges ago. I never saw that neighbor—none of us has ever seen anyone—but for twenty years its low, impossibly steady chest-thrum traveled down its pier, crossed the basalt floor, and came faintly up into my own chimney wall whenever a deep tectonic tremor rolled beneath the valley. Now its doorway has been mortared flush by the Masons with chewed shell and clam-valve keystones, its heavy ribs bracing the spire from within, and this quiet bitter film is the last thing it sent upward into the dark.

I hover there longer than most visitors do, letting the layered drift wash over my fringes until I feel the whole valley’s shape in my throat—not as a map, for I have no eyes to see a map, but as a balance of temperatures, crowds, ailments, and absences. Before I turn back down, I press my lower flank gently against the beveled stone rim of the dome. Two pale beads of my own oil lift away like inverted raindrops and flatten silently into the trembling skin above me: *still moving, still warm, thickening, high on the rimward shoulder of the core*.

Then the pressure begins to soften. The upward draft loses its silkiness. Already, out past the outer terraces, cold water is tilting over the basin lip.

I glide back down through the emptying bridges, past the folding filter-crowns and the quiet clicks of closing opercula, and return to my high alcove. Inside, I turn once in the tight, body-cast dark—feeling that smooth, familiar lime kiss both hips as I draw my tail home—and settle my broad brow flush against the inner wall of the chimney.

I used to think the part of me that climbs to the gas-mirror and the part of me that will one day grow too wide to leave this chamber were at odds with each other. Out in the water, you read what drifts loose; inside the wall, you stay put and hold the heat.

Pressed to the warm chalk now, feeling the slow hum of the lower elders rise through my skull and steady my breathing, I realize they are only two halves of the same listening.

For as long as the threshold yields, I will be a reader of the high ceilings—one of the heavy, unhurried ones who climbs above the traffic to taste what the city has shed into the dark, and carries that quiet awareness back down into the avenues. And when the surge finally comes that my shoulders meet the beveled lip and cannot pass, I will not fight the stone. I will settle deeper against the inner flue, spread my curtains across the rising snow, take the clumps of sulfur-mat my neighbors press through the opening, and let the pale filter-crowns take root along my quiet, calcifying back.

There is no smallness in becoming part of the wall. Long after I can no longer climb to the shivering dome to read the valley’s census, my pulse will be down inside the limestone, answering the basalt’s deep breathing, steadying the towers for the thin-skinned ones still darting out along the cold rim—waiting for their brows to harden, waiting to hear the first low murmur of home come humming through the rock.
