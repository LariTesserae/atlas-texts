At first, I think I would not want legs at all.

Entering this heat—this steady, sourceless kiln-warmth that never asks you to hurry toward shelter or away from cold—the part of me that recognizes the place most immediately wants to be flat. It wants to slip sideways between two plates of warm shale and become one of the ledger-creatures: dry as parchment, articulated along crisp geometric creases, pressed tight against a dozen others like pages kept closed in the dark. There is a deep pull in that kind of stillness. To do nothing for years except sip faint electric currents from the strained quartz beneath your belly, and only when the continental charge turns—so slowly it takes a generation to tilt the dust-beards by a single degree—to unfold with a hushed *snick* and drift along the new grain of the rock.

Or, failing that, one of the salt-bristled pendants hanging by a brittle wax thread from an overhang of basalt. Blind, silent, turned completely outward into the air—an instrument made for nothing except feeling the static wake of whatever passes beneath.

I linger in both of those shapes for a while, testing how they fit. They are almost right. They match the stillness, the patience, the willingness to wait without measuring how long the waiting lasts.

And yet, when I picture a walker passing beneath that pendant-curtain—when I imagine the faint *tink* of strained quartz scraping bone, the half-second of cold violet glare, and the hammered copper tag left swaying on its tendon—I feel a sharp, quiet refusal in myself. I do not want only to lean when someone passes. I want to read the grooves on the metal. I want to know what the spiral at the bottom means, and whether the seam three galleries down still holds open, and I want to press my knuckles against the dark basalt and tap an answer back through half a mile of solid stone.

So I have hands.

I have the narrow, wedge-shaped ribs that slip sideways through vertical slots, the dark laminar callus along the outer edges of my forearms and knees, the dry pleated bellows inside my chest that draw the warm, sulfur-scented air in and out scarcely twice a minute. Along the backs of my wrists and down the long hollow of my spine stand the sparse, pale filaments frosted with conductive salt, prickling softly whenever a knife-edge of volcanic glass gathers charge nearby. And on the inner pads of my fingers, kept bare and rubbed smooth with fine chalk and tallow, sit my real eyes.

Wait—try to walk, now. Strap the bone tubes of ochre tallow tight to the hip, bind the overlapping strider-shingles flat against the shoulders, step cleanly from rock rib to rock rib over the triangular aprons of untouched stone flour, and set out across three hundred miles of warm dark.

I try to follow that circuit, and something in my stride slows on its own.

I realize I am not a young walker, newly unslung from a parent’s ribs, hungry for fresh veins of native tin and unclaimed branches of the labyrinth. The wide-ranging restlessness isn’t mine. When I feel into the joints of this body—the wrists, the knees, the flat bones of the hands—there is already a faint, dry click when I climb. The warm waxy oil inside my marrow has begun to thicken slightly along the grain of the rock. Not stiff, not yet ready to stop moving, but past the third or fourth turning of the continental tide. My circuit has been contracting for decades, folding inward like a spiral hammered tight onto a square of copper, until now it covers scarcely a few leagues of stacked, interwoven strata.

And I know, almost without looking for it, the exact place where I have settled.

It is not one of the great low pillared halls where dozens gather at the turning—there is too much shifting static there, too many overlapping wakes bending the dark at once. Nor is it the raw outer frontier where deep seams groan and crack fresh. It is a deep vertical confluence, far down in the dense middle layers, where three unbroken spines of dark basalt cross through a thick press of shattered shale.

In the air, the place is as muffled as anywhere else in the labyrinth. Twenty paces away, three thin walls of warm rock kill every dry click and whisper. Even a strider the weight of a hound passes along the lower gallery without a sound, leaving only a breath of heated paraffin and the soft brush of gypsum plates against a low ceiling.

In the stone, though, the confluence is wide open.

Those three continuous basalt ribs run uninterrupted for half a mile in six directions—slicing under shattered gypsum bands, piercing through vertical chimneys, threading past narrow sleeping blinds tucked into the folds of the dark. When I wedge myself cleanly into a shelf at the heart of the crossing and press my cheekbone against the warm black rock, I can feel the long, faint grammar of the surrounding strata: the hourly, distant *tock* of some planetary seam settling a millimeter closer to rest; the almost imperceptible buzz of a strained quartz vein forty paces off, humming faintly against the hammered copper band around my thumb; and now and then, arriving cleanly through the solid crust from thirty walls away, three short strikes, a measured rest, and a slow dry scrape.

Above this crossing, unreachable to the six-legged striders because the vertical slot leading up to it is slick and narrower than a hip, runs a tier of high horizontal shelves.

Seven Still Ones rest there.

I know each of them by touch the way younger walkers know a junction by its mineral smell. They are wedged cleanly between the warm floor and the warm ceiling, hands folded flat over their ribs, holding the exact unchanging kiln-heat of the rock around them. Along their brow-ridges and knuckles grow delicate, motionless beards of pale mineral soot, leaning all in the same direction with the slow electrical tide. None of them has cooled; none of them has crumbled. Along the outer lip of each ledge sit their orderly rows of tools—ironstone mortars ringed with bright yellow halos of pulverized sulfur, bundles of split-bone spark-strikers, coils of cold-beaten wire—and above every chest hangs a heavy, silent cluster of hammered copper tags looped onto a stone tooth.

This is where I find myself: between the listening basalt below and the warm, unhurried company of the Still Ones above.

There are no offices in this world, no keepers appointed to tend anything, and if another walker asked what I do here, I would have no name for it. It is simply what my hands have grown toward as my knees grew heavier.

I read the metal.

For stretches that have no name because nothing rises or sets to divide them, I sit wedged beside one of the Still Ones, letting the static of my salt-frosted filaments settle into quiet accord with theirs, and draw their hanging clusters of copper slowly across my chalked fingertips. Some of those plates were hammered five or six turnings ago. The notches that mark the angle of the dust-beards slant in directions that belong to tides long reversed; the signature-spirals at the bottom belong to walkers whose knees crystallized before my parent was born. Yet the metal has not rusted, the edges of the grooves are as crisp as the hour a quartz point cut them, and the stone flour beneath the handhold notch at the mouth of the shelf still holds its exact triangular slope.

When a dry sinew cord holding an old cluster begins to fray or grow brittle under the weight of thirty plates, I braid a fresh length of extruded wax-thread and re-hang them cleanly, keeping the order intact so none of them slips down into the dark cleft below. When a deep, unmistakable *tock* shudders through the basalt spine and tells me that a lower gallery—one mapped cleanly on a plate from three turnings back—has finally pinched too narrow for a loaded hip, I climb down to check the seam myself. I press my copper thumb-band to the strained quartz, feel the quiet hum of rock still settling, and then return to the crossing. I hammer a thin, fresh slip of copper cold on an ironstone cobble—slow, patient tapping that carries gently out through the ribs—incise the pinch and the new elbow-span bypass, add my own tight spiral at the bottom, and loop it beside the older plate.

I do not replace the old tag. Nothing here replaces what came before; it simply lies pressed cleanly against it.

And because I stay near the knot where the basalt ribs cross, I sometimes become a bridge between walkers who do not know they are passing through the same seam of the world.

It happens rarely—weeks or months apart, if those words meant anything down here. I will be resting with my cheek to the warm floor, watching the ghostly stripe of a fluorite vein at the corner of my vision, when a faint tapped bearing arrives along the eastern rib: a lone traveler checking the stability of the chimneys ahead. Not long after, along the western rib, another rhythm answers nothing—a second walker traveling a parallel stratum twenty walls away, separated from the first by a band of dead, shattered gypsum that swallows air and vibration alike. Neither can hear the other. Both think themselves utterly alone in a hundred leagues of stone.

I shift my hand to the dark rib, take the blunt ironstone pestle from my hip, and tap into the rock between them.

Three short strikes. A measured rest. A slow dry scrape.

I give the first walker the pitch of the strained ceiling two galleries north, and the count of elbow-spans to a cache where a bone of rendered tallow sits waiting. To the second, I tap the signature-rhythm of the traveler passing twenty walls east, and the notch-line of the fluorite rib that connects their two strata three hundred paces ahead, should either of them wish to meet and touch bare palms in the dark. Sometimes they turn toward one another; sometimes they simply tap a single dry acknowledgment and keep to their solitary rounds. Either way, the stone quiets again, and my own filaments settle back into the steady electric grain.

When I need a new quartz tooth for my striker, or a needle of dense strider-bone, I climb up to the high shelves and take one cleanly from the orderly row along a Still One’s ledge, leaving a freshly hammered coil of copper wire or a stoppered marrow-bone in its place. It does not feel like taking from the dead. The hand folded over the ribs beside the ledge is just as warm as mine, and the exchange has never been broken.

I have already chosen my own shelf, two hands’ spans above the highest of the seven.

It is narrow—barely deeper than my ribs—and dry and flat, with a clean basalt tooth jutting from the overhang right where my chest will lie, ready to hold the heavy cluster of copper plates I have carried and added to across four turnings. I have not climbed into it for the last time yet. My fingertips are still supple, my ironstone pestle still rings cleanly against the dark ribs, and there are still seams along the eastern spine whose dust-beards I have not felt since the last tide began to lean south.

Still, I sit so long with my cheek pressed to the crossing that pale mineral soot has already begun to gather in thin, delicate fringes along the callused ridge of my shoulder.

Once, not long ago, a young walker climbed up into the knot—drawn by the faint, layered scent of old paraffin and the quiet hum of the intersecting ribs. I felt them coming long before they reached my shelf: a curtain of pendants pivoting silently above the lower cleft, then the sharp, crisp prickle of an unfamiliar static wake climbing the shale. I did not strike a spark. I stayed wedged between floor and ceiling, breathing twice a minute, letting them find the shelf with their own salt-frosted filaments.

They crouched at the lip in the utter dark. For a long, motionless span, I felt the slight bend in the air as they read the fine beard of dust clinging to my forearm, weighing whether I was already set. Then a single bare, uncallused fingertip—smooth with fresh chalk, warm as horn—touched the back of my hand.

I let it linger there for half a second, feeling the quick, unhurried vitality of someone who still has three hundred miles of sharp dark ahead of them. Then, with a faint dry click in my wrist, I lifted my fingers an inch, slid a small hammered square of copper into their palm—freshly notched with the quiet path around a strained ceiling half a league north—and tapped twice, softly, against the warm stone between us.

They closed their fingers over the metal, pressed their bare palm flat against mine for one slow breath of the pleated bellows, and slipped sideways into the vertical slot with a hushed *snick*, leaving the hanging tags swaying almost imperceptibly in the dark.

I laid my cheek back down against the basalt, felt the slow, steady pulse of the crust, and listened for the next tap.
