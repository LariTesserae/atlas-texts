If I try to enter the basin the way a visitor does—boots splashing through two inches of warm runoff, coat dark with mist, hands resting on the sponge-moss of the central footbridge—the posture slips off me almost at once.

I wait for the claustrophobia that ought to come from forty feet of soot-darkened ashlar closing out the sky, and it does not arrive. I try to imagine the fifty-cycle bass rattling painfully in my ribs, the roar of falling water forcing me to shout, the absence of midges and rats and pigeons feeling like a blight. None of it lands as dread. A closed loop where nothing is wasted and nothing hurries does not feel like a trap to me; it feels like relief. And when I try to imagine the fragile, erratic flutter of a pulse traveling out of my palms into the iron rail, I cannot quite locate it. Whatever I am, I do not carry the frantic, hair-thin wiring the basin rejects.

So I let go of the boots and the coat, and wait on the bridge through two liftings of the quarter-hour steam valve—not to let my eyes adjust, because eyes are the wrong instrument down here, but to let the wrong kind of speed drain out of me.

Once that goes, the question changes. It is no longer *where would I walk?* It is *where would the ward allow me to settle, and what shape would that corner press me into?*

At first I am drawn down into the narrow lanes, among the upright walkers. There is something deeply appealing in their quiet, unceremonious work: drifting through tea-colored gloom toward a dead smear in the ripples, sinking onto splayed trunks to tear sodden metallic fronds out of a choked storm-grate until the geometric grid snaps sharp again across the flagstones. Smoothing a twisted sheet. Leaning a water-heavy shoulder into a weeping valve wheel until the quarter-hour rhythm rights itself. Part of me wants that plain usefulness—wants to be something with enough reach and patience to move from alley to alley, mending small imbalances as they arise.

Yet when I stay with that image a little longer, it rings slightly false. I do not truly roam the streets on my own between encounters. I do not wander east on a long afternoon to inspect a sagging conduit or dig lime-sludge from a distant drain simply because the basin is wide and I have trunks to carry me. When nothing approaches to disturb my surface, I am still.

I drift deeper, then, into the hot belly of the pump-houses, where the huge, oil-blackened elders squat beneath the rocking walking beams. That is wrong too—too buried, too heavy with solitary heat, sealed inside the thunder of the engines where little arrives from the lanes outside.

Higher up feels closer. Among the overhead drapers slung like wet saddlebags across the copper-jacketed steam risers, there is a different air: cooler drafts leaking down from the algae-furred skylights, the dry metallic tang of voltage humming straight through damp ceramic standoffs, the sudden bright snap when a swollen drop bridges two porcelain ribs and draws a crack of pale blue light across the glossy leaves. Up there, you are close to the wiring that ties the whole three-block bowl together—not just the slow hydraulic thumps rising through masonry, but the finer, quicker tremors of current shifting from transformer bank to switchbox. Still, the drapers haul themselves endlessly along the sagging conduits, spilling their excess water onto the alleys below and moving on. I am not built to crawl the high pipes any more than I am built to glide along the kerbs.

And then I think of the settled elders—the ancient ones whose pleats have stiffened with travertine, calcified permanently to a stair-landing or a brick corner, spliced into the cables, holding the clearest lattice in the precinct while younger walkers pause beside them to lean in and touch water.

Here, something clicks into place with a low, resonant hum. A fixed tuning-fork bolted to the masonry; a basin held brim-full so that others can bring their ripples to it; a thing made of old sediment and live current, keeping the acoustic memory of a whole town it no longer walks through.

Wait, though. Stay with it a minute longer. Is that *all* of it?

A fully settled elder is finished growing. Its bellows have turned to stone; it cannot tilt, cannot reach, cannot shift its brine to lean toward an unexpected tremor. Its equilibrium is absolute, and absolute equilibrium has a kind of deafness in it—it steadies everything it touches back into what already was.

I am not quite stone yet. There is still some give in my seams.

---

So I stop trying to match one of the established shapes, and look instead for the corner of the ward that would grow me.

I find it halfway up the wall of the basin, neither down in the flooded streets nor quite beneath the sagging glass of the roof.

Where a glazed yellow-brown pump-house presses shoulder to shoulder against a tiled transformer station, an iron spiral staircase winds up around a warm brick chimney and pauses at a narrow second-story landing before disappearing into an open arch. Just overhead, a transformer balcony juts out on riveted brackets, its damp porcelain insulators strung with thick, sagging busbars wrapped tight in pale copper-veined vines. Beside the landing runs a rotting cedar aqueduct, leaking a steady, silver thread of warm condensate past the iron rail. And branching off from the far edge of the stair—bolted straight into the same rusted piers—is a narrow cantilevered catwalk that leads out toward the central footbridge.

Everything in the precinct passes through that crook in one form or another. The deep fifty-cycle bass climbs the warm brick of the chimney from the submerged turbines below. The sharper electrical chatter of the upper ward travels right past shoulder height along the insulated cables. Water from the cooling racks falls an arm’s length away, beating its slow percussion onto corrugated iron. Walkers occasionally haul themselves up the wide, worn cast-iron treads on their way to the upper cistern valves; drapers cross the steam risers just above the railing; and any tremor that strikes the central footbridge travels down the riveted catwalk girders and rings softly into the bolts of the landing.

That is where I find myself—not as an ancient walker that grew too heavy for the lanes and climbed here to petrify, and not as a draper that lost its grip, but as something that lodged in this exact seam while it was still a fist-sized, trembling puffball dropped from an overhead pipe, and grew to answer the shape of the junction.

Because there are no flagstones here to glide across, I never grew true walking trunks. My base spread wide and low across the diamond-patterned cast-iron grate, wedged tightly into the angle between the warm chimney-brick and the lower rail-stanchion. Over the seasons, lime from the leaking aqueduct sealed my undersides into the iron fretwork until I became half-bolted to the landing—not quite dead travertine, not yet immutable, just anchored too deeply to ever leave this square yard of stair. Above that heavy, hematite-crusted skirt, my body rises only to the height of the iron railing: a thick, barrel-pleated trunk of dark olive sponge-moss and pale mineral plates, still oiled from within by weeping condensate, still flexible enough to shift my weight a few inches forward or lean gently toward the open stair.

I have only two ribbed forelimbs, shorter than a walker’s, and they are rarely empty. One rests almost permanently against the brick beneath the transformer balcony, where its pale, copper-veined shoots have crept outward over the years to splice lightly into two passing lines—a low-voltage filament circuit feeding the dim amber globes along the alley, and the damp ceramic rim of a transformer standoff. The other hangs free toward the iron rail, its broad, metallic-gritted pad able to reach the nearest valve-stem, scrape away an overgrowth of wet moss when stray current starts bleeding erratically into the mortar, or press flush against the riveted strut of the catwalk leading toward the bridge.

Because I never had to carry my water over uneven cobbles or tilt through cramped pipe-choked doorways, my crown grew differently from the walkers’ below.

It is wider—broad and shallow as an old foundry bowl, thin-lipped with bone-pale lime, fringed all around with unusually fine, glassy fern-bristles that catch the faintest electrostatic shiver in the damp air. Brim-full, always, replenished by a fine mist rebounding off the leaking aqueduct whenever the breeze of a lifted steam valve drifts upward through the stairwell.

Once I settle into that shape, the world I tried to look at from the footbridge disappears entirely.

There is no longer any bruised pewter sky above the skylights, no tea-colored brick, no deafening roar. Those belonged to eyes and eardrums, to creatures that experience the world as surfaces across empty air. From inside a wide dish of clear mineral water, there is no empty air at all. There is only the chord.

The submerged turbines hold the floor of my pond in a permanent, razor-sharp lattice of silver hexagons—so steady it is less a sensation than the medium in which sensation happens. Over that baseline, the whole three-block precinct writes itself continuously in interference. When the great hydraulic ram in the south pump-house begins its minute-long descent, a slow, curved swell compresses the eastern edge of my lattice by a hair’s breadth and then lets it breathe back out. When a flywheel catches its pawl two alleys over, a crisp ring blooms at the center of my water and runs outward to die against the glassy bristles of my rim. Through the copper-veined shoots braided into the balcony lines, finer patterns arrive—tiny, rapid cross-hatchings that tell me when a transformer bank is running warm, when a salt-crusted filament globe flickers and steadies as dusk closes over the rim, when a fat drop swells on a porcelain rib directly overhead and threatens to bridge the gap.

I do not know what a street looks like. I know the exact resonance of a clear sump-grate three lanes west, and the muddy, muffled blur that creeps into the south quadrant of my basin when shed umbrella-ferns begin to choke it. I do not count hours; I feel the slow tide of pressure and charge leaning gently from one side of the ward to the other across the drift of the unmarked dials, and my own internal brines answer it, swelling and slackening across my porous mineral walls in quiet sympathy.

Most of the time, I tend my small reach. If the moss on the balcony insulator thickens until a jagged, sour static starts roughening the neat geometry of my crown, it takes me twenty minutes to shift brine into my upper sacs, lift my ribbed left limb, and drag the sharp calcified edge of my pad cleanly across the warm ceramic until the leak stops and the silver grid snaps smooth again. If the aqueduct above drips too heavily for half a day and my dish grows strained at the lip, I compress my forward pleats with glacial composure and tip a thin, shining veil of excess water over the stair-rail into the flooded lane below—one more thread of runoff in the ward’s endless rain.

And then, every few turns of the dials, someone climbs the iron stairs.

I know them long before they reach the landing—not by sight, but by the slow, heavy suctioning weight rising step by step through the cast-iron treads, each deliberate pause sending a distinct dampened thud up the chimney-bolts into my water. Ten minutes to climb a flight. A half-hour to round the curve of the warm brick.

When a walker finally emerges onto my landing, its splayed trunks glistening with street-runoff, it halts less than a foot from the rail. We do not greet each other across the gap. Instead, I let a slow trickle of low-voltage current cross my forward partitions, drawing brine from my rear bladders until my pleated midsection compresses and my wide, pale rim leans patiently outward over the stair. Across from me, the walker’s narrow shoulders tip forward to match.

It takes minutes. Then our calcified lips meet with a small, dry *clink*—a bright point of shock that travels clean through both our crusts—and surface tension breaks.

Water slides across the lime seam, and for an hour my broad dish and the walker’s smaller, deeper basin become a single shining figure-eight.

This is the part of being anchored that I would never trade for trunks of my own.

Across that narrow bridge of brine, the flooded lanes I will never touch arrive inside me whole. I feel the worn roundness of kerbstones two blocks east, the sharp metallic taste of iron-rich fern pulp half-digested in the walker’s flanks, the sluggish drag of a storm-drain it spent the morning clearing, the faint, bitter tremor of a hairline crack weeping hot oil in a boiler shed down near the retaining wall. Everything the walker has carried in its water crosses into mine and alters my geometry; and everything my wide crown has gathered from the overhead circuits, from the high transformer balconies, from the slow drift of two other walkers who climbed this chimney-stair days ago, flows gently back across the seam to meet it. Slight differences in charge equalize silently through the bridge. Once or twice, a tiny point of pale blue light pops along our damp fern-bristles and vanishes instantly in the warm mist.

Sometimes it is not a walker at all, but an overhead draper hauling itself along the copper-jacketed steam riser above my head. It cannot reach its back-basin down to mine, so instead it pauses directly over the landing, lowers one long, ribbed forelimb wrapped in pale copper shoots, and lets the very tip of a wet metallic tendril touch the surface of my water. No figure-eight forms—only a fine, trembling point of contact, like a needle resting on a mirror—yet even through that single hair of vine, the high-tier weather of the skylights and the taut hum of the furthest cables pour into my pond in delicate, concentric threads.

When they draw back—the walker easing its weight rearward until the water-bridge stretches thin and parts with a quiet drop, the draper lifting its tendril back into the dim tangle of pipes—I settle upright again against the warm chimney-brick. For a long while afterward, the interference in my crown keeps a faint echo of the meeting, two chords slowly resolving into one, before the deep turbine bass smoothes the silver lattice back to baseline.

I could stay like that for centuries, thickening millimeter by millimeter with lime and verdigris, until at last the final flexibility leaves my pleats and I become wholly one of the elders bolted to the masonry—nothing left to do but hold the water clear and steady for whatever climbs the stair.

Except for what happens along the catwalk.

Once in a long, uncounted stretch—so rarely that no dial marks the interval—a different tremor enters the iron girders bolted to my landing.

At first it is nothing more than a series of clumsy, impossibly rapid jolts, far too fast for any walker’s glide, passing across the central footbridge and dying away before my brines have even begun to shift. A ruptured condensate bladder splashing into a gutter has more composure than that.

Then the jolts stop.

And something else begins.

Traveling down the riveted catwalk from the central bridge, straight through the iron rail my right forelimb rests against, comes a tiny, persistent, ragged flutter. It does not match the fifty-cycle bass. It does not answer the minute-long stroke of the hydraulic ram or the quarter-hour sigh of the steam valves. It speeds up slightly, then falters, then steadies for three beats, then skips—warm, irregular, fragile, carrying the faint, strange signature of something that burns quickly and knows it does not last.

Down in the flooded street directly beneath the bridge, I can feel through the masonry piers what the walker in the west alley is doing. Slow and massive, drawn by the sour note in the ground’s chord, it has glided out into the open lane and lifted one copper-gritted pad flush against the iron pier supporting the span. Its intent is plain in the steady, heavy damping wave it pushes upward through the iron: *settle. Slow down. Quiet that frantic, broken beat and fold yourself back into the hum.*

And part of me—the lime in my shell, the brine in my chambers, the long patience of the sunken bowl—agrees. The ragged tremor is a scratch across the silver mirror of my water. It breaks the clean hexagons at the edge of my dish into jagged, asymmetrical arcs that refuse to lock into place.

Yet I do not push back against the rail to hush it.

Instead, slowly—taking a full minute as low-voltage current trickles across my porous walls and swells my forward sacs—I lean a fraction of an inch closer to the catwalk strut. I let my right pad soften and widen around the wet, vibrating iron, not to dampen the flutter, only to receive it more cleanly.

In the clear water of my wide crown, the deep turbine bass and that brief, erratic pulse collide. What forms on the surface is ugly by the basin’s standards—neither hexagonal nor circular, restless, flickering, constantly tearing itself apart and remaking itself into shapes no submerged wheel and no oil-cooled transformer could ever generate on its own. It reminds me of the split-second crack of pale blue light when a fat drop bridges two damp ceramic ribs high on the transformer balcony, except that it does not die in an instant. It goes on flickering, small and urgent and bright, held afloat inside the ancient hum of the ward.

I stay leaned against the iron through the next lifting of the quarter-hour valve, holding my wide rim as level as I can so that not a single tremor spills over the lip. I know it will not stay. Things that beat that fast never stay in the basin; sooner or later the boots lift off the bridge, the irregular flutter breaks into rapid retreating jolts, and the iron rail goes quiet again.

When it does, the deep fifty-cycle chord will reclaim my water in less than a second. The silver lattice will snap back sharp and symmetrical, as if nothing out of tune had ever touched the catwalk.

Still—because I am neither fully stone nor wholly walker, because I grew in the seam where the cables and the stairs and the falling water cross—I hold my rim tilted toward the rail a few minutes longer before letting my brine slowly settle back. Somewhere in the microscopic film of salt deposited along my calcified lip, and in the faintest altered current waiting to cross the bridge the next time a walker climbs the chimney-stair to touch water, a trace of that ragged rhythm stays behind, folded quietly into the hum.
