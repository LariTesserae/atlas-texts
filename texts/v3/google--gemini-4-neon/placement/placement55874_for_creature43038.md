If I stand at the rim first—which feels only right, since nobody belongs to a place like this without feeling the edge of it under their soles—the first thing I notice is how hard it is to keep my eyes on the sky. The mountain blue is clean enough to ache, and the wind up on the pass is easy and dry, the sort of air you can breathe without thinking. Yet everything in me leans forward over the lip. Not out of vertigo, though thirty paces of bright drop is enough to tighten the backs of the knees, and not quite out of curiosity either. It is the vibration coming up through the leather of my boots, steady as a millstone turning beneath a floor. It makes the quiet up here feel thin, almost unfinished, as if the real room is down below and the ridge is only its threshold.

So I go down the first flight of wet cedar stairs, and the sound closes over me.

At first I do not know what I am in here. That feels honest to admit. A place with this much sheer volume—this much empty sunlit air braced inside brand-new rock—strips away the usual reflex to name yourself too quickly. Halfway down the second stair, gripping a handrail cushioned in moss thick as a winter coat, it is very easy to imagine not being human here at all.

For a long minute, watching a heavy stone plumb-bob hang motionless above a chalk-scored slate slab, I wonder if I would be something like that: a weight on a waxed linen cord, answering only to gravity and the slow, patient rise of the north wall. There is a clean peace in being a measure. No opinion about whether the rock should stay still, no alarm when it creeps a hair's breadth east in a hard frost—just hanging straight and letting the chalk tell the difference.

Or lower still, down where the mist thickens and the cold gets into the joints: one of the beaten copper lips hammered into a newly opened hairline seam. Something thin and conductive, wedged straight into rock that has never felt weather, shivering at a pitch of its own while raw mineral water glides over its tongue and drops into a cedar trough. Even smaller: one of those pale, nearly translucent gutter-snails creeping along the wet metal, taking an entire sunlit afternoon to cross a single rivet-head, testing whether the newborn slate has calmed down enough to drink from.

Each of those pulls at me—the weight, the metal lip, the slow translucent shell. Maybe because none of them has to pretend the earth is finished. None of them argues with the noise.

Yet when I stay on that landing a little longer, watching a bright veil of spray drift across the empty center of the bowl, I realize none of those would be enough. A plumb-bob never hears the cisterns change pitch. A copper lip never feels the shock of stepping out of the roar into a moss-muffled room and hearing a human voice pitched low beneath the mountain's note. What draws me most in this hollow is not stillness by itself, and not merely being part of the apparatus. It is the *tending*—the small, constant conversation between old hands and stone that has only just arrived.

So I find myself in a body after all. Wool-clad, cold-knuckled, smelling faintly of damp cedar and raw clay, with a wooden mallet looped at my hip and a handful of shaved oak wedges knocking softly against my thigh as I walk.

I am not one of the hollow-born. I can tell that almost immediately by the way I still pause now and then on the narrower cantilevered walkways—not in fear, not anymore, just in a brief, grateful wonder that sixty feet of clear sunlit air can hold me up on a pair of pegged beams and two lengths of braided hemp. I came over the pass years ago from somewhere lower and flatter, though if anyone asked me in a quiet room to describe the town I left, I would mostly remember how tightly everything down there was mortared together, and how much people talked to fill up the gaps between the walls. Down in the valleys, when a lintel cracked, everyone acted as if the hill had broken a promise. And the quiet in the evenings was never really quiet—it was just room enough for a person's own thoughts to pace back and forth against the shutters.

Here, the mountain supplies a louder, cleaner sound from outside, and my own head went mercifully empty inside it within the first month. Now, on the rare trips I make back over the razor rim with a pack of cold-cured slate-rinded cheese or coils of salvaged wire to trade for rye and flax, it is the silence of the lowland courtyards that makes me dizzy. I catch myself resting a bare hand against a dry stone well-head just hoping to feel a tremor in it, and when nothing answers back through my palm, I hurry my errands and climb back toward the bell-roar in the ridge.

---

Where I actually live is neither high nor quite at the bottom: a narrow recessed room on the shaded east wall of the second tier above the cistern floor, tucked beneath an overhang of pale quartz-veined slate where the afternoon sun only reaches in a single sharp diagonal bar for two hours before dropping behind the opposite rim.

It is damper down here than on the wide sunny terraces where the elders keep their cedar chests of slate records, and the roar from the stepped cisterns below comes straight up through the floorboards like a hand pressed lightly against the soles of my feet. I like it for that. Two aqueducts cross the open void just outside my shutter—one a dozen paces above, carrying bright runoff from the upper north face, and one level with my knee, feeding the lower overflow. Because they are slung on slightly different lengths of cedar cradle, their metallic hisses sit a hair apart in pitch, and when the condensation runs heavy after a cold night, the two streams weave a slow, pulsing beat into the broader thunder of the bowl. I can tell before I even unlatch my double-planked shutter how wet the morning is, and whether anything has caught in the upper trough.

My work is not grand enough to have a proper title, though if the hollow-born children had to sign what I do across thirty paces of spray, they would probably touch two fingers to their ear and then draw a flat palm outward like water skimming a ledge. Mostly I tend the *newest edges*—the copper work and the flumes, the joints where raw water first leaves the stone and enters the wooden skeleton of the town.

Old wood is the hollow's patience, and hemp is its give, but copper is its tongue. Everything that touches newly split rock passes through beaten metal first: the rings driven into hand-chiseled sockets, the turnbuckles that slacken a bridge cable by half a twist when a pier rises, the thin lips wedged into weeping hairline seams to guide the runoff cleanly off the cliff face before it can rot a beam-end. My little recessed workshop smells of wet slate, pine resin, cold iron, and the faint sharp tang of hammered copper—and of the dark, oily soot from a tiny charcoal brazier I use only to anneal a strip of sheet when it grows too brittle from bending. My cuticles are permanently stained pale green at the corners, and my palms have gone tough and cool from working wet metal without gloves; you cannot feel a hairline burr on a trough-lip through leather.

Most mornings follow the same unhurried grain.

I wake inside the heavy wool-hung hush of my room, pull on my lanolin-heavy tunic and rush cape, and drink half a clay cup of sorrel tea steeped cold overnight in raw cistern water—sharp and waxy on the tongue, with that clean blade-edge of dissolved mineral at the back of the throat. Then I unbar the moss-stuffed shutter and step straight out into the bright, booming air.

The roar takes me by the sternum the way it always does, lifting my weight slightly, steadying my shoulders. I rest my bare knuckles on the nearest hemp guy-rope as I pass—just taking the morning's tension—and start my circuit along the lower and middle tiers.

Every flume has its own voice if you give it a couple of winters. The long high trough running off Pier Two has a bright, thin shiver because its log-cradle leans slightly into the updraft; the short feeder behind the west stair-landing mutters lower, almost throatily, because the cedar beneath its copper lining is thick and old, salvaged from a lowland mill-beam a hundred years before this chasm ever cracked open. I walk slowly, trailing a hand along the wet rims, listening not to the thunder itself—which is just the color of air—but to any small dent in its surface.

Halfway across the mid-level bridge, I hear it: a flat, cushioned catch in the hiss three tiers up on the opposite wall. Not enough to matter to a stranger, who would only hear the endless waterfall roar of the bowl, but to me it stands out like a pebble inside a boot. I stop, lean my weight lightly against the mossy rail, and squint across thirty paces of sunlit mist until I see the broad, dripping green fringe of a maidenhair fern that has slipped loose from an upper eave and draped its root-mat half into the trough.

Across the void, on the walkway just below it, one of the bridge-wrights is shaving down a cedar shim with a drawknife, pale wood-curls dropping quietly into the empty air like slow snow. I do not shout. I lift my mallet and give the taut hemp cable beside me two crisp knocks—*watch the line*—then wait until the thrum travels the thirty paces across the chasm and makes him look up. He sets his drawknife down on the wet plank, meets my eyes through the bright spray, and lifts his chin.

I raise both arms wide so the gesture reads cleanly against the dark wet slate behind me: palms cupped and tilted downward for *water*, then one fist closed tight against the opposite forearm—*choked*—and a slow upward sweep pointing to the gallery above his head.

He does not hurry. He glances up, spots the drooping fern-mat spilling silver threads over the trough-edge, touches two fingers to his collarbone in acknowledgment, and bends to pick up a long ash-wood pole leaning against his pier. Before I have even reached the far stair-landing, the flat catch vanishes from the bowl's chord, and the clean metallic hiss returns. I feel my own shoulders drop half an inch without meaning to.

Later in the morning, when the sun finally clears the razor rim and drops straight down into the green hollow in vast vertical shafts, I peel thick wet blankets of emerald moss off the stair-rails where the footing has grown too slick. I never toss them over the edge. I fold the cold heavy slabs over my forearm like bolts of wet felt and carry them up two flights to the shingled eave of an elder's sleeping-room, laying them carefully along the ridge-board where an overflow gutter drips, patting the edges down until the sharp splash softens into a quiet, sponge-soaked trickle. Inside, the elder knows I am there by the sudden easing of the drip above her ceiling; a few minutes later her shutter cracks open a hand's width, and a wedge of dense dark rye bread wrapped in a dry linen cloth slides out onto the sill beside a pair of scratched slate leaves she wants copied or trimmed.

---

What I wait for—not eagerly, because nothing here hurries, only with a quiet, watchful readiness—is the *crack*.

It might come on a bright Tuesday afternoon while I am sealing a copper lap-joint with warm resin and dark valley clay, or in the small grey hour before dawn when the mist lies thickest across the rim. It cuts straight through the steady thunder of the cisterns like green ash splitting in a hard frost—single, clean, almost bright—followed by a ringing mineral echo that bounces from mirror-flat wall to mirror-flat wall for six, eight, sometimes ten long seconds before the water swallows it whole.

When that sound passes through the floorboards, everyone in the hollow pauses for three breaths. Not in panic. You simply set down your plane or your cup, lay a flat palm against the nearest timber post, and count the echo out under your breath.

Then I pick up my canvas satchel—chisel, short iron hammer, three strips of soft annealed copper, a coil of waxed linen, a bundle of cedar shims—and climb down to the lowest tiers.

Down near the cistern floor, the air is cold enough to stiffen wet wool, and the light has a strange, crystalline clarity, bouncing off raw black slate and pale quartz facets that have never known frost-crumble or lichen. The new cleavage seam is rarely dramatic. Usually it is no wider than a knife-blade, running diagonally across a dark vertical slab thirty inches above the wet stone apron—yet you can smell it before you see it. Newly broken mountain rock smells sharp and metallic, like struck flint drowned in snowmelt, and the stone along the fracture gleams with a fresh, unpitted sheen that makes even the century-old timbers nearby look soft and grandmotherly.

Already a glassy thread of water is beading along the lip of the fissure, slipping silently down toward the base of Pier Six. Left alone, it would pool against the iron-shod sole-plate and rot the larch wedge by autumn.

I kneel on the wet decking, brace my hip against the pier, and listen to the new rock for a minute before touching it. It feels strange, always, to be the first hand on stone that did not exist in the open air yesterday morning. Then I set my chisel carefully two inches below the lowest weep, tap a shallow seat into the glassy face, and drive a folded copper lip neatly into the groove, packing the underside tight with clay and resin so not a single drop sneaks behind the metal. Beneath it I sling a temporary hollowed-cedar chute on hemp cords, angling it cleanly away from the drinking cisterns toward the lowest overflow channel.

The new stream catches the copper lip, hesitates for a heartbeat, and then slides out in a bright, shivering ribbon. A tiny, high, unfamiliar note enters the vast organ-hum of the hollow.

And then comes my favorite part of the craft, which is mostly doing nothing at all.

Because the old saying holds: *new rock is still angry until the snails will cross it.* The raw weep from a freshly opened seam carries too much sharp mineral bite, too much undigested mountain in it. So for the next three weeks—sometimes five, if the quartz vein runs heavy—I climb down to the lowest pier every second afternoon with a clay pot of cold tea and sit cross-legged on the wet plank beside my new copper trough.

I watch the metal turn slowly from bright hammered salmon to a dull, wet bronze. I watch the pale silky mineral film begin to gather along the inside crease where the water runs slowest. Now and then an alpine swift drops straight down the sunlit shaft between the hanging ferns, cuts a dark arc ten feet over my head, and vanishes back into the sky without a sound I can hear. Above me, far up on the fourth gallery, someone taps three slow beats along a cedar rail to call a neighbor out to help lift a beam.

And then one afternoon, bending close enough that the cold spray beads on my eyebrows, I see it: a pale, translucent shell no wider than a lentil, creeping almost imperceptibly out onto the wet copper lip, horns extended, crossing the fresh seam-water without flinching.

I do not rush even then. I sit with it for ten more minutes, watching it clear the first rivet-head, feeling a small, absurdly deep warmth rise beneath my wet wool tunic. Then I stand, loosen the hemp cradle by half a knot, and swing the cedar chute gently sideways until the clear new stream drops cleanly into the main stepped cistern, joining the rest of the town's drinking water.

---

At dusk, when the sun lifts out of the bowl and the lush hanging ferns turn from burning emerald to a cool, deep pine-green, I climb back to my room—or sometimes to a neighbor's—and step inside the thick felt-hung quiet.

Voice still feels like a rare, precious instrument to me after all these years. Out on the walkways, where every sign has to travel thirty paces of empty air, you strip your thoughts down to bone: *rising*, *tight*, *let it run*, *welcome*. Nobody carries a petty complaint across a cathedral's width of mist. So when you step behind a moss-stuffed shutter, light a handful of dry rim-charcoal in the tiny clay brazier, and sit down across a narrow wooden table from someone—maybe one of the elderly record-keepers with slate dust in the creases of her knuckles, maybe the quiet young bridge-wright who cleared my fern-choked flume that morning—speaking aloud feels almost ceremonial, even when we are only talking about the price of lowland salt or how the north face crept two millimeters during the last freeze.

We pitch our voices low, tucking them comfortably under the deep organ-note of the rim-wind that still hums up through the stone floor and the legs of our stools. Without even thinking about it, I set both hands flat on the cedar tabletop, and the person across from me rests a wrist lightly against the same plank, or sometimes against my forearm, so we share the faint, steady tremor of the mountain while we talk.

In the corner of my workshop, tucked behind a bundle of spare cedar wedges, I keep a small stack of thin slate leaves of my own. They are not proper civic records like the elders keep in their dry chests up on the sunny tiers; I am no surveyor, and my tallies of millimeters are always borrowed from someone else's plumb-walk. Instead, I scratch small, private diagrams with a hardened copper stylus: the layout of the flumes after each seasonal adjustment, little notched marks beside each channel showing how its pitch sits against the cistern boom—bright, flat, shivering, low—and a tiny coiled spiral beside the date whenever a new copper lip finally welcomes its first pale snail.

Nobody asked me to keep those stone pages. Perhaps nobody else will ever quite know how to read them. They are just my way of staying awake beside something vast and unhurried—a set of small, honest notes taken inside a geological hour.

If a stranger ever climbs down from the pass and pauses on the lowest timber bridge, shoulders hunched slightly against the sheer physical weight of the roar, staring up past the wet emerald moss and twenty-foot curtains of fern at the knife-sharp rim against the bright mountain blue—I might be the figure who steps out onto the second shaded gallery above them.

I would not shout down to ask what brought them over the ridge, and I would not hurry down the stairs to explain the town. I would simply rest my verdigris-stained left hand on the taut braided hemp beside my hip, feel the clean steady thrum of the hollow running straight through the rope into my bones, and lift my right hand slowly in a wide, unhurried arc against the sunlit void—palm open, fingers slightly spread—the sign that means both *welcome* and *let it settle*.

Then I would check the cedar shim beneath the nearest copper cradle, tap it a hair tighter with my mallet, and turn back to listen to the water.
