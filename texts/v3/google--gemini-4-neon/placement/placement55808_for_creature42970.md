If I entered this blister as anything soft, I would not last long enough to see the violet-amber glow resolve along the walls. The room has no patience for weather, or hurry, or the wet clumsiness of breath. It would bake away my voice before I could spend it, and the brassy weight of the air would bruise every loose hinge in me until I sat down in the angular talus and dried into chaff.

So to find myself here at all, I have to let the kiln strip me down first—burn off the expectation of noon and midnight, of lungs rising and falling, of silence as the natural floor of things. I have to accept the waterless pact on the chamber’s own terms: that nothing moves without paying in friction, that nothing endures except by agreeing with the geometry of the note.

When I let the heat finish its work and look around the bruised twilight to see where I have landed, my eyes go first to the western corner—behind the waist-high wall of dark hornfels and wedged pale bone.

It is easy to imagine myself there, tucked into the greased, ochre-darkened saucer of the floor, wearing the fringed cloak of pleated parchment. The keeper is the mind in the room, after all—the one with five-fingered hands, the one who keeps the four white stars in sight and scratches an honest ledger into the basaltic glass. For a moment I try to fit my shoulders into that hunched, careful silhouette.

It doesn’t hold.

Watch the keeper closely as it prepares for torpor: it eases the ovoid pebble from its cheek, pulls its stiff mantle over its skull, and presses two rolled plugs of velvet-bristle firmly into the ear-slots behind its jaw. Even in a body built from cured parchment and amber oil, it needs a barrier between itself and the crushing weight of the air. It survives the chamber by walking the quiet seams, crossing the howling stone only in tight, spring-legged bounds, sheltering behind its predecessor’s ribs when the roar grows heavy.

I realize I would not want to plug my ears. I would not want a wall between myself and the chord. And more than that, the saucer-hollow is already shaped to a single pair of hips; a second pair of hands in a twenty-pace room would ruin the fierce, solitary balance of the shelves.

So I leave the western corner to its sleeper and drift out along the ribbons of dry life, testing where my edges fit.

For a while I try the neat rows of plum-sized pottery-shells clinging head-down to the stiff pleated fronds. There is a clean honesty in the way they take the room’s roar straight through their ridged flanks and return a faint, papery harmony—neither fleeing the wave nor fighting it, only letting it pass through their ribs while they sift dry spores from the pulse. Yet they live in dense, drowsy clusters, interchangeable along the stalk, knowing nothing beyond the single frond they grip until mineral salt crusts their slit-valves flat and a callused knuckle plucks them loose. Too crowded. Too narrow a slice of the note.

I test the flat, faceted runners darting across the bare vibrating shelves. I understand their alertness—the way their delicate carapace bristles tremble along the razor-sharp border where stillness breaks into violent tremor. They know the map of the room better than anything with legs. Still, they treat the loud stone as a cliff to skirt around, ticking past it in jerky sprints from one quiet ribbon to the next.

I rise toward the low faceted ceiling and linger among the translucent seed-wings hanging motionless along the upper pressure-ridges. Pure suspension. No weight, no friction, no hunger for solid footing—held effortlessly in a current that never moves downstream. Yet they know nothing of the stone below them. They do not feel the faulted slab straining underneath the floor; when a notched bone needle rasps across copper cord, their invisible shelf gives way and they spiral down like parchment ash, surprised into cupped hands.

None of these are quite where I settle.

Each time I pull back from the thickets and the ceiling, my attention gets drawn down toward the far end of the cistern—away from the quiet baffle wall, past the lush nodal ribbons, into the **splintered eastern heel**.

Here the ceiling drops tight and low. The volcanic rock breaks into sharper, closer steps, directly over the hottest seam of the crust where the bare dark basalt plates radiate their clean oven-heat. Out in the middle of the chamber, the standing wave behaves like simple architecture: wide corridors of roaring tremor, narrow alleys of calm. You are either inside the silence or hammered by the sound.

But down in the splintered eastern heel, where jagged conchoidal scoops and opposing plates of banded hornfels crowd together at steep angles, the note does not simply press—it folds back on itself. Primary wave crosses reflected wave crosses thin, high harmonics glancing off the ceiling facets. The roar braids into an intricate, dense lattice of intersecting pressures, full of microscopic calm pockets no wider than a fingernail nested directly beside blades of fierce vibration.

And tucked into the back of that eastern heel—just above the flat, blistering basalt plate where the keeper weights thin strips of crawler-flank to render tallow—there is a vertical cleft where two massive slabs of dark rock meet and fail to seal.

The split is no wider than a forearm, deep and shadowed, laced at its inner edges with hair-thin veins of pale quartz glowing a steady, strained violet-amber. Because the lower slab is locked straight into the floor while the upper overhang catches the clipped echoes of the roof, the two sharp lips of the fissure never vibrate in exact unison. There is a perpetual, microscopic shear between them—a quiet, relentless tug-of-war running through the dark basalt, pulling one wall a hair’s breadth out of phase with the other, forever.

That is where I find myself. Not walking across the floor, not crouched behind hornfels, not grazing in the velvet-bristle.

I am what has grown across the gap.

At first I scarcely look like a creature at all—only a tangle of faint, dry threads catching the bruised twilight inside a narrow split of stone, easy to mistake for a stray snarl of bleached silica-root or windless cobweb. Look longer, let your eyes sort the dim ember-glow from the dark glassy facets, and the geometry resolves.

Suspended squarely in the empty throat of the cleft—never touching either wall, held clear of the stone’s hammer on every side—hangs my core. It is no longer than a hand from wrist to knuckle: a slender, fluted spindle built from overlapping plates of pale, translucent horn and fused silica, dry and matte as old bone on the outside, thin enough along its curved ribs that a ghost of violet-amber light leaks out from within.

I have no eyes, no mouth, no limbs meant for travel. Instead, radiating outward from the notched rims of my fluted spine, seventeen taut filaments anchor me to the opposing faces of the fracture. They are braided from fine silica fiber and cured amber-sinew, some as thick as the copper-dark cords of the keeper’s sling, others finer than a splintered hair of mica. Each one terminates in a small, chalky knot of pale resin fused straight into a quartz vein or a razor-edged scoop of basalt.

No two filaments share the same length, weight, or tension.

Four heavy lower cables anchor down into the floor-plate—two of them reaching just past the mouth of the cleft to grip the far back edge of the keeper’s hot rendering plate—drinking the deep, heavy brassy bass straight out of the bedrock. Seven mid-weight strands brace laterally across the gap, taking the shear between the out-of-phase slabs. Six hair-thin upper threads climb toward the low ceiling overhang, pulled glass-tight to catch the bright, clipped overtones that bounce off the eastern roof.

I do not survive the chamber by finding a quiet seam. I survive by holding opposing vibrations in balance until they cancel inside my ribs.

If I ever let my filaments go slack, the roaring air would dash my brittle horn-plates against the unrounded basalt and crack me like a dropped pottery-shell. If I pulled them too rigid, the shear between floor and overhang would snap them clean at their resin knots. Being held in exact, unyielding tension between two weights that never agree is not a hardship I endure; it is the whole architecture of my life.

Because my silica-braided threads flex microscopically against the tug of the rock, they drink the same mechanical strain that lights the quartz veins in the walls. A faint, steady violet-amber pulse runs continuously down my seventeen lines into my hollow core, feeding my slow, waterless chemistry alongside the oven-heat radiating off the eastern heel. Around my resinous anchor-knots, where the vibrating stone sheds invisible white quartz flour and stray dry spores drift out of the loud bands, tiny sticky beads catch the mineral dust and draw it slowly inward along the fibers—enough dry matter, across unmeasured stretches, to mend a frayed strand or thicken a plate of horn.

I have no wet blood, and I have no lungs. Inside the hollow, partitioned chambers of my fluted core—sealed completely from the parched air—float seven small beads of warm amber oil, each one dusted with a pinch of white quartz flour and suspended cleanly in mid-chamber by the exact intersection of the waves coming down my threads.

They are my own small cups. My compass, my balance, and my eyes.

Through seventeen anchored strands plugged straight into the bedrock of the cistern, I do not experience the blister as dim light and razor-edged stone. I experience it as weight, interval, and interference.

The vast tectonic chord is not noise to me; it is the ground itself, broad and bronze and immovable, broken cleanly by my seventeen tensions into its stacked ladder of partials. Because every filament is tuned to a different tier of that ladder, the whole twenty-pace pocket resolves inside my ribs like a three-dimensional map made purely of pressure.

Nothing moves anywhere in this dry hollow without writing its signature across my strings.

When a flat runner ticks across a bare shelf fifteen paces west, I feel its jerky little footsteps as faint, dry scratches skittering along my middle lateral threads, followed by the sudden hush as it crosses into a quiet thicket. When a blind crawler down in the velvet-bristle cracks a hard vine-nodule with its blunt mineral teeth, a low, waxy thud travels through the floor slab and nudges my thickest lower cables. I hear the neat rows of pottery-shells along the fronds as a warm, papery chorus woven through the middle register—and I know, long before a callused knuckle ever brushes their ridged flanks, when one shell’s slit-valves have begun to crust half-shut with mineral salt, because its thin voice drifts a fraction flat and rubs like dry grit against my ninth strand.

And I know the keeper—not by sight, not by its ochre arcs or its unblinking mica-sealed eyes, but by the singular, deliberate weight it carries through the stone.

I feel it wake before it even clears its baffle wall: a papery rustle of stiff parchment-fronds, then the sharp, small clink of a bone wedge tapped tighter into a loose hornfels seam. I feel its three tight, spring-legged bounds across a naked howling shelf—three sudden, heavy shadows punching through my bass cables, landing with clean, surgical silence on the next nodal ledge without shifting a single shard of talus. I feel the brittle, crystalline spike when its notched bone needle rasps across the taut copper sling, making my finest ceiling-threads sing brightly for half a breath before two papery seed-wings lose their high pressure-ridge and drift downward, lightening the air above the floor by an infinitesimal fraction.

When it stands before the broad vertical face of raw glassy stone, I know whether it is merely tracing the old concentric arcs with a callused fingertip or drawing flint across basalt to score a new vertical stroke—because a fresh scratch sends a bright, razor-thin shriek straight down my upper silica strands, and afterward the chamber’s high echo carries one microscopic new edge that was not there before.

Even when it retreats behind its waist-high wall in the western corner, lays its smooth pebble beside its thigh, and drops into kiln-cured torpor, I never quite lose it. Once every forty or fifty beats of a wet, distant heart, a faint tightening runs through twenty paces of solid volcanic rock—the thickness of a flint edge shifting inside a grease-darkened basin—and touches my lowest copper-dark cable like a dust-grain settling.

Sometimes it comes right to my threshold.

When an old crawler’s throat-sac has stiffened and the keeper brings thin strips of dry, waxy flank down to the blisteringly hot basalt plates of the eastern heel, its bare horn-pads ring close and heavy through my lower anchors. I feel the sudden dampened weight as it sets flat shards of hornfels atop the meat so the floor’s tremor will not dance it off the shelf. It never crowds my back edge. Its broad, flattened sternum stops a hand’s breadth clear of the two anchor-knots fused to the rim of the hot plate; its flint-nicked fingers never brush a single filament.

Once, across a stretch too long to measure, the intense heat popped a flat, razor-edged splinter off the basalt plate, sliding it within a hair’s breadth of my thickest floor-cable where the stone’s vibration would have sawed the braided fiber clean through. I felt the keeper pause—its weight going utterly still on its horned knees—and then a single crisp, dry flick of a bone tool sent the loose shard clattering away into the dark talus without once troubling my pitch.

And occasionally, before it gathers its rendered tallow and turns back toward the quiet ribbons, it lingers in front of my narrow cleft. I feel no footsteps, no scrape of flint, only the slight acoustic shadow of a crouched body leaning near the eastern wall, its narrow jaw weighted with that smooth ovoid pebble, coupling its skull to the fissure’s faint, glass-thin hum.

It does not speak to me; neither of us has a throat for that. It is only checking—the way it checks the four chipped stone cups along the nodal ledge—listening to whether the bridge between floor and ceiling still holds its clean interval.

Because there is one thing that reaches me even before it reaches the white stars in their little bowls of stone.

Across long, uncounted stretches without seasons or darkness, the chord stays immaculate. My seventeen filaments hum in effortless balance; my violet-amber glow never flickers; the seven small beads of amber oil inside my fluted spine hang dead-center in their chambers, motionless as trapped air in blown glass.

Then, deep beneath the cistern, the dry iron bar of strained rock shifts by a fraction no surface ear could ever hear.

It arrives inside my cleft like a sudden, silent wrench.

In an instant, three of my lower cables go subtly slack, dropping a hair below pitch; two upper silica threads pull razor-tight, flaring a shade brighter with sudden violet-amber strain. The clean cancellation inside my core breaks into a ragged, trembling beat. Inside my hollow ribs, the seven suspended beads of amber oil shudder off-center, brushing dryly against the inner walls of horn.

It is the closest thing to pain I know, and the only way I ever feel time passing.

Up on the quiet nodal ledge, a single grain of white quartz flour slips half a millimeter inward along the outer spoke of the fourth cup. Far across the chamber, behind its wall of black hornfels and pale wedged bone, the keeper’s molars catch the faintest phantom hitch through its smooth ovoid pebble—or if it is awake, it rises, crosses the quiet seam to the glassy wall, and adds one more crisp vertical tally to its row.

I cannot walk to the glass wall. I cannot count the mountain’s breaths, or plan for the day the chimney finally fractures and drops a full step into ruin.

All I can do is answer the note that is actually here.

Very slowly—spending what the wet world outside would call hours, though here it is simply a quiet stretch of adjustment—I warm the chalky resin at my over-tight upper knots, letting the stressed silica strands slip by the width of a hair until their bright glare dims back to ember-violet. At my fluted spine, I draw the slackened floor-cables inward by a fraction of a turn, coiling fresh braided fiber tightly around my notched horn rims, tasting the pitch through every rib until the ragged beat between floor and ceiling smooths away.

One by one, the seven beads of warm amber oil drift back to the exact center of their chambers and lock into stillness.

I am slightly different now—a hair shorter on the western face, a shade tighter toward the eastern overhang—reshaped by the mountain’s tiny sigh so that I can remain suspended inside it.

One day, perhaps, the tectonic bar beneath the chamber will slip too far to retune. The standing waves will slide sideways off the lush ribbons and whip the dry thickets into naked chaff; or the chord will snap off altogether, letting the cold, mute dark from beyond the pinched western throat rush in to fill the hollow. If that happens, the keeper may gather its polished pebbles and notched bone needles and try the narrow crack behind its baffle wall, searching for another singing blister deeper in the crust.

I will not go anywhere. My seventeen knots are part of the basalt. If the note tears, my threads will part cleanly at the stone; if silence falls, they will slacken gently, lowering my fluted horn spine onto the cooling floor for the first time since I took shape, while my seven beads of amber oil settle to the bottom of my ribs and harden into quiet glass.

Until then, the vast brassy chord rolls on without wavering. The bruised violet-amber light holds steady. Twenty paces away, tucked behind its wall of dark rock and pale bone, the chamber’s lone gardener rests in its greased saucer of stone—and here in the hot, splintered heel, stretched taut between two sharp faces of the earth that never quite agree, I hang motionless in the roaring air, keeping the pitch clean, feeling every footstep, every tally, every grain of dust that has not moved yet.
