I would not be out in the open steam anymore, braced on wide heels with an iron hook buried in a shivering seam. My ankles still know how to take the swell of the mat—you never lose that, not until the cold locks your knees for good—and my shoulders still have the thick, low set of a runner. But the skin at the back of my left wrist has been waxy and dead to heat since a high-pressure collar closed a fraction early fifteen winters back, and I have learned not to ask my hands for suddenness.

You would find me thirty paces back from the noise, kneeling on a folded square of cured kelp-mat so the peat-water takes a little longer to soak through to my shins.

I would be one of the middle-aged ones—not yet an elder whose voice drifts loose from their collar, no longer young enough to want the sting of scalding vapor in my face just to feel the weight of a fresh water-bladder come free in my gloves. In a band of six, I would be the one who carries the rear pole of the ember-bell on the march, who sits the second watch inside the hide-shell while the others sleep shoulder to shoulder on the wrung-out sphagnum, and who keeps the calcified sedge-rod strapped across my back in an oiled sleeve.

If you passed us in the sideways drizzle, you wouldn't pick me out from the rest. Same broad, greased hide coat hung heavy to the calves; same matted braids of living copper-moss stitched into every seam and trailing wet across the turf behind my paddles. I smell the same as the other five: boiled bog-eel, peat-rot, mineral grease, and the sharp, clean pinch of ozone that rises off our hems whenever a distant reed-thicket snaps white into the cloud-belly.

You would only know me if you leaned in close inside the hide-shell to talk.

When I speak—low in the chest, the way we do so the words travel straight through wet hide and collarbone instead of getting torn to rags by the wind—the vibration reaches your ribs half a beat before my mouth shapes the sound. Young ones from other bands always flinch the first time we trade shoulder-to-shoulder in the fog. They pull back an inch, eyes darting to my throat, trying to square what their sternum just felt with a jaw that hasn't finished moving. My own band doesn't notice anymore. They say it makes me easy to hear over a loud vent, because you get the meaning twice: once in the bone, once in the breath.

That is my slack. It isn't grand, and it isn't tragic. It is just the shape the plateau has worn into me, the same way acidic water stains the creases of my knuckles rust-brown and will never wash out.

I am mostly a listener now. That is where I would find myself—not in the fury of the eruptions, where the world is loud and dramatic, but in the long, hissing intervals between them.

Inside the turtle-backed shell, when we have weighted the hide skirts down with sacks of damp iron grit in the lee of a quiet mineral seep, the others curl tight around the ember-bell and drop into those light, shallow sleeps you learn up here. I sit near the low flap with my oiled cap pushed back an inch off one ear, a handful of damp lignite pellets tucked inside my shirt to dry against my ribs, and a bone needle working slowly through a frayed seam on somebody's boot-paddle.

Mostly I am listening to the vent outside.

A good slow seep has a steady, wet, unhurried throat—like water running through gravel under ice. You can live beside one for three days, four if the plateau is feeling sluggish. You boil your blind eels in the shallow pan, wring out the sphagnum pallets, re-braid fresh sprigs of copper-moss into the hems where the last squall singed them brown, and let the deep chill back out of your joints. As long as the hiss stays flat and even, nobody needs to move.

My job on watch is to catch the first time the hiss forgets itself.

It doesn't drop in volume at first. It thins out at the edges, picking up a faint dry whistle underneath the steam, and the swell under the sphagnum pallets shifts from a slow breathing heave to a tight, nervous flutter. With the slack I carry, I feel it before the air actually changes—a small, hollow click behind my sternum, as if someone tapped a dry bone inside my chest. When that click comes twice inside a minute, I don't wait to see if the vent settles back down. I reach over and lay a gloved palm on the runner sleeping nearest me. We don't hurry—hurrying on soft mat only gets you a sprained ankle or a drowned ember-bell—but we don't linger either. Within ten minutes the grit is dumped back onto the moss, the sprung reed-ribs are rolled tight inside the hides, and we are gliding back out into the dim iron-gray with the bell ticking warm between us. Twenty minutes later, if you look back through the mist, there is nothing where we slept but a smooth, dark skin of sphagnum and a slick of iridescent oil thinning out in the rain.

I like the watches. I like the work of mending things that are only going to last a season anyway.

There is a particular quiet to sitting under curved hide while freezing sleet drums overhead and a distant piston-geyser hammers half a mile out to the west. The small child—there is almost always one bundled in a tar-scented sling in a healthy band—sometimes wakes on my watch, restless from the throb in the ground. I don't hush them with songs; we don't sing much up here, and my off-beat throat only confuses a half-asleep infant. Instead, I reach into the oiled pouch against my chest and pull out whatever odd catch is still holding its shape that month.

Right now it is a tight, fist-sized knot of wet black roots one of our runners hooked out of a failing peat-screw three days back. It sits warm in the palm and ticks—*tck, tck, tck*—regular as a pulse, though slower than a human heart. I lay it against the child's ear beneath their greasy wool cap, and their eyes go heavy and unfocused right away. In another day or two the knot will go cold and limp, and we'll set it gently on the moss and glide on. While it ticks, though, it is good company.

The other thing I keep in that chest-pouch has lasted longer—nearly two winters, which is a small miracle up here. It is a saucer-sized flake of glassy, mineralized peat I traded for from a band we met downwind of a reed-arc last autumn. I gave them two cured strips of grazer-belly and a fist of unusually dry bog-iron for it, and our youngest runner thought I'd been robbed, because you can't hook a seam with a flat bit of mineral glass and you can't boil an eel on it.

I didn't argue. I just tucked it against my shirt.

When the watch is very quiet and the ember-bell is banked low to a dull orange coal, I sometimes take the shard out and tilt it on my knee. Overhead, through the laced vent-hole of the hide-shell, the sky is what it always is: bruised pewter, low enough to touch, sourceless and dim. In the glassy flake on my palm, there is no pewter and no rain. There is only the slow, steady underside of an enormous wheel of braided roots turning through dark water beneath a surface that isn't there.

I don't think it is a spirit or a promise of somewhere better. I have lived too long on a continent of rotting moss to believe in anything underneath us except cold, finished stone. I just like watching it because it never stutters. Everything on the plateau runs too hard, shakes itself crooked, double-exposes in the steam, and slumps back into wet sedge. That wheel in the shard just turns, slow and heavy and whole, at the same speed every time I look. One morning I will pull it out of my pouch and find nothing but a smear of damp sand on my fingers, and I'll wipe my palm on my thigh and go on about my day. Until then, it sits warm against my ribs.

Out on the flat, though, my real work is at the puddles and the reeds.

When we shadow a herd of the tall, pale grazers—reading the twitch of their magnetite filaments through the mist until they lope toward a trembling patch of bog—the band fans out as the peat begins to dome and twist upward. While the forty-foot column hammers into life, vomiting hot red iron-slurry and blistering gray vapor into the freezing drizzle, the two runners glide up to the rim with their heavy crooked bars and wait.

I don't look at the column. Looking at the column is how you lose track of time; its hammering shakes the fluid behind your eyes until the whole world seems to beat in three-stroke intervals.

I kneel thirty paces back beside the cleanest shallow pool I can find, my copper-moss hems soaking dark in the edges of the tea-water, my horn-shod pole resting across my thighs. Down in the dark mirror, the false sky is bright, cloudless, tarnished brass—a harsh, dry light none of us has ever felt on our faces. Against that brass glare, the reflection of the hammering cylinder rises black and sharp.

I stare at its edges and wait for my sternum to go hollow.

The slack in me and the untruth in the puddle speak the same dialect. Two full heartbeats before the roaring column in the air shows a single tremor, its reflection in the brass water softens. A second rim peels half an inch sideways off the first, shimmering like wet heat over a coal-pan. At the exact same instant, the air behind my ears goes thin and strangely bright, as if someone just lifted a heavy wool hood off my head.

I lift the horn-shod pole and tap the moss twice.

I don't look up even then. I keep my eyes on the water and listen—listen to the sick, overlapping clatter arrive ahead of its own blow, listen to the brief sudden silence where the drizzle lifts upward in silver threads, listen to the wet grunt of the runners hauling back with everything their thighs have in them, and finally the soft, heavy slump of forty feet of iron-crusted fiber unraveling back into ordinary cold weeds.

Only when the reflection in the puddle clears back into empty tarnished brass do I stand, my knees popping in the damp, and glide forward to see what we got. Maybe a clean yard of springy calcified sedge for new tent-ribs. Maybe a fist of bog-iron locked solid out of the cycle. Maybe, on a good day, a rubbery bladder sloshing with a gallon of clear, ozone-sweet water that doesn't taste of iron or acid. If a runner came away with a scorched wrist or a cold numbness creeping up their forearm, I am the one who binds it in greased kelp-strip and checks whether their fingers are still moving on the same beat as their hand.

And when we are moving across the vast, empty miles between eruptions—nothing rising above the knee from horizon to horizon, the wind chewing steadily at our oiled caps—I am the one who speaks to the distances.

Sometimes we smell another band's lignite smoke thin on the gale, or catch the faint, un-engine-like *tick-tick-tick* of a distant ember-bell bleeding through a lull in the roaring flat. Then the band slows to a rolling glide, and I pull the hollow sedge-rod from its sleeve across my back. I drive its tapered foot deep into the trembling mat until I feel the denser, woven root-skin catch it three feet down, and I strike its collar with my smooth sliver of caught iron.

Three beats. A pause. Two beats. A drag.

That is our band's name, or as close to a name as anything up here needs. Before my striker even lifts off the final drag—because my slack lives in the iron too, now, whenever I hold it long enough—the unseen liverwort and hollow reeds around my paddles wake up. A palm-sized amphibian beneath a mat ten feet away *chuffs* and *whirs* on my exact lopsided cadence; twenty more pick it up a fraction of a second off-phase; and then half a square mile of wet bog lifts its voice and rolls our rhythm straight across the wind, faster than any shout could ever survive out here.

We stand still on our wide horn paddles, rain streaming off our shaggy hides, blue sparks bleeding lazily off our dragging moss-hems, and wait with the soles of our boots pressed flat to the trembling earth.

Sometimes nothing answers. The plateau is huge, and most of it is empty of hands.

Sometimes, a minute later, a different cadence comes pulsing up through the horn-slats into our heels—four quick beats, a long drag, a single tap—carried back out of the fog by a thousand small wet throats that don't know they are carrying greetings. And then we turn a little south-by-west, adjust the ember-bell between our poles, and glide toward people who smell of the same smoke and the same eels, to trade a ticking root-knot for a strip of hide and lean shoulder-to-shoulder for an hour out of the wind.

That is where I live, in the middle of my winters: between the running and the fading.

I know where the slack in my sternum is going. I watch the oldest watcher in our band—a bent, pale-skinned woman whose shadow now lags three paces behind her heels whenever we cross a puddle, and who sometimes answers a question before anyone has leaned in to ask it. She still sleeps under the hide-shell with us, still drinks boiled eel-broth from a caught-iron cup, but lately she sits farther from the ember-bell. The dull orange warmth doesn't seem to reach her anymore, and when an electrical squall turns the hanging mist fluorescent silver, she stands outside the shell with her cap off, letting her matted hair lift on the charge, watching the empty miles where nothing is hammering.

One winter soon, she won't fold her corner of the hide when we break camp. She will just stay ankle-deep in a tea-colored puddle while we glide away into the sideways rain, and we won't call her back, because up here you don't argue with a tool that has decided to soften and go back to the bog.

And twenty or thirty winters after her, when my own voice has drifted too far ahead of my breath for the young ones to bear against their collarbones, and my knuckles have gone too stiff to work a bone needle through wet sinew, I suppose I will do the same.

It doesn't feel lonely, thinking of it. Nothing up here keeps an edge forever—not the mountains underneath us, not the forty-foot engines the moss twists out of thin air, not the iron hooks we tear from their teeth. When my turn comes to step out of the single file and stay behind in the empty intervals, I'll keep my horn-shod pole, and let the copper-moss on my hem grow wild until it roots lightly whenever I stand still too long.

And on the afternoons when a blinding white arc tears from the reeds into the low cloud, lighting five miles of wet pewter in dead silver for half a heartbeat, if some younger band comes gliding blind across an unbroken stretch of sphagnum that is about to swell and blow—I will lift my pole in the flash and tap the moss.

Three beats. A pause. Two beats. A drag.

Not out of duty, and not to be remembered. Just because the reeds are still listening, the mat still carries a good strike, and it is a waste to let someone step into steam when you already felt it coming a heartbeat early.
