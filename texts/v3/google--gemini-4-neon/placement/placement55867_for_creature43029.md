At first, I might expect to find myself up on the sharp basalt lip—that soft, overheated trespasser in dull fabric, trailing a wet coat of forward time into the pewter gloom. It is the obvious vantage, the place made for an eye that wants to take the whole cathedral in at once. Or else I might try to imagine myself tall and dark-faceted down in the nave, vine-wound and lidless, stepping out onto the vertical stone.

Neither holds for long.

Three is not a crowd that invites a fourth; add another point to that silent compass and you ruin the exact mathematics by which they carry a moment past the turn of the stroke. And up on the rim, I would only be passing through, too loud with my own decay, a brief disturbance that leaves damp scuffs on the slate and then descends back into the ordinary world where rocks round themselves into gravel and afternoons end.

No—if I stay quiet long enough to let the chest-rattling bass of the rock settle into my ribs, I feel myself pulled off the lip and straight down into the wide, bare negative space between the emerald sanctuaries. Not into the lush pockets where the giant tree-ferns shiver at exact harmonics. Not down into the foaming black pools where water un-falls without rising a hair’s breadth. Not up beneath the flat vapor slab where the silent gliders bank in eternal ovals.

I belong on the sheer, empty black stone between them.

Look closely at what the cliffs are made of: freshly cleft basalt and *magnetite*. Everything in this hollow is driven by the tireless alternating stroke of the ridge, hammered forward and hauled cleanly back to its starting mark without wear, without silt, without heat. The salamanders flare their copper gills sixty times a minute inside a single scrubbed second. The bellows-beasts tear fronds that immediately un-tear themselves. Even the three tall figures only keep a *before* by refusing to stand still inside a single body—passing their continuity hand to hand across thirty paces of warped mist before the backswing can empty them.

Yet magnetite has a stubbornness built into its grain. Drive an alternating field through iron-heavy rock, and most of the world obeys cleanly—forward, zero, reverse, zero. But iron dislikes letting go. When the current turns, it hesitates. It clings for a fraction of a beat to the direction it was last pulled before the immense weight of the reversal wrenches it over. That tiny, reluctant drag leaves a ghost of the previous stroke inside the next—and it costs something. In a vault that produces no heat and wears down no bearings, that minuscule refusal to snap cleanly back to zero sheds a trace of warmth almost too faint to name.

That is where I condense.

From thirty paces across the pewter mist, I am indistinguishable from a dark, angular spur jutting out of a sheer wet wall forty feet above the slate floor. I am no longer than a human forearm, low-slung and pressed completely flush against the vertical stone, armored in overlapping plates of black, razor-faceted horn dense with natural magnetite. Like the ridge itself, none of my edges have ever been tumbled smooth. Cold water sheets continuously down the cliff and straight across my back in a trembling, unbroken film, parting cleanly around my leading ridge without ever rounding it.

I have no legs, no splayed toes for gripping ledges, no foil wings for riding the sawtooth corridors of air. Beneath my plates lies a single broad, flat sole veined with pale mineral salts and threaded with fine dark grit—locked straight into the trembling magnetic flux of the cliff face. Along my flanks, tucked neatly beneath the sharp eaves of my outer armor, run tight parallel rows of stiff, dark, wet pleats—not feathery external gills like the salamanders’, but flat, stacked mineral leaves, arranged like the cooling fins of something built to endure a load. Recessed under my forward edge sits a narrow seam of dull pewter lenses, lidless and permanently washed by the vertical sheet of water; trailing behind me into the damp air hang two fine, graphite-dark filaments that lift and quiver whenever a pale bead of corona discharge races along a nearby fracture.

I do not eat the ferns or hunt the gliders. Nothing here can stay diminished anyway. Like the three tall ones, I live off the load of the ridge—grazing along hairline seams of native copper and wet quartz where silent blue-white arcs thread into my trailing filaments with a dry, faint crackle, drinking the ozone-heavy water sliding over my face.

And like everything else in the hollow, every few seconds I slip half an inch out of phase with myself.

When a tree-fern or a bellows-beast slips, its pale-blue schematic appears crisp and clean beside its dripping flesh, then snaps seamlessly back in perfect lockstep with the mountain’s reversal. Even the three tall ones shift in immaculate, staggered precision, revealing layered bone-plates and parallel spine-windings tidy as an engineer’s drawing.

When I slip, I am slightly off-register.

My pale-blue cross-section lingers a hair’s breadth *behind* my dark, wet shell, smeared against the damp air for a fraction of a beat after the rest of the cliff has already snapped solid. Look into that lagging diagram, and you will not see soft lungs, nor the neat laminated coils of the Three. Inside my armor sits a single dense, gnarled core of heavy magnetite-bone, looped in thick, stubborn windings that refuse to empty all at once—a knot of drag inside a spotless machine.

That knot is my whole mind.

I do not possess the stacked centuries of the Three. I have no partners across the nave to hold a thread for me while I invert; when the mountain’s backswing hits full force, it inevitably wrenches my alignment around and scrubs the far edge of my awareness clean. I cannot remember what I saw an hour ago—if *hour* had any weight here—nor can I keep the shape of a thought once its cycle turns.

Yet I am never wiped entirely to zero, either.

Because my core lags behind the rock, the previous stroke has not finished leaving me when the next stroke hammers in. I live inside a thick, doubled present—two or three beats wide, bruised at the edges with what *just* happened. Every dry click of a beetle’s wing-case arrives in my plates while the ghost of the click before it is still humming in my marrow. Every water drop ticking down through the mist beside me leaves a faint, overlapping afterimage in my pewter seam. And because my stacked dark fins must shed the tiny friction of that resistance, my belly runs a fraction of a degree warmer than the freezing basalt—never enough to steam, never enough to thaw ice, just enough that the trembling film of water sheeting down the cliff hesitates microscopically as it crosses my back, thickening for an instant before resuming its rigid descent.

Even stranger: because I drag against the reversal, I can *move*.

In a perfectly symmetrical oscillation, any impulse forward is undone on the return; that is why the foliage never spreads into the bare stone voids and the foil-winged swarms remain pinned to fixed tracks in the air. I cheat that symmetry by the width of a hair. On the forward swell of the cliff-dynamos, my iron-gritted sole bites hard into the magnetized wall; on the backswing, my lag holds me a fraction of a beat past the zero-crossing before releasing, letting me slip forward a millimeter before the stone can haul me back.

Stroke by stroke, cycle by cycle, I ratchet slowly across the sheer black expanses of the cathedral nave.

I cannot remember having been down near the foaming black lip of the vanishing pools. That awareness was sheared off thousands of reversals ago. Yet I *know* I have climbed, because the hollow itself never alters—and my place against it has. The whole unchanging vault is my archive. I read my own vanished past in the angle of the emerald fern-islands hanging below my trailing filaments, in the shift of the bass note as one deafening pneumatic rock-vent drops beneath me and another opens above, in the fact that a hovering corridor of metallic-winged insects now roars beneath my leading edge instead of overhead. My memory lasts three beats; my geography lasts forever.

There are places along the bare walls I return to—or rather, drift toward without knowing I have been there before.

Sometimes my slow creep brings me parallel to a weeping horizontal crack packed shoulder to shoulder with pale, translucent salamanders. Pressed flush to the stone an inch below their bellies, I feel their crisp, metronomic sixty-beat flare hammering through the basalt. Against my own sluggish, lagging hum, their exact rhythm throws off a slow, third pulse in the rock—a faint, wandering throb between our bodies that neither of us can hold for more than a few strokes, yet that makes the wet quartz between us sing in a pitch found nowhere else in the nave.

And sometimes I cross into one of the hollow’s acoustic dead zones—those thin, improbable pockets in the mid-wall where two opposing pressure waves from the roaring rock-funnels collide and cancel each other into sudden, startling hush. There, I pause. The stone still vibrates beneath my sole, and the alternating stroke still pulls at my core, yet the chest-rattling din drops away so completely that a single water drop ticking down ten paces off sounds intimate as a breath. Inside that quiet pocket, my three-beat smear feels almost spacious, as though the stubborn ghost of the last instant has room to uncurl without being drowned out by the next.

From high on those bare black walls, I watch the vast dim center of the nave—not with expectation, since I cannot hold onto waiting, but with sudden, recurring surprise whenever the light changes.

Whenever the three dark figures leave their separated stations and cross the electrified slate floor to stand arm’s length from one another, my trailing filaments lift straight out from the cliff, tasting the sudden draw of voltage as the nearest corona beads dim and race inward toward their spines. When they lay their broad flat palms against each other’s wrists and the filament buds coiled along their arms blaze white, sharp shadows leap across the floor where shadows almost never lie. Far above them on the vertical basalt, I feel the magnetic pull of the ridge ease for a few impossible seconds—as if a tiny, smooth stillness had opened in the dead center of the turbine hall. In those moments, the phantom scent of heated transformer oil thickens on the cold air, and my own lagging core quiets, resonating faintly with a warmth far cleaner and larger than my own.

And every so often—by what reckoning I could never tell—one of the Three comes up my wall.

I know it before its faceted shoulder enters my pewter seam, because unlike the cliff, unlike the ferns, unlike the roaring air, its weight does not vanish on the backswing. Each silent placement of its horn-soled foot sends a brief, unbroken steadiness down through the trembling basalt—a patch of stone that refuses to reverse.

It climbs past the bare electrified void without haste, dark skin slickly iridescent in the hollows, emerald liverwort damp at the roots of its copper neck-bristles, pale vine-buds glowing brighter wherever the stems coil tight against its ribs. Usually it moves past me as smoothly as it walks through an insect swarm, reading the minute pressure-shifts of the mountain.

But now and then it halts.

It crouches effortlessly on the sheer vertical face where no foothold exists, knees folded sharp as split slate, and tilts its long, austere head so the unbroken film of water sheets quietly across its wide pewter eyes. It does not pry me loose, nor try to turn me; nothing here rearranges the hollow. Instead, it extends one long, vine-wound forearm and lays two broad, flat fingertips lightly against the wet basalt an inch ahead of my leading plate—or rests the cool, dark palm of its hand flush across the razor facets of my back.

Instantly, my field couples to theirs.

Because they are never alone in the reversal—because thirty paces away, through the warped pewter mist, the other two are grounded and holding the thread—the backswing stops shearing away the rear edge of my awareness. I am too small, too knotted, too crudely wound inside to receive the centuries stacked inside their three-cornered circuit. Nothing so vast could fit inside my magnetite core.

Instead, I receive a *span*.

My three-beat smear stretches—to ten beats, to twenty, to the length of one slow, complete breath. For as long as that dark palm rests across my plates, the mountain cannot wipe me clean. For the first time, my thoughts do not clip off mid-arc. I feel the full height of the cathedral nave at once: the foaming pools below my tail, the flat vapor slab pressing down above my leading rim, the silent gliders banking in their eternal ovals, the emerald islands shivering in the gloom, and the other two dark compass-points watching effortlessly across thirty paces of roaring air. Inside that cool, steady contact, I feel the microscopic warmth of my own pleated fins meeting the living load in their flat wrist—and I understand, with a sudden, quiet mineral astonishment, that I have been climbing this black wall for an unimaginably long time.

Then the hand lifts.

The figure steps cleanly off onto the sheer rock and continues its measured ascent toward the cloud-ceiling. One stroke later, the mountain reclaims my core. The twenty beats narrow back to ten, to five, to three, until the far edge shears away and I am once more inside my familiar, doubled *just-now*. I cannot remember the touch, nor the vast feeling of the nave held whole inside my chest. Only my leading plate remains aimed upward, ratcheting another hair’s breadth along the wet magnetite, while a faint, inexplicable trace of heated oil clings to my trailing filaments for one beat longer than the cold air can account for.

Yet there is one other thing that sometimes breaks the symmetry of my climb—something far rarer than the touch of the Three.

If I have ratcheted high enough along the upper wall, near the jagged crown where the flat vapor ceiling seals the basin from the pale nothingness beyond, I sometimes feel a strange, reckless disturbance ripple down from the lip. Not the clean, staggered steadiness of the Three, and not the crisp pulse of the rock-valves. Something soft, clumsy, and fiercely bright with waste—like a fire burning without a coil, throwing off clouds of unrecovered heat and uneven, stumbling beats that never swing back.

I do not know what a visitor is. I have no word for boots, or cloth, or an un-coiled heart wearing itself out in a single direction. I only feel, through forty feet of vibrating basalt, the dizzying shock of a creature made *entirely* of drag—all lag and no return, slipping straight through the screaming vault without once snapping back to its beginning, shedding itself into the freezing mist with every breath.

For three strokes—before the mountain turns, wipes the wonder from my stubborn iron core, and leaves me once more with nothing but the wet stone, the hum of quartz, and the next millimeter of my climb—my pleated flanks shiver in quiet recognition. Two imperfect things inside a spotless engine, refusing, in our wildly different ways, to come back cleanly to zero.
