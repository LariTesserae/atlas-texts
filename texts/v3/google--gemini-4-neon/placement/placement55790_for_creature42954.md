At first, when I try to imagine stepping into that wet, kiln-heavy air, I do not immediately look for hands or a face.

The temptation is to become something simpler than a person—something that has already solved the argument with the heat. For a long moment, I think I would be one of the millstone crawlers clinging to a weeping basalt wall. There is an enormous mercy in that shape: a low ceramic dome, a grazing tongue moving at the speed of lichen, and a whole valley’s news passing straight through your ribs as a warm bronze hum. You would never have to decide when to speak or what to carry. You would just breathe your excess heat out through your dorsal slits and let your neighbors match your pitch.

Or else I would be one of the pale-blue stems pushed up through the crystalline peat in a shaded hollow—not thinking at all, just holding an older, quieter chemistry inside my stalk, drawing the furnace out of whatever passes too close with a faint glassy tick.

Yet the longer I sit inside the roar of that highland—the flat slap of condensing droplets on leathery leaves, the low hiss of springs meeting wet stone, the distant brassy horns of the sail-flyers circling under the invisible ceiling—the more I realize I would miss the *exchange*. A crawler holds the hum, but never chooses the wobble that turns a drone into a sentence. A blue stem holds the cold, but never feels the shock of relief when another’s reed-harness presses against its throat after a long climb.

If I am in this world, I want to feel the meeting between things. I want to be one of the tall, spare ones—the mineralized people of the seams.

***

Not young, and not yet truly old. Long enough in the damp that pale-green crusts of silica have begun to trace thin, delicate shelves along my collarbones and the outer knobs of my wrists, like the first water-lines on a boulder beneath a steady seep, though my joints still roll cleanly over wet algae without stiffening. My skin still holds its smooth, waxy soapstone sheen; my radiator-vanes still fold flat and glossy along my spine in the green shade, and still flush a clean, quick copper when I step into a break in the canopy.

I know straight away where I would *not* live.

I would not build on the prized outer rims where the perched marshes spill off into the white cloud-sea. I understand why others treasure that faint downward draft—that half-degree of lightness against the vanes—but the glare out on the bare lip feels too exposed, and the silence beyond the drop is too stark, too hungry. Nor would I settle near the crowns of the basalt domes where the seamless pearlescent needles stand half-swallowed by copper-veined vines. Even from a ridge away, the crystalline lattice in my bones picks up the pressure of those places as a faint, persistent ache behind the molars, as if the air overhead were pulled a fraction too tight.

My place would be lower and more folded in: a mid-valley pocket where a stoneware-barked canopy tree has braided its buttresses directly into a weeping travertine cliff, half a day’s unhurried glide along a Lean from the low octagonal basin of milky glass-stone.

The alcove itself would be halfway between basketry and stone. Years ago—perhaps started by someone before me who moved on, or simply begun with my own slow patience—copper-veined vines and swollen aerial roots were woven into a curved screen against the midday glare, and a thin sheet of mineral spring water was coaxed over the lattice. By now the lower arches have hardened into seamless, ochre-dappled travertine, while the upper ribs are still green and pliant, dripping steadily into a shallow fluted trough that keeps the sleeping ledge dry. Bracket-lichens grow in broad, stepped shelves along the damp inner wall, holding porous clay bulbs of cold-sap, coiled spare reeds, bone picks of half a dozen gauges, and strips of cured sail-flyer membrane.

Out behind the largest root-buttress, where the peat stays dark and faintly threaded with crystal, I would keep a modest, unhurried patch of pale-blue stems—not a grand grove, nothing to boast of, just enough that my harness never runs dry and there is always an uncut stalk ready to crack for whoever arrives off the mist-blind ridges.

***

As for who I am—what shape my slowness takes—it begins with my hands on a warm ceramic shell.

In a world that talks across valleys by detuning the cliff-crawlers, someone has to listen for when the walls go muddy.

It happens gradually, the way everything happens up here. A hot seep shifts half a span to the left along a bedrock seam, warming the algae and coaxing a clutch of crawlers to breathe a fraction sharper. Or a season of heavy mineral runoff builds stubborn crusts of pale silica inside the dorsal heat-slits of an elder crawler, choking its resonance until its hum turns breathy and ragged. When a speaker presses her sternum to the wall to send a pulse across the basin, the wobble snags on that clogged voice and arrives at the far ridge smeared—*a marsh is flooding* muddled into something unreadable, or lost altogether beneath the drumming wings of the shade-insects.

That is where I find myself, most mornings, when the high-altitude light turns milky above the canopy.

I climb the weeping apron slowly—heel rolling into webbed toes, pausing every few paces to let my inner lids wipe the condensation clear and to listen to the soft glassy ticking of the cold-reed strapped across my sternum. When I reach one of the great millstone elders, I do not begin with the pick. First I lay both palms flat against the mottled keratin of its dome and match my breath to its hum, letting the vibration travel up my long forearms into the fine crystalline threads of my own bones until I can feel where the note is catching.

Then, with a sliver of smoothed bone, I clear the slits one by one.

There is no hurry in it. Hurry would heat my blood, and heat would make my fingers clumsy, and clumsiness would startle a creature the size of a boulder into clamping its shell hard against the stone. You work at the pace of dripping water. Flake by flake, the silica yields. You feel the exact instant a vent opens—not in your fingers first, but in the hollow of your chest, where the crawler’s drone drops a hair and rounds out, rich and clean as a freshly bowed bowl. A dozen paces down the wet face, another crawler shifts its own breathing to meet the clearer note, and then another, until a whole quarter-mile of cliffside settles back into tune.

Sometimes I stay on that wall for an entire afternoon, riding an elder’s glacial climb up a slick mineral shoulder while I work, listening to the valley settle around us. Other times, I am the one who stays behind after a long-distance hum has passed through, checking whether a distant bedrock groan has nudged the local Lean half a thumb-width off its old line. You can feel it if you close your outer lids and tilt your head slightly—that faint, steady pull in the inner ear, like a palm resting gently against the hip. People assume the Leans are fixed forever, like the pearlescent needles that anchor them, and mostly they are. Mostly. Over twenty seasons, though, a hairline seam beneath the peat settles by a fraction of a grain, and the invisible path across a steaming marsh drifts just enough that a blindfolded traveler’s foot might kiss a scalding vent instead of firm moss. I like walking those edges with my eyes shut, keeping track of the quiet drift, sharing the corrections palm-to-forearm with whoever stops beneath my eaves.

***

Still, if you truly want to know what I am—what pulls me out of my own alcove on the days when the cliffs are singing clean and no one has called for a scraper—follow me down the western Lean toward the marsh-edge, where the milky octagonal basin sits tucked beneath overhanging ferns.

I am not a healer. When someone is carried here packed in cracked blue stems, their blood slowing to dark tar inside the ten-pace rim so that steadier hands than mine can stitch a torn vessel or cool a fevered brain, I stay well back in the reeds. If they need fresh sap tapped or a message hummed down the valley to call kin, I do that quietly and leave the threshold clear.

I come to the basin when it is empty—in the thick, amber-lit hush before evening mist swallows the ridges—carrying small, ordinary things.

A dried throat-sac from a fallen sail-flyer, freshly mounted on a hollowed stoneware-bark pipe. A coiled reed-tube from my own harness that has begun to tick unevenly. Sometimes nothing at all, just my own hands and a handful of peeled blue-sap beads.

I sit on the low, smooth rim of milky glass-stone, my long shins resting in the warm, popping mud outside the boundary, and lean forward until my wrists cross the invisible plane.

It is the strangest relief in the world to feel speed lose its teeth.

Outside the rim, everything is braided together so tightly you can only experience it as a wall: the roar of water, the hum of stone, the snap of amphibians, the brassy glide of the flyers, the relentless urgency of heat waiting to punish a careless heartbeat. Even a single sound is too crowded to see into.

Inside the basin’s ten paces, things unbraid.

When I release a drop of cold-sap from a tiny clay bulb above the mirror-flat pool, it does not fall; it hangs, drifting downward like a pale seed in thick honey, taking half a minute to cross the width of my palm. Leaning close—my breath careful, my vanes folded half-shut—I can watch the faint, pale-blue chemistry inside that suspended bead flash briefly into tiny geometric stars before clearing to warm dew. When I rest the rim of a newly cured flyer-horn across the threshold and press a single, low breath through it, the horn’s brassy call does not slap the air all at once. It unfolds. I can hear the separate ribbons of tone peeling away from one another—the hollow wooden rattle of the stoneware pipe, the oily tremor of the dried membrane, the faint secondary ring from the mineral lattice in my own jawbone—laid out side by side as cleanly as braided roots laid on a drying rack.

That is what I think I am, really, beneath the bone picks and the reed-harnesses: someone who keeps the intervals clean. Someone who listens to a tangled, roaring world and finds where the layers separate—so a horn matches the flocks overhead, so a cliffside carries a warning without smearing, so a traveler’s foot lands on cool stone in the fog, so a fast, frightened moment gets stretched wide enough to be understood instead of endured in panic.

People know where to find me. Now and then a musician brings a newly stretched wing-membrane to my alcove so we can walk down to the basin together and test where its grain runs tight. Now and then a young person who has just suffered the violent, shivering terror of biting open an emergency sap-bladder—having leaped to haul a sibling back from a slumping peat-shelf, now trembling and faintly ashamed of that stolen, whip-like speed—sits beside me on a wet ledge while I pack fresh wet moss behind their collar. We don't talk about it with mouths. I just rest four long fingers lightly against the inside of their forearm, feeling their racing pulse through the waxy skin, sending small, unhurried contractions of tendon and cool shifts of pressure until their heart remembers the tempo of the valley. *Slow,* my knuckle clicks gently against their wrist. *The shelf has stopped moving. You are back in the heat. Stay.*

***

Once—only once, so far—I climbed the folded basalt steps.

They rise out of the moss along a slick ridge-back with a cleanliness that makes the eye water: no tool-marks, no seams, just native rock folded upward like wet clay by hands no one remembers. I went alone, with a freshly tapped double-bulb of cold-sap rigged to my throat and sternum, because even a short stay up on that bare lip drinks a harness dry.

At the top, there is no canopy to turn the light milky. The cobalt sky drops straight onto your shoulders, fierce and pure, and my dorsal vanes flushed deep copper almost before my heel settled on the topmost tread.

Below the fluted buttresses lay the painted white floor of the clouds—endless, bright, and utterly silent.

I stood there for perhaps twenty slow breaths, pressing the ticking reed hard against the hollow of my throat, trying to picture what the old palm-to-throat stories say lies under that blinding lid. A dark floor where water turns hard as travertine and shatters if you drop it. Creatures with no radiator-vanes at all, packed dense and round to hoard their own fire, burning through whole lives between one sunrise and the next at a speed that would boil my brain to ash in three minutes. I tried to imagine what their noise must sound like—thin, brittle, dry—and whether they had any basin where they could lay a hurt thing down and make time wait for them.

The heat hit the back of my neck like an open kiln door, and a faint warning prickled behind my eyes. I did not wait for a freak eddy to lift a speck of foreign pollen or a melting shard of rime from below. Knowing the edge was there was enough. Knowing that this whole sealed, humming summer sat perched miles above an unimaginable cold made the weight of the wet air feel less like a burden and more like a cradle.

I turned my back on the white silence and glided down the basalt treads into the shade, where the first heavy droplet from a leathery leaf struck my shoulder and rolled cleanly across my pleated skin.

***

I know how it ends here, and I find I do not dread it at all.

Already, when I tap a fingertip against my collarbone in the quiet before mist-fall, the chime is a little cleaner, a little brighter than it was ten seasons ago. The silica shelves along my wrists will thicken by grains I cannot measure. One decade, the bone picks will grow too slender for my stiffened fingers, and the climb to the higher crawler-walls will ask too much of my heart.

When that slowness finally overtakes me, I already have my ledge picked out.

It is not overlooking the cloud-sea, and it is not beside the milky basin. It is a clean, rounded travertine apron halfway up a busy weeping wall, just below a broad seam where three generations of millstone crawlers graze their slow circuits. The seepage there is steady, rich in pale silica and copper-green mineral, never scalding, never dry.

My kin—and whoever still remembers which horn we tuned together, or which clogged slit I once cleared along their ridge—will bring me crushed marsh-tubers and sweet lichen-paste, resting their long palms against my crusted neck to tell me how the Leans are shifting and whether the eastern basin’s flocks have begun their long turn. Day by unbroken day, my breath will lengthen until it matches the slap of water off the canopy. And when it stops, they will tuck a single unbroken pale-blue stem into my folded palms and leave me cleanly beneath the seep.

Season by unhurried season, the mountain will fold me in, grain by microscopic grain, until my narrow ribs and folded vanes vanish into the smooth, settled shoulder of the cliff. And I like that best of all—that I did not have to choose forever between being the listener and being the stone. Even after the hands are still and the harness has rotted back into reeds, whenever a speaker down the ridge lays her chest against a crawler’s shell and bends the valley’s hum into a message, that long, bronze tide of news will still travel straight through what used to be my chest—forever warm, forever dripping, part of the resonance that keeps the roof of the world alive.
