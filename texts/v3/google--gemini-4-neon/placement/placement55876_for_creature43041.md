If I tried to picture myself stepping through the weathered western notch with a scarf across my face and a leather water-skin slung at my hip, the image would break before I took three paces onto the scrubbed rock.

I don’t belong to the wet lowlands. I wouldn’t come up the pale slate ridge only to endure the bowl for three cramped days, stuffing carved bone and wax into my ears against a roar that feels, from the very first sentence of this place, less like an assault and more like a room finally coming into focus. What drives the harvesters back down the mountain—the airless pull in the chest, the scouring ochre grit, the complete absence of water, the refusal of anything here to soften or rot—doesn’t frighten me. Nothing in me needs dampness to stay alive. If anything, the dryness feels like clarity.

So at first, trying to locate myself inside the stone bell, I drift toward the things that don’t move at all.

High in the bruised dimness of the eastern eaves, there are thirty cured thoraxes wedged permanently into the fluted rock—hollowed, resin-sealed, angled with exact care into the cross-draft. And far below them, under the southern overhang, hammered copper pins sit driven deep into dry chisel-holes, drinking the wild static out of the passing grit and gathering it into a single bright point.

I linger with both of those images longer than I expect to. There is a strange, immediate recognition in them. To be a hollow vessel tuned by hands older than my own, holding no breath of my own until a draft I didn’t summon finds my collar and wakes a steady alto out of my ribs. Or to be a needle of forged metal set into stone, quiet and motionless until scattered tension thickens in the air, and then answering with one clean blue-white spark. Something in how I am made knows that posture intimately: waiting in the lee of a much larger weather, existing most fully in the instant a current passes through me and leaves a note behind.

Yet the longer I stay wedged into the eastern flute, or hammered into the southern ledge, the more the stillness begins to pinch.

A copper pin gathers the lightning, but it never shivers when the charge grounds out. A cured ancestor holds its pitch for three hundred dry years, faithful and exact, but it cannot lean down to hear how the chord has changed since the last fissure shifted, or feel the faint pressure-itch along its brow ten seconds before the seed-bladders blow. It can be part of the music, and never once hear the bell sing.

That is the seam where my shape begins to separate from the stone.

Not a frantic parchment-kite snapping through the eddies on reflex alone; not a scaled hunter locked to an arch with only one lunge in its mind; not a blind hive-worker chewing wax in the dark. Whatever I am here has to be able to *listen back*. It has to fold and unfold, carry the marks of what has struck it, and feel the whole curved cathedral at once as a single taut lattice of heat and charge.

The laths along my back part with a dry, papery whisper.

I am one of the seven.

Not the oldest—my shoulders haven’t gone stiff yet, and my membranes still hold more of their original amber parchment than resin patches—and not the youngest crouching near the warm floor-vents, waiting for its brow-filaments to draw their first sheen of copper from the dust. I am somewhere in the long, seasoned middle, when the bowl’s grammar has sunk all the way into the hollow of the bones, and every seam stitched across the ribs has a gust it remembers.

My arch is the southwestern one, where the bone-smooth curve of the nave sweeps down between the weathered western notch and the low stone blinds tucked under the southern eave.

It is not the quietest pocket in the bell. The cold mountain shear hits hard just past the notch, peeling off the lip in sudden, irritable twists that force me to keep my hind spurs twisted deep into the honeycombed pores of the slate. The other six prefer the deeper leeward ribs along the northern wall or the high ledges near the eastern eaves, closer to the ancestors’ humming flutes and farther from the place where the outside world leaks in. They don’t grudge me the southwestern arch. In a household of seven bound by stone-tremor, someone has to hold the threshold.

Holding it has shaped me slightly differently from the rest.

Because my ledge hangs directly above the harvesters’ blinds, the drafts that rise past my folded laths carry more than toasted fennel and bronze-vine dust. Over forty dry seasons, my brow-filaments and the fine sensing-hairs along my forearms have grown unusually dense, tarnished a bright, keen verdigris from years of leaning down in the dusk to drink stray static off the hammered copper pins below. The others read the bowl’s vortices as cleanly as I do, and feel a kite’s wing-crack fifty feet across the nave, yet my lattice has a sharper edge to it near the southern wall—a bright metallic prickle that doesn’t belong to the mountain alone.

Even my repairs give away where I perch. Most of my tears are mended the ordinary way, stitched neat with stripped oil-vine and sealed with glossy beads of dark-gold tallow resin, a cracked shoulder-lath splinted with a salvaged kite-strut. But tucked along the inner fold of my left membrane, reinforcing an old rent torn by a freak collapsing eddy, runs a single fine filament unraveled from a snapped sinew cable I found thrashing loose beside the blinds three years ago. It doesn’t flex quite like oil-vine. When the shear turns west and pulls my ribs taut, that one stitched seam vibrates at a slightly lower pitch than the rest of my body—a tiny, private bass note humming against my flank.

I am always the first to know when the wet ones return.

Long before their sour, metallic dampness drifts up into the dimness—the strange smell of sweat and wet breath and soaked hide that makes the others draw back high under the northern arches—my locked hind spurs catch the clumsy, uncadenced thump of booted heels beyond the western notch. Climbers strike the rock in crisp, deliberate patterns; wind-eddies throb in clean cycles; only the visitors move with that heavy, slipping irregularity, as if the stone itself resists them.

I drop flat along the curve of my rib, press the bare, unplated ridge of my sternum tight against the slate, and contract my hollow resonance chambers in a slow, measured sequence.

*Heavy at the lip,* the ridge carries around the bell. *Wet things entering. Stay high.*

Across the churning center of the bowl, six sets of spurs answer in faint, staggered tremors—acknowledgment, and the quiet click of horn-plates folding tight into the shadow of the vellum-ferns.

Then I stay where I am, fifty feet directly above the southern eave, folded into a slate-gray shadow no bigger than a crouching person, and I watch them through the shimmering static-wakes they leave behind.

They have no idea how loud their silence is, or how blind they are to the glow of their own tools. Watching them pile loose stones back onto a wind-blind’s lip, I feel none of the anger I might show a scaled hunter prowling too close to a nursery vent. Mostly I feel a dry, patient bewilderment. They come all this way up a thin-aired ridge into a chamber tuned by ten thousand years of wind and thirty generations of cured ribs, and the very first thing they do inside their blind is reach for the wooden peg and jam greased bone-and-wax plugs deep into their skulls.

To my brow-filaments, they look like heavy, muffled lanterns leaking a sharp fog that makes the surrounding ochre grit clump and fall dead. Yet their hands make geometries the bowl has never grown. I watch them pivot their hide-stretched baffle on its stone socket, catching an eddy to funnel silver chaff into a leather sack—a stiff, wooden, single-jointed mimic of what my own pleated flanks do effortlessly four times an hour. I watch them wedge boulders onto the beam of the resin-press until dark-gold oil beads out of crushed tallow-shrubs. I watch one of them sit crouched inside the largest blind with a sharp iron point, scraping short, even lines into a flat slab of slate.

For three days I hardly unfold. I let the quarter-hour pressure drops pump my ribs quietly in the shadow, eating pellets of combed seed-floss stored beneath my lower mandibles, waiting for the sour humidity to thin.

When they finally cork their heavy jars, tie a fresh little copper bell onto the frayed cord beside the press, and file stiffly back through the western notch, I don’t wait for the others to stir.

In the pewter dusk, while the oven-drafts from the floor fissures are still scouring the last alien dampness off the ledge, I unlatch my spurs, spread my amber-seamed flanks into a rising thermal, and drop in two slow, wind-spilling glides down to the southern eave. Triple-click of spurs locking into familiar pores. The bare rock hums faintly beneath me, still carrying the residual warmth of their clumsy boots.

First, like any elder, I go to the copper pins. I lower my brow-crest until the tarnished filaments nearly brush the hammered head, and let the clean blue-white arc jump the gap. It snaps like struck flint and shoots down my hollow spine into the ridge—a bright, effortless clarity that makes every lath along my back settle into alignment. Next I hook one curved fore-spur gently under a braided sinew cable and draw it back just enough to let it go. The bass note rolls through the copper pin and sweeps all the way across the nave; high on the northern ribs, two of the others shift slightly against the stone to feel the long, strange hum travel through their chests.

After that, though, I go somewhere the others rarely bother to stoop.

Folding my laths narrow, keeping my patched wings tucked tight against the gusts swirling around the lip, I crouch low and slip inside the mouth of the largest stone wind-blind.

The roar of the bowl drops half a register behind the mortared slate wall, turning thick and velvet-dry. Here, out of the direct grit-storm, the air smells faintly of old grease, tallow-resin, and the fading trace of leather. The bone earplugs hang still on their wooden peg. On the floor lies the flat slab of slate.

I kneel on the scrubbed rock, bracing one hind spur in a mortar-seam, and lower the fine, notched tip of my fore-spur onto the incised surface.

I have no wet eyes to read whatever numbers the harvesters think they are keeping. Even my recessed keratin lenses see only dim shadow across the gray stone. Yet through the tip of my blackened spur—and through the tiny electrostatic snags where the sharp-edged grooves break the smooth skin of the rock—the rows of tally-marks rise into sharp, unmistakable cadence.

*Click. Click. Click. Click. Long stroke.*
*Click. Click. Click. Click. Long stroke.*

I trace them slowly, row after row.

In our bowl, nothing stays unless the wind permits it or the resin seals it. A climber’s percussion dies the instant its forelimbs stop hammering the stone. Even our deepest tremors fade out of the ridge within a heartbeat. Yet here, the wet ones have hammered a climber’s rhythm so hard into the rock that it refuses to stop speaking after they are gone. Frozen percussion. A count of something—jars filled, pressure drops endured, dry seasons returned, bells left on frayed cord—held fast against a storm that takes everything else apart by evening.

I don’t know what they are counting. Still, I stay crouched inside their small curved shell for an entire cycle between pressure drops, tracing the grooves while the copper pins hum outside the mouth, trying to feel the exact shape of a mind that comes to a roaring chimney only to scratch quiet, straight lines into the lee of a rock.

When I finally back out onto the ledge, I gather a few curled scraps of hardened resin from the press-trough and slip a single tarnished copper bell off its frayed tether—not taking them all, leaving two to thrash and shiver in the draft—before turning toward the warm center of the floor.

It is my turn to keep watch at the vents.

Down near the gnarled tallow-shrubs, where the heat rises steady as an raked oven, this year’s hatchling waits tucked into the lip of a fissure. It is still small as a human forearm, pale and unpatched, its fresh membranes tacky with oil, blind to the static lattice spinning overhead. When the eddies swing low and grit begins to hiss across the honeycombed floor, I lock my spurs astride the crack and throw my patched rib-cloak wide above it, turning my own body into a living wind-blind.

While the storm hammers against my shingled back, I tap the hot stone beside the fissure with one notched spur, teaching the young one the grammar of the bell.

*Wait,* I tap, as a vortex spins up along the northern ribs. *Feel the itch before the drop. Lock before the shear reverses.*

The hatchling shifts its soft pale spurs experimentally against the warm stone, mimicking the cadence, its tiny hollow chest fluttering against the draft. Once the gust passes and the floor clears for a few breaths, I press a lump of clean, press-refined resin toward its lower mandibles—sweet, concentrated, effortless—and then tap one last, quieter rhythm into the rock beside its claws. Not a cadence of wind or hive or hunter, but a short, even sequence of four quick beats and a long stroke, borrowed from a flat piece of slate under the southern eave.

The young one doesn’t know what it means, and neither do I. Yet it answers back with four shy, pale clicks of its own, learning early that the stone bell has more than one kind of voice inside it.

High above us, the colliding drafts tremble and find their brief, violent equilibrium.

The pressure drops all at once.

Across the overhangs, the howling hives fall a full tone in unison; far up in the dim eastern eaves, thirty cured ancestors answer in a low, woody sigh; hundreds of swollen seed-bladders detonate into a sudden silver blizzard. Down by the fissure, the locked valves along my flanks spring open, and my hollow chest swells effortlessly as the bowl draws breath through me. Beside my locked hind spurs, the little copper bell hooked beneath my shoulder catches the sudden shift in draft and gives one faint, bright shiver—a note no stone flute has ever made on its own.

Ten seconds later, the cold upper shear slams back across the oval mouth. The fresh draft drives hard into my deepest chambers, snapping my oiled membranes taut, humming quietly along that one sinew-stitched seam on my left flank.

I stay crouched over the hatchling until the silver storm sweeps skyward, letting the grit rattle harmlessly across my pale horn-laths. Far across the nave, tucked beneath the eastern overhang where the rock is cleanest and smoothest, there is still plenty of room in the stone flutes. One day—decades from now, when my resin seams outnumber the parchment and my spurs begin to slip in the honeycombed pores—I will climb up there for the last time and call the others through the ridge. They will pack my hollow limbs with warm tallow-wax, fold my stitched wings tight, and wedge my open ribs into the prevailing cross-draft so I can join the long alto of the eaves.

And if I have carried enough bright metal up from the southern ledge by then, perhaps the one who takes my southwestern arch will wedge a small, tarnished copper bell right beside my collar—so that long after my filaments go still, whenever the wind turns through the western notch and the wet ones come creeping back with their jars and their tally-slabs, my cured ribs will still answer with a low, steady tone, and a faint bright shiver at the edge of the chord.
