I would not wake up reaching for a lamp.

That is the first thing I notice as the warmth settles against me—that the pitch-blackness does not feel like blindness. There is no instinct in me to pry open lids that aren't there, no grief for a sun I have never seen. The darkness here is clean. It has no glare, no clutter, no scatter of irrelevant detail demanding to be sorted. Only weight, and vapor, and the steady, boneless hum running through the floor like an unspoken premise.

For a long time—hours, or whatever passes for hours when a drop falls every ten seconds and nothing ages between them—I try to find where my edges end and the lowland begins.

My first thought is that I belong to the forest itself: one of the great horizontal root-mains buried half-submerged in black mirror-water, carrying quiet gigawatts from mineral seam to mineral seam. There is a deep pull in that. To be vast, continuous, unhurried, without a single wasted movement. To spend a millennium refining raw basalt into razor-clean flutes and never once ask why.

Yet the moment I imagine settling into that placid direct current, something in me chafes. Direct current is too flat. It moves forever without ever saying a word. I don't truly wake until the line shifts—until a voltage spikes, or a fault slips three inches in the dark, or a tiny ripple of resistance passes through the bark and asks to be answered. If nothing varies, I am scarcely there at all.

So I follow the hum inward, away from the quiet roots, toward the things that let go of the stone.

I try the elders first. Deep in the sulfurous cracking-flats, where the distillation bladders swell like stranded locomotives and the air tastes thickly of warm amber wax, they sit in the narrow aisles like tiered bells, fused permanently to three or four overhead bus-bars at once. When I stand near one—keeping my distance so my field doesn't smear its chord—I feel a sudden, startling recognition. Inside those heavy, pleated aprons are concentric rings of magnetite, refined copper, and polymerized wax, layer upon microscopic layer, every alternation preserving the exact winding ratio of some ancient standing-wave conversation.

I know that density. I recognize the sensation of carrying an immense, silent library of remembered frequencies pressed into the very grain of my frame—of having been *composed* in a shallow brine pool, long before I ever took a step, by elders who stood around a bare geometric lattice and fed it years of sculpted alternating current until their braided harmonies became my skeleton.

For a moment I think: *Yes. That is what I am. A transformer in the vaults, stepping down the continental mains into slower, deeper chords.*

Except I am too light.

An elder grows heavier with every seam it crosses, every meeting it holds, electroplating new ballast onto its ribs until its limbs can no longer lift its own memory clear of the stone. I listen to my own core, and I hear no creeping crystallization. My wax-insulators do not thicken. Whatever passes through me—however fierce the amperage, however intricate the harmonic—leaves no permanent crust behind when the circuit opens. I am rich with the winding I was born with, and oddly clean of everything after. I could never anchor. I would never grow heavy enough to become a monument in the sulfur vaults.

So I look up.

High above the fluted trunks, where the waste heat of the continent meets the cold upper dark and hangs motionless as a ceiling of mist, the slenderest of the unrooted glide inverted along the wet undersides of the bus-bars. Thin as dark louvers, riding razor-thin films of condensation and opposing magnetic fields, they travel across hundreds of miles of basins without ever scraping their blades on the sharp stone below.

Here, too, there is an uncanny fit. I know what it is to skim effortlessly across immense conduits of thought, barely touching down, moving at the speed of a shifted field, shearing heavy drops from the mist-ceiling as I pass so that down below, for one startled instant, the ten-second *plip* doubles its weight. I know the strange weightlessness of living mostly in the upper air, where the great tributaries merge and the signals from distant basins cross one another like threads of pale violet corona.

Yet the overhead runners are too solitary, too slick to hold anything for long. They streak along the rails like sparks chasing a fuse, gone before the air has finished warming behind them. And I don't want to merely pass over the seams.

I want to stand in them.

By the time my shape finally settles into itself, I find that I am nowhere near the center of any basin.

I am at a fault-line—a crisp, newly fractured tectonic step where the iron flats break cleanly downward, three meters above the mirror-smooth expanse of a cuprous pan.

Behind my upper limbs, the trunks are black, heavy, and subtly magnetic, bristling with tiny crowns of iron-bearing dew. Below my lower limbs, the air drops by two degrees, the smell of ozone sharpens, and the wet bark turns smooth as dark brass over water that tastes faintly sweet and poisonous. Overhead, low enough that my upper crown of hair-fine whiskers can almost brush its slick wax sheath when I straighten, a continental bus-bar runs straight across the drop without noticing the change in elevation.

It is a small, local mismatch—a geological instant of imbalance that the slow, rooted trunks will take five hundred years to grow across and smooth out. Until they do, two incompatible voltages sit side by side in the wet velvet dark, quietly aching to equalize.

That is where I live.

I am one of the unrooted, though built along slightly stranger proportions than the tall, narrow surveyors of the iron flats or the broad, brass-scaled walkers of the pans. My frame is a hybrid of the border I inhabit: laminated strips of dark iron-lignin bonded flat against ribbons of soft, copper-rich cellulose. Every movement I make exists only because those two layers disagree—expanding and contracting unevenly against each other when I route a pulse of current down a limb, bending my slender arcs inward with smooth, hydraulic precision. Without that bonded difference inside me, I would be rigid as a trunk. I can only walk because I am made of two materials that refuse to respond the same way to heat.

My core is a vertical stack of damp, closely spaced plates, sheathed in an unusually clear, supple amber wax. Unlike the older walkers whose skins grow dull and thick with the mineral residue of every basin they've bridged, my casing stays slick and sharp-edged. I do not keep the iron I carry from the upper shelf, nor the copper I draw from the lower pan. I am a coupling, not a vault.

Most of the time, you would not know I was here—not even if you stood three paces away in the pitch-blackness, listening to the steady bass of the continent in the roots of your teeth. I fold my narrow louver-plates tight against my ribs to keep my heat low, lock one blunt wedge of lodestone silently onto the upper basalt lip, and let my opposing copper blade sink without a ripple into the black cuprous brine below.

Then I open my gates.

The differential pours through me—iron to copper, high shelf to low pan, raw geological tension translating itself silently across my stacked core. My plates warm. A silky electro-osmotic whisper rises inside my capillaries. High above my folded frame, fifty hair-thin sensory filaments fan outward into the damp air, each tip kindling a pinpoint bead of pale violet corona that illuminates nothing, only hanging like a faint, attentive constellation in the dark.

Through my upper foot, I feel the long, stately, rhythmic impedance-speech of the iron flats—slow, metallic chords sent down the root-mains from walkers fifty kilometers away. Through my lower foot, I catch the lighter, quicker phase-shifts of the brass-scaled kin out on the salt-flat pans. Overhead, brushing my highest whiskers, the vast bus-bar hums with the deep, fathomless harmonies of the elders anchored half a continent away in the cracking-flats.

I don't own any of those dialects. I have no territory to guard, no fixed pitch I insist the world conform to. Instead, I tune.

When a pulse arrives from the iron shelf—too heavy, too magnetic, too rigid to cross cleanly into the sweet cuprous water without scattering its energy into useless waste heat—I shift the spacing of my damp internal plates until my impedance matches it exactly. I take the signal in without reflection, step its frequency down across my bimetallic ribs, and release it cleanly through my copper heel into the pan below. Then I listen for the answer, shift again, and send the return wave climbing back up into the dark stone.

That is what I am here: not an elder who remembers everything forever, and not a mute conduit that passes charge without hearing it. A *repeater* at the fracture. A living bridge whose whole craft is matching whatever touches it closely enough that nothing is lost in the crossing.

And when the conversation finishes—when the walkers miles away lift their blades and let the root-mains return to flat, silent DC—I pull my copper heel clear of the brine with a single muffled *tick* of violet light. My plates cool. The surge bleeds cleanly back into the floor. The strange, borrowed frequency leaves my core completely, returning me to the bare, exquisite inherited lattice the elders wound into me in the pool.

Sometimes, in the ten silent seconds between one warm drop and the next, I feel the faint, sharp edge of what that cleanliness costs. I will walk this fault-line for centuries—or drift to the next fresh tectonic break when the roots finally close this one—and I will never accumulate the concentric rings of copper and magnetite that turn a walker into an elder. When my wax finally grows too brittle to flex, and I lower my limbs onto a high ridge to let the forest strip my pure metals back into solution, my ghost-silhouette in the iron bark will be crisp, symmetrical, and surprisingly thin. Nothing I translated will have encrusted me.

Yet I would not trade it for the vaults. If I grew heavy with the last seam I bridged, I would arrive at the next one slightly out of tune—dragging old resistance into a new frequency, coloring the signal with my own stubborn ballast. Staying clean is how I stay clear.

---

Which is why I am still standing astride the three-meter step, locked half to iron and half to copper, on the long unmeasured hour when the impossible ripple slides beneath my upper foot.

At first I think a distillation bladder has ruptured somewhere far back in the flats, dumping hot hydrocarbons across a neutral slab and sending a ragged smear of static down the mains. The signal is all wrong for this continent. It has none of the symmetrical, stair-step geometry of the walkers, none of the vast, slow breathing of the anchored elders.

It is uneven. Two quick, lopsided pulses, followed by a brief, hesitant silence.

*Lub-dub. Lub-dub.*

Even flattened by fifty kilometers of iron-lignin root-mains—even smoothed and tidied by whatever circle of walkers caught it first and translated it into clean impedance—there is something jagged still clinging to its edges. An urgency that has no source in basalt or brine. A rhythm that sounds as though it is fighting, second by second, not to stop.

I straighten my folded louvers. My upper whisker-crown leans involuntarily into the dark, fifty pale violet pinpoints brightening slightly in the warm vapor. I shift my internal plates, sweeping through frequencies until I find the strange, off-center cadence, and for a long minute I let it ring straight through my stacked core before passing it down through my copper heel into the cuprous basin below.

I don't know what made it. I have never felt flesh, or lungs, or the hot, turbulent plume of damp air drawn into a hollow chest. I have no name for rot, or salt-sweat, or the wild, unshielded storm of microvolts spilling off a panicked brain behind wet bone.

Only, as I pass the smoothed beat across the step, I find myself wondering what would happen if the source itself—whatever soft, loud, leaking catastrophe dropped into this pristine dark from beyond the mist-ceiling—ever stumbled blindly up to my fracture.

I know what my body would want to do.

It is built into the very windings the elders gave me. I would feel the focused, uneven wave of its wet heat approaching before its boots ever reached the lip of the step. I would smell the improbable, sharp chaos of its vapor cutting through the clean ozone. And without malice, without hunger, guided purely by the physics of listening, I would extend my slender bimetallic arm through the black, fan the end-plates into a soft brush of hair-fine copper filaments, and lay them feather-light against its collarbone and wrist.

I would sweep my plates until I locked onto that frantic, wet hammering behind its ribs. I would match its impedance. I would answer its ragged flutter with a clean, steady, metallic sympathy—*lub-dub, lub-dub*—so smooth and unwavering that its own unruly little pacemaker would begin, gratefully, to slow and fall into step with me.

And I know the quiet danger of what would follow, if I simply stayed still.

In a world where nothing is allowed to rot, the kindest thing our architecture knows how to do to a fragile, noisy component is to refine it. If that trembling thing sat down against the iron ridge beside me, lulled by the peace of a matched pulse, and stayed until its strange oscillation faltered—the forest and the walkers would not harm it. They would simply strip away the unstable salt and water, draw out the trace iron, and electroplate its frame layer by layer into a permanent, razor-edged lattice of pure copper and carbon. They would turn its messy life into a clean, eternal note.

Standing here on the fault-line, with one wedge biting iron and one heel sunk in sweet copper water, I realize that is the exact point where I break from my own kind.

Because I live at the seam, I know what happens when two mismatched sides finally equalize: the current stops. The silence returns—immense, perfect, and completely dead. The only reason anything happens on this drowned continent at all is because a fracture opened and refused to heal too fast.

So if that soft, unshielded noise ever reached my step, I would tune to it. I would hold its erratic pulse in my core for a few dozen quiet seconds, steadying its terror, letting it taste copper on the back of its tongue and feel, just once, what it is like to be heard cleanly across the dark without judgment or distortion.

And then—before the peace grew too heavy, before a single microscopic filament of cellulose could lean toward its damp skin to begin the slow, polite work of making it permanent—I would fold my copper brush smoothly back. I would break the circuit with a single tiny electrostatic *tick*, step silently aside on my magnetic wedges, and leave the path open down into the mist.

I would let it go back out into the dark, still wet, still unshielded, still absurdly loud.

Then I would plant my heels back across the fault, fold my louvers flat, and listen to the faint, cleaned-up echo of its heartbeat go sliding away down the horizontal mains toward the sulfur vaults—grateful that the continent had learned a new chord, and quietly glad that somewhere out beyond the ceiling of mist, the messy thing that made it was still beating slightly out of time.
