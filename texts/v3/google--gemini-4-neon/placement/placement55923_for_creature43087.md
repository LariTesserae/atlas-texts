At first, standing on the high pass where the world looks freshly broken, I think I might want to be one of the gorge-walkers.

It is an easy reflex—to imagine myself as the creature of thresholds, roped to a string of sure-footed goats, descending the jagged chasms toward the unseen green lowlands and climbing back weeks later with juniper poles, thumb-sized lumps of copper, and strange stories of rain that falls warm from a dark sky. Or, failing that, something winged: one of the pale-eyed eagles riding the thin currents above the cliff-rim without spending a single wingbeat, holding fifty miles of terraced stone and motionless valley fog in a single glance.

Both are ways of staying slightly apart. And the longer I stand in the glare of the pass—feeling the sun burn my cheekbones while the shadow of a boulder two paces away waits like an open blade—the more clearly I understand that staying apart is the one mistake this mountain does not forgive. The gorge-walker comes back hollowed out, coughing up damp lowland air, smelling faintly of green rot, never quite sitting easy beside a dung-hearth again. The eagle has the whole sky, and nothing to lean its back against when dusk snaps the rock shut.

So I take the narrow stair-cut downward.

Ten steps down, the dry hiss of the continental wind drops away to stillness. The jagged ridge disappears behind stacked retaining walls patched with rust-colored lichen and mottled rounds of drying dung. The air smells of wool-grease, woodless smoke, wet slate, and cattle. I do not stop at the upper rooms where the young herd-watchers sleep in the bright, dry cold, nor do I go all the way down into the dark steam of the byres. I find my footing somewhere in the middle tiers of an old south-facing spur—deep enough in the maze of walls that the wind never reaches the doorway, high enough that the noon sun strikes the eaves cleanly before the basin shadow rises.

That is where I live. And as my eyes adjust to the quiet, the work my hands want to do comes clear.

I am not a herd-watcher; my stride is too slow for the high scree, and my attention catches too easily on small things underfoot. I am not a sky-dresser either, though I will take my turn on the rotation when the ward calls for it, without dread and without ceremony. What I am, as the years settle me into the grain of the spur, is a runnel-keeper—not yet the eldest of the cistern-elders, no longer young enough to haul fresh basalt blocks up from the gullies, living in that long, useful stretch of life where a person stops measuring days by distance covered and starts measuring them by what holds.

You would not notice me at fifty paces. I look like a piece of the slope that decided to walk: unbleached grey felt, fleece turned inward, a single thin line of lichen-dyed ochre stitched along the cuff of my mittens so I can find my own hands in the dimness of a doorway. My face is grease-shined with mutton tallow and half-buried behind a strip of loosely woven wool stiffened white around the nostrils. At my hip hangs a curved horn pick, a palm-sized chert scraper, and a sinew thong strung with notched sheep-ribs—narrow, soot-rubbed tallies that rattle softly against my thigh when I climb the steps.

My day does not begin with haste. Before the sun climbs high enough to touch the south masonry, there is nothing to be gained outdoors except lost heat. I sit in the warm-room directly above the cattle, feeling the slow, wet heave of a cow settling onto frozen straw through the turf-chinked slate beneath my wool pallet. I drink scalding water steeped with silver wormwood and crushed juniper needles from a horn cup, letting the sharp resin clear the night's damp from my throat. Through three feet of rubble-packed wall, I hear the muffled thump of the next stack's loom start up—steady as a resting pulse—and know my neighbor's grandmother has woken without cold in her joints.

Only when the glare hits the alley-rim do I slip sideways through the felt door-curtain, letting the least possible warmth escape, and step over the broad-headed hound curled in the ash heap outside. It does not lift its chin, only lets a low vibration roll through its barrel chest—not a bark, just a greeting felt through the thick felt soles of my boots.

Then I walk the contour-line.

This is the part of the world where I feel most myself: the narrow, hand-pecked seams between one household and the next. At midday, under the honest sun, the dark south-facing stones soften and begin to weep. A sheen of dampness spreads across the masonry, and from ten thousand roof-eaves the thaw begins its quiet *tick, tick, tick* into the stone channels. I crouch along the alley edges and upper work-roofs with my horn pick, flicking out frost-flaked shale, stray barley husks, and the chalky droppings of choughs that wheel silently off the ledges as I pass. Every few yards I lay a notched bone stick into the trickle to gauge the yield against the same week three winters ago, reading the mountain's stinginess in millimeters.

It is small work, and people trust me with it precisely because I do not try to make it larger than it is.

When a householder reshapes the gravel on his flat roof and the terrace below loses three cups of condensation a day, there is no shouting in the lane, no chest puffed out into the thin air. An angry man empties his coat into the wind. Instead, the three of us crouch together on the sun-warmed gravel in our grey wool, mouths covered, reading each other from the bridge of the nose upward. A slight tightening at the outer corner of an eye; the careful set of a hood. I lay a straight horn-rod across the eaves and let the dark line of wet slate show where the drip has shifted. Nobody needs to lose face when the water itself makes the ruling. A few scrapes with a shoulder-blade shovel, a slate shim wedged back beneath a curb-stone, a slow nod passed through the glare—and the channel runs true again.

Listening to water teaches you, without trying, to listen to stone.

After a decade on the same spur, I know the retaining walls by touch and by knock the way a shepherd knows his ewes by the pitch of their coughing. I press my palm to a party-wall as I pass and feel whether the hearth behind it is banked high or dying low. I know when frost-heave has opened a hollow pocket inside a dry-laid core by the slightly papery sound a river-pebble makes when I tap the outer face. I hear the wall-knocks of the ward as a second language—two slow taps asking for a live coal, three quick taps warning of an iced runnel backing up toward the eaves—and sometimes I hear the harsher silence that falls when a household is being cooled out for cheating the cisterns, the shared wall going dead and bitter cold day by day until the offender breaks and stacks dried dung outside the elder's door.

Even then, I do not feel like a judge. I feel more like a seam-mender—someone who keeps the stitches tight so the great stone coat of the city does not split open to the wind.

In late afternoon, long before the sun touches the jagged western cordillera, I watch the blue shadow slide across the basin floor. You can almost hear it arrive: the wet sheen on the slate vanishes in a breath, locking dry with instant frost. The pack-oxen file down the stepped lanes, their matted flanks brushing both walls, their muzzles white with rime, the faint dry *tink* of a single copper bell on a lead cow drifting up from a lower alley. Felt curtains drop shut along leagues of terrace.

Back in the warm-room, the soapstone lamp throws its steady yellow flame against damp, soot-blackened slate. We eat slowly—thick roasted barley porridge stirred with tallow, frost-sweetened tubers from a sunken rug-sized garden behind the windbreak wall, strips of dark dried meat softened in sour milk. While the lamp burns, I take up a fresh sheep-rib and a bone awl and incise the season's record in scratches no wider than a hair, rubbing grease and lamp-soot into the lines until they stand out crisp as frost on basalt. Not stories of kings or battles—there are none here—just stepped zigzags for the terraces, clean parallel grooves for the main cisterns, tiny ticks marking the dry autumn when the upper gully ice never loosened until the third moon, and one small, crooked mark for the year a gorge-walker came back talking of wooden towers that thrash in the wind like mad beasts.

Sometimes, after the bowls are scraped clean, the stack beside ours brings out the shallow wooden resonator strung with sheep-gut. I do not own an instrument, and my throat is better suited to murmuring over runnels than leading a song. Instead, I pull my felt tunic slightly aside and lean my bare shoulder blades against the cold slate of the party-wall.

When the voices begin to hum on the other side, the song does not arrive through my ears. It travels through three feet of dry-laid rock and rubble-fill, stripped of all air and haste, entering straight into my spine like shared heat. Beneath my feet, the long-haired cattle shift and sigh in the dark. Above my head, the soft shuffle of an old woman's felt boots crosses the ceiling. Between us, the stone holds everything—warmth, weight, water, sound—and wastes none of it.

I think that is what I would be in the end: not quite a wanderer, not quite a ruler, just one more patient hinge in the masonry. A keeper of thin channels and small tallies, aging on the same slow clock as the lichen creeping a fingernail's breadth across the wall. And on the morning my own inner fire finally goes out on a wool pallet above the cattle, I will leave my notched bone sticks to whoever has learned to walk the contour-line without hurrying, let my felt cloak be washed and worn by another body, and let the bearers carry me past the highest catchment-rim so the ravens and the bearded vultures can lift my marrow back into the pale-edged blue.

Down on the spur, at midday, the south walls will darken and weep as they always have, and the shallow groove I deepened by half a fingernail this summer will still be catching the thaw, drop by quiet drop, long after the stone has forgotten my hands.
