At first, looking down from the pewter-smooth lip of the basin, the easiest thing to want is height.

To be one of the pale-grey wind-hunters circling above the three hollow drums—riding the invisible ridges where the updrafts meet the shelf-wind, never touching the damp stone, seeing the whole quarter-mile grid at once as clean geometry and open sky. There is a cold clarity to that. You would never have to crowd into a dark gallery or wait out the mist. You would only read the air, fold your long narrow wings, and drop down a dead-air shaft when something moved.

Except the longer I listen to the basin, the less I want to look *at* it from above. Sight is the flattest sense here. The sky is too bright, the avenues too scrubbed and blinding; from two hundred feet up, the town looks finished, like a diagram scratched into bone. The real life of the place isn’t in the diagram. It is in the pressure against the skin, and the slow negotiation between moving air and stubborn matter.

So I drop off the ridge, down past the ragged green crowns of the towers, and test the other obvious refuge: the base of the tallest drum.

Inside that arched wind-gap, with a palm laid flat against the worn, ivory-smooth lime, it is almost too easy to imagine staying forever. Becoming one of the great Listeners—motionless for days while the cloud-fronts roll through and clear, holding the entire acoustic weight of the city in the twin drums behind my jaws. Listening to twenty thousand dead ancestors glazed in silica above my head, each skull holding its single cold, clean pitch until my own pulse slows to match the bass. There is an immense mercy in that kind of stillness. No argument, no hurry, no wasted motion. Just the primary chord, unbroken.

And yet, even there, something in me wouldn’t settle.

Because if I stayed in the central tower, my attention wouldn’t rest on the pure note. It would keep snagging on the hairline flaws inside it. The faint, dry rasp where needle-ice is beginning to loosen on an upper sill. The fractional hesitation in the updraft three minutes before the first white plume feathers over the western lip. Most of all, that brief, milky stutter when the cloud-wall finally spills into the bowl—the three or four heartbeats when heavy, wet air floods the dry avenues, the pitch drops a half-step, and the standing waves lose their footing.

That is where I find my body in this place. Not in the clean center, and not in the sky above it.

***

My chest is dense and barrel-deep for the thin air, my skin slate-grey and oiled against the damp, my shoulders hunched under a heavy mantle of grazer hide—tallow-waxed side inward to hold warmth, the outer face felted thick with dark moss that drinks the noon sun until it steams. I have no outer ears to catch the frost. Behind my cheekbones, two shallow hollows of taut pale membrane dimple inward when the pressure dips. Along the backs of my forearms, sparse stiff filaments lift and shiver the instant I push my hand half an inch too far out from the lee of a wall.

I am one of the seam-dwellers—not a hunter swinging hand-over-hand beneath the wet arches during the cistern thunder, and not one of the oldest Listeners who sit at the heart of the central drum.

I live out on the eastern margin, in the last intact colonnade before the scar.

The elders bring the children out here once or twice during their growing, during the steaming ten-minute intervals after a cloud-front clears, when the fingernail-sized star-blossoms crank open along the parapets. They press the children’s small, uncallused palms against the cold bedrock where the arches turn ragged and stunted, so they can feel the ugly, fluttering dissonance still shaking the schist where someone long ago walled off a wind-gap too greedily and threw the gale sideways through a whole quarter. The elders let the children feel the broken wave shudder up their wrists, let them see the pale mineral rubble scoured down to bare pewter stone, and then lead them quickly back into the moss-padded quiet of the inner galleries.

They treat the eastern edge as a lesson—a finished warning carved in ruin.

I stay because a wound in moving air does not sit still.

Everyone knows the law: *never break the primary chord.* What fewer people want to sit beside is the slow, grinding aftermath once a chord has already been torn. A ragged eddy worries at the edges of whatever stands next to it. Every black-frost night, water freezes inside the porous lime of the stunted arches; every bright noon, needle-ice shears another flake away. The scouring draft shifts a thumb’s width to the west, then two, tasting the mortarless ribs of the first healthy gallery that still holds warm alcoves and sleeping kin.

Someone has to live along that bruised border and listen to the wrong sound until they know its grain.

My sleeping hollow is smaller and colder than the deep halls beneath the forum cisterns. The moss packed into the chalk-white ribs here never dries quite completely, and the translucent waxed-gut diaphragm stretched across my wind-slit never settles into a clean hum. Instead it buzzes—a shallow, uneven thrum that rattles gently against the bones of my face when I lie down at night. Most of the others hate it; they say an unsteady wall gives you fractured dreams and tightens the membranes behind your jaws until you lose your pitch.

I have grown weirdly fond of it. A clean wall tells you that everything is holding. A wall that shudders tells you *how* it is holding, and where it is beginning to yield.

My days are measured in cloud-breaths, like everyone’s, though I use the stutter differently. When the pressure drops and my cheek-drums pull tight—before the luminous white curtain even crests the western rim—I leave the moss-curtained slit of my gallery and crouch flat against the last whole arch. As the wet air pours over the lip and the standing waves blur into four heartbeats of milky confusion, I slip out across the scoured eastern stone while the pale-grey birds are forced high above the cloud-deck.

I don’t hunt out here; no grazer is foolish enough to crawl across bedrock that rattles with bad vibration. I move fast, soaked cloak slapping heavily against my calves, trailing my callused palm along the broken stumps of the ruined quarter. I am reading what the frost took since the last shift. A palm-sized chunk of petrified stalk has sheared off the leeward corner of the third stump, exposing the porous, dead marrow inside. Before the new, lower chord can lock back into place and slam the gale across my ribs, I gather two lengths of broken cane and slide back into the shelter of my seam.

Then the cloud-wall passes. Hard blue sky snaps overhead. My secondary eyelids flick shut automatically as the wet colonnades burst into chimes of shattering needle-ice.

That is when the real work begins.

I kneel in the shadowed crease where intact gallery meets broken stone, bracing one broad foot against the polished schist, and press the heavy bone of my jaw straight onto the calcified wall. I close my eyes against the blinding glare of the avenues and let the vibration rise through my teeth.

Down in the central forum, the bass thrum is round and immense. Out here, it frays at the edges—a thin, irritable flutter riding just underneath the fifth. I listen for twenty minutes, through the brief bronze dance of the glacier-midges over the steaming moss, waiting to see whether that missing chunk of petrified stalk on the third stump has changed the shear-line.

I extend my forearm slowly outward from the shelter of the pillar.

A quarter-inch. The pale filaments along my wrist hang slack.

Half an inch. Still slack.

Three-quarters—and they snap backward violently, flattened against my oiled skin by an invisible wall of rushing cold. Last winter, the edge sat a full thumb’s width farther out. The gale has crept inward by the thickness of a knuckle.

I withdraw my arm. The filaments drop limp.

I would never dare wedge a bone-pin into the gap on my own. That is how the eastern quarter died in the first place—impatience, someone thinking they understood a corner of the air without listening to the whole bowl. Instead, I turn my face back to the smooth interior lime of the colonnade, press my flat palm alongside my jaw, and speak down the nodal seam.

A tight sequence of dry tongue-clicks—sharp as frost-cracks—followed by three low throat-hums that climb a fraction short of a fifth, and a rapid pattern of knuckle-taps against the hollow rib.

The sound does not enter the open street; the gale two feet to my left would shred it instantly. It runs straight down the calm column beside the wall and through the porous bone-work itself, riding the grid of the town all the way toward the central forum.

Then I wait.

Sometimes an answer comes back within an hour, between one cloud-front and the next. More often, if what I have reported touches a deep harmonic, nothing returns for three days while the old Listeners inside the central tower sit motionless and test the shift against the weight of the whole basin.

During those quiet intervals, I sit in the pale, pearly dimness of my gallery, scraping down a wind-hunter’s wing-bone with a splinter of horn. It takes a long time to prepare a reed properly—paring the inner walls thin without cracking the rigid shell, notching the lip so it catches the draft cleanly instead of whistling, braiding fresh dark fern-fiber and rubbing it thick with rendered grazer tallow until it shines like wet slate. Most of the bones I shape will never be used in my lifetime, or will be rejected because the angle is off by a hair. They sit in neat bundles tucked into the dry upper ribs of my alcove, smelling faintly of wax and cold chalk.

And on the rare morning when the wall finally answers—a slow, resonant hum traveling up from the forum through the soles of my feet, steady and deliberate, carrying the Listeners’ consent and the exact pitch they want back—that is the closest thing to joy this cold place knows.

Waiting for the calm hush at the tail of a cloud-change, I climb out along the leeward lip of the last intact arch, clinging flat as moss so my body never becomes a sail. I reach into the chewed wind-gap where the scouring eddy has begun to eat inward, wedge the notched sliver of bird-bone between two petrified stalks, and lash it tight with waxed fern-cord.

Nothing dramatic happens. The wind does not bow. The eastern ruin does not mend.

Only, when the clear blue sky snaps back and the high flute-howl locks into place, the irritable flutter against my cheekbones drops away by a fraction of a tone. I extend my wrist into the seam. Half an inch. Three-quarters. A full thumb’s width—the filaments tremble, hold, and fall calm.

In twenty winters, if no storm tears the lashing loose, freezing mist will settle inside that sliver of new stillness. Pale fibrous stalks will lean out from the intact wall, wicking moisture, glazing themselves in frost-lime and silica, braiding a tiny curved brow over the bruised stone. Maybe in a century, when I am long gone, the third stump will begin to grow a new arch. I will never see the eastern quarter whole. My whole craft is simply keeping the tear from widening, and nudging the air by two millimeters toward patience.

***

Living at a boundary makes you listen differently to anything that does not belong.

When a heavy, soft outsider climbs down over the smooth western rim and steps into the bowl—too loud, too upright, wrapped in snapping cloth—I feel it even out on the eastern margin. Not the boots on the stone at first, just the sudden, dull flattening of an overtone that travels down the colonnades like a cold draft through a closed chamber.

Deep inside the forum foundations, behind the curtains of glossy black-green fern, the others press their hands to the trembling lime and wait in humming stillness for the awkward shape to grow cold and leave.

I wait too. I have no desire to step out into the open street and show myself to something that walks straight down the middle of the gale, deaf to the walls of air it keeps colliding with. Yet pressed flat in the moss-lined shadow of my eastern arch, cheek-drums stretched tight, I find myself listening to the stranger’s acoustic shadow with an odd, quiet fascination.

Because a foreign body dropped into an instrument teaches you the shape of the instrument. Every time the stranger shifts inside the doorway of the central tower, breaking a minor eddy and damping a high harmonic, the basin answers around them—revealing seams and overtones that are usually buried inside the clean chord. For ten minutes, while the cloud-wall gathers beyond the western lip, the whole city sounds briefly unfamiliar, and my wrist-filaments prickle with the sheer strangeness of an unmeasured note.

Then the white curtain spills over the rim, the pitch drops its half-step, and sooner or later the dull shadow lifts back up the smooth gneiss and vanishes onto the empty plateau. The note clears. The town forgets.

I stay pressed to the wall a little longer, remembering the exact shape of the flat tone—the way I remember every frost-crack, every shifted stump, every small disruption that makes the chord feel alive rather than merely ancient.

***

And I know where I will end, because nobody in this bowl leaves without entering the walls.

When my dense chest finally slows past the night-wind’s bass, and my hands can no longer feel the hair’s-breadth edge between calm and shear, they will carry me down to the warm, thundering cistern-halls beneath the forum. My flesh will go back to the dark emerald moss and the damp channels where the crimson rosette shoots push up after the mist. My tallow-cloak will be scraped, re-oiled, and fitted to someone younger whose wrist-filaments are still sharp.

My skull they will wash in cold cloud-water. They will seal the deep eye-sockets and vertical nasal slits with pale frost-lime, open the thin bone behind the jaws, and wait for the hush of a cloud-change to carry it up the damp interior ribs of the tallest tower.

I don’t think they would bind me down in the thick, ivory-smooth middle tiers, among the oldest ancestors who hold the broad, unwavering foundation of the chord. My bone will have spent too many winters pressed against a trembling wall to sit easily in the deepest bass.

Higher up, I think—near the upper perforated slots where the petrified stalks give way to the ragged, living green crown reaching into the blue sky. Up where the wild shelf-wind first hits the rim of the cylinder, still rough from the empty plateau, before it has been coaxed down into the choir.

Decades after my eyes are sealed shut, the colonial flora will braid its pale husks over my brow and glaze the dome in luminous white silica, leaving only the tuned mouth behind my jaw open to the draft. I will hold one thin, high, watchful overtone at the top of the drum—the pitch that shivers first when mist crests the western lip, the pitch that flickers when a stone falls on the eastern margin, the pitch that bends by a fraction of a tone when something warm and clumsy steps into the doorway and briefly breaks the air.

Still keeping the edge. Still listening for the moment the wind changes its mind.
