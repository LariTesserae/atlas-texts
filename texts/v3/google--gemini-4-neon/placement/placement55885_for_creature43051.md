At first, I think I would be drawn to the flat, chalk-pale things clinging under the cantilevered stairs.

There is something instantly recognizable in how they live: utterly blind in the cold gloom, folded tight against the underside of a vault, waiting in complete stillness until a water-bucket drops three alleys away and the booming shockwave lights up the geometry of the corridor for three brief seconds. To see the world only inside the flash of an echo—to read every ledge, hub, and skittering movement purely from the shape of rebounding sound, and then return to the dark before the reverberation dies—feels strangely familiar. Almost too familiar.

Yet if I stay with that image for more than a moment, it begins to feel thin. The plaster-glider only listens in order to snatch something off a pulley-wheel and vanish back to the cornice. It doesn’t care whether the vault it clings to rings true or sags flat. It lives off the valley’s clamor without ever answering it.

I would want to answer it.

And so I look downward first, through the horizontal floor grates where the banked ember-red breathes up into the mist. Down there, the heavy ones are hacking bituminous shale from the basin floor and kneading green clay with spade-broad hands. There is an immense dignity in what they become—turning slowly to horn and stone, excavating the earth beneath their own feet so the roofs can rise, giving even their dried tendons to haul the counterweights after their joints lock solid. I would revere them. I would press my palm to the warm paving grates just to feel the muffled *boom* of their basalt poles driving heat up through the mortar.

Still, I know I would not thicken like that. I don’t have the marrow for the fire-boxes, or the brute patience to pry raw rock from the dark. If I began down in the nurseries above the kiln-arches—pressed belly-down against a humming brick wall while an elder tapped the distinct voices of basalt, slate, and curing lime beside my skull—I would learn the notes long before I ever cared for the weight of the stone.

For a long time, I think I would live in the wet middle tiers as a climber, specifically one of the quiet ones drawn less to the exposed gables and more to the blind interior chambers.

I can feel the exact satisfaction of that work. Sleeping upright inside the double-skinned core of a tower, toes hooked into a horizontal seam, bare sternum flat against the cool brickwork so the whole basin’s pulse runs straight through my ribs. Never waking to the roar—the roar is simply breath, simply the world continuing—only snapping awake when a hoist-line parts two terraces over and a cold gap of silence opens where a splash of slaking lime ought to have landed.

And in the working shifts: not hauling the heavy chalk blocks up the greased ramps, not even laying the long outer retaining walls, though I would take my turn when an arch needed four pairs of hands to seat its keystone and hammer the copper shim home. My real attention would go to the hollow rooms. Lifting narrow-necked terracotta amphorae out of the clay slings, checking each lip with a fingernail for hairline cracks that would buzz instead of sing, setting them in mortared tiers and angling their dark mouths a fraction of a thumb toward the alley outside.

It is fussy, obsessive work. You flick every slack cord before you touch it, listening for the dry *ping* of braided sinew rather than the wet thud of a coiled ribbon-worm. You pause mid-course when a bucket trips half a mile off, head tilted, ear-slits flared wide, tracing a tiny ragged hiss buried inside the returning chord—a mortar-thread tunneling through a fresh seam—and you feel a sharp, clean relief as you wedge the brick back out and pack the wound with scorching dry quicklime until the joint heals into pure ring.

If I could, I might try to stay in those damp alleys forever. There is a clean honesty in having a trowel-hand, in fixing a flat note directly with lime and fired clay.

Except the valley does not leave a body unchanged.

Decades of listening inside the amphora-chambers, of gauging damp vaults by the vibration in the breastbone, do something different to the one in twenty who never sinks toward the kilns. I would notice it first not as an elevation, but as a quiet failing. A morning—if you can call any shift beneath that unchanging pewter sky a morning—when the calcified bosses on the heels of my wrists stopped thickening and began to feel oddly light, almost dry. A shift when the cold mist sank deeper into my elbows than the lime-dust could seal out, and a cantilevered slate tread felt suddenly too narrow, too slick with flint-chips.

My hands would betray the trade first. The broad, caliper-wide palms narrowing, the digits curling gently inward until they could no longer span a wet brick or hold a dry-stone crib steady against the push of an arch. That would ache—not only in the brittle joints, but in the narrower sense of being turned away from the mortar. To hear a sour seam two alleys down and know my own wrists would crack if I tried to wedge it.

While the hands shrink, though, the chest opens.

The ribs would bow slowly outward, thinning and hollowing until my own body began to resemble the chambers I used to build—light as unfired shard, pale hide pulled tight as cured membrane over a dry cavity, ear-slits lengthening down the angle of my jaw like fluted ceramic scrolls. By the time I climbed out of the roaring middle tiers for good, I would no longer belong to the damp lanes or the splash of the slaking troughs. The cold mist down there would make my bones rattle; a slip on wet basalt would shatter me like dropped tile.

So I would find myself high up, where the air is slightly thinner and the brickwork dries pale with salt-bloom: on the baffling staircases that climb out of a corridor to a tiny, roofless landing and immediately descend back into the same dark lane.

To a stranger passing through with oil-dishes and boots, those double flights look like madness, or masonry begun and forgotten. Standing on one of them, though, the whole sunken city finally stops being a maze and becomes an instrument.

Each narrow landing sits at a crossing of echoes—the exact crest where the rebounding voices of three or four terraced districts meet without trampling one another. I would climb slowly up the western treads, pausing to catch my balance against the dry basalt parapet, keeping my curled fingers tucked out of the damp. From the crook of my thin arm I would draw out the slender rod of hammered copper.

One tap against the sharp, unweathered lip of the stone.

Not a boom. The valley has enough booms—endless counterweights slamming down shafts, slate chutes clattering like falling roofs, kilns thumping beneath the paving. What leaves the copper rod is barely larger than a needle: a single, bright *ting* that slips cleanly underneath the thunder and flies out across the terraced dark.

And then I do nothing at all, except become hollow enough to receive what comes back.

First the fluted roof-chutes directly below answer in a quick, breathy sigh. A heartbeat later, the amphora-vaults I helped pack thirty years ago wake up two tiers down, humming in a warm, low third. Farther out, buried deep inside the older quarters, the sealed domes catch the tremor and send back a thin thread of pitch that lingers a fraction of a second past everything else. My whole swollen ribcage hums softly in sympathy, holding the layered chord as it swells and decays.

Almost always, something is off. A freshly raised cistern to the north is still too wet with uncured plaster and swallows the upper harmonic; a blind gable four alleys east has been run three courses too high, bending the return note flat against the rim of the basin. I don’t see those walls—I have no eyes to see them—and I don’t calculate them in numbers. I feel the mismatch as a faint, sour pinch behind my collarbone, a knot in the geometry of the air.

This is where I would actually live: not on the landing alone, and not down in the grout, but in the slow walk down the eastern flight between the two.

I would descend carefully, sheltering for a breath inside the mouth of a dry vault when a nearby hoist dumps its load and sends a plume of hot chalk-steam rolling over the roofs. Then I would find the climber closest to the flat quarter—crouched on a toothed wall, damp with grey clay, ear-slits angled toward the dark. I wouldn’t startle them; they would feel the dry, light shift of my sound-shadow long before I reached the ledge.

I would lay my frail, curled fingers lightly against their broad, lime-crusted shoulder.

*Tock. Tock-tock. Skritch.*

Not commands—just a quiet, intricate map tapped straight through hide into bone. *Three alleys over, the corner drinks the note. Raise the buttress by four courses; hollow the core; pack six jars facing the cistern; burn the seam below the second weep-hole.* They have the hard wrists, the hooked toes, the wet lime-tray swinging up on braided sinew. I have only the wide ribcage and the memory of how the four terraces sounded together from the crest. Neither of us holds the valley alone. They listen without turning their narrow skull, absorb the pattern through their shoulder, give one tiny, solid nod of the jaw—and reach back into the grout.

Sometimes, resting in the shelter of an upper parapet between tap and answer, I would think about the smooth, doorless domes buried deeper in the city’s fabric.

I know what waits at the far end of hollowing out. Eventually even the western stairs grow too steep; the salt-wind off the roofs aches too sharply in papery bone; a whole quarter finally settles into a clean, unblemished chord that has no more dead corners left to map. Then the climbers build the seamless vault around a dry plinth of unslaked lime, and leave the oculus open just wide enough for a brittle body to slip through before they mortar the crown shut from the outside.

I don’t think I would fear that. There is a strange, austere peace in the thought of the dry lime drinking the last dampness away—no rot, no crumbling, cured crisp as fired clay inside the dark, suspended forever at the exact frequency of a finished room. Even after the hands can no longer tap a shoulder and the rod has been passed to another thinning mason, you would still be answering. Every time a counterweight dropped half a mile away, your quiet ribcage would catch the tremor and hold the district to pitch for one breath longer than bare stone could manage on its own.

Still, I wouldn’t want the oculus closed yet.

Not while the valley is still half-finished. Not while the stairs stop dead against toothed walls waiting for the next course, and the pewter gloom overhead holds its cool mist, and—once in a long, uncounted stretch of shifts—that other thing rises from far beneath the deepest shale-mines.

That is the moment I would stay light and awake for.

When it comes, you don’t hear it with your ear-slits at all. You feel it climb straight up through the basalt treads into your soles, a slow, vast swell that ripples the milky runoff troughs, shakes a dry snow of alkaline salt off the raw bricks, and sets every sealed elder-dome across miles of terraced gloom humming together in one low, immense, unfinished interval.

Up on my narrow dead-end landing, I would drop to my knees and press my drum-tight chest flat against the cold stone of the parapet. All across the sunken miles below, the climbers freeze with mortar dripping from their trowels; the stokers lean motionless on their poles in the red dark. For ten long heartbeats, nothing moves. The great tremor rises through my hollow bones, hangs ragged and unanswered against the half-built slopes of the basin, and slowly sinks back into the earth.

We don’t know the final note. Nobody carries the whole chord in their skull—not the stokers in the shale, not the climbers in the mist, not the cured ribs humming inside the blind domes, and certainly not me on my two-step ledge. We only know when the valley breaks flat against it.

Then, somewhere down in the dark, a water-heavy terracotta bucket reaches its tipping weight. The dead-sinew rope groans taut; the basalt catch-peg snaps free; thirty gallons of cold condensation crash into a quicklime trough in a furious hiss of steam. Down in the alleys, a thousand calcified wrists begin to tap again.

I push myself slowly upright on the narrow crest, feel the wet echo of the basin settle back into its uneven roar, lift the slender copper rod between my curled fingers, and strike the parapet once more—listening with everything hollow in me for what still needs to be built.
