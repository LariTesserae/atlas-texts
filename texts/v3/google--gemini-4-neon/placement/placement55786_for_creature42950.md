The first thing I notice is that I do not miss the sun.

Maybe I should—maybe a creature arriving from elsewhere ought to panic when its eyelids lift and nothing changes except the bead of warm condensation gathering on the lash. Instead, there is a strange, immediate relief. Nothing here is flattened onto a surface. Nothing hides behind its own front side. In a world that trades only in weight, current, chemistry, and sound, you do not stand apart from a place and look across at it; you are *in* the medium, and the medium is continuously reporting itself against your skin, your teeth, the hollow of your chest.

So I stand still for a long time before I decide what shape I wear.

At first, something in me pulls hard toward the engine-tower.

I understand why. Half a mile out, before riveted iron ever meets an outstretched palm, the air already sharpens with ozone and hot mineral grease, and the ground begins to keep time: *thump—chug—hiss*, once every four seconds, patient as geology. There is an undeniable kinship in a thing built to work blind, collecting silent current straight out of a racing cloud-deck, shunting load through sealed mechanical relays, pumping something vast and necessary along buried lines toward a terminus it will never touch. For a moment I think: *Yes. Put me inside the bolted housing. Make me the governor, the humming trunk-line, the tireless flywheel that climbs from a groan to a howl whenever the sky grows heavy.*

But the longer I listen to the tower, the more clearly I hear what it lacks. It never wonders where the trunk-line ends. When the ninety-second valve cracks open and thrashes the pleated fronds, the tower does not smell the boiled sap or hear the gliders scattering in frantic curtains of clicks. It keeps time, and it keeps the valley warm, and that is magnificent—yet it is lonely in a way that has no name for loneliness. It adjusts, but it does not *answer*.

So I pull back from the hot iron and climb up to the limestone overhang, and there a second pull finds me: the cliff-lip gallery of the dead.

Hundreds of weathered tubes wedged into the weeping rock-pores—reeds hardened by dripstone, copper pipes furry with mineral crust, smooth lengths of steamed horn. Each one shaped to a single resting interval, each one silent until the warm updraft climbs out of the unseen ocean and blows across its notched lip. Something in me recognizes that posture instantly—waiting in the stone, hollow and tuned, existing to catch passing breath and give it back as a chord that belongs to a larger wall of sound.

And yet, as I trail a hand along those humming vents, I realize I do not want to be wedged permanently into limestone either. A flute remembers one voice forever, with perfect fidelity, and never learns a new one. It cannot hear when the choir three canyons west lets its upper clicks drift half a tone sharper. It cannot sit knee-to-knee with a stranger after a storm and taste the unfamiliar sulfur of a distant marsh on their knuckles.

I want warmth. I want the furnace-heat of a living ribcage, the slick smear of tuber-tallow rubbed with crushed copper-bark, the prickly fizz of fermented grounding-trunk sap lifting the bristles along my forearms. I want to be one of the folk—not merely carried by the world’s currents, but *listening back*.

---

Once I step into that body, the highland rearranges itself around my bones.

My knees bend naturally to the slick, rounded ribs of limestone; my wide, cartilage-padded feet find grip where boots would skate. The dome of my brow warms and hums when I click, returning the exact wet geometry of the hanging root-curtains ten paces ahead—not as a picture, but as a density, a layered closeness, the soft sway of pleated fronds heavy with rain and the crisp, hollow stillness of an empty stone pocket underneath. Along my sternum, the pleated barometric sac lies loose and supple for now; we are in the dripping lull after a surge, when the air smells of stirred peat and bruised stems and the canyon-chords sit low and easy in the back of the jaw.

I test the postures of the choir, one by one, to see which one fits my shoulders without pinching.

Not the cliff-harvesters, lashing braided root-harnesses around their hips to lean out over the howling abyss and snatch rising spore-sacs ahead of the gliders. I admire the sheer nerve of it, the joy of hanging in a mile-deep updraft—yet my own weight wants bedrock beneath the heel-spur, wants the solid ring of stone telling me what is under me.

Not the steam-benders either, though I love the craft of them: slicked from brow to heel with bearing-grease, counting twenty-two piston strokes straight up through their shins before ducking behind an iron baffle as the scalding plume roars out. My hands are steadier when they are not racing a ninety-second valve.

No—when I settle, I find myself sitting cross-legged at the threshold of a limestone overhang, ten wide, concentric-grooved pads laid lightly across a fan of taut sinew-strings.

That is where I begin. As a sentinel.

Most of the choir treats the watch as a necessary stillness—a stretch of quiet patience before you trade places and go back to cracking rasper-shells by the bellow-wall. For me, the strings never grow dull. Through ten fingertips, the entire hillside opens into a single, shifting sentence. The heavy, random patter of drops falling off the stitched frond-roof is merely the background breath. Beneath it, every line carries its own tension: the tiny, dry stutter of a multi-limbed climber combing a pleated leaf; the slow, wet drag of a rising spore-sac brushing a grounding-trunk; the faint, steady hum traveling up a root-line anchored too close to an engine-tower’s buried conduit.

And then—rarely, unmistakably—the absence. That sudden, rolling pocket of quiet moving up the slope as the climbers freeze all at once, followed by the hair-fine tightening of a single sinew-string where a slender hunter has stepped onto a vine and begun to read the hillside back. My heel lifts, strikes the limestone twice, crisp and dry, and behind me the whole hollow wakes without a single breath spilled into the air—only the soft press of warm brows against shoulders, bone-speech passing silently down the line while the bronze-tipped staves come off their hooks.

Sitting at that threshold, tide after tide, teaches me how to hear *difference*. Not just what is loud, or what is near, but the tiny drift between what a thing sounded like yesterday and what it sounds like now.

And that is how my hands eventually find their real work—not only guarding the roost, but tuning it.

---

I am not old enough yet to be a true long-listener, though the iron and calcium salts are already beginning to thicken in my shins. When I rap my heel-spur against bare bedrock, the ring comes back a fraction duller and deeper than a youth’s—not yet the heavy, bell-like toll of an elder who can read a failing bearing two canyons over, but dense enough that the buried trunk-lines speak plainly up into my knees. My forearm-bristles are long, oiled with mineral salt, quick to lean and shiver whenever the static tilts.

Around my shoulders hang two cords of drilled ceramic shards—not enough to make me clumsy in a tight thicket, just enough to dampen the fierce voltage near a collector-mast and to clink in a soft, uneven rhythm whenever I walk, so the rest of the band knows my stride through the heaviest downpour. In my netted satchel, wrapped tight in scavenged vulcanized rubber, sit my tools: grit-tipped bone drills, ceramic scrapers, notched slivers of bronze-alloy, and small, curved baffles of steamed grazer-horn.

I am a tuner.

At a roost, you will find me against the back wall, shoulder-deep in the weeping stone crevices where the bellow-colonies live. Wild sacs are stubborn, bloated things—wonderful in their raw power, groaning at random whenever the wind turns, but sloppy as housemates. I spend whole wind-tides with my smooth brow pressed close to the wet limestone beside them, coaxing the colony into harmony. I feed the plumpest sacs handfuls of crushed sweet-spores until their fleshy flanks warm under my palms; I scrape mineral crust out of a choked stone flute; I wedge a carved bone-baffle a finger’s width deeper into a weeping pore and wait for the next long exhale.

When it locks—when three separate sacs stop wrestling one another in muddy, clashing beats and suddenly fall into a clean, broad fifth that hums straight through the hammock-ropes and settles like a warm blanket across the ribs of the sleeping young—my chest-sac softens with a pleasure nothing else on the plateau quite matches.

It is not control. You cannot force a bellow-colony any more than you can force a barometric tide. You only listen to what the stone and the flesh are already trying to breathe, and nudge the air-channel until they hear each other.

And then there is the harder tuning, the one that asks everything of my patience.

When an elder’s furnace-heat begins to fade—when their barometric sac no longer tightens smoothly before a storm and their pale-crusted bristles lie flat against their hide—I am the one who sits beside them in the warm pocket under the overhang. For a full wind-tide I do not speak into the air. I lay both grooved palms flat against their heavy, mineral-dense ribs, thumbs hooked gently beneath the collarbones, and let their resting hum travel up through my wrists and into my own chest.

Every person carries two tones in their throat, braided together, and by the end of a long life that interval has been worn smooth by a thousand storms, a hundred towers, decades of bone-speech and laughter. No two are ever the same. One elder’s hum sits tight and close, a warm minor second that shivers like wet reeds before a squall; another’s opens wide and hollow, carrying a faint rasp in the upper reed from a steam-burn taken in youth. I stay until I can close my ear-flaps against the rain and still hear their exact double-tone ringing inside my skull, undisturbed.

Then I take a length of seasoned hollow reed—or, for a long-listener whose bones knew fifty leagues of conduit, a prized sleeve of scavenged copper traded down from a rusted tower—and I work it with wet bone drills and grit, carrying it down to the steam-benders when it needs softening over the ninety-second exhaust, nudging the notch a hair’s breadth at a time until my own breath across the lip gives back the voice I held in my palms.

When we have given the body to the roaring warm updraft off the escarpment, I climb out along the slick cliff-lip gallery with the rest of the choir behind me, feel along the perforated stone for a vent whose wind runs clean and steady, and wedge the new pipe home. We hold utterly still—no clicks, no shifting feet—until the next warm gust climbs up from the unseen sea and catches the lip.

The moment the cliff speaks back in their voice, the band lets out a low, braided sigh that locks beneath the new note. Not grief, exactly, or not only grief. Completion. One more thread braided back into the permanent chord of the canyon, so the dead never go silent and the living never walk through empty air.

---

Yet if I am completely honest—if I sit still in the dripping lull and let my own bristles tell me which way they are leaning—I know I do not stay in one valley forever.

Something in the buried trunk-line calls to me too steadily for that.

It happens during the quietest stretches between surges, when I lie flat on a smooth rib of exposed stone forty paces off from the roost, palms flush to the weeping bedrock, testing how far my thickening bones can hear. Beneath the local creek-plink, beneath the tuned thrum of our bellow-wall, beneath even the four-second *thump—chug—hiss* of our valley’s tower half a mile away, there is that deeper, slower pulse forty feet down in the mud. A continental vein, humming endlessly away into country none of us has mapped.

And I start thinking about the choirs I have never sat knee-to-knee with.

I think about the way accents drift across five hundred leagues of rain-hammered tableland—how a band living beside a double-fluted gorge pitches its air-speech higher to cut above the canyon’s shriek, while a choir settled near a stuttering, rusted giant learns to weave a limp into its laughter because their valley’s pistons hesitate every seventh stroke. I think about the wind-flutes wedged into distant cliff-galleries whose songs no one from my own roost has ever heard, and the strange knots another band’s steam-benders might be using to lash their harvest-hooks, and the new towers bolted to hotter, deeper escarpments twenty wind-tides west whose flywheel whines are missing from the sung map in my chest.

So I become what my bristles have been leaning toward all along: a tuner who walks the lines.

Not one of the solitary, haunted walkers who vanish forever in search of the mythical terminus—I love the warmth of a crowded overhang too much for that, love the wheezing, hammock-shaking laughter of a band mimicking a stiff-legged maintenance mechanism, love the feel of someone else’s tallow-slick palms rubbing fresh salve across my back. Instead, I travel in wide, slow loops—twenty wind-tides out along a buried trunk-line, thirty wind-tides curving back along an intersecting vein—carrying news, ceramic beads, rare strips of fresh gasket-rubber, and a chest full of other valleys’ music.

You hear me coming before my bristles drain the static beside you.

*Tock—tock.* A lone bone staff rapping wet limestone, followed by the soft, uneven clink of drilled ceramic cords at my shoulders and a double-toned hum that does not quite match the local canyon’s key—a traveler’s pitch, carrying the faint imprint of three distant escarpments. I keep my palms open and my notched chime-rod hanging clear at my hip, singing the chord of my home choir before I ever step under a stranger’s frond-roof.

When a distant band welcomes me in, we do not waste breath on small things. We sit knee-to-knee on the warm stone beside their bellow-wall, press our grooved palms to one another’s collarbones, and trade the state of the world. I sing them the bearing-whine of the tower forty leagues east—how it has climbed a quarter-tone hotter since the last great tide, how its steam-valve has begun to hiss two beats early. They sing me the snapping of a cableway across a mist-chasm to the south, and the birth of three soft-skinned young who have not yet struck their heels on bare rock.

While I stay—for three wind-tides, or ten—I earn my share of steamed metallic tubers and copper-sharp sap with my drills and my ears. I help clear dripstone from an old cliff-flute whose elder’s voice had thinned to a wheeze; I wedge a fresh horn-baffle into a bellow-wall that has drifted sour; I sit at their threshold for a watch or two, learning the particular shiver of their hillside’s sinew-strings so I will remember the texture of their rain long after I have walked on.

And sometimes, before I leave, I ask to press my brow lightly to the shoulders of their young, just so they can feel the hum of a voice that has walked through forty different storms and brought pieces of all of them back inside its ribs.

---

Right now, though, I am not walking. I am wedged into a sheltered lip of rounded limestone high above a perforated gorge, three wind-tides out from a choir that is not the one I was born into, though by now their bellow-wall feels like home in my chest.

Six hours ago, the tuned sacs along the back of the overhang dropped a quarter-tone and began to throb. The sentinel drew in the sinew-lines before the rising wind could snap them. The young are tucked deep into the dry pockets behind us, pressed cheek-to-chest against the heavy, iron-dense warmth of the elders.

Now the surge is here.

My inner ears pop. The air turns feverishly close, thick as warm broth, tasting violently of bruised copper and wet stone. Down in the unseen abyss, the boiling ocean hurls a river of saturated air straight up the cliffs until the hollow reeds shriek and the gallery of dead voices along the ledge swells from a murmur into a roaring, chest-rattling chord—hundreds of elders singing at once in wood, horn, and salt-crusted copper. Rain hits the pleated canopy like gravel poured from the sky.

Half a mile back in the thrashing dark, the valley’s solitary engine-tower drinks the climbing voltage off the storm-front and spins up. Its hidden flywheel climbs from a greased whine to a fierce, triumphant howl. Its reciprocating pistons double their pace, hammering straight through the bedrock and up into the dense spurs of my heels until my back teeth rattle with the rhythm.

I lock elbows with the walker on my left—their hide slick with fresh tallow, furnace-hot against my damp forearm—and the weaver on my right, whose ceramic cords are clinking wildly in the gust. We wedge our heel-spurs deep into the worn limestone seams and lean straight out into the wet, roaring dark.

My barometric sac swells taut as a drumhead. Every carbon-rich bristle along my skull, my spine, and my long forearms lifts and fans wide, drinking the harmless, intoxicating prickle pouring off the storm-front until my whole skin hums. I can feel the choir beside me, the tuned wall behind me, the howling tower half a mile away, and—faintly, stubbornly, underneath it all—the buried trunk-line surging beneath miles of sodden roots, linking this ledge to fifty other ledges where other choirs are locking elbows in the same blind rain.

I open my ridged mouth, catch the gap between two sledgehammer piston strokes, and braid my double-toned voice into the roar—a little of my home valley’s low drone, a little of the sharp upper clicks I picked up thirty wind-tides west, woven cleanly into the chorus of the cliff.

Not a prayer. Not a shelter against the dark. Just a voice answering a world that has never once needed light to be completely, overwhelmingly present—saying, in bone and breath and static: *I hear you. I am here.*
