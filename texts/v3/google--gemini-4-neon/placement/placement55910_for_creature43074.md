I would not find myself standing up.

That is the first thing this place would teach me, before I even knew what shape my hands were: anything that offers the wind a height gets argued with until it gives way. Even the mountains surrendered that argument before the first roots ever took hold on earth. So I would begin by sinking—letting a dense, mineral-heavy stillness pull me down through the knee-deep peat-stained rush until the soles of my feet met the smoothed gneiss beneath, horny ridges biting cleanly through the film of emerald algae. Not fighting the current. Not bobbing or bracing against it like something hollow. Just becoming weight, letting the cold tea-colored sheet-flow part around my shoulders and comb the wiry hairs along my spine flat in the direction of the spillway.

And the moment the horizontal rain and the chest-deep roar of the falls began to feel too loud—too shredded and frantic in the air—I would know what to do without having to be told. I would slide forward onto a broad, whale-backed rise of dark stone, fold my long forearms flat beneath me, and press my sternum and the broad, heavy bone of my lower jaw straight into the bedrock.

There. That is where I would actually wake up.

Above my back, the gale would still be tearing spray off the flooded sedge-flats and throwing white storm-petrels sideways through bright gaps in the clouds. A translucent inner lid would keep the stinging wind off my eyes if I lifted my head; vapor would curl faintly off my pewter flanks whenever a hard shaft of sunlight raced across the valley and turned the falling rain to blown glass. None of that would matter as much as the sudden, breathtaking clarity rising through the bone of my jaw.

Because in the stone, there is no static. There is only geometry and time.

Pressed flat to the Precambrian shield, I would feel the whole basin open out beneath me like a drawn map that breathes: the deep, unhurried boom of the thirty-meter chute two valleys west; the faint, tightening tremor far up-catchment where a maritime squall has just begun dumping its weight into the upper tarns, promising a half-meter surge before the light changes; the soft, rhythmic scuff of a herd of shaggy grazers wading chest-deep through the drowned meadows to the south; the dull, sodden thump of a torn peat-raft grounding on a quartzite shelf a mile away.

I think I would be one of the people of the sheet-flow—not one of the smooth-backed young who still throw themselves belly-down into the whitewater chutes just to shoot through the foam-drifts, and not yet one of the oldest elders whose pale beard-lichen hangs in foot-long banners down their ribs. Somewhere in the long, patient middle of a life. Old enough that the dorsal coat along my nape and spine has already become quiet soil, holding small rosettes of emerald liverwort and fine threads of moss that sway whenever I walk submerged across a run. Old enough that when I rest half-under in a braided spillway, a black-and-white water-dipper will mistake the arch of my back for a pewter dome of gneiss, land squarely between my shoulders to bob and whistle sharply over the spray, and dive straight off my flank into the rush after caddisfly larvae.

I like the thought of being patient enough to be mistaken for the ground.

If you asked me where on the plateau I would keep returning—knowing nothing up here keeps a permanent bed, not the channels, not the moss-islands, not our own woven shelters—I would point you not to the quietest outer marshes, where the world still sounds exactly as it did before the peat grew deep, nor right onto the concrete aprons where the iron engines sit. I would live in the middle catchments, along the high, drowned divides where two grammars overlap.

Out there, when a cloud-bank drops solid and grey across the plateau and visibility shrinks to three paces, I would still know exactly where I am. Partly by the stone humming under my soles, partly by the pull of the braided flow against my thighs, and partly by a faint, sharp, copper-tasting prickle gathering along the pitted skin of my muzzle and the fleshy heels of my palms.

Fifty paces before the steel leg looms out of the rain-curtain, I can taste it in the drenched air: the bright, fizzing aura of the ceramic insulators far overhead, snapping softly with blue corona as the transmission line marches straight from horizon to horizon across the domes.

That line would be my favorite road—if a road can be made of invisible electricity and wet air.

I would stop at the concrete footings even when I wasn't lost. There is a strange, rare pleasure in lifting upright for a moment—just long enough to lean a shoulder against a galvanized steel leg—and feeling the crosswind singing down through the metal from the heavy cables overhead. One resonant metallic chord carries the weather of the next two valleys at once. Before dropping back into the sheet-flow, I would reach into the cold rush for a long strand of dark sedge and a strip of tough dwarf-heath inner bark, braiding them underwater with blunt, deft fingers until the wet cord pulled tight. Maybe I would tuck a small pinch of iron-rust sphagnum into the twist, or knot a notched char-bone onto the lowest cross-brace just above the braid left by a cousin three rains ago.

I would like being the one who reads those knots carefully—rolling the wet fibers between my ridged fingertips to judge how recently the oil of another's pelt soaked into them, checking the tightness of the weave to see which family passed through the mist, which channel down-basin abandoned its bed overnight, whether the loud hammering machines had dropped out of the clouds over the eastern ridge. A quiet archive tied onto steel, looking to any distant eye like windblown debris snagged by a squall.

I would travel with a small cluster, of course—two or three adults, an elder, a youngster still learning how to drop a chest-note below the waterfalls—drifting across three or four neighboring catchments as the rains shift. I would take my place in the throat of a flooded spillway when the char are running, planting my broad hands and belly flat against the smooth rock lip alongside two others so the current backs up against our ribs and funnels hard into the leeward eddy where the rest wait. I would haul myself onto a torn raft of living sphagnum ten paces across when a sudden squall lifts the basin half a meter in twenty minutes, shifting my weight to one sodden edge and trailing a hind-foot as a rudder while we sail sluggishly downwind across the flooded tarns. In the three-day gales, when cold sleet mixes into the horizontal rain and combs the sedge flat, I would duck beneath the waterline into a low dome of bent evergreen scrub packed a meter thick with rooted, living moss—curling tight with the others in that dead-still, dark-green belly while our slow breaths fog the air and warm the peat-scented dark, mottled black-and-ochre salamanders clinging unbothered to the wet mossy ceiling above our heads.

I would belong completely to that life. And yet, within my cluster, I think I would be known for a particular, slightly eccentric patience—a lingering attention to the strange new weather made of concrete, cast iron, and wire.

I would just barely remember the years the machines arrived. I would have been smooth-backed then, small enough to press under an elder's chest when the rock-drills first bit into the Precambrian shield. Even now, decades later, the memory would sit in the back of my jaw like a bruised nerve: twenty miles of unbroken gneiss suddenly screaming with a high, jagged, blinding pain that drove every family off the central domes and deep into the silent outer marshes while the sky shook with the ugly chopping roar of the flying machines.

Perhaps that is why the silence after the drills—and what rose to fill it—would never stop astonishing me.

Because when the first black cast-iron penstock drank from an upper basin and spun its unseen steel rotor inside the riveted walls, the wound in the bedrock didn't fester. It began to sing.

A single, immaculate, unvarying bass note, bolted straight into the ancient stone. Every waterfall on the plateau swells and fades with the clouds; every peat-raft shifts; every channel forgets its bed. Only the gneiss beneath us never moved—and now, somehow, the gneiss had a heart.

So I would be the one who strays closest to the scattered instruments when the cluster passes through a powerhouse valley.

I would not be trying to meet the small, tall, fragile bipeds who arrive once in a long while in their loud machines. Their air-noise is still hideous, and their footing on the slick stone looks painfully uncertain. Long before a rotor-craft drops through the cloud-deck or a ridge-track vehicle grinds down a divide, I would already be tucked motionless beneath an undercut peat ledge, electroreceptors quiet, watching only the tea-stained current stream over my moss-furred shoulders until the ugly vibration left the rock.

What draws me is what they leave behind when they are gone.

I think I would be the one who tends the rattling boats.

Whenever a crosswind whips up a sheltered tarn where one of the flat-bottomed aluminum work-skiffs is chained to an iron mooring-ring, that hollow metal hull starts slapping erratically against the wet gneiss. To anything standing in the air, the sound probably vanishes ten paces downwind into the hiss of the rain. In the stone, it is a sharp, frantic, disharmonious clatter that rings half a mile across the basin floor—like a chipped tooth grinding right next to your ear while you are trying to listen for a storm-front or sleep in the moss.

I wouldn't want to break the boat or yank the iron ring. The skiff doesn't know it is shouting into the bedrock. Instead, I would wade out through the chop, gather thick handfuls of chartreuse and olive sphagnum and strip long ribbons of pliable heath-bark, and twist them underwater into dense, springy pads. Working slowly in the waist-deep wash, I would wedge those living wads carefully between the iron chain and the aluminum gunwale, lashing another cushion tight around the anchor-post where the links knock against the metal. Then I would lower my jaw to a submerged shelf of quartzite and listen.

The hollow clatter is gone. Just the soft, muffled thud of water against moss, folded cleanly back into the plateau's accounting. When the distant crews eventually return and assume the previous shift packed the chains to stop wear, that would suit me fine. The stone is quiet again; that was the whole point.

And in the long, milky dusks—when the thin cloud-deck finally dims to bruised slate and the wind lays its cold shoulder against the ridges—you would find me hauled up onto the concrete weir beside one of the solitary corrugated-iron gauge shacks perched above the normal flood-line.

Nothing else on the plateau looks like that single amber warning lamp glowing behind its wire cage. Our world is pewter and silver, luminous drenched green, iron-rust peat, and sudden blinding bursts of white sunlight. That small amber eye is the only warm, steady point of light for miles. I would sit motionless in the pouring rain beside the concrete lip, water sheeting quietly off my flanks, simply resting in its calm color.

After a while, I would lean forward and press the wide, ridged heel of one palm flat against the fogged glass port, wiping a clean circle through the condensation. Inside, completely dry—a state I find faintly alien, something that would make my nostrils itch and my skin pull tight—the brass-and-steel water-level recorder ticks steadily along, its inked needle tracing the endless rise and fall of the flood on a turning drum.

They measure the water in neat, dry lines behind glass. We measure it in the weight against our ribs and the pitch in the bedrock. Yet sitting there in the grey spray, watching that tiny brass arm tremble with the pulse of the upper catchment, I would feel an odd, quiet kinship with the instrument. I would click my teeth softly against my palate—a dry, rhythmic tapping—matching the clockwork behind the pane, answering its patient count for a few minutes before slipping back down the slick concrete into the dark sedge.

Still, if I have to choose the exact moment when I am most myself in this world, it is on the granite apron of a powerhouse during a forty-eight-hour sleet-gale—and then again, on the rare night when the engine stops.

In the worst of the storms, when the wind combs miles of sedge flat and the sky pours cold grey weight without a break, my cluster and I would wedge shoulder-to-shoulder against the leeward wall of the low, windowless steel building. The riveted plates bleed a faint, dry warmth into the wet air; the dense industrial thrum pours out of the concrete straight through our ribs and into our sleep. I would stay awake longer than the others, cheek and sternum flat to the wet granite apron, letting my chest fill slowly and pitching my own low hum down until it sat just a fraction of a tone below the unseen turbine inside.

Hold it there. Don't push. Just wait for the two frequencies to meet in the gneiss.

When the pitch is right, a slow, rolling beat emerges between us—neither mine nor the machine's, a third pulse that moves like a long ocean swell through the rock and sends concentric interference ripples trembling across the puddles on the apron. Sometimes a youngster from the cluster will creep over, flatten its smooth belly beside mine, and try to find the seam between the two notes, its own chest vibrating clumsily against my forearm until it slips into the groove and the ripples double.

And because I listen that closely, I always catch the subtle tightening in the stone half a minute before an automated sluice-gate trips—a rising strain in the turbine's song that no ear in the air could ever separate from the wind. A short tap of my knuckle against the concrete wakes the others. We slip silently into the sheet-flow and wade below the tailrace, anchoring ourselves in the lower eddies so that when the sharp pneumatic hiss and heavy iron clang shake the valley and flush a violent wall of whitewater over the lip, we are already there, waiting with wide hands for the shoals of stunned, silver-sided char swept straight into our arms.

That is the ordinary rhythm.

The extraordinary night comes only once in several seasons, when the ugly flying machines have lingered too long over a powerhouse valley and left a solitary biped behind in the grey rain for an overhaul.

Miles away—three valleys out across the flooded stone, lying half-submerged on a whale-backed dome of gneiss while the dusk turns to slate—I would feel it happen.

The immaculate bass note does not snap off. It begins to sink.

Slowly, heavily, as the main penstock valve cranks shut and the immense steel rotor inside the concrete housing loses the weight of the water, the note in the Precambrian shield slides downward—passing through pitches lower than the waterfalls, lower than my own chest, groaning softly into a slow, heavy coasting until it vanishes altogether.

For a few moments, the whole watershed feels weirdly hollow, as if a familiar headland had slipped under the water without a splash. Only the old sounds remain: the roar of the thirty-meter chutes, the shift of peat, the scuff of distant hooves, the hiss of rain on the drowned meadows above my back.

I wouldn't panic. I have learned the habits of the new weather well enough to know that the iron hearts sometimes sleep. Still, a quiet curiosity—something close to tenderness—would keep me from rolling off the dome to hunt.

I would adjust my forearms on the slick, pewter stone, settle the broad bone of my jaw firmly against the gneiss, and let the runoff sheet calmly over my liverwort-furred back. Somewhere three valleys away, perhaps the small biped in the heavy rubber boots has just stepped out onto the silent granite apron into the pouring rain, wondering at the sudden weight of the quiet.

I would draw a long, deep breath into my barrelled chest, drop the pitch below every spillway on the plateau, and pour a single, slow, patient pulse into the bedrock.

Not a warning. Not a demand. Just a low, living question sent swimming through miles of ancient stone, waiting calmly in the wet dark to hear if the iron heart will answer back.
