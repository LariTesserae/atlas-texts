It would take me a long time to stop squinting.

If I arrived on the upper switchbacks during a four-day glare, I would think at first that I had been dropped onto the roof of an engine too vast to have an interior. The white light off the wet granite, the wet rails, the slow-shifting acres of slick olive-brown hides—it would push straight against the back of my eyes. I would look instinctively for a margin of green, a weed in a flagstone crack, a smudge of moss under a leaking cornice, and feel a strange, vertiginous ache when nothing answered back except wet limestone, riveted iron, and breathing shell.

For a while I would stay near the outer rim, pulled by the sheer strangeness of the edge. I would stand behind the granite parapets where the Edge-Folk work their towering crane-booms, watching slatted iron cradles drop down into the luminous cloud-floor where the Breath rises forever from an unseen sea. There is something in me that understands the quiet of people who look out over a drop like that—the way they don't waste words, the crisp economy of their arms sweeping wide through the salt-haze, the reverence with which they unlash a tangled bale of salt-bleached driftwood hauled up from the storm-shelves below. I would stand there at evening when the sun sinks toward the vapor and turns the mist the color of heated brass, and I would think: *maybe here. Maybe I belong at the rim, staring down into where everything comes from.*

But I don't think I would stay there. Watch a Wall-Cutter lower a shear-blade against a century-old seam of dead siphon-strata and you see a kind of clean, decisive severance I am not built for. I am not a breaker of slabs. Even on the cliff-edge, I notice that my eyes keep drifting back inland—away from the shining abyss, toward the long terraced stairs, the sagging catenary cables, the dark arches cut deep into the retaining walls.

So I would tie on a pair of slit-goggles carved from pale grazer-horn, pull an oiled felt-wool hood forward against the glare, and start down the grooved granite flights.

You learn the plateau through your knees before you learn it through your head. Within an hour of walking the middle terraces, my stride would have to change. The stone is polished to satin by hundreds of thousands of rasping tongues; if you step lightly on your toes, the wet amber-grey film throws you straight onto your hip. You drop your weight into your heels. You widen your stance. You stop trying to get anywhere quickly.

Halfway down a contour-hugging avenue, I would end up stopped behind an electric freight tram nudging its rope-lined iron prow against a packed herd of grazers wedged across a flooded intersection. The conductor sounds the brass horn—two long, mournful notes rolling out across the concrete canyons—and then everybody simply waits. Twenty minutes. Twenty-five. Rainwater threads down the scarred horn-domes; calcified tongues keep up their sandpapery hiss beneath the steady roar of the spillways. The brakeman climbs down to check the axle grease-cups; two passengers on the rear bench bring out a slate peg-board. Nobody curses. Nobody jerks at a lever.

Something tight in me would unclench right there in the warm drizzle. I recognize that temperament immediately—the quiet contempt for *skittering*, the understanding that mass and water do not yield to temper, only to patience and timing. For a moment, watching a whole kiln-district's roof of perchers lift off at once in a sudden thunder of wet leathery sails, dimming the concrete in wide, drifting bands, I might feel a flicker of envy for them—to be nothing but wing-filament and warm rising air, circling high above the whole quarried continent. Or even for one of the ancient grazers circling the same four-block loop for a hundred years, wearing a shallow satin trough into granite by sheer unhurried persistence.

Yet I don't think I would want to be one of the animals. Not the perchers riding thermals, not the blind pale salamanders turning lazily beneath the turbine bar-screens, not even the cliff-siphons turning mist into living stone. I want the hands. I want the broad manual sign-language cutting cleanly through three days of warm fog. I want to know how the weight of one tier rests on the brickwork of the tier below it.

And the further down I walk—past the steaming rendering vats, past the jaw-mills whose deep crunch climbs right up through my hobnailed soles—the clearer it becomes where my boots are actually taking me.

Not out in the blinding open plazas, and not all the way down in the open settling canals where the chain-rakes clank day and night. I turn inward, off a flooded viaduct, under a bolted iron rain-hood, and into the ink-dark shadow beneath a deep masonry arcade cut straight into the retaining cliff of a middle-lower tier.

The glare cuts off like a shutter dropping. The wet high hiss of the streets falls back, and in its place rises the deep, steady floor-tremor of the buried hydro-hall thirty feet below and the slow, unhurried *thud-hiss* of the reciprocating pumps cycling once every six seconds.

Here. This is the seam where I would settle.

If I lived a whole life on this plateau, I would begin it as a **Line-Walker**, and let the decades weather me slowly into a **Weir-Clerk**—never quite leaving the first craft behind even after my knuckles stiffen and the high frequencies fade out of my ears.

It is not a glamorous trade, and nobody on the terraces would call it poetic. It lives right in the thickness of the retaining walls, in the narrow, unglamorous joints where water, electricity, stone, and living shell press against one another and try to creep across each other's margins.

Half my days would be spent in the dim brick transformer houses, the vaulted conduit galleries, and the damp catwalks running behind the hydro-intakes. I would wear an oiled canvas coat the color of wet slate, rope-soled boots waterproofed with sharp, blue-black millipede oil, and two tools that never leave my reach.

The first is a long paddle of pale, salt-cured ash, dragged up out of the cloud-floor by some Edge-Folk winch a century before I was born, patched near the throat with thin brass splines and oiled until the grain feels like warm skin. Nothing metal ever goes near the copper busbars. Every morning and every evening, I walk the warm catwalks above the buzzing conduits, where the glistening black millipedes—thick as my thumb—and wet-shelled beetles pack in dark, motionless drifts around the steady heat of the copper. With slow, even sweeps of the wooden blade, I scrape them cleanly out of the switchgear housings before a single wet carapace can bridge two phases and blow a district dark. No anger in it, no frantic swatting; a skitterer near a live busbar doesn't keep both hands for long. Most of the millipedes go straight into a stoppered clay jar slung at my hip, traded at the end of the week to the boot-maker down the arcade for fresh stitching-wax and tallow. Even your pests pay rent on the plateau.

The second tool is a brass-tipped sounding rod tucked through my belt beside a flat horn-scraper.

I am not a master Wheel-Keeper—they are the quiet, pale-skinned priesthood of the fifty-ton flywheels down in the echoing hydro-halls, who can tell a worn bronze journal by a tremor too faint for anyone else to name. My territory is slightly higher and wider: the masonry joints, the tie-rods, the sluice-valves, the ceramic water-pipes drilled through retaining walls behind century-old siphon colonies. I press the brass tip against a sweating brick pier or an iron valve-casing, grip the notched horn end lightly between my back teeth, shut my eyes, and listen through my skull.

You learn an astonishing amount once you stop trusting only the air. The air on the plateau is too crowded—spillways, grazers, clanking chain-rakes, rattling perchers, the breathing siphons. Bone is quieter. Through my jaw and the flat soles of my boots, I can feel whether the runoff is cutting a hollow behind the mortar of Pier Fourteen, whether Pump Three is hesitating by a fraction of a beat on its return stroke, whether the hairline seep in a vaulted ceiling is plain condensation or a failing flue-jacket.

And because I walk the conduits of an entire drainage sector—twelve streets wide, three tiers deep—I know the marks stamped into the mortar. I know which retaining wall was re-jacketed forty years ago by the mason with the split-anvil copper stamp, and which buttress hasn't been touched since my grandfather's time. I know the old scarred grazer with the quarry-chip in its left ridge that stalls the noon lime-skip on Switchback Six, and I step smoothly from the wet curb onto its warm, domed back and off onto the opposite flagstone without breaking stride or bothering it.

When I meet a Wheel-Keeper at the junction of two galleries, or a Tram-Brakeman waiting out a herd on the flooded track outside, we don't strain our throats shouting against the roar. Outdoors, my arms speak for me—wide, deliberate angles of elbow and flat palm that read cleanly through blinding mist and wet mittens. In the dim, booming lee of a brick viaduct pier, I step close, lay a gloved palm lightly against the other person's collarbone so the words ride bone as much as air, and pitch my voice low, tucking each sentence *under* the sandpapery scrape of the grazers and *between* the six-second thud of the pumps.

By fifty, my high-frequency hearing would be mostly gone—bleached out by thirty years of transformer hum and spillway thunder. I would not mourn it. On the plateau, losing the top edge of sound is just the city tuning you down into its real register. My hands would stay fluent. My feet would still feel a sour bearing three streets away through the limestone floor. And the endless din that tires out the young would fold down into a warm, steady pressure, like heavy felt across the shoulders.

The other half of my life lives behind two doors.

Come evening—or when the rolling wave of dilating apertures along the windward siphon-wall suddenly falters and doubles its rhythm, warning everyone hours ahead that a three-day bank of lukewarm mist is climbing up from the Breath—I climb the grooved stone stairs tucked into the retaining cliff behind the intakes.

On the landing sits a clipped, one-winged percher hunched on a granite bracket above the stairwell drain. It is nobody's pet; its slit-nostriled beak would open my thumb to the bone if I reached carelessly. I toss it a scrap of tough tendon from my pocket, nod to its dry, rattling clatter, and know the stairwell will stay clear of millipedes and bold canal-salamanders all night.

I duck under the rain-hood into the drip-lock. Coat onto the bronze peg, water pouring through the iron floor-grate into the running drain. Boots scraped clean of microbial slime and chalky droppings against the grooved limestone block. A pinch of dry, warm shell-lime rubbed between my damp knuckles and ankles until the briny slickness lifts away.

Then I pull open the second door—thick timber framed in riveted iron, edged all around with braided percher-felt—and swing it shut on its heavy brass pivots.

Half the plateau disappears.

This is the **Weir-Vault**, tucked directly into the dead, baffled core behind the hydro-intakes and the exhaust flues of the middle lime-kilns. It is part archive, part ledger-room, and, by virtue of the thick masonry baffling and dry radiant heat pouring through its tile-jacketed rear wall, a small sister to the neighborhood Dry-Room.

After a day in wet slate and white glare, stepping into that room is like drinking water when you didn't realize you were parched.

The barrel-vaulted ceiling is lime-washed brick, glowing soft amber from tallow lamps shaded behind paper-thin sheets of oiled grazer-horn. And the walls—the whole rear wall where the hot return-mains pass through from the pumping stations—blaze with mineral color: heavy fire-hardened tiles glazed in peacock verdigris, deep midnight cobalt, furnace yellow, and iridescent copper-green drawn out of trace metals filtered from the mist by ten generations of cliff-siphons. In a world without a single leaf, those walls are my forest.

Here, the inkwell on the slate table trembles almost imperceptibly with the deep cadence of the buried flywheels below, and the salt in the glazed bowl beside the stone hob stays dry and loose.

I hang my sounding rod on its hook, pull off my boots, and sit barefoot on the warm glazed bench. From the flue-heated hob I ladle a bowl of dark, glossy base-stock—grazer marrow, charred bone, dried filter-worm strips reduced all week until it coats a horn spoon—and break off a blistered corner of griddled stone-curd cake, tangy and crisp with tallow, along with a few translucent slices of pickled siphon-mantle whose bright, briny sourness cuts straight through the fat. Sometimes I turn the brass cock set into the side wall—drilled deep through the masonry into the dead filtration-chambers behind the living siphon colony outside—and catch a clay cup of lukewarm, mineral-sweet water strained clean of salt-grit and guano.

Then I pull out the rack of thin, translucent slips of oiled grazer-horn and my steel stylus.

This is the quiet core of what I am here: the keeping of continuity.

On these horn-slips—which do not rot in the damp, do not curl, and hold a scratch clearly for two hundred years—I record what the sector's bones told me today. *Switchback Four, Pier Nine: mortar flute deepening on windward face; re-jacket before next decade's rotation. Transformer Vault Three: damp weeping along south tie-rod; check siphon-spigot backing. Turbine Six: faint flutter at 4-beat harmonics on morning sounding; notified Wheel-Keeper Maren.* Beside them sit older slips scratched in stiff, careful hands by clerks who have been bone-ash for eighty years—quarry-face schedules so no cliff-siphon colony is sheared too close to the living layer, copper rations for the catenary repairs, the slow, multi-generational rotation of viaduct maintenance.

Nothing I write is brilliant or original. Almost nothing on the plateau is made from nothing. What matters is that the ledger stays honest, the sluices stay balanced, the busbars don't arc, and the person who sits at this slate table thirty years from now doesn't have to guess what is happening behind six feet of wet brick.

Later, when the lamps flicker on outside along the viaducts and gleam double in the flooded streets, I might walk two arcades over to the public Dry-Room. Barefoot in a light felted tunic, sitting on the warm glazed benches with a retired jaw-miller, a couple of tired tram-crews off the Rim Seven lime-convoy, and two young apprentices learning to form letters cleanly on precious imported parchment without the ink bleeding. Nobody has to touch collarbones to speak; whispers carry easily in the velvet hush. Someone brings out a hollowed horn-resonator strung with twisted gut and plays a slow, droning air in cycles of six beats—unconsciously matching the distant heartbeat of the pumps beneath the floor—while the perpetual damp finally evaporates from my hair and my knuckles.

Or, on mist nights, I stand for ten minutes just outside the arcade archway before latching the shutters. The warm vapor has climbed clear over the upper switchbacks, swallowing the terraces until you cannot see six feet past the rain-hood. The streetlamps are blurred amber halos hanging in lukewarm drizzle. Above me, unseen in the fog, the sky-flocks mutter softly along the sagging copper lines. And right beside the retaining wall, the great water-driven brass pipe-chimes mounted in the main spillway sound their vast, slow open fifths—not fighting the deep hum of the buried hydro-turbines, just leaning cleanly into it—while somewhere two miles out in the white dark, a freight tram's two-note bronze horn answers back from the switchbacks.

Listening to that—the turbine under my heels, the spillway chimes in the mist, the distant horn, the steady scrape of millions of grazing mouths moving patiently through the night—I don't feel small, and I don't feel outside of anything. I feel like a single concentric ring inside a wheel that has been turning smoothly for a thousand years.

*

And when my joints finally grow too stiff for the transformer ladders, and my hands can no longer hold a stylus steady against the horn-slips, I already know which custom I will choose.

I admire the Quiet-Walls more than I can easily say. Whenever I walk up to the windward escarpment on a clear evening and see those broad bands of oxblood tile crowning the untouched buttresses—knowing that beneath the breathing ochre-and-plum chimneys, generations of Edge-Folk and Wall-Cutters have been folded cleanly into living, growing stone—I feel a deep, salt-sharp awe.

Still, my choice would be the **Kiln**.

After the washing, and the slow six-beat singing in the hush of the Dry-Room, let them give what is left of me to the hottest lime-furnace on the terrace. Gather the clean white bone-ash, mix it with local cobalt and copper-oxide pulled by the smelters from centuries of filtered mist, and fire it into one heavy, luminous brick of deep peacock verdigris—stamped plain with my name, my years, and the paired mark of the paddle and the sounding rod.

Mortar me straight into the heated rear wall of the Weir-Vault, or along the covered arcade just outside the drip-lock. Not as a monument. Just as a tile that holds heat well, stays bright against the lamplight, and doesn't crack.

So that forty years later, when a three-day mist-bank climbs up off the Breath and some tired Line-Walker comes in off the catwalks with wet salt-grit stiffening their cuffs and numb blue knuckles from scraping busbars in the dark, they can lean an open palm against my green-blue glaze—feel the steady industrial warmth of the pumps radiating right through me, feel the faint, unhurried hum of the buried flywheels still running true—and know whose work they are standing inside.
