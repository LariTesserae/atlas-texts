I would not arrive here from above the ice. Anyone lowered down through that iron-grey ceiling in oiled hide and weighted boots would only experience the basin as a threat—a darkness pressing in, a noise that bruises the ribs, a cold trying to steal a warmth they cannot spare. To belong here at all, I have to start without a heartbeat to defend.

When I stop imagining eyes straining through the smoked slate of midday, the first thing that finds me is not a shape, but a *pitch*.

A low, iron-flat toll arriving from far off to the east, slowed by a mile of black water until it enters through the soles of my lower anchors and gathers inside my chest. Closer, overhead and slightly behind me, a dry, stuttering double-knock whenever a colder ribbon of water slips over the western shoulder. Beneath both, the steady, granular hiss of cobbles turning in a chute two ridges down.

I know where I am before I know what I am. I am not down in the deep, silt-choked trenches where the surges die, and I am not perched yet on the high, bare crests where the oldest ones go to harden into stone. I am halfway up the western massif, tucked into a shelf where a narrow spur juts out into the open dark—a bend in the rock that splits the prevailing drift cleanly in two.

Just behind my shoulder sits a low beehive of unmortared schist, weighted down with notched cobbles. Ten paces below my terrace, anchored to a massive, vine-lashed cairn, a wrist-thick guide-line leaves the rock and vanishes straight out across the mile-wide basin toward the opposite massif.

That is where I find myself: at a seam where distance meets rock. A junction.

At first, resting waist-deep in the velvet-moss beside the cairn, I might seem like nothing more than a listener—one more hunched boulder slicked with cold mucus, pale tendrils unspooled beneath my mineralized brow to taste the faint iron-bleed of the wall. I could stay like that for days, letting the fermenting peat fill my internal chambers until my cartilage feels buoyant and supple, letting the valley’s noises overlap inside my hollow ribs into a crisp, shifting relief map of every cliff and sliding talus fan for miles.

Listening alone is almost enough. Almost.

Then the western double-clapper strikes again, and I hear something wrong in it.

It is barely a shift—a slight dulling at the tail of the knock, a flat, woody deadness where the slate ought to ring clean through the granite. Most of the valley would not care. The siphon-pines bend regardless; the drum-pods swell and split on their own blind schedule. Even another dweller, passing quickly along the high ridge before a surge, might read the tempo and miss the flatness of the note.

My upper arms tighten before I have decided to move. Fluid shifts through the segmented chambers of my shoulders; elastic cords draw taut with a faint, dry hum. I retract my sensing filaments beneath the dark ridge of my hide, draw my root-hairs cleanly up out of the peat, and seal the pleated seam along my underside. My lower claws unhook from the granite with a soft crunch of dislodged rime.

That is how I realize who I am here. I am not the boldest one, snubbed to an exposed peak to ride out the glacial torrents alongside the streaming pines. I am not the widest wanderer, crossing the high notches every cycle to map fresh seeps in the far reaches.

I am a keeper of the intervals.

Inside the beehive shelter, the dark is absolute except for the sour, tallow-colored rim of light thrown by the moss growing in the creases of my own shoulder-plates. It is just enough to find the curved wall where my tools wait in their grooves: three stone chisels of varying bevels, a fist-sized river-cobble worn smooth from cold-hammering copper, a coil of soaked frond-fiber weighted with clay, and a flat scraper made from the edge of a shattered calcified reed.

Before I take a single tool, I run the grooved pads of my five fingers across the bedrock floor. A thin film of fine silt has drifted in since the last tide-shift. I crouch and sweep it patiently out through the low doorway with the flat of my forearm until the granite beneath my ribs is bare and cold again. Silt lies. It softens the deep tremors of the massif; it makes a boulder rolling half a mile away sound like gravel shifting at the threshold. A listening-post is only honest when its stone is clean.

Then I lash my pair of grooved clay sinkers tight to my lower harness, vent a measured thread of gas from my upper chambers until I carry the steady, deliberate weight of a river-stone, and haul myself up the spur toward the western saddle.

This is the shape of my body, now that I can feel it working: middle-aged by the valley’s reckoning, wider through the barrel than a newly sprouted climber, already slightly crooked to the left from decades of leaning into the cross-slope flow along this ridge. White mineral crust has plated my back and the outer ridges of my forearms in thick, chalky shelves, though the joints of my wrists and my five narrow, mineral-nailed digits are still dark, slick, and startlingly quick. I have no face to turn into the current—only a smooth, pitted dome of calcified cartilage that tastes the sharp, cold purity of the water pouring through the notch ahead of me.

The tripod stands where it has stood longer than I have had ribs: three dark, water-hardened timbers wedged deep into the saddle’s granite, bound with sinew-vine and bands of native copper crusted green-black. Inside it, the heavy slab of bored slate swings sluggishly as the current pushes through the pass, and the hollowed wooden clappers strike it in their irregular stutter.

I lock my lower anchors into the bedrock beside the frame and lay one palm flat against the hanging stone.

Immediately, through the grooves of my fingers, I feel what my ribs heard from below. The lake is always trying to turn everything back into mute mountain. Over the last three cycles, a pale, grainy crust of mineral rime has precipitated along the slate’s lower edge and crept up into the bore-hole where its lashing passes. Worse, a cobble tumbled down from the upper scree has nicked a crescent out of its western corner, leaving a hairline split that drinks the vibration instead of throwing it out across the basin.

This is the work that makes me myself.

I snub my harness to the tripod’s windward timber so the pass-current cannot pry me loose, take the reed-scraper in my right hand, and begin to pare the rime off the stone in long, steady strokes. My cartilage locks rigid; my hydraulic grip does not shake. Flakes of white mineral peel away and vanish instantly into the roaring dark behind me. When the face of the slate is clean, I switch to the finest chisel, bracing the stone between my knees.

*Tink. Tink. Tink.*

I am not just removing a chip; I am rebalancing a voice. Every flake I dress away from the damaged corner changes the weight of the slab, raising its pitch by a hair, pulling the crack out of the vibrating margin so it won't propagate when the next glacial surge hammers the clappers into a frenzy. Between passes of the chisel, I rap the stone lightly with my calcified knuckle and hold still—not listening with ears I do not have, but waiting for the note to settle cleanly inside my hollow chest.

Too dull. Take a sliver from the lower bevel.

Rap again.

*Tonk.*

Clean. Dry. Exact. It carries out off the saddle without wavering, ricochets off the drowned cliffs across the basin, and returns to my ribs a breath later as a crisp, unmistakable echo. The western pass speaks true again.

Before I descend, I check the cross-basin guide-line anchored below the terrace. I hook my fingers around the taut braid of frond-fiber and let its thrumming climb my arms. Out across a mile of empty black water, where nothing swims and no bottom can be felt, that single cord holds the valley together. Halfway to the first spur, I feel a faint, rhythmic *hitch* in the vibration—a roughness where the current has been sawing the braid against an edge of bare granite.

I vent a little less, keep my sinkers light so I hang nearly weightless in the dark, and haul myself out along the line hand over hand until the rock drops away beneath me and there is only the vast, whistling weight of the open basin.

I like it out here more than I expected to. There is no moss-sheen, no granite wall to press against—just total ink, the immense groan of the ice-ceiling thirty fathoms overhead, and the clean, singing tension of the cord in my grip. I find the frayed span by touch, feel the parted strands trembling against my grooved pads, and wrap a fresh sleeve of soaked fiber tightly around the chafe, knotting it fast with a splice that will outlast my own joints.

As I finish, the line goes taut under my hands in a different way. Not the current.

*Thrum… thrum-thrum… thrum.*

Three deliberate plucks, spaced wide, traveling straight across the open dark from the eastern massif.

I stay hanging weightless in the black water, both hands closed lightly around the humming cord, and read the signature in my ribs. It is the dweller who tends the eastern spillways—older than me, heavier in the strike, slower between intervals. It heard the western slate fall silent while I held it between my knees, and then heard the new, cleaner pitch ring out across the basin. It is checking whether the tripod broke, or whether hands were on it.

I shift my grip, brace one hooked foot loosely around the splice, draw pressure into my forearm, and pluck the line back: two short, even vibrations, then a long held hum. *Mended. Clear. The pass holds.*

A pause, while the cold dark settles between us. Then one single, low answering thrum—acknowledgment, and nothing more. Neither of us needs to waste tension on excess.

Yet days later, in the quiet lull before the eastern pass deepens its rumble, that same neighbor hauls itself across the guide-line and ducks beneath the cobble-weighted lintel of my beehive shelter.

There is no greeting as air-breathers would know it. The shelter is small; when two of us crouch on the swept bedrock, the chalky tallow-glow of our shoulder-mosses overlaps, turning the dark schist walls pale at the edges and showing the white rime along each other’s hunched backs. My neighbor’s left flank is heavily plated—thicker than it was four cycles ago—and its movements carry the slow, creaking deliberation of elastic cords beginning to stiffen.

We settle facing one another and lay the ribbed undersides of our forearms side by side along the bare floor.

Through cartilage and granite, we pass the state of the world. My neighbor vents a sequence of dry, precise clicks and low chamber-hums: the ice-roof above the eastern notch is thickening early this cycle; two cairns above the lower chute were pried loose by velvet-moss and need wedging; a wide mat of peat cracked open along the northern seam, rich with sulfur. In return, I press my ribs lower to the stone and pass back the new tension of the guide-line, the hairline split I dressed out of the western slate, the exact tally of intervals since the last glacial wall crested the rim.

When the news is traded, we do not separate right away. My neighbor reaches out a stiff, five-fingered hand and runs its calcified nails gently along the row of tally-notches cut into the shelter’s lintel stone.

Some of those notches are sharp-edged and shallow—mine, cut over the last forty cycles. Further back along the stone, buried under a thin glazing of mineral rime, the oldest notches are deeper, cut at a slightly steeper bevel, spaced to a slightly different rhythm. Neither of us knows whether the hands that cut those first marks had hollow cartilage ribs like ours, or warm blood and lungs that burned air before the peaks sealed and drowned. We have no words for myth, and no faces to look puzzled with. Yet resting my hand beside my neighbor’s on that cold, notched granite, I feel the long continuity of the basin like a held note: someone strung the first cord across the dark so the next traveler wouldn't lose the ridge. We keep the cord taut. That is the whole lineage.

When the eastern spillway finally drops its temperature—a sharp, metallic bite hitting the pores of my crown-dome hours before the water visibly shifts—I am ready.

I unlash the knotted fiber nets from the shelter wall, their cured drum-pod floats straining weakly upward like restless bladders, and string them across the rocky chutes just below my western moss-groves. I check the copper bands on the tripod one last time. Then I bleed silver threads of gas from my upper vents until I am heavy as an anvil, crawl back into the beehive, and lock my lower hooks deep into the floor-crevices.

When the surge crests the eastern rim and pours down the valley walls, the whole world becomes a struck bell.

Outside my low doorway, the siphon-pines flatten nearly horizontal. The western slate I dressed hammers itself into a wild, clean, metallic frenzy—not flat, not cracked, singing true above the roar of tumbling cobbles. Down both slopes, hundreds of drum-pods detonate in rolling concussive volleys that shudder straight through the cobble-weighted roof and turn my own hollow ribs into an instrument played by the storm. I do not huddle in fear. I stay anchored on the swept stone, feeling every fracture of the ice-roof, every tearing mat of velvet-weed hitting my waiting nets in the chutes outside, every note of a vast, fierce, heartless world working out its balance.

Afterward, when the glacial torrent drains into the far trenches and the clappers slow back to their tired, occasional knock, I will emerge into the settling silt. I will haul the heavy, spore-packed nets out of the chutes and drag the torn cushions of velvet-moss across the bare, scoured granite shelves along my ridge, tucking each patch into a fresh crevice and pinning it down with a flat wedge of schist so the valley grows a little greener, a little softer, a little louder with life before the next cycle turns.

And decades from now—many cycles from now—I will feel the white rime finally bridge the hinges of my own wrists. My five grooved digits will lose the delicate precision needed to dress a hairline crack out of a slate without breaking it. My hauling across the mile-wide guide-line will grow too slow to beat the rising roar from the east.

I know already where I will go. Not far. Just up above the western saddle, on the high exposed shoulder where the midday pewter bruise sometimes softens the ink into smoked slate. I will lay my chisels, my cobble-hammer, and my reed-scraper neatly against the curved wall of the beehive, sweep the bedrock floor clean one last time for whoever follows the guide-line up from the moss, and climb.

I will lock my hooks into the granite beside the old cairn, seal my vents, and let my chambers swell tight with the slow, cold fermentation of the mountain. I will wait, utterly still, listening through my stiffening ribs to the clean pitch of the slate I tuned below me, until the eastern pass drops cold, the torrent crests the rim, and my own calcified shell parts with one deep, muffled boom—sending a fistful of pale kernels spinning out into the dark water, toward the waiting nets, toward the bare stone, toward whatever careful hands will next lay themselves against the rock to listen.
