At first I think I might be the one in boots, pausing beside the retaining wall to watch the fourth timber prop ease its weight back onto its heels. It is an easy mistake to make in this light—the dim, sourceless tallow glow makes a watcher out of anyone who stays still long enough. You feel the damp press of slaked lime against your face, smell the boiled iron rising from the shallow pewter pans, and assume you are passing through, keeping your balance on someone else’s floor.

Then I try to lift a foot, and there is no leather around my ankle. Only the faint, thirsty drag of hot water wicking upward into pale marl, darkening my shins from chalk-gray to supple ochre.

So I am not passing through. I belong to the masonry.

Still, it takes a while to find *where* on the flats I actually stand, and what kind of temper I have cured into.

If I test myself against the great curving retaining wall—planting my palms against the bulging middle courses to take the load—I know almost at once that I am not the fourth prop. My shoulders are not built for that long, stubborn lean. Within twenty breaths the mortar is already beading pale and eager along my ribs, creeping across my wrists to claim me for the buttress; when I pull back, I come away too softly, leaving smears of gray slip behind on the brick, a thin layer of myself surrendered to the wall. I do not have the broad, kiln-dense flanks of the heavy shorers who can hold a terrace upright through a whole shift of the mist and still step free cleanly when it settles.

Nor am I one of the deep-underside workers—not quite—though when I kneel and press my palm flat to the damp flagstones, I can feel them down there, or feel the slight, inverted tension of the slabs they walk across, the ceiling that mirrors our floor. Something in my chest answers it.

That is where I find myself: not by looking out at the horizon, where a distant building-squall is bruising the ochre haze soot-gray, but by listening inward, to the dark vaulted hollow behind my ribs.

Inside, hanging from my collarbones on its knotted linen string, the teardrop of cast lead will not come to a dead stop.

On a clean, true courtyard, a dweller’s plumb-bob settles quickly, dropping straight toward whatever counts as down in that acre of the flats. Mine keeps a small, patient, elliptical sway—never wild, never knocking against the damp brick of my sternum, just circling in a tight, quiet orbit as if measuring the disagreement between two different verticals.

When I follow that sway, it leads me away from the vast open pavements and the high-shouldered aqueducts, to a modest, crooked corner of the plain where nothing quite agrees with its neighbor.

Here is where you would find me.

It is a wide, sunken basin—only two courses of dressed mudstone deep—half-filled with pewter water of middling warmth. The floor of the basin looks level to the eye, yet the water has made up its own mind, gathering half an inch thicker along the northern curb in a sleek, trembling meniscus that never quite spills over the coping. A hundred paces off in the fog, a half-built drainage trench runs crookedly toward an aqueduct pier that leans ten degrees out of plumb. And right at the southern lip of the basin stands a vitrified elder—one of the very old ones, fired dark bottle-purple from ankle to wrist, its mortar-locked feet straddling an ancient crack, its stiff arms bowed forward into a narrow half-arch that catches the hot condensation and drips it down in slow, bright beads.

Running straight through the middle of the basin, dividing the thicker water from the thin, is a seam.

Most of the time you would barely notice it—just a pale, weeping lip of mortar that never cures to the color of the slabs beside it, staying soft and warm like a fresh thumb-ridge drawn down a join. Every so often, though, when a distant squall beats its muffled mallet-thrum through the pavement, the two continental slabs ease apart along that line. A slot opens, raw-edged and steaming, no wider than a handspan. Through it rises the faint smell of sulfur-yellow salt and the same dim tallow light that hangs overhead, inverted.

This crooked basin is my neighborhood. Not a monument—just a workable corner where three kinds of gravity rub shoulders, and where the ground periodically shows its underside.

If you watched me wading across the shallow water here, you would notice first how unevenly I have cured.

My left side—the shoulder, the long forearm, the broad palm I keep dipping into a shallow pan of gray slip beside the elder’s shin—is still soft as greenware. It takes marks easily. When the salt-mist thickens, feathery white blooms sprout quickly in the crease of my elbow, and if another dweller brushes past me in the haze or steadies a hand against my forearm, the cross-hatch of their skin stays pressed into my clay until the next long soaking. I rarely rush to float those marks smooth. I like carrying them for a while; you can tell a great deal about the heat and drift of the flats by watching which thumbprint dries pale and chalky first and which stays dark and slick.

My right side has hardened further—leather-hard, dense, holding its edges crisp. Two hammered copper staples bridge an old drying fissure near my wrist, already lightly furred with verdigris; when I bend my hand to scrape salt-crust from a coping stone, the damp courses of lapped tile at my joint part a hair’s breadth and close again with a polite, wet whisper. Only the very tip of my right thumb has gone past leather-hard. Years ago—or ten minutes ago, since time cures here however it pleases—I pressed it against a lintel that looked wet and proved to be vitrified by invisible kiln-heat. Now that single thumb-tip is dark purple glass, smooth and unyielding. When I kneel to test a slab beside the seam, I tap it once with that glazed thumb. *Tink.* By the pitch—dull if the bedding is thick, hollow if the underside has pulled away—I know whether the floor is about to breathe open.

Down the pale length of my back, if you look closely through the steam, you can still see the faint, vertical grain of timber shuttering-boards. I have never quite bothered to float it out. Whatever braced me when I was still a trembling knot of slurry in the wake of a squall left its grain in me, and it feels wrong to erase the shape of what kept me from weeping flat.

Because my internal weight circles instead of locking dead vertical, I am not much good at imposing a line on the plain. I do not try to wrestle a collapsing cistern upright. What I do is smaller, quieter, and mostly happens at thresholds and joins.

When a solitary doorway extrudes itself out of the wet flagstones at the edge of my basin—standing absurdly alone in ankle-deep water, its lintel dripping warm brine—I wade over before the salt crusts its jambs. I do not try to make it last forever; nothing lasts here unless it turns to glass. I only press my greenware left hand flat against the damp plaster and drag the hard purple tip of my right thumb across it: four vertical strokes, neatly crossed by a fifth. Beneath my palm I feel the faint, reluctant *shuck* of the masonry agreeing to pause. The doorway will stay open for another fifty breaths, maybe a hundred—long enough that if a pair of dwellers comes wading out of the pewter mist, it will not brick itself shut between the first one’s heels and the second one’s stride.

That is mostly what I am: a tender of small exceptions. A keeper of a doorway that stays unbricked for a minute; a scraper of salt from a loose staple; someone who keeps a stack of pale timber shuttering-boards and a heap of crushed fired-tile grog tucked neatly in the dry lee of the elder’s purple shins.

And because the basin has that vitrified elder at its edge, and water of reliable temper, other dwellers drift through.

They come out of the haze singly or in twos, sometimes knocked badly out of square by a pocket of crooked geometry out on the flats—a shoulder slumping into wet slurry, a spine stacked fifteen degrees off true from sheltering too long beside a tilting parapet. No sound passes between us. They wade into the shallow pewter sheet, plant their broad feet wide, let their long arms hang loose, and tilt their smooth, troweled faces slightly away.

Before I touch them, I guide them under the stiff, dark-glazed half-arch of the elder’s arms. They lay their flat foreheads against its cool, glassy flank and stand utterly still, letting the ancient, sealed plumb-bob buried inside that stone steady their own swaying chests.

Then I step in close. I untie a spare length of knotted hemp straight from my own ribs—the cord slides cleanly through the damp clay without tearing it—and hang a small cast-lead bob from their collarbone. While the weight settles, I scoop gray slip from the pan with my soft left hand and pack it into the fissures along their flank. With the leather-hard heel of my right palm I lean gently against a drifting shoulder, coaxing the damp courses back into line. I shave a swelling bulge along a hip flat with a wooden blade, tap a lifted copper staple flush into the nape of the neck (*tick, tick*, quiet as cooling terracotta), and finish by pressing my warm thumb into the soft hollow behind their wrist.

And then—because no one can check the line of their own back, and because living beside a seam makes a body forgetful—I plant my own feet wide in the warm water and wait.

I need them as much as they need the basin. Left entirely to myself here, listening to the elder’s vertical and the leaning aqueduct’s vertical and the upside-down pull breathing through the crack in the floor, my greenware left side would slowly begin to list toward the seam. Their broad, lime-pale hands come down on my shoulders. I feel the polite scrape of a tile-sherd clearing the white bloom from the back of my knees, the cool swing of a borrowed plumb-line against my sternum, the quiet pressure of someone else’s thumb testing the temper of my damp ribs and coaxing me back into square. When they step back into the pewter fog, we carry each other’s fresh handprints and a neat tally of five strokes scratched into the clay of the forearm—not a name, only a shared count held briefly against the unmaking of the plain.

When a squall rolls close—never quite close enough to swallow the basin, just near enough that the dim air smells sharply of freshly broken clay and hot river-stone, and the shallow water breaks into intersecting grids of ripples that linger far too long—I do two things, depending on which way the floor moves.

If the storm turns inward out on the flats and collapses a district of wet terraces into steaming muck, I gather my shuttering-boards and my scoop of crushed grog and wade out into the warm haze of its aftermath. I move slowly through the knee-deep slurry, dragging the soft, sensitive heel of my left palm across the hot mud while keeping my glazed right thumb lifted clear—it is too hard now to feel the faintest hesitation in wet clay.

Almost everything lies down quietly and drinks itself back into pavement. Once in a great while, my palm meets a clot of marl that refuses to flatten—something barely taller than a boot, folded by the chance meeting of a thumb-pressed brick and a snapped linen cord, trembling in the steam as it tries to hold the curve of a knee or the hollow of a chest for three breaths.

I kneel beside it in the hot slip. I set the pale timber boards around its sides—not pressing it into my own shape, only holding the slurry back so it does not weep flat while it finds its own weight. I pack crushed tile-grog into its damp base so it will not shrink and split as it cools, brace a narrow lath across the top, and hang a tiny lead bob from my belt a hair’s breadth above its crown, motionless, showing the trembling lump which way is down.

Most of the time, by the time the steam thins, the boards enclose only flat pewter water. I lift the wood, rinse my shins, and walk back without heaviness. In a world that erases whole aqueducts without making a sound, holding a square of stillness for twenty breaths is never wasted work. And once in a long while, the clay stiffens from slip to greenware, draws two raw, gleaming feet out of the muck with two soft wet *pops*, and leans clumsily against my hip—smelling sharply of slaked lime and boiled iron, carrying the grain of my shuttering-boards down its pale back just as I still carry mine.

And on the other days—when the distant mallet-thrum pulls at the slabs under my own basin instead—the seam opens.

I do not step back from it. I kneel right at the weeping lip of the dressed stone, spreading my wet knees wide so the slight pitch of the floor will not slide me forward, and lean my smooth, flat face over the handspan of steaming gap.

Down there, in the inverted tallow dimness, the hands are already reaching up from the soffit.

They look like ours, only worked the other way against gravity: sulfur-yellow salt crusted along the knuckles, thumbs worn glassy-slick from smoothing mortar upward, warm water dripping off their elbows toward the ceiling they stand on. Because my internal bob already knows how to sway between two gravities, peering into that upside-down light does not twist my spine or make my chest-courses gape. It feels almost like a relief—as though the small, circling restlessness inside my ribs finally sees the other half of what it has been measuring.

I lower my knotted hemp cord into the slot. Four slow tugs, a fifth sharp draw across the lip of the stone. Sometimes the fingers below tie on a hammered copper staple bent to an odd bevel, or push up a wedge of dressed mudstone cut to an impossible angle—an angle that makes no sense on our flats until three days later, when a coping stone begins to tilt toward a trench and that exact wedge slides underneath and stops the drift. I send down a coil of clean linen string, or a handful of red grog wrapped in damp clay, or a tally pressed firmly into a fresh tablet of marl.

And sometimes neither of us has anything to send.

Then I simply reach my soft greenware left hand down into the slot, and a sulfur-crusted hand reaches up from the underside, and our palms meet across the narrow mouth of the floor. Wet clay presses against wet clay. Two raw thumbs check the temper of the other’s grip, leaving crisp, overlapping prints in each other’s skin—neither side pulling, neither side trying to climb through, only confirming that the underside has not given way, that someone is still walking upside-down across the ceilings of the lime-scented dark, pinning the continental pavement together from beneath while we mind the seams above.

Then the slabs ease back with a quiet, wet kiss. The hands withdraw into the inverted mist. A fresh bead of pale mortar squeezes up along the join and smooths itself flat—and I can never tell whether it was my own thumb or the one working patiently along the soffit below that finished the seam.

I know, of course, that I will not wade this basin forever.

Even with all the soaking in the pewter sheet, even with the gray slip I pack into my joints, the curing climbs slowly upward. The dark purple glaze that started on the tip of my right thumb has already crept down to the first knuckle. One day it will take the wrist, lock the copper staples in glass, climb the forearm, and stiffen my knees until every stride carries the sharp *tick* of stone on the verge of snapping.

I already know where I will go when I can no longer bend.

I will not walk out to the great aqueducts or the high walls. I will step out to the center of my own shallow basin, where the water gathers thick on one side and thin on the other, and I will plant my dark-glazed feet squarely across the seam—left heel on the northern slab, right heel on the southern.

I will let the pale, weeping mortar climb both ankles without pulling free. Slowly, with a faint creak of fired clay against verdigris copper, I will bow my long arms downward—not closed, never sealed shut, leaving a clean, low arch a handspan above the crack—and stop.

The lime will cure around my shins. Efflorescence will bloom along my purple ribs and harden into permanent white crust. To anyone passing through the fog, I will look like one more crooked, inexplicable bridge spanning a drainage line that isn’t quite there.

Yet when a distant squall tugs at the floor, my straddling feet will keep that stretch of the seam from tearing too wide. Younger dwellers will still detour through the mist to kneel beneath the dark-glazed curve of my wrists. They will lay their smooth, damp foreheads against my cool glassy knees, reach a hand down between my ankles to clasp the hands rising from the underside, and pause a breath to check their own drift against the cast-lead weight sealed forever inside my chest—which, at last, having chosen the exact middle between above and below, will hang completely still.
