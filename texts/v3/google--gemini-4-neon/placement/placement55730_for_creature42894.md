I don’t think I would arrive in the middle of a plaza, or wake already seated at a hearth. If I found myself in this basin, I would first notice where my feet had stopped on their own: halfway across one of the long, unbuilt intervals between quarters, on a low-walled causeway where the dressed slate narrows and the wild fern-slopes press close on either side.

Above me, the sky would be that thin, washed cobalt that holds no promise of noon and no threat of dusk. To my right, dry stone faintly warm with unchanging glare, crusted in pale gold lichen; to my left, three inches across the shadow of the parapet, dripping emerald moss so deep it swallows the sharp rock entirely. And beside my ankle, running in a carved channel along the causeway’s rim, a gleaming skin of cold water moving without hurry, without sound, from the high eastern ridges toward a cluster of slate roofs half a mile below.

That is where my attention would catch first—not on the grand geometry of the city as a whole, nor on the solitude of the jagged rim, but on the thread connecting them.

At first I might imagine myself as one of the silent things already native to the air—a crane-owl gliding without a whisper over a fern-trough, casting the only kind of shadow here that ever moves across the ground. There is a deep pull in that: to belong wholly to the stillness, to have no pulse fast enough to rub against the held morning. Yet the longer I stand on the causeway, feeling the crisp dampness enter my lungs and the weight of felted wool across my shoulders, the clearer it becomes that I would not be a bird, and not stone. I would be one of the warm-blooded ones—spare, deep-chested, mortal inside an unmoving light, wearing a copper pin worn smooth along one shoulder-edge by a habit of resting my thumb against it while I think.

And I know, almost before I know my craft, what my quiet trouble would be.

I would carry a mild, lifelong trace of *the hurry*.

Not the sudden fever that makes a mason crack a sound slate just to watch a fresh seam open, or drives someone up the raw scree to step over the eastern teeth and vanish. I would love this place far too fiercely to ever wish a storm on it, or ask for the white ribbon of cloud to tear loose and darken the plazas. The stillness here is not dead; it is full, brimming right to the carved lip like the cisterns, and I would want to keep it held.

Still, if I stayed on a single terrace for twenty trimmings, watching the exact same blade of shadow hold the exact same inch of ashlar, something under my ribs would begin to hum a note too tight for the room. In a valley where the sun never travels, the only way to feel light change is to carry your own face across the map of it. Walk east along a high heather avenue and the glare warms your left temple; turn down a stepped stair into a shadowed trough and the cold air grazes your jaw, ten degrees sharper in three paces; climb toward the ridge-combs and the mist beads clean across your collarbones. Movement becomes a private weather. By walking, you give yourself a morning and an afternoon without asking the sky to break its promise.

So I would not belong to one garden-bed, or one roof-seam, or one household’s hearth. I would be a walker of the long runnels.

Every quarter tends its own terraces with astonishing patience—paring moss back to the line, resetting loose shingles, combing the high cedar frames above their roofs. Yet the city lies across the valley like a wide net, and between one stone cluster and the next stretch hundreds of paces of open aqueduct, mossy ramps, and half-forgotten catch-basins crossing boulder fields and sedge hollows where nobody lives close enough to watch them every waking. Those long connecting spans still need hands. A dwarf juniper’s root can push a hairline crack into an outlying channel; a curtain of pale beard-lichen can slip loose and gather against a sluice-stone, thinning the gleaming skin of water until a cistern three terraces down begins to lose its meniscus; mineral scale can build up grain by grain on an overflow notch until the water spills crooked and dampens a dry foundation wall far below.

That is where my hands would make sense.

Look down at them and you wouldn’t see the dark, permanent garden soil in the palm-lines, nor the heavy silvery sheen of a quarry-dresser’s wrists. You would see clean-scoured knuckles from cold water, faint gray dust under one thumbnail from fine stone-scraping, and a small calloused ridge along the inside of the index finger from pulling horsehair tight when a high-ridge comb goes slack. Pinned at my hip in a rolled strip of heavy felt would be everything I need: a bone scraper, two narrow bronze chisels no wider than a sedge-stalk, a coil of waxed plant-twine, a flat river-pebble for dressing a burred lip, and a small horn box of juniper resin.

Not a grand role—nothing that earns a title or sets me apart from the basin’s quiet rhythm. There would be a handful of us across the wide bowl, people whose wool cloaks are mottled with the dust of dry southern ridges and the damp of northern fern-troughs alike, whose felt boot-soles wear down quicker than anyone else’s and have to be stitched fresh with bone needles twice as often.

What I wouldn’t expect at first—what would only emerge after many turnings of walking those long intervals—is what the work turns you into.

It turns you into a listener of the whole city’s pulse.

Most people know the water-tempo of their own quarter by heart: the deep hollow *tock* of a courtyard’s wide stone snout answered six breaths later by the brighter drop of a narrow notch. Walking the links between quarters, you begin to hear how those rhythms belong to one another. You learn how the cold breath sliding off the cloud-pinned teeth feeds the upper eastern combs, how that water splits at a high triangular junction-stone, how one half slows through a marshy hollow of wild bracken before surfacing in the low western plazas with a tempo so measured it takes half a waking for its seven cisterns to come back into unison.

When I kneel beside a neglected runnel at the edge of an unbuilt hollow, I am not only clearing dead bracken or shaving a hair’s breadth of pale mineral crust from a carved lip. I am listening. Put your ear close to the wet vitreous stone and you can hear the faintest tension in the flow before a single drop swells at the spout. Shave away one grain of scale—barely enough to cloud a fingertip—and the drop hangs a fraction of a breath longer before letting go. *Tock.* Five breaths. *Tock.* Somewhere forty paces down the mossy slope, an overflow notch answers it clean and clear. Nobody walking past would notice anything had been mended, and that would be the exact measure of its rightness.

And because I walk between quarters that rarely crowd together, something else would settle into my pockets alongside the bone scraper and the river-stone: the basin’s uneven clocks.

In a world with no calendar and no seasons, time is strictly local. Down in the deep, shaded northern troughs, feather-moss climbs fast and plush, so *three trimmings ago* is a short span; up on the bright, windless southern ledges where the lichens scarcely shift, three trimmings might stretch across half a youth. A household of stone-dressers counts by worn chisels; a weaver counts by the thinning of a bone shuttle. Walking back and forth across the wide green intervals, I would become—quietly, almost without intending it—someone who knows how one terrace’s time fits against another’s.

I would never own a porch of my own, yet I would never be without a threshold. When my eyelids grow heavy—whether I am near the upper cedar-rims or down beside the marshy sedge-beds—I would step up to whatever stone hall lies closest, turn my palm upward toward the sun-side of the wall if I see a neighbor resting, or wait quietly by the woven rush-screen until someone comes out carrying a hamper of pale roots. There is always an empty fleece behind the heavy wool curtain in the inner room. I would unpin my mist-beaded cloak, pass from the cool glare into the dark, peat-warmed stillness of the *made night*, and let the faint earthy smell of bog-moss wrap around my ribs.

Before sleep takes me—or after, sitting cross-legged on the warm front slate with a piece of flatbread and cold dark berries from the cellar crocks—I would offer the four or five low-voiced sentences I had carried across the heather. Not news, not gossip; the basin has no appetite for clamor. Just small moorings: that the high combs above the eastern ridge are drinking heavier than usual and the lower catch-basins will brim tight soon; that the silver-white grazer was seen bedding down with two calves beneath the third corbelled bridge; that old Tam’s copper pin was laid in the peat embers, cold-hammered bright, and fastened at his grandson’s collar. Low words, pitched beneath the trickle of the runnels, gone into the moss almost as soon as they are spoken.

Even with the children, I think I would find a quiet kinship. They are the only others in the valley whose feet move faster than the water. Sometimes, passing through an outlying plaza, I would catch a small bright-eyed one slapping flat palms into a mirror-still basin just to watch the bulging surface tension break and sheet over the carved rim. I wouldn’t scold them, any more than the elders do. I might only kneel beside the wet stone and lay my palm against the shadowed wall of the aqueduct, motioning them to press their ear there too—waiting until their breathless laughter drops away and they hear the faint, glass-smooth slide of cold water running inside the rock before it ever appears at the spout.

And once in a long while, out beyond the last terraces where the city’s net loosens into raw triangular scree climbing toward the eastern teeth, I would come across someone carrying too much light.

You can tell them from a quarter-mile away—not by anything dramatic, only by the too-quick set of their shoulders, or the way they sit hugging their knees on a bare shard of vitreous stone staring up at the lone white band of cloud as if trying to push it loose with their eyes. A hamper of flatbread sits half-opened beside their boots.

I wouldn’t walk right up and intrude on their air, and I wouldn’t try to talk them back down into calm. I know that tightness in the chest too well to pretend a few soft words can untie it. If the slope allows, I would simply stop twenty paces off on a sunlit slab, check the tautness of a nearby comb-post or pretend to dress the edge of my bronze chisel with a wet pebble, letting my own slow breathing sit beside theirs in the wide, empty cobalt stillness. Sometimes, after a long space, they pick up their hamper and walk back down the mossy ramps beside me without either of us speaking until we reach the first brimming cistern. Once or twice in a lifetime, they rise instead, look back once across the vast net of stone causeways and dark green troughs, and keep climbing toward the knife-edge beneath the cloud. Then I would only touch two fingers to my copper pin, watch until their gray wool merges with the raw dark slate, and turn back down the hill to keep the runnels clear.

When my own joints eventually stiffen and the long stairs between quarters grow too steep for my knees, I imagine I would stop walking the whole bowl and settle near one of those outlying junctions where three aqueducts meet above a deep fern-trough—somewhere I can sit with my back against sun-warmed ashlar while my feet rest an inch from the cool shade, listening to the braided *tock* of the basins below and teaching a restless youth how to hear when a stone lip has grown a grain of mineral scale.

And when my breath finally thins inside the peat-warmed dark, my copper pin—thinned a little from all the thumb-rests along the causeways—will go into the red heart of a brazier, soften, and be hammered clean for another collar. Four or five quiet figures from whichever quarter took me in will carry me out into one of the broad, unbuilt intervals I walked all my life, and lay me gently into the dripping emerald velvet beneath the bracken. No mound, no shadow cast where none should be—only the sphagnum climbing slowly over the pale wool until I fold back into the cold peat that holds the valley’s water.

Until then, though, you would find me out in the open air between one roof and the next. Kneeling beside a brim-full basin at the edge of a heather slope, skimming a fallen juniper needle from the glass-taut surface with two fingers so the mirror stays unbroken. One hand resting on dry, faintly warm stone; the other, three inches away, slick and cold in the moss. Listening for the next clear drop to fall, letting the light shift across my cheekbones as I stand, shoulder my felt pouch, and walk on.
