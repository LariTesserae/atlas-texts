At first I think I might stay in the visitor’s boots—just long enough to look around, to crouch with watering eyes beside the six mud-bricks and watch the skip-moths blink their pale yellow ghosts across the ceiling. But the longer I stand in that sealed noon, the less that body feels like mine. Too much heat. Too much noise leaking through the limestone every time my pulse beats against my soles. And behind my heels, that thick, untended spill of surface shadow drags like a wet coat I keep forgetting to watch. I would not last a day in a room where even my own silhouette is an open latch.

Nor would I be the mason. I feel an enormous, quiet awe for that curved spine, those long knuckles worn pearlescent from arguing with wet rock hour after hour so the dome does not heal shut. That is a kind of stubbornness made of lime and bone, a willingness to keep pinching boundaries into a floor that forgets them before the next rain. My patience isn't built like that. I do not know how to hold a ceiling up with my bare hands.

So I let go of height. I let go of boots, and warm blood, and the need to stand upright under a low sky of stone.

When I look around the pocket for where my attention actually settles, it isn't on the glaring open center where the twig-hunters streak, nor inside the solid rock where the hair-thin worms thread without leaving a mark. It goes straight to the seams—the damp, hairline joints where two unfinished slabs meet and haven't quite decided to become one piece.

For a little while I try on the stillness of the things already living there. A thread of colorless silver moss, drinking raw brightness and condensation. One of those glassy little lichen-cups, bleached clean of green, holding a bead of mineral water up to the sourceless white glare. There is real peace in that: no shadow wide enough to snag, no need to keep track of which direction is six paces and which is three. You just sit in the wet crease and let the pocket tighten and loosen around you.

Only—it is too deaf. A lichen-cup doesn't notice when the far wall slips closer. It doesn't feel the difference between the sudden, indifferent *tick* of a fresh ridge splitting out of the floor and the small, deliberate *tick* of a glossy pebble being set down against the rim of a thumb-sized niche.

Something in me wants to hear that difference. Wants to move, if only a little, when the room rearranges its inches.

So the glassy cup doesn't stay rooted. It loosens its underside from the wet joint, stretches out along the seam until it is about the length of a human thumb—narrow, flattened, low enough that the glaring ceiling feels miles away. Along its back, thin overlapping plates grow out of the same lime-rich water that sweats from the walls: not matte chalk like the mason’s skin, and not opaque waxy chert, but clear, papery layers of calcite, ribbed like the inside of a small shell and fragile as dried salt-crust.

Beneath those plates, there is only a pale, wet foot—cool as the cellar floor, clinging flush to the damp limestone.

I don't keep eyes. What use are eyes down here, where there is no dusk to read and the glare is so raw that even the mason has to slide milky lids across its irises just to bear it? Instead, the underside of my body is all listening. Every hairline rim along my clear plates is hollow, tiny fluted chambers open downward to the stone and barely a sliver upward into the dead air. What the mason has to lower its bare heel or jaw to catch, I live inside continuously.

And right away, the room changes shape around me.

Without eyes, the blinding white flatness disappears, and the pocket stops feeling empty or silent at all. Through the wet stone beneath my foot, the whole buried cellar is murmuring in tiny fractures of pressure. I can feel the slow, soap-soft swelling of a calcite blister three paces off—or six, depending on how the floor is folded today—long before it breaks the surface. I can feel the faint, rhythmic thrum of the mason’s weight shifting near the mud-brick threshold, the hairline shiver of a balanced slate arch losing its nerve a heartbeat before it dissolves into loose flakes. Even the invisible grazing of a ribbon of flat-bodied scavengers along the next seam reaches me as a faint, papery itch in the mineral.

Because I am nearly clear—layered glass over cool pale fluid—the room’s strangest rule bends around me in an unexpected way.

Under the sourceless glare, I cannot cast a dark smudge. The brightness doesn't stop at my back; it passes through my thin calcite ribs, bends through the damp chambers inside me, and pools an inch off my flank as a narrow, watery bow of concentrated light—a bright crescent laying itself across the white stone, lagging slightly whenever I glide along a crack.

The first time a twig-hunter spots that lagging shape, it shoots across the flat with its backward-jointed knees and flaring filaments, aiming straight for the curved bright edge trailing beside me. Its hooked foreleg snaps down on bare limestone. Nothing locks. Nothing stiffens. A shadow of darkness can be pinned because darkness is an absence you can hold still; a smudge of doubled light has nothing for a hook to catch. The hunter pauses for half a beat, sensory tufts twitching against the small glare it cannot pierce, and slips noiselessly back beneath a flake of slate.

Still, I am not invulnerable. If the stone beneath me cures too fast while I am resting, my wet rim could stiffen into the floor and become just another pale ridge the room absorbs. So I keep moving—not out into the glaring open stretches, never in a hurry, just sliding slowly along the dampest joints where the rock stays yielding and cool.

And when I want to cross from the north seam to the mud-brick threshold near the center, I don't crawl the whole bare width. I learn to use the pocket’s forgetfulness instead of fighting it.

The room knows how big it is in total, only not how its inches are arranged. When the far wall looks six paces off and takes three to walk, those missing three paces haven't vanished; they are pleated tightly into the wet seams, bunched like folded cloth along the edges where the floor hasn't committed to solidity. I tuck myself deep into one of those damp creases, pull my clear plates flat against the limestone, and wait for the subtle pressure shift—the faint tug in the rock when the pocket pulls itself half an inch tighter. When the pleat relaxes and shifts its slack across the cellar, I ride the seam with it. A span that would have taken me an hour of slow, wet gliding collapses beneath my foot in the space of three breaths, and I emerge quietly beside the base of a mud-brick, tasting fresh unhardened lime.

That is mostly what I feed on: the thin mineral milk weeping from fresh cracks, the microscopic salt-bloom gathering in the joints, the pale dust of shed grazer husks washed down when the ceiling sweats.

When the rain comes—when the air tightens and the glare sharpens by that single hair-thin degree—I feel it first as a change in the pitch of the dome overhead. Then the slow, dandelion-drift drops begin to fall over one narrow patch of stone, silent as snow. Some vanish before they reach the floor, reappearing as wide wet rings that shiver cleanly through my underside without making a sound in the air.

During those five frantic minutes, my clear chambers fill with cold runoff. Translucent grazers pour straight over my ribbed back in silver streams, their eyelash legs tickling my calcite plates like tiny dry needles. Above me, the skip-moths flicker so thickly from wall to wall that their cool yellow afterimages hang in the air right beside my seam—and for a heartbeat at a time, my own clear body catches that fading gold and glows gently from within before returning to pale salt-glass. I stay tucked beneath the lip of a mud-brick or the north ledge, drinking, letting the storm of silent appetite wash past without ever needing to be part of the ambush.

Then the ceiling dries. The blisters push up. The slate arch collapses.

And this—this is where I finally understand what I am doing here, why I didn't end up as moss or a grazer or a pebble in a niche.

The room forgets how to carry sound. Drop a stone into water and the rings spread in absolute quiet; exhale and your breath dies an inch past your lips. A chamber with no echo has no way to feel its own edges. All it has are the sharpest, briefest fractures of vibration traveling straight through solid rock—instantaneous, unanswering, gone the moment they happen.

Except when they pass through me.

My hollow, fluted calcite plates sit pressed directly to the damp limestone, holding tiny pockets of mineral water and thin-walled mineral air. When a sharp vibration travels through the floor—the *tick* of a splitting quartz ridge, the soft peel of a sole lifting from wet stone, or best of all, the clean, deliberate *tick* when the mason rolls a glossy wet pebble across its chalky palm and sets it against the lip of the next north niche—the fracture enters my underside and does not vanish at once.

It catches inside my thin ribs. It rings cleanly around my tiny hollow chambers for two slow heartbeats, softening its razor edge, and then spills back down through my wet foot into the limestone—a fraction quieter, a hairline later.

A tiny, delayed mineral chime. An echo, in a room that forgot what an echo is.

I usually live along the north seam, tucked just below the vertical lip where those three thumb-sized niches sit. It is damp there, cool, rarely crossed by the twig-hunters, and close to the mason’s patient clock. After every downpour, when the dome goes still and the white glare settles back into its long waiting, I feel the mason’s crouched weight fold down a handspan above my crease. I feel the dry brush of its heel against the stone. Then comes the pebble—*tick*—ringing crisp through the limestone.

Two heartbeats later, from the hairline joint beneath its knee, I give it back. *Tick.* Softer, rounded by water and thin glass, traveling just far enough through the rock to reach the bones of the mason’s bare heel and the flat of its jaw.

I don't think it knows, at first, what is answering it—whether the stone itself has finally learned to hold a note, or whether some pocket of trapped air beneath the floor is ringing back. Then one afternoon—if noon sealed underground can have an afternoon—its pale eyes drift down beneath the milky veils of its inner lids, past the three wet niches, past the silver moss, and pause on the faint crescent of doubled brightness lying an inch off my seam.

It crouches completely still for a long time, the way it crouches when it is deciding whether a flake of slate will bear the weight of an arch.

Slowly, with that same economical tenderness it uses to lift a folded amphibian onto its palm, it extends one long hand and lowers a single glossy, calcite-capped fingertip—not onto my fragile plates, which would crush under even a gentle touch, but straight down onto the narrow bright bow of light lagging beside me on the wet floor.

If I were anything else in this pocket, that touch would lock me rigid as stone. Instead, because my shadow is only gathered glare, the pressure of its fingertip against that bright crescent does something entirely different. A cool, clean shiver runs straight across the inch of bare stone between my light and my shell, waking every fluted chamber along my back at once.

I ring—not loud enough to stir a grain of dust in the dead air, just a clear, sustained mineral hum that travels straight from the limestone up through the pearlescent cap of the mason’s finger, into its thin wrist, all the way to its back teeth.

For two heartbeats, neither of us moves. I feel the slow, cool pace of its pulse, the chalky dryness of its knuckles, the exact, honest distance between the north wall and the center of the room measured cleanly in the time it takes a tiny vibration to go out and return.

Then it lifts its finger. My clear chambers settle back into quiet. The mason shifts one bare heel along the crack, collapses six paces into two, and goes back to pinching fresh mud-bricks out of the soap-soft floor before the pocket remembers how to harden.

And when something stranger happens—when the clay far overhead splits open, and a clumsy, hot-blooded storm drops down into the glare in heavy boots, trailing a real sky-shadow behind its calves—I feel it from my seam beneath the north niches before it even finishes blinking the tears out of its eyes. Every shift of its weight thuds through my ribs like distant thunder. Its heartbeat rolls across the limestone so loud and warm I have to pull my translucent plates tight against the rock just to hold it.

I stay tucked in my wet joint while the mason steps cleanly along the cracks, flicks a drop of condensation across a twig-hunter’s path, and touches a wet slate flake with feather-light curiosity to the outer edge of that astonishing darkness.

And when the mason returns to the north wall, six paces away in a single step, and sets its glossy pebble back into its hollow with a crisp mineral *tick*, I wait my two quiet heartbeats in the glare—and answer it, thin and clear through the stone, reminding the room how far away its own walls are, and that it is still hollow, and still heard.
