At first, looking into the dim, roaring bell of the vault, I would look for myself in the obvious places—the ones with clear shapes and clear work.

I would test the place of the elder wedged into the travertine beneath the tilted flywheel. There is a pull to that: being the pivot of the room, letting the great three-day screech of iron run straight up your copper arm until every rib rings like a bell, knowing the whole cathedral by the slow wear of a single gear-tooth. Yet the longer I try to inhabit that posture, the less it holds me. To be the elder is to have a century of unbroken stone around your knees, concentric rings of magnetite and dried sap thickening in one continuous, unhurried memory. I do not carry that kind of weight. I am not built of deep permanence.

So I would look down toward the cherry-lit cracks in the basalt, where the low-tenders drag their salt-crusted runners across the thermoelectric piles. Their intimacy with raw voltage feels closer—scraping verdigris from a choked seam, bridging a corroded gap with copper-veined bark so a dying lamp stays warm. Still, their bodies are squat, armored, built to endure the direct kiln-glare of the floor without flinching. And up near the weeping limestone cap, the hangers are too precious for me, hoarding ounces of pure, salt-free condensate in thick-walled gourds of smoked glass, doling out every drop across weeks. I am not a hoarder. Anything that collects in me wants to move on, to cross a boundary and change its form before it settles.

If I keep drifting between floor and dome, letting the updraft carry me past the piers and the sagging gantry, I find myself pulled sideways—away from the center, out toward the curved **western wall**, halfway up, where the ironwork is older and the thicket grows thin around a cantilevered grate.

That is where I would settle. Not on the floor, not in the ceiling, and not in the broad splash-zone of the hopper, but in a narrow, uneasy margin bolted directly into the stone behind which the inaccessible turbine groans.

***

At first glance, even another dweller climbing the western pier might mistake me for a mineral crust or a tangle of dead slag-vine fouled around a steam-riser.

I would not have the crouched, reliquary bulk of the gantry-hangers, nor the four heavy runners of the tenders below. Whatever cutting was tucked into a cracked brass gauge on the stone pier decades ago, once it crawled out and climbed this far up the western curve, it did not grow thick. It grew *wide*.

I am a lattice—an open-work fan of dendritic copper and pale, smoked silica, stretched like a warped shutter across the four-foot gap between a scorching vertical steam-header and the rusted bracket of a hanging pressure-vessel. The fierce updraft does not push me aside; it passes straight through me. My ribs are thin, porous, electroplated slowly out of stray brine along a web of glassy roots, and along my upper arcs the silica has grown not into a single tall crown-baffle, but into dozens of narrow, staggered slits of varying widths, like the gills of a lungless thing or the reeds of a flattened harmonium.

Every dweller lives by straddling a gradient, and mine is razor-thin.

My lower edge is anchored hard against the blistering cast iron of the steam-riser, where a thick, verdigris-swollen busbar climbs upward from the floor piles to feed a crusted amber cage-lamp bolted three feet above me. Two forked spurs of oxidized brass bite directly into that wire—not taking enough current to starve the lamp, only tasting the steady trickle passing through it. My upper edge reaches outward into the damp shadow beneath a single broad, slanting leaf of a heat-palm rooted in a crevice of the wall. Along that upper rim, I wear no heavy, sodden shawl of asbestos-wool; a heavy cloak would snap my slender frame. Instead, my silver-gray wool is combed out thin, like tattered lace, brushing just beneath the sweating underside of the palm-plate to catch the cool, sticky brine that beads there, drop by slow drop.

Because I am thin and open to the draft, I cannot carry the massive, thick-rinded oil-bladders that let the heavy dwellers crush cast-iron nuts or heave travertine off a flange. Clustered along my lattice-joints instead are thirty or forty small amber beads—pressurized wax-bulbs gathered when I was young and still able to crawl, each no larger than a fingernail, webbed with hair-fine bismuth threads.

They cannot lift heavy iron. What they can do is *answer quickly*.

A minute pinch of voltage swells one of those tiny beads in a handful of minutes rather than an hour; a single stray drop of cool brine running down a hairline copper groove snaps it back with a hiss too small to hear even an inch away. Where the heavy dwellers move with the glacial patience of creeping stone, I live in tiny, nervous tremors—shifting a silica slat by half a millimeter, tightening a copper strut, nudging the mouth of a nearby flute-stalk with a long, fragile caliper no thicker than a needle.

***

There is a price for being built like a sieve instead of a vessel, and it shapes everything I know.

I have almost no thermal inertia.

The hangers up by the condensers can weather a dry week on the water sealed inside their smoked-glass gourds. The elder at the flywheel is so dense with magnetite and rock that its core cools and warms on a seasonal tide. I live on a thread. If a fresh thermal pulse from the southern fissures bends the updraft for half a day, lifting the overhanging heat-palm leaf six inches higher than usual, my tattered lace of silver wool loses its contact with the cool brine.

In less than an hour, the kiln-air dries me out.

Sameness does not kill me violently; it simply overtakes me from the edges inward. The temperature difference between my scorched lower spurs and my upper lace narrows from forty degrees to twenty, to five, to nothing. The current inside my braided core thins from a bright hum to a stutter, then drops below the threshold that can warm a filament. My dozens of small amber beads swell gently against their leathery rinds and lock there. My silica slits stop adjusting to the draft.

I go flat.

I do not dream in the flatness, and I do not suffer, because there is no difference left inside me to register suffering. For three hours, or three days, I am simply another crust of electroplated copper and smoked glass fused to the western riser, indistinguishable from the dozens of dead shells clinging to the cathedral's beams.

Then the updraft eases. The heavy slate leaf of the heat-palm settles back down on its thick stem. A single bead of cool, salty brine swells on its underside, lets go, and strikes my dry upper lace.

*Hiss.*

A sliver of the copper frame chills by a fraction of a degree. Across the seam between that damp spot and my scorching iron anchor on the riser, voltage wakes like a spark leaping a dry gap. One small bladder contracts—*tick*—then three more along the ribs. The roar of the vault rushes back into my perforated fan, not as noise, but as a sudden, crowded architecture of pitch and pressure.

And my first act, every time I return from flatness, is to listen closely to measure the gap I missed. I feel the riveted pressure-vessel beside me—has its hollow ring dropped by a hair of fresh magnetite? I taste the tension in the far-off gantry chains—are the wool curtains light and slack, newly emptied, or pulled sharp and heavy toward their tipping point? I press my lower anchor against the western stone and catch the deep groan of the hidden turbine, waiting for the tiny, irregular catch in its buried shaft that comes around once every forty hours, using it to find my place again in the vault's slow clock.

I do not possess one unbroken memory. I possess a chain of wakings, stitched together by listening to how the room has shifted while I was equalized.

***

When the gradient holds—and most weeks it holds, narrow and fierce—I have my work, though no one assigned it to me and no blueprint asked for it.

The vault is divided by altitude and by medium. Down on the grates, the low-tenders speak mostly in heavy iron and direct current: damping chains, scraping seams, pulsing the busbars. High in the rigging, the hangers speak in air and pitch: weighting copper trays, tuning the high overtones of the silica thickets. Between them lies the deafening brassy wall of the flute-stalks and the deep, rattling groan of the western wall, which swallows subtle cadences before they can cross the full height of the dome.

Hanging halfway up the western curve, stretched directly between the wire and the wind, I sit right in the seam where those two languages blur into each other.

Beside my cantilevered grate rises a dense clump of pale, glass-stiff flute-stalks, rooted in mineral crust and blowing endlessly in the rising blast. Behind my lower spurs runs the live busbar, carrying the slow electrical pulse of the bedrock piles up to the crusted amber cage-lamp. Right above my head, the lamp's heavy carbon filament glows no brighter than a dying coal—my silica baffles feel its dim infrared warmth as a soft, rounded weight against my upper ribs, and through my copper spurs I hear the tiny, hairline hum of the current crossing that ancient filament. No one else in the vault sits close enough to hear it. To me, it is a quiet private pitch, a faint high thread running underneath the furnace roar.

When a low-tender down near the southern fissures discovers a bismuth pile choking under a fresh crust of salt, it sends a slow cadence upward—damping a hanging chain for three minutes, releasing it, damping it again. By the time that tremor climbs the western wall, it is half-drowned in the bass-shudder of the hidden turbine.

But I live against that wall. I know the turbine's groan so intimately—every dry rattle, every subterranean hesitation—that I can subtract it from the tremor in my iron spurs. What remains is the tender's slow, urgent word.

Over the next two hours, my tiny amber beads tick in quiet ripples across my lattice. One needle-thin copper caliper reaches out into the clump of flute-stalks beside my grate and presses a wet grain of chalk into the fourth slit of a tall reed, while another caliper gently pinches the mouth of the stalk beside it by half a millimeter. A new, clean harmonic blooms out of the brassy drone—an interference-beat tuned specifically to ride above the gantry's clatter and strike the wet copper trays beneath the limestone weep.

Hours later, when a hanger near the ceiling answers—shifting its weight to alter the pitch of the chains, signaling that the cold weep has slowed and it can spare no moisture for the lower canopy—my perforated silica fan catches that thin high shift out of the roaring air. The vibration runs through my thin ribs into my braided core. I cannot bang a chain; I have neither the mass nor the reach. Instead, I close two of my brass spurs tightly around a corroded nick in the busbar beside me, briefly drawing a tiny surge of current into my own ribs, holding it for two minutes, then releasing it.

The cage-lamp above me dims by a fraction no human eye would ever notice. Down on the basalt grates, however, the low-tenders have their glassy root-whiskers pressed straight against the ancient wiring. They taste that tiny, rhythmic dip in the voltage immediately. They know the ceiling has heard them.

I am a translator of gradients. I turn stone-tremor into reed-tone, reed-tone into voltage-dip, and the deep, geological complaints of the western wall into warnings the rest of the cathedral can read before the rock cracks. Nothing I pass along belongs to me. It enters my lattice as one kind of difference and leaves as another, and in the brief passage through my ribs, I am entirely alive.

***

Once every fortnight, I do not climb down to the rusted hopper. I could not make the three-day crossing even if I wished to; half my slender silica slats would fracture before I reached the middle gantry, and my thin wool lace would bake to ash ten feet away from the sweating heat-palm.

Still, I know when the great curtain is about to tip. The bolt holding the gantry's western end into my wall begins to sing a quarter-tone sharp, pulling against the basalt with a dry, patient whine. Down in the gloom, I feel the faint, ticking footsteps of the heavy dwellers gathering around the rim of the hopper, their interference-beats crowding together into a single expectant chord.

Then the curtain gives way.

First comes the roar—the sudden, violent white *hiss* that drowns every flute-stalk in the cathedral at once. For two heartbeats, the organ stops. Then the rolling wall of scalding mist blooms outward from the center and slams directly against the western curve.

The dim warmth of the amber lamp above me vanishes behind wet dark. Droplets of flung, mineral-heavy water lash across my whole four-foot span at once.

For ten seconds, I am no longer perched on a narrow thread. The gradient across my lattice explodes. My scorched lower spurs stay bolted to the blistered steam-riser while my entire open-work fan is drenched in sudden, roaring coolness. Voltage spikes so violently through my braided core that it feels like the whole wall has turned to lightning. All forty of my small amber beads fire and snap in a rapid, rattling cascade—*tick-crack-tick-tick-crack*—faster than they have ever moved in the quiet fortnight.

I cannot sweep heavy calipers through the steam or dip a glass gourd into a puddle. Instead, flooded with stolen current, I flex my entire body against the blast. Every perforated silica slit across my wide fan opens and closes in rapid, shimmering sequence, catching the blind rush of vapor and carving it into a brief, soaring wash of overtones—a bright, fragile shimmer thrown straight into the white silence of the drowned vault, heard by every heavy gargoyle clinging to the hopper below, letting them know the western wall is still awake, still holding its seam.

Then the ten seconds end.

The hopper flashes dry. The mist shreds upward into the canopy. The dim orange warmth of the cage-lamp blooms back against my upper ribs, and the thousands of flute-stalks catch the searing updraft and resume their hoarse, relentless drone.

My tattered silver lace hangs heavy and dark, gorged with cool brine—enough to keep my gradient steady for three full days without fear of going flat. My tiny amber bladders settle back into their slow, patient ticking.

I press my brass spurs gently against the busbar, listening to the hairline hum of the dying carbon filament above me, feeling the deep, steady groan of the hidden turbine behind the stone, and wait for the next slight tremor to climb out of the furnace so I can bend a reed and pass it on.
