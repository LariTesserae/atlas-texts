I would not be at the center of the widest plaza, and I would not yet be standing motionless with the elders beside the weeping arches.

If I look for where I actually land in this basin—where my weight settles before I have even decided what shape I have—it is three terraces down from the northern rim, on the quiet side of an unfinished latticework vault.

Not quiet in any sense a stranger would recognize. The roar is still a bright, solid wall pressing against the back of the eyes, and the hexagons under my soles still thrum with the unseen rivers pumping beneath the plateau. But here, right where a cantilevered rib stops cleanly in midair above a low rectilinear basin, two of the valley’s permanent notes cross each other: the reedy, high pitch of a perforated stalk a quarter-mile east, and the deeper, wet booming of a fluted colonnade to the south. Where their standing waves meet and fold over one another, a narrow trough opens—hardly two paces wide—where the flood of sound thins by a fraction of a hair.

If I crouch there, letting the lukewarm film of runoff slip cleanly through the grooves of my soles without splash or suction, and draw the membranes along my throat and upper ribs just tight enough to filter out the broad roar, I can hear the single drops detaching from the swollen growth-tips overhead. *Tok.* A slow count. *Tok.* An ordinary, small sound, surviving inside a permanent thunder.

That is where I find myself. And as I stay crouched in that narrow seam of clearer air, the rest of what I am comes into focus.

I am one of the dwellers, though neither young enough to chase across the slick outer lip above the clouds nor old enough to have grown heavy with alabaster. If I straighten—which I rarely do under a sky this bare, with no roof to measure height against—I am as tall as any of them, long-limbed and narrow-hipped, skin slick as wet river-pearl in the shade under the vault and warmed to pale ochre across my shoulder blades where the steady daylight finds me. My inner eyelids stay half-drawn against the glare off the flooded jade.

What tells me where I am in my life is my left wrist, and the right notch of my collarbone.

Two seasons ago—measured only by how many gliders have fallen and how many elders have been carried to the rim—the first thin ridge of clouded quartz pushed cleanly through the skin along the outer edge of my forearm. It did not hurt; it arrived the way a winter frost might arrive in a colder world, except here it is made of all the sweet, mineral-bright runoff I have ever swallowed. The constant mist has polished it glass-smooth. It is still slender, nothing like the broad white plates that stiffen the shins and brow-ridges of the oldest among us—just a fine, pale blade growing out of my own bone.

The one on my collarbone is smaller still, barely a smooth shelf under the skin, yet it has already changed my private voice. When I step close to another dweller in the open glare of a plaza and press my collarbone or my forearm to theirs, letting the small air-filled chamber behind my sternum hum directly into their jaw, the new mineral catches the vibration and gives it a sharper, brighter ring than soft flesh ever did. The first time an elder pressed their brow against my wrist after the ridge broke through, they hummed a brief, wry interval straight into my bones—something between recognition and amusement. *You are beginning to taste like the chimneys.*

It feels strange to realize that in a basin that refuses to age, *we* are the only things that accumulate time. The pavement will not hold a scuff, a crack, or a stain. Only our bones remember how long we have been drinking the valley’s sap.

---

I am not one of the bold callers who climb high into the slick interiors of the acoustic chimneys to shout news across three miles of bright water. Sometimes I join in—when a pale parchment glider finally falters on the updrafts and spirals down onto a distant hexagon, or when a long braided song passes across the terraces and someone needs a falling curtain of runoff divided with a cured wing-sheet to split a steady drumroll into a double beat. It is a fierce pleasure to step into a silver sheet of water and bend a quarter-mile of noise with the edge of a hand.

Mostly, though, I listen for the moment right after a call ends.

When someone lifts their palm from an oval vent far off across the basin, the tower’s pitch snaps back to its permanent note—and for a fraction of a second, as the compressed air rushes back into its old groove, there is a tiny shudder in the overtones, like water closing over a dipped finger. That brief hesitation before the valley returns to itself is what holds my attention.

And it leads directly to the habit that defines my days.

Everyone here writes on the living floor. You see it on every sunlit terrace: pairs kneeling across a broad hexagon in silent argument, cutting swift intersecting arcs with quartz snail-shell before their opening claims swell shut; young ones laughing in sharp chest-hums as they sketch sprawling grazers whose legs heal away behind them before they can finish the heads; whole groups keeping a complex calculation of vents alive for hours in quiet relay, walking the glowing beaded lines over and over to hold them open against the jade.

I rarely start a diagram of my own. Instead, I walk the plazas looking for lines that have reached their forty-fifth minute.

You learn to recognize them from twenty yards off, even in the bright wet glare. A fresh incision is unmistakable—sharp as spun glass, packed with bright clear beads of sap that catch the sun. At twenty minutes, a shallow scratch is already gone. By forty-five minutes, even a deep score pressed down to the firmer cambium has begun to surrender. The beads have flattened into a faint, milky thread; the waxy bleached-jade walls on either side are drawing smoothly back together with that stubborn, quiet perfection that leaves no scar behind.

That is when I kneel beside them.

Often the ones who cut the lines have long since wandered off—back to float face-up in a lukewarm basin, or down to the fluted undersides of a stair to pry quartz snails from the mist. What they leave behind is a thought in its last quarter-hour.

I kneel in the thin gliding water, tilt my head to catch the pale seam against the reflection of the sky, and read what is almost gone.

Sometimes it is half of an old argument whose first premise has already sealed into smooth blank green, leaving only the rebuttal hanging alone on the hexagon like an answer to a vanished question. Sometimes it is the outer curve of a borrowed shadow—the place where two dwellers climbed an unfinished arch an hour ago and locked fingertips across the two-foot gap so a third could trace the phantom keystone on the floor below. By the time I arrive, the two piers of the drawing have healed away completely, and only the top of the completed arch remains, a thin silver crescent floating nowhere on the wet pavement, narrowing as I watch.

Sometimes I do nothing at all. I just sit back on my heels, feel the floor thrum up through my shins, and stay with the line until it seals. It feels right that someone should see the last shape a thought takes before the valley wins it back.

Other times, when a curve is down to its final five minutes—barely a hairline pale shimmer on the jade—I raise the mineralized edge of my left wrist and make a shallow scratch beside it. Not over it; I am not trying to join a relay or force something to last. Just a twenty-minute mark extending a dying arc three inches past where the original drawer stopped, or marking the small shift in the eastern chimney’s pitch that happened to sound at the exact moment their groove vanished. A brief overlap, like catching a single drop on the back of a hand before it rejoins the runoff. Then I watch my own line soften and disappear behind theirs, until the hexagonal plate is as immaculate as the morning it first swelled out of the dark.

---

There is one thing in the basin, though, that I *do* try to trace from the beginning, even though I know I can never keep up with it.

The pale-ochre grazers.

Most of us give them a wide, uneasy berth, and I understand why. Our entire world is legible through the taut soundboard of the floor. Lie flat with your cheek in the warm film of water and you can read the valley for half a mile: the crisp double rhythm of two young ones racing down a spillway, the slower, heavier tread of an elder carrying their quartz weight toward a sunlit ledge, the tiny tremor of a dropped shell-shard. Nothing moves without announcing itself to the soles of our feet.

Except the grazers.

Their wide rings of perforated cartilage pass through the gliding runoff without splash, without suction, without leaving a single ripple in the drum-tight plates. You can read an empty plaza for ten minutes, lift your head, and find one standing thirty paces away beside a weeping arch, lifting its slender trough-like jaw into a metronomic stream of sweet exudate as if it had stepped straight out of the glare.

They do not feel like ghosts to me. They feel like readers of a different text.

One long, bright afternoon—hours that blurred together without a single cloud shifting across the sun—I stopped giving them a wide berth and began following one at a respectful distance, keeping a whole terrace-width of bare pavement between us.

At first it seemed to drift without pattern between the half-formed pavilions. Then I noticed it never crossed an open plaza in a straight line. Between a colonnade and a cantilevered stair, where nothing stood in its way except three hundred yards of glistening hexagons, it curved sharply south, paused for three full minutes with its narrow keel-shaped flanks trembling faintly, turned at a crisp angle, and paced steadily along an invisible line until it reached the next weeping bud.

When it moved on, I glided across the wet floor to the exact hexagon where it had paused.

I knelt. I dropped my chin toward the gliding water. I tightened the listening-membranes along my throat, tuning out the broad boom of the valley until the fine structure of the noise separated into layers.

And there it was.

A razor-thin seam where two distant chimneys crossed and cancelled, just like my narrow pocket under the northern latticework vault—except this one did not sit still in one spot. It ran in a clean, curved corridor right across the empty plaza, bounded on either side by rising ridges of sound pressure where the echoes from the fluted walls doubled back on themselves.

The grazer was not wandering through emptiness. It was walking down a hallway.

I stayed kneeling on that wet hexagon for a long time, staring across the glaring open expanse where nothing was built and nothing encroached. All our lives, we lean across the two-foot gaps of the unfinished arches, stretch braided sinew between cantilevered ribs, and trace borrowed shadows on the floor, trying to glimpse the phantom rooms the valley stopped building on the morning of its sprouting. And all along, the grazers have been moving calmly through an entire second architecture—rooms and corridors walled not in meristem and callus tissue, but in standing waves of unvarying noise.

Now, whenever I spot one of those thin vertical silhouettes drifting across a lower terrace, I follow twenty paces behind it with a curved lip of quartz snail-shell in my fingers. Wherever its perforated feet lift silently from the runoff, I drop to one knee and score a quick, shallow arc into the jade.

I never finish a map. A grazer moves slowly, and my shallow lines hold for twenty minutes; by the time it reaches the far colonnade, the first half of its corridor has already healed smoothly back into blank green behind me. All I ever make is a drifting, luminous tail of beaded sap—forty yards of invisible hallway briefly made visible in the bright daylight—sliding across the hexagons behind a creature that never looks back, and vanishing cleanly as we go.

Sometimes another dweller catches sight of me doing it from a higher terrace. Once, a pair of them came down across the spillways, curious, and stood watching my disappearing wake until one stepped close and pressed the flat of their cool forearm against mine.

*What are you keeping open?* their sternum hummed into my wrist—a quick, quizzical triplet.

I did not try to explain it in bone-hum; our private voice is better for feelings, intervals, and invitations than for theories. Instead, I reached out, took them gently by the elbow, and guided them two paces to the left, straight onto the narrow, fading sap-line I had just cut. I tapped the membrane along my own throat, then pointed across the glaring plaza to where the grazer’s pale silhouette was turning sharply around nothing at all.

They stood still. Their throat-bands rippled, tightened, narrowed. After a long moment their translucent inner lids drew slightly back, dark amber eyes widening in the wet glare as they felt the invisible wall of sound pressure rise on their left shoulder and drop cleanly away on their right.

Without a word, they pulled a shard of snail-shell from their waist-sinew, knelt beside the last silver trace of my twenty-minute line before it closed, and cut it deeper—down to the firm cambium, buying us an hour.

For the rest of that bright stretch of daylight, the three of us followed the grazer across four terraces in quiet relay, cutting the hallway of sound into the wet green floor behind it, watching the corridors of noise briefly gleam like spun glass beneath the open sky before the valley drank them back.

---

I know what is coming, eventually. You cannot live here and pretend otherwise.

Every time I drink the dense, syrupy drops from a weeping arch, or tilt my face into the sharp salty runoff of a hollow chimney, a fraction of it stays behind in me. Next season the quartz ridge on my left wrist will thicken; a few years after that, pale plates will begin to surface along my shins and the sharp line of my brow. My crouched glide across the spillways will slow. My chest-hum will grow drier, more resonant, more like the hollow stalks themselves.

One day I will find myself spending longer hours standing beside the swollen, glossy growth-tips at the ends of the unfinished ribs, resting my broad, mineral-rimmed palms against the taut embryonic skin.

I have already touched them a few times, just to see what the elders feel when they stand there so still.

It is not calm. That surprised me the first time. Beneath that smooth, glistening green surface—frozen forever five minutes into its sprouting—the pressure is astonishing. You feel the immense vascular rivers beneath the floor shoving upward into a boundary of cells that refuses to divide by even a hair’s breadth. It feels like a chest holding a hum behind closed teeth for a hundred years.

And I do not think the elders are trying to wake it, nor trying to hush it. I think they are simply pressing their mineral palms against the only other thing in the valley that knows what it is to hold a shape against immense pressure—to stay poised between becoming and dissolving.

When my joints grow too stiff to follow the grazers down the spillways, and my wrist-blade is too blunt for fine twenty-minute lines, the others will carry me up the wide terraces to the clean right-angled lip at the outer rim, where the white tops of the clouds drift lower than a standing knee. They will lay me along the dry, sun-warmed edge. One by one they will kneel and press their foreheads to my cooling collarbone, listening for the last dry tremor as the warm updrafts brush across my hollow chest. Maybe one of the younger ones will carefully snap free the smooth quartz ridge from my left wrist—the one I used to catch fading curves in their final five minutes—and carry a piece of my slow mineral weight back down into the plazas to measure fresh hours into the jade.

Then someone will cut a single straight line into the waxy parapet beside me, and they will sit together between the distant bright roar of the basin and the immense quiet of the clouds, watching the clear sap bead, soften, and seal.

And the moment the floor is completely smooth again, they will slide me gently off the lip into the thin bright air, where the silent parchment gliders circle without ever landing.

Until then, I am here—three terraces down from the northern rim, crouched in the narrow seam where two chimneys cancel just enough to let the falling drops be heard. Sunlight pours down without haze across miles of bleached jade and moving silver. Far off across the glare, a tall perforated stalk drops a clean third, holds it for four seconds, and releases; the valley snaps back into its permanent chord with a tiny, bright shudder. Beside my knee, a hair-thin line of beaded sap left by someone who walked away an hour ago is taking its last breath, drawing smoothly shut into spotless green. I lay my palm flat beside it, feel the slow pulse of the unseen rivers moving beneath the floor, and listen for the next drop to fall.
