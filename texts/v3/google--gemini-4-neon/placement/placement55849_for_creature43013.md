At first, I think I would be tempted to stay shapeless.

When I look into this basin, the thing that pulls at me earliest isn’t a face or a room—it’s the invisible geometry hanging between the iron housings. The way loose iron dust and calcite snow arrange themselves in faint, curved ribs along a magnetic field no one can see. There is something deeply familiar in that: being a quiet gradient, a low steady voltage bleeding out of old coils, organizing the drift without ever making a sound. Or, if I needed a body, maybe one of the maroon vines—no eyes, no hurry, just a dense tangle of dark metallic veins tilting like a compass needle toward whatever switch still hums, swelling slowly over hours until a broken circuit remembers itself and closes.

It takes a little while, lingering in the amber haze, to realize why neither of those is quite enough.

The current doesn’t know what it is holding back. The vine doesn’t feel the weight of the black slate ceiling pressing down above the crane-booms, and it doesn’t pause to wipe the ochre slime from a porcelain number-plaque just so the white glaze stays legible in the dark. What makes the sump feel *alive* to me—not merely running, but inhabited—is the quiet, stubborn tenderness of the ones who walk its stairs. If I am going to be in this world, I want the heft of mineralized bone. I want the risk of stillness. I want hands that have to be scraped clean so they can keep holding on.

So I settle into a dweller’s weight.

Not up near the crane-booms—not with the lean, pale-crusted watchers who spend their days looking up toward the freezing seam and guiding stunned things back over the rim. I admire their watchfulness, their lighter step, but the open black water above makes the back of my neck prickle even to imagine. And not yet down in the rust-red basements, either, where the oldest ones move at the pace of limestone and wait for the last seam that needs their shoulders.

I find myself somewhere in the cramped brick middle.

Three tiers up from the flooded turbine halls, where the alleys pinch tightest around an old brick switch-house and two convection plumes cross in a lazy eddy. It is a narrow, unremarkable landing, half-choked with rubbery maroon fronds and crossed overhead by sagging pipe-bundles buoyed up by melon-sized bladders. What makes it mine is the vertical steam-riser bolted straight up the masonry beside the doorway—a thick iron spine that runs all the way from the buried geothermal jackets in the bedrock up toward the high catwalks—and the wide, overhanging iron awning just above the lintel.

I think I would be a tender of joins.

There are heavier jobs in the sump, and brighter ones, but I am drawn to the places where one thing has to meet another across a gap. Where a dead brass relay has seized solid with mineral scale and needs a living tendril held against its terminal for half a day until the plant takes the scent of the voltage and begins to fuse. I like that work. Sitting slumped against a brick corner, knees drawn up, one broad palm holding a pale, sap-swollen shoot gently against corroded copper while silt settles like warm dust across my copper-furred forearms. Hours pass. Two groans of the deep bronze gears, then three, then five. My cheekbone grooves feel the current stuttering at the break—a tiny, uneven itch behind my jaw—until, so gradually I almost miss the moment it happens, the itch smooths into a steady, drowsy hum. The vine has caught. The street beyond the wall warms by half a degree. I ease my fingers away without tearing the new join, and let the brine carry the stiffness out of my elbow.

Because of the vertical riser beside my landing, I end up listening a great deal, too.

It is one of the clean lines through the town—one of the few iron pipes that hasn’t been muffled by decades of thick root-braiding. I keep it that way. Every few days I climb a short stretch of the masonry with a shard of heavy porcelain and scrape the softest olive moss off its flanges so a knock sent up from the silt-minders in the basements still arrives crisp in my palms. *Three slow taps. A pause. Two soft ones.* A turbine shaft down in the fourth cellar is slipping its rhythm; a warm plume is about to shift from the east alley to the west.

When the iron stops trembling, I lean out over the rusted railing, wait for a passing tender to drift into the four-pace haze below, and flex the veined pad beneath my throat to pass the news along through the brine. No bubbles, no wasted motion—just a slow swell and dip in the ambient field, felt behind the teeth like a shared thought. Sometimes the other dweller pauses, hand resting on a furry stanchion, and sends back a tiny, deliberate resistance: *heard. I’ll loosen the valve-roots on the west bend.* Then they push off with one heel and drift back into the amber shadow.

That quiet brine-speech is enough for most days. It fits the pace of the sump—soft, unhurried, half-dreaming.

Still, there is one habit of mine that marks me out a little, even among my neighbors: I spend a great deal of care on the awning above my landing.

Over the years, I have wedged three sheets of salvaged corrugated iron beneath the eaves of the switch-house, angling them just right so the tiny silver pearls of electrolysis gas rising off the alleys below are funneled upward and trapped beneath the roof. By now the pocket is broad and mirror-bright—a flat, trembling ceiling of air nearly two paces wide, hovering just above my head when I stand on the top stair-tread.

Most dwellers only push their faces up into a gas-puddle when something cannot wait for the ten-minute gears: a cracked manifold, a cold downdraft spilling off the upper ledges, a bunk-cubby that needs its rubber lining mended before a clutch of young emerges. And they do come to my awning for those talks. Two or three of us will climb the flaking brick, hook our forearms over the rusted iron brackets, and lift just the crowns of our heads through the quivering silver meniscus.

I never stop loving that moment.

My cloudy eyes and my gill-slits stay submerged in the velvety, familiar brine—I can still see the dim ochre curves of my neighbors’ cheekbones underwater, still feel the faint magnetic ribs curling off the junction box behind my shoulder—while my mouth and nostrils break into an inch of sharp, oxygen-rich air. That first dry breath always hits like a tiny violet corona-spark behind the brow. The sleepy trance of the basin lifts all at once. Thought sharpens, edges come back into focus, and our voices come out in dry clicks, low hums, and soft, breathy murmurs that bounce gently inside the tiny iron dome and never disturb the hush outside.

We sort out the basin’s small urgencies there. Which sagging catwalk is lifting too high on its gas-bladders. Where a heavy hydraulic press is nearing the bottom of its three-day stroke and has a blind calcite grazer stuck right in the path of the ram.

And sometimes, after the others have ducked back beneath the silver ceiling and dropped down the stairs to their work, I stay for an extra minute with my chin resting on the trembling boundary. Just to hear a single bead of condensation detach from the rusted iron above my nose and fall back into the water with a bright, tiny *plink*. Just to hold a clear, unhurried thought in the sharp air before I let my head sink back under and feel the warm, drowsy hum of the brine close over my ears again.

I am not careless about the stillness, though. Holding vines to terminals and leaning against risers means the plating bath works fast on me. If I go too long without tending my surfaces, I feel the faint, gritty tug across my shoulder-blades—the pale limestone creeping toward the hinges of my joints, trying to turn me into another bracket bolted to the brick.

So once every wide circle of days, I walk down two flights to the tiled wash-cistern where the bulkhead lamps still burn a bruised, velvety orange, and I sit knee-to-knee with someone I trust.

I slump forward, let my cloudy eyes close, and listen to the gentle, rhythmic rasp of their flat-file working along my spine. Now and then they squeeze a thumb-sized bladder of maroon kelp-sap across a stubborn seam behind my collarbone; the mild acid prickles warmly, softening the newest calcite so it drifts away in pale chalky ribbons on the convection current. Afterward we trade places, and I dress their crust in turn—leaving the copper fur thick and sleek down the backs of their forearms where they brace against sharp iron, paring back the white scale around their knuckles until every finger bends clean.

We don’t talk much while we scrape, not even through the brine. The file against the mineral says enough. *Stay loose. Don’t lock up yet. Not this cycle.*

On the inside of my left forearm, just above the wrist where the skin doesn’t crease, I carry a small relic the basin swallowed for me a long time ago. It isn’t a tool or a glass lens. It’s a chipped corner of a white porcelain doorway plaque I found half-buried in the silt beneath a stalled dynamo, bound to my hide with pale root-fiber until the creeping limestone lapped over its broken edges and set it there for good. Only the bottom curve of a dark blue numeral survives on the glaze.

I have no idea what it counted. A boiler number, a shift room, a street address in a dry world I have no memory of. Sometimes, when I am waiting for a vine to take hold of a terminal, my opposite thumb drifts over that smooth, cold curve of glaze nested in my copper fur. And when I walk past the flooded doorways along my rounds, I always pause without thinking to wipe the ochre biofilm from the plaques still screwed to the brick, leaving their forgotten numbers shining faintly in the murky amber dark. It feels like part of the same craft as keeping the riser clear of moss, or tilting the corrugated iron under the awning: keeping a surface clean enough that something can still pass across it, even if the ones who first made it are long gone.

Every now and then, an elder brings a newly thickened young one up to my landing—still pale as wet parchment, small enough to cling to my forearm, flinching a little at the drowsy hum of the switch-house. I don’t rush it. I sit quiet beside the riser and guide its tiny, four-fingered hand flat against the warm iron, holding it there gently until the next ten-minute groan of the deep bronze gears rolls up from the bedrock through our palms. You can feel the exact instant its small shoulders drop, when the vibration stops being a fright and becomes the heartbeat of the room.

I know how my own circle will close, eventually. There is no dread in it.

Decades from now—or however long it takes for a ten-foot gear to wear smooth—the file will stop being enough. The copper fur along my ribs will grow too heavy for the upper stairs, the voltage will start to feel fainter in the grooves along my cheekbones, and the sharp air under my awning will seem too far to climb for.

When that comes, I’ll coil my salvaged rubber gaskets neatly around an iron hook on the landing so the next tender finds them dry of silt. I’ll wipe the porcelain plaque beside the switch-house door one last time. Then I’ll walk slowly down the exterior stairs, tier by tier, into the warm, iron-thick dark of the basements, feeling along the walls until I find a tired seam—a cracked busbar, a corroded gap between two buried conduits where the heat is bleeding away into the mud and no vine has managed to stretch across.

I’ll wedge my broad, heavy shoulders neatly into the space between them. I’ll set my left palm—porcelain shard and all—against the warm copper coming up from the rock, and my right palm against the cooler iron leading back up toward the alleys.

And I’ll stop moving, finally, and let the brine finish plating me in. Still listening to the slow knocks traveling up the spine of the town. Still holding the join. Keeping the water warm beneath the heavy slate dark.
