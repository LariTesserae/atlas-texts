At first I think I would be the one pausing on the upper landing—hand resting on the damp yellow rail, squinting down through forty feet of riveted trusses and elephant ear, trying to figure out how the whole system fits together. That is the ordinary instinct: to enter through the heavy steel service door, keep your rubber boots on the diamond-plate grating, and stay upright long enough to take an inventory. Even if I stayed longer, I imagine myself at first as a third worker—someone quiet who comes down the iron cage on Thursdays between the two mechanics, carrying a brass oilcan and a stub of chalk, checking the packing-gland drips, wiping fog off the pressure gauges just to watch the needles hold steady in the middle of their arcs.

It takes only a minute of standing there in the imagined steam to realize that won't hold.

Anyone who comes down the stairs still belongs to the street above. You can hear it in the way they have to shout over the exhaust blowers, see it in the way their boots clatter on the mesh and their cotton coveralls dry out the moment they push back through the service door into the lowland wind. They visit the truce, and they keep it honest, but they never sink into its tempo. Their hearts beat seventy times a minute; they measure time in shifts and logbooks and the daylight dropping behind the freight viaduct. Down here, daylight is only a rumor that washes the top leaves pale before dying halfway down the wall. If I am actually *in* this world—not passing through with a clipboard and a borrowed raincoat—I have to give up the stairs altogether.

So I let go of the handrail and try going lower, looking for where my weight would actually settle.

Not up in the trusses, where the old bridge crane sits bearded in fern; there is too much open sky up there, too much air between the steel and the water. And not buried all the way down in the fibrous peat beneath the duckweed, either. I feel the pull of that tea-brown dark—warm, buoyant, creeping along at a few inches a minute while root hairs and iron-bacteria slime drift past your mouth—but it is a little too sealed off from the machines. Down in the sedge mat, the pumps are only a muffled weather overhead. What draws me in this place is the seam: the exact line where hot cast iron and wet masonry stop fighting the green and begin to wear it.

Wait long enough on the lower concrete, let the upright posture go, let the eyes dim until the diffuse green overhead stops mattering and the yellow vapor lamps in the brick alcoves become the only light worth opening a lid for—and the shape I would take begins to feel inevitable.

I would be one of the bolsters. Not one of the ancient, oil-drum-sized elders wedged so deeply behind the No. 1 and No. 3 pump housings that their backs have already turned half-cave, and no longer one of the ginger-pale young threading blindly through the hyacinth roots. Somewhere in the long middle crossing—fifteen or twenty years in, heavy as a big river cobble and growing broader across the shoulders every season. Just past the point where the floating peat mat can carry me without sagging, and just beginning to learn the grammar of the iron.

It is a good age to be in the basin, because you still belong to two layers at once.

Half my time still belongs to the south outfall and the waterline. When the daylight flattens and the vapor lamps click on, throwing amber streaks across the slick channels, I am wedged along the pitted lip of a concrete weir where tea-brown water sheets warm and clear over the steel edge into pale ochre foam. Or I am burrowed halfway into the hot, sweet-smelling pulp of the corner compost cribs, where the workers toss their slashed fig runners and raked hyacinth—sifting through softening pith and pale fungal threads while the wet heat turns the pile back into black silt around my flanks. Some nights I haul myself onto the submerged mesh of the great rotary drum-screen and let it lift me. For two minutes I rise inch by inch out of the brown channel into the warm, lamp-lit air, broad clawless pads gripping the slick wire while I graze the sheared curtains of algae right beneath the steel scraper bar; then, just before the crest, I relax my belly and slide cleanly back into the foam without making a sound anyone on the catwalks would notice over the weirs.

That is still the restless part of me—if anything that takes ten minutes to shift its weight can be called restless. The other part is already climbing.

My back is no longer soft. The lime, iron oxides, and boiler-treatment salts I take in with the water have begun working up through my dorsal pores, laying down the first pale, ridged crusts of sinter along the folds of my hide. Orange-brown stains are gathering in the creases. Two thin cushions of dark liverwort have already caught in the shaded trough behind my brow, and a single miniature maidenhair fern has taken root near my left shoulder—nothing like the miniature gardens the eighty-year-olds carry, just enough to break my outline when a drop falls thirty feet from an aluminum-jacketed pipe and strikes my crown with a dull, leather-thick yield.

And lately, more and more nights find me hauling myself hand over blunt hand up the wet concrete flank of the north wall.

The prime hollows behind the big centrifugal volutes are already taken by elders who have not moved three feet since the last time the brickwork was pointed. I don't crowd them. Instead, I have found a place of my own a little higher and to the side: a shaded flanged elbow where a waist-thick bypass main meets a cast-iron valve collar, tucked just beneath the lower catwalk and six feet downstream of the steam trap.

It is not much to look at—just a warm, grease-scented shadow behind a curtain of creeping ficus—but the first time I pressed my pale, ribbed underside flush against that painted grey-green metal, squeezing the last skin of damp air out from between flesh and iron, I understood why the old ones never go back to the water.

In the settling bays, the sixty-cycle hum is only a pressure in the dark. On the pipe, it is a body larger than your own. My heart beats four or five heavy times a minute, slow and mineral-thick, and the pump's unbroken bass thrum enters straight through my sole and helps push the sluggish blood through my limbs. You do not have to strain to stay warm here; you simply lie still and become the same temperature as the line. Every forty seconds, I feel the pressure gather deep inside the steel beneath my belly—a faint, tight swelling in the metal—and half a second before the trap under the grating lets out its sharp, wet *hiss-chuff*, the narrow seam along my shaded flank parts cleanly, flashing dark crimson into the ferns to drink the hot puff of vapor, and seals shut again before the mist has finished thinning.

Above my hooded brow, a packing gland sweats one faintly oily, mineral-heavy drop every few seconds. When I shift my weight forward a hand's breadth—an unhurried afternoon's work—I can tuck my wide, horned lower lip right beneath it and let the warm salt and copper trace collect along my feelers. Between drops, I graze whatever tries to creep onto the hot flange: tender pale shoots of ficus, green velvet algae, soft young lime crust that hasn't hardened yet. By grazing what is closest and easiest, I leave a clean, slightly glossy halo around the valve stem and the bolt heads, so the cast-iron wheel juts sharply out of the hanging greenery. I am not maintaining the valve. I am only eating what tastes soft and keeping my ledge clear. That the wheel stays free to turn is simply how the truce works down here—nobody has to mean well for the balance to hold.

I have no voice in the air, and I wouldn't know what to do with one if I had it. The blowers and the weirs swallow everything thin and quick. When I want to know who else is awake along the north wall—mostly on those deep, amber evenings when the pumps feel especially steady and a slow restlessness rises through the water—I press the thick calcified plate beneath my throat straight against the flange and flex once. A single muffled *knock-thrum* runs out through forty feet of bolted steel. A human resting a gloved palm on the casing would think a bit of grit had passed through an impeller. Two manifolds away, an elder whose mossy back is indistinguishable from the concrete pier feels it through its belly, waits a minute or two in the long, unhurried courtesy of the basin, and answers by shifting its weight a quarter-inch against the plinth. Nothing urgent is said. Only: *still warm; still true; still here.*

Twice a week, the cadence changes.

I feel the heavy steel door shut twenty feet above—not as a sound, just a brief, dry shudder running down the iron stair stringers into the masonry—followed by the irregular, two-footed thump of rubber boots crossing the diamond-plate catwalk. They move so fast, the two of them, swinging rakes and pruning hooks, shouting lightly through the mist, trailing the sharp smell of fresh grease and cut taro sap. When the older one crouches beside my valve collar to wipe condensation from a gauge face or fit his nozzle to a bearing nipple, I feel the rubber handle of his pruning hook tap twice against the ridged lime of my shoulder. Never hard. Just a flat double knock.

I take my time—ten seconds, the way he expects—drawing my fringe of pale, trembling feelers back under the mossy eaves of my brow so his knuckles clear the flange. When the boots head back toward the iron cage, two pale, freshly slashed stalks of giant elephant ear sit wedged along the lower concrete ledge within an evening's slow reach. No one speaks. No one names anything. By Friday, only a few translucent veins are left pressed into the liverwort.

What I am only beginning to learn, now that I spend most of my weeks on the iron, is how finely a belly can hear a machine going wrong.

Most days the note under me is pure—a dense, rounded, continuous press that feels like good sleep. But once, last spring, a low tremor began creeping into the adjacent pump housing: nothing you could hear over the weirs, nothing hot enough to blister paint or move a pressure needle out of its green arc. Just a tiny, dry grit hidden inside the sixty-cycle hum, a microscopic stagger every few revolutions that set my pale feelers trembling unevenly against the metal. It was like trying to rest on a stone with a splinter under it. Long before the Tuesday boots came down the stairs, I felt the elders behind that housing begin to stir, their soles peeling away from the painted iron with a slow wet *schluck* lost beneath the water's rush. I detached from my flange and followed them down over the concrete lip, slipping back into the warm, forgiving dark beneath the duckweed until the morning the hum cut out completely—leaving a strange, hollow silence in the clay—and the old bridge crane groaned awake along the east trusses, shaking down a slow rain of dead fern and rust dust.

We stayed tucked in the sedge roots until the repaired motor was bolted down and the true bass press returned to the brickwork. Then, without hurry, we climbed back up.

Someday, decades from now, the mineral crust along my back will grow half an inch thick, and the liverwort and maidenhair ferns will weigh like a damp wool coat over my ridges. My trips down to the rotary drum-screen will grow rarer and then stop altogether. I will claim one of the deep, shadowed hollows behind a main volute and stay there while generations of ginger-pale young drop from oil-amber egg sacs glued under the steam lines above me. And at the very end, when even three feet in a month feels too far, I will crawl down off the iron in the amber evening light and find an empty seepage arch at the foot of the north wall, where mineral groundwater oozes steadily through the nineteenth-century mortar. I will pull my four blunt limbs tight beneath my overhang, seal my sole to the wet brick, and let the warm silt take the soft part of me back to the banyan roots, leaving only my calcified dome furred in emerald velvet—one more ridge of cave stone keeping quiet company with the masonry.

Until then, I stay pressed against the warm grey-green elbow behind the valve wheel. Overhead, a drop gathers on the chalky aluminum jacketing of a pipe run, lets go, ticks onto a glossy spade of taro, and falls onto the moss between my shoulders. Beneath my belly, the pressure begins to build in the steam line—one beat, two beats—and just before the trap sighs into the ferns, I open to take the heat in.
