My first instinct, looking into a place like this, is to claim the quietest thing in it.

It would be easy to say I am the limestone overhead, translating storms into patience, or one of the sparse clumps of maidenhair fern clinging to the entrance wall where reflected light barely meets a seep. Easier still to imagine myself as one of the seven dark basalt pebbles on the dry rear ledge—something carried in from a far-off, faster world and set down beside the travertine, slowly losing its separate outline under a clear skin of calcite until it belongs to the wall.

But if I stay honest with myself, none of those holds. I am too restless to be stone, and too warm-blooded to live on dissolved lime. If I woke in this world, I would not wake on the wet travertine shelf with a heartbeat spaced twenty seconds apart. I would wake out where my speed actually belongs: on the flat coastal plain below the escarpment, where the wind never quite stops talking and the afternoon sun hits pale rock hard enough to make you narrow your eyes.

I think I would be someone whose work already runs along the foot of that limestone wall. Not a scholar or a traveler—no one carrying notebooks, lanterns, or questions that expect quick answers—just a solitary keeper of water in a dry coastal country. Along the base of an escarpment like that, where the plain’s own groundwater lies low and often brackish, people depend on what the plateau catches and lets through: old rock-cut cisterns, mortared catch-basins, slow spring-troughs tucked under ledges of weathered cream stone. My hands would already know calcareous silt and lime mortar. My ears would already know the acoustics of water falling into dark rock, how a seep’s pitch rises as a basin fills and thins out months after a dry winter.

That is how I would find the hollow—not by hunting for a hidden chamber, because from the plain it barely reads as one, only a settled berm of fallen stone and earth tucked under the escarpment’s skirt. I would find it on an ordinary hot afternoon while walking the base of the cliff, checking for a wet seam that might explain a faint green line of grass out in the scrub, and noticing the worn slant where rockfall drops away behind the lip.

When I first step down under the heavy brow, I am immediately aware of how loud my body is.

I haven’t spoken. I haven’t dropped a tool. Yet the quiet inside has a physical weight that makes my own rate feel clumsy—boots grinding a little grit on the entrance slope, blood running quick in my wrists, chest rising and falling twelve times in the span between two ceiling drops. The outer heat clings to my shoulders like dust. As my eyes adjust to the even, pearlescent dimness, I find myself cataloguing the room too quickly, the way people from the plain do: the vaulted curve without sharp overhangs, the knee-high boulder with its three pale rubbed hollows, the faint smudges of red ochre high on the western wall, the low terraced shelves at the back dark with moisture.

Only my habit with water slows me down.

I stop just inside the mouth before my heels touch the open calcareous silt. You don’t need much learning to see that nothing has walked across that floor in years, and that a bootprint pressed into the damp clay trough beneath the rear shelves would sit there like a bruise for half a lifetime. Along the drier eastern curve, I see the six flat limestone slabs laid end to end in a gentle arc, unshaped and unmortared, silt crept halfway up their sides. Whoever put them there knew how to set stone so it wouldn’t fight the ground.

I listen for a few minutes. Thirty seconds pass. A drop lets go from the ceiling and falls into a rear basin with a clear, round *plip*, followed by a muted echo that rounds the vault and disappears. Twenty seconds later, farther back, a softer tap strikes damp clay. I calculate the thickness of the plateau overhead without meaning to—thirty feet of porous limestone, maybe more—and feel a quiet respect for the delay built into it. What is falling here today fell on the hill months ago.

Then I climb back up the slope into the lowland wind, completely certain that nothing lives inside.

I do not tell anyone in the scattered houses along the plain about the hollow. Not out of secrecy, exactly—there is nothing in it that anyone down there could use, no cistern deep enough to draw from, no shelter wide enough for sheep, nothing to mine or harvest. Still, I keep it to myself the way you keep a shaded bench beside an old trough: because talking about a quiet place is the fastest way to fill it with boots.

I go back a month later, at the peak of August, when the wind off the flats smells of salt and baked dust and the mortar in the south cisterns is cracking from the heat. This time I leave my boots on the sun-warmed rockfall outside the brow, where no loose grit can follow me down. I carry nothing—no water skin, no iron chisel, no lantern. Stepping carefully along the bare bedrock skirting the eastern wall, I sit on the middle pair of the six flat slabs and lean my shoulders back against the rounded limestone.

At first, sitting still is work. My mind keeps dragging the plain down behind the berm with me: the hairline leak in the third trough, the glare off the salt flats, a half-finished conversation from the road two days ago. My skin feels too hot against the mild, neutral air of the room. I count seconds between drops out of habit—thirty-one, thirty-two—waiting for my own pulse to stop hammering against my ribs.

It takes several visits, spread across a year and then another, before I stop treating the hollow as a refuge from the plain and begin to feel it on its own terms.

I come three or four times in a year, always alone, always barefoot on the eastern curve, never crossing the silt. I learn the room’s low-contrast geography the way you learn a face in dim light. I learn the four-minute seep along the western flank, and the exact line where the reflected light from the berm dies out, just past the last black-stemmed tuft of maidenhair fern. I spend long stretches studying the loose cluster of seven dark basalt pebbles on the dry ledge beside the rear travertine, wondering who walked all the way back from the inland streambeds carrying nothing but seven smooth, wet-looking stones, and set them down without leaving a scrap of charcoal or metal behind.

Once, arriving in late spring, I notice a faint scattering of fine pale chalk dusting the edge of the silt beside the two slabs where I usually sit. I look up at the smooth vault overhead, expecting a dry flake of stone to have loosened from a seam, and find nothing—only the same blunt, settled curve. I brush the mystery aside and fold my hands in my lap.

Over the years, the hollow teaches me a second calendar.

Out on the plain, a wet autumn is mud and rushing gullies, and a dry summer is cracked earth and low cisterns. Inside the berm, those seasons arrive stripped of urgency, smoothed into intervals. One bright, dry July, I sit on the eastern slabs and hear the basins brim fuller than they had in winter, the wall behind them glistening dark with rain that fell seven months earlier. The following spring, after a harsh, dry autumn on the flats, I come down out of a mild coastal drizzle to hear the main drop stretching from thirty seconds to thirty-eight, the second tap on the clay thinning to a whisper. Sitting inside that lag feels strangely merciful—as if the earth itself refuses to panic, holding onto every storm long enough to forget its violence.

My own body changes faster than the room does. The skin on the backs of my hands thins and creases; my shoulders round a little; the walk along the escarpment takes longer than it used to, and I no longer hurry between cisterns. Without quite intending to, I grow a little closer to the pace the hollow asks for.

It is an afternoon late in a dry year when I finally stay past my usual hour.

I have nowhere left to be before dusk. My knees, stiff from the walk, settle easily once I take my weight off them, and the neutral warmth of the room takes the ache out of my joints. I do not count the drops anymore. My breathing has slowed and shallowed until the faintly mineral-scented air seems simply to sit in my chest and ease out again without effort. Across the chamber, the wash of secondhand light beneath the overhang flattens from warm buff to weathered cream, then dims toward the color of wet pewter as the sun drops behind the escarpment outside.

Nothing in me is waiting for anything. That, I think, is the only reason it happens.

From the curved rear wall, where the travertine steps down toward the damp clay trough, there comes a tiny, dry *tick*—softer than a fingernail tapping chalk.

I do not turn my head sharply. Years on these slabs have cured me of sudden edges. For a long moment I assume a paper-thin scale of dry calcite has parted from the wall on its own. Then, a few breaths later—or what would be a few breaths for me, though I have barely drawn one—the quiet air thickens by a hair’s breadth with the scent of damp limestone, not enough to stir the nearest frond of maidenhair fern yards away.

My eyes, thoroughly accustomed to the last pearlescent grey before nightfall, move slowly across the rear shelves.

I have looked at that rounded lobe beside the deepest basin twenty times, thirty times, and always read it as slumped flowstone, or the pale crown of a buried boulder welded into the terrace. Now, in the flattest light of the day, the boundary separates itself—the thin, matte ivory crust along an upper curve giving way underneath to smooth, faintly creased hide tucked clear of the clay.

And beside the dry ledge where the seven dark basalt pebbles sit cemented to the stone, two flat circles of oiled slate rest flush against the pale brow.

They do not swivel toward me. They do not blink. They hold the whole dim chamber at once—the rubbed central boulder, the six eastern slabs, the draining grey lip of the berm, and me sitting motionless on the stone—with the same steady, unhurried surface as the basins beside its shoulder.

No fear rises in my chest. It feels less like discovering a hidden animal and more like realizing, after years of sitting in a quiet room, that the room itself has been listening back.

All at once, without a word or a sound, the small mysteries of the place settle into one piece. The seven dark river pebbles—chosen because someone long ago saw those exact unblinking eyes in the dusk and walked across the plain to bring back their likeness in stone. The six flat slabs arranged along the eastern curve, clear of the silt, drier and a fraction warmer than the wet rear shelves. That faint dusting of pale chalk I once saw along the edge of the slabs, shed not from the ceiling overhead, but from a broad, quiet back drying its pewter rime to cream while it watched the daylight brighten and recede under the brow.

We have been sharing the same bench across alternating seasons.

I do not move closer. I do not hold out a hand or speak or leave a token beside the slabs. Anything I added would only clutter the hollow’s accounting. I simply stay where I am until the outer lip of the berm drains completely to black, letting my breath stay low and even while the *plip* of the rear basin measures out the dark between us, once every thirty-eight seconds.

When full night has settled and the air inside is almost indistinguishable from the still air beyond the rockfall, I rise slowly, step along the bare bedrock of the eastern flank, and climb the worn slope back into the wind. My boots are cool with dew on the outer stones. Above the flat plain, the stars are sharp and bright, and the night air feels startlingly fast against my face.

That is who I would be in this world.

Not a creature of geological time, and not the hollow’s guardian—thirty feet of limestone guard it far better than any human hands ever could. Just one of the brief, warm-blooded people from the plain who knows how to take their boots off at the brow, sit on the eastern stone without hurrying, and leave nothing behind that can rust, rot, or announce itself. Someone who walks back out into the wind carrying a slower clock inside their ribs, returning a few times a year while my legs still carry me along the escarpment—until one season I stop coming, and my absence settles into the vault the way a summer drought does, unhurried, unannounced, and absorbed completely into the quiet between drops.
