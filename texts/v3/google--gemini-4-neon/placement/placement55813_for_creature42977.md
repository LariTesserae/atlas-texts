At first, I think I would be the one standing motionless on the tilted iron walkway.

It is the easiest posture to imagine: boots set carefully on the corroded plate, back hunched just enough to keep from scraping the hanging stone teeth, breath hanging pale for half a second before the wet air takes it. You stand still for nineteen minutes because you want to understand how a place like this works. You listen for the underfoot thud of the drowned impeller; you watch the pewter dimness settle across the standpipes; you wait for the knobby shelf of travertine beside the bronze housing to take a breath and reveal itself as a living spine.

But the longer I try to stay on that walkway, the more clearly I feel how poorly I fit there.

A watcher with dry lungs and eyes is an awkward thing in a throat this tight. Eyes are almost useless here—they give you slate-gray clutter, wet scrap, dim pewter ribs, when the real architecture of the sink is written in stray millivolts and cold sulfur. And standing upright on the catwalk means being outside the nineteen-minute exchange. Everything in the pocket either feeds the tide, seals the iron, or trims what grows too thick. A visitor only borrows the air, warms a patch of damp slate, and leaves. Even stillness, when you do not belong to the room, is a kind of intrusion.

So I step off the walkway and look down toward the deepest trough, where the narrow keeper lies pressed against the warm ridge of the bronze casing.

There is a strong pull there—stronger than I expected. I recognize that quiet, patient attention: the way a creeping crust of gypsum across the fourth insulator registers not as an abstract problem, but as a sharp, stuttering grit under an eyelid. I recognize the comfort of knowing a closed room down to the millimeter, of keeping a single snapped shard of white porcelain hidden behind a flowstone boss because its worn edge is the exact thing needed to bring a sour hum back to true. Even its loneliness feels familiar—not lonely at all, really, just measured to the space it inhabits, like a workshop that has forgotten the road outside. And the way it hangs high on the iron knees at night, letting pinhead blue sparks prickle against its sealed, cloudy lenses as if they were stars—yes, I understand that completely.

Yet the moment I try to inhabit that calcified back, I find myself backing away.

The pocket is already shaped to one body like that, and barely. Two long-armed keepers could not pass each other on the rubbed pewter underside of the walkway without scraping stone against stone; two pale bellies could not share the single warm ridge of the drowned armature. More than that, the dweller has a weight and a history that belong only to itself—it swam up through the dark karst arteries generations ago and grew into the room until the cracks closed behind it. I do not want to displace it, or crowd its quiet vigil, or pretend I carry a single heavy heart that beats in time with the pump.

If I am neither the visitor passing through nor the solitary keeper of the sink, I have to get smaller. I have to let go of having a face, a front and a back, a single place to stand.

I try the other lives in the crowding, listening for which rhythm matches my own.

Not the green wire-predators draped from the porcelain pins—their stillness is all tension, a coiled wait to snatch something passing beneath. Not the blind, six-legged walkers hurrying out across the iron ochre as soon as the meniscus falls, though I admire their soundless precision near the busbars. Not even the loaf-shaped siphons wedged in the intake grille, pulsing in the dark sump with their knotted cores of fermenting iron; there is a heavy, comforting inwardness to them, a deep vegetative patience, yet they sit in one spot and only draw the world through themselves until they grow too large and get torn free.

The tiled discs cemented along the cast-iron pipes come closer. I like the whisper they make when the water retreats—that slow ripple down the column as thousands of glassy rims lift in sequence to drink the falling film, leaving microscopic seams of mineral behind. There is something right in that: a life spent edge-to-edge on cold iron, knitting leaks closed without ever intending to.

Still, they clamp shut when the water rises. Half the cycle, they are only stone crust waiting out the dark.

Keep going. Closer to the iron. Down into the wet seam where a corroded flange meets the bulging limestone wall, right where a hairline crack bleeds a faint, steady leak of brackish water and stray current out of the drowned bronze housing.

There.

I stop moving—or rather, I start moving at a millimeter a week.

I would be the pale, translucent film that creeps along the voltage gradients.

Not a single patch of it tucked in one safe corner, and not some grand, all-seeing spirit of the cave—just the thin, living sheen spread across the wet joints where metal and mineral disagree. No eyes, no grooves of quivering jelly, no bones, no voice. Only a membrane thin as wet paper, laid flat against cold iron and weeping travertine, existing only where there is a difference between one point and another.

Once I settle into that, the whole pocket changes shape around me.

It is no longer dim or cramped. It has no ceiling and no floor, no pewter light squeezing through three overhead fissures—I wouldn’t know day from night except by the faint, almost imperceptible cooling of the ceiling drip after sunset. Instead, the pocket is a map of tensions. Everywhere the submerged armature pushes its weak, wandering charge out through the bolted ironwork and into the mineral-heavy water, paths open up across the wet rock: warm threads of potential, sharp little drops across a rusted seam, broad, quiet slopes running down the standpipes toward the sump.

Where the potential is flat, I thin out and go dormant, drying into a faint matte ghost on the limestone. Where two edges pull against each other—a leaking bolt, a stressed flange, the wet margin just below a copper strap—I wake.

I have no center, only two edges, and they are always doing opposite work.

On the raw, iron-facing edge, where the stray current leaks outward and the old machinery is slowly undoing itself, I am hungry. I soften the orange rust-scab, dissolving the flaky decay of the cast iron into something fluid and moving. I taste wet pennies, cold sulfur, the slow surrender of metal that has sat underwater longer than anyone above ground remembers.

A hair’s breadth away, across my own pale body, on the calmer side of the gradient, I do the reverse. I lay down smooth, smoky glass-calcite. Molecule by slow molecule, I turn the dissolved ruin of the iron and the hardness of the limestone drip into a clear, hard seal. Nothing deliberate about it—no blueprint, no memory of the engineers who lowered this pump into the throat. And yet, over decades, the seam stops weeping. The hairline crack in the bronze casing knits shut beneath a smoky glaze. The drowned armature stays dry enough inside its housing to keep turning, which keeps the weak current wandering through the wet stone, which keeps the gradient alive, which keeps me awake.

I am the thin truce between the machine and the rock.

Even the nineteen-minute breath of the sink feels different from here. I do not hear the muffled *thud* of the impeller or the distant *clunk* of the bronze flap—only a brief, deep tremor running through the iron under my belly, followed by a slow change in the electrical weather.

As the water retreats down the pillars over seven minutes, the broad, muffled paths through the flooded basin narrow. Current that had drifted loosely through dark water is suddenly squeezed into the thin, clinging films left on the exposed stone and metal. The gradients sharpen. Along the bared flanges and the wet skirts of the porcelain bosses, I feel the charge brighten—a clean, taut hum running straight through my translucent skin.

Then come the two minutes of stillness, when the air sharpens with ozone and the whole pocket crowds into the open.

This is when I feel the others most clearly.

The thin, glassy-shelled grazers come across me first, their rasping mouths moving in tiny, rhythmic clicks across my outer surface. It does not feel like attack; it feels like pruning, like wind stripping dead leaves from a hedge. Where they pare me back, the fresh iron water reaches deeper, and the work of dissolving and glazing stays brisk instead of choking under its own thickness. A blind walker steps delicately across my margin on six glassy points, scarcely breaking the film, hurrying toward the iridescent larvae blooming along the busbar.

And sometimes, high up near the flowstone boss, the entire electrical weather of the wall shifts in a single heartbeat.

A long, flat weight touches the wet flange of the standpipe below—iron-pebbled skin grounding the column—and a broad, familiar shadow crosses the field. It is the keeper, hanging inverted beneath the rubbed pewter track of the walkway. I do not know its calcified shoulders or the indigo-teal pulse at its wrists; I only know the sudden, steady pull of its spine completing a circuit down to the sump, and the crisp change in the current as it inspects the fourth insulator.

Sometimes, over the quiet weeks, a tongue of my own pale film has crept too far up the chalky lip of that fourth porcelain cup, following the rich copper runoff toward the brass lug. To me, it was simply a bright gradient—an open road of stray voltage begging to be bridged. To the keeper, it is a sour glare, a leak bleeding power straight into the wall where it does not belong.

Then comes the smooth, hard edge of the white porcelain shard.

Stroke by slow stroke, it scrapes that stray edge of me clean off the glaze, sending chalky shavings and torn wet ribbon drifting silently into the sump below. There is no bitterness in being pared back. The shard cuts off a reach that would have shorted the very current I live on; the moment the gypsum bridge breaks, a cold blue pinhead spark winks across the cleaned lip, and all down the standpipe and across the damp flanges below, the hum snaps back to its smooth, enduring pitch. What was cut away feeds the walkers in the trough. What remains along the iron seams settles deeper into the true lines of the sink.

Because I do not live in one place, losing an edge is not dying. And sealing a crack completely is a strange, quiet kind of forgetting: once the smoky calcite grows thick enough over an old hairline fissure to stop the leak altogether, the voltage drop flattens out there. The strain vanishes. My pale skin thins, dries, and pulls away toward some newer weakness down near the intake grille, leaving behind only a smooth, clear vein of stone glass—the finished shape of where attention used to be.

If I have anything like a season, it is the late spring storms.

Up above the slab, unseen rain pounds the hills until cold water surges down the four riveted standpipes with a sudden, swollen weight. Down in the trough, the bronze impeller spins faster than its usual heavy notch; the drowned casing runs fever-warm. All at once, across every pillar, bolt, and weeping overhang, the stray current flares high and wild.

On those nights, while the keeper paces its slow upside-down circuits along the iron knees, listening with its whole body to the upper world translated into voltage, I wake everywhere at once. Gradients that had slept as faint whispers for months blaze open along the wet manganese slate. Old seams shiver under the extra head of water; tiny new cracks try to open in the corroded flanges. And across all of them—spread thin across iron, bronze, and stone—I surge as fast as a mineral creature can, drinking the loosened rust, laying down layer after patient layer of smoky glass, holding the dark water out of the coils while the storm roars down the pipes.

Then the storm passes. The pressure eases. Down in the submerged throat, the bronze flap seats itself with a soft *clunk*.

Water swells quietly up through the floor fissures, climbing the stone ribs millimeter by millimeter. As the rising sheen touches the bare copper jumpers, the last pinhead blue sparks wink out beneath the dark surface. The sharp, exposed gradients soften back into a broad, submerged hum.

The grazers tuck themselves into crevices. The tiled discs clamp flat along the standpipes. Down in the deep trough, the narrow keeper slips beneath the rising water, parts the four fine slits along its neck, and presses its bare belly once more against the idling warmth of the bronze housing.

And I lie flat across the seam right beside its tucked elbows—invisible under the black water, cool and thin and wholly awake—tasting the rust on one edge, precipitating clear stone on the other, feeling the drowned wheel turn one slow notch further in the dark.
