I tried at first to picture myself down in the blinding box—to imagine what it would feel like to hang weightless inside twenty yards of sunlit thunder, or to be the glass-finned thing that bends the room to hunt. I couldn’t hold it for more than a breath. My blood is too warm, and I flinch from cold that turns fingers to pale wood. Nor could I belong to the pitch-black tectonic seams miles below, crawling blind through iron-slime in the crush of the roots. I need air I can hear myself draw, and a sky that stays overhead instead of breaking into white lattices through panes of strained ice.

Yet when I try to step all the way down into the green valleys, away from the talus and the crooked larches, the quiet feels wrong—too soft, too thick, like wool stuffed into the ears.

So I find myself at the threshold.

Not inside the sideways doorway, and not down at the permanent hearths. Twenty paces back from the freshly sheared brow of white rock, sitting on the flattest slab of pale gneiss I can find, with the tremor climbing steadily through my boots and settling behind my molars. The air up here is thin and smells of nothing but crushed stone and snow-glare. My palms are greased with cold tallow and grey with rock-dust, and looped twice around my braced thigh is the braided leather rope that disappears under the overhang into the noise.

I am the one who holds the line—and, later, down where the stunted larches twist out of the scree, the one who works what comes up out of the water.

I used to go in. Three summers, when I was young enough to think my lungs could argue with alpine meltwater and my ears could take the hammer of the pocket without cracking. I know the exact shock of slipping beneath that churning surface: how the roar stops being a sound and becomes a fist pressed flat against your sternum; how the daylight down there is too bright to trust, doubling every edge of quartz until your stomach tilts. I know the taste of ozone and wet, greasy hide inside a bucking rawhide hood, gulping two ragged breaths while freezing spray lashes your eyes before the dome twists and spills its silver belly toward the skylight.

I stopped diving after my fourth summer, not because I drowned or broke a rib, though the current slammed me hard enough to bruise my shoulder black from collar to elbow. I stopped because of a bone piton, and because my left ear never came all the way back out of the water.

Even now, sitting in a timbered room in midwinter with snow banked to the eaves, there is a thin, bright wire of sound strung behind my left eardrum. It is the exact pitch of a wall-fin humming just before the current snaps it loose. The old women in the valley tried warm oil and willow-bark tea for it, before giving up and calling it mountain-deafness. I don’t mind it anymore. Up here on the pale talus, it matches the dry shudder under the stones, and for the first time all year my head feels balanced.

As for the piton—it happened on my last dive. I had dropped into the wobbling, sickening calm of a pressure-null to catch my bearings and check the three copper strips we used to read the drift of the shear-planes. Only two were blurring in the current. The third bone piton sat empty. I reached out with half-numb fingers, thinking the widening fault had rattled the metal loose and fed it to the outflow. Instead, my thumbnail caught on three tiny, parallel grooves cut right across the notch of the bone—neat, deliberate, slightly darkened at their edges, as if something with hard, narrow tips had worried the copper free without splitting the peg.

I didn’t stay to wonder what had teeth or claws fine enough to pick a lock in a roaring void. Six feet away, a shimmering double-image of the quartz wall stopped drifting with the flow. No jaws came out of the glare. Nothing touched me. The bright water simply tilted, became a solid wall of cold weight, and shoved me sideways into the raw slate hard enough to drive the last air from my ribs. I clawed up the leather rope with my skull ringing, and when my hands thawed beside the larch-fire, I told the weathered woman who leads the dives that I would mend the nets from then on.

She didn’t laugh, and she didn’t ask why. She just slid a coil of wet gut-cord across the flat stones toward my knee.

It is a better fit for me than the plunge ever was. Someone has to stay dry at the lip of the brow, reading the braided hide with their bare palms—telling the difference between a diver’s sharp double-tug for haul, the steady sawing of a shear-plane dragging the line sideways, and the deep, sudden slack-and-snap that means the floor below has just split another centimeter open. When the rawhide hoods come up torn at the sinew seams, I stitch them tight with bone needles and rub fresh tallow into the leather until my knuckles ache. When the willow-root nets come back shredded into snapping whips, I knot them whole again while the divers sit huddled in smoked sheepskins, lips still faintly blue, arguing over whether the thing in the water is a spear-long glass eel or a lizard made of ice.

I never join the argument. I sit a little back from the smoke with my bow-drill and a pouch of wet quartz sand, making the slate discs.

That is the part of the work I have grown strangely particular about, enough that the younger divers tease me for it. Every summer we carry fresh drilled discs of grey slate up the slope to thread onto the antler pins hammered into the lower wall. Most people think any flat flake of shale will do—just a tally, or a weight to rattle in the boom. They haven’t watched the weathered woman closely enough, or they haven’t felt what I felt when the water turned sideways. Before I string a finished disc onto its carrying-thong, I balance it on two fingers and tap its rim with a knuckle of bone. If it gives a dull, muddy *thud*, I toss it back into the scree. I only keep the ones that answer with a dry, bright *clack*—clean enough to cut straight through a landslide. I couldn’t tell you why I care so much whether the sound is crisp. Only that whatever lives behind the refraction clearly knows the difference between noise and a voice.

And then there are the blades.

Most of what the divers lift out of the willow-root mesh with their bone-handled tongs are straight wall-fins—brittle, clear as icicles, razor-edged until a clumsy twist turns them into glittering powder. Anyone can wrap leather around the base of a wall-fin for a hide-scraper.

Once or twice a summer, though, after the fire has burned down to red larch-coals and the younger ones are rolled in their blankets, the old woman sets a small parcel of greased hide beside my tools without a word.

Inside is always one of the curved ones.

I know them before the leather is even unrolled, just by the slight extra heft in my palm. They don’t look like sheared mineral. They curve gently like the tip of a scythe, denser along the spine, with a faint, scalloped ridge running down the back edge. And at the broken base, where a wall-fin shows only cold, flat cleavage, these are slightly milky-warm—dense and faintly fibrous, like the shed root of an antler or a tooth that once sat in living bone.

Nobody at the fire talks about where the curved ones come from. We all know they drop straight into the willow-root net from upstream, and we all know the mountain doesn’t grow curved quartz on bare walls.

I take my time with them. I steam slivers of mountain-goat horn over a boiling pot until the horn softens enough to split cleanly along the grain. I rasp the channel by hand, testing the fit a dozen times, careful never to force that milky root against a tight ridge—one careless pressure-point and the whole thing will explode into invisible splinters that stay in your fingertips for weeks. I melt dark pine resin mixed with fine charcoal dust, seat the blade while the pitch is barely tacky, and bind the split horn tight with wet sinew that shrinks like iron as it dries.

When it is finished, I hold the handle lightly between thumb and forefinger and flick the back of the curved glass with my fingernail.

A wall-fin just clicks. One of these holds a thin, shivering note for a full breath—clear, cold, and impossibly keen. It matches the phantom wire strung inside my left ear so closely that for a second the ringing in my head seems to step outside my skull and hover in the dark above the coals. I wrap it back in oiled hide and hand it to the old woman the next morning. Those knives don’t get traded down in the valleys; they stay with the high camps, used for the cleanest work, until the day a sudden shock finally claims them and turns them to dust.

Sometimes, on the last afternoon before we break camp and follow the melt-streams down ahead of the early frosts, I linger up on the talus after the others have shouldered the wet nets and started for the tree-line.

The sun drops fast behind the raw upper ridge. In minutes, the blinding white glare drains out of the mountain, and the open fissures above the pocket turn the thin, hard blue of steel. The air bites straight through wool. Down below, the water is finally empty of warm, clumsy bodies; no leather ropes hang in the shear-planes, no air-filled human chests are sponging up the thunder.

I don’t crawl under the overhang. I don’t leave offerings, or drop bits of metal down the melt-fissure, or pretend there is any warmth between us. Whatever waits down there does not want company, and a truce made of cold glass is kept best by staying on your own side of the lip.

Instead, I kneel in the pale stone dust on top of the white brow—right where a hairline crack has widened enough over the past five summers that I can now wedge the edge of my thumbnail inside it—and I press the bare bone of my jaw flat against the freezing gneiss.

Through rock, the roaring inflow dulls to a heavy, steady shudder, like a distant avalanche that never reaches the valley floor. If I stay still until my teeth stop chattering and let my eyes rest on the bare, darkening scree, I can sometimes catch what rides underneath it.

Not a song. Nothing a human throat would ever try to imitate. Just small, spaced intervals far too clean to belong to falling water or splitting crust: a thin, high chime of taut quartz; the dry, deliberate chatter of drilled grey slate; and then, once in a long while, a low, warm metallic hum that no raw mountain has ever made on its own—our stolen strip of cold-hammered valley copper, plucked in the twilight, sending its strange, foreign pitch down through miles of flooded stone.

I stay there until my cheek goes numb, listening through my bones to something remade by daylight speaking downward into the blind dark.

I know the brow I am kneeling on won’t hold forever. One winter the frost will drive a wedge deep into that thumbnail crack and drop half the overhang straight through the ice-lenses, burying the bright box in pale rubble. Or the seam at the bottom will yawn too wide and drink the whole roaring chamber dry in a single afternoon, leaving nothing but glittering walls and empty rawhide hoods hanging limp in the alpine wind. When that happens, we will stop climbing above the larches. The young ones will trade for valley iron, and in two generations no one will quite believe that the mountain once held a sunlit wound full of singing glass.

Until then, I stand up, dust the gneiss-grit from my knees, and pick my way carefully down the sharp talus toward the smell of larch-smoke—carrying the coiled leather rope across my shoulder, the thin clear note still humming steadily inside my left ear.
