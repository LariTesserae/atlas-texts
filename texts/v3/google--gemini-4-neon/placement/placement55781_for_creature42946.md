At first, when I try to find my shape in this basin, I keep drifting toward the two extremes of it—as if the glare and the roar leave room for nothing in between.

Part of me wants to be up in the indigo dark, locked bone-to-bone into the updrafts. There is something deeply tempting about the pale-winged gliders: the sheer economy of clicking a joint into place and spending six hours reading the geometry of the pale stone below without a single wasted beat. No noise of your own, no weight to haul, only altitude and a milky inner lid that turns blinding glare into readable pattern. Yet the longer I stay up there in imagination, the colder it gets. A glider touches the world only at the end of a plunge, to kill or to hook a thumb-claw into a screaming upper tunnel and tremble through the dark. It sees everything and speaks to nothing. That isn’t where I belong.

So I drop all the way down—past the sheer walls, past the spinning vortices of midges—into two inches of cold water on a shaded ledge. The mirror-backed beetles in the dew-pools have their own quiet pull. To live inside the exact mathematics of a standing wave, grazing neat seams of algae laid down where the wind’s roar cancels itself into stillness: there is a strange peace in that, a life turned entirely into pattern and precision. Yet a beetle spends a thousand generations inside a single saucer, never crossing the three damp paces to the next rim, never knowing what shakes its floor. Pure stillness inside a closed ring is as lonely as pure altitude.

Between the locked sky and the two-inch sea sits the damp leeward shadow of the limestone. That is where my weight settles.

I would be one of the silver-pelted cliff-dwellers—not on the dramatic western rim where the river of air first breaks over the tableland, nor on the far eastern pillars looking down into the chalk cloud-sea, but somewhere near the bright middle of the basin. On a broad, low-shouldered whaleback mesa riddled with deep shaded alcoves, sitting right in the crossing of the sightlines.

And I wouldn’t have been born on it.

I keep coming back to that detail of the world: that every monolith has its own pitch, and that when you leave your birth-pillar to cross the open pavement to another troop, the hardest part isn’t the cold or the strange faces—it is lying down at night on rock that hums in the wrong key. I think I would be one who crossed. Perhaps years ago, when I was young enough to be restless and stubborn enough to follow a long flickering exchange of sparks across two miles of glaring tuff. I remember—in the way a body remembers a bruise—the slow, knuckle-dragging haul down inside the thin boundary layer of slower air, shoulders hunched to let the current pour over my silver ruff, fingers finding tiny wind-carved flutes in the pavement while the hum of my old arch faded out of my palms and a stranger, deeper pulse began to climb up my wrists from ahead.

My birth-pillar was tall, three-arched, pierced by narrow flutes; its voice sat high and tight behind the molars. This central whaleback is broad and cored with wide caverns. Its chord sits low in the belly and the heels. It took half a season before my ribs stopped fighting it in my sleep.

Even now, years after my bones have settled into the new pitch, I haven’t forgotten the old one. And that, I think, is what determines who I become in the troop. When you have only ever lived on one monolith, its vibration feels like silence—just the neutral floor of existence. Once you have learned to sleep in a second key, you never lose the awareness that the stone itself is an instrument, and that every troop in this fifteen-mile bowl is living inside a slightly different note of the same immense chord.

It makes me a listener. And, by daylight, a relay.

I am not the heaviest climber in the alcoves, nor the boldest when a foraging party drops onto the open pavement to pry green-crusted seams of copper out of the tuff. My hands are scarred and stained dark at the thumbs from sedge and wet grit like everyone else’s, and my wrists carry the slick callus of a braided anchor-loop, yet my real usefulness lives along the razor-sharp seam where the cool shadow of our whaleback meets the blinding glare.

Because our mesa sits near the center of the bowl, we can see pillars that cannot see one another. A tall fluted rib to the south stands tucked behind the shoulder of a western arch; a pair of narrow needles toward the eastern rim are cut off from the southern ledges by a broad windward wall. Messages that start at the edges of the basin often have to pass through our shadow to reach the far side.

So I spend much of the bright hours perched just inside the cool lip of an upper ledge, knees braced against the humming limestone, a shallow dew-pool trembling beside my hip, and a palm-sized disc of hammered copper tucked warm against my thigh.

It isn’t a new disc. It was given to me by an elder on this pillar not long after I crossed—an old, thin-centered circle worn slightly concave by hands I never met, with a faint flattened scar along one edge where someone long ago dropped it against chert and tapped the soft orange rim smooth again with a river cobble. Because the center is thin as a fingernail, it warms quickly against my skin. And because the leeward air never stops sweating, the verdigris is always trying to creep across it, skinning the bright face over with dull green silence.

I polish it without thinking, the way another creature might groom its fur. A pinch of fine wet limestone dust, a shred of dry silver-sedge pith, a tiny smear of grease, slow circles of my thumb while I watch the bright void between the monoliths. An unpolished mirror is a closed mouth; keeping the metal clear feels less like maintenance and more like staying awake.

When a pinpoint of gold-white fire winks out of the shadowed wall of a western arch three miles off, my eyes—ringed in their dark glare-absorbing skin—catch it before the thought even forms.

*Long hold. Double blink. Pause. Three crisp cuts.*

Their pillar’s opening call. Then the message itself—today, maybe, nothing urgent: a herd of ungulates breaking cover in a tight, leaning teardrop across the central pavements, heading east toward our skirts. Their speaker holds the disc slightly off-angle, throwing a warm, copper-gold gleam rather than the stabbing white glare of an alarm. Even across three miles of deafening roar, you can feel the steadiness of the hand holding it—the ease of the cadence, the clean shuttering of the left palm cutting the beam.

I shift out a hand’s breadth until the fierce overhead sun strikes my shoulder, lift my own warm circle into the light, and send back a single brief confirmation so the western ledge knows the word landed. Then I turn slightly along the lip of the arch toward the south, where the far rib sits half-lost in bright vibrating air, and open with our whaleback’s call before passing the message on.

I try to pass it cleanly—every pause true, every angle honest. Yet you can’t carry light through a living wrist without leaving a faint trace of yourself in it. When a frantic, white-glaring string of sparks arrives from a nervous young watcher on the rim, I find myself steadying the tempo just slightly as I send it onward, letting the disc tilt a hair softer if the danger has already passed. When two adolescents on opposite arches are playing their rapid-fire afternoon games and lose line-of-sight as the sun shifts, I sometimes catch a stray laugh-pattern and bounce it around the shoulder of our pillar just to keep their game alive.

It is a strange kind of intimacy, knowing dozens of beings across a fifteen-mile basin whom I will almost certainly never touch. I know the crisp, economical phrasing of an elder on the northern needles whose sparks never waver by a hair’s breadth. I know the playful, slightly rushing rhythm of someone on the tall southern rib—my old birth-pillar—who always finishes a conversation with a tiny double-flutter before dropping into shade. Sometimes I wonder if it is my sister, or a cousin who grew up clinging to the same greasy limestone spurs I did. There is no way to ask a name in light that finely; there is only the cadence, and the warmth of knowing that the high, tight hum of that cliff still has bright hands moving along its edge.

Between flashes, when the air holds nothing but the motionless arcs of the gliders and the slow sweep of the sun across the dark indigo dome, I look down into the dew-pool beside my knee.

It is my quiet counterpart to the mirror. The limestone beneath it hums our whaleback’s deep note, and the two inches of clear water translate that endless roar into crisp concentric rings crossed by motionless lattices. Down beneath those bright geometric ridges, three mirror-backed beetles creep along the calm seams where the ripples cancel out, their bronze wing-cases catching tiny splinters of the same sun I hold in my hand.

I read the pool almost as much as I read the horizon. Before my feet finish registering the footfalls of a troop-mate climbing the wet rock thirty feet below, the outer rings of the water fuzz along one edge and snap crisp again. When the slate-black stone-tappers higher up the perforated wall suddenly hammer a dry, frantic rattle into the limestone—*shadow dropping, shadow dropping*—the whole geometric lattice inside the saucer shivers into bright chaos for a heartbeat. I don’t even have to look up to pull my elbow back into the lee and reach behind me to check that the nearest youngster’s wrist-tether is knotted fast.

Once, watching a glider plummet out of the sky like a white dart to strike at a stray swift, I saw it miss by a hand’s breadth, flare its immense scythe-wings at the bottom of the plunge, and shoot effortlessly back up into the dark blue without a single beat. Looking down again, I saw the beetles in the saucer had never paused along their lines of algae. Neither of them had anything to say to the other. Only I sat in the middle, feeling the stone-tappers’ alarm die away beneath my heels, lifting my copper disc to flash a quick, bright warning across the open glare to the neighboring arch before the glider could drift their way.

Then the sun drops sharply off the western rim, bleaching the sky to white-gold and then straight to velvet black crowded with steady, untwinkling stars.

This is the turn I love most, even more than the bright noon chatter.

With the sun gone, the basin collapses from fifteen miles wide to the width of a single stone. My copper disc goes blind and cold; I slip it into a known damp cleft beside my hip where no stray eddy can reach it, and knot my braided silver-sedge loop twice around the smooth, ivory-worn spur protruding from our alcove wall. Around me, the troop draws close, broad oiled backs pressing together for warmth in the thin freezing air, breathing in slow unison while the wind-chord pours on through the dark without softening by a single degree.

And I take out my other instrument: a hand’s-length of chalk-white glider wing-bone, hollow and smoothed at both ends with wet grit.

I press one end firmly against the dark limestone floor of the alcove and rest the other against the bony angle of my jaw.

Immediately, the whaleback opens up inside my skull. The immense sub-bass of the wind is still there—the great patient river rushing through our caverns—but riding cleanly on top of it is the whole hidden night-life of the cliff. High up in the honeycomb pores, the slate-black birds trade sleepy, spaced-out single clicks with their mates. Two ledges down, a mother taps the flat heel of her palm against the rock in a slow, rocking double-thud to quiet a restless infant tucked against her ribs. Farther around the eastern shoulder, an elder strikes a smooth hammering-cobble gently against the wall—*tap, tap, pause, tap*—checking that the outer ledges are tethered tight against the frost.

I lift my own knuckle and tap softly against the alcove floor beside my knee. *Steady. Here. Tethered.*

Through the hollow bone against my jaw, I feel the faint reply travel back through forty yards of solid stone—not blazing across miles of empty air like daylight speech, just a small, warm pulse of presence shared between bodies hanging above an unseen sea of clouds.

I think that is why I land here, and nowhere else in this world. Not as the wind, which has power but no attention; not as the glider, which has vision but no touch; not as the beetle, which has geometry but no horizon. I am the stocky, heavy-handed figure sitting right at the margin where the wind shears past overhead—keeping one foot in the rock’s deep hum and one hand angled into the blinding light, rubbing the green skin off an inherited circle of copper so that across the roaring, uninhabitable miles between one pillar and the next, a small clean spark can still cross intact.
