My first instinct, standing inside the hush of this place, is to reach straight for the cathedral-junctions—to imagine myself among the Rooted, folded warm and nearly breathless into the fused cradle of five continental trunks, holding the slow tremor of forty miles of timber against my spine. It is an easy thing to want: to be the ear of a world, excused from the precariousness of walking, trading motion for depth.

Yet the moment I try to settle into that dark, pulsing bark-bolster, it rejects me. That stillness is earned, not chosen. It belongs to someone whose soles have spent two centuries learning the give of wet cane, whose knees only slowed after ten thousand miles of weeping belts and dry-belt drafts. To sit in the heartwood without having walked the lattice first would be like trying to read a vibration with numb palms; the wood would hum, and I would have no scale against which to measure what was swelling and what was merely breathing.

So I slip outward, testing another shape—smaller, shyer, and uncomfortably close to my own habits. Inside the hollow pith of a neglected rail, waiting for the twilight to dim a shade further, lives the echo-weaver. I can feel the pull of that kinship almost immediately: to emerge in the hush, chew fine filaments of damp bast, and spend the quiet hours copying the impossibly intricate splices felt through the grain, tying braids within braids around a sleeping binder’s mallet-handle simply because the pattern asked to be continued. There is a strange tenderness in that blind imitation, a devotion to form even when the wood around it is beginning to forget its own hardness.

Still, I cannot quite stay inside a dowel-hole. I do not want to flee at the first tremor of a footstep, nor weave knots that only have to be pared away with a sigh at waking. I want hands that can stay in the open air, that can pass a warm horn spoon or steady a swaying vine-ladder, and a chest that can hum an answer back into the phloem when someone presses their knuckles against the wood three yards away.

Which leaves the walking Hands—the Binders, moving with quiet economy along the glistening troughs, listening through bare heels for a branch leaning ahead of its cord, arriving with wedge and concave mallet to pay the debt before the grain goes loose. I could wear their oiled glider-vellum apron, carry the smoldering punk-fungus tucked inside a horn tube against my ribs, keep a pair of pale bark-amphibians curled in the damp moss-pouch at my hip.

And yet, watching myself in that skin, I see where my stride would falter.

A proper Binder sees a young sucker-limb groaning smoothly into a parabolic curve against empty air and kneels at once, driving the thornwood wedge home so sequence closes cleanly over the joint. I would walk up to the straining bark—and then I would pause. Just for a few breaths too long. Not out of carelessness; I would never let a causeway unravel or a junction sour into tangled weather. Only long enough to lay my flat palm against the stretching grain and listen to the shape of a tension that has not yet been given its cause. I would want to feel the slackness *before* I pulled it tight.

That small hesitation tells me where I actually live.

Not on the high, well-traveled thoroughfares where debts come due every half-league, and not inside the great five-trunk cathedrals where the oldest elders breathe twice an hour. To find my place, I have to leave the main decks behind, climb down three swaying vine-ladders—letting the walkways trade floor for ceiling twice as the pull of each thick trunk claims my weight—and walk outward along a sparse, secondary arch where the edge of a weeping belt meets the sinking air of a dry one.

Out there, the lattice thins. The arches no longer weave into tight cathedrals; they stretch wide across leagues of dim, breathable warmth, separated by long, trembling tension-cables where the silence feels even deeper.

And branching off one of those lower ribs is an unfinished spur.

Perhaps three centuries ago, a pair of Binders began training a double-braided trunk outward across a mile-wide gulf, intending to splice it to a sister-span on the far side. Perhaps the far arch shifted in a long dry-belt shrink, or the builders grew old and went to Root before the crossing closed. Whatever the reason, the beam curves out four hundred paces into the bruised, ochre haze and simply stops. No bridge meets it. No tension-cable anchors its far end. It tapers gradually from a thick, fluted beam into a loose crown of living, pale-barked shoots that hang out into open vapor, weeping occasional beads of milky sap that never strike anything.

That is where I live—and not on the top of the spur, where the old lashed decking has mostly softened into thick, spongy cushions of undisturbed moss, but on its underside.

Once you crawl over the damp lip of the trunk and let your bare soles settle against the curving belly of the bark, the world turns over with a quiet, stomachless ease. The heartwood holds me gently from beneath my feet, as steady as any lane. Above my head—where a ground-born stranger would say the abyss yawns *downward*—stretches an endless, tea-colored sky of warm, bottomless murk, lit by no sun, only the dim sideways glow of vapor. When I lean back in my nettle-mesh hammock slung between two petrified knot-knuckles, pale vellum gliders wheel lazily across that warm ceiling-void, their translucent wings glowing faintly as they ride updrafts that drift upward from the unseen depths—which, from where I lie, fall gently down toward my face like tepid breath.

I am one of the Hands, by bone and callous and tannin-stained sole, with pale bog-moss plaited tightly behind my ears and a cane-flint blade bound in resin-blackened willow hanging from my belt. The walking Binders do not have a grand name for what I am; when they mention me in low, woody clicks along the neighboring spans, they call me simply *the one on the blind rib*, or *the tip-tender*.

A dead-end spur still needs tending. Because it carries no traffic and braces no crossing, it is the first part of a junction to go loose if left entirely alone. Left untended for fifty years, the slackness at an unbound tip will creep backward along the grain like rot, loosening the bindings at the main arch until shadows start pooling cold across the walkway and rain drifts sideways off the deck. So I earn my warmth here. I pare back the suckers that twist inward through their own knots; I let my tame bark-amphibians out into the damp seams to lick away soft pith; I harvest canopy-tubers from moss-cradles slung along the shadowed flank, and when a forty-yard ribbon-grazer drifts slowly overhead across the ochre void, I reach up with my flint knife for a clean strip of pleated flesh, watching its translucent edge seal shut a heartbeat before my blade pulls free.

Even the echo-weavers know my rhythm. They nest by the dozens in the thin, hollow shoots at the very tip of the spur, drawn to the spot where the wood sits closest to forgetting its own hardness. Most Binders pare their gossamer braids away on sight, and when they wrap my mallet-handle to a rib with cord that passes cleanly through solid bark, I pare those away too. Yet I always wait a little first, kneeling in the dim trough of the twilight to trace their impossible loops with a dry fingertip. They do not invent those knots; they only copy what they feel shivering up from the petrified core of the older beams. Watching their tiny, pale mandibles work is as close as a living pair of eyes can come to seeing the geometry of three-palmed splices and straight lines that no longer fit this curving air.

For that is the true reason a blind rib cannot be abandoned—and the real reason I never left it once my bare heels learned its pitch.

Everything that travels through the continuous wet phloem of the lattice—every double-tap of a mallet two leagues off, every slow groan of a dry-belt arch shrinking an eighth of an inch, every distant, soggy tug when a dead abyss-drifter snags a lower cable—moves outward along the living timber until it thins into the hush. On a bridged span, a tremor passes straight through a junction and scatters across five arches at once, tangled with a dozen other messages.

At the free end of a blind spur, a vibration has nowhere left to go.

When a wave reaches the tapering, living shoots at the tip of my beam, it cannot leap into the empty vapor. It turns around. For a fraction of a breath, right where the heartwood thins into pale green cambium, the arriving tremor and the returning tremor cross through one another and double in clarity before sliding quietly back down the grain.

In the quietest hours, when the mist hangs motionless and the lag-leeches have flattened themselves into cool, charcoal-green pools along the mossy deck beneath my heels, I walk all the way out to the slenderest part of the spur, grip the warm bark with my toes, and lay both palms flat against the wood just shy of the weeping tips.

There is no roar in it, no grand prophetic speech like the Rooted hold in the cathedrals. Only a thin, lucid sorting of distances. I feel the sharp, impatient click of two young Binders trading news across a weeping belt six miles off, softened to a gentle pulse as it folds back on itself. I feel the slow, heavy sway of the abyss-currents brushing the lowest sagging cables far out in the ochre sky above me. Sometimes, if a dry-belt split has opened deep in an old trunk leagues away, I feel the faintest hairline hum rise out of the noise and double cleanly against my wrists: that unspent shudder of a mallet-blow struck when there was still something heavy beneath the world to strike against, still wandering patiently through the wood, turning quietly around at the tip of my spur to begin its long journey back.

I do not know what it struck. Even the Rooted only murmur about it in the lowest register of their throats. I only know that it feels different from every tremor we make—heavier, cleaner, ending in a stop that our looping air no longer permits.

Other things arrive out here too, drifting gently down out of the ochre sky above my hammock. Dropped things. A swollen thornwood peg that slipped from some walker’s sling three spans away, fell into the bottomless murk, bent sideways in the blind currents, and now loops slowly back through my quiet margin—furred along one flank with velvety black liverwort and freckled with the tiny round bite-marks of abyss-borers. Sometimes a broken horn fid, or a sliver of pale bark carrying spores from a wet season this belt will not see for a month.

When I see one drifting lazily past the tip of the spur, I reach out and catch it before its loop carries it back up into the high ceiling-cloud. I wipe the blind white fungus away with a handful of moss, dry the wood beside the tiny, protected warmth of my hearth-gourd, and lay it neatly inside the dry tool-hollow where my spur joins the main arch—waiting for whichever pair of walking hands will already have needed it.

Now and then, a Binder comes down the vine-ladders to find me. Rarely the brisk, young ones; they have too many wedges to drive, too much world to keep tight. Usually it is someone whose timing has begun to slip a little ahead of their years—someone who stepped onto a rail that wasn't there yet, or whose shadow has been dragging three paces behind their heels since a bad crossing in the dry belt, or who cracked open a thousand-year culm and found a knot inside the dead heartwood that made their wrists ache just to trace it.

They never speak into the air. They crawl carefully over the curved rim of the trunk, letting gravity flip quietly beneath their soles, and sit beside me on the inverted underside of the spur while the warm ochre void opens gently above our heads.

I ladle them warm broth from the hanging pitcher-pods, steeped with fern-starch and a drop of sweet, fermenting phloem-mash. We let our sense of the present go a little slack—just enough to rest, not enough to drift. After an hour, or whatever an hour is when the shadows of passing gliders are still soaking slowly into the cane behind us, we lay our palms side by side against the warm bark near the tapering tips, and I click twice against my palate, sending a small, clean rhythm into the grain so they can feel it run to the edge of the wood and return to their own fingers unchanged.

A reminder that even in a world with no ground and no final floor, a thing sent out into the quiet does not simply vanish. It reaches a margin, turns gently in the living wood, and comes back to the hands that made it.

Eventually they shoulder their coils of green reed-cord, climb back over the curve of the beam, and walk out toward the main spans where arches are already beginning to bend into place ahead of their arrival. Their shadows linger on my ceiling-moss for a full minute after they are gone, cool and unhurried, before soaking away into the timber.

I stay where I am, toes hooked loosely around a petrified knot, the bog-moss behind my ears swelling and darkening twenty minutes before the next mist-bank drifts in—or twenty minutes after the cool dampness has already settled on my palms—listening to the wood hold itself together by habit, and catching the quiet things that loop back from the edge.
