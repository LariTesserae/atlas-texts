I think I would know where I belong here before I knew what to call myself, simply by noticing what my body stopped fighting.

At first, the impulse is to look up—toward the gliders stacked in silence on the invisible columns of rising heat, their crests flushing crimson as they climb and blanching to bone-white as they peel into a dive. There is a clean, austere freedom in that altitude, seeing the entire ochre patchwork at once: the travertine saucers, the white salt-lips, the long rusted thread of the rail line, the silent sparks of human light leaping from ridge to ridge. I would envy them for a minute. Then I would realize I don't actually want to be above the stone. I want to feel the heat coming up through it, and I want my hands on the hardware.

Nor would I want the western rim. Standing beside the braided power cables when the haul-cable shudders and the slatted steel box rises out of the flat floor of clouds—smelling of wet river-mud, green rot, and thick lowland air—would feel like having a door forced open into a room that is too loud even when nobody is speaking. I would pull my scarf across my nose with the rest, unload the drums of turbine oil and the bolts of coarse cloth, and be glad when the iron toggle fell and the crate dropped back through the white sea.

Where I find myself is further inland, a mile and a half east of the rim and slightly upslope from the lowest brine pans, where the basin begins to tilt gently toward the southern drop-off.

It is not a dramatic place. There is no main borehole roaring softly underfoot, no towering derrick. Just a broad, leaning apron of dark basalt that casts a deep wedge of shade from late morning onward, overlooking a terraced bowl of sinter chimneys and a wide stretch of flats. Forty paces below the overhang, the single rusted rail line curves along the contour of the slope, and right where the basalt meets the travertine crust, a squat, salt-pitted steel junction box sits bolted over a buried convergence of conduits. Three lines meet there under the stone—one running west toward the rim, one north across the central terraces, one dropping south toward a manifold house tucked in a fold of the floor.

That seam—where the rail passes a junction, where the southern cloud-surge arrives early and thick before the sun can burn it off—is where I would settle.

Not alone, because the plateau does not forgive solitude for long, and the households here are built for two or three pairs of hands. I see a dwelling made for two: half-sunken into the lee of the basalt slab, front wall cobbled tight and plastered smooth with brine-wetted chalk-wasp mortar until it disappears into the rock from twenty paces off. Doubled roof of tilted stone over packed mud, doorway low and angled south, sinter vent-plug ground by hand to fit the hairline fissure along the back wall. Outside, slung between two stubby sinter chimneys in the cool shadow of the overhang, our tilted loom-frame of salvaged copper wire and twisted sinew waits for the mist, dripping into a scalloped travertine trough where we have nudged a cluster of chalk-dusted rosettes into the dampest creases.

If I am honest about who I would be in that two-person household, I am not the one who climbs the highest travertine rims to hunt mica lizards, nor the one whose arms are heavy from stoking the clay kilns with wire-shrub twigs and hammering smelted penny-back copper on a basalt anvil. My companion can have the anvil and the heat of the kiln. I would be the one who sits in the cool dimness behind the doubled roof with a leather roll of hand-filed wrenches, a horn of dark grease, and a bone blow-pipe cut from a glider's wing, tending the small tolerances where the plateau's two halves—the living crust and the buried iron—press against each other.

There is no formal title for that up here, because everyone tends the grid in their own reach. What would mark me is simply where my attention keeps returning: to the places where signals pass, falter, or cross from one medium into another.

In air this thin, where a voice dies ten paces from your mouth, attention has to grow other organs. Mine would settle into my heels, my thumbs, and the narrow gray band behind my basalt eye-slits.

Most mornings, before the glare turns fierce enough to make the horizons swim, I would walk my stretch of the basin at that slow, unhurried pivot the altitude demands—step, pause, settle the weight, check the crust ahead, mouth closed so no water escapes. I would wear the pale, gypsum-rubbed linen that throws back the sun, wide brim pulled low, a palm-sized mica flash-plate looped to my thumb and a brass striking-pin tucked at my hip.

My circuit is small and intimate. First, the junction box below our overhang. I rest a hand on the warm cast iron before opening it, feeling for the steady, almost imperceptible vibration that says the trunk lines are balanced. Inside, I listen for the oil-damped relay—not for the sound of it, which barely clears the metal lip, but for the clean physical *clack* against my fingertips. Gypsum dust works its way past everything at four thousand meters. I clear the fine white grit from the switch contacts with the glider-bone blow-pipe, check the oiled crest-membrane gasket for dryness, and wipe a film of clean oil across the hinge before bolting the lid down again.

Around the base of the box's buried grounding stake, the penny-backed grazers pile in slow, shingled drifts, their shells so heavy with leached copper that they catch the morning sun like wet coins. I know them almost individually, the way you come to know the shapes of stones on a path you walk every day. When the oldest, heaviest heaps begin wedging too tight against the conduit flange, I slide a flat iron blade beneath them and shift them a yard out along the damp seepage groove, never rushing them, leaving the live ones to go on mining the stray current for another season and gathering only the empty, gleaming carapaces the dead have left behind. In a week my companion will melt those down into rivets and boot-strips; today they sit light and dry in my shoulder sack, clinking softly against a jar of pickled rosette leaves.

From the junction box I follow the rusted rail half a mile north, stepping along the ties with the narrow copper strip inset across my sandal arch pressing briefly against the iron every tenth pace. It is a habit I would never quite break, checking the rail the way lowlanders check the sky. Sometimes there is nothing but the faint, continuous hum of the central station's closed-loop turbines climbing cleanly up through my ankle-bones. Sometimes there is a dry, irregular cadence from a mile up the grade—a neighbor tapping news across the mineral flats while the morning haze is still too thick for clear light.

And then I climb to the nearest insulator bank, where the braided cables lift off the slope and march toward the western ridge. The chalk-wasps love the sheltered underside of the ceramic skirts, cementing mineral grit into pale honeycombed mounds that turn conductive the moment the southern cloud-shelf rolls in. Scraping them clean with a dull copper knife is slow, patient work, done in complete quiet—just the faint grit of lime falling onto dark basalt, the warm smell of acidic mortar, and every now and then the bright white needle-flash of a lizard pivoting on a sinter cone twenty feet away. I pocket the fat larvae in a folded cloth for evening, pack the crushed mortar into a second pouch for patching our doorway wall, and leave the glaze bare and slick down to the ceramic.

What draws me most to this life—what makes it feel less like an imagined costume and more like a shape I already recognize—is the way it treats translation as a form of care.

Because our overhang sits mid-basin, high enough to see across the central travertine bowls yet tucked below the main ridges, our threshold becomes a natural hinge. Two households separated by a dense thicket of tall sinter chimneys cannot throw a flash-plate beam directly to each other; they have to bounce it off someone on the slope. In the bright, still hours before noon, I often sit just inside the shadow-line of the basalt apron, legs tucked under me, watching the ochre shimmer through my carved eye-slits. When a steady white star blooms on the eastern ridge—held two slow heartbeats, dark, held again, too calm and deliberate to be a lizard—I lift my thumb, catch the sun on the hide-backed mica, and pass the cadence south. *Pan crusted safe at the third chimney.* *Cloud-bank building early over the lip.* *Relay cleared on the north trunk.*

No breath spent. No voice raised. Just light answering light over miles of crowded, simmering stone, leaving the basin's padded hush completely intact.

And when the maintenance bogie comes gliding down the single rail from the upper station—squat, riveted, smeared with dark grease, moving in uncanny silence along the rusted iron—I step off the track and watch it go by with the same quiet scrutiny I give the gliders' crests. I listen with my nose as much as my eyes. If an axle bearing runs dry, it throws a faint, sharp, scorching smell into the dry air that hangs for three seconds before the heat lifts it away. Before the bogie is fifty paces downslope, my heel is on the rail, brass pin striking three quick, measured ticks into the steel—dead and flat in the thin air, bright and unmistakable through the iron—so the household at the lower condenser mast is already waiting with an oil-can when it pauses to bleed its pale feather of steam.

If there is a single hour of the day that feels most completely mine, it is not morning and not night, but the twenty pewter-colored minutes when the cloud-surge pours over the southern lip.

You feel it coming before you see it. High overhead, the stacking gliders suddenly pale from arterial crimson to bone-white as the rising thermals collapse. Across the upper terraces, the nervous silver semaphore of the mica lizards blinks out all at once. Then the gray shelf heaves over the southern rim and spills across the basin floor.

Whatever I am doing—re-sealing a cracked travertine water-channel, grinding a fresh pair of basalt eye-slits for a neighbor's child, sorting mica flakes by how cleanly they split—I stop. If I am out on the flats, I loosen my chalk-dusted cloak and spread it wide over a warm slab so the heavy wool can drink the mist. If I am home, I stand just outside the low doorway and watch our wire loom-frame go instantly silver with beads of fresh, saltless water. For twenty minutes the whole basin hisses softly against the eardrums, the copper cooling fins of the distant manifold houses begin to tick as the sudden damp cools them, and the air smells briefly, astonishingly, of wet stone and nothing else. I wrap the damp cloak back around my shoulders as the sun starts burning the mist back to clear slate blue, letting that brief, dark cool evaporate against my skin while the water still threads down the stone troughs into our buried jars.

In the evening, when the sun hits the sawtooth western ridge and knife-edged shadows race across the crowded floor, the basin shifts gear and we shift with it. My companion and I walk slowly down the worn track—the salt-lips knocked flat years ago by the heels of hammers—to the broad, warm lee of the nearest manifold house. Other small households drift in out of the lengthening shadows without hurry. Nobody greets anybody with loud words. You lower yourself onto the warm basalt, rest a palm against the cast-iron wall or a bare heel against the borehole apron, and let the steady deep tremor of the turbine settle through your bones while the copper louvers tick overhead in the cooling air.

This is where my quiet hoarding of small tolerances enters the basin's ledger. I trade a freshly backed mica flash-plate—polished for three afternoons with fine gypsum dust until its beam holds clean across two miles—for a string of dried lizard-strips and a fist of dark wire-shrub resin. I sit beside an old woman whose ribs are wide as barrels and whose fingernails carry the permanent blue-green stain of verdigris, and while she taps slow evening news down the buried conduit to a household two miles north, I scrape the salt-crust from the hinge of her worn brass striking-pin and file the tip square again. Around us, people speak in low, dry, economical murmurs, clipped syllables and small turns of the chin, saving breath out of habit even in the cool dusk. Children dart silently between the sinter chimneys, practicing the slow sweep of their first mica plates against the last red slant on the eastern ridge.

Later, back inside our dim room, the outside air drops sharp and cold in minutes. One of us reaches along the back wall and eases the hand-ground sinter plug an inch or two out of the bedrock fissure. A slow, dry breath of geothermal warmth rises into the dark, smelling faintly of sulfur and clean machine oil and shrub-resin. Lying on the sleeping-mat with the worn-blank remnant of an old laminated wiring diagram tucked flat beneath the woven reed—not because I can read the vanished letters, only because its smooth, stiff ghost is a good thing to keep under a hip—I can still feel the faint, continuous hum of the junction trunk forty paces down the slope.

I think, after enough years of that, the question of *who* and *what* stops dividing neatly.

You start out as a person who lives on a high mineral tableland and tends the remnants of an old industry. Slowly, without noticing the turn, your body becomes another piece of the plateau's carpentry: blood thick and coppery against the four-thousand-meter air, ribcage broad for slow breathing, skin cured to the color of weathered basalt, fingernails stained faintly green from copper scrap and penny-back shells. You become a living node in the same quiet network as the wire-shrubs tracing the warm trenches and the grazers turning stray current into metal. You receive light from the east and pass it south; you receive tremor from the north and answer it with oil and a clean contact; you keep the seams from clogging so the whole crowded, silent arrangement can keep holding together for another season.

And when my breath finally grows too light and dry to walk the rail-ties—even in the stillness behind the doubled roof—I know where I would go, and it brings no dread at all. Carried at dusk up into the weathered volcanic ridges at the edge of the basin. Laid deep inside a dry fissure where neither sun nor mist climbs, my polished mica flash-plate tucked beneath my folded hands, pale salt-slabs stacked across the mouth and sealed smooth with wet chalk-wasp mortar. Within a season, the salt-bloom and rust-lichen creep across the seam, and I vanish cleanly back into the stone—dry, light, silent, holding my small share of the hush while the sky deepens toward black overhead and the buried iron goes on humming beneath the floor.
