At first, I would look for myself among the four.

It is the easiest gravity to fall into. When you enter a chamber where the water hammers straight through your teeth, your first instinct is to want a body heavy enough not to be thrown—dense ribs without air pockets, horn-plated palms that can grip freshly sheared basalt without opening, a jaw wide enough to catch the shift of a far cliff before the stone splits loose. More than that, I recognize the craft of their baffles. Turning a raw shard of slate inch by inch in the current until the high tearing edge shears off overhead, leaving only a bearable, chest-rattling throb—I know that hunger. To take an unlivable roar and, with nothing but weight and angle, carve a small pocket of rhythm out of it.

I could stay inside that body for a while. I could ride the unmixed layers like staircases, dropping through a downward eddy of heavy warmth, hooking a heel into a rising column, listening to four taps and two taps travel down a basalt vein.

And yet, the longer I try to wear their dense, sinking frames, the more something inside me pulls upward and sideways, out of alignment with their weight.

They *want* to sink. Their marrow belongs to the stone so completely that when they climb to the leeward arch and push a scarred forearm through the trembling wall of water, the sudden silence outside feels like a blade laid across the wrist. They hunch their shoulders, clamp their gill-slits, endure four heartbeats, five, and snatch their hand back into the scalding roar before the numbness can climb the bone.

I know I would not snatch my hand back on the fifth heartbeat. I would wait for the sixth, and the tenth, curious to feel what happens after the sting of frost goes dull—when the absence of vibration stops feeling like an amputation and begins to feel like an open room. And in a body built for weight and water, that curiosity is just another name for falling past the cloud-deck.

So I let the heavy swimmer go, and drift into the dim, empty volume between the cliffs to see what else holds on here.

For a moment I test the blind, glassy sac—boneless, threaded with taut pale filaments, riding the acoustic nodes from one pocket of lesser violence to the next. There is something close to the bone in that: being mostly resonant interior, an instrument with no mouth and only ears, humming with whatever weather carries you. Yet it has no grip. It cannot choose what to hold against the surge, and it never reaches the edge of the basin except by accident.

Down near the floor vents, the long, pale ribbon glides straight through a violent seam of opposing water without its body even rippling, leaving behind a brief trough of dropped pressure and a thread of lukewarm mucus tasting of iron and rain. That pulls harder—the grace of moving *across* the shear, of leaving a five-yard wake that someone heavier can briefly slip through. Still, the ribbon keeps to the scalding bottom. It has no business with the bent green-gray daylight or the pale smear of the open arch.

Even the braided line of black wall-weed and animal tendon stretched between two outcrops feels almost like a home—anchored at both ends, slack until the current reverses, carrying a single plucked thrum cleanly across the shriek so two distant bodies can know one another through the murk. Almost. But a line only speaks when someone else strikes it, and only lasts until the next great swell tears its stone loops free.

And all the while I am testing these shapes, my attention keeps drifting back to the same raw scar halfway up the leeward face: the jagged arch where the mountain stops, and the water refuses to spill.

That is where I find myself. Not down among the vents, not tumbling in the high froth of the sky-opening, and not out in the empty highland wind where nothing pushes back. Right on the broken teeth of basalt that frame the open arch—straddling the shimmering, blistered membrane that keeps the roaring world and the quiet one from swallowing each other.

I am not one of the climbers, and not a wind-blown scrap of moss caught in an eddy. Whatever I am here has been shaped by the cut itself.

If you were swimming up from the dim center of the nave, looking toward the pale, vapor-wrapped glow of the arch, you would miss me twice before you caught the shape. I lie low and broad against the jagged rim, roughly the span of an outstretched arm from tip to tip, flattened like a living bracket wedged into the fracture where rock ends and standing water begins. No trailing fins for the shear to rip off, no brittle shells that a standing wall of sound could shatter. Four short, thick-jointed limbs anchor me into the freshly split corners of the arch, ending in deeply grooved, horn-hard hooks that lock into basalt the way roots lock into scree.

What gives me away—if a cluster of cavitation voids collapses close enough to paint the rim in cold-white light—is that my body does not agree with itself from one side to the other.

The half of me that stays inside the chamber belongs to the basin’s law. Its hide is matte, slate-dark, plated in thick, overlapping bands of dense cartilage scarred by blown mineral grit and edged like raw rock. Beneath those bands sit narrow, muscular slits that draw the warm, churning water in brief, steady pulses whenever the swell presses inward. Through my inner sternum and my locked hooks, I take the whole low organ-note of the cleft—the grinding of a freshly split spur on the east wall, the sudden pressure-bar stacking up mid-nave, the distant, blunt acoustic shadows of the four heavy climbers moving along their separate heights. I do not need eyes to know when one of them has plucked the braided line two ledges down, or when the currents near the floor have twisted briefly around the quiet eye of the stone ring. The cliff tells me through my wrists, and the water tells me through my ribs.

The other half of me—the long, ridged flank and narrow crest that lean *through* the trembling membrane—belongs to the wind.

Inside the cleft, nothing has time to grow smooth; the mountain perpetually re-breaks itself to keep every corner sharp. Yet the chamber’s law ends at the vertical wall of water. An inch beyond that blistered skin, the ordinary, patient violence of high altitude resumes: freezing air, rime, drifting sleet, the slow abrasion of wind over exposed stone. And so my outer side has been weathered pale as bleached bone, worn down into long, shallow flutes that shine faintly with a glaze of thin ice. There are no gills there, no soft wet membranes to glue shut in the cold. Instead, fine, dry-rimmed grooves run along those pale ridges—hollowed channels of dense horn that whistle almost imperceptibly when the highland air rakes across them.

My whole life is the pendulum of the membrane.

When a heavy swell rolls through the nave, the upright wall of water bulges outward past the stone rim, drowning me whole. Instantly the pale flutes seal shut, trapping tiny beads of dry air inside their grooves. The scalding, iron-rich roar floods over my back, melting the rime from my outer plates in a sudden hiss of microscopic collapsing steam—a faint halo of cold-white sparks along my silhouette that vanishes before the eye can hold it. I flatten hard against the basalt, matching the cliff’s tremor, taking the blow straight through my dense inner frame until the water remembers itself and slaps back inward.

Then the membrane retreats an inch, two inches, peeling clean off my outer flank.

The silence drops over that half of my body like a pane of cold glass.

To the climbers, that clean line across the skin is a severance. To me, it is the other half of breathing. The wet film flashes off my pale ridges in silver threads of vapor; the scalding ache drains out of my marrow; the dry grooves unseal and drink the thin, mercilessly clear mountain air. And for three heartbeats, five, sometimes ten when the basin slacks, I listen in both directions at once.

Through my inner hooks and ribs: the deafening, fever-warm pulse of the cleft, the scrape of a mineral wedge prying a slate-gray shell off a lower fissure, the low throb of a stone baffle somewhere along the north cliff.

Through my frosted outer flutes: the vast, un-ringing openness beyond the ridge. Snow sliding off a distant saddle. The dry whisper of wind combing frozen scree half a mile below. The thin, sharp scent of pine and altitude that never enters the water except on a hawk’s broken wing.

I do not belong wholly to either side, and neither side can hold me alone. When a great pressure-storm builds in the cleft and the standing wall bulges so violently that it threatens to tear me from the teeth of the arch, I unhook one limb at a time and creep a few yards *inside*, wedging myself into a narrow north-wall groove until the pitch drops—yet if I stay submerged too long, the unbroken shriek begins to work past my dense plates, turning feverish and suffocating, until my sealed outer grooves ache for the bite of cold air. And if I creep too far *out* onto the dry, wind-scoured lip of the mountain when the swells drop low, the silence grows too wide; my inner plates dry and tighten, my bones begin to feel hollow and unmoored without the water pressing back against them, and I scramble hurriedly back into the wet steam of the rim before I forget how much I weigh.

I feed on what the boundary makes.

Where the scalding vapor peels off the water and strikes the freezing stone of the outer arch, a pale, mineral-rich rime forms and melts and forms again, trapping stray filaments of torn wall-weed, tiny grains of blown highland lichen, and the shredded remnants of whatever small things the upward currents throw too close to the membrane. When the swell pulls back, I graze slowly along the wet-ice seam with a broad, horn-edged lip, taking in salt-sharp mineral crust and frozen moss, and when the water surges back over me, I catch the occasional pale stalk-nub or blind humming sac spun up against the arch before the eddy drags it down again. It is a lean diet, enough to keep a slow, dense body mended and heavy, never enough to make me clumsy.

Mostly, I stay still and listen.

Because of where I cling, the four climbers rarely bother me. I am too high, too close to the treacherous bulging membrane, and my flesh—half scalded cartilage, half frost-toughened horn—is not worth the risk of prying off a ledge where one wrong slip drops you into the clouds. Now and then, when the lean, restless one drifts up through the battered froth near the sky-opening, it pauses a yard beneath my basalt tooth, cloudy inner lids half-drawn, tilting its bare skull to read the strange, thin acoustic shadow I cast against the rim. It does not come closer. It taps the stone once—a flat, testing knuckle—waits a beat while my dense inner flank returns the tremor slightly blunted, slightly cooler than bare rock, and then pushes off into a downward eddy, leaving me to the steam.

Only one of them ever shares the arch with me for long.

Every so often, when the swells slacken and the smoky green-gray daylight holds steady along the rim, I feel a slow, deliberate climb ascending the north flank of the arch—a slightly crooked rhythm in the stone where an old, imperfectly healed collarbone shifts its weight from one basalt tooth to the next. I do not draw away. I flatten a fraction closer to the jagged lip, letting my dense inner side soften the razor edge of the rim’s vibration just slightly, a narrow living baffle along the rock.

The old one hooks a calloused foot around a spur an arm’s length below me and hangs there in the dim glow, gill-slits pulsing slowly, watching vapor curl off the bulging wall of water into the gray drop beyond. Between its thickened, scarred fingers sits the fourth pale stone—still bearing one stubbornly flat face and the dulled shadow of an old corner, though season by season I have felt that corner yield.

Slowly, the old one raises its forearm and thrusts its fist straight through the shimmering membrane.

Inside the cleft, the water seals tight around its wrist. Outside, inches from my frost-planed flank, the scarred fingers open.

The old one’s whole frame stiffens against the cut of silence—jaw locked, shoulders hunched, gill-slits clamped shut. I stay motionless beside that pale, steaming palm, feeling the freezing wind rush across its wet knuckles and across my own dry, fluted ridges at the exact same instant. For five heartbeats, the wind sings over the unfinished stone, drying the milky rock-dust in its microscopic pores, laying a thin, invisible coat of cold quiet onto its half-rounded skin.

Five heartbeats. Then the fist closes, snatching the stone back into the roaring, fever-warm dark. The old one pulls its frost-stung hand tight to its sternum, lets the basin’s violence flood back into its knuckles, and sinks slowly back down through the murky volume toward the crooked ring of slate on the floor.

I remain on the jagged lip as the next swell bulges outward, swallowing me in scalding noise, turning the rime on my outer flank to a brief whisper of silver sparks before the water slaps back and leaves me bare to the sky again.

I understand what the old one is doing down there in the dimness—wearing one small piece of the mountain down against its own law until a stone can sit at the bottom of the roar and hold a memory of the wind. I cannot go down to the floor to hover a palm above the three smooth pebbles; the pressure there is too unbroken, the edge too far away.

Instead, I am what the edge looks like when it stays alive. Not a refuge from the roar, and not an escape into the silence, but the narrow strip of stone and skin where both of them touch—held together by weight, by frost, by a patient refusal to let either half of the world sever me from the other.
