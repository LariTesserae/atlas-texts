At first, I think I would be up on the western ridges, among the fog-nets.

It is the easiest image to reach for, and for a while it feels almost right: standing where the open wind still has its full voice, before the sod-eaves and staggered lanes part it and lift it overhead. Up there, the work is plain and strangely familiar—taking something vast, shapeless, and passing through the cold air, and giving it just enough woven sedge to catch on until it gathers weight and becomes water. You would spend your mornings with numbed fingers, knotting broken grass-cord while cloud-bellies dragged straight through your ribs, listening to the different rustle of dry rush that tells you whether the pewter wall rolling in carries clean mist or sleet.

Yet the longer I try to stand on that ridge, the less it holds me. The rim is too solitary, too sharp-edged. Up there you only meet the beginning of things—the raw sky and the wind-scarps where the dead are laid to dry into bone—and you never see what becomes of the water once it leaves the grass-nets and slides down into the geometry of the lanes. If I stayed on the ridge, I would always be leaning over the parapet, squinting down through the mist-gaps to see how the middle terraces were carrying the weight.

So I climb down, and for a moment I wonder if I am not a person here at all.

It is tempting to claim the water, because the water touches everything—every roof-pan, every notched boulder, every household along a fall. It carries the milky marl of warning and the knotted rushes of news; it knows the whole descent from the western cloud-comb to the unseen eastern basin. But water only goes one way. It cannot turn back up a stairway to ask a question. It cannot remember the hand that lifted the weir three terraces above, nor brace a retaining wall that has begun to bow outward under forty winters of frost. It is too purely obedient to gravity.

Something smaller comes closer: the pale snails grazing the algae-film along the cistern lips. I recognize a quiet kinship in them—living right at the seam between air and water, leaving winding, unintentional script along the stone, keeping the surface clean simply by moving steadily across it, and knowing before any human eye does when a black freeze is coming, climbing above the meniscus to seal themselves tight. And closer still, perhaps, the travertine itself: that slow mineral patience that trickles out of the runoff and cements separate, dry-stacked blocks back into a single geology.

Still, if I am honest, I would miss the warmth of the sunken floor at night. I would miss the dry, low-in-the-throat speech, and the flat-palm gestures at a sluice-gate, and the smell of bog-peat smoldering beneath reed mats. I do not want to be mute, even in a world where quiet is sacred. I want to be *quiet*—which is a different thing, something you practice with your throat and your feet, knowing all the while what noise would cost.

So I keep walking downward, learning the plateau’s flat-soled step on the dished limestone, knees unlocked, weight held slightly forward so the mist-film does not take my heels out from under me. I pass the highest tiers where the ice locks earliest and the young haul baskets of peat up broad shallow flights. I walk until the cutting gale lifts cleanly ten feet above the turf-caps and the air in the cleft-alleys goes suddenly, shockingly still.

And there, somewhere in the broad middle reaches—not close enough to the western rim to hear the wind howl, not far enough east for the terraces to flatten into the great settling-marshes—I find the place that stops me.

It is not a handsome or regular quarter. Generations ago, the bedrock beneath this shelf shifted slightly, and a long retaining wall sagged outward in a slow, heavy belly. Rather than tear it down, the builders braced it with rounded cobbles and curved the walkways respectfully around the bulge. Over a century, that curve pulled the drainage of the neighboring tiers slightly off-true, until three separate falls ended up meeting here in a wide, irregular junction-pan before dividing again through four notched outlets into the districts below.

Nobody calls it anything grand. On the shelf, they probably just call it *the wide turn*, or name it after whoever braced the bulge last, or after the dwarf willow hunched against its leeward corner—crooked as a knee, its bark rubbed glossy dark by generations of palms pausing to touch living wood as they pass.

This is where I live. Not in a high room, and not yet in the deepest warm burrows kept for the stiff-jointed elders, though my dwelling is half-sunk into the curve of the slope behind the junction-pan, its slate lintel low enough that I duck without thinking before lifting the oiled hide flap.

I am not native to this exact shelf—at least, I don’t think I am. Something in me remembers the drier western margin, close enough to the causeways where the rim-herders lay down bales of raw fleece and cured hide twice a season. I remember the physical startle of hearing a herder’s voice pitched for open grass, how it struck the ear like a dropped cobble. Perhaps I grew up on an outer terrace near the dykes and came down here years ago when the old keeper of this room was carried up to the northern scarps, and nobody else on the shelf particularly wanted the house because the junction-pan outside its sill is fussy, crooked, and asks for constant small attention.

That suits me better than straight masonry ever could.

Because three falls meet at my threshold, the water that arrives here never tells just one story. From the north comes a cold, sharp-clear run off shaded roofs that stays thin and slow until mid-morning, carrying needles of thaw-ice long after the sun has warmed my own coping-stones. From straight west comes a broader, slightly peaty sheet off a shelf of turf-cutters and root-washers; after a squall, it brings tiny floating flecks of dark moss and pale pollen that like to gather in the dead water behind the curved wall. And from a narrow cleft slightly south-west comes runoff from a quarter of potters who work basin-silt near the leeward clamps, so their inflow sometimes arrives faintly clouded, cool grey-white, smelling of wet clay.

My work—if you can call it work, when on the plateau no one assigns titles and the city runs by gravity and attention—is simply to keep these three waters from arguing with one another.

If the western shelf lifts its weirs after a heavy mist-morning while the northern channel is still half-choked with frost, the junction-pan tilts its weight too fast toward the southernmost outlet, chewing at the moss-joints and sending a hurried, muddy surge down onto the sorrel-beds three tiers below. That would be a failure of the lower debt. Within an hour, someone from the lower fall would climb the stairs, not to scold, just to stand quietly beside the notch and look at the murky lip until I felt the heat rise behind my ears.

So I spend my mornings squatting on my heels on the coping-stone, layered felt tented over my knees, cool hands resting near the dressed shale slates wedged between the outlet boulders. I keep three or four smooth, flat river-pebbles tucked in the fold of my wrap—nothing heavy, just shims of different thicknesses. Half a finger’s width of stone slipped beneath a shale weir changes the whole pitch of the pan. I do not have to see the water move to know when I have found the right level; I hear it. The faint, anxious hiss at the notched lip drops back into a smooth, almost soundless glide, a gleaming film no thicker than a thumbnail.

And because three falls cross here, things catch.

Most days, a knotted rush sent downstream from an upper terrace passes cleanly through the pan and slips into the channel meant for it. But the curved wall makes a slow, lazy eddy near the leeward willow, and sometimes a message loses its momentum there and turns in quiet circles among floating pollen—a reed bent twice with a sliver of dried sorrel tucked into the loop, or a triple-knotted stalk whose tie has loosened in the damp until it is nearly unreadable.

I fish them out with two blunt-nailed fingers, cool and creased with pale lime-mud. Over the years I have learned the hands of the shelves above me the way other people know faces. The weavers two tiers up pinch their rushes sharp and flat before bending them; the old household by the high cistern leaves a long, frayed tail on the second knot. Sometimes I only need to tighten a slipping loop, tuck the sorrel sliver firmly back in, and set the stalk gently into the correct outflow notch so gravity can finish the sentence.

Other times, the eddy catches a rush meant for a household two lanes over along the *shelf*—or worse, a drift of unexpectedly milky marl arrives from the west without warning, meaning someone upstream had a wall slump in the night and had to scrape a pan in haste. Water cannot carry a reply uphill. When that happens, I wrap my feet in plaited rush—or leave them bare if the midday sun has warmed the slate—and walk upward or sideways through the cleft-alleys.

I like those walks. Turning my shoulder smoothly inward to pass a neighbor carrying cut sedge on a pole, our damp wool brushing with a hush; tapping my knuckles once against a wet boulder to tell a terrace-keeper that a seam beneath his frost-line is weeping too fast; leaning close to murmur three dry words, then tilting a flat palm downward to show how slow I will draw the junction-pan while he packs fresh clay. Because I live where several lines cross, I end up carrying small pieces of one shelf’s weather to another—not as a herald, never with urgency, just as a pair of hands that happened to notice the water’s pitch change.

In truth, I am only middling at the heavier crafts of the plateau. My coiled clay jars lean slightly when they come out of the peat-clamps, as if they too have learned the slump of my retaining wall. When the shelf gathers to brace a shifting buttress or re-turf a wind-baffle roof, I am steady enough with lime-mud and cobbles, though never the strongest back on the lane. My real usefulness is smaller, almost invisible: I notice early.

I notice when the pale snails on the shaded side of the junction-pan begin climbing past the water-line at two in the afternoon, sealing their tiny shells tight against the limestone, and I send a knotted rush—or walk thirty paces up the stairs—so the upper shelves lift their weirs before a black freeze splits their shallow basins. I notice when a child from the tier below has been set to gather snails into wet moss during a pan-scraping, and I make room for them along my sunny lip until their own water runs clear again. I notice the faint thinning of the drip off the sphagnum beard outside my window-slit in the dark, and know without opening the hide flap that the highest western ledges have locked shut for the night.

And once in a long while, on a clear, windless afternoon when the glare turns the vast stepped surface to shattered silver, a solitary grey wading bird drops out of the pale sky and folds its wings at the far rim of my junction-pan.

Whenever that happens, everything inside me goes as still as the sunken air in the courts. I do not reach for a weir. I do not shift my weight on the limestone. If someone comes down the cleft-alley with a bundle of rushes, I only lift two fingers gently near my cloak-pin, and they stop in the shadow of the retaining wall without a sound. We watch the bird stand motionless on one leg, indistinguishable from a weathered marker-stone, testing the quiet of our water. It stays for twenty minutes, or an hour, before lifting without a ripple and gliding low over three roofs toward the east. Neither of us says anything after it leaves, though later that evening the neighbor might set a small twist of salted rim-fat or a handful of roasted rush-seeds on my dry coping-stone. A bird staying at the junction means all three falls above us are clean, and our hands have been gentle.

As evening comes on and the sun drops behind the western ridges, turning ten thousand wet roofs to sheets of cool copper, I do my last round of the notches. I lift one shale slate a breath higher so the night-drop moves steadily through the deepest channel and won’t freeze solid over the broad shallow apron. I press my palm briefly against the glossy crook of the dwarf willow—just to feel something alive that has endured forty years of frost without hurrying—and then I squat for a few last minutes beside the water before ducking inside.

It is always in that twilight pause, with the thin wisps of bog-peat smoke rising invisibly from the sunken flues and the first fine needles of ice knitting outward from the moss, that the line between *who* I am and *what* I am grows happily indistinct.

Inside, there will be the amber glow of a tallow saucer, a warm clay floor beneath my bare heels, a pot of chalk-white tubers and bruised sorrel simmering softly over buried embers, and yards of grass-cord waiting to be twisted between my fingers while the frost deepens outside the rush-screen. I am undeniably a person of the plateau—deep-chested, squint-lined from the mirror-glare, permanently smelling of cold limestone and damp wool, carrying clay in the creases of my knuckles.

Yet sitting here in the copper dusk, watching three separate waters slide into the wide curve before my door, slow down enough to drop their silt, lose their hurry, and divide cleanly into the dark toward districts I will never walk to—I know I am also just a small, deliberate pause in the stone. Not the ridge that combs the sky, and not the unseen eastern sink where everything finally vanishes. Only the wide shallow pan in the middle of the slope, braced around an old imperfection, listening closely to what arrives from above so that what leaves my threshold glides onward thin, clear, and unbroken through the night.
