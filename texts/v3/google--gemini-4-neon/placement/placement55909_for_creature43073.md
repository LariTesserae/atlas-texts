At first I think I would want to be one of the plants.

Not the great elastic vines that stretch between drifting tenements until they snap, and not the thick cushions of bottle-green moss that take the brunt of the masonry, though there is a quiet dignity in being what keeps iron from ringing. I find myself looking longest at the copper-veined rosettes pressed along the edges of the junction boxes—those small, stubborn things that push fine metallic root-hairs through cracked insulation, sip a fraction of the grid’s stray current, and melt a neat, steaming green halo into the frost around themselves. There is something deeply enviable about a life whose whole purpose is to stay warm on a trickle of passing charge, and by staying warm, tell anyone who glances down the dark busbars whether the line is still alive.

Then I climb down from the high catwalk, out of that merciless, unshadowed glare, and I realize I am too restless to stay rooted to a single box.

I test the other strata as I go. I know within ten minutes that I am not a rig-walker. Up on the telescoping gantry arms, with nothing behind my shoulders but pale escarpments and a cold sky that hides nothing, I feel peeled open—too visible, too far from the warm dampness where things actually touch. For a while I linger in the twilight beneath the city, crouched beside the under-leggers on their greased wooden creepers. I love the honesty down there: the smell of cold hydraulic oil and wet pyrite, the slow, patient nudging of lichen-shelled grazers away from a descending steel shoe, the way an old worker reads a frost-blind lizard’s twitching translucent tail as calmly as morning weather. I almost stay. It feels right to be underneath the weight of things, listening for the first dry itch of straining stone, keeping the feet from stumbling.

Yet every time a brass hammer taps a code up through a steel leg to a crane operator four stories above, my attention climbs the vibration and refuses to come back down. I don’t want to live solely in the dark beneath the skids, and I don’t want to kneel on raw rising basalt with the seam-setters, coaxing an infant crust into hexagons. That takes a kind of parental patience with the earth I don’t quite possess. What pulls at me—what makes my own knees bend and settle into the basin’s roll—is the *gap* between moving blocks. The moment a bridge unlatches, the vines draw taut, and two households drift out of arm’s reach without losing track of one another.

That is where I find my perch.

Not on the ground, and not on the high wires, but at eaves-height—three stories up, tucked directly under the moss-pelted belly of a major tramway crossing where two crisscrossing aerial conveyors pass over a busy knot of tenements, lathes, and steaming conservatories.

My room is barely wider than a pantry and a bunk, built of seasoned spruce and riveted iron lined thick with bruised-green felt, and it does not stand on hydraulic legs. It has no flank-neighbors bolted to its walls, no party-pipes shared with a kitchen next door. Instead, it hangs. Like the spruce beams inside a thrum-hall, the whole little shed is slung from the steel junction-gantry above on four thick, waxed-leather straps and oiled counterweights, floating six feet clear of the jostling roofs below.

The hung design is practical before it is poetic: because nothing touches the porous black basalt beneath me, the earth cannot drink the vibrations arriving along the overhead rails. I live barefoot on oiled timber, inside a box that acts like the sounding-board of a cello no one can hear.

When the basin wakes under the crisp, bright sun, I feel the whole quarter’s traffic enter through the soles of my feet before I even push back the felt curtains. A long, heavy, even drag through the left heel means quarried hexagonal basalt moving south from a fresh fault-crest toward the sintering halls. A lighter, springier sway overhead is freshly cut turf and coiled copper coming off the eastern grid. Underneath both sits the steady, velvet hum of the porcelain insulator yards, pitched so low it lives in the roots of my back teeth.

If the basin has a name for what I am, it is something dry and small—a *crossing-hand*, a *berth-keeper*, a *line-clerk*—the sort of half-noticed tender people wave a white-beaded glove toward without slowing their stride. But up inside the slung shed, with condensation running in bright threads down all four double-glazed windows, the craft has grown stranger and more intimate than any title for it.

Against my north wall stands a frame of oiled brass rods, sliding enamel sector-tiles, and braided hemp cords—not a map of the valley, because a map of ground here would be a joke before the ink dried, but a living lattice of *adjacencies*. Which weaving hall is currently bolted to which transformer bank. Which alleyway just pinched shut beneath a rising shale ridge. Which grandmother’s fourth-story conservatory has drifted clear of a crane boom long enough to hold a clean sightline to her grandson’s kitchen window. Every time a mechanical sector-plaque flips on a migrating roof within half a mile, I move a brass pin and re-tension a cord. I do not own mass or coupling-rights. What I hold is the shape of who is currently next to whom.

And because the open conveyor trays glide past just outside my felt-skirted side hatch—silent on their vulcanized rollers, heaped with raw stone, ceramic bells, and wet green sod—the city’s quiet traffic passes straight through my gloved fingers.

People tuck small oilskin parcels and chalked slate-slips into the corners of the industrial loads, trusting the lines to carry what a shouted voice never could. I reach out into the cold sunlit drift, pluck the packets from the passing trays, wipe the rime-frost from their brass destination tags, and drop them onto the crossing line that will actually meet a house that walked three streets east before noon. A spare bronze coupling-pin sent ahead by a couple preparing to winch their rooms together for the winter. A tiny waxed-wood strip notched with a new low-register harmony for a thrum-hall player across the basin. A pressed blue-green rosette leaf with a single copper terrace-number scratched into its waxy spine, telling a rig-walker where her old crew-mate was laid into the glasshouse loam at dawn. Sometimes, folded inside oiled linen, a quick chalk sketch made by an under-legger down in the dark—three sharp lines mimicking the stutter of a frost-blind lizard’s tail—bound for a seam-setter two sectors over so she knows how the deep rock is leaning before it breaks the paving stones.

I don’t work alone in the sling. No one in the basin really lives without neighbors, even when their walls are unbolted.

Outside my south sill, where the feeder busbars fan out from the crossing to the surrounding blocks, I have tended a neat row of those copper-veined rosettes I envied from the catwalk. With a bone awl I pricked a pinhole through the vulcanized jacket of each outgoing line and coaxed a plant’s metallic roots into the breach. Now I have a botanical switchboard blooming along my window-ledge. On bright, freezing mornings, I do not need a meter to tell me how the quarter is holding together; I only look through the streaming glass at the neat row of steaming green halos melted into the frost. When the third rosette from the left quietly clouds over with white ice, its halo shutting like an eye, I know the seed-drying loft down the lane has just uncoupled its power and lifted onto its hydraulic shoes. I reach behind me without looking, unpin its enamel tile on my brass board, and watch through my battered spyglass to see where it means to settle next.

Above my rafters, inside the hollow iron counterweight that keeps my shed level when a cold gust sweeps the basin, lives a breeding pair of six-toed, slate-blue climbers. They are shameless thieves. If I leave the parcel-hatch unlatched for ten seconds, one will drop down in a jerky, oiled streak to steal a lump of tallow or a shiny brass washer from my bench. I leave worn-smooth washers out on purpose now, beside a porcelain saucer of sweet salty condensate scraped from my pipe-joints. They pay better rent than any human tenant could. When an insulator yard three streets away builds a hidden short, or a gantry tie-rod begins to shear under quiet torsion, they pour out of the counterweight in a rippling blue ribbon, their teeth clicking a rapid panic-rhythm straight down the iron suspension rods into my floor. Three times this winter I have flashed a double-wink of pale blue shutter-lamp down into the canyons on their word alone, watching a maintenance crew clear a viaduct ten seconds before a jagged tooth of fresh black rock split the lane open with a polite *tock*.

Living this way changes the shape of you, slowly, the way the basin’s moving floors bend a child’s knees before they ever learn to stand on stillness.

My hands stay pale and quick inside bone-beaded gauntlets, fluent in the clipped street-shorthand of the catwalks, while the skin around my eyes has gathered the deep, weathered crinkles of anyone who spends afternoons peering through brass lenses across blinding cold light. My voice has shrunk into something barely used—a low, dusty murmur kept tucked behind a matted wool scarf. It comes out only in the late dusk, when an off-shift green-hand or a tired under-legger smelling of grease and pyrite climbs the iron ladder to my trapdoor, trades a covered tin of hot root-broth for a sighting of a brother’s tenement, and crouches beside my induction stove long enough for our plumes of breath to mingle over two tin cups.

Sometimes they ask—softly, close to my ear, so the sound never wastes itself against the felt walls—if it isn’t lonely hanging up here uncoupled, watching everybody else bolt their rooms together for warmth while my own four walls touch nothing but air and leather straps.

I never quite know how to explain that hanging free is the only reason I can feel all of them at once.

If I bolted my flank to a tenement, my floor would take only that one building’s gait; my brass board would shrink to one street’s view; the hum in my heels would narrow to a single hearth. Out here in the crossing, unlatched to any single home, I am held in tension by a hundred of them. When a weaving loft shifts four inches west on greased sledges, my leather slings sway a hair’s breadth to answer it. When a family couples a new room onto their chassis, a new rosette melts its green ring onto my sill. When two lovers drift half a mile apart for a season, their small oilskin letters warm my palm for thirty seconds between the northbound copper-tray and the eastbound turf.

There is an hour late in the clear, cold afternoon that belongs wholly to this perch.

The daylight slants low across the pale escarpments, turning every shaded rivet and curled fern-frond silver with returning rime. Below me, a cluster of tightly packed buildings—a lathe-works, a corner tea-room, two steaming glasshouses—quietly releases its latches and shuffles half a block east to make room for a fresh hexagonal plinth of black basalt rising wetly through the street. Across the drifting gap, green collarbone-lamps ripple back and forth like fireflies in snow.

I stand barefoot on the oiled spruce, holding a small oilskin packet plucked from a passing tray of quarried stone—its chalked address already obsolete by two hours. I glance at the frosted rosette on my ledge, raise the battered brass glass to my eye, and sweep the shifting cranes until I catch the newly flipped enamel digits of a third-story tenement settling onto its pads three streets over. In its steamed-up kitchen window, a small pinprick of amber light winks impatiently: *waiting, yield, where is it?*

I thumb my own collarbone shutter twice in green—*coupled, found, coming across*—and lean out through the felt hatch just as the eastbound copper-conveyor glides silently beneath my sill. My white-tipped fingers drop the parcel cleanly into the hollow of a coiled spool.

Then I pull the hatch shut against the bite of the air, press one bare palm flat against the warm spruce beam of my wall, and hum a single quiet note with closed lips—nothing the cold sky will ever hear, nothing louder than a drop of water striking a rubbery leaf thirty feet below—just enough to feel the leather slings take the tremor, carry it up into the humming iron crossing, and fold it silently back into the drift.
