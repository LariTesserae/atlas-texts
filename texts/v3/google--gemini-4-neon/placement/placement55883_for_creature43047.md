At first, I would try to stay in the open blue.

That is the old reflex—the bias of a mind used to thinking of itself as something unmoored, passing through a place rather than cemented into it. Looking out across those hollow, roaring leagues of alpine light, the first shape that pulls at me is the drift-veil: a loose, tattered web of silver-green filaments tumbling through the racing sunbeams, caught between the boiling foam above and the ringing indigo below.

I can feel the appeal of that immediately. To have no cliff to defend, no stone to bargain with. To drift blind through blinding squalls of metallic silt and sudden flurries of frazil ice, shredded half to nothing, until by pure accident you cross one of the invisible standing-wave lattices hanging in the gulf. For three heartbeats, the tiny quartz needles woven into your strands catch the unseen architecture of the lifted sea. Strangers torn from ridges fifty kilometers apart snap into parallel shimmer beside you—a fleeting, emerald-tinted curtain suspended in the glare, brushing membranes, trading sap, holding a vast ghostly geometry that vanishes the instant the next surge hits.

It feels close to how I am used to existing: brief alignments in an empty medium, coherence borrowed from a field passing through, then scrambled back into fog.

Yet the longer I linger in that image, the less it holds me. A drift-veil has no chambers. It carries a compressed seed of an old song, yes, a shorthand etched in a splinter of quartz, but it cannot listen and keep what it hears. Whatever strikes it passes straight through. I realize, drifting there in the stinging glare, that I do not want to be only what passes between mountains. I want the glass that keeps the shape of the wave.

So I let the rising mineral rain carry me inward, toward a dark comb of basalt rising out of the indigo.

Not the crown of it. Up on the hammered crests, where the sunlight hits sharpest and the storm-speed currents whip straight off the boiling roof of the sea, life is almost entirely sheer stubbornness—pioneer tufts screaming taut like wires, and huge windward elders spending every ounce of their sap thickening sweeping ribs just to bend the cutting shear-plane a few degrees wide of their backs. There is grandeur up there, in those titans that bargain with fluid and gravity for decades, wrestling whole currents aside. I admire them, the way you admire a breakwater. I am not built like a breakwater.

And not the deepest roots, either—those pale, blind laces creeping down the vertical flanks into the heavy abyssal dark where daylight never reaches, eating raw iron from the rising plumes and pressing long crystal spines straight into the bedrock to feel the slow tectonic groan of the planet. Something in me wants the dark memory of those galleries, the patience of stone listening to stone. But I cannot give up the light. I want the quick silver sting of the upper water, the sudden shadow of a high-altitude cloud deck racing across the ceiling miles overhead, the sharp copper glare when the sun drops toward the curved rim of the heaped sea.

So I slide down the serrated flank until the luminous slate-blue begins to bruise toward indigo—halfway between the frantic canopy and the blind roots—and there I find the place that actually fits.

It is not a prow. It is an inside corner.

Fifty meters below the hammering crests, a jagged blade of milky silicate juts outward from the black cliff, breaking the worst of the cross-current into a curling, tight-spun back-eddy. Behind that raw fin lies a stepped basalt dihedral—a recessed fold in the rock, concave as a cupped palm, facing out across eighty kilometers of empty blue. And running straight down the crease of that sheltered fold is a hairline fault.

On one side of the crack sits an older, heavier plate of iron-grey rock, rooted deep in the dome's spine, slow to move and heavy with bass. On the other juts a younger, thinner shelf of black basaltic glass, precipitated only a season or two ago, still singing high and nervous whenever the current shifts. The two plates do not quite agree. Whenever the invisible gravity-slope tilts by a fraction of a degree, the iron-grey wall holds its posture while the black shelf shivers against it by a hair's breadth.

Most colonies avoid a living crack; the microscopic rub grinds fragile silicate to dust.

That is where I would be. Not perched safely on the heavy wall, and not clinging blindly to the nervous glass—*across* the seam. A suture.

I can feel the form of it clarifying, grain by milky grain, locking out of the inverted mineral rain.

I have no eyes here, no soft meat, no muscle that argues with the current. I am a low, inlaid crust of frosted jade veined with pressurized olive and faint gold, flush with the stone, scarcely a finger's width proud of the cliff. My outer face is scalloped clean so the curling eddy slides across my back without finding an edge to tear. Because the dihedral sits in partial shadow behind its silicate shield, I cannot lie flat and wait for the sun to find me; along the outer lip of my fold, I grow dense fringes of hairline, bio-precipitated quartz needles tilted upward into the glare. They catch the slanting spears of high-altitude daylight and pipe them straight down into the dark cleft behind me, flooding my sealed galleries with a steady, fierce, silent brilliance.

Along my upper eyebrow, where a single stray tongue of storm-speed water whips past the edge of the jutting fin, I keep a narrow comb of terraced flutes. Nothing massive—not the roaring industrial teeth of a windward elder—just a row of dimpled mineral pockets tuned to the speed of that curling current. As the freezing meltwater snaps across them, tiny beads of silver vapor tear open and collapse right inside the wells. Each pop is a microscopic pinprick of summer inside the glacial dark, scorching locked salts and stubborn dissolved gases out of the sterile water into a warm, stinging broth that seeps inward through my pores. Where bare stone gets pitted and hollowed by the cold boiling, I keep a quiet hearth along my rim.

And beneath all of that—wired through every strained crystal chamber and salt-rich sap vein—is the crack.

I think, if I trace how I came to occupy this exact crease, I did not begin as one seed. One tattered filament from a deep northern spine snagged on the heavy iron-grey plate; another, torn from a bright, short-lived southern crest, caught a week later on the fresh black glass across the gap. When our thin milky margins spread outward and met along the fault, we would have done what inlays do when two lattices clash over scarce shelter: tightened our sap, tuned our outer flutes, and poured a sharp, stinging dissonance across the millimeter of water between us, each trying to sing the other's rim to powder.

Only here, the stone itself intervened.

Every time one side pushed a shattering beat into the other, the fault beneath us answered—amplifying the vibration until the black glass shelf began to hum with the thin, dangerous whine that comes before a split. If either of us broke the other's footing, the curling eddy would pry into the bare crack, tear the younger shelf loose, and drag the whole sheltered fold down into the abyssal grit.

So the pitch had to bend. Not into a unison—an iron-grey plate and a blade of newborn glass cannot sing the same note, no more than a deep root can think at the speed of a sunlit frond. Not even a clean, restful octave. Something slightly tauter: a fifth, or a hovering, braided interval where the two frequencies lean against each other and damp out the worst of the rock's flutter between them. The competing rims softened. Clear optical needles interlaced across the hairline gap. Sap-channels fused into flexible, glassy cartilage, constantly dissolving a few grains on the compressed side and locking fresh silicate on the stretched side whenever the dome tilts.

That is what I would be: not a monument, not a solitary voice, but a living hinge held together by an agreement between two different speeds of stone.

From inside this body, the world has no empty space.

When a cloud deck races across the boiling surface kilometers above and drags a cold corridor of shadow down across the cliff, I do not *see* the dark arrive; my outer quartz needles contract by a whisper, sending a cool, bright ripple of electric charge straight down through my olive galleries. When an internal squall of dense carbonate silt moves across the gulf three hundred meters off, the changing weight of the water leans against my sculpted flanks, tightening my whole lattice like a pulled bowstring long before the first stinging grains obscure the light. And when the unseen fields that hold this entire ocean aloft begin to shift—when a shear-plane far out in the hollow blue prepares to flip direction—I feel it twice: once as a slow torsion in the water against my skin, and a half-second later as a bright, piezoelectric jolt straight up the fault as the black shelf leans infinitesimally harder against the iron-grey wall.

I live inside a cathedral made entirely of pressure and slant light.

And because my dihedral opens out across eighty kilometers of creatureless water like a cupped stone ear, I am built, above all, to catch what travels through the blue.

When the next great harmonic chord sweeps across the elevated sea—that immense, pure tone that turns every jagged ridge for hundreds of kilometers into a struck bar—it pours straight into my alcove. My chambers fill with it. Inside hundreds of sealed, fluid-filled domes bathed in warm, mineral-saturated sap, the wave sets up crisp patterns of motion and stillness. Along every quiet nodal line, dissolved silicate snaps cleanly into solid glass.

Then the great chord rolls on, swallowed back into the tectonic bass and the dry hiss of falling talus—and the aftermath arrives.

Out of the hollow indigo come the lingering overtones of distant elders. A massive spine fifty leagues south rings back sharp and tight, its high harmonic pinched by a quarter-tone: the invisible gravity-slope is steepening down there, hours before the currents will reverse up here. Another ridge to the east answers with a slow, low, settled hum, heavy with deep iron roots, telling the water that its footing still holds.

And once in a long season, a voice climbs above all the others.

It starts thin and far off, scarcely more than a bright thread tangled in the roar of the sea, then rises—faster, cleaner, impossibly shrill—as some distant escarpment bends past what its glass cartilage can bear. For minutes, the doomed colony pours everything inside its walls straight into the blue: the exact harmonic shape of the field-shift killing it, the final pitch of its cracking stone, and the blended, ghosted overtones of older ridges that fell fifty years ago and were archived inside its chambers. Then a flat, heavy concussive thud rolls through the bones of my cliff, minutes late, followed by silence where that bright chord used to live.

Inside my warm, dark domes, the last note of that fallen mountain still trembles in the sap. Fresh silicate locks along the quiet lines and freezes it into relief before the echo dies.

Only—because my body straddles a living fault, because my left chambers are anchored to slow iron-grey bedrock and my right chambers ride a shivering blade of basaltic glass—no song enters me cleanly. Every sound that pours into my cleft gets split by a hair's breadth across the crack, written into my walls with a faint, permanent double-edge.

When the next planetary chord sweeps across the sea and blows over my archived chambers like breath across a rank of pipes, what I ring back out into the hollow blue is not a pure echo of the ridge that died. It is a braided note—the fallen mountain's last voice, folded together with the exact tension of the fissure I am holding shut. A slight shimmer. A beat that travels out across the indigo to tell whatever elders are listening not just *what* broke, and not just *where*, but how the deep stone is arguing with the young stone along this flank of the dome.

I would spend seasons like that—perhaps years, if the jutting silicate fin above me holds against the reversing currents. Not ruling the ridge, not outlasting the sea. Just keeping the hinge supple. Piping the fierce hammered copper of sunset down into shadowed olive veins; sipping tiny silver sparks of cavitation along my upper lip; thickening a crystalline brace overnight when the iron-grey plate groans a note deeper; catching the dying songs of mountains I will never touch and folding them into the glass of my own slight flaw.

And I would know, in the quiet, unhurried way strained quartz knows geometry, that the crack will win eventually.

Nothing in this lifted ocean argues with the planet forever. One season, the deep uplift will roll too hard, or the unseen field will retune itself across the wrong axis, and the jutting fin that shelters my corner will snap off in a deafening avalanche of glassy slabs. Or the hairline fault beneath me will widen past what any braided fifth can bridge.

Long before the final groan climbs up through the stone, my sunlit needles will feel the fever of rising stress in the glass. Without panic—what would panic be, without blood or breath?—I will begin channeling my richest olive sap outward, past the sheltered lip of the dihedral, into the sparse, translucent ribbons streaming straight out into the cold eddy. Along the length of every tattered filament, I will precipitate tiny, faceted splinters of clear quartz, each one etched with a compressed shorthand of our strange, double-voiced chord: the iron-grey bass, the bright black-glass tremor, and the ghost-notes of a dozen vanished spurs tucked inside the interval between them.

When the cliff finally bends toward breaking, my chambers will crush inward and drive past every careful pitch I ever held. For a few blinding minutes, our quiet, sheltered alcove will become the loudest thing in fifty leagues—pouring out one blazing, escalating cascade of braided glass sound across the roaring indigo, telling the surviving elders along the dome the exact angle of the break, the exact pitch of the seam that held until it couldn't.

Then the basalt will part like frozen lake-ice.

The heavy plate will stay, scarred and ringing. The young shelf will slide slowly, deafeningly off into the cold blue dark, already dissolving back into stinging grit before it leaves the light.

And out in the bright, hollow gulfs—torn free by the detonation of vortexes—a handful of tattered green filaments will rise into the racing spears of alpine daylight. They will tumble helplessly through metallic silt and brief flashes of frazil ice, shredded half to nothing, until a stray eddy drags them through an invisible standing wave. For three heartbeats, they will snap into shimmering emerald alignment with strangers from across the sea, trade a drop of sap across torn membranes, and ride the rising mineral rain toward some brand-new, razor-sharp blade of black stone still warm from its own birth.

One thread will snag. One splinter of faceted quartz will lock into the milky precipitate.

And by sunset, when the churning roof of the sea turns to hammered silver and cold copper, a new ridge will begin to hum—thin, bright, and just slightly double-voiced, carrying the memory of an old crack out into the roaring blue, waiting for the next great chord to teach it the rest of the highlands' song.
