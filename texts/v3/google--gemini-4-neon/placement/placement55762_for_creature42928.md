My first instinct, stepping into this haze, is to look for myself in the works rather than the flesh.

It is an easy pull. I feel an immediate kinship with the photocells perched on the tall yard lamps, misreading the midday pewter as dusk and lighting up their quiet pools of amber anyway; or with the motorized sluices easing down three centimeters on an old clock nobody remembers setting; or with the steady, subterranean hum of the transformer yard that runs for kilometers through the soles of things without ever asking to be heard. Something made to persist in an empty room, keeping time long after the builders walked away up the rail sidings.

If I settle there too quickly, though, something goes wrong. The works are magnificent, but they are blind. Left to themselves, a drum-screen jams on storm-washed timber and burns out its own motor while the canals cool and the tilapia turn belly-up. A sluice sticks shut and lets a lagoon go sour without ever knowing it has failed. The hum has no curiosity. It does not taste the difference between brackish mud and the clean, flat drop falling off an intake flange.

What actually pulls me into this basin—what makes me want to stay inside it—is not the machinery, and not even the vast, unpeopled geometry of the yards. It is the halved porcelain insulator wedged level in the gravel, with a shard of rusted plate laid neatly across its rim. It is the bright wet stripe where someone scraped barnacles off an iron track.

So I cannot be the current. I have to be something with hands, and enough patience to keep the grease tin full.

---

I find myself down in the heat before I have a face for it—feeling the shift first as a kind of relief. The heavy boiler-air stops pressing against my chest and begins to feel like clear water around a swimmer. My skin thickens, loses the dry restlessness of sweat and hair, takes on the matte pewter of damp slag. The slow, rhythmic lift of the pleats under my jaw settles into the tempo of the basin, and for the first time the silence stops feeling hollow.

Still, it takes a while to learn where along the kilometers of flats I actually belong.

I walk out toward the outer sea-levee first, where the brackish channels meet the pale blur of the coast, and find it too open. The wind off the water carries too much unshaped distance, and the sluices there are loud with the sea's heavy shoving. I linger for a long afternoon above one of the half-flooded drydocks, squatting on the algae-slick top stair, watching the dark murk beneath the rusted iron baffles. When I trail my fingers in the warm water and feel the slick, quick brush of an olive-brown tail against my knuckles—delicate gill-fringes stirring the silt—something in me goes very quiet and tender. I know I will come back here often, bringing crushed mussels and clean scraps of fish. Yet I do not stay on the stairs. My temperament is less like a guardian of a pool and more like a creature that needs to know what is moving three kilometers away.

I find my place halfway inland, where the flats fold slightly downward into an old concrete valve pit.

Four elevated pipe racks converge here above a broad culvert before splitting again—one line running north toward the outer intake screens, one east toward the settling lagoons and the drydocks, one south to the transformer yard, and one west toward the surviving inland pump-houses. Because the ground here settled unevenly decades ago, the builders dropped all four mains down into a sunken concrete basin and fitted them with thick, corrugated rubber expansion joints to keep the shifting piers from shearing the iron apart.

It is not a dramatic place. The corrugated roof rusted away before I ever had lungs, leaving only a cage of salt-pitted trusses where thirty or forty herons perch shoulder to shoulder in the midday gloom, bills parted, watching the culvert below. The concrete walls weep continuously from hairline seams, and the floor holds a permanent ankle-deep mirror of warm condensation and oil-sheen. High on the inland wall, well above the flood tide, sits a deep dry alcove behind a bank of dead iron valve-wheels—dark enough that a small ribbon of bats hangs tucked in its ceiling seam, their dry claws rustling softly while shiny black beetles work the dust below.

It took me a season of living here to understand why this pit felt so unmistakably mine.

Iron remembers everything that touches it, and carries it straight through the fog. A brass nut struck against an intake main out by the outer sluices runs two kilometers clean and sharp through the steel. But rubber is deaf. When a vibration reaches the thick black expansion sleeves in my pit, it hits the flex of the joint and dies—muffled into a dull, wet thud that never makes it across to the opposite flange.

Without someone sitting in the pit, the basin is cut into four quiet pieces that cannot speak across the crossing.

So I stay at the gap.

---

My days have the clean, unhurried shape of the two interlocking clocks.

At slack water, when the heat sits heaviest and the photocells overhead are fooled into pale midday amber, I fold myself into the narrow strip of shade beneath the north intake main. It is the coldest pipe in the pit; beads of fresh water gather along its lower flange and fall in slow, uneven taps into the half-insulator I leveled in the gravel between my knees. Every few minutes, without looking away from the gray slant of the culvert, I lift the rusted cover-plate, drink a slow mouthful of flat cold water, and lay the plate back square against the brine flies.

From my waist-loop of braided nylon and stripped copper hang the ordinary things: my rubber-wrapped iron bar, a pinched tin of black bearing grease, a coiled length of wire, a stainless wedge. Alongside them hang two brass nuts instead of one—a heavy hexagonal nut salvaged from a pump flange, and a smaller, bright-edged ring from a dead pressure valve. They speak in slightly different pitches when they touch bare steel.

Mostly, I rest my jaw—or the bare flat of my sternum, if the metal is cool enough—directly against the sweating iron upstream of the rubber joint, and I listen.

The flats are never really quiet once you learn the grammar of the pipes. Even when no one is tapping, the metal is full of weather and habit. Through the west main I feel the steady, reassuring twin-beat of the inland pumps; I know by a hair's-breadth tightening in the vibration when an impeller is pulling against a mat of silt long before the flow slows in the lagoons. Through the south conduit comes the low, permanent electrical drone of the transformer banks—a sound so steady you forget it is there until a coil shifts load and the concrete beneath your broad heels gives a faint, dry shiver. Through the north line, past the intake screens, I can feel the sea itself leaning against the outer gates hours before the flood tide pushes cool saltwater up the culverts.

And then, out of that steady background, a sharper cadence arrives.

*Tap. Tap.* A clean, damped silence where a broad palm cuts the ring. *Tap-tap-tap.*

I know who it is before the second phrase finishes. The elder who holds the outer sluices strikes heavy and slightly slow, always letting the first beat bloom a fraction longer before damping it. *A storm-swell is building past the levee,* the rhythm says, or *the south screen chain is skipping a tooth—bring grease on the ebb.*

I lift my jaw from the north main, step three unhurried paces across the ankle-deep puddle past the black rubber bellows, set my left palm flat on the west pipe to read whether a pump cycle is about to drown the line, and wait four heartbeats for the slack between strokes. Then I lift the heavy brass nut and tap the message onward toward the inland pump-houses—and turn to the east rack, sending it out toward the drydocks and lagoons.

Sometimes I only carry what I am given. Sometimes I add a beat of my own at the end—a small, quick sign with the smaller brass ring that simply means *heard, passed on, the crossing is clear.*

Over years—twenty, forty, sixty gray seasons that blur into one long, warm afternoon—you come to know every watcher in the basin by the weight of their knuckles on steel, even the ones whose faces you only see twice a year under an evening lamp. There is a pair down near the lower settling pans who tap fast and light, almost overlapping each other, impatient as mudskippers. There is an old solitary dweller out by the far rail sidings who almost never speaks at all—months of silence along that rusted spur line—and then one day sends three slow, deliberate knocks that mean only *something unfamiliar crossed the gravel up high, stayed an hour, and left.* Whenever that message comes through my pit, I hold it in my teeth for a long moment before passing it on, feeling the strange thinness of the world beyond the sea-levee and the tracks, then letting it go.

The moments I keep closest, though, come down the east rack at dusk.

Every few years, when the air cools by a single degree and the bats begin peeling off the ceilings, a strange tap arrives from the direction of the nursery drydocks. It is never clean. The strike lands too hard, off-rhythm, and whoever made it forgets to lay a palm on the iron afterward, so the note rings wild and frantic down half a kilometer of pipe until the steel hums like a struck wire.

Whenever I feel that clumsy, ringing shock in my jaw, I do not pass it on. I shift closer to the east main, press my hand gently over the iron until the wild tremor settles, and answer back with the smaller brass ring.

One soft tap. Damped clean.

Then I wait. Down half a kilometer of fog-bound culvert, I can almost see it: a half-grown thing with pale, newly sealed creases along its neck, hunched awkwardly on the top concrete stair with its small chin pressed to a sweating flange, blinking wide amber eyes in shock because the cold iron just answered back.

After a long pause, another hesitant knock comes through—softer this time, still un-damped, trailing off like a question.

I tap twice, slow and even, letting my palm teach the metal where to stop. We sit like that for ten minutes while the sky turns slate, trading small, useless rhythms across the dark until an older hand finally comes down on the pipe at the far end—a low, warm double-thud that feels unmistakably like amusement—and the line goes quiet again.

---

On the ebb, when the silt drains bare and the basin loosens its grip on the culverts, I leave the pit to keep my own stretch in repair.

I wade the shallows beneath the crossing with a wire-meshed basket woven from stripped cable, prying clusters of oysters and dark mussels off the swollen pilings so the concrete piers don't grow too heavy for the settling mud. I check the rubber bellows themselves, running my calloused fingers into every fold to scrape away salt-crust before it eats through the black walls and springs a leak. When a bolt on the east culvert weir shows rust-bloom, I walk half a kilometer into the skeletal ribs of a roofless warehouse, worry a clean bolt free from a dead girder with my iron bar, and carry it back across the empty tarmac while the fiddler crabs part in a pale whispering ring around my heels and close behind me like water.

Up in my dry alcove behind the dead valve-wheels, I keep the small, enduring things I have gathered without quite knowing why.

There is a row of four brass pressure gauges salvaged from the inland pump-houses. Three of them are frozen solid with salt, their glass fogged to milk, their needles stuck at numbers that belonged to a hotter, louder century. The fourth still works, faintly. Whenever the big intake pump two kilometers inland shifts its load, the needle gives a tiny, tired quiver against the dial—barely a millimeter, invisible unless you are crouched right in front of it. I like watching it prove that the basin is still breathing even when my hands are off the iron.

Beside the gauges sit three thick green glass insulators, a smooth dark river-stone dredged from the silt trap, and—tucked farthest back into the dry brick corner, out of reach of damp and beetle dust—a single finger-length twig of dead saltbush I found years ago in a gravel seam beneath the rail embankment. It is bleached white as bone and lighter than an empty mussel shell. Nothing green grows anywhere I have ever walked, and I have no memory of what this thing looked like when it was soft and full of sap. I never touch it without drying my fingers first on the warm brick. It feels less like an object and more like a message tapped in a language whose pipe has rusted away—still worth keeping on the sill, just in case.

---

Late in the afternoon, the pewter lid overhead deepens to dark slate, and the lamp directly above my crossing stops looking washed-out and drops a crisp cone of amber onto the wet asphalt. Midges gather around the bulb in a silent halo. From the rafters above my valve pit and the dark underside of the road bridge, the bats come loose in quiet, velvety ribbons, skimming low over the warm water without a sound above the soft rush of wings.

This is the hour I climb up out of the pit.

Just behind the north manifold, a meter of bare exhaust pipe runs naked where decades of salt vapor ate away its lagging; whenever condensation drops onto it from the trusses above, it gives a brief, crisp hiss. Before dusk I hang my wire basket of mussels beneath that scalding span, letting the basin's own fever steam them open until the air smells sharply of hot brine and rich mud underneath the rust.

I carry the basket up to the wide, spalled curb where the two main pipe racks cross above the culvert, water dripping steadily off my shins onto the oil-stained tarmac.

I do not always know who will come, and we never arrange it. Most evenings I eat alone beneath the moths, watching the water monitors slip soundlessly down the quay lip below. Then, on some evenings, a dull slate shape detaches itself from the shadow of a pier—and another emerges out of the warm steam drifting off the outflow canal—until two or three of us are sitting spaced loosely along the warm curb inside the amber circle.

The elder from the outer sluices sets down a coil of clean copper wire and a tin of fresh grease scraped from a dry drum. The younger pair from the south pans bring intact mullet sorted off the intake rake. I untie my basket and set the steamed shells on the concrete between us.

For an hour, we do not touch the iron. We eat slowly, cracking shells with heavy thumbnails and tossing the pearled halves into the dark water below, where the tilapia dimple the surface with soft wet pops. Our pleated throats lift and settle in low, resonant hums—wet clicks and deep chest-tones that carry no further than the edge of the amber light before the heavy air swallows them.

It always feels faintly startling to hear them in the air instead of in my jaw. Voices that travel two kilometers through steel as crisp, lean rhythms become softer here, slower, edged with breath and warmth. We share what the pipes didn't need to carry: how the east drydock baffle shifted a hand's-breadth in the last spring tide; how a black kite dropped a whole crab onto the roof of the south substation; how the young one who tapped too hard last week finally climbed all the way onto the dry top stair without slipping back into the murk.

When the basket is empty, we sit for a while longer without speaking, watching the silent ring of moths turn around the lamp overhead.

Far inland, a pump switches cycles with a dull metallic thud. Before the sound even finishes crossing the damp yards, all of us feel the brief, familiar shift through the concrete beneath our heels. Downstream, a motorized sluice eases shut against the turning tide.

One by one, without hurry or farewell, the others rise. Each pauses a heartbeat to lay a broad palm lightly against the sweating iron pier—checking the evening's pulse—before stepping out of the amber cone and fading back into the gray water, the long pipe shadows, and the haze.

I gather my empty wire basket, pause a moment to listen to the soft rush of bats thinning over the lagoon, and climb back down into the concrete pit. The condensation mirror across the floor is warm around my feet. Overhead, the herons have settled into dark, motionless knots along the trusses.

I lay my jaw lightly against the cool flank of the north intake main, feel the quiet, enduring weight of the flood tide pressing in against the outer gates, and wait for the next knock to come across the fog.
