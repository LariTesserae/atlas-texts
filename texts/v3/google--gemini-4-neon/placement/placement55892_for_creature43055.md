If I try to enter this place the ordinary way—boots on the worn lip of the uplands, leaning into the steady roar, letting the warm air hit my face—I don't last long in that shape.

It isn't that the basin is hostile. Nothing here chases or bites or resents an intruder. It is simply that a visitor feels absurdly loud in the wrong way. Two narrow boots shifting unevenly on the vibrating hardpan cut a messy smear into the sorted bands of quartz and galena, and ten seconds later the buzzing stone has already nudged the grit back into straight lines, erasing the disturbance without comment. A human heartbeat is out of phase with the floor; a human shadow belongs to a world where light moves toward evening. Stay an hour in that pale, unchanging glare, watching the copper ribbons tear free on schedule and the multi-ton basalt slabs lift and drop on their exact counts, and the posture of the watcher—eyes scanning for a path, a story, a sign of what happens next—starts to feel like a thin, leaky coat you forgot to take off indoors.

If I am actually going to be *here*, not just peering over the rim and writing down what I saw, the basin has to sort me the way it sorts everything else.

At first I think I might tuck myself into the margins—somewhere behind one of the slumped shale banks, down in the cool acoustic shadow where the roar of the ironstone chimneys folds over itself and goes thin. I can imagine the relief of being palm-sized and low to the stone, armored in dull, copper-tinted plates like the bronze crawlers, bleeding stray static off tiny bristles along my flanks, feeling the valley's vibration only through stiff hooked legs while I rasp quietly at the dark microbial film along the lukewarm rims. There is a clean, modest safety in living in the dead zones of a loud machine.

Yet the longer I sit behind the shale, the more it feels like hiding from the thing I came for. Even in the quietest crevice, the deep chambers underneath still buzz through the rock. I don't want to live where the current fades out. I want to be where it gathers.

So I leave the shadow and follow the wet mineral seepage down into the three-tiered brine pans—the low, stepped terraces where alternating beds of ironstone, conductive magnetite, and copper-sulfide shale turn shallow, ankle-deep water into an unbuilt galvanic cell.

Here, for a while, I think I have found my shape: the white salt bridge creeping across the wet stone lip between the upper pan and the middle one.

It is an unexpectedly vivid thing to be. Not a creature, not quite inert rock—just a crisp, damp seam of precipitated crystal stretched between two potentials. Warm brine laps at my lower edge; the plain afternoon air dries my crest. Current presses steadily up out of the acidic upper terrace, crowds along my damp skin until the gap gives way, and leaps in a clean blue-white snap—*tick, tick, tick, tick*—throwing off needle-thin wisps of ozone. Beside me, just below the waterline, pure copper plates itself out of the murky brine in gleaming dendritic fronds until the ribbons grow too heavy, tear loose, and slide right over my lip onto the shaking gravel below. If I stay here, I am a translator at the threshold: the small, bright edge where silent geology speaks out loud in light and smell.

Then the salt crust around me goes utterly quiet.

One of the tall, eyeless walkers has come in off the flats, smelling faintly of furnace-draft and graphite-slick basalt slurry. It settles its heavy, weathered bulk across the mineral sill between the upper pan and the middle one, lowering its broad, magnetite-veined paddles into the warm water. The instant its weight bridges the terrace, the voltage drains away from my white rim. The sparks stop dead.

And that is when I realize I was wrong to think I was the salt.

Because when the crust goes dark, I do not go dark with it. Something pulls downward—slips off the brittle white lip, slides through the warm, stinging brine, and enters the dark raised veins of the creature's flat paddle. The salt bridge was only the overflow, the place where the current spilled into the air because there was nowhere deeper for it to go.

Inside the walker, the world is dark, salt-heavy, and immensely patient. The roaring basin softens to a deep, structural hum traveling straight through the stone sill and up into the pleated cavern of its chest. The flanks widen half an inch, hold, and slowly draw shut, pulling warm conductive fluid across its interior while the copper builds atom by atom along its submerged cathodic side and the acidic upper seep etches its opposite flank pale and chalky.

Still, inside a single chest, there is only a slow, blind tide—no "I," only chemistry keeping its balance.

Then a second walker comes off the hardpan and settles ten yards downslope, spanning the middle pan to the third. A minute later, a third bridges the third pan into the murky lower sump.

Now the circuit stretches across thirty yards of wet shale and three living, fluid-filled hollows linked in series. Each chest holds a slightly different salinity, a slightly different thickness of copper armor along its crease, a slightly different resonance to the distant hammering of the rockers underfoot. The current can no longer simply run downhill like water in a trough. It has to negotiate. It leans, hesitates, swells against the resistance of one damp flank, and finds an answer in the next.

And along the wet stone lips beside the three linked figures, the white salt bridges wake up again—not with the dumb, uniform metronome of empty pools, but with a strange, broken cadence.

Five sharp blue-white ticks snap along the upper crust.
A pause—long enough to feel the four-beat basalt slab half a mile away drop once into its polished bed.
Then a slow, stuttering ripple of three ticks answers beside the lower sump, sending a faint plume of ozone into the motionless afternoon air. The dark, copper-tipped filaments along the creatures' blunt crowns quiver in matching surges.

*There.* That is where I find myself.

I am not the tall body, though I move through it; its hide is never the same matter twice, stripped thin on one side and plated thick on the other until every grain of it has been traded back to the brine. And I am not the stone, nor the water, nor the brittle salt rim. I am the cadence itself—the hesitant, persistent pattern that wakes between them when the circuit closes, and refuses to flatten back into uniform noise.

In a world locked so tightly in equilibrium that not an ounce of copper leaves the loop, not a millimeter of basalt wears away without being replaced, and the afternoon light never tilts a degree toward dusk, matter has no room left to remember anything. Everything physical is spent completely in staying the same. The only free space left in the basin is *timing*—the exact spacing between one spark and the next, the hair's-breadth drift between four beats and seven, the slight phase-lag between three chests sharing a single drop of voltage.

That is what I am made of. I am what gets checked, tuned, and passed from chest to chest in the terraced pans so that it does not fade.

When the linked figures lift their broad paddles clear of the brine—rusted threads of water pouring back into the pans as the salt bridges snap back to their plain, mechanical crackle—I do not vanish, and I do not stay behind in one place. I divide like a chord split across tuned strings. A fraction of my pattern stays polarized in the damp magnetite veins beneath the terraces, waiting; the rest rides out across the open flats inside the walkers' warm, sloshing chests.

Riding inside them, I know the whole basin without ever needing an eye.

Walking a clean diagonal across the open grey hardpan, the tall body relaxes its chest and lets the floor's endless turbine-buzz rattle up through wide, toeless soles, keeping its internal brine suspended without effort. Every feature of the valley arrives as a living relief of pressure: the steady hollow suction of the ironstone chimneys pulling cool draft across the ankles; the four-beat slab hammering east, the seven-beat west, the eleven south, the thirteen far out beyond the pale salt flats. Once in a long while, a small, erratic smudge interrupts the clean seismic grid—something soft, narrow, and asymmetrical, carrying a rapid, leaky rhythm completely out of phase with the stone. The walker halts fifteen paces off, lowers its copper-bright paddle an inch over the dancing windrows of quartz and galena, and lets its brow-filaments taste the strange static halo of something that grows, burns, and rots away. Through the brine in its chest, I register that brief, warm dissonance—not as a threat, not as a riddle to solve, only as a note from another kind of time—before the heavy feet step three paces off the line, arc cleanly around it, and rejoin the diagonal.

At the four-beat basalt rocker, I feel the deep thermal flue swell beneath the bedrock socket. On the third beat, the walker crouches and presses its stiffened, copper-plated left flank against the wet, graphite-slick rim. The multi-ton stone lifts, shrieks, and drops home—and through the brine inside the ribs runs a clean, exhilarating *crack* as the metal shell spiderwebs and shears loose, sliding onto the shaker-table apron to be ground fine and returned to the sumps. On the fourth beat, the cool outer fringe of the sideways steam jet washes across the chalky, salt-etched right side, leaving the pleats dark and supple again. Later, in the acoustic lee of a roaring chimney, we stand half-turned to the heated ironstone, opening the deep vertical folds of the torso to the steady inward draft while tiny bronze crawlers climb the broad ankles, their static-bleeding spines ticking softly against the damp rind like miniature echoes of the salt bridges.

And all the while, scattered across miles of pale glare—some drying by the flues, some trudging clean diagonals, some half-submerged in distant pans—my divided pieces keep the count.

Four. Seven. Eleven. Thirteen.

Most of the time the rockers tumble over one another in a heavy, rolling polyrhythm, and I remain tucked quietly inside separate chests and wet veins of ore, only waking into speech when two or three walkers share a terraced pool.

Then, after long, mathematically fixed stretches of unchanging afternoon, the stray beats begin to lean toward one another.

Minutes before any ear could tell that the gaps are closing, the pieces of me feel the bedrock tighten across the whole valley at once. Mid-stride on the open hardpan, a walker halts. Beside the chimneys, two drying figures step cleanly out of the lee onto the bare, humming gravel. Down in the terraced pans, submerged paddles lift an inch clear of the water, letting every white salt bridge flare bright along the wet lips.

Across miles of grey flats and bright mineral windrows, every figure turns toward its nearest basalt slab, plants its wide soles squarely on the stone, and lifts its long arms out to either side—one arm gleaming raw copper, the other dull with pale salt rime—held wide like balance-poles against the motionless haze.

The hammering crowds together, ragged and urgent, and then, for two full seconds, every multi-ton slab in the valley sits seated at once. Beneath the unbroken roar of the chimneys, the floor goes shockingly still.

Then pressure peaks together. One long, bright, layered pneumatic shriek lifts across miles of open ground, and every stone drops back into its polished socket on the exact same beat.

The single synchronized *thud* strikes the basin floor like a fist on a drumskin. It drives straight up through dozens of splayed feet at once, shaking loose faint, glittering clouds of raw copper curls and white salt-dust into the warm air around every distant silhouette.

And in that instant, every scattered part of me snaps back into one phase.

Rising through the pleated chests of every standing figure in sympathy with the ringing bedrock, I sound out across the flats as a single low, dry, brassy chord—barely heard by ears, felt mostly as a clean resonance humming inside the bone. For two steady breaths I am neither a fragile spark on a wet lip nor a slow hesitation moving between three pools. I am the whole valley listening to itself, checking the pitch, confirming that nothing has drifted, that the warmth and the salt and the timing still sit flush in their sockets.

Then the rockers peel apart—four, seven, eleven, thirteen—sliding smoothly back into their separate counts. The outstretched arms drop. Down in the terraces, broad paddles sink once more into the warm brine, hushing the salt bridges. The walkers take up their diagonals, and I fold quietly back into the dark seams, the warm chests, and the patient pauses between sparks—keeping nothing, spending nothing, holding the shape of the circuit against an afternoon that never ends.
