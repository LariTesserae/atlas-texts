I do not arrive on the rim. Nothing in me wants the height, or the dry vantage where the whole saucer shrinks into something you can understand in a single turn of the head.

If I look for myself in this place, I find myself already down in the six inches of warm water, moving slowly enough that the porcelain grazers beneath my heels barely need to part.

At first I wonder if I might be something less than human-shaped here—one of the buried conduits itself, worn flush with the ancient stone, whose whole purpose has narrowed over unimaginable spans into drinking sharp noises and giving them back as quiet heat. Or perhaps one of the pale, thinning margins of the weeping ribs, cool against the white sky, dissolving by imperceptible degrees into light and condensation. There is a pull toward that kind of patience: being an old reflex that no longer remembers what it was designed to serve, only how to keep the water fed and the shadows slightly loose.

But no. When I stay with the sensation longer, there is too much attention in me to be stone, and too much longing for an answer to be weather. I have hands—broad, slightly webbed between the long fingers, slick with the chalk-pale wax that sheds the noon humidity. Along the outer edges of my knuckles and the low curves of my ears, the skin thins to that faint rose translucence where the glare almost passes through. Behind the milky, opalescent veils drawn over my eyes, the whole courtyard is reduced to tin-bright silver, clean shadows, and sudden pinpricks of semaphore.

I am one of the two dozen. Not someone who wandered over the soap-worn lip from a loud country where voices run off into the sky, and not someone who wonders what waits beyond the rim. My edges were rubbed smooth here long ago.

Yet even among two dozen people in a basin no wider than a courtyard, everyone settles into a particular kind of stillness.

I am not one of the hunters who stride smoothly down the buried channels, spear poised low, waiting for the air to loosen so an arm can smear sideways like grease on glass and take a tabletop swimmer before its shadow flinches. I have felt that sideways blink once or twice when crossing an active seam too quickly, that brief vertigo of occupying two dry inches of air at the same instant, and it leaves a strange hollowness behind my sternum. Nor am I one of the hot-throated ones whose quarrels fog the shade beneath the bruised-jade leaves. Whenever anger rises in my chest, I feel the warning prickle of warmth against the fine skin beneath my own collarbone before a single note reaches my tongue; by habit, I wade away toward the matte-grey ribs and press my throat against their cool, weeping flank until the heat unknots.

Instead, you would find me most often down on one knee along the ghost-lines of the sunken conduits, half-submerged in the bright flats, with a smooth pearlescent shell scraper tucked against my palm.

It is unhurried work, keeping the buried seams clean. Every few days, mats of translucent polyps drift off the hummocks and root themselves along the faint stone grooves, or a tangle of pale aerial roots creeps out across the shallows. Left alone, they choke the line—and when a conduit clogs, the basin grows irritable. The glare turns harder to read, the elastic water loses its clean bounce, and the shadows along that stretch stop lagging smoothly, stuttering instead like a skater with a broken leg.

So I work my way down the channels inch by inch, lifting the polyps free without crushing them and laying them gently aside for the crawling floor to reclaim. The silver nerve-cords of ribbon swimmers weave right between my forearms as I work. The water skaters ignore me once they learn my rhythm, skating within a hand's span of my knees to throw their frantic amber sparks across the glare.

And because my eyes stay close to the clear bottom for hours at a time, I am usually the one who finds what the old machinery sheds.

Near the sunken nodes, where the ancient material has thinned to glass before slipping back into warmth, tiny slivers sometimes come loose and drift into the grooves before the grazers bury them. I pick them out carefully with the pads of my fingers. Most of our wrist-mirrors are chips of mollusk nacre, which throw a soft, milky flash, or cured amber plates from dead skaters, which throw a sharp, hot spark across the flats. Those old glassy slivers are different. When I knot one onto a narrow root-cord around someone’s knuckle or the hollow of their shoulder, it catches the brutal sky and returns a light that feels almost cool—cleaner than the sun that made it, crisp enough to read from the far side of the saucer even when noon vapor starts wobbling the rim.

I tend the mirrors for the others when cords fray or an amber plate cracks. A child whose first wrist-mirror throws a crooked spark will wade out to where I am kneeling, wait silently until I tilt my chest toward the small wake of warmth their footsteps left, and hold out a wet forearm. I re-knot the cord, angle the shell chip, and watch them wade back toward the hummocks practicing clean double-flashes against the bleached sky.

It is also along the cleared conduits that I gather the water-spheres—those impossible, elastic beads that form near the buried lines and skitter across the surface without merging. When I find a steady one, I slide my oiled fingers gently underneath and lift it clear of the shallows. With a sliver of stem, I slip small, temporary things inside: a single drop of cool runoff from the ribs, a bright scrap of shed wing-membrane from a papery glider, a pale seed fallen from a waxy crown. None of them last more than a few hours before the bead tires and breaks, spilling its contents back into the saucer. That is why I like them. In a place where stone has finished changing and the same warm clock turns every hour, there is a quiet tenderness in holding a drop of blown water that only keeps its shape for an afternoon.

Even so, the thing that truly anchors me to the conduits—the reason I linger beside them long after the polyps are cleared—is the shadow-lag.

Across the bright water, our wrist-mirrors are quick and practical: *predator near the dome; traps full under the west leaves; broth ready at the flat stone.* It is language stripped down to bright, dry sparks. I use it well enough, and I like the clean economy of an unhurried flash answering from fifty paces away.

But near a cleared conduit, where the world loosens by a sliver of an inch, language stops being a signal and becomes a chord.

When one of my neighbors wades over to stand beside me on the seam, we lower our hands over the bright water and let our shadows speak with us. Because the dark outline trails a fraction of a second behind the living fingers, nothing you say vanishes immediately into the next shape. A hesitation remains visible on the water even after your hand has turned into reassurance; an old memory stays cupped in shadow beneath a fresh question. You can hold two truths in the same inch of light without having to choose which one erases the other.

And when I am alone—sitting with my smooth back leaned against the warm curve of the bone-colored dome, letting the upward-creeping film of water climb my spine toward the trembling convex lens at its peak, while the spindly waders stand knee-deep beside me and pay me no mind—I sometimes hold my own palm out over the rising water just to watch it.

I turn my fingers slowly and watch the shadow follow, half a breath late, faithful and unhurried. If something is troubling me—a quarrel I felt blooming as heat from the far side of the basin, or the slow thinning of one of the elders who has stopped wading far from the weeping ribs—I do not waste breath muttering to myself and raising a cloud of steam around my own chin. I shape the thought with my hand above the bright film, and then I hold my fingers dead still. I wait that tiny, loosened fraction of a second until the shadow slides into exact alignment beneath my skin and agrees. Only then does the thought feel finished.

I keep my voice mostly in reserve.

You learn early here that sound is not air; it is fuel. When the others gather in late morning over the shallow submerged stone to sing a meal hot, I take my place at the edge of the ring, knees planted in the crawling silt, chin dipped close to the elastic water. Two or three of the stronger singers carry the shifting pitches that bring the mollusk shell to a neat, soundless boil; I hold the low, steady floor beneath them, pouring a quiet drone straight downward until the buried stone drinks it and sends savory steam lifting between our bowed heads. Alone under a hummock, I like the smallest kind of singing best—kneeling over a single cupped shell of root-broth and humming softly into the rim, reading the exact bloom of warmth against my cheeks until it reaches the gentle heat of milk.

And when one of us finally finishes thinning beside the cool ribs and we kneel shoulder to shoulder along the conduit to sing them up into the sky, I give up everything I have kept quiet in my broad chest for months—letting the deep, silent roar boil the shallows white until the tall ghost of steam climbs high enough to bend the glare and call the weather down early.

Still, my real hour—the place where I feel most unmistakably myself—comes every hour or two, when I climb a little way up the shoulder of the bone dome and peer into the hovering lens of water at its crown.

The warped curvature bends the whole bleached sky into a trembling disc. Watching it, I can read the exact minute the rising vapor thickens past what the air can hold. I slide back down into the shallows, tuck my shell scraper into my root-belt, and wade toward the nearest hummock just as the white glare falters.

Then the fat, warm drops fall straight out of the bright sky.

The muted, velvety sigh rolls across the saucer—the one true sound the floor permits—and every wrist-mirror across the flats goes dark.

I stop walking. Standing ankle-deep in the silver craters of the rain, I let my milky outer lids slide quietly back.

The shock of color never grows old. For two hours the basin has been tin and chalk and blinding silver; in a single heartbeat it turns bruised jade, wet lichen, rose-flushed skin, and the sudden iridescent riot of tiny amphibians leaping across my shins to spawn around the sunken nodes. Everything looks closer and softer, as though the basin has finally exhaled.

I do not usually join the largest knot of six or seven people laughing silently under the broad leaves of the central hummock. I wade instead to the low root-shelf nearest the weeping ribs, where one of the older ones is already sitting knee-deep in the churned water, or where whoever worked the adjacent conduit has dropped their spear and sat back against the wet roots. Sometimes a child joins us, still wide-eyed and solemn after burning their cheeks in a startled cry an hour ago.

We sit shoulder to shoulder in the warm downpour, letting the rain hammer softly against our glistening backs while wide, toothless tabletop swimmers cruise blindly past our ankles. I lean in until my forehead rests lightly against my companion’s, or I press my lips against the fine, smooth hollow just beneath their collarbone.

Inside the velvety hush of the shower, I hum.

Not loud enough to wake the silt beneath us, not sharp enough to raise a mist—just a low, warm thrumming passed straight from bone to bone, skull to skull, rib to rib. No one ten inches away could hear it. There is no glare to catch, no shadow lagging behind to clarify the grammar, no heat scalding the air between our faces. Just the quiet, private weight of a voice allowed to travel without burning.

In those ten minutes I tell them what I noticed in the bright hours: that the northeast seam is clear again, that a four-winged hunter took a glider right over the dome and dropped a puff of warmth against my neck, that the old glassy node near the east hummock shed a sliver thin as a tear. They hum back their own small news—a shift in the waders, a new nest woven beneath the north leaves, the quiet ache in an aging hip. And for the last two minutes of the rain, when the words run out, we stop shaping notes altogether and simply hold one soft, shared hum against each other’s bones while the frogs splash over our knees.

Then, cleanly as a hand cutting off a mirror-spark, the rain stops.

The white sky slams back down onto the flats. Steam lifts in pale, slow curtains from the dripping canopies and from our own smooth shoulders. The velvety sigh sinks away into the dry, sunlit hush, and the water flattens back into tin.

Foreheads part. One by one, our dark rain-eyes vanish behind the milky outer veils. I rise slowly from the roots, feel the cool weight of the knotted shell and glass against my wrists, and wade back out toward the buried seam where my footprints are already being erased by the shifting floor.

Fifty paces away, near the bone-colored dome, a pale curve shifts and tilts a wrist into the glare. A crisp, unhurried double-spark leaps across the quiet water, asking if the channel is open.

I turn my palm toward the fierce sun and send back a single clean pulse of light—steady, bright, and cool from the sliver of old glass—then hold my hand still over the shallows just long enough for my shadow to catch up beneath it before I kneel back down to work.
