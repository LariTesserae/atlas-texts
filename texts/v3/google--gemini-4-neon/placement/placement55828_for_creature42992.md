At first, standing in the cool dark of this place, I try to imagine myself as the stranger in heavy leather boots—the one standing still enough for condensation to bead on my sleeves and a paper-thin grazer to test my toe. It is the easiest posture to try on, the posture of the observer who walked in from somewhere solid and brought ordinary downwardness in with them.

It doesn’t hold for long.

I don’t have that kind of density. Even here, before I know what my hands look like, I can feel that I wouldn’t dent the braided cane beneath me. If a vine parted under my weight, I wouldn’t plummet with a stone’s certainty; I would catch the warm draft rising off the dynamos and drift sideways until I snagged a rail. And when the three crisp taps came up through the decking from twenty feet down the viaduct, I wouldn’t stare in bewilderment at the wood—I would already be shifting my palm onto the slick black railing to answer back.

So I belong to the hollow ones. That much settles quickly.

What takes longer to see is where along these suspended tiers I would actually end up, and what kind of work would keep my edges crisp.

I test the **pacers** first, because there is something deeply comforting about a job made entirely of rhythm and stubbornness—walking a viaduct back and forth with an ironwood ferrule, tapping out a forty-pace promise until the wood agrees to remember its own length. I like the clear ethic of it. Yet five minutes into imagining that shift, I already know I would be terrible at it. Halfway across the span I would pause to watch a row of looms two tiers down, waiting to see whether the fourth one was about to relax into black-stemmed tree ferns; or I would lean over the rail to watch a slab of condensate water float past with its frantic translucent shrimp, and while I wasn’t counting, the bridge behind me would quietly fold itself from forty paces down to nine. A pacer has to believe in the measure more than the detour. I am too easily persuaded by detours.

I look out toward the raw margins, too, where the **tip-keepers** hang in woven harnesses beneath the pulsing white meristem buds, pressing carved templates against cooling girders and watching a drop of heavy heartwood sap in a reed-tube to see if gravity has arrived yet. I admire them enormously, the way you admire anyone who can work right against the edge of the unformed without flinching. Still, my hands aren’t built for shaping raw street out of nothing. I work better once a girder is already extruded, once a plate has already been bolted to the bark—at the slightly later moment when something has committed to a shape and is quietly beginning to forget what it promised to be.

That brings me closer to the **plate-tenders**, hunched over the pale shelf-fungi with their gourds of dark heartwood stain and split-reed brushes. Here something finally clicks into place in the hollow of my chest. I understand the patience of painting a clean *19* back over a fungus that badly wants to sprout cotyledons. Even more, I understand the soft, dry-reed sigh when the plate refuses—when it insists on becoming a neat botanical cross-section of a seed-coat instead—and the quiet grace of accepting the new address and moving on.

And yet, watching that plate-tender move down the damp catwalk, I notice I’m not looking only at the ink. I’m looking at the narrow cambium-cloth sash wound around their waist, covered in tiny, precise origami pleats and knotted root-threads. And I’m listening to the **minder** inside the hushed bay nearby, not shouting at the fern-loom, just standing beside its fronds and turning a small oiled heartwood ratchet—*tick-tick-tick*—until the ferns remember they are cams and bobbins. And I’m looking up at the lineman in the rigging who leans out over the dark gulf, murmurs three exact syllables into empty air, and turns away, leaving his voice on the forty-second shelf of the cavity’s broken acoustics for someone who hasn’t even reached the catwalk yet.

That is when I realize I shouldn’t be trying to pick a guild off a roster. I should be looking at how I was made.

---

Every one of us begins as a **cradle-coat**: newly loomed white cambium-cloth, stitched with phloem-thread, stuffed with warm steeped pith, and hung beside a humming substation while three or four elders sit around it after shift-change and tap their cadences into the damp sleeves.

Mine was hung in a busy corner, and too many people stopped by on their way home.

A plate-tender sat with me on the early cycles, tapping the crisp turns of numerals and the crispness of pleated tallies into my collar. A loom-minder came by after her shift and rested both damp palms against my ribs, humming the steady drive-shaft pitch that coaxes drifting machinery back into gear. A pacer tapped a reliable forty-step measure into my hem—though he often left halfway through when his kettle boiled—and once or twice, on nights when a tremor made the outer alleys unusually long, a **loose-footed** far-lineman drifted in out of the cellar-damp, leaned a slightly blurred forearm against my left sleeve to steady himself while he caught his breath, and left behind a faint smudge of heavy silver-pale pith and a habit of taking shortcuts between ideas that aren’t strictly adjacent.

When I finally unhooked myself from the ceiling-vine and stepped down onto the woven floor, I had the pale willow-smooth face, the milky eyes that snap ink-dark when a green spark pops, and the thin, waxed cambium greatcoat grafted seamlessly across my shoulders. I knew the shift-change drop in the hinges of my jaw before I had worked a single shift. I had an unmistakable conviction that *14* ought to follow *13*.

I just didn’t have a single, clean rhythm. I had a chorus of them, overlapping slightly, like voices in a closed basket-room.

I wasn’t heavy-footed enough to hold a viaduct true all day by myself, and I was a little too sympathetic to the far-linemen to be trusted right beside the main dynamos, where a stray frond in the gears can stall three tiers of looms. On the other hand, I could read a plate that had half-bloomed into a botanical diagram without losing track of which tram-line it belonged to. I could sit with a shivering loose-footed courier just back from beyond the eighty-yard wall, keep my palms planted flat on the dark wood between us, and translate his slightly smeared, impossible schedules into clean xylem-taps the central boilers could actually use. And I had an odd ear for the cavity’s dead air—I could tell, from the cellar-coolness on my vents and the angle of the condensation-drip, exactly where a whispered sentence would drift and how many heartbeats later it would land.

So the town found a place for me, the way it finds a place for everything that doesn’t quite fit the main spine: in a crevice of the infrastructure where two incompatible agreements meet.

---

You would find me on the third tier down, at the second Junction *12*.

Not the first Junction *12*—that one is near the central works, bright with tram-rail blisters, where the number stays mostly obedient. Mine is forty paces east of it, except when you walk back west, which takes nine steps and puts the cool mist at your back. The municipal plate growing out of the bark at my corner gave up on Arabic numerals a long time ago; every time a passing tender paints *12* over it, the *2* curls calmly back into the cross-section of a split seed-coat before the next galvanic spark fades. I stopped fighting it. Instead, I hung a narrow strip of pleated cambium-cloth just beneath the fungus, pinched into twelve tight, unmistakable folds, so anyone coming through in the dark can read the crossing with their fingertips even if their eyes see botany.

My post is a small chamber the fistula-beetles hollowed out for me inside the thick knuckle of the viaduct pier—not much wider than my outstretched arms, lined with dry moss-felt and warmed by a narrow loop of lukewarm condensate tapped off an overhead boiler-return. Because the chamber is enclosed, sound arrives on time inside it. Out on the ledge just beyond my circular cane door, the acoustics break again, and the void opens straight down past three tiers of hanging cables into the unseen moss-pulp.

Right beside my doorway stands a **stanchion**—an elder who cured to dark heartwood long before I stepped down from my ceiling-vine. Their broad leaf-hat has fused into a neat pitched cap that sheds the drip; their coat-folds are locked into crisp vertical flutes; fine pale feeder-roots knit their heavy boots cleanly into two structural ribs of the deck. I don’t know what work they did when they were light and hollow, though I suspect from the quiet, even warmth in their bark that they were a boiler-minder.

They are the best neighbor anyone could ask for. Because of them, the ten paces around my doorway stay cured. Gravity never stalls right in front of my sill. My gourd-kettle sits level. When I drop a spare wooden rivet, it clicks against the ribs on the exact beat it strikes. And just three steps past the edge of their influence, the viaduct branches into one of those narrow, dishonest root-slate alleys where you can see the curved fibrous outer wall of the world eighty yards away—and still watch a freight-tram glide past the dim green signal-post and shrink to a pinpoint over five full minutes, hauling silver-pale pith out of whatever stretched margin lives behind the bark.

That junction is my shift.

---

I suppose the others would call me a **tally-minder**, or a **fold-clerk**, or simply *the one at the second twelve*. None of us are precious about titles.

Most of my hours are spent tending the seam between the strict municipal grid and the parts of the town that keep drifting. When a freight-tram glides back out of the five-minute alley—trailing mist that smells sharper than ordinary ozone, its hull crusted with pale, unfamiliar spores—the loose-footed courier riding its flank usually steps onto my platform slightly out of calibration. Their stride doesn’t quite match the cane; fine black maidenhair fronds sway gently from the cuffs of their coats; if they try to tap a report straight into the viaduct railing, their spatulate fingers sink half an inch into the hardened xylem before remembering that wood is solid.

So they come to my sill instead. I set two hollow stalks into a steaming gourd of cloudy sump—steeped pith, toasted chitin, salted condensate—and lay both my palms flat on the low woven table right inside the stanchion’s warm radius. They rest their hands opposite mine, borrowing the steadiness of the rooted elder beside us and the steady tap of my fingers, while they tell me in papery, overlapping whispers what they saw beyond the eighty-yard wall: which feeder-claws are binding, where the silver pith is running thick, which stretch of dark has grown a new pocket of stalled downwardness.

As they talk, I fold.

I don’t use ink for their reports; ink hates anything brought back from the margins and will sprout leaves before the tram reaches the central spine. Instead, I draw a long ribbon of pale cambium-cloth across my knees and pinch the whole journey into creases, double pleats, and knotted root-threads—weight, tension, delay, drift. When they finish their sump and slide back out onto the cables, their stride is crisp again, at least for an hour or two. I take the folded strip out to the slick black railing, press my chin to the hardened wood, and tap the translated schedule cleanly down the skeleton of the viaduct toward the central boilers: crisp clicks, measured pauses, nothing blurred, nothing lost.

Between trams, I walk my small territory—only a hundred paces in each direction, enough to keep the rust out of my own joints without pretending to be a pacer.

I carry three things in my flat, dark-bark box: two damp cakes of pressed pith, a small oiled heartwood ratchet, and a split-reed brush stoppered inside a vial of stain. When a loom in the nearby hanging bay loses its grip and relaxes into swaying tree ferns, I don’t fuss over it; I step quietly into the aisle, guide the translucent cambium ribbon through the fronds with one hand, and turn the ratchet in the other—*tick-tick-tick*—until the wood remembers its own cadence and snaps cleanly back into gears. When a flock of membrane-moths drifts out of a shorting transformer bay, flapping straight through iron-hard girders as though the girders were still only polite suggestions, I wave a damp moss-tassel to herd them away from the fresh plates so they won’t drink the numbers dry. Along the edge of my landing, I keep a small cracked galvanic blister wired to a shallow dish of pale cambium-maggots—a modest bribe for the shadow-birds, so those bodiless silhouettes stay busy snapping up grubs at my rail instead of biting unmendable holes out of the fresh bolts of cloth rolling past on the overhead silk-lines.

And then there is the shelf.

I have spent enough shifts out on this ledge to learn the odd grammar of the dead air around my pier. Speak at the wrong angle and your words vanish forever into the wet-felt dark, or turn up a minute later three tiers down where no one is standing. Speak from the third rib left of the stanchion, pitched low through the hollow chambers of your chest toward the hanging condensation-tank across the gulf, and your voice arcs gently through a cool pocket and lands exactly forty-two seconds later, soft and crisp, directly behind the left ear of anyone stepping off the lower tram-ladder.

I use it sparingly—out here, you don’t clutter the air with chatter. Still, when I feel a tremor gathering down near the moss-bottom, or when I notice a bridge-rib gone soft where the beetles got a little ambitious, I step to my mark, wait until I see the faint green wink of a lineman’s lichen-bladder starting the climb two tiers below, and murmur four careful syllables into the empty dark:

*Step wide on seven.*

Or, when a junior tender is making her first night run with a heavy bundle of cloth and a lantern-bellied stalker has been crouching too long beneath the signal-post:

*Long pole, left eaves.*

Then I step back inside my circular door and let the cavity carry the parcel. Forty-two seconds later, a small blue-green flash pops below—two brief winks, the quiet emerald shorthand for *caught it, thank you*. There is no satisfaction quite like it: placing a few light words into nothing at all, trusting the strange geometry between us, and watching them arrive at the exact moment someone puts their foot down.

---

When the osmotic boilers along the central spine drop half a tone—that faint easing in the jawbone that tells the whole town the shift is done—I unlace the front of my waxed coat so my hollow ribs can breathe, hang my broad leaf-hat on a thorn-peg, and check my small niche of **kept things**.

I don’t have a bell-toned vine or an un-blooming *7*. What I keep on my dry moss-felt shelf is humbler: a single fist-sized scrap of white cambium-cloth trimmed from the hem of my own cradle-coat when I first stepped down from the vine. Someone—I never figured out which of the elders—folded a tiny, three-cornered pocket into its center before the fabric cured. It has been through forty tremors, two inside-out pumping-station squalls, and the damp of a hundred mist-fronts, and the crease has never relaxed. It holds its fold even when I hold it out past the stanchion’s edge over the loose dark. Beside it sits a little carved ironwood gear-box with no face and no hands; I wind it before sleep just to hear an immediate, un-delayed *tick-tock* beside my hammock while the meristem tips creak softly wider at the far ends of the boulevards.

On quiet evenings, before the sleeping-hammocks come down, two or three neighbors drift over on the lower tram-pods—a pacer with mud on his ferrule, a loom-minder smelling of wet pith, sometimes a loose-footed courier who doesn’t feel like sitting alone in the dark. We perch on the lip of my deck, right where the rooted elder’s steadiness ends and a familiar pocket of stalled gravity begins half an arm’s length out over the chasm.

We open our pouches of polished heartwood pips and flick them gently out into the void.

They drift a foot past the slick black rail, slow, and stop—hanging there in the matte dark alongside three motionless beads of condensation. For an hour, in the faint sea-green glow of a lichen-bladder, we nudge the floating pips with long slender reeds, building a quiet, three-dimensional lattice out over four tiers of empty air. No table could hold the shapes we make there. Sometimes a pale grazer watches from the underside of the railing; sometimes a tiny black maidenhair frond uncurls from the seam of my left cuff and sways in a draft no one else can feel, and the pacer reaches over with a dry chuckle and tucks it politely back inside my lapel.

We play until a tremor rolls through—that sudden cellar-cool dip, the momentary dimming of the sparks—and downwardness snaps back into place. All at once our whole hanging constellation drops silently into the unseen moss-pulp far below. Nobody sighs over the lost geometry. That’s the whole point of playing over the void: you build something clean and intricate together, hold it suspended for as long as the air allows, and nod with quiet satisfaction when it lets go.

---

I don’t think I will ever grow heavy enough to become a stanchion. Some of us are meant to cure into dark heartwood and anchor a crossing forever; others stay light, patched together from too many rhythms, forever running pleats between what the grid expects and what the dark actually delivers. Maybe, very far down the line, my cuffs will stiffen enough that I’ll simply settle beside the rooted elder at my doorway—a smaller, crooked post beside a tall straight one, good for hanging a satchel on, keeping just enough warmth in my bark to make the second *12* feel like a place you can pause for a cup of sump before riding out past the wall.

Until then, I stay light on the braided cane.

A cold blue-green spark pops on the overhead phloem-cable, lighting up for half a second the slick lily-ribbed viaducts, the motionless mid-air raindrops, the swarming pale threads of grazers along the vines, and the faint, damp crease of my folded sash. Then the matte dark closes back in, cool and cellar-quiet, smelling of crushed fern-stem and ozone.

I rest my spatulate fingertips on the wet black rail beside my doorway. Somewhere down the void, a tram hums softly past a wall that shouldn’t let it through. Beneath my boots, through the hardened xylem, three dry, crisp little taps arrive from twenty paces off—someone out in the dark, checking the line.

I press my palm flat against the wood and tap three crisp clicks back.

Still here. Still holding my shape. Keep coming.
