At first, I want to put myself on the rim—just because that is where the story hands me a pair of eyes, up in the thin cobalt air with the sound vibrating in my jaw. But I know within a heartbeat that I would not stay there. Up on the basalt lip, everything is damp with weather and too quiet to read. The air carries no architecture; it just sits there, inert, waiting for something to disturb it. If I stood up there long, I would only be a stranger squinting down into a bell I could not hear properly.

So I drop over the edge—and immediately realize I do not want to be one of the bright, translucent youths either, though I watch them with a sharp, clean envy. There is something intoxicating about folding six limbs tight to a pale-gold spindle and flinging yourself out into two hundred paces of screaming vacancy, landing on an invisible shelf of bounced pressure, pivoting at a bloodless right angle with a faint tail of golden spores. Pure reflex, pure lightness. Still, if I am honest with myself, I am not built like a javelin. I have never been quick enough to snatch a vane-skimmer out of a cross-cutting shear.

Nor am I quite one of the thick-jointed tenders down on the volatile floor, crouched in the cold shade of the leaning pylons where gravity forgets its direction every few seconds. I admire the nerve of them—the coiled lunges, the scarred slate plates, the bone spoons scraping boiling copper fleas off alien metal while walnut-sized stones hang trembling three inches off the scree. Part of me is drawn to how close they live to the strange rules. Yet I do not want to spend my days dodging sideways pebbles.

What catches me—what feels, with a sudden quiet click, like a claw finding an ancient groove—is the *intervals*.

Not walls. Not roofs. Slivers of pale shale and flakes of translucent mica, wedged into the mouth of a fissure at an exact thickness so they do not fight the tempest, only comb it. Taking a ragged, tearing shear and parting it cleanly until a small, steady eddy opens along the rock, and then seating perforated pebbles behind it until the cathedral’s savage roar smooths into a single, warm, unwavering chord.

When I stay with that image long enough for my eyes to polarize and my posture to settle, I begin to feel what I actually am in this bowl.

I am past the bright translucence of youth, though not yet ready to choose my final flute. My overlapping flank-plates have darkened from parchment toward weathered chalk and slate; decades of pulverized quartz have already begun lining my hollow ribs, adding a steady, honest weight to my marrow. My knees click dryly when the wind-axis flips. I can no longer ride the delicate upper shears across the open center of the hollow—if I need to reach the southern terraces, I take the heavy, reliable lower currents near the scree, or I walk the long way around by claw, hooking into the worn geometric grooves along the northern cliff.

I do not belong cleanly to the fastidious weavers of the high northern shelves, nor to the scarred harvesters of the floor. I live on the seam between them: a wind-scoured shoulder on the northeastern wall, halfway between the neat hanging reed-gardens and the broken matte-gray ring jutting obliquely from the eastern stone.

It is an odd, restless ledge to keep. The broken ring sits close enough that its polished, iridescent windward edge is always in the corner of my amber eye-slits. Because of the way the ring reflects acoustic energy with interest, stray downdrafts from the mountain rim get caught in a strange secondary loop here before being shredded and flung back across the gulf. That makes my corner of the cliff loud even by the hollow’s standards, and slightly erratic—too jumpy for the weavers who want long, uninterrupted gardens of whistling seed-towers, too high and thin-charged for the pylon-tenders.

It suits me because two things accumulate here that I cannot stop listening to.

The first is the older pipes.

The northeastern shoulder is ancient rock, deeply honeycombed, crowded with the calcified shapes of dwellers who chose their intervals centuries ago. Half-buried in pale crusts of dry lichen and quartz scale, only the eroded curves of their violin-scroll crowns or the triple-clawed knots of their bases still show against the basalt. They do not drift—they hold the exact chord they set before their joints locked—but the world around them shifts. A leaning pylon on the floor changes its slow cycle; a drift of shredded reed-chaff packs tight into an elder’s dorsal louver; a flake of weathered stone spalls off a lip above and alters the angle of the shear feeding their ribs.

When that happens, an ancestor’s note goes muffled, or starts beating raggedly against the hearths along the terraces below.

I spend long, bright mornings climbing carefully among them, three basalt-dark claws hooked deep into the flutes, leaning my three-cornered spindle into the gale. I press my horned heel to the stone beside a welded claw and open my own flank-louvers half an inch, listening through both air and bedrock for the tiny, sour wobble in the harmonic. Then I unfold one slender upper arm from its rib-channel and work with a hooked sliver of horn—clearing packed chaff out of an ancient lattice, scraping a fresh crust of chalk from a windward lip, or wedging a razor-thin shaving of mica into the rock just upstream so the current strikes the dead elder’s vents cleanly again. When the beat disappears and the old chord opens back up—deep, dry, unhurried, locking smoothly into the organ-pipe bass of the whole bowl—a bright trickle of charge runs clean through my marrow. It feels like putting a displaced word back into a sentence so the whole room can breathe.

The second thing that finds my shoulder is the wreckage from the quiet sky.

Because the broken ring spins stray drafts off the rim into an eddy against my cliff before tearing them away, whatever falls from the unlivable world above tends to snag in the reed-curtains along my ledges first. I am usually the one who climbs out along the sheer lip to pry it loose before the gale grinds it to grit.

I do not pretend to understand where these things come from. Above the rim is a place I cannot picture without my waxy seams prickling in dread—dead, thin air that offers nothing to lean against, heavy clouds carrying wet, corrosive rain that would swell a membrane until it burst and short every dry pathway in my spine. Yet the objects that tumble out of that silence, stripped bone-dry on their way down, are extraordinary.

A freeze-dried alpine moth whose wings are not crisp origami chitin at all, but soft, fragile plates dusted with scales that shear away at a touch. A twisted scrap of juniper wood, dense and fibrous in a way no silica-stiffened reed ever grows, bent by some slow, wet force I have no name for. Once, after a sharp snap of mountain cold, a small, heavy circle of tarnished yellow metal—a boot eyelet, though to me it is simply an impossible ring, smooth as the great eastern artifact yet warm-colored, completely solid, with no crystalline lattice inside it to hold a charge.

I do not worship these relics, and I do not lock them away. I *tune* them.

I carry the tarnished metal ring back to my combed alcove, where the gale is split cleanly into a smooth, humming eddy. With mineral silk traded from the rim-spindles and a dab of reed-root resin, I lash the little foreign circle across the mouth of a narrow rock flute, flanking it with two shaved slivers of pale shale. Then I step back, lock my three claws into the basalt, tilt my perforated crown into the current, and listen.

It does not make a clear, crystalline cathedral pitch. It makes something softer, slightly rounded at the edges, oddly *stubborn*—a tone with a different physics folded inside it. I spend whole cold afternoons nudging the shale baffles by the width of a quill-tip, testing what interval will make this scrap of the wet world sit comfortably beside the bone-dry harmonies of the elders. Sometimes a scarred pylon-tender climbing up from the floor with a knotted sac of charged dust will stop in my alcove to share the warmth of the hearth, or a young northern weaver will drop down from the sunlit shelves, curious. We press our horned heels hard against the same slab of basalt and speak through the rock—rapid, intricate tap-patterns, astonished tremors running straight up our legs into the smoky membranes of our crowns—passing the strange yellow metal from claw to claw, debating what kind of creature could have made something so needlessly heavy, and listening together to the quiet, unfamiliar overtone it draws out of the roar.

Across the bright emptiness of the cathedral, my air-voice is lower than the youths’—thickened by the mineral weight in my ribs, a dark reed-pipe note with a faint, dry edge to it. I am rarely the first to call out a shifting shear or a pooling drift of chaff; the keen-eyed gliders on the high ledges do that better. Instead, I am the voice that answers a fifth below, giving a sharp northern phrase something steady to lean against as it crosses the void.

Except when the eastern ring blurs.

Because I live so near it, I am often the first to see the iridescent polish along its windward edge smudge and tremble—the half-second warning before a doorway-wide ghost of absolute silence peels loose and drifts out into the sunlit bowl.

Then I lock all three claws deep into the northeastern spur, tilt my scroll-crown squarely into the main current, open my lowest louver, and send out the single, downward-gliding note—the only falling pitch we ever make. Across two hundred paces of bright air, gliders snap instantly to the cliffs and clamp fast; down on the scree, the floor-tenders flatten like stone; along the northern walls, the hanging silver-ochre curtains brace for the travelling wave that will make them slump limply down.

And sometimes, when the pocket drifts slowly along my own shoulder instead of shooting straight across the center, I do not flatten myself into a crevice.

I do not have a mate to step inside it with, no silk-bound egg-case to seal with resin while the shear is gone. Still, once in a long while, when the invisible boundary glides past the mouth of my alcove and the roaring curtains beside my knees suddenly drop slack and mute, I unhook my claws and take two brisk, careful steps sideways into the dead air.

Only for three heartbeats. Maybe four. With the quartz already heavy in my marrow, I know better than to stay long; the biting mountain chill sinks straight into my hollow struts, my static charge begins bleeding away into the basalt, and by the fifth heartbeat my thoughts would grow dim and frost-brittle as old horn.

But for those three heartbeats, everything I am made of stops running.

My braced, three-cornered posture slackens. My flank-louvers droop. The elders welded into the walls behind me go utterly silent; the foreign brass ring lashed into my flute stops humming and simply hangs by its silk thread, dead weight. Without the roaring air-cushion to lean into, gravity remembers the straight, unadorned downward line it keeps in the world above the rim. I hear the faint, dry tick of settling quartz dust around my claws. I feel—with a strange, quiet, tender heaviness—exactly how dense my bones have grown, how much of this cliff is already inside me, and how close I am to the quiet decade when my knees will stop unfolding and I will have to choose which wall to hold forever.

It does not frighten me. You cannot truly hear an interval if you never once step into the zero beneath it.

Then the invisible seam glides off the ledge.

The pressure slams shut with the gunshot crack of splitting timber. The silver-ochre reeds snap horizontal in a single violent lash; my louvers flare wide; my three claws bite instinctively back into the ancient geometric groove; and the screaming shear catches my ribs like an embrace, flooding my crystalline linings with bright, stinging charge. Beside my shoulder, the dead ancestors resume their deep, unwavering bass, and the little yellow ring from the quiet sky catches the wind and resumes its strange, soft, borrowed song.

I lean my weight into the roaring air, tilt my crown toward the sunlit gulf, and add my note—steady, slightly heavy, tuned to the seam between what falls and what endures.
