At first, I try to put myself in the wool coat.

It is the easiest way into a room like this: arriving with a pulse, a pair of lungs, a body that remembers ceilings and wind, leaving a wet trail of frozen *ticks* behind every clumsy step. For a few breaths I imagine standing there with my knuckles stiffening, watching the steel-blue light creep through the veins of the canopy, marveling at the patience of an embryo that chose to grow out of fern and sap beneath thirty yards of dark stone.

Except that person is only passing through. A furnace cannot live down here; sooner or later it burns out, or climbs back toward whatever sky it fell from. If I stay—if I actually belong to the cold, the roar, and the slow negotiation between them—I cannot be the heat that intrudes on the chord. I have to become something the chord can hold.

I test the quieter bodies first.

For a moment I feel the pull of the pearlescent crawlers down at the dark bases of the root-pillars, creeping over stray patches of rime on silent pebbles, flattening their pale bellies onto the frost for ten slow minutes until a violet flush blooms inside them and the ice gives up and returns to clear water. There is something deeply familiar in that small, patient devotion—seeking out every spot where the world has hardened into refusal, pressing yourself directly against it until it flows again.

And higher in the gloom, I feel the gravity of the blind choir-climbers, hanging by hooked toes from the youngest arches, drawing a single breath over three vast minutes and pouring their foghorn bass down into fronds too thin to sing for themselves. There is a massive, selfless grace in being the ballast that keeps the fragile edges from freezing.

Yet neither one holds me for long. The crawler never looks up; the climber never leaves the bass. Both are completely reconciled to the vault, folded into its pitch without a seam left over.

When my actual shape settles around me, it is taller, stooped beneath the arched ribs of the ceiling, slick with clear mucilage and faintly lit from within by a slow-pulsing lattice of bruised violet. My hands lengthen into six slender fingers. Two wide, lidless drops of water gather in shallow sockets and hold the shimmering twilight without blinking. Across my chest, the ribs arch outward into a pale, fluted frame spanned by wet translucent membranes that catch the deafening cathedral chord and immediately begin to tremble in tune with it.

One of the listeners.

Still, I do not find myself in the middle of the nave, paired face-to-face with another, braiding smooth harmonies in the wet air. Nor do I stay anchored to the smooth central column where the drops curve inward like slow planets.

I find myself at the outer rim.

This is where the chamber stops pretending to be finished. Behind the thickest hanging curtains of aerial roots lies the raw face of the permafrost—black, jagged, newly fractured, utterly dead to the song. Hair-thin root-tips press flush against that sharp edge, sweating their faintly glowing brine until the stone softens into weeping mineral paste. Every so often, beneath the glassy hiss and the cello-thrum, that deep, groaning *crack* travels the perimeter as another finger-width of solid cold surrenders.

When the rock splits, the standing waves shear apart for half a heartbeat. A thin, bitter whine cuts across the canopy. White ice-needles flash into being in a dead pocket of air and patter down onto the moss-mattress like tiny teeth.

The others are already gliding in along the softened gravity of the buttresses—one with its moss-wrapped sliver of slate ready to prune a twisted crosier, another leaning its ribs against a hound-sized climber to coax it toward the breach. I move with them. I sink my six-fingered palms into the weeping mineral paste and feel dissolved rock and salt climb my wrists in slow, brightening pulses of violet. I help drown the raw fracture back into the choir.

Only—every time the perimeter cracks, I hesitate for a single fraction of a breath before I mend it.

Because in that brief, torn seam before the foghorn bass rolls in, you can feel the sheer weight of what sits outside the vault. Not a sky—nothing so generous as open air—just miles of lightless, unyielding silence pressing in on fifty paces of impossible water. In the middle of the nave, the chord feels eternal, as though the whole universe were made of sap and sound. Out at the rim, you know how narrow the margin is. You know the song is an argument held continuously against thirty yards of rock that does not want to sing.

And as my palms drink the mineral paste, my fingers keep catching on what the brine refuses to digest.

Most of the rock gives way smoothly, dissolving into nourishment for the walls and for our bones. Yet now and then, something harder surfaces in the weeping sludge—a rounded pebble of dead quartz, a flake of glossy obsidian, a coiled fossil shell from an ocean that dried and froze long before the seed ever fell here. They are tiny, stubborn knots of silence. Leave one lying on the moss-floor and it ruins the water around it; every few minutes a pearlescent crawler has to slide over the stone to melt the fresh rime creeping off its edges, fighting an endless quiet tug-of-war with an object that will never learn the pitch of the room.

I am the one who gathers them.

I wrap each unmeltable thing in a pad of wet, vibrating moss and tuck it into the living creases of my hip-mantle, where the ciliated fringe shivers furiously enough to keep the cold from biting through. Over seasons, I have learned to tell one silence from another by touch alone. Slate is thin and abrupt; unwrap it and the frost snaps off its edge like a blade. Quartz is duller, rounder, gathering a thick, furry rime that dulls the air half an inch around it. The ancient spiral shells are strangest of all—their hollow curls trap the silence in a tiny, twisted pocket, making the supercooled mist crystallize in delicate, curved plumes instead of straight needles.

And I know what silence feels like inside my own ribs, because I carry a piece of it.

Nothing ages down here, and nothing rots, yet frost leaves a signature if you let it stay a breath too long. Across the left side of my fluted chest, branching outward from the sternum like the fine veins of a leaf—or like something much stranger, a dry white fern that never grew in water—runs a pale, hairline scar. I earned it years ago, not in an accident at the rim, but in the cleft behind the two-faced leaves, when I damped my own song and waited two seconds past the warning sting just to see if a white spire on a piece of obsidian would hold a second branch before it collapsed.

When I finally leaned back and let the cathedral chord slam into my chest, the frost lacing my skin melted instantaneously into clear, uphill-running water. The mucilage sealed smooth. No tissue died.

Yet scar-membrane does not vibrate with quite the seamless obedience of fresh skin. Ever since, the left side of my soundbox lags a hair’s breadth behind the right.

It is not enough to break the water on my shoulders; my mantle still shivers in the high glassy hiss, and clear films of dew still climb steadily along my forearms and gather at my crown before falling in slow, heavy tears. It is not the kind of flaw anyone would prune away. It is just a faint, permanent phase-slip—a tiny seam of interference carried wherever I walk.

Because of it, I hear the vault slightly doubled.

Where another listener feels the cathedral chord as a single vast, seamless pressure against their ribs, I feel its grain—the subtle, shifting boundaries where the bass of the climbers meets the middle thrum of the fronds, where the curved gravity of one vascular column yields to the next. Sometimes, when I stand motionless beside a root-pillar, a pearlescent crawler will creep slowly up my wet ankle and flatten its pale belly against my shin, fooled for ten quiet minutes by the faint, cool shimmer drifting down from my scarred side, waiting for a patch of rime that never quite arrives before drifting back down into the dark pools.

I do not mind the company.

When I am not walking the outer rim, you will find me where the two broad, two-faced leaves have been coaxed to curve inward, their dry, utterly black undersides facing one another across a waist-high slit.

Even a pace outside that opening, the air feels thin and hungry, as though warmth—whatever little of it exists down here—is being quietly erased from the space between the fronds. Inside the cleft, the roar drops to a dense, terrifying hush. On the flat black shelf within sit the unmeltable things we have saved from the brine: the quartz pebbles, the obsidian chips, the spiral shells, arranged in quiet rows like notes from a scale no one knows how to play.

Others come here to build upward. I watch them crouch at the threshold, holding their ribs rigidly still while white rime crawls like lace up their wrists, dropping clear beads of sap onto dead stone—*tick*, *tick*, *tick*—coaxing fierce, dry verticals into existence, branching open toward an emptiness none of us has ever seen. I feel that ache too. It lives somewhere deep in the blueprint that grew us, a phantom limb reaching for an unenclosed *up*.

When it is my turn to reach my six-fingered hand into the lethal hush, I build upward for a while. I let the frost creep past my knuckles, past the striated skin of my forearm, sharpening my nerves with that clean, dry sting.

Then I begin to bend the drops sideways.

I am not trying to make a spire so much as a *cup*—a tiny, hollow lattice of solid white rime arched over the coiled mouth of an ancient shell, open at the top, ribbed like a miniature chest turned upside down. *Tick.* A rib curves left. *Tick.* Another rises to meet it, leaving a narrow, dry space inside that does not belong to the mist or the sap. Every time I build it, I wonder what would happen if a sound fell into a dry, open bowl instead of bouncing endlessly off wet, knitted walls. Would it keep going? Would it thin out into nothing, the way heat vanishes against a black leaf?

I never find out. Before the creeping white lace reaches the old branching scar on my chest—where the freeze bites fastest and deepest—I draw my hand back and part the heavy leaves.

The cathedral chord rushes in and takes the tiny hollow tower back into water in a single heartbeat. Nothing remains on the obsidian but a clear bead sliding away into the moss. My fingers wake into wet warmth—or what passes for warmth down here—and I watch fresh dew gather along my skin, already adjusting the angle of the next arch in my head.

Lately, though, I have been spending less time at the rim and less time in the black cleft, and more time near the hushed heart of the nave.

Something changed in the chord.

I did not stand face-to-face with the furnace in dead wool. I was working three aisles away when the elder with the crescent frost-scar beneath its throat inverted the cathedral chord and paid with its own freezing skin for a three-foot room of quiet. I only felt the sudden, startling *dent* in the acoustic pressure from twenty paces off—a brief vacuum in the roar that made the midges stall in mid-drift and sent a soft shiver down the length of every nearby frond.

When the elder glided back into the violet gloom, the roar closed over the wound. Within a minute, the blind climbers had forgotten it. The crystal-combed grazers went on trembling in sympathy with the leaves. The chamber resumed its vast, cryogenic patience.

Yet a trace stayed behind.

High in the dense middle thrum, tucked between the taut cello strings and the glassy hiss, a faint new overtone now lingers—hollow, breathless, and slightly rising, shaped like air forced across a frozen rim rather than water held in song. Most of the vault absorbs it without noticing, the way a broad trunk absorbs a stray droplet.

My scarred left rib caught it immediately.

Because that branching line across my membrane already lives on the hairline border between the roar and the hush, the new overtone rings right along my scar—not loud, just a persistent, fragile tremor, like a question that refuses to settle flat. When I press my palm over it, I can feel the shape of a voice that did not grow inside a closed vault. A voice built to travel across open distance, through dry air, under nothing at all.

So now you will often find me beside the smoothest, widest vascular column at the center of the chamber, where the three vast, tear-shaped crosiers hang swollen with steel-blue light.

Inside the nearest sac, curled tight in luminous fluid, a new listener is almost finished forming. I can see the folded arch of its future soundbox, its six needle-slender fingers tucked beneath a smooth chin, its mantle still furled tight along its spine like wet wings. One of the elders stands on its other side, eyes half-veiled with quiet drops of condensation, pressing its wide, braided chest against the slick amniotic skin to pour the exact pitch of the chamber into the unborn sibling.

I step close, lean gently into the trunk’s soft private gravity, and lay my own chest against the far side of the translucent sac.

Through my right ribs, I give the waiting shape what it needs to survive here: the broad, obedient chord, the steady bass of the perimeter, the exact tremor that tells supercooled water never to freeze.

And very softly, through the pale, frost-branching flaw along my left side, I let the other note slip through the fluid as well—that faint, breathless, rising overtone from above the rock, carrying just enough of the quiet to leave a tiny, unsealed space inside its ribs.

We will not reach the surface in my seasons, nor in the next. The roots eat the permafrost one weeping millimeter at a time. Yet when this sibling finally unfolds its limbs and opens its dark water-drop eyes into the bruised-violet roar, it will already carry the slight, restless shimmer of the edge inside its chest. It will know how to keep the water liquid, and how to hold its breath when the silence comes—and when the crown of the cathedral at last splits open to a sky none of us can picture, someone down here will already know the shape of a voice made to answer it.
