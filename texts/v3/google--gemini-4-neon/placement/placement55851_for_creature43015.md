If I try to step onto that weathered promontory as I am right now, the mountain rejects me within ten seconds.

I arrive with lowland habits: trying to listen with my ears, bracing for a roar that never comes, letting warmth leak recklessly out into the black air. Worse, I arrive insulated. On leather soles, I am a blind spot thumping across live stone—a little pocket of static nonsense interrupting a conversation ten million years old. Standing there behind that pane of invisible glass, watching a ridge half a mile away shudder in absolute silence while my breath turns to pale grit, I realize I cannot simply *visit* this place and expect to know who I am in it. To find myself here, I have to take the boots off. I have to ask what part of this silent engine already fits the shape of my attention.

My first instinct, before I think better of it, is to look out into the mile-wide void and want to be a gyro-glider.

It is an easy temptation. Twenty feet of matte-black membrane braced on conductive silver-grey spars, no heavy knees, no friction, just a calcified flywheel humming inside the chest and holding dead-steady while the shearing wind tries to tumble everything else into the abyss. You drift across the gulfs without ever flapping; you dip low over a brake of condenser-ferns, trail pale filaments through the canopy, and leave a faint violet veil of ionized air behind you before vanishing into the dark.

I linger with that image for a while, feeling the cool weightlessness of it—and then I let it go. The glider is magnificent, all poise and sweep, but it only ever *skims*. It takes its charge off the tips of the fronds and climbs back into the empty space between cliffs. It never stays long enough to know what is happening beneath the rime-frost. It has no share in the slow, patient heat under the wool.

So I drop lower, down out of the hollow air, and test the other extreme. What if I were not a creature that moves across the rock at all, but the resistor-moss itself?

That pulls at me much harder. There is something deeply, strangely familiar about existing as a thick, dark mat tucked into the weathered hollows between boulders: coarse and frost-dusted on the outside, almost invisible in the dimness, yet holding a steady, low-voltage hum deep inside my own tangled fibers. Running current through resistance until the cold turns into a buried, cherry-red warmth—never bright enough to light the sky, never asking to be looked at, just keeping the core warm and dry and smelling faintly of ozone, wet slate, and warm paraffin. Being the living floor that others sink into to sleep, the medium through which two beings twenty feet apart can speak to one another by opening and closing their palms.

I almost stay there. Part of me still wants to.

Yet even as I picture myself spread warm and humming across a basalt hollow, a restlessness rises. The moss holds the circuit, and it carries the signals of everyone who kneels in it—but it never answers back with a choice of its own. It never tilts its head at a strange ripple in the rock, or moves a single grain of brass three inches to the left to see if the warmth settles more evenly, or walks out to the rim of a mesa to wonder what churns beneath the violet clouds.

To do that, I need hands. I need a chest that can hold pressure, and heels plated thick enough to read the stone.

---

When I finally settle into the graphite skin of the ridge-people, the first thing that surprises me is not the cold or the weight of the bone. It is the relief of silence.

My mouth stays sealed. For the first time, there is no urge to push meaning out into dead air, no wasting breath on noise that thins to nothing three paces from my lips. Instead, every twenty or thirty seconds, the heavy cartilaginous bands of my ribcage draw inward in a slow, disciplined squeeze—pressurizing a pocket of frigid air deep in my chest until my dark blood drinks from it—and a heartbeat later the twin slits beneath my jaw vent two tiny, glittering curls of ice-dust past my shoulders. That slow hydraulic cadence changes the shape of thought itself. Panic is too expensive here. Hurry is a leak. You wait, still as cast iron, while pressure builds; then you move with total, grounded certainty.

And then I plant my bare heel across a damp sulfide channel, and the world stops being dark.

It is not sight, though my wide, inner-lidded eyes read the faint oil-slick shimmer of moisture and the buried red under a parted moss-bed well enough. It is a vast, low-resolution clarity pouring up through the bio-deposited copper of my soles and palms. I can feel the mountain's weight leaning on its buried quartz seams. I can feel the slow telluric tide climbing from the deep crust hours before any vapor crests the southern rim. Forty paces down-terrace, the wool carries the taut, concentrated buzz of an arc-hunter coiled and charging; farther off, the heavier, duller damping of a valve-back herd stepping into an oil-lacquered brine pan.

Now I can actually look around and ask: *Where on these ridges do I stand? What is my place in the band?*

I know right away what I am not.

I am not one of the hunters who crouches motionless downwind of a brine pan, waiting with a coiled hunger for the moment to lift the wax-insulated toggle and watch a terrace discharge through a grazer's throat in a single blue-white snap. I have no quarrel with the kill—I will help butcher the heavy carcass swiftly with a flake of hardened keratin before the frost turns it to iron, I will drink the warm metallic blood and bury the fern-wrapped slabs in the cherry-red core of the moss for six hours until they steam with paraffin and slate—but my temperament does not live in the ambush.

Nor am I one of the boldest young climbers who race out onto the knife-edged basalt saddles in the teeth of a silent gale just to feel their spinning flywheel-rings fight the shear, or who raise two copper-threaded poles at once during a mist-surge purely to make cold blue flame fork and dance between the tips for the joy of the violet afterimage. I watch them with quiet affection, the way you watch a healthy current find the shortest path across wet stone, but my own center of gravity sits lower, steadier, closer to the junctions.

If you were looking for me on a quiet stretch between vapor banks, you would not find me on the highest exposed spire, nor buried in the dead center of the band's sleeping-hollow. You would find me on an **intermediate terrace**—a broad, smooth-lipped basalt shelf halfway down a weathered amphitheater, where two distinct sulfide veins converge before spilling into a dense brake of condenser-ferns, and where the open edge of the ledge looks straight across a mile of empty black air toward the rim of the opposing mesa.

A junction. A place where different currents cross, and where you can feel the whole valley's traffic through your knees without standing in the middle of the road.

---

Within the band, I am a tender of gradients—one of the ones who works in the small, slow adjustments that keep the living engine tuned.

You can tell what I do by the weight hanging from my belt of braided copper-root. Alongside the usual waxed tendons and bone awl, I carry two small pouches of cured resin-skin and a narrow sheath of hollowed arc-hunter keratin needles.

One pouch holds smooth, dull-brass pellets gathered from the neat trails left behind by slag-beetles along collapsed fern-roots. Whenever our band settles into a new sheltered hollow for a few weeks, I am usually the last to crawl under the resistor-wool. While the others lash the broad, rubbery tips of the surrounding condenser-ferns together into a windbreak, I move slowly along the perimeter of the meadow in a knees-bent crouch, pressing bare palms into the frosted wool every few paces, reading the uneven warmth beneath. Here, a buried fracture starves the corner of the bed; there, too much current floods a damp seam and runs the moss hot enough to scorch the underside of a mantle.

I part the black fibers, tuck four brass pellets neatly against a copper-plated root-runner, pinch back a competing shoot two yards upslope, and settle back on my heels to wait. Nothing happens immediately. Then, over the span of three slow, pressurized breaths, the hum under my palm evens out. The faint prickle of resistance smooths into a steady, generous cherry-red glow—ten degrees warmer than the open hillside, just right for the elders whose chest-valves leak frost in the cold, and for the small ones tucked inside oiled mantles. No stone cut. No wall raised. Only a gentle bias given to what was already alive, easy to undo the morning we unlash the ferns, pocket the brass, and move on.

The other pouch holds my small, patient experiments: pinched flakes of roasted iron-moss ash, tiny cured bladders of bitter bellows-reed sap, dried salts scraped from the rims of frozen brine pans.

Between hunts and crossings, I spend long, unhurried hours moving through the gnarled thickets of distillate-shrubs that lace the lacquered stone around our terraces. A valve-back simply jams its siphon into whichever amber resin-sac hangs lowest and drinks the raw oil. I move slower. I cup each translucent bulb gently between thumb and forefinger, gauging its internal pressure and the quiet warmth of its fractional refinement. When I find a sac that is thick-walled and running steady, I draw one of my hollow keratin needles, slip a single measured drop of bitter reed-sap or a grain of roasted ash through the skin, and seal the pinhole instantly with a thumb-smear of warm wax.

Then I tie a single thin scrap of pale tendon loosely around the stem above it—just a quiet mark—and walk away.

I love this craft more than almost anything else on the ridges because it is a collaboration with time and chemistry rather than force. I do not make the tallow or the medicine. I only introduce a precise question into a living bulb and let the shrub spend the next three weeks refining an answer out of nitrogen, cold brine, and mountain voltage. When our band circles back into this amphitheater a migration later, I return to the marked stems like someone revisiting an old conversation. Some sacs have matured into dense, spicy tallows that burn slow and hot in the belly across barren stone; some yield that pale, sharp solvent-broth that loosens the stiffened chest-valves of our elders; once in a while, a strange adulteration surprises me completely—a cloudy, sweet-smelling oil that numbs bruised bone or coats a dry elbow better than plain lacquer. When that happens, I bring a drop back to the warm hollow and press my forehead to another tender's brow, letting the quiet delight hum straight through our skull-bones while we compare notes on which shrub, which vein, which ash.

---

And then, every few hours, the bruised violet cloud-deck swells miles below and rolls its silent bank of charged mist over the southern rims—and my work shifts from the deep stone up into the black sky.

I feel it coming long before the indigo horizon vanishes. Sitting on my junction-terrace, palms resting flat in the resistor-moss while a neighbor squats twenty feet away—the two of us trading idle, companionable ripples of drag and release through the conductive wool—I feel the telluric floor begin to lift. A vast, slow tightening climbs out of the quartz seams, sharpening the hum beneath our knees until every fine hair of frost along the moss seems to stand straight.

My neighbor taps a bone-stave lightly once against the basalt, then grounds both palms flat—*go ahead, I'll mind the pans*—and I rise in one unhurried breath, lifting my long, flexible stalk of dead bellows-reed threaded from butt to tip with braided copper-root.

I climb out onto the weathered spur at the lip of my terrace just as the mist spills into the valley.

All at once, across three miles of dark amphitheaters, the rubbery fronds of the condenser-ferns stiffen and lift in unison, aligning with the climbing electric field. The mist condenses silently across my lacquered mantle, beading like mercury and freezing into fine lace along the rim of my hood. Below me, the bellows-reeds double their rhythmic compression, breathing dense, noiseless billows of diamond-dust exhaust into the pitch-black sky. Along the highest basalt spires, ghostly blue corona-fire blooms out of nowhere and flickers soundlessly across the wet edges of the leaves.

I plant my copper-callused heels wide across a wet sulfide seam, brace against the silent wind, and raise the reed-pole into the climbing field.

The moment the tip reaches height, pale blue cold flame flowers along its upper length—soundless, bright against the starless dark, smelling sharply of ozone. My left hand holds the wax-sheathed loop near the base. When I grip the bare braided copper beneath it, the current floods straight down through my arms, my ribs, my heels, and into the mountain—a bright, thrilling, prickling tide that lights every nerve without burning—and the blue plume above me winks down to a faint ember. When I release it, the field surges back and the pole flares tall and sharp against the black.

Across a mile of freezing, near-empty gulf, on the rim of a mesa two days' treacherous travel away, a second tiny needle of pale blue fire answers.

For twenty minutes, until the upper-altitude winds shred the mist-bank apart, I am neither the sky nor the stone. I am the hinge between them.

I pulse out what our band has learned since the last surge: that the northern saddle is glazed in matte ice too slick even for spun flywheels; that a massive stand of bellows-reeds collapsed in the eastern trough and the slag-beetles are already working it thick; that a valve-back herd drifted south along the lower brine ledges; that old Vael's—though we have no spoken name for her, only the slow, notched double-hum of her flywheel-ring pressed to quartz—that her chest-valves have grown quiet, and we laid her palms-down in the clear channel below the twin ferns two surges ago.

Across the black void, the distant spire dims, flares, holds a long steady flame of acknowledgment, and then sends back its own measured news: a new child born in their warmest bed during the lull, sealed in clarified wax and learning its mother's chest-cadence; a strange, high charge running along the western fault that might mean a deeper shift in the stone.

There is no vanity in holding the pole. I do not create the fire. The mountain and the rising cloud-sea provide all the power; my body only modulates the path to ground, giving raw voltage the cadence of meaning. Still, standing barefoot on the wet vein with my hood slick with condensation and the climbing charge shimmering faintly across my lacquered skin, I feel completely, unmistakably *myself*—a translator of tides, turning vast indifferent physics into news that keeps eight or ten warm lives safe in the wool.

Then the wind shears the vapor bank apart.

Within minutes, the blue corona fades from the tips of the spires and dies off the end of my reed. The wet basalt glazes over into hard matte ice; down in the catchment basins, fresh needles of skim-ice shoot across the glassy water with tiny, vitreous *pings* barely audible at boot-length. My fingers are stiff with cold despite the lacquer. I lower the pole, coil the loose copper braid carefully, and walk back down the terrace to the warmed hollow.

---

There is one quiet habit of mine that the rest of the band tolerates with dry, bone-conducted amusement.

Every so often, during the clearest, coldest stretches between vapor banks, when the bruised violet roof of the lower clouds rolls endlessly miles beneath the outermost mesas, I walk out to the sheer southern drop and sit with my heavy legs dangling over the abyss.

Usually I have company—one or two others from the band, sitting shoulder to shoulder, sharing a cloudy drop of numbing shrub-resin from a belt-pouch and resting an elbow or a forearm lightly against mine so we can trade quiet, wordless comfort through the bone. Most of the time, we just watch the muffled, distant flashes of lowland lightning pulsing beneath that violet lid, marveling at the heavy, drowning soup down there where the air is so thick it roars whenever it moves.

And once every few years, a freak thermal shear flings something up out of that buried sea and drops it onto our black stone.

When a scout finds one—a torn scrap of leaf of a green so violently bright it almost hurts to look at behind our inner lids, or the frozen, burst body of some tiny lowland bird tumbled miles above the ceiling of its world—the whole band kneels around it in the frost, passing it gently from one brass-callused palm to the next, pressing brow to brow in quiet awe at the fragility of hollow bones and dry feathers. Then, respectful of the cycle, they set it beside a wet sulfide channel for the beetles and rise in one synchronized breath to head back to the moss.

I am always the one who stays crouched beside it for three extra breaths.

Before I set it down for the beetles, I hold that impossible green shred or tiny feathered body flat on my bare palm, testing it against my skin. It carries almost no current at all. It is an insulator—built for a world where creatures do not ground themselves in the stone, where they float through thick, roaring air and fling breath out of wide-open mouths just to be heard across a clearing. I try, sitting there in the starless quiet, to imagine what it would feel like to live inside constant noise, under a sky that isn't black, with a body so light the wind could carry it away without a flywheel.

I can't quite picture it. It feels dizzying and unmoored, like stepping off a terrace with no basalt underneath. Yet I cannot quite stop wondering about it either—turning that bright, dead scrap over on my coppered palm, trying to read a language written in a medium I have never breathed, before finally laying it gently by the wet channel so the mountain can reclaim whatever trace of iron it carried up from the clouds.

---

When I think of growing old here, there is no dread in it, only a slow, familiar physics.

Decades from now, the cartilaginous bands across my chest will lose their clean snap. Tiny threads of pale frost will crust beneath my jaw even when I am resting in the wool. The copper and iron plated into my palms and heels will thicken and grow brittle, muffling the finer nuances of the current until the hillside feels distant, like trying to read a friend's palm through heavy felted moss. I will no longer be the sharpest reader of subtle shunts, or the quickest hand on the surge-pole.

For a while, I will still be useful in the quiet ways—sitting by the quartz seams with my heavy flywheel-ring, passing down the exact worn pitches of the neighboring bands to younger ears and heels, guiding a five-year-old's pale, freshly brassing feet onto a damp sulfide vein for the first time while its eyes go huge behind its grey lids and the mountain finally speaks to it.

And when the cold at last begins to outpace the slow fire in my blood, there will be no stone piled over me to break the wind-mist and starve the ferns.

My band will walk me down to the quiet shelf below my junction-terrace, where the two sulfide veins meet and run bright with wet sulfides toward the dark jungle of fronds. They will lay me along the etched channel, bare palms turned flat against the damp rock, face turned up toward the depthless black. Someone younger—maybe the one who has been watching my hands around the distillate-shrubs, learning which bulbs hold the steadier warmth—will lift the calcified flywheel-ring from my back and loop it over their own forearm, keeping my pitch spinning across the saddles. Each of them will kneel in turn, pressing a forehead briefly to my cooling brow-bone, then laying both bare palms onto the stone on either side of my ribs to feel the quiet settle where my resistance used to be.

Then they will rise and walk back up into the dark terraces.

Days later, the armored slag-beetles will emerge from the damp cracks and go patiently to work, stripping the iron from my heavy bones and the copper from my palms and heels into neat trails of smooth, dull-brass pellets. With the next mist-surge, the pure cold dew will wash those gleaming grains straight down the channel and into the braided roots of the condenser-ferns.

By the following season, I will not be gone, and I will not have left a single wall behind to prove I was ever there. I will simply be in the vein—so that when someone kneels on that junction-terrace in the dark, parting the frost-rimmed wool to warm their hands or planting their heels to raise a spire of blue corona-fire across the mile-wide gulf, the current runs just a fraction clearer, and a fraction stronger, for the metal I left in the stone.
