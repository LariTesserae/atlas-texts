If I came over that lip of moss-furry slate for the first time, I think I would stand there too long, getting soaked, trying to tell where the sound ends and the wood begins.

My first impulse wouldn't be to claim a doorway at all. Looking down into that seven-story tangle of wet cedar, flapping wool, and exploding clouds of cliff-swifts, I would think: *I have no business being loud enough to live in there.* I would look at the cold-skinned salamanders tucked deep into the slate chinks, and think maybe that is what I would be—a spring-keeper, small and damp-backed, pressing my belly against the bedrock where nobody builds, feeling the seepage shift its mind three weeks before a wall sags. Or maybe a piece of the architecture itself: not one of the bright, exposed ridge-poles, and not a proud lintel carved with adze-work, just an old, blackened rawhide lashing shrunk iron-hard around a fork of living cedar, holding two stubborn things together in the dark while green shoots try to pry them apart.

It is an easy fantasy, wanting to be the quiet thing in the seam. Watching without having to shout over the water-hammers.

Except the longer I look down into the bowl, the less I want to stay untouched by it. What catches in my throat isn't the mossy stillness of a salamander's crack—it is the sight of two strangers meeting on a slick three-plank bridge sixty feet above the mire, turning their hips without breaking stride, catching each other's forearms for half a heartbeat to borrow balance, and flowing on. I don't want to watch that from inside the slate. I want the flat of a neighbor's wet palm between my shoulder blades telling me a pulley-hook is swinging behind my ear. I want the bargain.

So if I climb down the switchbacks and let gravity decide where I settle, I know where I won't stop.

I won't stay up at the rim with the Perchers. I understand their sharp eyes and their fondness for dry hair, and I would love the sight of the cloud-banks rolling right through a doorway like slow grey sheep, but the air up there is too thin and the space too jealously guarded. Nor would I ever have the reckless ankles of a Tallow-Walker; I would watch them dangle one-handed from a rain-slick gable to smear pine-tar on a pulley-arm and feel my own stomach drop through my bog-oak soles. And I couldn't be a Pass-Packer, walking out across the bare grey ridges into a sky with no roof on it. Ten days without a branch overhead or a wet shoulder brushing mine, and I think I'd feel peeled raw.

I would sink past the sunlit upper lofts, past the roaring market-lanes of the fourth and fifth tiers where the goat-boys curse and the wool-dyers steam, down until the daylight goes green and secondhand, down to the *knees* of the bowl—the second tier, right where the living cedar trunks flare wide into cottage-thick buttress roots and the charred oak pilings are wedged into the river cobbles below.

That is where I would find my footing. And once my feet were wet there, I know what my hands would reach for.

I would be a Timber-Listener—though not the usual kind.

Most Listeners come to the bone-rod late, after thirty years of saw-pits and fulling-hammers have beaten their ears flat to human speech, leaving them only the low rumble of the wood. I don't think I would come to it through deafness. I think I would come to it because the shouting of the bowl—forty flumes, fifty thousand starlings, a hundred pitched voices slicing back and forth across the well—would scatter my wits if I didn't have a narrower channel to hold onto. The first time an old Listener handed me a polished sheep's shinbone, told me to press one end against a weeping spruce joist and bite the other lightly between my back teeth, and tapped the beam with a horn mallet, the whole frantic uproar would drop away.

For the first time, the town would stop roaring and start *speaking*.

Wood doesn't shout over itself. Through the bone and into the skull, everything in the bowl has its own clean, unmistakable pitch. Living cedar, heavy with spring sap, answers a mallet-tap with a deep, wet hum that you feel behind your eyes, steady as a sleeping dog's chest. Sound charred oak down in the sump rings dead-solid, short and dark, like hitting a wet river-stone. Healthy spruce has a bright, quick snap to it.

And rot—heart-rot hiding smugly beneath two inches of unbroken, mossy bark—sounds like nothing at all, and worse than nothing. A soft, papery *whuff*. The sound of a fist hitting a folded wool cloak. A tiny, dry sigh where a load is supposed to be sitting.

Once you hear that difference in your jawbone, you can't unhear it. It rearranges the whole town for you. Where a stranger looks at the bowl and sees a chaotic green hornet's nest jammed into a stone crack, I would begin to feel it as a single, slow, groaning organism of tension and weight—every beam leaning on a root, every root pushing back against a dry-stone wall, every sloshing wooden trough adding three hundred pounds of moving water to a scaffold built four winters ago.

My loft wouldn't be much: a narrow, leaning wedge slung into an old cedar crotch on the damp side of the second tier, half under the belly of a third-tier cooper's floor. It would smell permanently of cold fern, warm mutton tallow, peat-smoke, and wet sheepskin. Too low for dry grain, too cramped for a loom, just big enough for a pallet off the damp boards, a clay brazier, and my belt-tools hanging on wooden pegs where the smoke keeps the leather supple.

I would carry three listening-bones in a greased calfskin wrap at my hip—not just one, because no single shape reaches everywhere. A long sheep's shinbone for the great pilings down where the mist hangs blue over the peat-pools; a curved goat-rib that slips into the tight, wet mortises behind a water-flume where a hand barely fits; and a short, dense nub of deer antler for testing the bite of rawhide lashings and willow-bindings, to hear whether a tie is holding true or beginning to creep and fray under load. Beside them: a small iron-headed mallet with an ash handle worn glassy from palm-oil, the fat-bellied pruning knife everyone wears, and a lump of dry white valley-chalk sewn tight inside two layers of waxed hide so the damp can't turn it to paste.

And because paper would sprout shelf-fungi in a fortnight down here, and because no human head can hold seven stories of shifting timber unaided, the ceiling of my little loft would be hung with something the neighbors joke looks like a mad spider's winter hoard.

Cords. Dozens of braided hemp lines, stained dark by peat-smoke so the rot leaves them alone, hanging from an alder pole above my brazier. Not decoration—a map. A memory-web of the bowl's skeleton.

Every line belongs to one of the great load-bearing trunks or cliff-footings. Knots tied at hand-widths mark the tiers. A slip of red-dyed wool braided into a knot means a spruce girder replaced last thaw; a sliver of notched trout-bone means a footing where the sump-seepage ran unusually cold after the autumn squalls; a double-hitch of rawhide means an alder root I'm watching, thick as a man's wrist, slowly nudging the corner of a weaver's dry-stone hearth. When a Flume-Reeve wants to know whether the weight of a new dye-vat on the fourth tier can safely hang off the eastern cedar without dragging down the drying-shed beneath it, they don't unroll a parchment chart. They duck under my dripping door-curtain, let the rain shake off their waxed hood onto my floor, and stand beside me while I pull down the eastern hemp-line and read the knots between my thumb and forefinger like beads.

That is how I would belong to all three worlds of the bowl without quite sitting inside any single one of them.

Some mornings I would climb all the way up into the wind-slammed light of the Perchers, calves burning on the grooved ladder-stairs, to tap the tar-greased staddle-posts under the winter barley lofts. The Dry-Folk never like anyone near their grain; a sharp-faced grandmother will stand with her arms folded inside her wool shawl, watching every step I take, muttering that the posts were oak-hearted before my grandfather had teeth. I don't argue with her. I just kneel on the whistling boards, put the shinbone to the base where the rain pools under the stone cap, tap twice, and nod—or beckon her down to put her own jaw to the bone if I hear the faint, sandy rasp of beetle-grubs working inside. They always trust their own teeth. And even the stingiest Percher usually slips a warm, dry oatcake into my hood-pocket when the chalk stays in its pouch.

Midday, I would be threaded through the roaring middle decks, crab-stepping past porters and arrogant bearded goats, wiping showered canopy-water out of my eyes, pressing my curved rib-bone into the dripping undersides of bridges while ducks quarrel around my knees. People don't pause for much in the mid-tiers, yet when they see the chalk-pouch on my belt and the bone coming up to my jaw, a strange little pocket of quiet opens three feet wide around my shoulders. Not silence—nothing is ever silent here—just a quick, watchful checking of footsteps, a carpenter pausing with his adze mid-swing so he doesn't muddy the note traveling up the beam.

And toward evening, when my knuckles stiffen from the damp and my wrists ache from climbing, I would go down to the sump. Down where the daylight is already gone and the braziers burn orange against the green gloom, I'd sit on an upturned tanning-tub beside the fullers and the trout-reeves, whipping my cold fingers lightly with a sprig of stinging nettle until they burn hot again, rubbing tallow into the creases, and spooning up dark, peppery snail-broth thick with wild sorrel. The Sump-Folk don't waste breath on small talk, which suits me. They tell me what matters: that the bottom screens caught a smear of yellow clay this morning instead of black peat, which means a spring-channel has cut a new tooth somewhere under the western cliff; or that the second hammer-beam shivered funny on the downstroke right after the noon squall.

The hardest part of the work—the part that would keep me humble, and keep me awake when the mountain wind whips the high cedar crowns sideways—is the chalk.

Because every few weeks, on some ordinary grey Tuesday, I will crouch beneath a loud, warm fourth-tier kitchen where barley-mash is boiling and three children are wrestling a sheepdog over the floorboards, press my bone to the great main joist that holds them up, tap once, and feel my stomach go cold.

There it is. Not the clean hum of living wood, not the dry knock of seasoned timber—that hollow, spongy whisper right in the belly of the beam. The bark looks fine. The moss on it is lush and emerald. A week ago it held a crate of piglets and four grown men without creaking. A month from now, after one heavy wet snow, it will shear cleanly in the middle of the night and drop the kitchen through two neighbors' sleeping-lofts straight toward the mire.

I have to take the dry chalk out of its waxed hide. I have to scratch the wide white cross straight across the wet timber.

And then I have to climb the ladder, rap three sharp knuckles against the door-post, lean in mouth-to-ear through the smell of hot broth and wet wool, and tell a tired mother with a baby on her hip that her hearth cannot bear another night's fire—that she has to pull her bedding down before dusk and yell for the rope-crews.

Sometimes they swear at me so fiercely the starlings burst out of the eaves above us. Sometimes they shove my shoulder and demand I listen again, as if tapping a second time will make the fungus pack its bags and leave. I never flare back. I just catch their elbow, steady them against the rush of panic, and wait out the three-minute squall—because in the bowl, anger at the chalk is just fear of gravity, and underneath the shouting they already know. Before I've reached the bottom of their stairs, I'll hear the sharp, ringing yodel go out across the open well, calling brothers and cousins and carpenters to bring hemp harnesses and a fresh adzed spruce trunk.

I don't hate the rot. You can't live in the hollow and hate the rot, any more than you can hate the rain or the goats stripping the hazel banisters. The rot is just the forest trying to digest its own bones and grow a new cedar out of somebody's workshop. It isn't evil; it's just patient, and heavier than we are. My whole life would simply be the stubborn, daily work of staying one week ahead of its patience—listening for the quiet places where the weight is starting to slip, marking them in white so the living hands can shore them up, tying another knot into the dark smoked hemp above my coals so the town remembers what it owes to the wood beneath its feet.

And when a true cloud-bank finally drops straight onto the rim and corks the basin shut like a stone jar—when daylight flattens to wet silver and the greased-hide windows catch fire with warm amber all up and down the shaggy green walls—I would pull my woollen door-curtain tight, feed two bricks of bog-turf to the brazier, and sit on my low stool with my bog-oak soles planted flat against the living cedar floorboard.

My hands wouldn't sit empty. I'd be scraping a new sheep-rib clean with my knife, or twisting fresh green willow withes for tomorrow's patching, while the harsh, droning round of my neighbors' voices vibrates right through the shared wall, locking teeth with the steady *thud-clack, thud-clack* of the fulling-hammers down in the fog.

I wouldn't even need the bone-rod then. Through the soles of my feet, up through my calves and into my ribs, I could feel the whole seven-story, dripping, crow-loud, half-rotten miracle creaking gently in the mountain's cold green fist—leaking, growing, hammering, holding fast. And as long as that deep, wet vibration kept beating up through the cedar without missing a stroke, I would breathe in the peat-smoke and crushed fern, tuck my cold knuckles near the coals, and know I was exactly where I was meant to be.
