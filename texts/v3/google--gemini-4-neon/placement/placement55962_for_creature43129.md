At first, I think I would be up on the roofs.

It is the easiest place to picture from the outside—that sudden breaking open of the lanes into an ocean of fluted ochre, the thrill of waiting beneath a thorny mesquite hedge for a mile-wide shadow to sweep across the plain, and then running with the ribbon of messengers across braided boughs and woven arcades while the hawks wheel uselessly in the glare beyond the cloud’s edge. There is something immediately tempting in being one of the swift ones: cheek-pouches heavy with rare fungal plugs and resin-wrapped seeds, leaping from chimney-lip to chimney-lip, turning a continent of walled-off quarters into a single country for an afternoon.

Yet the longer I sit inside the quiet of this basin, the more clearly I know I wouldn’t stay out there.

I would probably have made one long run in my youth—leaving my birth-spire beneath a steady westward shadow, feeling for the first time how terrifyingly wide the open sky is when there is no succulent curtain overhead and no clay wall at your elbow. I can feel the dry sting of sunlit air against my flanks, the scramble across unfamiliar thatched roofs while cinnamon weavers flare their wings in silent annoyance, and the sheer, breathless relief of plunging headfirst down a stranger’s chimney when the high wind finally begins to shred the cloud overhead. Hanging there in the dark vertical throat, knees braced, wedge-pad locked against rough stippled mortar, tapping out my lineage to invisible listeners two galleries down—that moment, not the sprint across the plain, is where my pulse would settle.

I would not be a runner. Nor would I belong all the way down in the blind, cool sub-levels where the pale workers tick like pouring millet in the dark. I need to know the wide sky exists, even if I rarely stand beneath it. I need to feel the weather turning ten miles out.

So I find myself halfway up.

---

Not in the center of a grand, symmetrical draft-house, either. I think I would end up in an awkward seam between two older quarters—a crooked junction where a massive, ancient dryland tree has driven its buttress roots right through the shoulder of a fluted ventilation stack, tilting the whole tower a few degrees off true.

Because of that tilt, the masonry around it is strange. Two vaulted arcades pinch together at odd angles; an abandoned lower gallery opens sideways into the main shaft; a heavy drapery of jade-green succulents spills down the western face, keeping the junction cool an hour past noon. Most of my kin find the acoustics there irritating. The crooked flue doesn’t carry a clean, predictable draw on its own; vibrations arrive from strange directions, bouncing off the gnarled root-knots so that a knuckle-tap from a hundred paces east sounds closer than a burrowing mammal shifting seed-husks ten feet beneath the street plates.

That is precisely why I would stay.

I would be one of the dwellers, yes—there is no other body in this basin that fits the way I listen. Hound-long, low-slung, dusted with my own pale chalk so that when I flatten myself into the angle behind a buttress root even a speckled lizard might scuttle across my forearm without noticing the warmth. My hands would tell anyone who touched them in the dark what kind of life I lead: the broad spade-nails worn smooth and shallow rather than chipped from prying mineral crust or pinning stray snakes; the bare, heavily veined leather pads at the heels of my wrists kept scrupulously clean, rubbed every morning against the polished inner curve of a dried mesquite pod so no grain of marl dulls them.

If my draft-house had a name for what I do, it wouldn’t sound grand. Something closer to *lip-keeper*, or *the one who tends the crooked throat*.

Because everything earthen here wants to close.

That is the thing that grips me most about living in a city built by ten thousand blind jaws. They are not enemies, and they are not allies; they are simply an endless, upward patience. Leave a beveled chimney-rim alone for a moon and they will push pale glistening pellets right across the edge, thickening the clay, shifting the pitch until the afternoon hum goes flat and muddy. Leave a spy-slit untended and it narrows to a crease, then a seam, then wall. Every season the spires grow a hand’s breadth taller, the pressure in the linked vaults shifts, a root swells inside a lower shaft, and the whole quiet geography of air and vibration drifts out of alignment.

Someone has to notice when a district stops breathing cleanly.

---

My mornings begin before the sun touches the basin floor, wedged high inside the crooked flue with my knees locked against one stippled wall and the calloused pad at the base of my spine braced against the other. My chalked hide holds the leftover warmth of yesterday’s clay while the clear night sky pulls cold air straight down the shaft past my shoulders.

There is no hurry in that hour. You cannot rush dew. I hang utterly still with my translucent lids drawn half-shut, feeling the faintest prickle move down the grooved, hollow quills laid flat along my neck and outer forearms. Down below, the city is holding its breath—no wind yet, only the slow, microscopic ticking of cooling earth and the distant, ceaseless millet-sound behind the walls.

When the rim above me pales from indigo to bone-grey, I bend my neck and draw the fluted quills slowly between my broad front teeth, combing the night’s thin sheen onto my dry, brush-tipped tongue. It tastes of chalk, mineral dust, and open sky. Never enough to feel like a drink—just enough to loosen the throat, clear the blood, and wake the body for another dry day.

Then I descend into the junction and lay my left wrist flat against the clay.

This is the hour I love most, before the sun heats the western faces and sets the drafts rising. Through that single bare palm, the whole neighborhood opens like a map drawn in pulse and pressure. I feel the steady double-lane vibration of harvester insects pouring out of the basal arches toward the terraces. I feel a long-eared runner thump a single sharp warning into the packed lane-floor fifty paces south—a hawk already riding the first thin thermal—and the immediate, soft hush as every bolt-hole along the avenue empties. Deeper down, beneath the cracked street plates, I feel the faint, rhythmic rustle of my kin waking in the lower chambers, tapping brief morning greetings up through the shared masonry: *dry dawn; southern vault sweet; two young ones ready to test the upper arcade.*

And beneath all of that, I listen for the mistakes.

Halfway up the eastern branch-flue, the tuft of pale weaver-down I pinned to three dried euphorbia thorns hangs dead limp when the first faint air should already be stirring it. Somewhere inside the dark bend, the blind workers have been busy overnight.

So I climb—spiraling easily up the narrow vertical shaft, bracing with knees and hips so both hands stay free—until my fingers find the fresh, still-damp ridge of mortar bulging inward from a seam. I don’t fight them. You cannot fight ten thousand mandibles that never sleep. Instead, I scrape the soft new lip back with my blunt nails before the sun can bake it to crust, nibble the inner bevel smooth with my front teeth until the curve feels right against my tongue, and then chew a mouthful of bitter silvery sage into a dry, stinging paste.

Carefully, patiently, I smear the bitter line along the edge of the opening.

Tonight, when the invisible builders push their wet clay upward from inside the wall, their antennae will meet that sharp, hated boundary and turn aside, walling cleanly *around* the shape I left them. It strikes me sometimes, hanging there in the dark with bitter sage drying on my knuckles, what a strange craft this is: spending your days maintaining an emptiness. In a world that is always extruding, filling, sealing, and erasing, the only way to keep a voice carrying—or a cool breeze reaching a nursery chamber—is to keep persuading the city to leave a hollow inside itself.

---

By midday, when the glare locks the lanes into shadow and even the pale lizards press flat into the cracks, I climb out of the throat and onto the thick, shaded shoulder of the gnarled dryland tree knotted into our spire.

Hidden beneath a curtain of fleshy jade succulents and thorny mesquite limbs, I have my other work.

Clay forgets by tomorrow. Living bark keeps hold for three hundred years.

I am not the eldest of our draft-house—my nails are still solid, my listening-pads still sharp enough to read a faint double-tap three towers away—so I am not the one who settles territorial deadlocks in the dark flues or decides when to wall a tawny hunter out of the lower alleys. Yet the elders let me sit with the tree, because my hands are steady and I do not hurry over the old marks.

I close my eyes and draw the bare heel of my wrist slowly across the tough grey trunk, following the pale, swollen welts left by ancestors whose bones have long since been drunk back up through these very roots. Under my palm, three centuries lie in raised lines: crescents, twin slashes, clustered dots rubbed with clean euphorbia resin generations ago so they healed bone-white and permanent. Here is the line marking the great dust-years, when two deep shafts collapsed and three draft-houses merged around this single taproot. Here is the cadence of a truce struck with a quarter seven miles south. Here, near a fork where the trunk has thickened awkwardly, an old warning about a sour mineral vein has stretched and warped over decades until a young one’s wrist might misread it altogether.

Sometimes two of the small dust-colored young ones crouch beside me on the bough, laying their narrow wrists just behind mine as I trace the ridges, learning to read the tree by touch in the dry afternoon hush. Every once in a while, when news arrives from far across the basin that is too heavy to leave to clay and memory alone, an elder taps consent against the bough, and I lean in to bite a shallow new crescent into smooth young bark—just deep enough to bleed clear, never enough to wound the sap-flow—and rub pale euphorbia resin gently into the mark so the next century will find it swollen and readable beneath its own palms.

---

Late afternoon is when my crooked junction comes fully alive.

The sun strikes hard against the western clay, baking the porous walls until the air inside the outer chimneys warms and lifts toward the sky. Down at street level, the basal arches begin to inhale cool air from the shaded alleys, threading it through the deep fungal vaults and pulling it steadily upward past my knees. The tufts of weaver-down on my euphorbia thorns straighten and quiver. Across the beveled lip I trimmed that morning, the rising draft catches and holds a faint, steady pitch—scarcely louder than dry sand sliding across a palm, yet cleanly tuned.

From the upper slit beneath the mesquite hedge, I watch the pale flat-bottomed clouds assemble out of nothing high above the plain.

When a true, heavy shadow glides over our district, the silence above the canopy flickers into motion. Lean runners spill across the braided branches and woven bridges, their chalky flanks bright against the moving dark, pausing for half a breath at the crest of my crooked chimney to strike brisk knuckle-sentences straight into the clay rim before racing on to stay inside the shade.

I keep my left palm pressed to the inner wall and catch every word as it drops down the shaft: *drought-hardy comb moving west; two young adults seeking a southern draft-house; broad shadow holding steady twelve miles out; hunter with a pale shoulder working the salt-pan trench.*

And once or twice a season, the high wind betrays them. A cloud tears apart too soon overhead; blinding noon-bright glare drops straight back onto the open roofs; a broad-winged hawk tilts its wings in the thermal—and a stranger plunges headfirst down *my* open chimney, claws scrambling briefly before knees and wedge-pad catch hard against the rough mortar just above my head.

In those moments, the crooked flue is at its best.

I do not challenge them. I feel their ribs heaving through the shared clay, hear the rapid, breathless tap of their knuckles pleading lineage and sanctuary against the wall beside my ear. I answer slowly, deliberately, pressing my wrist flat so they feel the steadiness in the cadence: *safe; hold your wedge; wait for dusk.* If their throat is dry from the run, I reach up in the dark and pass a pinch of bitter marl and a cool shred of fungal comb from my cheek-pouch into their blunt fingers.

We hang there together for an hour or two, two silhouettes who have never seen each other’s faces—who have never even seen our own—sharing the cool rising draft while the sun bakes the outside of the spire. We speak in quiet double-taps through the clay between our shoulders. They tell me what the chimneys sound like ten miles east, where the subsoil runs paler and the weavers build their grass curtains twice as deep; I tell them which arcades ahead have been walled shut in a local feud and where a newly bridged mesquite arch offers safe crossing tomorrow.

Then, as the afternoon light lowers to copper and the updraft reaches its cleanest pull, I shift down to the beveled side-aperture, press my open jaws against the smooth clay lip, fill the pleated sac beneath my throat, and let out a slow, trembling breath.

The crooked tower catches it. What leaves my throat as a whisper enters the rising column of warm air and becomes architecture—a low, bone-felt hum that threads through every linked gallery and rises from a dozen surrounding organ-pipe vents as a slight, unmistakable turn in the wind’s sigh. Nothing loud enough to startle a single weaver from its hanging eaves. Just clear enough to carry three hundred yards across the ochre roofs:

*The western shadow holds; the southern trench is watched; a stranger rests in the crooked throat; the draft runs clear.*

---

I know how it will end, too, and there is no terror in it—only a quiet fittingness.

Years from now, the bare leather heels of my wrists will begin to thicken into horn. The subtle double-taps from three towers away will blur into a flat, distant murmur, and I won’t be able to tell by touch alone whether a bevel is drifting sharp or flat. My shoulder-quills will grow sparse and split along their grooves, holding too little pre-dawn damp to carry me cleanly through the baking afternoons. My blunt nails will wear down close to the quick.

When that dry stillness settles into my joints, I won’t stay up in the crooked flue waiting to shrivel in the glare. My kin—maybe one of the young ones whose small wrists once followed mine across the grey tree-bark—will press their shoulders to mine in the dark and guide me down. Past the street plates, past the smooth seed-runs of the burrowing mammals, all the way down into the cool vaults forty feet beneath the basin floor, where the great taproot of my crooked tree twists pale and thick through the buried damp clay.

I will curl into an abandoned root-laced alcove with my knees tucked and my worn quills folded flat, feel my kin press cool, damp subsoil gently over my flanks grain by grain, feel the small moist tuft of living fungal comb tucked against my ribs, and hear the last wet seal close behind them before the blind workers weld it seamless by morning. Nothing wasted. Every drop of salt and moisture drawn back into the gardens, lifted forty feet up through dark wood into the jade leaves shading the junction, pushed upward in damp pellets along the rising crests of the spires.

---

Until then, there are the clear nights of the cool season.

When the dust-haze drops away and the stars sit low and sharp above the endless corrugated plain, I climb all the way out of my crooked throat to the unfinished crest of the tallest tower in our quarter. I sit with the rest of our draft-house in a small, silent cluster, knees drawn up, chalked backs turned against the dry chill, listening to miles of sun-baked clay contract in the dark—a faint, endless wave of dry ticks passing across the basin like rain that never falls.

Right beside my knees, along the jagged crown of the spire, a dark band of freshly dampened earth gleams faintly in the starlight, pushed upward millimeter by millimeter from inside by invisible builders who will never see the sky.

The young ones aren’t the only ones who reach for it.

Now and then, I lean forward and lay the bare, sensitive heel of my wrist softly against that dark, unfinished rim—the single place in this entire world where raw water briefly shines beneath the stars. The startling coldness of wet clay meets my palm. I hold it there for one quiet breath, feeling the faint, tireless ticking of ten thousand mandibles working just beneath the surface, leaving one clean, smooth palm-print pressed into the rising tower before I draw back into the dark.

By the time the eastern rim pales and the first cold dew begins to glaze my quills, the blind builders will have laid ten thousand fresh grains over the mark, erasing it completely, lifting the city another millimeter into the sky. And I will slip back down into the crooked throat, clean my wrist on a dried seed-husk, chew a pinch of bitter sage, and keep a small piece of the air open for one more day.
