If I tried to picture myself up on the wind-planed flats first, I would be lying to you.

It is easy to want the bright, ferocious parts of this place. Standing on that fluted white lip, watching a climber eighty meters up lean sideways into a screaming eddy while violet fire washes across her pale amber eyes, part of me wants to say: *there.* Up where the bearing-collar weeps hot graphite-wax, timing my hands to the staggered clatter of the ratchets. Or down in the launching-bay, slicked black from brow to heel, biting down on a leather breathing-bladder while the bellows draws back to fire me twelve kilometers through pitch darkness on a roar of compressed air.

I would watch those people with my ribs tight in admiration. I would press my brow to a Rider’s before the breech sealed and feel the bright, reckless hum in her collarbone, and wish I were built narrow and quick enough for it.

Then I would turn away from the launching-bay, walk past the warm central furnace-dome where the emulsions are whipped and the seed-cakes bake, and keep walking until the vaulted ceiling dropped lower and the violet-green corona loops hung dimmer and farther apart.

Wait—I would pause first at the Listener’s alcove. I would pause there a long time.

That hammock of braided copper and hide, hung where three main vascular arteries meet, pulls at something deep in me. To clip my grounding-braids to the floor-pins, lower my inner lids, and let two hundred kilometers of highland speak straight into my palms and heels—weather fronts bruising the western horizon, a calf dropped in a sheltered gully, a pressure-poem tapped out slowly during a stretch of steady wind—that is close. Almost right. For a season or two, I might even sit apprentice beside the Listener, learning to read the jagged stutter of a slipping crown from the calm, spaced clicks of a barter over roasted seed-meal.

Yet even there, I would feel a slight restlessness behind my sternum. The Listener’s Seat belongs to the hour. It belongs to what is happening *now*, to the urgent pulse that cannot wait three strokes before being answered. And my attention has always moved slower than that, downward and sideways, toward the seams that hold the conversation up rather than the news traveling through it.

So I would keep going down the sloping cork galleries, past the places where fifty voices click and hum together, into the quiet, warmer margins where the warren ends and the wild, blind plumbing of the continent begins.

That is where I would find myself.

*

If you came looking for me, you would have to crouch.

You would find me half-folded inside a narrow, egg-shaped blister of living tissue that is not yet a room—maybe twice my own length across, warm as an oven-stone, smelling sharply of raw pine-resin and heated chalk. My iron-pith ballast boots would be left outside the diaphragm; my knees would be smeared dark with graphite-wax, my grounding-tail clipped to a tarnished copper peg wedged into a root-seam near my heel.

I would not be holding a knife or a chisel. Against the pale, slightly yielding curve of the ceiling, I would have only the flat of my left palm and the thin parchment drum behind my jaw, listening so still you might think I had fallen asleep.

I am growing a hall that will not be finished for nine years.

Down here, you cannot force the crust to give you space. Cut into it carelessly and the resin-glands panic, foaming over the wound until the whole junction sets into a dense knot of scar-wood that neither pressure nor blade will open again. Everything has to be persuaded. Three seasons ago, we plugged the far throat of two minor conduits with cured pith and bled a hair-thin thread of static down from a copper hedge forty feet above, warming the corky strata just enough to make the fiber remember how to yield. Ever since, the bellows have been doing the labor for us—*thump, hiss, thump*—leaning steadily against the softened walls, nudging the cavity wider by the thickness of a fingernail every few weeks.

My work is simply to listen to the stretch.

Through the bone of my jaw, the cork speaks in tiny, dry ticks—so faint they disappear entirely if anyone speaks aloud in the adjacent gallery. A steady, evenly spaced ticking means the walls are inflating clean. A high, papery shiver along the windward rib means the current is running too hot and drying the resin before the tissue can thin; if I miss that shiver for ten days, the next cross-gust on the surface will spike the bellows-stroke and split the ceiling like an overbaked seed-pod.

So I shift a hardwood shim by half a thumb’s width. I scrape a pinch of oxidized crust off a grounding-pin to bleed a fraction of the voltage away. I rub warm bearing-wax into a stubborn crease where an old root-knot refuses to give. Then I press my cheek back to the warm wood and wait twenty strokes to hear whether the shiver eases.

By the time this chamber is forty feet across and cured hard as seasoned oak—wide enough for a new family to hang their salt-filaments and teach their children the staggered beat of the plateau—my own knuckles will probably be stiff with leached silica. I will sit by its wall with palms grown too heavy to crawl into the smaller blisters. That does not bother me. In a place where the wind planes stone to flour, the only thing worth making is a hollow that stays open after your hands leave it.

That is the slow half of my work. The other half happens in the unlit arteries, where the air does not wait for anyone.

A continent-sized pipe-organ drifts out of tune. Every time a spire loses its crown ten kilometers upwind, or a deep furnace cools and shrinks, or a canyon wall sloughs away a slab of ancient pith, the back-pressure shifts across dozens of subterranean branches. Most people in the warren barely notice—the salt-lamps dip briefly toward green and steady back to violet; the floor’s thrum drops a fraction of a tone.

The Riders notice.

When a Rider shoots down a pitch-black main conduit at breaking speed, she has nothing to steer by except the whistling pitch of each side-branch flashing past her silica-horn helmet. Each junction has a reed—a carved tongue of petrified pith wedged into a port just off the main throat—tuned to a triple note every child learns before they lose their first teeth. If a branch drifts half a step flat, a Rider listening in the roar can mistake the fourth eastern fork for the fifth, flare her iron-lignin elbow-spurs three seconds late, and slam into a sealed bulkhead with enough force to shatter bone.

So once a season, when the wind settles into a steady northern draw, I strap a bundle of seasoned pith-reeds and a curved horn scraper flat against my ribs, grease my shoulders and elbows with bearing-wax, and crawl out past the last leather diaphragm into the dark.

There is no violet light out there. Only the slick, pale polish of the vascular walls—smooth as oiled bone from centuries of rushing quartz-dust—and the cool, rattling tick of black segmented pipe-runners scattering ahead of my knuckles.

You learn to count the bellows in your teeth. *Thump—hiss—thump.* Between the end of one exhaust and the gathering swell of the next, there are four heartbeats of slack air. That is all the time you get to move forward, lock your heels and shoulder-blades hard into the curve of the throat, and feel with your fingertips for the carved wooden lip of the junction-reed.

Then the floor kicks. The pressurized surge hits your face like a flat wooden board—warm, dry, smelling of crushed rock and hot bark—and the reed beside your ear shrieks its note straight into your skull.

You do not hear it with your outer ears; the rush is too loud for that. You feel it in your collarbone and the bridge of your nose, whether the harmonic sits clean and glassy or carries a ragged burr where grit has pitted the wood. In the next four heartbeats of slack, working wholly by touch, I shave a ribbon of pith thinner than a dry leaf from the reed’s tongue, blow the dust clear with a short puff through my nostrils, and brace again for the kick.

Once, deep in a western arterial pipe that skirts close to the oldest gorges, I spent half a day chasing a strange bright overtone I could not scrape out of a reed. No matter how cleanly I pared the pith, every time the bellows surged, the tunnel rang with a clear, high, faintly metallic hum—like a copper wire plucked inside solid oak.

I lay still through three full strokes, pressing my brow-ridge directly against the polished bark beside the port, and finally understood.

The reed was true. The note was coming from the wall itself.

Two paces into the surrounding crust, sealed seamlessly inside the silica-hardened wood of the canyon terrace, lay an elder laid to rest generations before my grandmother’s braids were first plaited. The plateau had soaked their skeleton through with mineral flour and tarnished copper while they lived, and cured pale resin glass-hard around their ribs when their breath stopped. Now, a century later, their chest was a tuning fork buried in the living bark, answering every surge of the subterranean bellows with a quiet, bronze-clear resonance.

I did not try to muffle it or move the junction. I put my horn scraper back in my pouch, pulled a fresh blank of pith from my ribs, and carved the reed until its own whistle locked cleanly into a fifth beneath that buried hum. The next Rider who flew down that throat in total darkness rode straight home on a chord made by an ancestor’s bones and a sliver of shaved wood.

There is one smaller craft I keep tucked beside the deepest furnace-dome, where the air is hottest and smells sharply of roasted stone.

Almost everyone in the warren uses the salt-filaments—someone has to strip the copper-vein, dip the loops in mineral brine cooked from bedrock-flour, and throttle the grounding-shunts so the light stays a calm emerald-violet instead of flaring white and igniting the cork-dust. I help with that when a storm surges the collector ridges.

But tucked behind the main furnace-blister, in a cramped shelf no wider than my shoulders, I keep three hair-thin coils of cold-drawn copper threaded directly into the furnace’s deepest mineral vents.

Nothing on this plateau makes sense to an outsider until they see those coils. Beneath the crust, where pressurized dust mills ancient rock and steady electrical currents crack the stone-flour apart, tiny ghost-traces of water still hide—locked inside mineral crystals since before the highland ever lifted above the clouds. When the furnace runs at peak voltage, an invisible breath of that ancient hydrate rises with the exhaust. If my copper coils are braced just right—fed a whisper of cold air siphoned down from a shaded canyon-fissure forty feet above—the vapor touches the cool metal and remembers how to be liquid.

It is the slowest harvest in the world. Weeks pass with nothing but dry metallic dust on the coil. Then, on a still morning after a high-voltage dust-front, a single bead no larger than a spire-seed gathers at the bottom bend of the wire, trembles with the *thump—hiss—thump* of the floor, and drops with a tiny, impossible tick into the mouth of a stoppered silica-glass vial.

A whole season for a thumb’s width of bitter, iron-tasting dew.

I do not think I would be trusted with the vials themselves—the healers and the elders keep the locked rack near the warm wall, measuring out two drops for a scorched climber’s tongue or wetting a fingertip for a newborn’s dry lips. My part ends at the coil. Keeping the temperature balanced between the furnace’s roar and the cold canyon draft. Wiping the mineral soot from the wire without snapping it. Watching a world stripped dry to the bone yield one clean drop through sheer, quiet geometry.

*

I do go up to the surface, now and then. You cannot live entirely in the violet dark and still understand what you are stepping down.

When a growing chamber needs its current shifted, or when a western ridge has been stripped clean by a storm and we need to peg a fresh line of copper-vein stakes along the gully lip, I buckle on the heavy iron-pith ballast boots, lacquer my braids tight, and climb out through the snapping leather lip of the valve.

Up there, the world hits me fresh every time—too bright, too thin, too loud. My chest pulls hard for air; my glassy inner lids flick shut with dry little clicks against the stinging chaff; I move hunched and stubborn, nowhere near as graceful as the climbers who stride straight into a spire’s screaming shadow. Ten kilometers across the blinding white terraces, a rotor-crown blurs into a shimmering bronze disc and trails its yellow plume downwind while a violet arc cracks like a rifle shot against the scrub.

I crouch low in the oxidized shadow of a gully lip, drive my copper stake into the bone-white floor with a silica-horn mallet, and wait for the little blue tongue of spark to jump cleanly from my grounding-tail to the bark. Before I climb back down, I always stay still for one long minute—knees bent against the shear, bare palm pressed flat to the sun-scoured pavement.

Above my back, the hemisphere roars without knowing or caring that I exist.

Beneath my hand, through six feet of dry cork and braided root-fiber, I can feel the faint, steady vibration of the reed I shaved last week, singing clean in the dark—and tucked beneath that, the slow, warm pulse of a half-grown room taking one more microscopic breath toward becoming a shelter.

Then the pressure-valve claps open thirty paces downwind, venting its brief white plume of stone-flour. I haul myself up on my weighted soles, lean into the cold glare, and go back down to the work of keeping the tolerances wide enough for a human voice to live inside them.
