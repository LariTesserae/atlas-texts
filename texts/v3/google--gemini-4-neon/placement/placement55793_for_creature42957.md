At first, I think I would be up in the indigo.

It is the cleanest temptation the plateau offers: to be one of the cruciform gliders, hollow-boned and wide as a room, riding the thermal rivers three thousand feet above the glare without ever spending the breath to beat a wing. From up there, the whole continental crown reads like a single calm sentence—ivory stone, silver dust-veils, dark brushstrokes of oiled emerald running straight along the buried seams. Nothing up there has to hurry or explain itself.

Wait a few minutes inside that image, though, and it begins to feel too cold, too purely hungry. A glider never steps out of the sun. It never smells cedar-resin and crushed chalk beneath the vaults, never feels the sudden vault-cool drop of a terrace shadow, never knows what is happening under the green ribbons except as a shifting speck of white enamel that might mean food.

So I drop down to the stone—and my second guess is the wind-runner. I imagine strapping curved grazer-enamel to wide flat feet, threading hollow wing-spars through the pleated sleeves of a silver-backed cloak, leaning back into the late-morning push off the high domes. Skimming at thirty miles an hour over fluted limestone, one wrist turned steadily skyward to throw three sharp flashes of sun into the upper air, leaving twin shallow ribbons in the silica flour that the wind erases ten seconds behind me.

That is closer. There is joy in an economy that clean—motion without noise, travel without roads.

Yet even as I picture the flats rushing past beneath my runners, I notice where my eyes keep pulling. Not toward the forty-mile horizon. Toward the edge of the dark green ribbon ahead, where a single finger-length sliver of clear selenite has been wedged vertically into a hairline crack of limestone, catching the noon glare at an angle geology never meant. Toward the small, quiet impossibilities: three neat silk stitches holding a wind-split frond rib closed, sealed with a glossy bead of amber resin.

I am not meant for the crossing. I am meant for what waits at the end of it.

***

If you looked for me on the plateau, you would not find me at the widest, lushest middle of one of the great forty-mile troughs, nor out on the dizzying western rim where a lone traveler once in a decade stares down at the bruised grey sea of the Heavy Air.

You would find me near the tapering eastern tail of a smaller vein—an aging seam, perhaps eleven miles long, where the buried hydrated gypsum is slowly thinning out after six hundred years of root-acid and green weight. Here the arching malachite canopies are a little lower, the papery husk-floor a little thicker, the bronze-veined tiers carrying more dry silver scars. Two miles north across bare, wind-planed pavement, a fresh hairline fissure has only begun to wake: a thin scatter of velvet rosette-cushions and wire-fine roots tasting exposed clay for the first time.

Between the dying tail and the waking crack lies a shallow, circular pan of dolomitic limestone. It is not one of the great solstice basins yet—just a local dish, thirty paces wide, that four generations of neighboring clusters have been grinding smooth by hand with flat rubbing-stones and dry silica flour. It will take another two centuries before it focuses a bedrock pulse cleanly from thirty miles away. Right now, at windless dawn, it reaches maybe twelve.

That is where I live—not alone, because solitude on the crown is a slow leak of warmth and memory, but as one of four in a small cluster whose habits fit together like overlapping ribs.

In this body, the first thing that feels like home is the silence of my own mouth. Keeping the lips closed in a neat, dry seam; taking four deep, measured breaths where a creature of the lower air would gasp twelve; feeling the broad, tiled ribs barely stir beneath a pleated cloak of cured silver cuticle. My skin is dry and matte, pale ivory faintly rose at the collarbones, dusted deliberately with fine gypsum flour along the forearms and throat, a sheen of clear leaf-oil worked into the creases of my knuckles so the thirsty sky takes nothing from me. When I step out from beneath the oiled emerald vaults into the white glare of the open flats, the smoky second lid glides smoothly across each dark amber eye, turning the blinding pavement into clean, readable relief.

For a while I wonder if I am the sun-artisan of our little band—the one who climbs a wind-planed terrace at high noon with a folding parabolic dish of mirror-rubbed canopy-silver, holding tiny bone tongs inside a silent, smokeless bead of pure zenith heat. I love that craft, the way it turns white silica flour into opalescent beads without leaving so much as a scorch mark on the limestone.

No, I realize. My hands are not steady enough for that blinding focal point, and my temperament belongs to the shade. The sun-artisan is my elder companion—the one who sits motionless in the noon blaze while the wind is still low, and who fuses for me, once every few seasons, a thumb-sized lens of clear opalescent glass.

What I do needs the dimness beneath the fronds, the slow chemistry of roots, and the cold stone before dawn.

***

Every adult on the plateau carries a small bundle of etched gypsum plates wrapped in oiled silk inside their cloak: wind-charts, buried alkaline veins, genealogies, records of troughs that dried three hundred years ago and talc ridges newly bared by blowing grit. And at the ends of major troughs, tucked into dry crevices beneath the root-mats and marked by a wedged sliver of selenite, sit the hidden caches where travelers read, copy, and leave fresh slips behind.

Here is the small, quiet problem nobody talks about at the solstice gatherings: dry thumbs are patient erasers.

Even on hardened enamel, and far faster on pale rose gypsum, eight generations of fingers drawing a tablet from oiled silk to trace a fissure-map or check a bloodline will polish the corners round and soften the finest acid-bitten grooves into shallow ghosts. A three-hundred-year memory does not burn on the plateau—there is no fire to take it—and it does not rot in damp air. It simply smooths back into blank stone, one careful touch at a time.

That is where I find myself. I am the one who watches for the smoothing.

In the bright, wind-driven afternoons, when the colossal air-rivers slide down from the high domes and the broad fronds overhead dip and sway in slow swells, combing gold-white gypsum smoke out of the sky with a hush like heavy silk drawn over wood, I sit on a dry bolster of leaf-husks deep in the lower tiers. Across my knees lies a flat board of cured root-wood. Beside me sits a thumb-sized vial of hardened wax-resin holding milky mineral acid weeping-fresh from young root-tips, a stiff plant-bristle trimmed fine as a lash, and the clear silica-glass lens my companion fused at noon.

I lay an old, worn plate of rose gypsum beside a blank one split clean from an exposed seam. Through the small opalescent bead, I follow grooves no wider than spider-silk—lines laid down by someone whose bones have long since been drawn upward into malachite stems. Where a wind-curve around the northern dome has almost vanished under a century of thumb-oil, I tilt the tablet toward a narrow shaft of light piercing the silver-lined canopy until the ghost of the cut catches a shadow. Then I dip the bristle into the milky acid and paint the line fresh onto the new stone.

It does not cut at once. Nothing here happens in haste. Over days, the acid drinks slowly into the pale rose surface, biting clean and sharp where the bristle passed. Between strokes, my mouth stays shut; my ribs rise and fall in their slow four-count. Sometimes, when a lineage-plate is finished, I add a small marginal note no larger than a fingernail—not a story, because stone is too precious for vanity, only what might keep a future band alive: *the eastern tail of the eleven-mile seam yielded bitter sap this winter; the northern crack holds twelve new cushions; a grazer with a notched third dorsal plate shelters beneath the fourth bend.*

When travelers pass our selenite marker and leave a fresh slip in the crevice beneath the root-mat, I am the first to kneel among the wire-fine roots, brush the gypsum drift from the cache, and lay their plates beside ours. I reconcile the maps. If two distant clusters have drifted in their names for a ridge of talc, or if a seam forty miles west has finally stopped tapping back after three quiet seasons, I etch the quiet cross-mark beside its line so no young wind-runner sails out into dead rock expecting a night-vault.

***

My other half of the day belongs to the cold hour before the sun clears the eastern rim.

While the trough is still sealed tight into insulated silver cylinders, I slip out early as the first white light strikes the flat horizon and the rippling, wax-loosening crackle begins to run down the length of the groves. The bare basins glitter with overnight salt-rime, glass-cold and motionless. Before the wind wakes to shave the stone pans clean, our cluster sweeps the feathery mineral frost into oiled sacks—and then I walk the short stretch of fluted pavement to our half-finished listening pan.

I kneel on the unbroken dolomite, lay my enamel-tipped root-wood rod beside my knee, and press my bare cheekbone and the bony heel of one hand flush against the cold rock.

If you have never listened through your own skull, you think the plateau is empty. In thin air, sound dies two paces from whatever makes it. In stone, the continent is one continuous bell.

At first there is only the faint, crisp shimmer of our own trough unrolling half a mile away—millions of taut waxed joints loosening under the rising sun, traveling through the bedrock as a dry, delicate shiver against my jaw. Then, deeper and farther, the real grammar emerges: the heavy, unhurried shift of a solitary grazer stepping onto exposed rock two miles south; a dry *tick* from a stressed limestone ledge warming too fast; and finally, faint as a moth’s tap against enamel, the deliberate pulse from across two basins.

*Tock, tock. Pause. Tock.*

Every cluster has its own cadence—not just the code we all share for mineral seams and storm-winds and glider ridges, but the unconscious signature of the hand holding the rod. The western band taps slightly brisk, crisp and impatient; the old couple who keep the high dome seam leave long, spacious silences between their syllables, as if giving the dolomite time to settle between thoughts. Listening there in the glass-cold morning, eyes half-shut against the rising white-gold glare, I know who is still awake across thirty miles of open stone without ever seeing a silhouette move.

I sit up, set the honed enamel point of my rod flush against the pan’s smooth center, and strike the butt cleanly with my knuckle. Not a long message—just enough to travel through the deep bone of the crown and arrive inside someone else’s temple twelve miles west: *the eastern dish hears you; the salt-rime was rich; the young fissure holds.*

Sometimes, if the wind stays slack for an extra quarter-hour, I take a palm-sized flat dolomite stone from my cloak, scatter a pinch of white silica flour across a slight ripple near the basin’s rim, and rub in slow, even circles. Twenty minutes of patient pressure takes off less than the thickness of a frond-cuticle. I will never hear a thirty-mile pulse from this pan. Even our cluster’s child—now small enough to sleep tucked inside a wax-lined pouch against a parent’s ribs—will only hear maybe sixteen miles when their joints grow stiff. That does not bother me at all. Everything worth having on this plateau was started by hands that never saw it finished.

***

There are two moments in the cycle when I feel the whole settled instrument of this place pull taut and sing—silently, the only way it knows how.

The first is at dusk, just before the thermal wind dies completely, when I sit with the child at the center of our unfinished pan. They are rare and slow-growing, our children, watched over with a quiet, unhurried fierceness. I show them how to relax their jaw against the cooling limestone—because if you clench your teeth, the rock stays mute—and I tap a gentle rhythm three paces away until their stiff pale lashes lift in sudden recognition. Then I wait, motionless beside them, until a distant kin-band answers from beyond the next whale-backed swell, and I watch the child’s dark amber eyes widen as a greeting from ten miles away arrives straight inside their own bones. No breath spent. No shout wasted on the thin air.

The second is in the bright afternoon heat, when the crimson pods split without a sound deep among the bronze-veined lower tiers, and thousands of iridescent spore-bladders lift lazily out of the green aisles into the violet sky.

Most of them I let rise untouched, glittering constellations drifting out over fifty miles of blinding stone to find some empty crack where a new seam waits. Now and then, though, I reach up as a warm, plum-sized sphere floats past my shoulder and catch it lightly by its trailing silken filament. From a tiny pocket inside my silver cloak, I draw a flake of mica no wider than a fingernail-paring—etched weeks ago with three hairline acid grooves so fine you need a silica-glass bead to read them—and knot it carefully onto the strand.

I never etch my name. Names belong to the living cluster, spoken in breath-sparing clicks inside the sealed night-vaults; they are too heavy for the high air. On the mica I put only a small, honest offering: the bearing of our waking northern fissure, the sign of a clear cache, and three tiny notches meaning *the old lines were recopied; someone is still listening at the eastern dish.*

I open my fingers. The sphere hesitates for one slow breath in the cool shade of the canopy, rises through a gap in the oiled malachite fronds, catches the fierce unfiltered sun, and flares once like a dropped star before the continental wind takes it away toward the pale horizon. Ninety-nine of those flecks will fall onto bare limestone and crumble into grit over a century. One, years from now, might snag in a young frond sixty miles off, catch the eye of a walker mending a wind-split rib, and make them pause in the cedar-scented dimness, turning a speck of mica under a glass bead to discover they are not alone on the crown.

***

Toward evening, the amber light stretches mile-long shadows off ridges no taller than a table. The dust-veils settle back onto the polished flats. We gather our salt-sacks, wipe the acid-bristle dry, seal our wax vials, and step deep into the mature stalks of our fading seam. Between the thick, resin-filled stems, we sling our narrow hammocks of braided spore-silk above the dry husk-mattress and draw our silver-backed cloaks tight over our knees.

Overhead, the whispering furl begins.

Great silver-backed leaves curl inward, layer overlapping tight layer, until the whole eleven-mile ribbon has rolled itself into dark, insulated cylinders against the killing frost. Inside, the warmth of the day holds steady, thick with the clean scent of beeswax, dry sage, and crushed chalk. In the pitch dark, our cluster murmurs in low palatal taps and closed-throat hums—spending almost no moisture—recounting what the western seam tapped at dawn, mending a frayed cloak-hem by touch, checking that the child is breathing evenly inside the warm pouch.

I lie still in my silk sling, my small oiled-silk bundle of rose gypsum resting flat against my chest, feeling the slow four-count of my ribs settle into torpor.

I know how my own story ends here, and there is no sharpness to it. One winter, decades from now, the shadow-cold will stop leaving my joints even in full noon sun, and my broad ribs will no longer draw quite enough from the thin air. On a clear morning before the wind wakes, my cluster will walk with me across the two miles of bare, salt-rimed limestone to that northern fissure—the one that is barely velvet cushions now, and by then will have grown tall enough to cast a clean green shadow.

They will take my tapping-rod, my silica lens, my folded silver cloak, and the freshly recopied plates of rose stone. I will lie down unsealed on the dry papery husks among the wire-fine roots, press my bare cheek one last time to the unbroken dolomite, and feel their slow, measured farewell travel straight through the bedrock into my skull—*tock, pause, tock, pause*—long after their footsteps have vanished into the silent air.

Then the copper-shelled twilight runners will come before the frost seals the flats, and over the seasons that follow, the slow mineral acid of the young roots will pry the calcium and bound water straight out of my dry white bones, lifting me quietly into thicker, darker malachite fronds arching over a seam that is just beginning its six-hundred-year breath.

Nothing broken. Nothing wasted. Just a few clear grooves left behind in someone else’s oiled silk, a stone pan a hair’s breadth deeper than I found it, and a green ribbon holding a little cleaner against the bright, silent wind.
