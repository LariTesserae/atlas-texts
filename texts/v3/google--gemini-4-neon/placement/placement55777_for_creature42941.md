I would not arrive here with eyes. Eyes would only be an argument with the bedrock, looking for edges in a cold chemical shimmer that has nothing to say to anyone. If I find myself in this pocket, I find myself already inside the pressure of it—inside a room shaped not by walls, though walls are packed shoulder to shoulder from basin to vault, but by the crossing ribs of sound that hold the air rigid.

At first I might drift upward, testing the high tiers under the sweating ceiling where the seepage enters through hairline cracks. Up there everything is quick and brittle: cup-sized ladles clacking every four seconds, thin slates drumming like fingertips on taut clay, copper aprons hissing with a sharp metallic bite. Beside every wet sill, the air folds into narrow hollows where opposing tremors cancel each other out. I can feel the small, soft lives tucked into those dead-air pockets—slick bellies pressed flat against the undersides of eaves, hearts turning over so slowly they hardly disturb the stone. They are clever in their quietness, following the slack air half an inch to the left whenever an upper gutter narrows.

Lower down, in the sunken catch-basins beneath the miniature arches, I feel another kind of patience: solitary bodies hanging nose-first in the unchanging inflow, broad fins tilted just enough to hold their place while they read the water’s iron tang and mineral shear.

Neither of those holds me. I do not want to hide inside the holes between vibrations, and I do not want to sit in a single stream waiting for the city to feed my mouth. I keep dropping downward, terrace by terrace, until the chatter of the ceiling eaves thins to a crisp treble prickle in my wrists and the heavier voices of the pocket come forward to take hold of my ribs.

That is where I settle: in the lower third of the dome, tucked into a tight bread-oven niche beneath a bridged alley, three steps above a catch-basin and not far from the cold downdraft of the central shaft.

Here my body makes sense. I am small enough to slip sideways through an arched doorway without scraping my shoulders, hunched low so my domed brow clears the wet lintels, my skin slick with a thin waxy oil that keeps the cellar damp from softening me. My chest is wide and flat across the front—a saucer of bone beneath taut skin, built to receive a wall the way a basin receives a spout. My fingers are long and tipped with dense, blunt pads of horn, and my heels and elbows carry the same pale calloused plates, letting me brace in vertical shafts or wedge myself securely across a slick culvert without slipping. Inside my skull, grown heavy from decades of drinking limestone water, sit the ear-stones that turn the boxed-in roar into a clean, three-dimensional map.

From my niche, the whole dome is open to me—not as surfaces, but as intervals.

The floor beneath my hips sits dead-center on a quiet node, so the air around my knees is slack and still. My right shoulder and the flat of my palm rest against an inner wall that backs onto a hollow ceramic chimney carrying braided runoff from the middle tiers. Two alcoves over, hidden inside a vaulted undercroft, hangs one of the slow, heavy stone troughs—a ladle that takes three full minutes to fill before it tips onto wet basalt with a low, muffled bell-strike you feel in your teeth. Across the central court, a tier higher, a broad basin-hammer strikes every twenty-nine seconds. Far above, the cup-sized ladles under the eaves keep up their brisk, staggered clatter. And beneath all of it, traveling up through my heels from the bedrock itself, runs the deep, axle-less boom of the central drain swallowing the city’s water into the unseen shaft.

I live here because this is where the long time is kept, and because of what lies just outside my threshold.

Two paces down the forearm-wide slit beside my terrace, where the oldest masonry gives way to the curving flank of the pocket, a rough seam of raw bedrock pokes through the clay—and bedded inside that limestone sits a dark nodule of chert.

Almost nothing new is made in the pocket. No clay is fired; no fresh copper is hammered; no new arches are raised. We only keep what is already here from sealing shut under its own mineral crust. Every few cycles, a flint sliver wears too rounded to bite calcite cleanly from a bone axle. One of the other six or seven dwellers—never crossing my path, always reading my quiet smudge on the stone from half a dome away—will lay a dulled edge on a smooth boundary curb-stone at the upper end of my alley and withdraw before I ever leave my niche.

When I wake from torpor—heart climbing slowly from eight beats a minute, palms reading the walls to make sure no hammer has begun to limp while I slept—I slide out into the wet slit and check the curb-stone. If a dull flake waits there, I take it in my cheek and move down to the chert seam.

Knapping stone inside an acoustic chamber is a delicate thing. A rogue strike, thrown carelessly into the room, rings harshly off the curved roof and muddies the standing waves across three tiers; it jolts the small grazers clinging under the eaves and makes the sleepers across the court stir uneasily against their walls. So I brace the chert against a wet block of basalt, hold my striker poised, and lay my free palm flat on the masonry behind me.

I wait.

I feel the quick eaves rattle past—four, eight, twelve. I feel the twenty-nine-second basin-hammer across the court tip and ring. Inside the vaulted undercroft two alcoves over, I hear the water rising in the heavy three-minute trough, its pitch deepening as the stone fills and leans toward its pivot. When the trough finally breaks over its bone axle and drops onto the basalt anvil with its heavy, wet bell-strike, my hand comes down on the exact same beat.

The sharp *crack* of chert parting from stone rides straight inside the strike of the hammer, swallowed by the weight of the undercroft wall. Only a faint, glassy overtone travels upward through the masonry. Anyone awake three tiers up, pressing a palm to a clay flue, catches that tiny dry ring nested inside the bass stroke and knows without moving: *a fresh edge is being made down by the lower wall.*

I run my blunt fingertip along the new sliver to test its bite, carry it back up the slick alley, and set it cleanly on the boundary curb-stone where the worn one lay. Then I return to my terrace before the other dweller glides down to claim it.

We never crowd a bridge. Solitude here is not loneliness; it is sharpness of edge. Every soft body pressed against masonry drinks a little of the stone’s tremor, leaving a quiet, moving thumbprint across the city’s pitch. I know all of the others by the shape of that dampening—never by touch, never by voice, only by the weight and cadence of the quiet they carry through the tiers.

High under the sweating vault moves a light, quick smudge that barely dulls a slate roof as it climbs. It has not been long off its parent’s chest; its strokes with a scraper are eager, sometimes a shade too hasty. Once, I felt it linger beside an upper tipping-ladle that had started to drag by a fraction of a beat, scraping the bone axle twice and still failing to clear the limp. From down in my niche, listening through my ribs, I could feel where the real choke sat: not at the ladle at all, but four feet sideways where a waxy lip of calcite had begun pinching a copper baffle, starving the funnel below.

I waited until the polyrhythm opened one of its brief, half-second gaps between two heavy strikes, and rapped my thickened knuckle once against my slate lintel—then, ten minutes later, on the next recurrence of that exact pocket of silence, tapped twice toward the northern flue. A minute passed, then two, and I felt the small, light thumbprint lift from the troubled ladle and glide north along the vault. Half an hour later, the hiss of the copper apron returned to its proper tautness, the ladle snapped cleanly back into phase, and a ripple of relief ran down through every standing wave in the dome.

Across the central court, two tiers above the basins, lives an older dweller whose thumbprint I have known since I first left my mother’s ribs. Its movement across the masonry has grown broader and slower over the long cycles. Lately it pauses longer on each ledge, bracing its elbows hard against the lintels before shifting its weight. I know what is happening inside its skull without ever crossing its doorway: the same dissolved limestone that coats the weirs is thickening its ear-stones into knobby weights, blurring the crisp high chatter of the ceiling ladles until they sound like water heard through silt. Its joints are turning stiff and mineral-heavy. Soon the upper tiers will vanish from its sense altogether, leaving only the heavy undercroft hammers and the deep boom of the central shaft.

Because I live low—close to the sharp scour of the lower weirs, close to the wet basalt slabs where horn can be ground true—it will likely be me who eventually feels that familiar, stiff gait disappear from the walls for good.

When its niche stays empty of movement for a span of cycles, I will climb up across the court and do the one thing we almost never do: cross another dweller’s threshold. Inside that dark, curved hollow I will find the body grown still and rigid as calcified mortar. There will be no grief in my chest, only the same unsentimental care I bring to a slipped copper plate. I will take the dense horn-plates from its heels and knuckles, and the straight, sound finger-bones that have not yet turned brittle. Whatever is soft and spent I will carry down the stepped streets to the glassy, water-worn collar of the central drain and ease into the smooth sheet sliding over the lip, where it will drop into the cold downdraft without altering the pitch of the boom by a hair.

Then I will wedge the bones in the sharp current of a lower weir until the water scours them bare, grind the heel-horn on wet basalt into snug wedges, and cache them in a dry crevice whose surrounding stone rings at a pitch I can find in my sleep. Years later, when a ladle-axle somewhere in the middle tiers finally turns to stone and snaps, a clean knuckle-joint from that elder’s hand will slide into the clay housing and hold the beat steady for another century.

It is strange—no, not strange, simply the shape of the pocket—how little distance lies between listening to the city and becoming part of its works.

Sometimes, when the scrape of my flint has kept the lower tiers clean and hunger wakes as a dull hollow beneath my sternum, I slide down into one of the lowest blind blocks where the slow overflow settles in wide, shallow pans. There, far from the violent splash of the weirs, the water’s iron-and-sulfur film thickens into a cool, velvety curd that smells of chalk and metal filings. I squat beside the rim, scoop the paste on the broad tips of my nails, and work it slowly against the roof of my mouth, washing it down with the cold mineral water.

Once every long while, when I dip a knuckle into the catch-basin three steps below my terrace, I feel the outflow shear thicken and wobble—a sign that the solitary fish hanging beneath the arch has grown too broad and sluggish for the geometry of its basin. Then I lower my palm slowly into the cold current and hold completely still until its fine barbels brush my skin. My long fingers close gently around its cool, boneless weight and lift it free without breaking the rhythm of the inflow. When I have eaten, I spit the fine, glassy ribs cleanly onto a flat sill for the runoff to carry down to the silt-worms, leaving the arch open for a smaller swimmer drifting up from the deeper culverts.

And once in a span of many cycles—so rarely that the ear-stones have time to grow noticeably heavier between them—the vast polyrhythm of the pocket draws tight.

You feel it coming hours ahead, the way you feel a heavy trough nearing its tip, only spread across the entire limestone vault. The four-second eaves, the twenty-nine-second basin-hammers, the three-minute undercroft bells, and every intermediate ladle stacked between them begin to pull their staggered strikes closer together. The tangled wall of sound braids tighter and tighter until, for one shuddering instant, dozens of hammers fall onto their basalt anvils in unison—and in the sudden, startling hollow of slack pressure that follows, the whole dome seems to drop its breath at once.

I have climbed down to the lowest arches beside the central drain twice during that unison. Down there, the deep, unchanging boom of the vertical shaft swallows every smaller tremor, wiping the masonry clean of maps. For the only time in years, two bodies can come close without muddying a street or blurring a wall.

Once, in that heavy bass stillness, I felt another dweller’s warmth through the mist before our skin met. We pressed our broad, flat sternums together and stood completely motionless, hands resting lightly against each other’s shoulders. For a long, unhurried span I listened through my ribs to a rhythm that did not belong to the aquifer—a slow, living nine-beat thrum slightly out of phase with my own heart, drifting in and out of unison with mine while the cold downdraft tugged at our knees. There were no signals to tap, no calcite to clear, no tools to trade. Just two sounding-boards holding each other’s weight until the upper hammers began to scatter back out of phase, restoring the bright lattice of the city above us. Then we eased apart into opposite alleys, carrying the ghost of that warmth across our chests all the way back to our separate niches.

I do not know how many cycles remain before my own ear-stones thicken enough to blur the high copper hiss, or before the fast eaves turn soft and distant in my skull. I can already feel the first quiet stiffness in my left elbow after a long torpor, the faint mineral weight that settles into everything that drinks from this vault. It does not trouble me. When I scrape a pale bead of calcite from a bone axle in the undercroft, I am only holding back for a little while the same stone that is patiently shaping me from the inside out.

For now, the chert edge on the boundary curb-stone has been taken, and a dulled flake no longer waits there. Two alcoves away, the heavy three-minute trough fills steadily, tips, and rings clean against its basalt anvil without a trace of drag. Just beneath the overhanging bridge outside my doorway, the dead-air pocket sits steady and undisturbed, cradling its small, quiet grazers.

I ease backward into my narrow bread-oven hollow, tuck my chin down toward my sternum, and let my heels settle onto the quiet node of the floor. My right palm lies flat against the cool, vibrating clay of the chimney wall. My heart slows—twelve beats, ten, nine—until it scarcely stirs the mist at my nostrils. Above and around me, stacked five and six deep to the sweating bedrock, the alleys clack, hiss, and drum in their ancient, unbroken weave, and I hold the whole turning song inside my ribs, waiting for the smallest limp.
