My first instinct, standing at the edge of this place, is to put myself on the dry side of the concrete.

It is the familiar posture—the one of the person who looks *into* a world rather than living inside it. I can picture myself easily enough in the dry galleries behind the perimeter walls, or out on a high service deck above the wire-glass skylights during the quiet hours of a night shift. A hydrographer, maybe, or a junior acoustic engineer walking the catwalks beside the axial-flow turbines with ear-defenders clamped over my head, feeling the fifty-cycle bass thrum climb up through the soles of my boots. I would be the one watching the pressure logs, checking the impeller bearings for cavitation wear, leaning over the railing of an open drainage grate to look down eighty meters into the pale bottle-green shafts of daylight and wondering what the floor looks like without an LED cone washing it out.

I could even picture myself finding the anomaly—sitting in a narrow monitoring room under a pump-house apron, staring at a waterfall spectrogram of the central bays, noticing that every few hours during the Surge, something thickens the fifty-cycle baseline. Not a loose flange, not a bearing grinding dry, not the iron-gravel hiss of the bronze impellers. Something rounder. Something deliberate, braided so tightly into the turbine harmonics that the automated diagnostic software strips it out as background vibration. Maybe I would take the yellow inspection drone down on its armored umbilical, guiding it past the crisp black enamel of *-40M* and *-45M*, sweeping the twin white beams across the fresh marine primer, hoping to catch whatever was hiding in the math.

And yet, the longer I hold that picture, the thinner it feels.

Up on the service decks, the basin isn’t a place; it is a utility. It is megawatts, tidal head, silt-clearance tolerances, epoxy cure-rates. Every time you try to reach down into it from the dry side, the bright lights and the high, thin whine of the drone’s thrusters empty the room two bays ahead of you. You would spend your whole career pressing a stethoscope to the outside of a cello, trying to guess the shape of the hollow wood from the scrape of the bow, while the water itself stays cold, bright, and closed to you.

If I am honest about where the gravity of this world actually pulls me, I do not stay behind the wire-glass. I slip through the bar-screens.

---

Not as one of the silver baitfish—those vast, flashing sheets of smelt and shad that live entirely in the reflex of the school, banking in unison beneath the skylights as if sheer numbers could substitute for a hiding place. And not as the pike or heavy trout holding station in the pier eddies, still obeying the old river logic, treating a half-kilometer hall of poured concrete as though it were nothing more than a very large, very sterile logjam to ambush from. Even down along the floor, the flatfish and whiskered bottom-feeders keep their noses pressed into the rubberized expansion joints, shrinking a continental room down to the width of a single mortar seam.

Only the pale dwellers actually inhabit the volume. Only they hear the whole eighty-meter column as a single instrument.

So if I let myself sink past the drainage grates—down through the pale bottle-green upper water, through the white-gold halos of the borosilicate lamps, down into the steady, nine-degree clarity—it is their skin I find myself wearing. Heavy through the chest and shoulders, three and a half meters from the oil-filled dome of my brow to the trailing linen of my ribbon-tail. Slate-gray along the back so I vanish against the surveyor-flat aggregate below; wet chalk along the belly so I dissolve into the sunlit grid overhead.

Even then, I do not settle immediately into a single posture. There are too many ways to live in a room this new.

I don't think I would be one of the calves born upside-down in the dark concrete coffers of the ceiling, who has never known a world without right angles, nor one of the reckless, half-translucent juveniles flattening their bellies onto the top crossbeam of a creeping silt-scraper just to ride it half a kilometer toward the roar of the sump trenches. I watch them with a cool, steady amusement—and if one drifts too close to the screaming pull of the eastern intake screens, I would be one of the heavier shapes that cuts across its path, leaning a broad, flat-palmed shove against its flank and snapping two short, sharp clicks into the water until its gill-valves clamp shut and it turns back toward the middle bays.

I think, instead, I am one of the early arrivals.

Not the oldest, perhaps, not yet slowed down enough for barnacles to crust deeply behind my elbows, though the slate along my shoulders has started to darken into a faint, mottled bottle-gray. I came in when the basin was still being flooded for the first time—before the finest intake screens were seated, when the water still carried the sharp, chalky tang of newly cured mortar and fresh coal-tar epoxy. I was barely longer than a human forearm then, thread-thin and nervous, swept out of the dark, silt-choked river-mouths outside where sound died three body-lengths away in mud and weed, where staying alive meant pressing yourself flat into cold clefts and never growing large enough to draw a shadow from above.

I don't carry that outside world as a story. I carry it as a contrast—a quiet, bodily astonishment that has never quite worn off.

Out there, the world was cramped, murky, and muffled. Here, the water is so clear that a pier half a kilometer away still stands out as a crisp pale bar in the blue-gray haze, and the concrete is so flat that a single sound can cross six kilometers and arrive with its edges intact. Having come from the mud, I have never taken the emptiness of this room for granted.

---

If you want to find me, you should look during the four-hour Drift—not near the floor scrapers, and not deep in the warm, shimmering central gyres where the mothers and calves turn in lazy rings, though I visit both. Look in the middle bays, at the exact elevation of the crisp black *-50M* stenciled on the piers.

There is a ledge there made of nothing at all.

The fifty-cycle bass thrum from the axial-flow turbines behind the perimeter walls never stops; it pours endlessly into the water, bounces off the poured floor, bounces off the gridded waffle-slabs eighty meters overhead, and collides with itself all across the open halls. Right around fifty meters down, the rising wave and the falling wave meet out of phase and cancel each other out. It isn't silence, not truly—it is a thin, invisible horizontal shelf of lessened pressure suspended in the middle of a roaring machine, felt more in the ribs and the oil of the forehead than heard.

That is where I spend much of the Drift, hanging parallel to five or six others, limbs tucked flush into the shallow recesses along my flanks, nose angled a fraction of a degree into the cool north-south breeze. My ribbon-tail scarcely ripples. My three pairs of pleated gill-valves part by millimeters, letting narrow plumes of spent water curl backward like heat-shimmer.

We talk there without moving our jaws. A slight tautening across the smooth throat and brow, and out goes a long, pure, pressurized tone pitched directly inside the fifty-cycle turbine harmonic. To feel your own voice lock cleanly onto the roar of the walls is a strange, deep comfort—like leaning your back against something vast and immovable and discovering it will carry your weight for miles. Through those braided low notes, and through single sharp clicks bounced cleanly off the flat faces of the piers, we trade the state of the room: where a bait-ribbon is gathering near the eastern light-bays, which floor scraper is creeping past the third row of columns, how the water coming off the northern intakes has dropped half a degree since morning.

And yet, even among the assembly, I have a habit that pulls me away from the shelf whenever the four-hour clock nears its hinge.

There is a ten-minute interval just before the eastern intake tunnels crack open, when the stately north-south Drift slows and goes almost slack. That is when I unfold my four long-jointed limbs, drop out of the quiet band, and glide upward—past the pale shadows of the piers, past the crisp bevels where I sometimes pause to scrape a stubborn pennant of bright green hair-algae off my flank—until I am hovering just below the high service gantries.

My silver-amber eyes pinch down to hairline vertical slits against the white-gold halos of the tungsten-halogen cages. Up here, the water smells faintly of galvanized iron and cold air drifting through the open grates overhead. Heavy mooring chains and steel-armored flow-meters hang down into the current like dark pendulums.

I brace my rear clay-pale palms in the shallow tie-rod dimples of a concrete beam, let my body hang tilted slightly downward so even the dying breeze pins me gently to the stone, and reach out one broad, four-fingered hand.

I nudge a galvanized link.

It swings—slowly, heavily, shaking loose a tiny necklace of silver micro-bubbles that wobble upward toward the wire-glass. The iron knocks against its neighbor: a dull, clean metallic *tick-knock*.

I hold completely still, gill-valves shut, brow angled outward into the open bay, and listen to the room answer.

The sound shoots out across half a kilometer of nine-degree water, strikes the razor-crisp face of the next city-block pier, and comes back thinned, softened, and slightly darkened by the distance. A heartbeat later, a fainter double arrives from the concrete waffle-slab above, and then a long, pale wash off the surveyor-flat floor eighty meters below. In the spacing and weight of those three returns, the whole bay stands up inside my head—not as a picture of light and shadow, which only reaches as far as the blue-gray haze allows, but as an exact, three-dimensional architecture of pressure. I can tell if a dense school of young herring has moved into the corridor between piers because the echo comes back slightly fuzzy, absorbed by ten thousand tiny swim-bladders. I can tell if the thermal curtain of the central gyre has drifted closer because the return bends a fraction of a second late.

I nudge the chain again, a touch harder, tilting my head to catch the new angle of the echo. It isn't hunting, and it isn't quite play, either—or if it is play, it is the grave, unhurried kind: the pleasure of tapping the glass of a huge, clear vessel just to keep learning its pitch.

---

Then, before the chain has even finished settling, I feel it through the concrete under my rear palms.

Six kilometers east, a sector gate drops onto its sill. The vibration arrives through the poured stone moments before the dull metallic *clang* reaches me through the liquid. Seconds later, the invisible wall of pressure rolls across the empty hall as the submerged eastern diffusers crack open.

I push off the gantry beam, tuck my limbs flush, and let myself drop back down toward the middle water as the glassy, refractive ribbons of colder river water begin curling up from the floor. Down at *-50M*, the pale shapes are already peeling away from the quiet shelf. By the time the laminar breeze accelerates into a roaring cross-current—tugging hard at the mooring chains I just left, spinning long twisting vortexes off the downstream edges of the piers—three of us are holding station in the calm lee of a column, while two heavy trout quietly yield the eddy and slip lower toward the floor.

Hunting in the Surge is the one time I feel the full size this place has given me.

Out in the sunlit corridor, a vast silver ribbon of smelt is fighting the accelerated flow, stretching thin. We do not lunge after them in blind bursts. We glide out from behind the concrete in wide, effortless arcs, using the pressure of the rushing water against our backs to slice across the current without a wasted stroke. No clicks now, no humming—only the exact spacing of our bodies, read by the displacement of water against our flanks. We press a section of the flashing ribbon sideways until it has nowhere left to go except the broad, sunlit face of the next pier, balling tightly right across the crisp black enamel of *-45M*.

Ten meters off the wall, the three of us fan out, level our broad brows at the packed silver mass, and fire together.

It isn't a noise so much as a sudden, hard tightening of the chest and forehead—a single concussive pulse that slams straight into the school, strikes the razor-flat concrete behind them, and rebounds cleanly through the fish a fraction of a second later. The mooring chains high overhead rattle faintly at the dull *thump*. Hundreds of smelt go limp at once, spinning slowly downward in the bottle-green light like dropped coins, scales shedding into a bright drifting cloud.

We glide in without haste, wide mouths parting, smooth throats expanding like pale pleated bellows as we draw the stunned silver in. There is no frenzy in it, no snapping or tearing—only the cool, clean efficiency of an architecture used well. When we have had enough, we simply tilt our fins into the vortex spiraling off the beveled corner of the pier and let the machine lift us effortlessly out of the current.

---

There are two moments, though, that feel more like *mine* than even the quiet shelf or the hunt.

The first is when the sky above the wire-glass turns to pewter.

I feel a northern squall coming long before the rivers outside swell and deepen the roar behind the eastern walls. The rectangular light-bays eighty meters overhead dim swiftly, throwing a rolling continent of shadow across the bare concrete floor. When I see that gray light shift, I almost always leave the middle depths and rise into the upper ten meters, drifting directly beneath the open drainage grates.

Up there, invisible above the roof lattice, rain is hammering onto the surface of the world. Down here, it arrives entirely in translation: a fine, shimmering veil of silver micro-bubbles driven down through the grates, drifting slowly into the cold green gloom.

I glide straight into the falling fizz, roll slowly belly-up, and spread all four wide, webbed palms out into the water. The tiny bubbles brush across my smooth chalk throat, across the flush pleats of my gill-valves, along the pale underside of my flanks—tickling, airy, weightless. It is the softest thing in this entire engineered world, the only thing here that has no straight edge, no pump schedule, no bolt or bevel. Floating upside down in the dim, pearly shimmer, watching the pewter grid overhead dissolve behind a curtain of rising and falling silver dust, I feel the brief, strange warmth of belonging to a sky I will never actually break through.

The second moment is much quieter.

It happens perhaps once a month, usually during a bright afternoon Drift, when a sharp human shadow crosses one of the wire-glass light-bays or a maintenance hatch cracks open along a high service deck. Two bays away, a thin, high-pitched whine cuts across the deep familiar hum of the axial turbines—an alien frequency, tight and insectile, nothing like the grave bass of the walls.

The yellow inspection drone, sliding down on its armored cable.

I do not bolt. Bolting churns the clear water and flashes a chalk-pale belly right into the beams. Instead, I glide without hurry to the nearest city-block pier, slip cleanly around its blind side, and press myself flat against the uncured freshness of the stone. Four wide, river-clay palms settle into the slip-form ridges and tie-rod dimples right beside the painted black border of *-45M*. My gill-valves pull tight and completely still. My ribbon-tail lies flush against the concrete.

Twin cones of stark white LED light sweep out of the blue-gray haze, lighting up drifting smelt scales, a single swaying pennant of green hair-algae, and the thin rust-tear weeping from a galvanized conduit bolt where a wrench nicked the zinc during assembly.

I stay completely motionless on the blind face of the stone, my body pressed flat as a shadow, except for one thing: I keep my brow aligned just shy of the razor-crisp beveled corner, so that a single wide, silver-amber eye—lidded down to a hairline slit against the glare—watches the little yellow machine hum past.

I do not hate it, and I do not fear it the way I fear the screaming bronze impellers behind the intake screens. Watching it pass, trailing its armored tether back up toward the dry world above the waffle-slabs, I feel only a cool, steady attentiveness.

The concrete under my palms is still sharp enough to polish the skin of my shoulders. The paint on the sluice-frames hasn't blistered yet; the expansion joints on the floor are still mostly bare. This colossal, scoured cathedral is brand-new, poured to a surveyor's tolerance to balance rivers and feed inland power corridors, and the ones who built it still believe they are looking down into an empty machine.

When the twin white beams sweep on into the next bay and the thin whine of the thrusters fades back beneath the fifty-cycle bass of the walls, I uncup my palms from the tie-rod dimples, let the laminar breeze catch my back, and glide quietly back out into the bright, roaring emptiness—down to *-50M*, where the noise cancels itself into a floor of stillness, waiting for the slack water when I can go back up to the gantries and listen to the chains.
