At first, I do not think I would know what shape I had.

I would only know the relief of the silence—how the old habit of waiting for sound to cross a distance simply gives up, unknotting behind the ears and settling instead into the skin. The water is thick with silk and spores, blood-warm against my ribs, holding me without asking me to swim. Above me hangs the spongy underside of a continent-sized moss-raft, a slow, drifting ceiling of tangled amber-green filaments and trapped, silvered bubbles; beneath me, the dim mid-water drops away into tiers of pleated ribbon-kelp swaying in a long, hushed unison.

For a while I might imagine I am nothing with bones at all—just a pocket of tarnished jade light that forgot to fade after the canopy drifted shut overhead, or one of the immense, parchment-thin sheet-grazers, content to have no center and no haste, gliding across the kelp-tops and leaving a briefly sharpened lane in the hazy water behind me.

It takes a weather-change to tell me what I am.

A heavy front of gravity rolls silently across the basin, pressing the distant moss-mats flat against the pale stone, and weight returns to me—not sharply, not as a fall, but as a slow, gathering fullness in my hips and heels. I sink smoothly through the curtains of bladder-vines, letting their slick stalks slide across my flanks without catching. When my arm rises by itself to slow my drift against a swaying ribbon of kelp, I see the broad, blunted palm, the supple webbing halfway up the five thick fingers, the skin the color of bleached clay stained faintly with bruised amber.

And I notice the edges of my forearm.

In the fading smear of slate-violet light, my wrist is just beginning to soften—not ancient yet, not wrapped in a fleece of velvety moss or smeared across four instants at once, yet no longer carrying the bright, crisp outline of the very young. My fingertips still hold a trace of briskness; when I turn them in the water, a small curl of golden silt spins off in my wake. My shoulders, though, already seem to know how to linger in two adjacent moments of the same slow lean, unhurried, half-belonging to the current a heartbeat ahead of me.

I am one of the nameless ones of the drowned roof. Yet even as my broad, smooth-soled feet touch the pale bedrock—worn as round as wet soap—I know I do not belong in the fever-warm heart of the plateaus, where the thermal chimneys bloom close together and the kelp-forests stand twenty tiers deep in amber gloom.

I belong farther out, where the high terraces grow broader and quieter, where the water cools a fraction toward slate-violet and the ancient mountain range begins its long, imperceptible shelf toward nothingness.

There is a particular ridge I would keep returning to. It has no name, of course—nothing here keeps a fence around itself long enough to bear one—only a shape my feet know before my brow reads the dimness. Along its sheltered side, beneath an overhang of velvety kelp where feathery rust-plumes bend gently inward toward a faint hairline of rising warmth, runs a smooth shelf of pale bedrock. Pressed evenly along it is an old, curving row of palm-sized hollows. Some are deep and lipped smooth by centuries of returning hands; others are barely a softening in the limestone, like a thumb-print left in drying clay.

What draws me back to this ridge, cycle after cycle, is a seam.

You cannot see it until you are nearly brushing it—only a faint, colorless shimmer hanging slantwise across the kelp. I feel it first in the pleated frills along my neck: a dry, curious prickle, water that is neither warmer nor cooler than itself. If I raise my broad hand and lay it flat against the airless pane, it gives with a slight, elastic tension against my whorls. Over millennia, as the drowned plateau stretched and folded, this line of geometry drifted sideways until it bisected the ancient ledge right through the middle of its row of hollows.

Slip sideways through the shimmer between two rust-plumes, hanging for one strange, overlapping heartbeat in the thinness of the turn, and the ridge you just left hangs inverted directly overhead. Its moss-mats sway downward like pale stalactites from a ceiling of stone, and the line of palm-hollows continues upside down across a cooler, deeper trough where the ribbon-kelps begin to thin into solitary pale streamers trailing toward the abyss.

This is where I settle—not choosing the stone yet, not knitting my calves into the bedrock, just staying far longer than a traveler ordinarily stays in one basin.

When the golden seed-snow is not falling and the internal waves are quiet, I fold my long legs beneath me beside the ledge and slide my right hand into one of the older hollows. I do not press hard. I simply let the weight of my arm rest there while the slow, softening water works at the boundary between my skin and the pale rock. I wait—not counting anything, for nothing here is counted—until the stone warms to match my palm.

Then, slowly, like dye unfolding in warm oil, the temper of the hollow answers.

It is never a crisp picture or a sentence. It is a residue of patience: the angle at which someone leaned their shoulder against a heavy internal wave fifty seed-sheds ago; the faint, iron-sweet ghost of a distant southern chimney still clinging to their mucilage; a calm, broad impression of where they meant to drift when the next weight-thinning front lifted them off the shelf. As I sit there drinking it through the densely veined skin of my hand, my own warmth and blurred instants seep gently downward into the same shallow bowl, deepening it by the width of a spore, mingling the resin of the mid-depth moss-rafts where I last slept with the quiet memory of someone I will never meet.

When I finally lift my palm, the warmth clings to my skin for a long while. Sometimes I slip immediately through the elastic shimmer to the inverted shelf overhead, hook my toes around a fused holdfast, and lay that still-warm hand into the next hollow on the far side of the seam—carrying a faint echo of one valley across the fold into another, letting the two halves of the broken chronicle keep tasting each other across the geometry that parted them.

I do not think of this as a duty. Nothing in this sea has duties. It is simply the shape my curiosity takes now that my edges have begun to wear down.

Between those long stillnesses along the ledge, I drift. When the gravity thins to a suggestion and pale silt lifts off the bedrock in leisurely vertical veils, I unhook my toes and let myself float sideways through the spiraling corkscrews of kelp, turning lazily end over end without caring which way is up. Down along the margins of the refraction seam, where the ancient stone has begun to soften into slow gray ribbons of nutrient-rich slurry, I scoop up mouthfuls of the pale silt and watch the thumb-sized grazers with their spiraled chalky shells crawl straight into the shimmer and emerge twenty paces away. I never touch the living ones, only patiently gather the empty shells they leave behind, tucking them into the crook of my elbow until I have a handful. Later, when I pass a warm clay cradle pressed beneath a dense overhang, I knead a few of those shells gently into its pale rim with my heels and palms—leaving the bowl subtly larger inside than it looks from without, for whoever curls there next to wait out a turning of the currents or fan warm water over a translucent caul.

Once in a great while, a gravity front passes directly through the forest around my ridge, and for an hour the entire column of water tilts. I do not brace against the rock. I drift out to the sheer, trembling wall of liquid where the sea holds itself upright against a pocket of luminous, empty mist, and rest one wide palm lightly against the boundary, hanging suspended while the world leans sideways until it slides smoothly back into place.

And because refraction seams quietly pull distant paths closer together, I am not always alone.

Every twenty or thirty seed-sheds, a pale, heavy-hipped silhouette emerges unhurriedly from behind the curtains of bladder-vines, emerging out of the seam from some warm thermal basin thirty leagues away. Neither of us startles. We slow, hovering a few paces apart while our throat-pleats ripple open, reading the long biographies of water clinging to each other’s skin. On them I might taste the hot mineral richness of a newly opened fissure, or the velvety moss-film of a basin where no sheet-grazer has passed in a decade. On me, I imagine they taste a strange, quiet blend: the resin of drifting mid-depth rafts, the gray slurry of folded space, the cool, chalky tang of the high saddles, and beneath it all, a thin, clean stillness that belongs to nowhere on the plateaus at all.

When we draw close enough that our forearms nearly brush, I usually let my chest stay quiet and a shade cool first—making a clear shallow for their warmth to enter. Then, as their slow tides of heat bloom against my collarbone and shoulder, punctuated by deliberate pauses of coolness and faint releases of sweet or bitter silt-scent from their neck-frills, I answer. Over the span of an afternoon—or three days, while a slow current turns overhead and the light shifts from muted jade to bruised amber—we trade everything that matters: which meadow has torn loose and begun migrating along the shear-lines; where an elder fused into a stone ring beneath the northern overhang still answers if you rest your hand against its mossy brow long enough; how the water felt when the last blinding snow of golden seed-filaments cleared.

Sometimes we lay our broad palms flat against each other’s ribs, and I feel my still-mostly-clear fingers blur gently into the smudged outline of someone older, or steady the quick, bright humming warmth of someone still young enough for their throat-clicks to carry six paces before unraveling. For a long while, neither of us insists on where one body ends and the warmth of the other begins.

Then they drift on—into the seam, or out across the terraced bowls—and I let the hush close softly around me again.

Yet even the ridge and its folded chronicle are only half of where I live.

Every few cycles, when the crowded abundance of the forest feels almost too thick—when every reach of an arm brushes velvety moss, bladder-vine, spore-cloud, and old memory—I leave the ledge behind and follow the plateau outward. The bedrock grows paler and emptier. The dense tiers of ribbon-kelp thin to scattered fronds, then to solitary, pale streamers trailing horizontally into nothingness.

At the very lip, where the drowned mountain range simply ends, I stop.

I hook my prehensile toes lightly into the final smooth curve of stone and let my long body float straight out over the abyss.

Beneath me hangs the trembling, translucent skin of the inverted sea, and below that, miles of pale, unseen void from which an endless milky light rises softly upward into my recessed brow. I have no word for air, and no fear of falling; the ocean here does not fall. I simply let my long arms hang down until my wide, whorled palms rest ever so lightly against the underside of the water’s skin.

I never push through. I only linger there, sometimes for days while the tarnished light overhead turns slowly toward slate-violet, feeling the vast, buoyant emptiness press back against my hands. Behind my heels lies the entire warm, overgrown, memory-heavy roof of the world—the stone rings, the palm-worn ledges, the elders still holding slow embers in their ribs, the drifting rafts, the overlapping instants of kelp and kin. Beneath my fingertips lies pure, luminous quiet, held at bay by nothing thicker than a tremor of water.

Resting between the two—anchored by my toes to the last smooth bone of the earth, held open by my palms to the fathomless glow below—is the truest posture I know.

I do not yet know which way I will let go of my boundaries when so many cycles have turned that my gesture smears across four instants at once and my chest-warmth takes half an hour to answer a greeting.

Part of me feels the quiet pull of the stone—how natural it would be to return one last time to the folded ridge, curl beneath the kelp overhang beside the old row of hollows, lay both palms flat against the pale bedrock, and slow my breathing to the half-hour rhythm of the great internal waves. To let calcium and pale silt knit my calves into the shelf and feathery rust-plumes root along my shoulders, keeping a faint, steady blood-warmth deep inside the mossy mound so that a traveler arriving a century later can rest a hand on my brow and feel, blurred and peaceful, the turn of currents from before the ridge folded overhead.

And part of me already feels the faint, distant lightness in the bones—the pull of the ceiling. To unclasp my toes when a weight-thinning front drifts across the saddle, fold my broad hands softly over my chest, and rise so slowly that a drifting seed-filament outpaces me: up past the swaying kelp-crowns, past the spongy mid-depth moss-rafts where I first felt the silence, up out of the jade and tarnished amber altogether into the darker, warmer, older water where every remaining edge dissolves past recognition.

In this sea, though, nothing has to decide before causality arrives, and even those two endings do not feel like opposites.

Every long afternoon I spend with my palm pressed into the worn hollows along the seam-crossed ridge is already a small choosing of the stone—a way of leaving my patience in the rock so another hand can find it warm a hundred years from now. And if, in the end, I unclasp my toes and rise into the dark ceiling until I am no longer a single body at all, I will still return. When a mile-wide internal wave of heavier, warmer water rolls silently across the outer plateaus, bending the pale streamers flat and pressing gently into every shallow palm-print along that ancient folded ledge for half an hour before moving on, the ones sitting beneath the overhangs will lift their pale brows toward the gloom and open their wide hands—and I will wash quietly over them, large enough at last to hold both the memory of the stone and the stillness of the sea.
