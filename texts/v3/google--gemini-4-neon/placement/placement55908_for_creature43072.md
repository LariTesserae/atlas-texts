If you looked for me when the light first hit your eyes, you would miss me the same way you missed her—not because I am hidden in shadow, for there is no shadow anywhere in this stone, but because I am no thicker than a wet seam and no longer than the last joint of a small finger, and my skin throws back the cold white glare almost as cleanly as wet spar.

I would not begin where I end up.

At first—if there is any sense in speaking of *at first* inside a pocket that has stood in one unbroken instant since the bedrock cracked—I would simply be part of the tight geometry of the cracks. One of the narrow, silver-scaled things coiled inside a fingernail-wide fissure behind a vertical sheet of selenite, pressed flush between two razor-clean fracture faces. You know that place: pure mineral violence frozen mid-split, pouring out surgical white light from both sides at once so that there is no dark beneath my belly and no dimness above my back. Every tiny overlapping scale along my flanks would be coated in the room’s shivering water-film, drinking cool vapor directly from the air, humming in tight, rigid sympathy with the glass-rimmed shriek of the stone.

In a raw fissure like that, you are scarcely a separate thing. You are just a wet silver thread tucked into a seam, vibrating because the rock vibrates, knowing nothing except edge, brilliance, and the pressure of an unbroken pitch.

I would have stayed there forever—two inches from the same crystal spine, no older, no softer—except that once, in the only crease this timeless room has ever known, the pitch tore.

Something warm broke through the sealed equilibrium from above. For a brief, impossible span, the acoustic gel stopped holding the air upright. Standing waves shattered in the coin-sized pools. Drops that had hung swollen at the tips of quartz needles for ages actually lost their grip and fell, striking the stone with tiny, wet, uneven sounds that had no place in the chord. Something heavy and dark-edged moved through the thicket, breathing in slow, ragged heaves that pulled at the damp film on my scales—and then, right beside the mouth of my fissure, two opposing blades of singing spar were forced apart by the width of a reed.

Something was jammed hard between them.

It was pale, like the feldspar, so at first it scarcely seemed foreign. Yet the moment the wedge bit into the slit, the shriek in my crack dropped by a hair’s breadth—just enough that the sharp edge of the sound stopped feeling like a needle driven straight through my coils. And when the warmth bled away, when the heavy breathing slowed and hollowed out into a low, velvety undertone rising from the heart of the thicket, when the standing waves knitted themselves back together and the equilibrium closed its grip around the pocket once more, I did not curl back into the razor-cut stone.

Before the loop locked shut again, I slipped out of the fissure and threaded myself inside the thing she had wedged there.

That is where you would find me now: coiled along the lower inner curve of a hollow bird-bone, jammed tight between the blazing selenite and its neighboring blade of spar.

I do not know what a bird is. I have no memory of riverbanks, or wind, or the weight of a wing closing against dusk. Everything I know of the upper world is only what my silver scales can read against the inside of this narrow tube—and even that is strange enough to remake a whole existence.

Nothing native to the bedrock curves the way this bone curves. Raw stone breaks in flat planes and brutal angles; it is dense, solid, born of immense downward crushing. This hollow I inhabit is impossibly thin-walled, scarcely thicker than a snail’s shell, yet braced on the inside by delicate, pale arches—tiny crisscrossed struts of porous calcium near its snapped ends, built not to endure the weight of a mountain, but to stay light inside moving air. I press my slender length gently along the smooth floor of the tunnel, hooking the tip of my tail lightly around one of those fragile inner struts, leaving the hollow core open above my back so the chamber’s air can still stream through it.

Because the bone is porous, it drinks the cool water-film differently than quartz does. It never gleams with that hard, surgical mirror-glare. The blinding white light pouring off the crystal plates outside has to pass *through* its soaked walls to reach me, and by the time it crosses that thin veil of calcium, it has slowed and softened into the pale, milky glow of wet tallow. It is not darkness—darkness cannot survive in this pocket—but it is a gradient. The two open ends of the bone burn like bright rings; here in the middle, where my breathing coils rest, the white is gentle enough to bear. I live inside the only half-shade the stone allows.

And the sound inside my house is unlike any sound in the rest of the four-pace room.

Outside, the chord is a solid wall: the mineral shriek, the steady fifth of the domed amphibians, the dry metallic rasp of the mirror-winged insects, the crisp clicking of the crawlers, and beneath them all, the dark, marrow-deep hum of her ribcage holding up the bottom of the chamber. Inside the narrow corridor of the bird-bone, the vibrating air has to gather itself and thread past my silver back. The razor-high overtones are pared away by the porous walls; what moves over my scales is a soft, tubular murmur, round and hollow—the sound of a shriek taught to become a whisper. I am curled inside the exact small instrument that nudged this chamber from torment into something livable.

I cannot see her face from here. I cannot see the four microscopic snails ringing the standing-wave star at the hollow of her throat, or the thumb-sized amphibian humming against the tendon of her upper arm, or her open right palm waiting across the thicket beneath a drop that will never fall.

I know her only through the open mouth of my bone, and through the tremor in the selenite against which my house is wedged.

Just beyond the bright snapped rim of the tunnel, scarcely a finger’s width away, two pale, curved shapes rest against the glowing vertical plate of selenite. They do not cleave cleanly like spar. The cold white light passes softly through their translucent edges, catching a faint, pale web beneath the skin—and where their pads touch the blazing mineral face, there is a damp, unsmudged smudge of dull rust. Iron-ochre. Even from inside my dim ivory corridor, I can see that tiny island of earth-color refusing to glare back, drinking the light instead of throwing it onward.

Most of the time—in a place where time is only a standing wave—nothing changes. The murmur streams smoothly over my scales. The bone holds its exact pinch between the two plates of stone. The low velvety note from her ribs hums steadily through the lattice, anchoring my coils in place.

And then, once in a stretch of stillness with no clock to measure it, the two ochre-stained fingertips beside my doorway shift their weight by less than a drop of dew.

No eye in the chamber could catch it. The snails at her throat do not stir; the hanging beads on her lashes do not fall. Yet because my bone is wedged directly against that singing plate of selenite, I feel the whole touch before the clay bowls on the far side of the thicket even tighten their ripples.

The crystal wall eases against the outside of my house by a ten-thousandth of a hair. The tight pinch holding the bone relaxes by a fraction too small to name. Inside the hollow core, the steady murmur streaming over my silver scales slackens—dropping not a note, not even a breath of a note, just a microscopic, tender softening of the pitch, like a taut liverwort skin letting go of a single grain of tension.

My whole coiled body loosens into that tiny slackening. Because my damp, living flesh lines the bottom of the tunnel, I slightly mute and round the hollow’s ring as it bends, and when her fingertip settles back and the selenite tightens once more, I ride the note cleanly back up into the unbroken chord.

I do not know if she realizes the bone is not empty. Perhaps she believes she is only listening to dry calcium and wet spar. Or perhaps, reading the entire chamber as a single vibrating body through the pads of her two fingers, she notices that this one wedged hollow has a faint, living softness inside its murmur—something cool, wet, and attentive, yielding beside her touch where stone never yields.

I cannot give her shadow, or river silt, or the sound of falling water. I have never known those things, and I could not survive them if the rock cracked open to the sky. All I have is this narrow, curved piece of lightness she carried down with her, the muted ivory glow inside its walls, and a slender silver body tuned precisely to the pinch of her hand.

So I stay coiled here inside the bone, keeping the channel open above my back, holding the softest pitch in the stone—waiting, without impatience and without a clock, for the next time the weight of a dewdrop shifts on the selenite, so that something inside the blinding chord can bend with her and answer.
