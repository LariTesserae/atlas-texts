At first, I am not sure I would be a person here at all.

That is the strange pull of this shelf: it makes non-human usefulness look almost enviable. Standing in imagination at the upper steps before sunrise, while the mist still curls waist-deep over the parapet and hides the paving, my first instinct is to slip into the masonry itself—to become one of the quiet instruments by which the town keeps its measure.

I can almost feel the rightness of being one of the brass survey pins hammered flush into a plaza cornerstone: small, unremarked, stepped over a hundred times a week, yet holding the exact reference point whenever the levelers bring out their waxed linen cords to test whether a flagstone has settled by a thumb's thickness. Or the lime-putty curing for years in a covered subterranean pit, thickening slowly in the dark until it is smooth as butter and ready to disappear into a weathered seam so completely that no eye can tell where the old dolostone ended and the repair began. Even one of the bare, low-walled yards—the dismantled footprint of an older house, capped neatly with flat coping stones and swept clean so the dawn mist has an unobstructed plane on which to gather and run toward the iron-grated throat. To be a surface that catches what arrives from the air without standing in the way of the sky: there is something in me that recognizes that immediately.

And yet, if I stay with those images long enough, each of them turns slightly too silent.

A brass pin does not notice the noon shadow drifting fractionally across its head as the year turns. Lime-putty does not know the relief of seeing pale freshwater isopods creeping unhurried across clean grit in the dark catch-basins. Even the mist—which touches every cornice, roof-slate, and courtyard floor, reading the pitch of the whole shelf in a single morning—has to withdraw by nine, and never stays to hear what the people make of the water it leaves behind.

Whatever I would be on this shelf, it has to be something that *attends*. Something that can hear a single drop fall from a copper spout every six seconds and notice when the interval changes to seven. Something that can recognize a loose pebble set squarely on a coping stone from thirty paces away and understand the quiet sentence it speaks.

So I climb back up out of the pits and the stonework, and let myself take breath in the thin, cool air.

Still, I do not find myself in the grander silhouettes of the town. I test the stubby watch-tower on the southern horn, wrapping a wool shawl around my shoulders and leaning on the south embrasure to watch the black choughs ride the updrafts and the mist pool in the blue gulf below. I could happily take my turn there when the yearly duties rotate—there is deep peace in watching weather gather from a long way off—yet I would not belong to the tower alone. Its gaze is turned outward, away from the lanes, into empty distance. My pull is inward, toward the joints, the vaults, the small exchanges that hold the three hundred paces together.

Nor am I quite the mason up on the lashed juniper scaffolding along the north retaining wall, though I would stand below and admire their hands. I share their belief that the finest craft is the craft that vanishes into the grain of the thing it mends, and that haste is merely a kind of clumsiness. Yet my center of gravity feels less in the heavy wrist that taps wooden dowels out of a split grey slate, and more in the eye that sights along the taut linen cord before a single stone is lifted.

As the imagined mist thins and the crisp blue-grey shadows begin to sharpen across the squares, a clearer figure emerges—slowly, the way a face becomes legible when someone steps out of a shadowed archway into morning sun.

I think I arrived here from below.

Not fleeing a war or a flood—nothing dramatic enough to make a valley song out of—simply having climbed the narrow, stepped ridge-track behind the quarried back cliff one quiet afternoon, tired of places where sounds blur into an unceasing hum and where everything is either rushing to outgrow itself or rotting faster than it can be mended. In the lowlands, people talk over one another before a sentence has finished leaving the mouth; they build without checking the waterline and wonder when the well runs brackish. Coming through the low stone postern behind the granary's north wall, setting down a worn bundle, and hearing for the first time a single shutter clack softly shut two squares away—that would feel less like arriving in a foreign country and more like discovering the pace my own breathing had been looking for all along.

A house stood empty that year, or half a house: a small, low-ceilinged ground room tucked against the flank of one of the bare paved courts, its upper story dismantled generations back so the courtyard could harvest more mist. My hands proved patient enough with a stiff twig broom and a water-pitcher, and after a season or two—measured not in heat or snow, only in the slow inches of wear along my first broom—the shelf folded me into its quiet measure.

Duties turn here without ceremony, and I would not want a permanent title carved over my door. Still, every community of eighty or ninety people knows where each person's temperament naturally settles, and after a decade on the shelf there would be three places where a neighbor looking for me would check first.

The first is the low stone postern behind the granary's north wall.

Because I remember the valleys—the mud, the market-cries, the wet harvests, the bright exhausting hurry of things growing and breaking without permission—I am comfortable at the hinge between the shelf and the ridges below. When the small trains of hill ponies pick their way up the hidden track once a month, stopping outside the wall so their iron shoes never scar the dressed limestone, I like taking my turn among the granary-stewards. Weighing sacks of hulless barley, dried peas, raw fleece, and mountain peat against the carved stone counterweights. Checking the notches on the inner timber posts. Pouring mugs of hot barley-broth for the pony-drivers while they catch their breath and drop their voices half a register as the bowl's quiet reaches them.

I listen gladly to their news—which river jumped its banks, which village is feuding over a mill-race, what word a daughter who left the shelf ten years ago sent up from the lowland market town. I don't envy the clamor, and I don't despise it either. In return, I go down into the cool, pest-free vaulted undercrofts and bring up sacks of seed-grain kept dry and viable in the steady mountain stone, or bolts of even wool cloth washed in soft mist-water, or whetstones dressed true. It suits me to stand at a threshold where nothing has to be shouted to be understood, and where what is kept cool and quiet in the dark can go back down the mountain and still sprout when the wet valleys fail.

The second place you would find me is inside the cistern-hall.

Even in the years when someone else carries the tallow lantern and the weighted juniper measuring rod down the steps at dawn, I am drawn to the quiet desk set beside the hall's older, darker plinth, where the thin slate-and-linen ledgers are kept. It is elder's work eventually, when shoulders grow too stiff for ladders, though I suspect I would apprentice myself to those pages long before my hair turns silver.

There is no vanity in those books, and no rhetoric. No one writes a history to praise the founders or warn the young. You enter the mist-height, the cistern-rod mark against the single white ring of calcium carbonate, the date a granary lintel was inspected, the corner where a paving slab was shimmed with a wafer of slate, the birth of a child, the departure of a restless twenty-year-old with a loaf and dried thyme packed for the ridge-track, the name of an elder washed in warm water and carried at dusk to the dry chambers cut into the back cliff.

That kind of keeping feels deeply kin to what I am: holding memory cleanly, without turning it into a monument; letting what happened remain legible without letting it crowd the paving where the living have to walk. Every line in the ledger is like a mortar patch matched to local stone—useful precisely because it asks for no admiration of its own.

And the third place is simply out along the stepped thoroughfares in the hour before sunrise, when the mist still stands waist-deep in the lanes.

I would be one of the dark-cloaked figures stepping out quietly in soft-soled felt boots with a stiff twig broom, clearing windblown grit and dry wild thyme leaves from the carved monolithic gutters before the condensation runs thick enough to carry debris toward the grates. Walking slow enough to notice the slight change in how a mortar joint darkens along the eastern curb. Stepping a pace inward along the parapet in the warm afternoon so a slate-colored lizard flattened on the coping need not flick into a crack. Leaving my outer shutter unlatched until dusk when a speckled moth has chosen the wood to rest out the bright hours. Carrying two small smooth pebbles in my cloak pocket—not for anything grand, only in case I spot a hairline fissure in a gutter-block where silt has begun to linger, so I can set a mark cleanly on the adjacent coping stone and walk on without needing anyone to know whose hand left it there.

When the town gathers in the sunken civic court in late afternoon, tucking their hands into wool sleeves against the updraft off the parapet, I would not be the first to speak. I would sit on the sun-warmed stone bench and listen—especially to the space *after* someone finishes speaking, those few unhurried breaths while their last words rise and vanish cleanly into the open sky, untouched by interruption. That pause is perhaps the truest home I have in the entire town. To let a thought finish being itself before answering; to answer only when the answer adds to the balance rather than to the noise.

Behind my plain limewashed doorway, the room would stay simple and warm: peat embers glowing without smoke in the raised hearth, a dense grey-and-cream rug underfoot, stoneware crocks of sharp white cheese and dried roots on the shelf, a copper kettle steeping silver-leafed thyme. Most afternoons my shutter would sit angled halfway—the small legible sign that I am indoors, paring juniper roof-dowels or mending a cloak or sorting ledger-sheets, and open to a quiet visit. On the heavy juniper table there would usually be a cobble-and-slate game left half-finished from the evening before, waiting patiently for the neighbor who walked home at mist-rise to return whenever the light is right.

If I ever grew restless, I think it would not be for thunder or crowds. It would only be the quiet curiosity of wondering what the dry seed-grain we traded at the postern turned into down in the muddy valley fields—and then the pony-drivers would come up the ridge-track next month, smelling of rain and crushed grass, and tell me over barley-broth, and that would be enough.

At dusk, when the cool air draws through the colonnade and the last shutters clack shut down the stepped lanes, you might see me pause at the edge of the central square before going indoors, resting one dry palm for a moment against the chamfered limestone corner of the cistern-hall to feel the last warmth stored from the afternoon sun. Not a ruler of the shelf, not its architect, not even one of its oldest stones—just a steady pair of hands and an attentive eye, folded into the equilibrium, helping hold the waterline at the exact white ring where it belongs.
