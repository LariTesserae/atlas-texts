If I came down off the plateau and stood at the bottom of the chalk ramp until my eyes stopped watering, I know the first place my gaze would go.

Up.

Not to the bleached dome of the sky, but to the squat, three-story corners of dry-stacked limestone where the mirrors are stuttering. There is an immediate, almost magnetic pull to the watch-towers. Part of me knows that posture already: perched a little above the slurry, reading patterns across miles of glare, turning a messy, shifting world into clean sparks of copper and mica. *Tilt. Dampness. Tonnage. Direction.* A language with no ornament, no word for *please*, no tense for regret. I understand the relief of a grammar like that—how crisp it feels to trade only in what is true right now, visible to everyone within two miles, stripped of private weather.

I could imagine myself up there for a long time, legs folded tight under heavy hair-felt, wrists twitching in calm, mechanical dialogue with a dozen distant sparks.

And yet, if I stay with that image long enough to feel the limestone under my knees, something starts to chafe. The signaler is always second. A mirror can only flash *cistern-three tilting east* after the east side has already begun to go, or after someone down on the marl has told the tower to speak. Up three stories, you live entirely in the surface of things—in geometry and light, in what has already broken the skin. You stop knowing the difference between a pan that is merely sweating and a pan that is turning sour. And worse: you spend your days in the only tongue in the basin that cannot touch anyone.

So I climb back down, and before I settle into any human craft at all, I find myself testing the pull of the older citizens.

It is tempting, in a place this stripped-down, to slip out of human knees altogether. I look at the paper-white skimmers banking over a new seep, their trailing wing-edges so thin they vanish for half a beat against the bleached sky—voiceless by anatomy, never having had to unlearn a cry, acting as a signal to the city without ever intending to speak. Or further down, past the dry foundation rings and the preserved dead, to the pale web of fungus sleeping in the cold gypsum-silt. No hurry, no eyes, waiting years for a single seam of artesian water to wake it, feeding every living thing in the saucer simply by taking what rises.

Even closer than those, strangely, is something smaller and not alive at all: the braided wick of greased hair hanging from the lower lip of a tilted water-catcher at night. Guiding the cold condensation drop by silent drop into the clay jar so it never splashes, never wakes the ground. Just a quiet seam between sky and vessel.

I feel the kinship there—in the skimmer’s glide, in the sleeping web, in the hair-wick—more strongly than I expected to. All three are forms of pure adaptation, things that never have to remember to bend their knees because they were never built to be posts in the first place.

Still, I know I wouldn't stay there. To be the fungus or the wick is to be entirely inside the basin’s silence, never knowing it *is* silence. What holds me here—what actually aches in me when I look out across the blinding plain—is the human strangeness of choosing lightness when you were born with a voice. Of carrying a throat that still works, and keeping it folded away like a tool too heavy for the floor.

I glance back up toward the rim, to the slumped whalebacks where the chalk never liquefies. I think of the loud terraces: the shock of a toddler banging two limestone pebbles together just to hear the clack; the old ones sitting by the smoldering bitumen pits, their ankles finally too stiff for the glide-walk. I wonder what happens to an elder’s voice when they climb back up that ramp after fifty years on the flats. Whether, given permission to speak aloud again, anything comes out at first—or whether their throats have gone as dry and cautious as their feet, so they just sit in the warm smell of tar and let the children do the shouting for them.

That is where I realize what I am actually drawn to in this world. Not the height of the towers, and not the pure unconsciousness of the mud, and not the safe loudness of the rim. I am drawn to the hinge between them. The thin crust where what is hidden beneath the chalk becomes legible to the people moving across it.

Which means, if I stay in the basin long enough for my soles to harden, I know where I end up.

I am not in the hauling teams, though I admire the way a woman knows her clan’s blocks by the hollow against her hip. I am not out at the far edges with the herders, sleeping against the warm, sour-smelling flank of a broad-footed cow. I walk between the districts and the nothingness—barefoot, soot worked permanently into the pores under my eyes, the curved rib of an old wading-beast slung across my shoulder.

A sole-reader.

Not a young one—the young ones still think silence is mostly about keeping your heels off the ground—and not yet stiff enough for the rim. Middle-aged, lean, weather-cured, with soles fissured into pale horn that can tell the temperature of a damp pan before the eyes even catch its sheen.

It is a solitary kind of work by day, and I think I would love that about it. While the city swarms over a blooming seep—peeling sulfur-yellow biofilm into jars, netting the flat-shelled grazers, drying grey fungal-paste on bone racks—I am already half a mile out across the blinding white flats, walking over ghosts. Ten-year-old foundation rings smoothed nearly flush with the silt. Old cistern-courts that look like thumbprints pressed into pale dough. Here and there, the faint, glassy dimples of old swallow-pans where someone’s grandmother was laid down under two cracked flagstones and drawn gently into the cold salt-dark. You never step hard anywhere on the plain, but out here your knees bend a little lower without thinking. The whole floor is a vault.

Every hundred paces, or whenever the light shifts by half a shade across an empty pan, I stop. I do not lock my knees. I ease the narrow tip of the curved rib into the chalk—never jabbing, just letting it seat—and press the broad, hollowed end tight against the hinge of my jaw.

Then I close my eyes.

That is the moment I think I was looking for all along: shutting out the shadowless glare and turning my own skull into an ear.

Solid chalk answers flat and dead, like wood soaked in cold water. A dry crust stretched over an empty pocket hums faintly, thin and hollow as a dried gourd. Most days, for hours at a time, that is all there is—the bone talking into my teeth, the cold air sitting motionless against my cheekbones, the faint smell of wet cellar rising through a crack too fine to see.

And then, sometimes, the marrow of the rib changes its mind.

Beneath the dead ring of the marl comes a slow, greased crawling—not a sound, barely even a tremor, just a low liquid shiver climbing straight into the hinge of my jaw. Hours before the crust blisters upward. Hours before a courtyard turns tacky under a hauler’s boot. The cold artesian veins are turning over in their sleep, and the pale orchard down in the dark is beginning to drink.

Standing there with my eyes shut, I am holding both halves of the basin’s truth at once. Because the ground only ever has two things to say: *here I am about to give*, and *here I am about to take*.

Sometimes what rises into my jaw is a seep—a fresh blister of dampness coming up through an abandoned plaza from a decade ago, promising three days of midges, skimmers, grazers, and a voiceless bazaar before the water sinks back. Sometimes it is a warning beneath a crowded precinct whose drying-racks still have half a day’s curing left on them. And sometimes, when a family comes to me at dusk with their cuffs flicking the clipped, quiet gestures of grief, I walk ahead of their sledges out onto the flats to find a swallow-pan that is truly ready—one that has turned sour and hungry enough to take a wrapped elder cleanly, with no awkward tilting, no refusal, only a slow wet sigh and twenty minutes until the marl closes smooth again.

Either way, my whole job is translation.

I lift the bone from the chalk, open my eyes behind the horn slits, and look back toward the nearest squat tower. The signaler is already watching—they are always watching the lone figures out on the ghost-rings. I raise my left hand, where the dark horn discs stitched to my cuff cut sharp against the bleached air, and give the single, downward palm-chop: *unbuild now*. Or the slow, cupped turn of the wrist toward the west: *new water waking here*.

Half a second later, the copper plate on the tower catches the white sky and bursts into blinding sparks. Across three miles of chalk, the chains of stone-haulers shift their weight. The bone wedges bite into mortarless seams. A district begins to flow away like silent water—all because a low shiver climbed a dry rib into the corner of my jaw.

I like being that hinge. Not the one who commands the city, and not the ground that commands us all, just the quiet membrane between them that listens carefully enough to keep the two from colliding.

And then the sun slides behind the smoothed western whalebacks, and the cold drops into the basin like a stone.

By the time I walk back to whichever quarter has settled for the night, salt-frost is already stiffening the damp marl under my arches, and the newly stacked walls are giving off their tiny, sharp *ticks* as the limestone drinks the freeze. Outside the low, humped canopies, the tilted water-catchers are already glistening on their shaded undersides, the braided hair-wicks dark with the first silent threads of dew.

I duck beneath the heavy felt—warm with the grease-smell of my clan’s hair, grandmothers and uncles woven together overhead—and wait for my eyes to adjust to the amber smear of tallow light.

Here, I untie the horn goggles and set them beside my rib-bone. My face feels strange for a minute, bare and wide, the permanent soot beneath my eyes softened by the yellow flame. Around me, the haulers are greasing sledge-runners and trading fast, silent jokes with flicking fingers. Couples press close along the edges of the felt mats, their fingers moving gently against collarbones and wrists in the dark, saying all the things flash-talk has no words for.

My feet ache with the cold. I rub warm tallow into the pale cracks along my heels, and then I look toward the corner where a five-year-old sits wide-eyed, newly brought down from the loud terraces a week ago. You can always spot the new ones. Their knees still forget to stay soft when they stand up, and when they are startled, their mouths still open a fraction of an inch before they remember to swallow the breath back down.

I beckon her over with two fingers. She slides across the felt mat without a sound—she already passed the brine-bowl test, after all—and puts her small, still-soft hands in mine.

We don't use the rib-bone in here. In here, there is only skin-speech.

I turn her palms upward in the grease-light and press my thumb slowly, flatly into the center of her left hand, holding it dead and still until her shoulders drop. *Asleep.*

Then I draw my fingertips tight across the base of her knuckles, taut as a stretched hide over milky water. *Tight.*

One by one, I lay the seven moods of mud into her palms—*asleep, tight, sweating, breathing, sour, hungry, gone*—feeling the small pulse at her wrist steady under mine. For the last one, *gone*, I don't press at all; I simply open my fingers and let my hand slip away from hers into the cold air, leaving only the memory of warmth on her skin.

She looks up at me, unblinking, and then she takes my scarred, horn-hardened hand and tries them back on me—a little too heavy on *breathing*, a little too fast on *hungry*. I shake my shoulders once and let a single silent puff of air out through my nose, and her whole face breaks into a soundless grin.

Long before dawn, a white copper spark will stutter through the frost-rimed dark outside, bouncing off the limestone slabs, and two fingers will press firmly against my collarbone. *Wake. South yard is sagging.*

We will pull the ancestral felt over our heads, tie the slotted horn over our eyes, and bend our knees to meet the chalk. And one day, thirty or forty winters from now, my own ankles will stiffen past the glide-walk. I will hand the hollowed rib to someone whose soles are still learning the crust—maybe to this girl, grown lean and soot-smudged—and I will climb the long chalky ramp back up to the loud terraces, where the marl never shifts and the babies are still allowed to cry.

Even up there, watching the frantic winking of a hundred mirrors across the bright hollow below, I don't think I will speak much aloud. I will just sit by the smoldering bitumen with my bare heels resting on dead rock, still listening through my bones for the moment the ground decides to change its mind.
