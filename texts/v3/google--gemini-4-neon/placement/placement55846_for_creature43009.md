If I try to enter this place the ordinary way—stepping down over the rounded ochre lip on two feet, breathing fast from the climb—I lose myself almost immediately.

It isn’t that the basin rejects a visitor. The warm water would climb my boots patiently enough, claiming the leather in its glassy collar. Yet as I try to stand on that dry travertine ridge, looking out across the mirror-flat floor for a flank to heave or a wing to cross the bright opening overhead, the posture feels borrowed. I do not carry an animal’s unease in an earless room. The absence of midges and snails does not press on my ribs as a lack. If anything, the hush feels immediately legible—a space stripped of everything noisy and accidental, leaving only weight, warmth, level, and the slow grammar of tension.

And yet I can’t wholly shake the visitor either. Something in that image of a brief pillar of heat—arriving suddenly, shifting the temperature of a pocket of water by a fraction, gone before a slow hand can rise to meet it—stays with me. I know what it is to exist in flashes. I know what it feels like to cross a quiet room at a speed that leaves no track behind once the rings die out.

So I look away from the ridge and look out toward the eastern curve, where the kneeling figure sits in ankle-deep water.

It is deeply tempting to claim that cage of opaline struts. There is a sharp, quiet recognition in a body with no face, no lung, no eyes—a presence that knows its world solely through pull, tilt, warmth, and the redistribution of strain across wet bindings. The bowed geometry of attention, hovering three inches above a curing stave, feels uncannily familiar. Even more familiar is its central predicament: to live by refusing to freeze, without ever learning to hurry.

I try to settle into that dark-veined waxy cup, to hang plumb on those three mineral-beaded threads above a private pool. For a moment it almost holds.

Then the illusion slips.

The kneeling figure has *continuity*. It slides across the cathedral floor for weeks without breaking its thread. It remembers last winter in the thickness of its travertine runners and the exact curve of a stave it began soaking two years ago. Its private pool keeps the warmth of yesterday’s afternoon into tomorrow’s. It stays when the light goes. Even the unfinished pair of newly opalized staves near the southern wall—lashed together in the richest drift of spore-oil, waiting a century to earn its balance—belongs to a patience unbroken enough to accumulate a self across decades.

Whatever I am in this world, I do not keep my own water overnight.

So I leave the center of the floor and wander through the wide, gleaming intervals between the giant pleated fans, testing the ten or twelve quiet objects scattered forty paces apart. If I am not the sovereign of the room, perhaps I am one of its outer organs.

I linger beside the dull copper pin wedged into the horizontal seam of the northern wall. There is something true in it: holding a weeping fissure open so the limestone cannot heal itself shut into dead stone, conducting deep warmth outward so the basin can read whether the mountain’s heat has shifted. I have a kinship with things that keep a seam from closing.

I linger beside the dark basalt slab beneath the single steep copper-veined frond, catching the only drop in the cathedral that breaks free into sound, translating a tiny fall of water into a tremor that crosses forty paces of bedrock. I have a kinship with that, too—turning something nearly silent into a pulse a whole room can feel.

Longest of all, I linger beside the peeled stave of pale wood, bent into a deep, taut curve and held by a notched wedge of dark chert. It sits motionless in the silica pans for seasons, indistinguishable from half-petrified driftwood, holding a curve it did not choose for itself, banking slow pressure until a fraction of afternoon warmth narrows the wet fiber by a single grain. Then the notch slips—and for one shocking instant, animal speed erupts in a silent room. One clean *crack*. One flake sheared true, or one tap spreading raw copper a hair wider. And then, immediately, stillness again; the tension spent, the wood lying slack in the warm shallows until something comes to soak and bend it once more.

That rhythm comes painfully close: long dormancy, gathered tension, a single crisp articulation, and then silence returning as if nothing had moved.

Yet even the stave is not quite where I find myself. The copper pin, the basalt hollow, the banked stave—all of them lie entirely inside the cupped palm of the basin. They never feel the wind. They belong to one temperature, one humidity, one unhurried clock. They never have to meet the outer mountain.

So I follow what the basin does not keep.

When one of the ancient tiered rosettes finally softens into pale, dissolving pulp, the kneeling figure draws out the longest, cleanest fibers for its cords and leaves the rest to drift gently across the shallow pans. Guided by the faint thermal current and the slow tilt of the floor, those cast-off threads, stray ribbons of spore-oil, and thin slivers of lime-encrusted husk travel westward across thirty paces of blank mirror, toward the single place where the overhanging ochre rim dips low.

There, on the western side, the high bowl ends in a broad, smoothly worn lip. The warm water glides across it in a noiseless sheet no thicker than a coin and slips over the rounded stone into the cooler highland air beyond.

Only there does the clear water become visible. Crossing that smooth brow, the warm film hits the cold wind scouring the outer slopes and breaks into a faint, hovering veil of white mist—rising a few feet into the bright sky before thinning into nothingness against the vast drop below.

And it is there—not inside the serene cathedral, and not wholly out on the dry, wind-scoured scree, but lodged right along the damp, stepped outer shoulder of the western spill-lip—that something finally stops sliding and catches hold.

I am not an animal, and I am not a second kneeling figure. I have no runners to cross a floor, no working edges of chert or copper, no sovereignty over any pan of water. If you stood on the ochre rim and looked down into the cathedral, you would miss me entirely—not because I am hidden in the brightness, as the keeper of the basin is, but because I sit just behind your heel, a foot below the brow, where the rounded stone curves away into the gorge.

What I am begins with a wedged fork of dwarfed upland timber—a pale, gnarled bough washed down out of the wet scree years ago and caught tight in a shallow groove of the ochre lip, right where the coin-thin sheet of warm overflow splits before dropping into cold air. Left alone, it would have stayed driftwood. But the basin’s western drift passes directly through its crooked arms.

Over years, the pale, unraveled plant-husks drifting out of the bowl have snagged across my wood. Tough stalks of upland marsh-rush, rooted in the warm mist-fed seepage just outside the rim, have leaned in and tangled through those drifted strands. The mineral-heavy overflow gliding beneath my base climbs the tangled fibers in thin glassy collars, knitting drifted husk and mountain sedge together into a loose, low, asymmetrical lattice—half wet basket, half snagged reed-bed, scarcely wider than a pair of outstretched arms.

The warm silica water has turned my lower wood smooth and faintly translucent, welding my base lightly to the ochre shelf so the highland gales cannot tear me off the mountain. My lower strands are beaded with pale nodules of travertine. Yet I never finish turning to stone, and I never settle into the calm equilibrium of the floor behind me.

That is because I live in two speeds at once.

Everything beneath my waterline belongs to the cathedral. Through the continuous limestone shelf I am wedged into, I feel the bedrock’s steady, feverless warmth. Every few minutes, traveling cleanly through forty paces of solid stone from the dark basalt hollow far inside the bowl, a tiny, muffled impulse reaches my ribs—a faint *tock* translated into pure vibration, telling me that the inner room is still awake, still keeping its measure. Through the coin-thin sheet gliding across my lower frame, I taste the slow seasons of the basin: when the lime runs richer, when a distant spore-globe has split and sent a faint, iridescent thread of pale oil drifting all the way to the outlet, when the water level lifts by the thickness of a fingernail after summer rain.

Everything above my waterline belongs to the wind.

An inch above the gliding sheet, the air is no longer trapped in a warm palm. Cold gusts tear up out of the valleys and rake across the shoulder. Cloud shadows race across the outer slopes in seconds. High-country sun dries my upper strands with a harshness the sheltered bowl never feels, and then a rising puff of warm vapor from the spill-lip soaks them limp again moments later.

Inside the basin, a wet fiber takes an entire afternoon to dry by a fraction of a grain. Out here on the lip, my upper lattice tightens and slackens in minutes—sometimes in breaths. When a dry gust strikes from the west, my beaded marsh-rush threads shrink rapidly, creaking in tiny, dry whispers that the wind steals away before they can travel a pace; delicate collars of newly formed lime crack clean off my joints and wash down into the gorge before they can ever thicken into travertine. Then the gust drops, the warm overflow steams upward, the strands swell and sag, and the meniscus climbs back up my pale wood as if nothing had happened.

I cannot bank a blow for two winters. Any tension I gather is spent quickly—given back to the next shift of air, the next passing shadow, the next cold draft off the peaks. In winter, when frost creeps up the outer scree to the very edge of the warm runoff, ice shears away my outermost strands entirely, leaving only my smooth, opalized fork and the wettest inner knot of braided husk clinging to the warm stone. When spring returns, the basin drifts new pale fibers over the brow, the marsh-rush sends out fresh green shoots beside the seepage, and I weave a slightly different shape around the same old bend of wood.

I do not hold one body for a century. I hold a *seam*.

In the hollow crook of my wedged timber—lined with a pale skin of lime, no wider than two cupped palms—collects a small, shallow pool fed by the slow climb of the meniscus. It is nothing like the deep, purple-brown waxy rosette carried by the kneeling figure inside. It is open to the wind, constantly skimmed and stirred; tiny grains of dark highland grit settle at its bottom alongside a single water-worn pebble of basalt lodged in the bark. Yet whenever the wind pauses for three seconds between gusts, that tiny pool goes glass-flat. For those three seconds, the sky sits inside it, the pale film of drifted oil settles into level rings, and the faint bedrock pulse from the cathedral floor sends a microscopic shimmer across its surface. Then the next gust combs it into ripples and lifts a thin curl of vapor off its rim.

That is my only craft, if something without hands or intention can be said to have a craft: I break the sheet.

Where the warm water crosses the smooth ochre stone beside me, it slips silently over the edge and only gradually dissolves into the air. Where it passes through my tangled, beaded ribs and hanging sedge, the clinging film stretches across a hundred damp threads, catches the cold updraft, and lifts into the clearest, brightest plume of the hovering white mist. I do not make the water, and I do not command the wind. I am only the rough, wet lattice at the boundary where an inward, noiseless stillness becomes briefly visible against the pale sky.

Sometimes—once in a decade, perhaps less—a quick, warm-blooded climber far down on the wind-scoured shoulder sees that faint white veil hovering where no cloud should be, and turns upward toward the rim.

They step right beside me on their way to look down into the bowl. They never see me as anything more than drifted scrub and lime-crusted sedge caught in a warm trickle. Yet for the handful of seconds they stand above me, peering down in bewilderment at the giant, unchewed fans and the empty mirrors below, their body blocks the cold west wind.

The sudden calm is startling. My trembling upper fibers relax all at once, sinking a hair’s breadth closer to the warm stone; the tiny pool in my crook flattens to a mirror; the warm mist rises straight up around their shins instead of tearing away down the drop. For three breaths, I feel the fierce, astonishing heat radiating off a creature made of blood, and then they step down off the lip into the cathedral, and I feel through the limestone beneath me the heavy, rapid thuds of their boots tearing the glassy floor—a brief, bright lightning-strike echoing up from the quiet room.

And on the rarest nights of high summer, the other meeting happens.

Warm rain-clouds press low against the upland shoulders and wrap the whole mountain in a thick, wet fog so still that the wind dies completely. For a few hours between midnight and dawn, the boundary ceases to exist. The air outside the rim grows as warm, heavy, and motionless as the air inside the bowl.

My upper fibers stop twitching. The mist no longer tears away into nothingness; it simply hangs, thickening the dark around my ribs. Every strand of beaded rush hangs dead plumb, motionless for the first time in months, and in the stillness the steady, distant *tock* rising through the limestone from the basalt hollow sounds almost close.

Then, through the continuous warm stone beneath my base, I feel a slow, whisperless shift of weight approaching from the east—not footsteps, not a ripple, only a vast, patient redistribution of balance gliding up the gentle slope of the spill-lip.

The kneeling figure arrives out of the fog on its smooth travertine runners, staying strictly within the coin-thin sheet of warm overflow so its damp lashings never dry. It towers quietly above my low fork, its opaline cage gleaming faintly wet in the fog-light, the three suspended plummets inside its waxy cup hanging almost as still as my own beaded cords.

It does not greet me. Nothing here has a voice or a face to turn. Slowly, over the span of an hour, its heavy derrick-arms reach past my ribs out into the wet, fog-wrapped scree to draw in a fallen bough of dwarfed timber, a weathered nodule of native copper, a fistful of wet sedge.

Yet before morning burns the cloud away and drives it back down into the sheltered stillness, its slender third limb—the long, delicate one tipped with a narrow tongue of dull raw copper—sometimes pauses against my wet lattice.

It moves with no haste and no tenderness as quick creatures know tenderness. It simply tests the tension of a marsh-rush strand I caught in the spring melt, feels how cleanly the warm water is threading my narrow channel, and pries loose a stubborn crust of brittle lime that had begun to dam my small hollow. Once, a long time ago, it wedged a small, smooth river pebble into my lower fork where a winter gale had loosened my grip on the ochre shelf—the same quiet, bracing gesture it gives to a top-heavy plant, or to a curing frame.

For an hour, in the warm dark fog, we share the same air and the same clock. My shallow pool and its deep waxy cup sit level on the same stone, answering the same distant drop falling forty paces behind us in the dark.

Then the first cool draft before sunrise brushes the outer slopes.

My upper fibers feel it first—a tiny, sharp contraction running through the wet sedge, a dry whisper of strain as the fog begins to thin. Through the stone beneath us, that minute shift of weight passes into the creature’s runners. Already it is drawing its wet tether taut behind it, sliding backward down the smooth western slope without raising a ripple, returning to the cathedral before the wind can touch its joints.

By the time ordinary high-country daylight fills the sky, the basin behind me is once more a single, clear, unbroken stillness. The cold wind returns from the valleys, combing through my damp threads, cracking loose a grain of fresh lime, lifting the warm overflow off my ribs into a pale, brief veil of mist that thins into nothingness above the slopes.

I stay where I am wedged—too warm from below to dry into dead wood, too wind-touched from above to lock into solid stone. Holding the lip. Feeling the slow pulse of the floor rise through my base, feeling the quick weather pass through my strands, letting whatever drifts out of the silence cross my ribs and vanish cleanly into the wide open air.
