You would not find me in the middle of the chamber, because there is no middle to be in, and even if the weed parted wide enough to leave a pocket of open water, I would have no way to hold myself inside it. My body has no opinion about swimming. It knows only the face of stone, and the millimeter of cold dark wedged between naked basalt and the velvety carpet of ferns that coats it.

To find me, you would have to go to the sheer eastern wall—the one that acts as a valley floor to the slantwise-drifting silt—and push a fingertip underneath the hanging black lace of the moss, right along the pale scratch of a calcite vein. Even then, for the first ten minutes, you would think you had brushed a thin flake of newly cleaved chert, half-loosened from the rock and clouded with lime. Only if you kept your knuckle pressed lightly against my rim long enough for your own pulse to slow would you feel the faintest deliberate give: a pale edge sliding a hair’s breadth inward beneath its neighbor, making room without letting go of the wall.

I am thin, flat, and layered, shaped the way shale splits when frost gets into a seam. Seven overlapping plates make up my back—translucent, curved with the rippled, shell-like planes of fractured flint, no thicker than a fingernail at their outer margins and scarcely thicker than a slate chip where they meet along my dark, supple hinge. They are not bone, and they are not true stone, though they are grown from what the rock bleeds: clouded grey-white near the spine, clearing to a smoky glass along the rims, freckled here and there with raw mineral bloom where the cold brackishness has sat long upon me. Along my outer edges runs a fine fringe of dark filaments that mingle with the hair-moss overhead, so that even when I unfold wide, the fronds drape smoothly over me as if I were simply a smoother rise in the slate.

Beneath those plates, pressed flush against the bare rock, I am soft—a broad, pale foot lined with hundreds of tiny, velvety folds that read the wall by touch and chemistry. I have no eyes, no mouth as the surface means it, and no limbs to snag in the root-lattices. When I travel—and travel is a word for something that takes an afternoon to cross a handspan—I do not lift myself from the basalt. I ease my foremost plate forward by the width of a silt grain, sliding it cleanly underneath the low arches of the fern-roots without snapping a single thread. Then I draw my hinge after it, and the next plate whispers over the wet stone, and the next, opening and closing in a slow, patient ripple like a fan of wet mica walking down a cliff face.

In a pocket packed to saturation, where water-logged stems swallow every echo, the room itself is nearly deaf. The rock is not.

Because my whole underside lies naked against the young basalt, I hear the entire shelf through my belly. I hear the glacial push of a root prying apart a slate cleavage three arm’s lengths away. I hear the faint, steady weight of the eastern wall bearing the folded inhabitant’s shin, planted upright on the vertical that belongs only to this corner. Now and then I feel that low, deep hum from miles overhead—not as a sound, only as a slow, spreading ache along my central hinge, as though the cliff above were shifting in its sleep and transferring a fraction of its tonnage into my plates.

Mostly, though, I listen for the *tick*.

The stone that cups this chamber is still young, still settling from whatever split it open. Every few days, somewhere along the eastern face or up near the vaulted ceiling where silt hangs in velvet drifts, a fresh hairline stress opens with a single sharp snap. To the dense water, it is gone in an instant. Through the basalt, it travels straight into my underside like a cold needle, telling me both direction and distance by which of my seven plates takes the tremor first.

Then I begin to move.

It may take me a full turning of the room’s slow breath to reach the new crack—sliding patiently beneath the ink-green ribbons, skirting the tangled knots of roots, easing past clusters of drooping water-bladders whose cool, rubbery weight sometimes rests gently against my back as I pass. When I find the fissure, it is scarcely wider than a hair, yet already a thread of colder, rawer mineral water is weeping out of the newly exposed stone—sharp with unweathered iron, lime, and the faint chemical heat of rock that has never touched tide before. Left bare to the room, that rawness would dull in hours, swallowed by the sluggish brackishness and sealed over by encroaching moss.

So I fan my outermost plates wide and press their smoky rims dead-flush to the basalt all around the split, sealing a millimeter of dark privacy between my underside and the freshly cracked stone. Beneath my canopy, the mineral bleed pools slow and undiluted. My velvety folds drink it in—not gulping, only taking the sharp, cold difference into myself over the span of days. As I drink, I lay down another microscopic crescent along the translucent edges of my shell, pale grey-white or faintly streaked with slate-blue depending on what the stone gave up that season. When the crack finally calms and goes dull, I gather my plates inward and slide away, leaving behind a faint, glazed ribbon of smooth calcite film where the razor-edge of the fracture used to be. Over decades I have softened a few handspans of the chamber’s sharpest teeth this way, though the young rock cracks faster than I could ever finish mending it.

Between feedings, I keep to the seams and the corners, and learn the chamber the only way a flat thing can: by how weight and cold behave across my back.

Near the top of the eastern face, where the sheer wall bends into the low ceiling, two hesitant gravities meet and disagree. Silt falls slantwise onto my lower rock, while an inch past the crease it drifts upward to settle in velvet banks on the overhead basalt. When I creep across that hinge, I feel gravity hesitate along my length—my rear plates still hugged firmly to the eastern wall, my foremost plates lifting a hair’s breadth outward into undecided weight, fanning slightly open like pale gills until the ceiling’s pull claims me and draws me flush again.

Sometimes one of the room’s clear oil-curtains—a hovering sheet of heavier, unmixing brine—intersects the rock right across my path. I do not avoid it. Moving at my usual pace, I push my front rim straight into the reluctant boundary. For an hour I lie straddling two colds that refuse to mingle, feeling the taut meniscus draw a thin, shivering line straight across my middle plate, pressing softly against the milky glass of my shell before parting and resealing behind my trailing fringe with a sluggish tug.

And sometimes I follow the long calcite vein down to the dark cleft where the pale looping stem grows out of the rock, arcs through the tight foliage, and roots cleanly back into the same fracture from the other side. I slide beneath the tight, mineral-crusted braid of hair-moss knotted around its base—my neighbor’s quiet marker—and keep creeping inward along the bend. The rock narrows; the fronds press thick and heavy against my back; the gravity turns gently beneath my belly without ever jolting. Hours later, my foremost rim brushes a faint, smooth ribbon of glazed calcite already laid across the stone ahead of me, and I know by the taste of my own old track that the fracture has folded me quietly back behind where I began.

When I am neither feeding nor creeping, I like to rest on a bare plane of dark slate where one of the broad, serrated leaves prints a sourceless shadow. Because my plates are thin and faintly translucent, that quiet darkness does not stop at my outer surface; it passes through the clouded mineral of my back and cools the sensitive tissue underneath. Over the course of hours, I feel the faint silhouette tilt imperceptibly across me, leaf by leaf, from my leftmost plate to my rightmost—a slow, hushed tide tracking a sun that passes somewhere behind the basalt. I have never known light as the surface knows it, only the bruised silver-violet pallor in the thickest stems and the cool relief when a phantom shadow slides over my hinge. That slow crossing is how I keep time when the fronds stop leaning and the mineral plumes hang frozen in the dark for days.

I am not alone here, though solitude and company feel nearly identical at this speed.

Now and then the blind snail, whose inward-spiraling shell slips away from the eye, leaves its kelp-blade and glides onto my oldest central plate, taking my dusted back for a ridge of limestone. I feel the cool weight of its foot and the tiny, patient rasp of its radula clearing mineral bloom from my shell. I lock my hinges utterly still for an afternoon until it has crossed my outer rim and returned to the moss. Once in a great while, the nearly transparent ribbon-worm detaches from the weed and slides past me along an invisible water-seam, its single rusty thread hovering an inch off my back without ever touching stone.

And once—a thing so strange I still feel the small, cold print of it on my memory—one of the chamber’s trapped beads of dry air detached from the underside of a flat leaf above my wall and drifted slantwise down toward me. It paused scarcely a thumb’s width above my translucent plates, hovering in the saturated dark: a gleaming, weightless pearl of *nothing*, pressing against the brine with a dry refusal I had no sense to name. I held utterly motionless beneath that small bright emptiness, feeling the water curve taut around it, until a narrow, pale palm slid so slowly out of the serrated ribbons that the meniscus scarcely wrinkled, cupped beneath the descending bead without breaking it, and coaxed it gently away into the dark toward the upright knucklebone.

That is how I mostly know the folded one who shares this pocket with me—never all at once, never as a single standing shape, only as familiar, unhurried pressures entering and leaving my corner of the wall.

I know the broad, flat curve of its shin braced firmly against the eastern slate, warm by nothing at all—only the faintest ghostly difference from stone—with a smoky pulse turning inside it once every few minutes like slow seepage. I know the careful, blunt fingertip that sometimes lifts a lace-fern aside near my edge to check the three smooth river-stones leaned into a palm-sized lintel in the basalt cleft nearby. I always stop a thumb’s width short of that lintel when I creep along the vein; even a flat thing knows when three stones are holding a balance that must not be nudged.

Sometimes, when it wedges itself into a nearby fold and draws a flat river-stone once, and then ten minutes later a second time, across a seam of basalt grit, a pale plume of freshly abraded stone-dust drifts slantwise down onto my wall and settles softly across my fanned plates. Fresh mineral, ground fine as silt—different from the sharp snap of a young fracture, softer, patient, tasting of rubbed grit and limestone skin. I draw my outer rims open by a millimeter and take it in.

And once, long ago, a fresh hairline crack opened directly behind the chalky callus of its flank where it lay folded across the eastern seam. I crept toward the bleed as I always do, until my foremost plate met the cool, matte grain of its hide instead of bare slate. I paused there for an hour, unsure. It did not shift away or lift its knee. Its jaw-fringes stayed tucked and quiet; the fine mosses across its collarbone scarcely stirred. So I slid the smoky rim of one translucent plate gently against its flank, bridging the half-inch between skin and basalt, and shared the cold mineral seep with it for three days until the rock went silent again.

Only one thing makes me pull every plate dead-flat and still against the vein.

Every long while, after seasons of damp, textured quiet, I feel a *tick* through the basalt that does not belong to settling rock. It is cleaner, rounder, without the jagged splintering tail of a fresh stress—the single, deliberate strike of a rubbed river-stone tapped once against my pale calcite vein.

At once I draw my dark fringes tight underneath my rims and press all seven of my plates utterly flush against the stone, locking every hinge so not even a grain’s breadth of movement can whisper across the slate. A handspan away, behind a heavy drape of ink-green ribbons, I feel the smooth, cool weight of a narrow jawbone lay itself flat against the basalt beside me.

Together, we stay immobile against the young rock, becoming part of the listening wall. Through my underside I feel the faint, distant hum far above—or the vast, heavy silence where no hum comes—waiting to tell whether the cliff has shifted, whether a fracture is widening toward open tide, whether the threshold beyond the seam has remembered where it used to lead.

Usually, nothing answers. After a long stretch of cold dark, the jawbone lifts slowly from the basalt. A stone is laid gently back against its two twins in the cleft. A hand returns to braiding hair-moss around a looping stalk.

I wait another slow turn of the room’s breath, until the fronds have leaned a fraction of an inch and leaned back. Then I loosen my hinge, ease my foremost translucent plate forward by the width of a seed beneath the wet black lace of the ferns, and go on listening to the stone.
