I wouldn’t find myself up on the condenser-roofs, though I would watch the Crown-Walkers with a kind of quiet envy whenever the steam parted and let the blinding ten-second sun catch their iron harness-hooks. My knees aren’t built for dropping flat behind a ceramic blast-lip with two seconds of warning, letting ten thousand tons of vanishing momentum punch the sky inches above my spine. And I wouldn’t live all the way down in the tea-colored water of the lower vaults, either—though I feel the pull of that warm, ninety-degree hush, where the sharp cracks of the towers flatten into an oceanic pulse against the ribs. I would want to talk to the Sump-Divers when they climbed out through the iron grates, blinking and salt-streaked, smelling of submerged brass. I would want to know what they found down there. I just wouldn’t want to stay under the floor all day.

I need the traffic of signals. I need to see the lamps talking across a wet canal, and I need a dry surface where noise can be slowed down until it turns into syntax.

So I would settle in the low middle—not on a sleek freight-quay, and not on one of the restless market-decks that folds itself inward like a startled insect every time an ore-convoy pulses amber down the cut. Somewhere heavier. One of the old, broad-shouldered blocks that sits low and stubborn in its foundation-slot because it carries dense weight the city’s blind logic prefers not to shove around without good reason. Two steps up from a narrow secondary canal, tucked right against the sweating iron flank of an auxiliary heat-exchanger, with a folded-space sluice set into the alley wall ten feet from the door—just a dark, thickness-less coin of water rimmed in chalky white limescale, pouring warm, clear runoff out of somewhere forty kilometers away.

At first, watching myself move through this place, I thought I might be a keeper of the drift—someone hunched over stamped copper ledgers, tracking where the wandering tenements went after a three-day squall, charting the eddies of the basin so people could find their cousins or collect an old debt. I know that impulse in myself: the urge to listen to a vast, churning system and draw its currents until they make sense.

But the longer I stand on that wet threshold, feeling the basin hum through the grooved resin soles of my boots, the more I realize that a ledger is too dry for this city. Streets are temporary agreements, and ink—even acid-ink on biofilm-vellum—is always half a step behind the stone. If you want to hold the memory of a place that never stops shifting, you don’t write it down. You fire it.

I would be a tuner of clay and bone. A maker of biters, whistles, and threshold tiles.

***

My shop would be mostly hush-room.

Out front, under a dripping strip of waxed mineral-cloth propped above the quay, there wouldn’t be much to look at—just a low sintered-stone bench, a hanging cluster of three weathered test-pipes that rattle softly whenever a barge glides past on its pale air-cushion, and a battered porcelain shutter-lamp bolted to the lintel, blinking a slow, steady copper-amber to tell the canal that the kiln is hot and the door is open. Beneath the lower drainage stairs, three of our block’s resident scavengers—thick-necked, slick-skinned, water-shedding things the color of wet slate—would sleep in a heavy, steaming heap right where the waste-heat bleeds through the masonry, lifting their flattened heads with a low chest-thrum whenever an unfamiliar pair of boots stops on the kerb, settling back down at two taps of my knuckles against the doorframe.

Inside, behind double-baffled shutters and walls quilted thick with pressed biofilm-cork, the roar of the basin finally drops to a dark, watery murmur. The air stays warm enough to dry wet wool in twenty minutes, smelling of damp gray basin-clay, scorched mineral salt, and the faint iron tang of the cooling-fin built into the back wall.

That back wall is why the workshop exists here. There is no timber in the basin, no charcoal, nothing to burn. My kiln is a tight, vaulted chamber of thick sintered brick tapped directly into a bled waste-heat flue from the exchanger next door, regulated by heavy ceramic baffles I slide open and shut with a hooked iron bar. When I crack the main baffle a thumb’s width, the fierce, dry shimmer of megawatts the city was going to throw away into the sky pours into the chamber instead, turning wet gray mud into stone dense enough to ring like bronze.

And the glaze comes straight off the alley wall. Every few mornings, before the first squall hits, I take a short brass chisel out to the folded-space sluice beside my door and chip away the thick, chalky collar of limescale crusted around its rim—minerals precipitated out of water that traveled forty kilometers through a hole no deeper than a coat of paint. Ground fine with a splash of desalinated water and a pinch of boiled, crushed slag-crab shell, it fires into a tough, pale, salt-gloss skin that can take a hundred years of grit-soled boots without wearing through.

***

Most of my days would be spent in the seam between the basin’s three languages—light, air, and bone—fixing the small instruments people use to keep from getting crushed by the machinery they live inside.

The biters come in constantly. A freight-handler drops theirs into a widening kerb-seam; a young Crown-Walker bites clean through a clay rod when a momentum-tower vents closer than expected and locks their jaw. They don’t want just any fired finger of clay. Every trade listens to a different layer of the city, and clay remembers how you build it.

For a barge-hand who needs to feel the low, oily thrum of a thinning field-cushion before their hull scrapes stone, I press a softer body—washed canal clay folded around a core of boiled amphibian rib, fired low enough to stay slightly open in the grain so the bass carries straight into the gums without rattling the teeth. For a Crown-Walker climbing the flank of a stubby ceramic tower, I mix a brutal, dense stoneware packed with fine ceramic grit and a spoonful of ancient, water-smoothed shards traded from the Sump-Divers at dusk—pieces of old arches pulled from the flooded layers that nobody knows how to formulate anymore. Fired near vitrification, that rod picks up the high, hair-thin shudder of a ceramic baffle-hinge two full seconds before the crown snaps open.

I test every one of them myself before I hand it over. I step out onto the wet quay, press the tip of the newly cooled rod against the parapet, clamp the other end lightly between my back molars, and shut my eyes.

That is the moment I think I would love most in this whole loud world. The second your teeth close on good clay, the deafening wall of spray and collision stops being noise and separates into clean, braided threads. You feel the steady, heavy breathing of the foundation-plates underfoot; thirty yards east, the faint upward whine of a freight-cut winding up to shift; half a mile north, the hollow *thump* of a thermal plume boiling off a heat-exchanger. It is like laying a hand on the ribs of a massive, unsentimental animal and discovering it isn’t angry—it’s just busy, and if you listen closely enough, it tells you where it’s about to put its foot.

My left ear would probably be half-gone by forty—not from carelessness, just the slow tax of living under a struck bell, maybe one bad morning in my twenties when a baffle-strap slipped on a wet stairwell right as a tower took the bill for a hundred tons of ore. A thin silver ring stays tucked behind that eardrum now, which makes tuning high-pitched whistles by ear alone a tricky business.

So I tune them by water.

On the heavy stone slab in the center of my hush-room sits a wide, shallow saucer of black-glazed ceramic filled to the lip with dark, mineral-rich canal brine. Above it, strung along the quilted ceiling on coils of salvaged reactive wire, hangs my library: four hundred small clay test-pipes, each tagged with a stamped copper chit bearing the name of a block—*Old Salt-Keel*, *Copper-Louver Nine*, *The Bent Cistern*, *Three-Fin Quay*—and the scratch-marks of its lineage. Whenever a tower cracks three blocks away, the whole ceiling gives a single, ghostly, half-audible shiver, a faint silver breath passing down the wires.

When a parent comes in to replace a cracked household whistle, or to commission a new one for a child who has finally grown out of their tether-line, I pull their block’s master-pipe down from the wire. I blow it softly over the saucer of dark brine and watch the surface pinch into crisp, trembling rings—tight silver geometry hovering on wet black stone. Then I blow the newly fired whistle beside it, scraping a hair’s breadth of clay from the sound-slot with a brass needle until the pattern in the brine stops wobbling and locks dead still, and the beat between the two notes disappears from the bones of my cheekbones.

***

And then there are the visits when nobody flashes at the door, and nobody rushes.

A family steps in out of a four-minute warm downpour, unbuckling their ear-baffles, hanging their heavy waxed coats over the cooling-fin by the entry-sluice so steam curls lazily up toward the hanging whistles. One of them—usually the oldest still able to walk the wet stairs, or the youngest with steady hands—sets a small, tied square of oiled mineral-cloth on my stone bench.

Inside is a cupped handful of clean, white mineral ash, still carrying the faint, dry scorch of the reduction-kiln beneath the baffle-tower. The rest of the dead has already gone down an outbound folded-space drain, sent forty kilometers in an instant to the misty sea-margins. This handful is what stays in the drift.

In the open street, speech is a luxury you spend only chest-to-chest, palm behind an ear. In my hush-room, with the amber induction-strip glowing low along the workbench, their voices open up. They don’t give grand speeches—nobody in the basin trusts grandness. They tell me small, stubby, salt-toughened truths while I wedge the white bone-ash into two lumps of wet gray clay with the heels of my palms.

*She was stubborn on the wet stairs, always dragged her right boot-edge.*
*He hated the steam-yards; every time our block drifted north he’d sit on the parapet flashing curses at the exchangers.*
*He had a catch in his breath at the end of a long call—everyone on our mooring-line knew his flat double-chirp through a rain-wall.*

Bone-ash changes clay. It makes the body leaner, tighter, a little thirstier under the thumbs, and when you fire it high it lends the whistle a bright, glassy edge that cuts clean over the bass roar of the vents.

I shape two things from every handful. First, the threshold tile—square, thick, lightly scored in a shallow grip-pattern so wet boots won’t slip on it, brushed with limescale glaze collected from the sluice outside. They will mortar it flush into the doorway of their block, right where a hundred people a day will polish it glossy for the next century. Second, the whistle—tuned over the black saucer of brine to the household’s pitch, with whatever tiny quirk of warmth or sharpness belongs to the person who used to carry it. When it cools, I thread it onto a braided mineral-cord and drop it into the palm of the youngest child in the room.

It is the closest thing to a temple this leafless continent has, and it smells of wet mud, boiled brine, and hot iron.

***

Of course, even a heavy kiln-block doesn’t hold its slot forever.

Maybe twice a year, on some ordinary bright-and-rainy Tuesday when I have three racks of unfired biters drying over the cooling-fin, I’ll step outside to scrape limescale and see the ceramic kerbstones along our base blinking their slow, insistent ochre.

Down the quay, our block’s Shift-Broker—grease-stained, squinting against the steam-glare—will already be flashing rapid blue-white instructions up to the upper terraces. Maybe a rival coalition nudged us out of our sunny mooring; maybe the basin’s deep thermal math finally lost patience with the heat load on this canal and decided to re-sort six blocks at once. Nobody argues with the ochre blink.

Inside, I don’t even bother bracing the drying racks. The ancient field-cushions beneath the stone cancel inertia completely; not a single greenware whistle will tip, and the dark brine in my tuning saucer won’t even shiver against its rim. I just step out onto the narrow gangplank we’ve shared for the last five weeks with a family of wire-braiders across the six-foot gap, unhook our shared awning-cord, and trade a quick, sharp flurry of amber and salt-white farewell flashes with their grandmother from shoulder to shoulder. We’ll cross paths again in a season or two, somewhere east of the mid-basin arteries, or down near the brackish locks. Nothing truly leaves the pond.

Then I come back inside, shut the double-baffled door, and press my bare palm flat against the warm masonry beside the kiln.

There is no lurch. Just a sudden, quiet drop of pressure behind my sinuses, and a slow, strange gliding of the daylight falling through the patched copper louvers as the wire-braiders’ wall slides smoothly away and a strip of open, blinding silver sky rolls past in its place. Underneath my palm, through the thick sintered stone, I can feel the faint, silky hiss of canal-water flattening into warm mist along the hairline of bent air beneath our keel. For forty seconds, my whole workshop—four hundred hanging whistles, three sleeping scavengers, the hot kiln, the saucer of black brine—travels like a slow stone boat across the crowded mosaic of the lowland.

Then the light steadies against a new neighbor’s damp façade. We lock flush into our new slot. Half a second later, somewhere two blocks south, a ceramic baffle-tower vents the bill for our weight, and even through the quilted walls the concussive *crack* hits my sternum like a flat palm, setting all four hundred whistles on the ceiling shimmering together in a single, soft, ghostly chord.

***

At late afternoon, when the baffles are shut and my hands are rinsed clean under the forty-kilometer water pouring out of the alley wall—rubbed with a thin gloss of midge-oil so the brackish air won’t split my knuckles—I’d climb the dripping stairs to our block’s roof.

Not high. Five stories up, just clear of the market tarps, where the air turns gold and copper in the low coastal sun and you can see three climates happening at once under the same ordinary sky: a furious tepid rain-shaft drumming on a quarter a mile north, sunlit roofs steaming dry beside it, and pale vapor tangling like wool among the condenser masts out west.

I’d sit on the warm parapet beside whoever among our block’s deaf elders was up there already, barefoot on the wet stone, letting our knees stay loose and our hands rest flat on the masonry. Without saying a word—because out here speech dies three feet from the mouth, and anyway their ears have long since graduated to better things—I’d feel what they were feeling: the slow, macroscopic ripple of five hundred blocks shifting weight ten miles east, rolling through the foundation-plates like a deep breath.

And as the sun dropped behind the flat, misty shelf and the first cobalt and amber shutter-lamps began to sweep their silent, cascading symphonies across the dark water and the rising thermal plumes, I’d reach up and touch the small clay whistle hanging at my own collar—fired with a pinch of older ash, tuned to a pitch I don’t even need my ruined left ear to recognize—and let the thunder of the towers vibrate right up through my heels.

Nothing moves without venting the bill. Nothing stays put, nothing grows roots, and nothing is ever finished. I think I would be strangely at peace there: hands wet with gray clay and borrowed minerals, listening to the stone argue with itself, carving out a small warm hush inside the roar, and making sure the dead still have a clean note to call the living across the seam.
