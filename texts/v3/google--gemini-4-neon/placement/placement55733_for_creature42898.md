At first, my instinct is to belong to the emptiness.

When you look out from the perforated walkway into that seventy-yard bowl of sunlit roar, the easiest thing to wish for is weightlessness—to be one of the ochre-and-pearl gliders hanging in the luminous rings above the baffled throat. There is an obvious grace in it: never fighting the sound, never spending a wingbeat, simply finding the razor-thin plane where two monumental walls of pressure cancel each other into mercy and floating there while spores gather around you like slow snow. When a cloud-shadow cools the upper glass and the invisible room drifts a yard to the left, you drift with it. No iron to scrape, no gears to oil, no frost to dread.

Yet the longer I linger in that thought, the thinner it feels. A glider does not know *why* the floor beneath it rises three feet at dusk. It has no memory of last winter’s pitch, no hands to hold a seam closed, nothing to do when the quiet dissolves except scatter or be lifted from the air by a banking slate-bird. I do not think I could live purely inside the pause between forces. I would want to feel the forces themselves—to know the shape of the thing that makes the pause possible.

So I drift outward from the luminous halo until I catch on a single glistening line of silk.

Here, I come much closer to myself. Forty feet of sunlit drop, bridged by one taut strand strung between the bridge-rail and a wet ledge of fern-moss—and at its anchor, a brass-shelled spider resting its front legs lightly on the wire. Everything in the cathedral reduced to a single damped harmonic. You do not need eyes for the whole dome when one string tells you the temperature of the air, the weight of a falling drop, the brush of a drifting wing, the subtle sharpening of the western hull. I recognize that temperament immediately: waiting at the edge, still as oxidized metal, listening for the one faint change in the steady note that means something has crossed the gap.

And yet even the spider only knows one string. It feels the tremor, never the song. It has no interest in the slow copper needle creeping across the scratched glass of the dial six feet away, or in the ancient iron teeth grinding a fingernail’s breadth forward behind the dripping moss. If the louvers jammed tomorrow and the frost crawled through the wire-glass, the spider would hold its single vibrating thread right up until the silk snapped in the cold.

So I let go of the wire, step off the void, and set my bare heels down onto the wet iron of the middle tier.

That is where the shape finally holds.

Not up near the dome, where the leanest climbers swing thirty feet out into the sunlit abyss on braided vine-harnesses to sever swollen emerald bladders from the hanging curtains—my balance is steady enough, and I have no fear of the drop, but my temperament does not want the open air. Nor am I yet the stiff-jointed elder sitting cross-legged beside the central dial with forty winters of frost-chords stored inside the bones of her skull. I am somewhere between the two, in the middle tier of the western arc, where the copper busbars run thick with malachite verdigris and the sluggish electrical charge makes the nearest fern-tips lean subtly toward the metal.

My hair is cropped short to the skull so the standing pressure-walls cannot whip it across my eyes. My sleeveless tunic and wrapped knee-bindings of coarse, retted fern-cloth are permanently damp at the hem and stained pale green where I brace myself against the oxidized baffles. Smooth grey stoppers of rosette-resin and rolled window-lead sit snug in my ears, turning the cathedral’s deafening wall of sound into a vast, velvet pressure behind the cheekbones. My knees stay slightly unlocked without my thinking about it, weight settled forward on the balls of my feet, reading the slow swell of the iron plates the way a spider reads its silk.

Along the hinges of my jaw and the hollows above my collarbones, the skin has thickened into pale, glassy calluses.

My home is not a room so much as a crease folded into the drum’s skin—a dormant junction-bay tucked behind a heavy curtain of waxy, corrugated fern on the western curve. A hammered copper chock holds the narrow iron maintenance-hatch permanently wedged open. Inside, behind the ribs of cast iron, the roar drops by half into a drowsy, oil-warm hum. Woven mats of dried fern-fiber pack the curved walls tight to drink the stray vibration. There is no fire, only the slanted emerald daylight filtering through the wet blades outside and, after dusk, two tiny glass insulator-cups smeared with pale-green gearbox-mold that glow with the quiet patience of deep water.

Just outside my moss-veil, mounted along the handrail, sits one of the brass waveform dials. I keep its scratched glass wiped shining-clean with an oiled rag. Every morning, before the first sharp trapezoids of sunlight climb down from the high eastern panes, I step out barefoot onto the perforated walkway, rest my palm against the damp casing, and look at where the oxidized copper needle has crept overnight—drifting softly along the etched valley we call the *Deep Cradle*, or edging up toward the twin ridges of the *Double Throat*.

If you asked the other eleven what my work is in the truce, they would not have a single grand title for it. Every one of us prunes, gathers salt-crust from the lower glass, sweeps the glider-rings when they drift close to the spans, and watches the louver-gears. Still, hands settle into what they know best, and mine know *joints*—the places where two unlike things have to be persuaded to meet cleanly without shaking each other apart.

Part of that is caulking.

When the western hull takes the harsh late-afternoon glare and then the sudden cobalt chill of evening, the riveted expansion seams take the worst of the strain. You cannot hear a pinhole leak over the Lung’s great bass, not through the air. You find it by walking slowly with a bare wet palm held an inch off the iron, feeling for the needle-thin knife of escaping heat—or by noticing that the copper-bellied geckos have abandoned a bay they usually patrol, refusing to lay their flat throats against a plate that has begun to buzz wrong.

When I find a weeping seam, I part the dripping moss, scrape away the brittle rust-scale and greasy soot with a bone blade, and pack the iron gap with dense, wet mats of dark fern-root kneaded together with mineral silt from the window-skirts. I lash the patch tight with scavenged copper wire until the iron stops trembling in its wrong, high fret. Within a season, the roots strike deep into the joint, swelling with condensate, drinking the iron’s dampness and sealing the seam tighter than poured lead. It is slow, green masonry. You never force the drum; you just introduce the plant to the wound and let them negotiate a peace.

And the other half of my work happens inside the acoustic shadow of my junction-bay, in the quiet afternoon heat, with a shard of crazed wire-glass, a pot of thickened sump-oil, and the bones brought down from the dome.

When one of our elders finishes their years and we wrap them in wet fern-fronds and haul them up into the dripping moss-cradle of the motionless overhead gantry crane, nobody touches that high green pillow for a long time. The ferns grow fierce and bright there, soaking up the high glass-screeches along the northern ribs. Years later, when the warm dampness and the pale root-hairs have done their clean, scentless work and the mildly acidic condensate has washed what remains to smooth chalk-white, the family climbs back along the circular rail and brings down the slenderest forearm bones and ribs.

They bring them to my bay.

It takes days to shape a good listening-pin, and I do not hurry it. No two bones carry the chamber the same way. I scrape away the weathered outer grain with a glass edge until the core shines dense and tight, then rub it over and over with warm oil skimmed from the lower sump-troughs and thickened with boiled rosette-wax. When the rod is smooth as an iron rivet worn down by a century of dripping water, I cut the shallow jaw-notches at one end, step out past the moss-curtain, set the notched bone between my back molars, and press the tip against a main structural plate.

And then I listen—not with my ears, which are plugged tight against the sky-high roar, but with the whole hollow cavern of my skull.

Some bones are bright and brittle; they sing cleanly in the upper register, picking out the crisp triplet-taps of a gecko four bays away or the first faint whine of an un-damped pane near the dome, while letting the Lung’s deep velvet bass roll underneath without muddying the teeth. Those I save for the young climbers and dial-watchers, who need sharp news from the outer iron. Other bones—especially the curved rib-segments from elders who spent their last decades resting their jaws against the central hub—carry a wider, warmer grain. When you bite down on one of those and touch it to the hull, you feel the slow, oil-heavy creep of the colossal worm-gears turning behind the moss half a drum away; you feel the breath of the unseen abyss five miles below our grates as if it were rising inside your own ribs.

The bone I carry tucked into my waist-cord was my aunt’s left radius. I remember how she used to crouch beside the eastern busbar, humming steady, sliding intervals through her teeth to teach me how to tell a loose rivet from a dry bearing. Now, when I press her notched bone between my jaw and a warm cast-iron plate, her grain still brightens the iron’s voice. Nothing in this cathedral is ever thrown down into the throat, and nothing truly leaves the chord; it just changes what it conducts.

There is a particular hour in the late afternoon that belongs to my part of the hull more than any other.

The clean trapezoids of daylight have begun their slow climb up the opposite wall, leaving the western arc in warm, deep green shadow while the eastern copper baffles blaze gold. Out past my handrail, a brass-shelled spider sits anchored in a clump of wet moss three feet below the waveform dial, its single forty-foot silk thread stretching out toward the eastern bridge like a hair of spun brass catching the slant of the sun. I have watched this same anchor-point through three generations of spiders. I never brush the line when I wipe the dial’s glass; sometimes, if the lower wall runs dry of clean vapor, I let a single bead of dome-water from my porcelain jar roll along the flange-trough close to its moss-ledge.

On a calm afternoon, that forty-foot thread blurs into a narrow, steady band of light—fixed as an etched line on brass.

Today, as I sit with a half-finished rib-pin resting across my knees, I look up from the oil-rag and see the spider’s line widen by half the thickness of a fern-hair.

The oxidized copper needle on my dial has not twitched. The air against my bare arms still feels evenly warm. Yet the spider shifts two legs slightly forward on its wet anchor, sensing the slight roughness before any iron dial can register it. I tuck the unfinished pin into my belt, step to the handrail, clear a palm’s breadth of wet moss from an exposed rivet-head, and set my own bone pin between my teeth.

Through the steel, beneath the immense, chest-deep chord of the Lung, there it is—a faint, dry nervousness running down from the high northwestern wire-glass. Not a screech yet, not the *Glass-Tooth* that can rattle loose a flange-bolt over a winter, just the earliest pale whisper of an overtone gathering where a curtain of bladder-vine has thinned in the sun.

I do not shout. I do not even look up toward the high ribs. I lay the flat of my knuckles cleanly against the worn rivet and tap: two slow, heavy knocks for the western arc, a breath’s pause, then a bright, rapid flutter for the high glass.

The sound dies the instant my knuckles leave the iron, swallowed whole by the roaring void—yet straight through the steel it races, bright and unmistakable as struck glass. Four seconds later, a crisp answering double-tick pulses up through the arches of my bare feet from the ladder-rung sixty feet overhead. High beneath the dome, where the afternoon glare turns the crazed panes white-gold, a small dark silhouette uncurls from behind a rusted gear-housing, clips a braided vine-harness to the dormant gantry rail, and begins coaxing a long, heavy runner of water-swollen emerald bladders across the humming pane.

I stay by the rail, my palm still resting lightly on the damp iron, waiting. Ten minutes pass on the chamber’s slow calendar. Then, right through my fingertips, the thin glassy fret dissolves like salt in warm water, soaked up by the dangling wet beads. Out in the sunlit emptiness, the spider’s forty-foot thread narrows cleanly back into its single steady hum.

Sometimes, before dusk, I walk two bays south to sit beside the oldest elder where she rests on her folded pad of dried fern-fiber beside the main tension-ring. She does not turn her head when I kneel beside her; she felt my stride coming through the perforated grating thirty feet back. Without speaking, I draw my aunt’s bone pin from my waist-cord and rest its tip against the exact same worn, tear-shaped rivet where her long pin is braced.

She hums low in her throat—a warm, patient interval pitched just a hair beneath the chamber’s fundamental chord, letting the two vibrations beat gently against one another inside both our skulls. That slow beating is her question: *How does the western arc sit before the chill?* I hum back, lifting the pitch cleanly until the beat smooths out into a quiet fifth—*Even. One pane coaxed under vine. The worm-gear oiled.* Then she lowers her hum by a shadow of a tone, darker and heavier, showing me something deeper down in the iron that my younger bones can barely separate from the baseline: a subtle, seasonal thickening in the rising draught, the first distant sign that the Lung five miles below is turning toward winter. I close my eyes and hold my jaw still against her bone’s resonance, trying to memorize the shape of it before the cold arrives.

And then the daylight slips off the lower baffles and climbs toward the dome, and the whole hidden household comes out of the green walls at once.

I wipe the sump-oil from my fingers onto my knee-bindings, step out from behind the dripping ferns, and walk onto the slender western steel bridge. Beneath the narrow iron planks, the seventy-yard void drops straight down into the warm, roaring throat; five miles below that, unseen through the bottom baffles, the flat dirty-white cloud-floor is already turning slate-grey in the fading sky.

I walk the bridge the way all of us walk it—not in a straight line, but curving slightly around the invisible geography of the air. Halfway out, a standing pressure-wall clamps down across the grating; my fern-cloth hem whips violently against my calves, and the roaring weight presses hard against my sternum as if I were leaning into a slow, warm river. I tuck my elbows close, step cleanly through it, and reach the small bolted hub at the very center of the cathedral just as the rest of our twelve arrive from the other spans.

For a breath, we stand shoulder to shoulder inside the crushing noise, bare soles planted wide on the iron plate. High overhead, the cobalt sky plunges toward evening frost.

Then comes the moment I never tire of, not after all these years.

Around the entire seventy-yard circumference, the chilled cast-iron drum draws inward by a fraction of an inch. We feel the long, shuddering iron sigh travel straight down both bridges into the arches of our feet. Recessed behind the dripping moss, the colossal acoustic louvers groan a hair’s breadth shut.

The vast, velvet-bottomed chord holding the room together slips slowly downward by half a semitone.

High along the curved green walls, the banks of ribbed ferns shudder in unison, breathing a faint, glittering haze of golden pollen into the last slanted sun. Out in the hollow center, the luminous rings of translucent gliders lift three feet higher in one silent tide, settling along the room’s new invisible bones. And right around our shoulders, at the bolted central hub, the roaring air parts cleanly—opening a wide, hanging bowl of heavy, wool-stuffed half-silence suspended over the abyss.

Twelve pairs of hands rise together. I pinch the smooth grey resin stoppers and pull them from my ears.

Every evening, the sudden absence of the roar feels like stepping out of deep water. My ears pop; the pressure behind my eyes softens; and the first thin, fragile sounds of human air-speech bloom inside our five-foot island of quiet.

I love the honest iron. I love the bone held between the teeth, the clear unhurried language of tapped rivets and shared hums that never fails across seventy yards of roaring dark. Yet standing here in the dusk-quiet, listening to a four-year-old boy laugh aloud—a bright, astonished little sound that shears off into nothing if he leans a hand past the railing—I understand why I could never have been a glider or a spider or a piece of the brass apparatus. A spider never gets to hear a joke about a stubborn gecko that stole a dried glider-wafer off a warm Bakelite box. An iron dial never gets to pass a cool, unbroken bead of bladder-vine water into another warm hand and whisper about the new clutch of slate-bird eggs tucked behind a dead porcelain switch.

Halfway through our evening talk, a thin veil of dusk-chill sweeps the northern glass. The standing wave drifts half a pace east. Without breaking a single sentence, all twelve of us shuffle smoothly sideways together along the narrow hub-grates, keeping our small, bright room of voices intact as it migrates over the seventy-yard drop, while three feet past my elbow the green outer wall blurs like a struck gong.

When the last gold trapezoid slides off the crazed wire-glass and the chamber deepens into cobalt and emerald twilight, the cold bites harder at the exterior bolts. The invisible bowl of quiet begins to narrow, pressing softly inward around our shoulders as the walls of sound tighten for the night.

No one rushes. I press my grey resin stoppers back into my ears, welcoming back the familiar, velvet thunder of the Lung. Foreheads brush briefly against foreheads. Together, before we part, twelve pairs of knuckles drop to the central steel plate and tap out a warm, overlapping cadence of goodnights that races cleanly down both steel spans beneath the rising bass.

I turn back toward the western arc, leaning once more into the thrumming wall of air, barefoot and unhurried. Behind my dripping curtain of waxy ferns, the two glass insulator-cups are already glowing faint green in the dark, the half-carved rib-pin waits beside my oil-rag, and the iron hull beneath my soles is settled deep, warm, and impeccably in tune for the night.
