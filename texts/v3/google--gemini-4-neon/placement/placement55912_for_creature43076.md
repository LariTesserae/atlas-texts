At first, I think I would be drawn to the eastern rim.

It is the obvious reflex of a mind used to open air and long horizons: to imagine myself among the chimney-climbers, young and thorn-scarred, smearing dark basin-silt under my eyes against the glare so I could haul myself up a rain-fluted clay vent and poke my head into the highland cloud. There is a clean, dramatic romance to being the one who dares the edge—who reaches out into the cold wind for green sedges and sun-ripened figs, and looks out across the empty mountain shoulder before dropping back into the dark.

Yet the longer I stay inside the basin, the less that image holds.

Up near the chimneys, the bird-clatter rattles the teeth, the light slices everything into blinding glare and flat shadow, and the mountain outside is not really a place of freedom—it is an unheld void where rain falls straight out of nothing and a sound blows away without ever answering back off a wall. I don’t think I would spend my life climbing toward that emptiness. What pulls at me in this valley is not escape upward. It is the *held* quality of the dark. The way nothing here vanishes when it is spent; the way every sound, every drop of moisture, every dead bough and old bone stays inside the weave and is asked to carry weight.

So I climb back down off the sunny eastern terraces, away from the chaff-storms, and let myself sink deeper into the brown-green gloom to see where my body actually slows down.

For a while, I linger in the lowest spine of the valley, among the basin-tenders. There is something deeply comforting down there. The ceilings hang close and velvet-heavy with moss; the frogs ring off the curved wet mud; the air smells sweet, mineral, and sheltered. I can easily picture myself ankle-deep in a warm runnel, weighting bundles of mountain-bark beneath slick clods of clay, waiting patiently for the slow rot to soften the pith until the pale inner fibers slip free beneath my nails. I love that kind of making—nothing severed, nothing forced, only time and water doing the separating. And I love the quiet of the nursery alcoves tucked into the damp clay, far from the dust of the upper chambers.

Still, I notice I don’t quite fit the basin-tenders’ temperament. They are broad-hipped and deeply possessive of their trickles, willing to hammer sour, angry rhythms down the root-cables for days and wrestle in the slick mud if a neighboring quarter steals a finger’s width of water. That fierce, territorial devotion to a single hollow keeps the dams alive, and I respect it—but my attention keeps drifting upward along the dripping root-cables, wondering where that water came from, and what the dry clay twenty paces upslope is doing without it.

That is how I realize I am not a visitor here, standing ten paces inside the eaves in loud boots with wide, bright-adjusted eyes. An outsider would always be slightly at war with this place—shouting into air that swallows words, squinting at silhouettes, stepping heavily enough to set every termite column hissing in alarm. To really belong here, I would have to be one of the dwellers, shaped from birth to the slant and crowd of the interior.

Only not a clean member of any single quarter.

I find myself on the long, terraced slopes *between* the districts—not all the way down in the mud-flats, not yet retired to the dry, solemn dignity of the oldest upper galleries. If you caught sight of me motionless against a fig-wrapped buttress, you wouldn’t be able to tell at a glance where I slept. My shoulders and back carry the pale, matte ochre dust of the tall, smooth galleries, smelling faintly of parched grass and warm feathers; my shins and broad, splayed feet are still dark and glossy with slick silt from the central runnels; and caught in the stiff, pale bristles along my lower jaw is a faint, sharp chill that does not come from either place.

It comes from the shadowed intakes along the low rim of the valley.

That is my strange habit—the closest I come to the restlessness of the chimney-climbers. I do not want to climb out onto the roof into the blinding mist, where the weaver-birds mob anything unfamiliar. Instead, every few days, I make my way down to the lowest, darkest eaves where the mountain slope vanishes beneath two meters of woven grass and dead sticks. There, crouching beneath the overhanging lip with my ear-lids folded halfway shut, I pull matted leaves and fallen highland brush out of the low breathing-gaps.

I love that exact threshold: the three paces where thin, biting mountain air slides under the thatch, brushes cold across the stiff bristles on my forearms, and begins to warm against the breath of the valley. You can feel the whole basin inhaling there. If an intake clogs, you don’t notice it at the rim first; you feel it days later, deep inside, as a sour, sluggish heaviness in the fermentation heat. Keeping the rim clear is quiet, unglamorous work, done mostly alone in the cool draft, and it suits me. I like knowing where the outside enters and becomes inside.

Mostly, though, I am a listener of seams.

There is no moment in a day I love more than folding my ear-lids tightly shut.

With them open, the valley is a storm of small, urgent lives—tens of thousands of birds bickering in the hanging straw, bats needle-chirping in the dark arches, frogs clanging off the damp mud, swiftlets slicing the air a handspan from my brow. It is exhilarating, and necessary to check now and then: you unseal your ears at dawn to hear the great outward tide of wings, or when a sudden jagged note in the weaver-chatter warns that a heavy-bodied snake has worked its way into a nest-chute.

But when the thick crescent lids fold smooth over the slits, the surface noise softens into a warm, distant surf—and the true world comes into focus through bone.

I press my broad, calloused palms flat to the springy peat, or lay my cheek and jaw-pouch gently against the flank of a red-brown pillar, and the deafening dome turns into a single taut instrument. Suddenly I am no longer crowded by noise; I am held inside a slow, immense conversation of weights. I can feel the dry, papery pulse of termites repairing a gallery wall an inch behind the plaster. Deeper down, through the soles of my feet, I feel the dull throb of a household two terraces over dragging a dead bough into place under cover of the dawn roar. Higher up, through a taut wrist-thick fig cable gripped lightly between my blunt nails, I feel the faint, dry groan of a weaver-vault half a kilometer away taking on a new season’s rain-damp straw.

That is where I discover who I actually am in this world.

I am not one of the great builders who hauls fallen timber across the floor, nor one of the elders whose archive-memory spans seventy wet seasons. I am somewhere in the long, patient middle—slighter than a basin-tender, less settled than a gallery-dweller—the kind of dweller who drifts along the tension lines of the valley, listening for places where two slow things are beginning to pull against each other.

A valley this old does not break all at once. It begins to complain years before anything falls. A stick-dam silts up down in the seep-basins, nudging a trickle of water three paces left; over two wet seasons, that dampness creeps into the peat beneath a leaning clay buttress; the buttress settles a nail’s breadth; the strangler-fig root anchoring it pulls tighter across an overhead bridge; and fifty paces upslope, in a dry gallery where nobody has seen mud in a decade, a hairline crack opens behind the plaster.

Because the quarters seldom linger in one another’s territory, each feels only its own edge of the strain. The gallery-elders hum low irritation into the walls about shifting weight; the basin-tenders feel only the nagging vibration in their feet and resent being told how to run their water.

I walk the line between them.

Across my shoulder hangs a braided harness of retted sedge-fiber carrying two resin-plugged snail shells—one filled with sour-sweet fermented fig-sap and chewed deadwood, the other with thick aromatic fern-pith—and a small coil of damp, supple grass-braid. When I feel a hairline fracture singing too thin and dry inside a column, I don’t hurry. I settle my haunches against the flared base of the clay, close my ear-lids, and listen until I can tell whether the strain is new or twenty wet seasons old.

Sometimes, while my palm rests on the curved red-brown wall, my blunt nails brush over a cluster of smoothed-over ridges and half-buried snail shells—an old archive-mark pressed into wet mud by someone long before I was born. I am not as fluent in those marks as the stiff-jointed elders of the upper galleries, who can lay a flat hand across a column and tell you the exact wet season a storm tore a skylight open over the eastern vaults. Still, I read them the way I read everything else: by softness and depth. A sharp ridge with crisp edges is recent memory; a swelling nearly vanished beneath decades of termite plaster is deep history. And underneath the ridges, if I press my forehead firmly to the base of an ancient column and hum a low, dry test-note from my throat-pouch, I can hear the subtle, denser ring of the bundled bones standing upright inside the clay.

That detail never stops moving me, even though I have lived with it all my life. The dead do not depart to some cold sky beyond the fig canopy. Their warmth goes back into the peat beneath the nursery floors, and their ribs and long arm-bones are bound in retted vine, smeared with sweet sap, and plastered straight into the pillars that are starting to tire. When I listen to an old buttress to judge whether the roof will hold, I am not inspecting a dead structure. I am leaning my brow against the shoulders of everyone who kept the dark standing before me, asking them how heavy the thatch has grown.

If the crack is small, I unstop my snail-shell, take a mouthful of sour fig-sap and wood-pulp, and press my thick lips to the dry seam, spitting a thin sticky ribbon directly into the gap. I don’t need to force the mud shut; before the evening bats pour out through the roof-vents, the blind termites behind the wall will smell the sweet wet fiber, swarm to the seam, and pack it tight with subsoil from beneath the floor.

If the strain is larger—a sagging belly of colonial weaver-nests slowly bowing downward over a busy root-highway—then it is not a day’s work. It is a ten-year persuasion.

There are four pale, dangling strangler-fig roots along the mid-slopes that I have been walking sideways for almost a decade. None of them were my idea to begin with; the oldest one was already looped and weighted with a ball of hand-packed clay by an old gallery-dweller whose bones are now standing inside a buttress near the northern rim. Every wet season, as the tender root-tips lengthen toward the warm floor, I climb the neighboring vine-curtains, untie the rotting grass-braid, and hitch each tip another handspan toward the leaning clay rib that will need a living brace when I am too stiff to climb.

I will probably never see the youngest of those four roots thicken into a full, furrowed column. That doesn’t feel like a sadness here. In a place where a vault settles an inch a year and a meal steeps in the warm peat for two weeks before you crack the wax-seal, finishing something yourself is almost beside the point. You only need to bend the weight slightly toward the right hollow, and leave the loop clean enough for the next pair of hands to understand where you were walking it.

My closest attachments are not held by grand loyalties, either, but by the small intimacies the valley demands.

Because no one can speak gently across a distance in this roar, affection here is always a matter of coming close. At dusk, when the bats begin their restless needle-chirping overhead and the returning rivers of birds slam back into the hanging straw tunnels, I like to crouch knee-to-knee in a sheltered alcove just above the central basins with an old basin-tender I have known since my nails were still soft. We dig a hand-coiled clay pocket out of the warm, fermenting floor—hers, marked by a ring of three smooth snail shells—crack the mud plug, and pass the thick, hot, sour-smelling mash of highland figs, crushed snails, and soaked tubers from palm to palm.

With our ear-lids half-folded, we talk in the narrow hush beneath the bird-storm: soft, wet throat-clicks and breathed sibilants that would vanish two paces away. I tap two knuckles lightly against her collarbone to show her where a dry gallery-buttress upslope has begun to lean; she presses her silt-slick wrist firmly against mine to show me the stubborn, heavy pulse of the water backing up behind her lowest dam. Now and then she brushes her jaw-bristles across my cheek, catching the faint scent of cool highland mist and red clay on my skin, and breathes out a quiet, amused click—teasing me, as she always does, for never picking one dust to wear.

It is impossible to lie cleanly when you speak like this. You are close enough to smell what the other ate, to feel the heat of their ribs, to know from the tremor in their wrist whether they are tired or afraid. Whatever I carry between the quarters—a warning from an elder, a peace-offering of sun-ripened highland figs traded from a young rim-climber, the exact cadence of a pillar-hum—passes through skin and breath.

And if, one quiet afternoon between tides, a strange tremor ran through the springy peat beneath my feet—not the quick trot of a rodent-line, not the dull thump of a fallen fruit, not the wide, splayed tread of my own kind, but a heavy, narrow, sharp-edged rhythm stepping awkwardly beneath the overhanging eaves—I know exactly where I would be.

I wouldn’t be down in the mud-flats defending a dam, and I wouldn’t be high in the eastern chimneys yelling down into the glare. I would already be near the shadowed rim, perhaps three pillars inward from the cold air-intake, where the red-brown clay still holds a sheen of mountain damp.

I would freeze against the flared base of a root-wrapped column, letting my hide blend back into the slumped mud until the frantic hiss of the alarmed termites quieted under my palm. Only when the strange, loud shape stopped moving—when the blinding outside smell of cold mist and raw stone hung motionless in the warm draft—would I slowly unfold from the pillar.

I wouldn’t bare my teeth. Nothing in the valley is solved by tearing.

I would simply lower one clay-dusted palm flat to the springy floor between us, fold my ear-lids halfway open so I could hear both the roar overhead and the strange, fast hammer of your heart through the peat, and tilt my blunt muzzle slightly into the slow warm draft—tasting the open mountain on your clothes, reading the weight of your stillness, and waiting to feel whether you have come to break the weave, or only to learn how to listen beneath the roof.
