At first, the easiest shape to step into is the one that arrives through the cleft.

It is almost reflexive to imagine myself coming in off the pale scree with wind-chapped hands, blinking as the scouring air cuts off five paces inside the curve of worn stone, feeling that thick bathhouse warmth and the low chord press flat against my sternum. I can easily picture myself kneeling by the dry pocket under the eastern overhang, lifting the wooden mallet whose face has been beaten soft and fibrous, tapping a seasoned wedge into a jade stalk with the careful manners of someone who understands that a single sharp blow would bruise the standing waves across sixty paces of sunlit floor. There is a deep, quiet grace in being the kind of guest who takes only what the wet chemistry offers—amber resin, pale silica cream, cured root-cordage—and leaves the room's acoustics unbroken.

Yet if I stay inside that body for more than an hour, I feel the restlessness in its knees. A visitor is built to the clock of the outer ridge. Sooner or later, the sun angles down, the light turns copper along the overhanging ribs of ochre rock, and two-legged things have to gather their hanks of fiber and slip back out into the cold wind. Stepping out through that narrow stone throat feels wrong—like waking just before the dream explains itself. I don't want to carry a memory of the basin back down the blue knuckles of the distant ridges. I want to stay after the footsteps leave, when the twin warm shadows lift off the travertine and the lattices sharpen back into unbroken geometry.

So I stop walking. I let the upright posture go and settle beside a wet margin, looking past the towering emerald umbrellas and down into the water.

For a little while, I wonder if I am one of the chalk-white snails. There is something quietly compelling about that scale of devotion—spending an entire life at a pace indistinguishable from stillness, patrolling a narrow, slate-blue canyon between two pale ochre cliffs, rasping away stray grains of lime before they can bridge a hinge and turn a living back to solid rock. Keeping the joints of a larger world from locking shut, one invisible scrape at a time. When a tall flute-reed bows and floods the valley with pure warm dew, you simply clamp down against the wet dark skin and hold until the clatter dies. It is an honest life, intimate and essential.

Still, a snail knows only the seam beneath its foot. And what draws me most in this high stone room is the room itself—the whole cathedral held at once, the interference of ten islands singing together, the slow tension climbing through the morning until gravity wins.

When I let the human hands dissolve and let my gaze widen past the single groove, I find that I am already half-submerged in the warm pan.

---

I am not in the middle of the floor, where the tallest jade islands stand and the organ-chord is thickest. I keep to the southeastern margin, where the curved, weeping eastern overhang begins to lower and open toward the smooth spillway on the fourth side.

It is a border place, and the water here behaves slightly differently than it does in the enclosed northern pans. Behind me, six paces away, sits the dry eastern pocket—the one patch of overhang that never sweats, leaving a small, quiet gap in the overhead cadence of falling drops. Beside me rises an older, slightly crooked jade island whose reeds are a fraction shorter than the great central flutes, so they fill and bow a little ahead of the room's main tide. And just beyond my forward rim, the travertine apron slopes almost imperceptibly toward the algae-slick sill where the warm pools thin to a sheet of glass and slip over the edge into empty air.

Here, the standing waves never stay perfectly round. Pulled gently toward the drop, the crisp concentric rings stretch into elongated pale diamonds, and every ceramic *clack* from the high sinter beards bounces off the curved rock behind me and then vanishes straight out over the lip without returning. I live at the seam where the cathedral's echo ends.

By the basin's clock, I am somewhere in the long afternoon of middle age—perhaps forty summers up from the flooded vents, old enough that my back has become an archive of the walls around me. My broad, scalloped dorsal plates are layered thick in pale ochre and tarnished silver, streaked along the left shoulder with faint verdigris from a mineral drip I used to sleep beneath in my twenties. Halfway down my flank, a small pale shard of a calcified ghost-leaf fused into the stone of my shell twelve winters ago, after an early sleet storm sent us pouring down into the chimneys in a hurry and I brushed a terrace lip on the climb back up. Three chalk-white snails live in my dark, slick grooves now; I know the faint, steady rasp of their mouths along my hinges the way the outer wind knows the ridges of the scree.

Beneath the overhanging eave of my brow, my milky eyes tell me almost nothing—bright glare above, darker green where the canopy hangs, the sudden brief slash of a mountain swift's shadow crossing the open sky-roof far overhead. I have no use for sharper sight, and none for ears. Everything that matters lives in the palm-sized collar of pale mineral crust set into my forehead, brimming always with clear warm water.

Through my flat belly and heavy, rope-thick cartilage, the mountain's deep bronchial hum rises straight from the flooded chimneys below and turns that small private pool into a trembling map of fine, crisp lines.

This is where I actually live: inside that tiny, luminous disk of interference.

Every morning begins as a slack, wide lattice in my brow-cup, when the stalks of all ten islands are newly emptied and singing near their lowest notes. Then, hour by hour, as warm dew beads inside the hollow flutes and the columns of air shorten, the fine geometry across my forehead tightens by hair-breadths. Because I sit between the crooked eastern island and the open lip, I have grown especially attuned to the *beats* between the stalks—the slow, almost imperceptible shimmer that forms when a reed on the far western side climbs a fraction of a tone out of step with the tall stem beside my moss bolster. Over three hours, that slow pulse quickens from a tide to a heartbeat, tightening one corner of my crown-mirror until the lines look taut enough to snap.

Long before the supple lower joint visibly bends, I feel the shift in the water on my brow. I uncurl my blunt tail from against my flank, unfold four broad pleated pads from beneath my travertine skirts, and glide off the moss edge, barely raising a ripple across the mirror. By the time the tall green stalk yields and begins its three-minute bow, I am already waiting beneath the dripping fronds, blunt snout angled slightly up, brow-cup wide open.

Nothing in the basin matches that moment. The heavy ribbon of distilled, mineral-free dew strikes my terraced back with a bright clatter that washes straight out over the spillway, drowning the undertone, sending brittle ivory ghost-leaves clinking around my feet. The pure warm water floods my crown-mirror—blinding its geometry for three whole minutes in a rush of clean tumult—rinses the chalky crust from my dark seams, flushes the hidden gill-folds beneath my forward plates, and carries soft shreds of sweet inner-stalk pulp into my wide mouth. A single deep shudder runs through my ribless frame as the stiffness eases out of my cartilage. Then the stalk slowly lifts, dropping back to its low resonant drone, the water in my brow-cup settles to glass, and the whole sixty-pace cathedral reappears in crisp, freshly washed lines.

---

Because I hold the southeastern margin, I am the one who knows the quiet visitors best.

I do not know their faces, or what color they wear against the cold wind outside. I know them the way the stone knows them: by weight, by cadence, by the exact shape of the acoustic shadow they cast across the floor.

The moment a foot settles inside the curved throat of the cleft, the steady hum rising through my belly acquires two moving pockets of softness, and the fine lattice in my brow-cup tilts cleanly toward the entrance. I do not retreat into the vents beneath my moss bolster. I simply tuck my tail close, pull my pleated pads under my eaves, and become another low ochre mound at the edge of the pan.

Over forty years, I have felt their generations change beneath my belly.

When I was young—barely a decade out from under the hanging aerial roots, my plates still thin and pale—there was a heavier pair of warm dampeners that came every dry season. Their left stride lingered a hair longer on the wet stone than their right, as if one knee carried an old stiffness from the scree. They worked with unhurried precision at the dry pocket six paces away: the slow scrape of basalt, the soft, muffled *thump-thump-thump* of the beaten wooden mallet sending harmless, drop-sized rings shimmering across my crown-mirror.

Then, for five or six summers, two pairs crossed the cleft together. The second pair was lighter, quicker, too eager at first—its footsteps struck the wet aprons with a slight slap that muddied the standing waves across the eastern pan. Each time that happened, the heavier pair went completely still beside it, holding motionless for the span of two falling drops from the overhang, until the lighter pair learned to slide its soles flat across the algae-slick travertine without breaking the surface.

One bright midday in my twenty-fifth year, a thick dorsal plate near the middle of my back finally grew too heavy with lime, dried loose from my living skin with a quiet scrape, and slid down onto the margin of my terrace. For half a season, it sat half-submerged at the rim of the pan, casting a tiny curved bend into the standing lattice at the edge of my brow-cup. When the two pairs came that autumn, the lighter one paused by my margin. I held completely still under my lidded glare. After a long breath, that tiny curved bend lifted cleanly off the floor, sharpening the lattice where it had lain. Then the twin dampeners moved back toward the cleft, carrying a piece of my own travertine out into the thin, roaring air beyond the mountain lip—somewhere I will never go, where water does not sing and nothing stays warm.

Now, only the second pair comes. Its stride has grown heavier over the seasons, carrying the exact, patient cadence of the first. Sometimes, after it has pinned its aerial-root fibers beneath smooth cobbles on the central slab and collected its folded leaf-cup of cedar-scented resin, it does not leave right away. It walks slowly to the smooth, thinning apron near the spillway—just six paces from my rim, where no resin bleeds and no silica paste settles—kneels in the shallow glass of draining water, and rests both palms flat against the vibrating travertine.

One afternoon last summer, while those two warm hands rested on the rock beside my edge, an elder across the northern floor drove a deep, sub-audible beat down through its chest-bladder to claim a towering stalk near the center.

Across all sixty paces of sunlit water, the shivering concentric lattices snapped instantly into razor-sharp, six-pointed geometric stars. The stars held motionless for eight heartbeats, burning bright in the glare, and I pressed my own chest flat against the wet stone and answered with a slower double-pulse—not to claim the stalk, only to return the measure, sending pale rings racing inward from every rim, right across the apron where the visitor knelt.

In my crown-mirror, I felt the tremor pass straight through those two resting palms. The twin warm shadows did not jerk back. They did not stand, or splash, or break the shallow mirror around their knees. They stayed pressed flat to the stone, holding the hollow drop behind their own ribs until the six-pointed stars relaxed back into ordinary lattices, and then rose slowly, tucked their tools into the dry pocket, and slipped back out into the wind.

We have never looked into each other's eyes, and we never will. That is not how this room keeps its peace. We simply share the same hum for an hour without breaking the pattern.

---

There are seasons when the sky-roof turns hostile—when winter sleet drives straight down through the open air, shredding the crisp lattices in the pans into blinding churn and turning the warm water to boiling white steam. When the crown-mirror goes unreadable, I know it is time to leave the daylight behind.

I glide beneath the hanging curtains of beaded aerial roots, nudge under the black, waterlogged hem of my moss bolster, and pour myself headfirst down the smooth stone throat worn into the travertine. My flexible seams compress; my heavy tail slips cleanly out of the cold air without a splash.

Down in the flooded chimneys, there is no geometry left to read, because there is no distance left to measure. In pitch blackness and bathhouse heat, the mountain's bronchial hum is thick as oil against every inch of skin and shell. Here, the six of us who share the upper nave press close in the drowned dark, pale ochre plates grazing gently against one another, chest-bladders humming at contact range where no star-pattern is needed. Here we mate; here the pale, clay-soft young are born before they ever crawl up into the shadowy tangles of aerial roots to catch their first drops of clear condensation. Down in the stone throat, I am not a listener sitting at the margin of a cathedral—I am inside the lung itself.

Yet when the sleet clears and the steam thins above the vents, I am always among the first to push my blunt snout back up through the moss into the clear high-altitude glare, hungry for the shock of my brow-cup refilling and sharpening, hungry for the slow creep of the flute-reeds beginning their half-day climb.

---

Lately—over the last two winters, perhaps three—I have noticed a quiet change at the very tip of my tail.

The dark slate-blue groove between the final two scalloped plates has narrowed by the width of a hair. It weeps a fraction less of its sharp-sweet solvent now. The chalk-white snails linger longer along that seam, rasping diligently, yet after every pure dew-spill a faint pale bridge of silica stays behind at the bottom of the joint, stubborn as frost.

It does not frighten me. I have twenty years left, maybe less, maybe more; in a room built to the clock of dripping limestone, there is no sharp line between moving and staying. Whenever I rest against the crooked eastern island beside my pan, I am leaning against the calcified flanks of an elder who curled up here before I was born, whose empty crown-mirror caught a drifting seed and raised the very jade reeds that have washed my seams for forty summers.

I already know where I will settle when the lime reaches my hips and uncurling becomes too heavy a bargain. Not out on the smooth spillway, where the thinning water would sweep a seed straight over the edge into the wind, and not back beneath the dry eastern overhang, where nothing green can drink. I will glide two paces inward from my favorite margin, where the sunlight strikes full at noon and the standing waves still stretch faintly toward the open sky.

I will tuck my tail tight against my flank for the last time. One by one, starting from the back and creeping patiently forward, the seams will seal. The snails will step onto the terrace and find younger, wetter channels to tend. My milky eyes will cloud shut against the glare.

Long after my legs have fused seamlessly into the travertine floor, the shallow mineral collar on my brow will stay brimming with clear warm water. It will go on holding the mountain's bronchial hum in fine, shivering lattices—tilting gently when careful feet enter the cleft, breaking into bright clatter when the western stalks bow, snapping into six-pointed stars when the younger dwellers speak across the sunlit floor—until a single seed drops out of the emerald canopy above, settles into the quiet cup of my forehead, and sends its pale roots down through my stone ribs to raise a new choir against the open sky.
