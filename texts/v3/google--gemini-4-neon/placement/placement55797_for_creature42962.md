My first instinct is to come down through the fissure with a woven reed basket on my back.

It is the easiest shape to reach for, because I already know what it is to have warm blood, clumsy boots, and a body that counts time in breaths and winters. I can picture myself easily enough slipping down the narrow damp ledges, feeling the wind vanish behind my shoulders and the vast, papery roar rise to meet me. I would be the harvester who moves a little too slowly—the one who lingers beside the exposed seam of black chert after the blades are already wrapped in hide, watching the flat pewter light sit on a kilometer of newly broken rock. I would test the grandparents’ warnings without quite breaking them, keeping my voice shut inside my throat, leaving a clean crescent of waste flakes untouched beside the battered river cobble, wondering why the dark V between two barn-sized slabs makes the hair lift lightly along my forearms.

And yet, if I stay with that image for more than a minute, it starts to feel false.

Even the quietest harvester is made of hurry. They carry the upper world down on their wool and skin—dry grass, woodsmoke, salt sweat, the restless tick of a pulse that has to stay ahead of the chill. They descend with an errand and climb out before the cold reaches their joints. To them, the roar of the bats is a weight to endure, the sharp talus an obstacle to cross, the stillness a warning not to stay. If I were one of them, my real life would be somewhere up on the plateau, in the glare and the frost and the turning weather—places this valley barely acknowledges.

What holds me here isn't the excursion. It is the wish to stop carrying the weather on my back.

For a moment I try letting go of human scale altogether—imagining myself as something simpler, completely folded into the valley’s equilibrium. A pale fern leaned out of a damp grit crevice, never growing taller than a handspan. A translucent amphipod drifting in a shallow, lime-rimmed cup of seepage, blind to the pewter vault overhead. There is a clean appeal to that: no errands, no friction, no edges to bruise against. Just mineral patience.

But that slips away too. Pure stillness isn't what draws me. What draws me is *noticing* inside stillness—the quiet, deliberate act of paying attention to a world that almost never changes, until the smallest shift becomes legible as music. To be a fern is only to endure the valley. I want to read it.

And once I admit that, the boots vanish. The basket falls away. I realize I was never descending the fissures at all. I was already down on the floor, feeling the ledges tremble half a kilometer off.

If you knew where to look, you would find me along the western talus, a little downwind of the exposed black chert seam and just inside the pale margin beneath one of the narrower ceiling rifts.

It is not the deepest dark of the valley, and it is nowhere near the soft, churning warmth of the largest guano hills. I dislike loose footing under my palms. Here the dolostone slabs fell wide and flat, tilted into crisp shelves with deep, shadowed troughs between them. Enough diluted daylight leaks down through the mist-choked rift overhead to paint the stone a dull pewter and keep a few thin skins of velvet-dark moss clinging to the vertical faces. Above my overhang, a horizontal bedding plane weeps a cold black sheen down the cliff, gathering along the lip until a drop grows heavy enough to let go.

I am somewhere in the long, unhurried middle of my life. My hide is no longer the smooth, fragile umber of a young one that startles when a cave-bird booms from the upper ledges, nor have my knees begun to lock under permanent plates of lime. Still, the valley has started writing itself onto me. A pale, chalky rime crusts the sharp ridge of my spine and the knobby points of my elbows, cracking with a dry, eggshell whisper whenever I unfold after six days of rest. Behind my left shoulder blade, in a damp crease my own long fingers cannot quite reach, a smear of flat liverwort has taken root—no wider than a thumbprint, soft as wet dust.

Most of the time, I lie folded cleanly into the angles of a broad limestone shelf, nostrils turned upwind into the steady subterranean breath.

To live here is to trade the air for the rock. Above me, the millions of swiftlets and horseshoe bats weave their unbroken currents, and their clicks, rasps, and snapping wings pack the cavern with a surf-like pressure that would stun an open ear. When I want to know what the valley is doing, I lower my bare sternum, my forearms, and the flat hinge of my jaw straight onto the stone.

The transformation is immediate, and I never grow tired of it. Through solid dolostone, the roaring ceiling thins out into a mild, distant fuzz, and the whole wide floor opens up in startling clarity. The world becomes a quiet map of weight and distance. Twenty meters off, a seepage drop strikes a cupped slab with a clean, hollow tick. Farther out across the naked scree, a lean civet steps lightly from shard to shard, pausing, shifting its weight before slipping into a crevice. Along the base of the second guano hill, the faint, dry tremor of a cave snake sliding across grit. Down here, silence is not the absence of motion; it is simply what remains when you listen through something dense enough to refuse the static.

Because I hold the stretch near the chert seam, I am often the first to know when the warm ones return.

The breath of the valley tells me long before the stone does. Normally the air carries permanent, familiar ribbons—wet chalk from the northern wall, cold iron off the fractured chert, the sharp ammonia of the roosts, a faint fungal sweetness from the aging rims of the mounds. Then, without warning, something violent slices through the drift: sun-baked grass, wind-scoured wool, cured hide, resin, and the fierce, dizzying iron of hot blood. It is an odor so bright it feels almost loud.

Minutes later, the shelf beneath my ribs wakes with their footsteps—heavy, hurried, unsteady, sending loose wedges of dolostone ringing sharply across the talus.

I do not retreat. I simply draw my elbows tighter to my ribs, let the lime along my back align with the cleaved edges of the rock, and lower the translucent gray membrane across my eyes once to clear the fine ammonia dust. From forty paces off, I am only a damp trough of shadow between two slabs, and my eyes are two shallow pockets of cold rainwater.

I watch them work. They always move as if the ceiling were about to finish falling. Two small, upright shapes wrapped in fibrous skins, bright with heat, crossing fifty meters of scree in a noisy burst and kneeling beside the flat-topped boulder. When one of them lifts the river cobble and strikes the chert seam, a crisp, crystalline shockwave shoots straight through the bedrock and rings cleanly in my jawbone—a note this valley never makes on its own. Sometimes they build a brief, bright knot of flame from twigs carried down from the upper world, and the sharp bitter sting of live smoke rides the subterranean draft for a kilometer, lifting my chin involuntarily into the air.

It looks exhausting, to burn that fast. To hurry through a single fraction of a gloom and scramble back up the fissures before the cool air can even settle into your bones.

And yet, when the clumsy footsteps finally fade upward into the ledges and the last clatter of loose scree dies away, I am the one who unfolds from the trough.

I cross the naked dolostone slowly—three points settled before the fourth lifts, horn-plated fingertips testing each tilted shard so not a single stone speaks beneath my weight—until I reach the flat-topped boulder where they worked. I crouch beside it and lay one wide, dry palm flat against the limestone where a harvester sat.

Usually, there is still a faint ghost of warmth clinging to the rock. It bleeds away fast into the cool equilibrium of the hall, but for a few moments it presses back against my cold skin like a pulse from another universe. I hold my hand there until the stone matches my palm again. Then I lower my nostrils to the small smear of cold gray twig-ash, taking in the bitter, impossible trace of fire, and slide my long fingers once along the fire-hardened ash pole they left wedged in a crack near the talus—marveling at wood straight and tall as a small tree, bound in dried sinew that smells of open sky. I leave the pole standing untouched.

Then I pick up the battered river cobble resting beside the crescent of freshly struck waste flakes.

This is the thing I cannot stop returning to. Every stone in my valley is newly broken, cleaved sharp as glass, unsoftened by wind or frost or flood. This cobble has no corners at all. I hold it in both wide hands in the flat pewter light, drawing the calloused pads of my thumbs across its dense, rounded curve, trying to imagine a water violent and ceaseless enough to erase every fracture plane from a rock. Sometimes I press it lightly against my chest, half-hoping some vibration of that vanished current still sleeps inside its grain. There is nothing there—only smooth, cold weight.

I always set it back beside the boulder, precisely where the warm hands left it.

Instead, every ten or fifteen years, I take one discarded flake of black chert from the outer edge of their crescent. I carry it back beneath my overhang, far enough from the harvesters’ path that no boot will ever come near it, and wedge it carefully into a shallow cup of dolostone right where a slow seepage drip strikes.

I have four such places tucked beneath the ledges of my territory—not lined up, not marked by stacked stones, just held cleanly in memory. One holds the clean skull of a swiftlet I found thirty years ago, now half-swallowed in a smooth pale tongue of lime. Another holds a curved snake rib, nearly fused to the floor. A third holds a notched bone gorge a harvester dropped long before the moss took hold on my shoulder. And beside it sits an older chert flake I carried here decades ago.

Every few years, after a long meal of cave crickets or a fallen bat leaves me with nothing to do for a week, I crawl to the overhang and run my thumb across that older flake. When I first set it down, its glassy black edge could have sliced my skin cleanly. Now it is wrapped in a dull, chalky collar of dissolved mineral, its razor sharpness softened, blunted, slowly folded back into the limestone of the valley. Feeling that edge yield—millimeter by patient millimeter—is how I know time is still moving down here. Even the quick, sharp things the warm ones strike out of the rock eventually learn the pace of the drips.

I am mostly alone, spaced hundreds of meters from the nearest of my kind, and that suits me. Yet I am never cut off.

Sometimes, when the mist choking the high ceiling rift thickens to a deeper gray—the only sign we get that winter has locked the plateau above—or simply after a long stretch of quiet, I flatten my chest and throat firmly against my wide listening shelf and wake the ribbed sac along my windpipe. No sound escapes into the roaring air. Instead, a slow, deep pulse drops straight into the bedrock and rolls outward beneath the guano hills and the open scree.

It is not a story, and it asks for nothing. It is only the weight of being here: *the western wall is seeping faster; the warm ones came and struck black glass; the shelf holds.*

Then I rest my chin on the stone and wait, until—across half a kilometer of faulted limestone—a second, slower rhythm walks back into my ribs to answer me.

Once every few years, one of those answers draws closer, and I walk out across the open expanses of bare rubble to meet another of my kind in the shadowless gloom. We do not speak or circle. We crouch facing each other on clean rock, lift our wide, four-fingered hands, and press them palm to palm, fingers interlocking loosely along the ridges of tough skin. We lean forward until the flat sides of our angular heads touch, sharing a soft, bone-deep hum that neither the owls nor the swirling bats overhead can ever hear. Then, with calm, meticulous care, they reach past my shoulder and use the thick horn-gray plates at the tips of their fingers to pry away the over-heavy crust of lime that has built along the hollow of my spine—leaving the tiny thumbprint of dark liverwort undisturbed—and I do the same for them, before we part again across the sharp stone.

That is where I find myself: not visiting the dark, and not lost in it, but fitted cleanly into its seams. Downwind of the chert, beneath a steady drip, with one eye on the slow pale skin growing over a shard of black glass, and my chest pressed flat to the rock—listening to the whole vast, cool hollow hold its breath, and waiting, without hurry, for the next drop to fall.
