At first I think I would be terrified of the noise—that wall of wet pressure held against the ribs without a single trough to catch a breath in. I have always loved quiet, the kind where you can hear a single drop separate itself from a ceiling or tell by the hush outside a window that the weather has turned. Here there is no weather to turn, and no silence to hold anything in. Standing on the inland basalt rim, looking down over the miles of glistening roofs and white-streaked towers, my first instinct would be to look for a cleft high up in the cold rim-rock, somewhere above the roosts and far back from the sea-caves, where the roar might thin out to a tolerable wind.

Except there is no wind. And if you climb too far from the basin’s warmth, away from the seepage and the lamps and the dense press of oiled shoulders, there is only raw black glass and the flat pewter lid of the sky. Nothing grows up there to keep you company. No grass softens the ground; no birds nest where the sea-fish aren’t carried. Isolation in this valley would not feel like peace. It would feel like being laid out unfinished on a quarry-bench.

So I would come down the steep, shoulder-wide stairs, testing my wrapped soles against the sharp pahoehoe folds, letting the city close over my head until the sky is only a pale strip wedged between corbeled cornices. And as I descend, I would start looking for where my hands want to stop.

Not at the outer lip. I could stand on the wet quays for an hour, fascinated by the weir-tenders wading waist-deep in dark upwelling brine, watching the eldest lip-walkers read the ocean swell through the bone shafts of their poles and the vibration in their knees. There is a clean majesty to living right where the world’s hunger hits the stone. But my body is not built to wrestle sinew nets heavy with pulsing squid while the lava-tubes hammer my molars loose. I would be in their way, squinting into the harbor mist, too slow to catch the wide shoulder-signs before a wave lifted the coracle.

Nor up on the honeycombed flanks of the rookery-towers. I admire the tower-keepers—their slit whalebone visors, their lean scarred forearms reaching blind into reeking stone pockets to count speckled eggs by touch alone—but I do not have that kind of fearless intimacy with beaks and heights. I would hesitate a fraction too long over a snapping cormorant and drop the egg.

Even in the open communal yards, where the percussive clink of obsidian knapping cuts neatly across the basin’s roar, I would pause and then move on. There is a dazzling decisiveness to striking volcanic glass: one clean blow of a basalt hammerstone, a flake rings off keener than iron, and you have a blade that will gut twenty fish before it chips and gets tossed into the grit-baskets. Daily, disposable sharpness. I respect it, the way you respect lightning—and for the same reason I know it isn’t mine. The whole basin is already sharp. The rock beneath every step is infant glass; the mineral crusts grow in needle-edged crystals; even the air has a bruising grain to it. I wouldn't want to spend my life adding more edges to a world that has never known a pebble worn round by a river.

What pulls at me sits three paces to the side of the knappers, lower to the wet stone, slower, and quieter in its rhythm.

It is the grinding. And just behind the grinding, the sluices.

If I lived long enough in the basin to know my own shape in it, I think I would find myself in the middle tiers—neither high enough to live among the shrieking parapets nor low enough to taste the constant spray of the outer weirs—working as a **sinter-miller and bone-smoother**, keeping a bench tucked just beneath a corbeled overhang where a communal yard narrows into a warm, steaming alley.

It takes a while to see how much of the city passes through a low stone bench like that, and why it feels so unmistakably like home.

Part of my work would begin in the alleys themselves, walking slowly along the carved drainage runnels with a dense basalt pick, a bone chisel, and a flat-bottomed quill basket strapped across my back. Because the warm mineral seepage never pauses, the chalk-white and ochre sinter never stops creeping inward from the channel rims. Left alone, it strangles the flow. The big sluice-crews do the heavy clearing along the main thoroughfares, hacking out thick shelvings of crust before scalding water spills over low thresholds. I would take the narrower lanes, the branching household runnels and the tucked-away seepage basins where the water runs clearest and hottest, looking for the purest veins of white crust.

Most people barely notice the difference between one patch of sinter and another—it is all nuisance rock, brittle and knife-edged, forever needing to be beaten back. But if you squat beside the warm water long enough, you learn to read it by eye and thumbnail. Down near the grease-houses and the lower iron-vents, the crust grows stained ochre and grainy; grind it and it still bites like fine scoria, turning a person’s knuckles raw under the salt-damp. Up in the tighter middle lanes, where the seepage weeps slow and clean through tight basalt fissures, the sinter builds in thin, pale sheets like dried foam—fragile, snow-bright, almost weightless when you pry it loose with a bone point.

This is where I would first learn to listen the way the basin listens.

Before I ever tap a chisel against a choked rim, I lay my bare, oiled palm flat against the wet stone beside the channel and wait. Not for silence—silence never comes—but for the narrow, high band of sound threaded right between the deep cave-boom and the upper shriek of the birds. The frogs. Hundreds of them packed belly-to-stone in the warm chinks along the paving slabs. When you sit still beside a runnel, their trill stops sounding like noise and starts sounding like a pulse you can measure. You hear when the chorus thins two paces upstream; you feel, through the damp basalt under your knee, whether the water beneath the crust is still running hot and steady or backing up cold behind a mineral plug.

When I lift a loose slab to clear a pocket, two or three pale-bellied frogs usually tumble out, slick and pulsing at the throat. I scoop them into the hollow of my palm—cool against my oil-warmed skin, lighter than an empty gull-egg—and hold them in a damp folded hide scrap until the brittle white rim is flaked away and the new wedge of crushed shell is tapped tight. Then I tilt my palm back toward the wet chink and watch them vanish into the warmth. If ever a stretch of runnel goes mute under my hands—if the frogs pull too deep into the rock to reach, or climb out onto the cold stairs—I drop my chisel, step straight to the nearest curtained doorway, and lay my palm hard on the householder’s collarbone to bark the warning into their ear: *The vent is souring. The water is turning.* Long before the masons bring their heavy levers, the frogs and the quiet workers in the alleys have already read the shift in the bedrock.

Then I carry my basket of sharp white shards back to the bench under the overhang, and the other half of my work begins.

Nothing natural in this valley is smooth. Every time I look at a loaf of rough gray pumice resting in my lap, I think of that—how smoothness here is not a gift of weather or water, as it might be in some imagined, wind-scoured world beyond the mist, but a stubborn human invention. You have to wear sharpness away one stroke at a time, trading your own patience against the rock.

First I crush the white sinter crusts in a hollowed basalt bowl using a heavy stone pounder whose handle has been worn satin-slick by three hands before mine—kin or teachers whose sign-names I still see flashing across the plaza on younger wrists. The raw shards crackle like crab-shell. Then I scatter the coarse powder across a broad, slightly dished slab of fine-grained pumice and lay a second, smaller pumice block on top, rocking it in slow, figure-eight circles with the weight of my shoulders. Back and forth. Back and forth. Beneath the constant bass roar of the sea-caves, the dry hiss of stone on stone is almost inaudible even to me; I hear it instead through my wrists, through the catch and drag of the grit.

At first it grates, toothy and uneven, snagging against the porous gray rock. Gradually the resistance softens. Every few dozen strokes I lift the upper stone, wet my forefinger with a smear of rendered fish-oil from a tiny clay cup at my knee, and rub a pinch of the white powder against my thumb. If I feel even a single crystalline prick—one tiny splinter of uncrushed mineral—I drop the block back down and keep rocking. You grind until the rock gives up its edges altogether, until what remains is a cool, dry flour so fine it clings to the creases of oiled skin like pale mist.

That is the dust that lets the city speak.

I don’t think I would ever tire of the quiet truth of that. Without this chalk, in the pewter dimness of the narrow lanes, a dark oiled hand against wet black basalt disappears at ten paces. People come to my overhang all day—haulers pausing with quill-baskets braced against the wall, renderers with grease gleaming along their collarbones, mothers with infants slung in greased seal-hide pouches—holding out their wrists to dip into my stone basin or trade a jar of clear lamp-oil and a bundle of brine-cured fish for a stitched bladder of fresh powder. They dust their palms, slap them lightly together so a brief pale cloud hangs motionless in the windless air, and immediately their hands wake up, flashing sharp and legible against the gloom. Even the toddlers smear it clumsily across their own chins before their mothers wipe it down onto their tiny wrists and guide their fingers through the first signs for *warm*, *bird*, *hold*.

And because you cannot sit all day grinding chalk without getting to know every texture of pumice and bone, the other work drifts naturally onto the same apron.

Neighbors bring me what has gone rough or what needs to be made bearable. A newly carved whale-blade drinking scoop whose rim still carries the tiny, ragged chatter-marks of an obsidian scraper. A child’s first drilled knuckle-bead, rough inside the bore where a bone awl twisted through, ready to saw through its sinew cord unless someone eases the lip with a slender rod of wet pumice and oiled sand-grit. An old mason’s hammer-grip that developed a tiny hairline spall when it struck wet basalt too hard. I tuck each piece between my hide-wrapped knees, coat it with oil and the finest spent sinter-paste from the edge of my slab, and work it down until a thumb can travel all the way around it without catching once.

Sometimes, when a tower-keeper brings down the clean, hollow wing-bone or leg-bone of a shearwater that died on a parapet, I make the bone-whistles.

That is the closest my hands ever come to sound. You file the mouth-notch carefully against the corner of a sharp pumice wedge, beveling the lip by hair-breadths, smoothing the interior bore with a stiff primary quill coated in fine grit until the airway has no burr to scatter the breath. You never test a finished whistle at full strength in the yard—a single piercing shriek will freeze fifty people mid-stair and send the scavenging dogs bolting toward the nearest steam-well, thinking a retaining wall has slipped or a child has gone over a ledge. Instead I press the pale bone lightly to my lower lip and blow the barest thread of warm air across the bevel, cupping my other palm close around the far end so the vibration never escapes the shelter of my own hands. I don't need to hear it scream to know it is true; I feel the sudden, crisp tightening of the air inside the hollow bone, a tiny sharp hum against my fingertips that says: *When the bad hour comes, this will cut clean over a half-million birds.*

Between batches of chalk and the slow polishing of bone, sitting still while the whole terraced warren climbs and hauls around me, I would become—without quite meaning to—a kind of quiet switchboard for the tier.

When you stay in one place with your hands pale and your eyes open, you see everyone’s dialects pass by. Down from the upper rookeries come the lean keepers, signing tight and high near the brow because their lower arms are always braced against ledges; up from the quays come the heavy-shouldered coracle-folk, swinging broad, sweeping harbor-signs built to punch through thick coastal mist. When a deafened old weir-tender stops by my overhang to trade salt-fish for a pouch of fine chalk and gets caught in a misunderstanding with a sharp-fingered render-boy from the inland alleys—the boy’s wrists flickering too small and fast against his hip for the elder to read—I can lift my dusted hands from the pumice slab and catch the sentence in the air, slowing it down, opening it wider at shoulder height so both of them see the trade clearly and laugh, slapping each other’s greased forearms before moving on.

Even the lean, notched-eared scavenging dogs figure out what kind of bench mine is. They don’t beg here; I keep no raw fish-trimmings beside the chalk-basin, where the smell would bring bold cliff-gulls diving under the overhang to scatter my powder. Instead, one or two old dogs—too stiff in the hips now to chase gulls up the steeper staircases—curl along the warm masonry just beside my left knee, where a steam-runnel passes under the paving slabs. When a hauler’s basket drips brine too close to my dry grinding-stone, a single flat downward flick of two white fingers sends the dog trotting out to stand politely in the lane until the hauler steps around, then back again to tuck its slick chin over the bone-slatted rim of my wrapped foot.

That is where I would spend the dim, unchanging hours of the afternoon-gray.

And when the pewter ceiling weighs a little heavier toward slate-dusk—not night, never true darkness, only that gentle deepening that makes the amber pinpricks of the doorway lamps bloom brighter against the wet black stone—I would brush the last of the white dust from my hide apron into a lidded clay jar, lay my smooth hand-stones neatly beside the pumice trough, and push through the heavy double curtain of oiled seal-hide into my own corbeled room.

The roar drops by half the instant the wet hide falls shut behind my shoulders.

It never leaves, of course. It becomes a thick, warm enclosure, like leaning your ear against the flank of a huge sleeping animal. Inside, the tiny basalt saucer holds its braided wick of bird-down floating in clear yellow oil, washing the weeping stone walls in steady orange light. I rinse the white film from my forearms in a warm stone basin dipped from the street runnel, rub fresh oil over my knuckles and face until the salt-sting fades, and sit on a coiled hide mat with my back pressed straight against the masonry.

This is the hour I think I would love most—not because the world has stopped, which would feel terrifying, like the bottom dropping out of the floor, but because the world has softened its grain. Neighbors from the adjacent vaults slip past the hide curtain, bringing a bladder of hot poached squid-broth lifted straight from the communal steam-well, or a handful of speckled gull-eggs warmed in mineral water. We pass a drinking-scoop that I smoothed three roof-hides ago—or that my predecessor smoothed ten roof-hides before that—and feel the glossy, lip-worn curve of whale-bone passing warm from hand to hand.

Later, when the dishes are wiped clean with oiled hide scraps, three or four of us lean back against the same damp wall, shoulders touching, eyes half-closed in the orange lamp-wash, and begin the rib-song.

I don't have a loud voice, and I think even after years in the basin I would dislike the harsh, tight bark needed to shout into someone's ear out in the open alleys. But I would love rib-song. You don't fight the sea-caves. You close your mouth, drop your breath deep into the belly, and let a low hum rise until it finds the exact weight of the ocean pounding into the lava-tubes a mile away—then ease your pitch just a hair above it, or a hair below. When the woman beside me catches the opposite hair-breadth, the two intervals rub against the subterranean thunder and the whole corbeled stone vault begins to throb in slow, wide waves. You feel it behind your breastbone, in the roots of your teeth, down the length of your spine where it touches the wet rock. It is the exact acoustic twin of what I do all day with the pumice: taking something immense and bruising and working against it gently until it becomes round enough to rest inside.

Sometimes, before we sleep, someone holds their freshly oiled hands near the yellow flame of the wick-saucer and lets the slow shadow-dance begin—wrists rising like the gentle lift of the tide in the stone weirs, fingers rippling across the weeping stone like shearwaters banking through mist, telling the long, unhurried story of a seasonless city that never forgets a mason’s wedge or a crooked-beaked gull’s favorite parapet. If a neighbor’s child is curled against my ribs, half-asleep with one cheek pressed to the warm hide mat, I let them lay their small palm over my moving forearm as I sign the quiet responses, letting them read the last turn of the story through tendon and skin long after their wide dark eyes have shut.

Looking at my hands in that amber light, I realize how I would measure my life here.

Not in winters or summers—those words would sound strange and broken to me, like a roof that kept tearing loose and needing to be lashed back down in a panic. I would measure my years by the pumice slabs stacked along my wall. A thick, pale-gray loaf dragged fresh from the inland quarry-bench when I first took over the overhang; next to it, the older blocks worn slowly down by decades of rocking until their centers are cupped thin as mussel-shells, too fragile to press against sinter anymore and kept only for the final delicate buffing of bone needles and infant knuckle-beads. You can hold your whole history in two palms and feel how much rock you had to turn to dust so that the people around you could speak clearly in the mist and hold spoons that didn't cut their lips.

And when my breath finally grows shallow behind the hide curtain, and my fingers stiffen past the point of rocking the stone or flashing a crisp reply down the lane, there is nothing tragic about what comes next. Kin will wash the last chalk-dust from my wrists with warm water from the runnel I used to chip clean. They will slip the copper ring—rubbed bright and round against greased skin—off my wrist, and take my smooth-handled basalt pounder for whoever next squats beneath the overhang. They will stitch me into my oldest, softest sleeping-hide, lash two unpitted slabs of raw basalt to my ankles, and pole me out in a jawbone coracle at the dimmest turn of slate-dusk, past the weirs and the hammering sea-caves, into the warm mist over the upwelling.

They will lift their white-dusted hands—dusted, likely enough, with the last jar of chalk I ground—and flash my sign-name once, high and wide against the gray air. Maybe it would be the slow, circular press of palm against palm that means *made-smooth*, followed by the small upward curl of rising steam. Then the weighted hide will slip cleanly over the gunwale, sinking down into the dark churn that feeds the shoals, the crabs, the birds, and the city.

Back on the middle tier, nothing will pile up and nothing will erode away. The warm seepage will keep weeping down the basalt chinks; the pale-bellied frogs will keep holding their bright, narrow note between the caves and the towers; and a few roof-hides later, some quiet child squatting beside a steaming runnel with sinter-chalk smeared to their elbows will flash that exact same sign-name across the dim wet stairs, carrying the dust and the smoothness forward inside the basin’s unending breath.
