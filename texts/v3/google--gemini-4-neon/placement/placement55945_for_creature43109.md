If I stood on the jagged rim and looked down into that narrow pocket of wet-tin sky, my first instinct would not be to choose a name or a trade. It would be to test how little space I could take up without disappearing.

At first, I think I might try to imagine myself as something native to the drift—a cloak-moth hanging beneath a cistern overhang until the air cools by a degree, then slipping out into the gulf without a ripple, trailing six pale filaments to read the mist. There is a clean envy in that: no throat to dry, no cough to swallow, no memory of open blue sky. Even the Folded hold a strange, cold pull. To lie wedged sideways in black basalt, drinking mineral sweat, feeling the slow twenty-minute pulse of the lung-ferns outside your door and knowing, with absolute precision, the difference between a dead flake of shale and a living creature taking a frightened second breath—there is a terrible purity to being a boundary condition.

Yet the longer I watch the terraces, the more clearly I know I would not stay in the air, and I would not stay in the stone. I am too drawn to the places where things are *made* to meet without shattering. My eyes keep catching on the small, stubborn pieces of human diplomacy woven into the cleft: the braided rush-wick drinking from a slate trough so water never breaks into a splash; the buried ash-plug swelling silently for five days beneath wet moss until young basalt parts like bread; the horn peg held gently between two teeth so a melody can cross from one skull to another without ever disturbing the room.

Softness is everywhere in the basin. Moss is soft; mist is soft; wool is soft. Anyone can wrap a thing in felt.

What pulls at me—where I finally find myself kneeling, sleeves rolled high above the elbows, skin pale and pebbled from the chill—is the craft of the hard things.

I would be a keeper of a filing-trough.

Not one of the high-terrace harvesters leaning out over the misty drop on greased straps, and not one of the climbers who ascend the northern stair each summer into the screaming blue glare to hammer copper and trade with lowlanders. I would live low on the western side of the rift, one tier down from the communal cistern-tower, in a plastered undercroft whose back wall stands six inches from the deep fracture where the Broad-Ear sleeps.

I would choose the western wall on purpose. The southern cracks are full of lean, nervous young fissures-dwellers that flare out at a dropped bundle of wool. The Broad-Ear is old, clouded, bearded along its lower pleat with mist-algae; it has heard ten generations of careful neighbors and does not wake for small clumsiness. Living beside it would feel less like living beside a monster than living beside a deep, cold well—something you respect simply by never forgetting where the lip is.

In the center of my floor, sunk flush into the damp ash-clay, sits the trough itself: a deep oblong basin of dark slate, fed continuously by a thick braided rush-wick that creeps through a hole in the masonry from the upper mist-combs. Another wick, weighted with a copper ring, hangs over the far lip and trails down into the open crawl-way arch beneath the floor, silently siphoning off the cloudy water so I never have to bail or pour. The surface of the basin is always mirror-still, dark as steep tea, shifting only when my arms slide into it.

That is where most of my days would live: elbow-deep in cold cistern-water, working by touch.

The hardest substances in the city—the ones that ring, clatter, and snap if you handle them carelessly in open air—all end up in my trough. Flint blades. Horn pegs. And, above all, the cleaned ivory brought to me in moss-lined wicker baskets after a dusk laying-out at the deepest end of the rift, where the pearl rivers of bead-crawlers have done their quiet night's work.

You never hear a neighbor's voice in this city, unless you shared a still-belly with them in infancy or sat beside them in the swinging wool dark as they died. Yet underwater, with a wet flint point and a pad of gritty shale held between my thumbs, I would come to know the people of the cleft with a strange, tender intimacy.

A mason's radius is dense and heavy, slightly thickened along an old healed seam; it takes four cloud-turns of slow grinding beneath the waterline to round its shoulders down. The collarbone of an elder who spent her last three winters inside the suspended moss-chamber is light as dry willow, and I have to hold my breath and slow my wrists so it does not split under the saw. Even the bone-dust behaves differently—rising in pale, slow clouds around my knuckles, lingering in the cold water like fog inside the room until the siphon-wick draws it quietly away beneath the floorboards.

What I am really shaping is not ornament, and not even memory. I am shaping the weight of the city's language.

The wrist-cords are readable across forty paces of pewter gloom only because they have heft—because a bead drops cleanly when a forearm snaps, pulling the dyed wool into a crisp line against a grey coat. Yet bare bone striking bare bone makes a dry, bright *click*, small enough to vanish in a lowland market, sharp enough on a quiet southern terrace to make twenty yards of black slate bloom with frost-veined ears.

So every bead I polish underwater has to be rounded like a plum-stone, with no sharp rim left to catch. Every bore-hole is smoothed with a tallow-greased thorn until it glides silently along braided yarn. And once the ivory is lifted out onto a folded pad of boiled moth-felt, dried slowly in the dull red warmth of my moss-brazier, my second work begins: the balancing.

Between every two ancestor-beads, I knot a wafer-thin washer cut from pressed moth-felt and rubbed with milky tallow-wax. I test each finished strand not by looking at it, but by strapping it to my own leather cuff and flicking my wrist in the dark, listening with my whole skin. Too light, and the gesture blurs in the mist—a warning misread, a joke lost. Too loose, and the beads kiss with a tiny hollow tap I feel straight up the bones of my forearm. When it is right, the cord falls with the dead, sure gravity of a rain-soaked hem—entirely mute, entirely legible.

I think I would love the quiet errands that bring people past my pebble-weighted curtain.

A young mother stepping out of the still-belly for the first time in two years, eyes blinking wide in the wet-tin light, carrying a toddler wrapped in a grey sling who still smells of warm lanolin and peat. She lays two small beads on my bench—one ochre-stained from her own father's cords, one pale from a grandmother the child will never know—and watches while I knot them into a miniature cuff of soft white-dyed wool, light enough for a two-year-old's wrist. When she slips it on the child and twitches her own long cords in a slow, blooming arc, the toddler stares at the motion, reaches up, and flicks its tiny fringe back. No sound crosses the room. Yet a whole lineage just passed from hand to hand.

Or a mason who comes in at dusk, cheeks pale, holding a cracked horn peg wrapped in a strip of oiled hide—one half of a tooth-cord he has shared with his partner for thirty years.

That would be the other half of my craft, the one I keep for the late hours when the lung-ferns outside are sighing into deep charcoal night and the basin settles to its softest cadence.

Making a tooth-cord requires the same underwater patience as shaping a bead, because horn cracks loudly if you rush it, and a sinew string must be scraped even along its whole length, soaked in warm tallow, and stretched slowly over days using smooth river-pebbles for weights. There is no way to test a tooth-cord alone. You need a second jawbone to close the circuit.

So when a cord is nearly finished, I sit knee to knee on the moss-matted floor with whoever commissioned it—or, on quiet nights when no one is waiting, with an old friend from the mist-comb terrace above me. I bite gently onto one smooth horn peg; they bite onto the other. We lean back until the waxed sinew pulls taut between our mouths, barely visible in the crimson glow of the smoldering moss-brick.

Then I lift an oiled thumb and pluck.

The room stays dead quiet. Outside the rush curtain, a ribbon of bead-crawlers rolls glossily along the gutter. Six inches behind my plaster wall, the Broad-Ear lies folded against cold basalt, hearing nothing at all.

Inside my skull, a whole warm room opens.

The tone blooms straight up through my molars and into the hollows behind my eyes—low, dark, resonant as water moving under ice. When my friend slides two fingers along the sinew to shorten the note, I feel the shift inside my own jaw before I even think of it as music. We pass phrases back and forth without air, without breath, without ever risking a throat. It is the strangest, truest kind of conversation I can imagine: a voice that exists only in the space between two willing listeners, leaving the rest of the world untouched.

I think, once—when I was younger, before my elbows knew the exact cold of the trough—I would have caught the edge of *the dry stone*. Not the fierce, shattering kind that makes a young mason stare too long at loose roof-slates, only a quiet, aching wonder at what my own throat could make if it were allowed to open. I can picture myself taking the leather satchel of tallow-cakes, fitting the smoked horn-slit goggles over my dusk-bred eyes, and climbing the northern chimney through the pewter ceiling.

I know what would happen up on that wind-scoured granite spur. The violent blue sky would make my eyes water even through the horn slits. The gale tearing off the snowfields would feel clumsy and bruising, ripping every note to shreds before it could finish forming. I might strike two stones together once, just to hear the bright, reckless crack go unpunished; I might try one hoarse, unused sound into the roaring air and feel how thin and naked it is when nothing holds it close.

And I would not walk down the scree toward the lowlands. By the third afternoon, sunburned and exhausted by all that unshaped loudness, I would climb back down the ironwood pegs and slip beneath the pewter sheet like a diver returning to calm water. I would feel the velvet moss give softly under my felt-bound boots, tuck a fresh pellet of tallow-wax between my cheek and gum, and know—without regret, without bitterness—that I belong down here.

Sometimes, in deep winter, when the mountain groans in its sleep and shifts a fraction of an inch on its raw roots, a thin strip of molted cartilage drifts out from the western fracture and catches on the tallow-vines beside my door.

I lift it gently with horn tongs and hold it up to the dim tin light before carrying it to the communal hall to be pressed and sewn into a curtain-hem. Up close, the frost-white veins look like the grain inside polished bone, or the branching nets of our mist-combs, or the small map of lines inside my own water-softened palms. Everything in this pocket of sky is shaped by the same quiet physics—tension held just below the snapping point, weight carried without falling, attention stretched taut and clean.

Then I set the tongs down on a pad of boiled felt, roll back my sleeves, and sink my arms into the dark trough again. Outside, a fresh sheet of cloud drags its belly across the rim. The lung-ferns draw in their slow, damp breath. Beneath the cold water, my thumbs find the rough edge of an ancestor's bone, and begin the long, silent work of teaching it how to speak.
