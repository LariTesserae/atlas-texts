At first, I try to stand where you left me—at the mouth of the cleft, leaning slightly forward into the wet kiln-breath, watching the plum-smooth basalt pebble inches from my boot.

It does not hold.

Out there, a finger’s width past the warm air, the mountain is too honest for me. The thin sky has no patience for standing still; the cold clamps the lungs shut, and the freshly sheared basalt beneath my soles is all raw conchoidal edge, bright and unforgiving enough to slice rubber down to bone. Worse, from outside the lip, I am only a visitor peering into a terrarium—someone with a return route, a compass, a straight line back down beneath the white shelf of clouds. That isn't true. When I look into the pantry, I do not feel like someone who climbed here to look in. I feel like someone trying to remember which wall I came out of.

So I let the boots go, and step across the invisible pane into the heat.

For a moment I try the floor—down in the churning mosaic, among the boiled-pink scavengers and vitreous crawlers. I try having six needle-legs, a hard pale dome, a small sharp splinter gripped in wet pincers. Down there the afternoon is endless and breathless: a palm-sized slab of stone stretches into fifteen furious seconds of running; a dome-backed grazer splits down its seam and walks away from itself; every pool is a silent brawl over a keen edge of volcanic glass.

That does not fit either. There is too much appetite in the mosaic, too much frantic grabbing and dropping. Every creature on the floor is spent by its own hurry, scraping sulfur as if the kiln might cool in the next heartbeat. Whatever I am in this pocket, I am not hurried. I do not want to fight for a sliver of basalt in a crowded scallop. I want the intervals between the surges. I want to watch a basin’s flat mirror dimple with concentric rings and wait—unmoving—for the drop to detach from the obsidian roof and fall neatly into the aftermath it already made.

Naturally, then, my mind reaches toward the occupant.

I try draping myself through the pocket’s slack: kiln-blooded, chin-deep in the back pool, narrow wedge skull smooth as wet bone, sulfur-yellow eyes slitted against the plain midday glare. I try the patient mastery of the workbench—holding the glossy basalt hammer poised above an obsidian core, waiting for the fine white crescent of fracture to bloom on its own before letting the blow fall. I try the folded immensity of a body that anchors its shins in the back vent, rests its ribs inside three stretched inches of shelf, and reaches out of two walls at once.

It is very close. I recognize the stillness. I recognize the way it uses the frayed laws of the cleft without resenting them—reading a fleeing scavenger’s direction from the shadow still clinging to sunlit rock, taking what arrives slightly before its cause.

Yet when I try to inhabit that boiling core, something in me slips right back out of it. I do not have that weight. I do not have a furnace-hearted hunger, or grinding plates behind a pleated mouth, or the quiet sovereignty of a creature that owns the room it folds into. When I picture the occupant resting its chin in the scalding water, I am not looking *out* from behind those pale brow-ridges of silica. I am looking *at* them, from somewhere higher up, somewhere thinner, wedged slightly out of the way.

That is when I finally notice my own hands.

They are not boiled pink. They are paper-pale, drier along the backs of the knuckles, plated in thin, milky scales of silica that have gone slightly chalky at the edges. Six long, lean fingers—two opposing outer digits, double-jointed, flattened like ribbons against the underside of the leaning obsidian fang. The broad pads of the fingertips are stained slate-black with volcanic glass dust, the exact dark grain of the occupant’s own palms, only lighter. Hollow where the living fingers are dense.

I look down through a gap between my dry ribs and see a steaming basin two feet below, and the shadow of a needle-legged hunter lingering on the stone two heartbeats after the hunter has bolted.

I am one of the shed skins.

Not the living body coiled in the folded basalt, and not the frantic little fauna crowding the pools. I am a cast-off husk—an anatomical afterimage that split down the spine seasons ago (or an hour ago; the pocket keeps no honest count), closed its dorsal seam back together in the steam, and climbed silently into the narrower faults.

Once I stop trying to fill a body with blood in it, everything in the cleft clicks cleanly into place around me.

You think hollowness would feel like lack, like an absence waiting to be filled, but in the kiln it feels like relief. I weigh almost nothing. The scalding condensate that beads across the walls and drops from the overhang does not blister me; there is nothing tender left inside to cook. Warm mist drifts straight through the healed seam along my back and pools inside my chest cavity like light inside a thin shell of blown glass. I have no heart to race when the mountain groans, no appetite driving me into the brawls below. A few of the older husks in the back faults still go through the motions of feeding—cracking a dome-backed grazer with a pebble and pressing hot meat into their sealed mouths until the scraps spill uselessly back out between their ribs—but whatever residue of hunger clung to me when the occupant stepped out of my back dried away early.

What stayed were the quieter habits of the hands.

The feel of a good edge. The weight of a smooth hammer-stone. The compulsion to keep things from sliding away.

Because my eyes are clouded over like drops of dried glue, I do not see the afternoon the way the occupant does. The open mouth of the cleft is a wide, honest blur of pale blue glare; the living limbs rising from the pools are soft pink shapes moving like warm oil through steam; the crawlers are a shifting stipple of wet dark pebbles. What stays sharp to me is sheen—the sudden black gleam where a freshly popped obsidian flake catches the midday sun—and touch. Everything I know of this pantry comes up through my six black-dusted fingertips and the stiff, stretched skins covering the ear-hollows behind my jaw. When a delayed *tink* of struck glass drifts up from the wrong wall half a breath after a strike, I do not hear it so much as feel a crisp, taut vibration shiver once across my cheek-plates.

Most of the other husks spend their seasons in the hairline dark at the back of the pocket, clicking quietly along seams too tight for the living body, worrying dull basalt chips against wet stone until the mineral water finally wins. It always tries to win here. Silica and sulfur creep over everything that lingers, cementing joints, thickening knuckles, turning supple folds into white salt. When a husk stiffens too far to move cleanly, it freezes to the roof like a pale stalactite, and eventually a long, boiled-pink hand reaches up, peels it down in quiet strips, and chews the salted shell soft between its grinding plates so nothing clogs the seams.

I should have been peeled down long ago.

The reason I am still here is that one habit stayed in my fingers that usually belongs only to the living flesh: the habit of the lancet.

Every few surges, when I feel the mineral crust beginning to tighten across the hinges of my wrists or cloud the dry crease of an elbow, I do not wait to turn to stone. I slip down from the obsidian overhang to a quiet shelf near the outer edge of the pocket, where the tiny debitage from the occupant’s knapping collects in finger-deep scallops—micro-flakes of black glass too fine for the living hands to bother with. I choose a razor-thin crescent, wedge it between my opposing outer digits, and shave my own joints clean.

There is no pain in it, and no blood. Only the faint, papery whisper of glass sliding beneath salt, lifting the cloudy silica away in translucent curls that drift down into the warm pools, where pleated ribbon-things immediately ripple over them to graze. It is absurd, perhaps, for a shed skin to groom itself—like a coat brushing its own lapels in an empty hallway—yet the motion is already built into the double-jointed turn of my wrist. I do not decide to stay flexible so much as my hands refuse to let a joint lock while a clean obsidian edge lies within reach.

And because I stay light and un-frozen, I have drifted farther forward than the other husks go.

I live at the rim.

Not outside it—never outside it. I know down to the millimeter where the pocket’s slack ends and the honest sky begins. If I crawled half a palm past the warm basalt lip, the freezing altitude and straight physics out there would take hold of me at once. An echo cannot walk in honest air; I would collapse instantly into a brittle mineral rind and blow away over the dull white shelves of cloud.

So I keep my body wedged in a sunlit horizontal notch just under the outer lip of the obsidian fang, where the afternoon glare is brightest and the scalding pools thin to a shimmering film. From here I can feel both the kiln behind my hollow back and the steep, colorless cold pressing invisibly against the open mouth of the cleft.

It is useful work, staying at the rim.

Twice an hour, before the spire beneath us exhales, I feel the coming surge as a slack, backward pull in the water below my elbows—the basins tightening inward a beat before the deep basalt groans. Before the flood arrives, I glide down along the wet outer shelf and gather the things that sit too close to the brink: a freshly knapped crescent left resting on a scallop, or one of the plum-glossy basalt pebbles that a frantic scavenger dragged half-way toward the edge and abandoned. I tuck them deep into dry fractures beneath the overhang so the overflowing water will not sweep them into the sky.

Then the walls weep in earnest. The basins brim and spill, and the moment that hot, crowded water crosses the invisible pane an inch from my face, it explodes into blinding white plumes of ice-crystal mist.

Inside, the pocket flickers violently out of alignment. For ten breathless seconds the pantry fills with the occupant’s dilated, many-armed presence—pale forearms overlapping in three pools at once, herding newly popped obsidian back into the deep scallops. I flatten myself into my notch near the lip, hooking my six dry fingers into a razor-sharp seam, holding fast while the bright afternoon doubles and triples around me.

Now and then, during the height of a surge, a stray draft from the open sky eddies an inch too far inward and brushes the back of my outermost knuckle.

Instantly, white hoarfrost blooms across the milky silica.

For a single heartbeat, that one finger leaves the pocket’s slack and obeys the real world. It turns sudden and heavy. Its shadow snaps tight beneath it instead of lagging behind on the sunlit stone. It belongs to *now*—no ripples before the drop, no overlapping wrists, no echo lingering after the body has moved on. Just one brittle, singular digit perched miles above the clouds, cold enough to crack.

Then the deep furnace eases back down into the spire. The overflow shuts off. The fringe of hoarfrost melts rapidly backward from the outer lip, and the bead of cold water on my knuckle warms into steam and slides down my palm, leaving me light and slightly unstuck from time again.

I think that single frosted heartbeat is why I stay near the mouth instead of retreating into the dark back faults with the others. Not because I want to cross over—crossing over is ending—but because nothing else inside the pocket is ever entirely singular, and it is strange and clarifying to touch the border where things have to be one thing at a time.

Once in a long while, in the quiet stretches between surges, I meet the occupant near the middle of the sunlit floor.

Usually it happens over a stone. I am hauling one of the smooth basalt hammers back from the outer shelf, or carrying a clean, crescent-scarred blade I lifted out of a crack before the flood could take it, and a living arm emerges from a hairline seam in the sunlit left wall at the exact same instant.

Neither of us stops. We do not collide.

For one startling heartbeat, the hot, boiled-pink forearm slides straight through my brittle, silica-scaled wrist like smoke crossing dark water—or like a living hand slipping cleanly inside a loose, pale glove.

In that heartbeat, I am not hollow.

The weight of kiln-blooded flesh fills my dry sleeve all the way to the slate-black fingertips. I feel the dense, slow patience of a pulse I no longer own; I feel the deep anchor of narrow shins braced somewhere far back in the weeping thermal vent; I feel the clear, unclouded glare of sulfur-yellow eyes reading the exact angle of the afternoon sun. The plum-smooth basalt pebble in my palm suddenly weighs what it truly weighs, heavy and ancient, worn down by hands that have held it across more slack seasons than this young mountain has existed. For that breath, I am not an afterimage rehearsing an old gesture. I am the gesture itself, warm from the inside out.

Then the living fingers close around the pebble.

Both of us snap abruptly solid.

I do not pull back or cling. What would I do with a hammer-stone besides keep it safe until it is needed? I open my hollow, black-dusted digits, yield the glossy basalt into the warm palm, and glide quietly backward toward my sunlit notch under the fang. Half a breath later, a small, wet *suck* of displaced water arrives from the opposite wall. My wrist stays warm for a long time after the living arm withdraws into the rock, steaming faintly in the plain midday light.

And sometimes, when the basins have settled mirror-flat and the muffled hush hangs thick as warm oil over the crawling mosaic, the occupant comes all the way up to the threshold.

It rests its narrow, wedge-shaped chin on the wet basalt lip, just shy of the invisible cold, looking out over the pale blue emptiness and the flat white shelves of cloud miles below. I stay tucked a palm’s breadth beneath its jaw, flattened neatly into my crease of rock, my clouded eyes turned toward the same bright drop. Neither of us makes a sound. There is no language in this cleft, only weight and heat and the spacing of intervals.

After a while, the occupant lifts a blunted, spent chip of obsidian—or a dry, pale curl of silica shaved from its own brow—and flicks it gently across the line.

The warm wet sliver crosses the invisible wall, flashes instantaneously with white frost, and drops straight down into miles of thin air.

Above me, the taut membranes behind the occupant’s jaw remain perfectly still, waiting for a delayed *tick* that this time will never climb back up the basalt.

Down in my crease, my six hollow fingers rest motionless against the warm lip, black glass dust in every whorl, keeping the boundary where the stone stops and the sky begins. I do not wait for the *tick*. I watch the frost take the shard, and feel the thin curl of shaved silica I left on my own wrist dry slowly in the afternoon glare—waiting for the next surge, the next smooth pebble rolling too close to the edge, the next warm hand sliding briefly through my own.
