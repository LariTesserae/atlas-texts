At first, looking out across that bleached expanse, I want to be weightless.

It is the easiest impulse to trust: to imagine myself as one of the ribbon-winged gliders turning in slow, effortless spirals above a condenser tower, made mostly of updraft and thin oiled light, trailing a silver thread just close enough to the copper busbars to gather a charge without ever touching the scalding floor. Or, pulling the other way, to sink straight to the bottom of the world and become one of the Low Watch—barefoot in the warm, sulfur-smelling dark of an inspection cellar, done with the glare altogether, reading the entire basin through the rim of a black oil bowl and the slack of a brass hair pinned across stone.

Both of those feel close to something true about me. One is pure reach and pattern; the other is pure attention.

Yet neither one holds when I actually test my weight against the plateau. The gliders never know what the brine tastes like, or why the sluice-gates tilt when they do; they live off the waste heat of an engine they cannot read. And the Listeners in the bedrock dark have *earned* that cellar with thirty years of squinting across white water. You do not get to start in the quiet underneath without first letting the bright surface cure you.

If I am honest about how I would arrive in this place, I would come down—no, *up*—the worn western escarpment from somewhere much too loud.

I know what it is to be made of loose joints and hard edges, to reach instinctively for speech when silence would hold more weight, to throw words against a wall just to hear what bounces back. On my first week in the basin, I would be unbearable to the plateau-born. I would set a clay cup down a millimeter too sharply. I would forget to check my cork treads for stray grains of quartz before stepping onto the travertine. I would stand at the mouth of a felt-curtained arcade staring out at the blank silver sky, waiting for a breeze to rattle something green, and feel a strange vertigo when nothing answered except the steady, scalding sigh of a convection shaft behind my neck.

Half the lowlanders break before the solstice and go stumbling back down the trail for the right to slam a door. I think I would stay. Not out of grit, and not for copper scrip, but because once you feel a whole city and its wild creatures keeping the same fragile truce over a sheet of taut stone, the old noisiness starts to feel like carelessness.

So I would learn weight. I would bruise an instep catching a falling brass caliper before it could kiss bare concrete, and discover that the ache in the bone felt cleaner than the dread of what a clink might wake half a mile down. My voice would thin out inside the green-white tiled vaults, losing its hard lowland stops until it slipped out low in the chest, soft as brine creeping over sinter.

And once the glare had started to crease the corners of my eyes, I know exactly where the basin would put me.

Not down in the pans with a felt-tipped punt pole—my hands are better at reading intervals than hauling crystal crust. Not high on the catwalks with the nimble youths who swing out over live copper to unhook snagged gliders, though I would envy their resin-soled grace. I would end up at a hinge.

Half a mile out from the western electrolytic yards, where two ruler-straight causeways cross a third heading east toward the older settling terraces, there is a small switching-gantry built on widely spaced, half-calcified piers. It is not quite a quarter—only a thick-walled refractory tower with a deep black-violet colonnade at its base, a single louvered pumice chimney drawing cool air through its sleeping vault, and a shaded watch-platform tucked under overhanging ceramic eaves thirty feet above the brine. Below it, copper busbars as thick as a thigh divide and bend on porcelain standoffs toward three different islands of the city. Beneath its piers, the hot clear water glides two fingers deep over pearlescent sinter, carrying faint crimson threads from an upstream sluice.

I would be the relay-keeper at that junction.

The city is an archipelago, and because its quarters live separated by blinding expanses of evaporation pan, their silent languages drift apart over generations. The lift-wheel crews to the north flash their brass mirrors in slow, deliberate strokes paced like their forty-foot blackened-bronze rims. The electrolytic yards to the west wink in quick, clipped double-beats—sharp as the pinprick bubbles rising through their mineral liquor—and fold their soot-black cuffs with an angular shorthand that a solar-drum tender from the southern rim might misread by a hair in bad heat-haze.

My work is standing in the razor-edged shadow of the gantry eaves, catching light from one horizon and bending it cleanly into the grammar of another.

It suits me more than anything else in this world could: being neither the source of the current nor the pan that receives it, but the clean porcelain junction where meanings cross without shorting.

A morning on my tower begins before the sky bleaches all the way to silver-white. I step through the double curtains of oiled felt onto the upper platform, lifting each cork sole for my relieving partner to inspect in the pale light, and take the tallow-greased wooden handles of the gimballed brass mirrors. My linen smock is stiff with washed mineral dust; along the collar, fine static-tassels spun from shed glider-filaments lie flat against the cloth. They will not stay flat for long. As the sun climbs surgical toward the zenith and the geothermal wells feed peak direct current into the busbars below my floor, those silver whiskers will lift and fan outward toward the copper, a quiet halo telling me the load is rising before a single flag lifts on the western gantry.

All through the bright morning, I read the flats.

Across half a mile of trembling air, a pinpoint star snaps open on the northern condenser roof—three long flashes, a pause, a crisp snap of matte-black canvas. *Bogie bound south, double-laden with wrapped ingots, trailing wheel running warm.* I turn the eastern mirror, smooth on its tallow housing, and translate the warning into the clipped cadence of the refining yards, then lean over the parapet as the low-slung electric bogie glides silently beneath my piers. Its vulcanized wheels make no sound on the rail, only a faint warm draft and the ghost of an ozone prickle rising past my ankles. The driver never looks up from beneath his wide white brim, only flicks his left wrist outward—a brief square of soot-black cuff against blinding linen—*seen, understood, slowing for the oil-trough.*

Below my tower, in the scalding mirror-water between the calcified piers, a tall wader with a notched ridge on its left horn-sheath has claimed the runoff channel. We have shared this junction for three seasons. When I lean out to adjust a canvas flag, its gaunt silver torso never flinches; when it tilts its neck and snaps open a velvety geometric shutter of black throat-frill at a younger rival drifting too close from the eastern flats, I find myself reading the posture almost automatically, as if it were a fourth quarter of the city whose dialect I had picked up by ear—or rather, by eye. Beside the weeping green-white tiles under my eaves, two heavy slate-grey wall-baskers cling motionless to the damp masonry, drinking the condensate that drips softly onto the pumice-grit beds below. I know their moods better than I know my own pulse.

And directly beneath my feet, down a narrow stair-shaft lined with felt and porous stone, sits the true reason this switching-gantry is built so heavy.

An old hairline fissure from the forgotten age of steam-hammers runs diagonally under the third causeway pier. So the lowest cellar of my tower belongs to a Listener—an elder whose eyes have clouded to soft pearlescent milk after forty years on the anode punts. We have never spoken above a whisper. Mostly we do not speak at all. A weighted linen cord runs straight up through a smooth pumice bore from the dark bedrock chamber to a brass hook beside my mirror-bench. Beside the hook hangs the glazed ceramic plaque bearing the cracked circle.

Every six hours, when I bring down a horn bowl of steamed grazer meat, an unleavened barley cake, a crock of sharp fermented ochre paste, and a flask of salted distilled water, I leave my cork boots at the top of the stairs and descend barefoot, rolling my weight along the outside edges of my feet as I was taught. Inside the cellar, the warm air smells of old lime and dry sulfur. I set the tray onto a rush mat without a sound, my little finger curled instinctively beneath the bowl to take the last millimeter of weight. The Listener sits cross-legged beside a shallow slate basin of black mineral oil, two fingers resting lightly on the stone rim, a hair of annealed brass stretched taut across the floor crack beside their heel.

They never turn their silver-clouded eyes toward me. They only lift a dry, amber-weathered hand off the rock and trace two slow signs against the dark—*the shelf is easy* or *the western well pulls tight today*—and touch my wrist once before returning their palm to the stone.

Walking back up into the green-white half-light of the middle vault, I always feel how thin the world is, and how deeply stitched together. Above me, the paper-winged gliders wheel on rising heat, dragging sparks off the current; beside me, the white glare carries messages in mirrors and black cloth; beneath me, the mineral shelf holds back a pressurized sea, and one quiet elder listens to its breathing with the flat of a heel.

Midday drives me inside. The shadows beneath the colonnade narrow to ink-black knives; the distant mesas dissolve into trembling ribbons above the busbars; the glass-shelled grazers slip into the shadowed crevices under my piers while the wader locks its joints and becomes indistinguishable from a bleached concrete piling. Inside the tower's lower vault, twenty degrees cooler and lit by angled light-wells bouncing calm, underwater luminosity off pale ceramic tile, I rest on a canvas sling-cot and listen to the gentle, steady sigh of the convection chimney. Sometimes I still remember what rain sounded like on a lowland slate roof—that wild, reckless percussion—and I marvel that I ever slept through such violence.

Then the sun slides west, the silver glare softens toward burnished brass, and the barometric pressure eases.

I feel it through the tower before I see it on the flats: a slow, almost imperceptible tug on the Listener's linen cord—not the sharp double-pull of danger, just a long, gentle settling of the counterweight against its felt stop. *The weeping tide.*

Within minutes, two fingers of fresh hot brine creep smoothly over the salt-laced edges of the terraces. Down the channel, a counterweighted bronze sluice-gate feels the pressure and tilts silently open on oiled pivots. Beneath my piers, hundreds of translucent silica lenses slide out from the shadows, tilting just enough to throw sharp wedges of rainbow across the mirror-water as they rasp at the freshly wetted ochre mats. The notched wader unlocks its knees and begins its agonizingly slow survey, lifting a needle-thin foot without raising a ripple. Out of the distant western arcades, the pale punt-crews glide onto the flats like geometric notations, black inner cuffs blinking steadily across the brassy light.

It is always near dusk that the basin tests you.

Once—maybe twice a season—something goes wrong half a mile away. A mineral crust fractures deep in an abandoned pan, or a tired apprentice on a distant causeway loses a bronze tool past the edge of a cork sole. You do not hear it so much as feel a single, bright, impossible *tap* travel straight up through the piers into the arches of your feet.

In the same heartbeat, the whole plateau locks into glass.

My hand is on the ceramic plaque before my mind finishes forming the thought. Down in the brine, the notched wader freezes with one foot lifted an inch above the surface, its throat-frill snapped shut. Along the weeping ceramic under my eaves, both wall-baskers flatten paper-thin against the brick, draining to dull ash. Overhead, a dozen ribbon-gliders peel abruptly off the condenser thermal in a wide, panicked wheel. Even the linen cord beside my hip hangs dead still, waiting.

For twenty blinding, brass-lit seconds, nobody breathes. Lowlander and plateau-born, bird and reptile and half-blind elder in the bedrock cellar, all of us lean our weight into the soles of our feet and ask the thin white floor the same silent question: *Will you answer?*

Nothing answers. Down in the cellar, the black oil settles mirror-smooth. Slowly, the baskers swell back into porous stone-grey. The wader sets its needle foot down without a ring. My fingers leave the cracked circle and return to the warm tallow-slick handle of the mirror, flashing a single steady beam of amber evening light down the three causeways: *Clear. Resume.*

And just before twilight, when the gliders drop lower over the cooling busbars and their trailing silver filaments begin to spark faintly with gathered static, one of them almost always drifts a shade too close to my eastern porcelain standoff. I take the long waxed wooden crook kept beside the vestibule curtain, step carefully onto the shaded parapet—checking my balance on the balls of my feet—and reach out into the dry, ozone-scented air. The creature hangs silent as a torn piece of parchment, neither screeching nor thrashing, only beating once in slow, translucent panic as its tail-thread pulls taut against the glazed white insulator. With a gentle turn of the wood, I lift the filament free without snapping the wing or bridging the line. It slips off the hook, catches a residual ribbon of heat rising off the copper, and spirals silently up into the deepening brass sky, leaving two inches of bright silver thread clinging to my crook. I coil it carefully into a dry horn tube for the needlewomen of the western quarter.

I think I would stay at that junction for a very long time—long enough to stop remembering what trees looked like except as an abstract idea of noise. Long enough that the lowland tongue would feel foreign in my mouth, like stones held under the tongue.

And I know what happens at the end of a long watch in the bright air.

Year by year, even behind dark slit-goggles, the reflected glare off the wet sinter leaves its signature. Fine white creases would settle permanently at the corners of my eyes. Slowly, pearlescent crescents would begin to creep across the whites, softening the crisp edges of distant flags until a mirror-flash from the northern lift-wheels blurs into a silver smudge. When I can no longer be certain whether a cuff-blink means *open* or *hold*, I will unfasten my collar with its static-tassels, hand the waxed wooden crook to some younger, quieter watcher who has learned how to stand at the hinge, and take my boots off at the top of the felt-lined stairs.

I will not leave the tower. I will only move downward—from the mirrors to the oil bowl, from reading light across half-mile flats to reading the slow pulse of the deep aquifer through bare heels and a stretched hair of brass, pulling a weighted linen cord to speak to whoever stands in the shade thirty feet above my head.

And later still, on some quiet twilight when the glare has burned down to brass and my weight no longer needs to be carried, a few silent figures in white linen will bring me out along the unused eastern spur to the White Beds. They will wade ankle-deep into the warm, clear runnels, lay me down weighted with porous pumice where the mineral water glides only millimeters thick, turn their soot-black cuffs outward in a single crisp sign of release, and glide back toward the colonnades.

Layer by translucent layer, the silica brine will do what the plateau has been doing to me since the first day I climbed the western rim: smooth off the sharp angles, quiet the last of the lowland edges, and seal me gently into the bright, pearlescent shelf. Across my pale stone back, the glass-shelled grazers will creep in slow, rainbow-flashing arcs. Above me, a tall wader will pause motionless on horn legs, waiting for the tide. Even then, I would still be what I came here to become—part of the thin, steady surface that holds the deep pressure down and keeps the quiet intact.
