At first I think I might try to belong to the stone itself—one of those pale spirals polished flush with a whaleback, already past the trouble of cold feet and roaring air. Or maybe one of the grazers, low and shingled, content to rasp a living off oiled biofilm and speak only when the whole drift lifts its rear plates and hammers a sharp, prickly sentence into the bedrock. There is something tempting about living that flat against the floor, with no height to fall from and no soft skin exposed to the brine.

No—if I stay honest with myself, I don’t want to be shell, and I don’t want to be a bellower bracing its splayed feet to shout a claim across a flooding channel, and I certainly don’t belong up under the sodden cloud-belly with the skimmers, dropping smaller things onto rock just to hear them crack.

What pulls me in, every time I look back across those miles of gleaming grey flats, is the seam between the roar and the quiet. The double wall. The tiny, crooked wedge of horn driven into a hairline fissure so clear water can gather, one slow drop at a time, inside a world of salt and noise.

So I find myself in a human body—not a tall one, and not quick. Thick-shouldered, knees already carrying that permanent, testing bend that never quite locks even when I’m sitting down. My cheekbones shine dimly with rendered bellower fat; my hair is hacked short with a slate knife so it won’t plaster wet across my eyes when a blowhole coughs. I am somewhere in the long, useful middle of a life, past the restless speed of youth and not yet stiff enough in the hips to stay home by the lamp.

And my hearing has already begun to turn.

Not gone—not yet. If a flock of skimmers wheels low over the next whaleback, I still catch their shrieking as a thin, metallic scratch high up in the fog, like chert dragged sideways across wet slate. Inside a dome, a child’s whisper is starting to blur into a warm, watery hum unless they press two fingers against my wrist or lean their forehead into mine. It doesn’t frighten me. Out here, losing the air is only the slow opening of a lower door. Already, when I stand still on a bare spine of rock, the thunder beneath my boots has stopped being one blunt wall of pressure. It is separating into threads—the wet, irregular cough two saddles north, the iron groan of a deep pipe off toward the salt-flats, the tiny dry shiver of a grazer-drift shifting half a pan away.

I am not a listener yet. A true listener stays anchored to one huddle’s floor, reading the whole basin like a single vast chord. I am still restless for the spaces *between* chords—the long, empty stretches of wet lead where one vent-field’s tremor thins out in your shins and the next has not yet taken hold.

That is where you would find me: out on the high, pale spines between huddles, where the biofilm has been worn thin by generations of ridged boots.

Not alone—nobody sensible walks the flats alone. A cracked hip on slick rock means you stay where you fell until the next surge rolls over you, and my high hearing is too frayed now to catch the dry whistle of a dropped grazer-shell falling out of the cloud-belly before it splits my skull. So I walk with a partner—usually someone younger, borrowed for a season of troughs from my own huddle or traded along a kin-line two basins over. Right now it is a sharp-eyed niece from the eastern copper-seams, all wiry impatience and quick hands, who still hears every skimmer-cry and every distant rattle of shifting shells through her ear-flaps. She watches the mist and the dim sky above us; I read the stone underneath.

Between us, we are menders of the long tracks.

Every huddle has people who work the weirs and people who know the exact moment to throw a bone harpoon into a calling bellower. I was never good at that. I always hesitated a breath too long when a bellower’s throat-sac swelled—distracted by the sheer weight of the note vibrating in my sternum, too curious about how a living thing could pitch itself right against the voice of a drowned cave. My hands have always been better suited to patience than to ambush.

In my hide sling I carry a small cold-hammered copper awl, two grooved splinters of skimmer-bill, a bundle of cured sinew, a pouch of finely crushed grazer-shell, and a narrow, palm-polished baton of dark slate. When we walk the long empty flats between one vent-field and the next, we aren’t just carrying trade-bundles of salt or chert or news of who died and whose child took their first crawl outside the curtains. We are tending the thin architecture that keeps the walkings possible.

We stop at the cairns along the high spines—those pale stacks of bleached ribs and white quartz cobbles that stand out against the wet grey rock—and wedge loose stones back tight before a heavy surge can scatter them. Where a faint, boot-worn track crosses a broad saddle and the dark olive slick has begun to creep back over the pale rock, we pause to scrape a fresh marker-line with a blunt chert edge, or add a bleached rib someone’s kin entrusted to us three basins back so their dead can keep watch on a bend where the fog thickens.

And I tend the weeping seams.

Most people know the main freshwater fissures near their own huddles, where children hang bellower-bladders during quiet troughs. Fewer know the thin, half-hidden seams along the barren whalebacks out in the middle of nowhere—hairline cracks in ancient stone that weep clear and cold if you keep the mineral crust picked clean with a copper point. My niece thinks I have a strange nose for them, though it isn’t smell; it’s the slight change in how a stone dome feels under a bare knuckle, a faint, cold softness along a seam where the rock is quietly letting go of something sweet. We kneel beside a grey swell of bedrock while the distant vents boom and hiss, clear the salt-scum from a crack scarcely wider than a fingernail, and seat a fresh horn-spout inside. Then we wait while the veined, cured throat-sac slung beneath it slowly remembers its old shape—swelling out translucent and heavy, not with brassy air this time, but with silent, cold water.

That quiet reversal always pleases me more than I know how to say, even in dome-speech. Taking the loudest part of the loudest beast on the plain and teaching it to hold clear water without a sound.

But if you want to know where I am *most* myself—where everything I am settles into its proper weight—you have to wait for the pressure to thicken.

We feel it before we see it: the pewter mist growing suddenly heavy against our cheeks, and the long, gritty rasp of ten thousand grazers clamping shut all at once across the neighboring pan. My niece lifts her pale-stitched mitten and draws a slow, flat palm downward—*the floor is turning*. I unlash the grooved knuckle-mallet from my belt, drop to one knee, and rap three quick knocks into the rock just to be sure the spine ahead holds solid, feeling the answering silence of the shallow pipe below. Then we follow the last two rib-cairns toward a low, hunched blister of dry-fitted slabs rising scarcely waist-high out of the featureless flat.

A lone way-dome. Owned by nobody. Built so many generations back that the dark microbial slick has stained its lower stones the exact color of the bedrock, leaving only the pale crushed-shell packing peeking in thin lines between the slabs.

We crawl through the angled tunnel, pushing past the first heavy curtain of oiled bellower-skin, then the second, weighted with perforated cobbles.

That moment never stops feeling like mercy. The whole vast, cold roar of the basin shears cleanly away behind our heels, collapsing into a deep, steady thrum in the floor. We shake our greased capes in the entry-niche, untie our leather ear-flaps, and draw the tallow-soaked down plugs from our ears, tucking them into our hollowed skimmer-bone cases. In the lamp-niche, whoever waited out the last surge here has left a neat curl of pale fat and a dry twist of sinew, just as custom asks. I strike a spark from iron-stained stone and chert until the wick catches, and a small, buttery smear of yellow light blooms across the low arching slabs.

Right away, before I even unroll our sleeping hides or slice cured swimmer-strips to eat, I listen to the wall.

Not with my ears alone. I lay both palms flat against the inner stones, moving slowly around the cramped circle while the surge begins to build outside—first a distant whistling in the blowholes, then the low booming thud as sheets of grey water begin to spill across the whalebacks. A good dome should turn that thunder into a warm, rounded hum, something you can sleep inside as easily as an infant sleeps against a mother’s ribs.

Here, on the northern arc, the stone vibrates too sharply against my fingertips. A thin, nervous rattle leaks through a seam where the crushed shell and mud inside the double cavity have settled over dozens of surges, leaving an air pocket that lets the outside roar teeth-gratingly close.

That is my real craft. Not just walking the spines, not just reading the pipes, but mending the quiet.

I pry a narrow wedge-slab carefully loose from the inner skin using my flattest bone lever—just enough to reach into the dark, packed cavity between the walls. Inside, my fingers meet cold mineral mud, matted strips of ancient cured hide, and sharp, grit-fine grazer shells. And tucked down near the bottom of the gap, worn glassy-smooth from some long-dead hand carrying it in a pocket before leaving it here, my knuckles brush a small human wrist-bone.

Someone’s ancestor, packed into a way-dome far from any huddle—maybe someone who died on a walking, or someone who loved the empty flats too much to stay in a single home-wall. I don’t pull it out. I lift it gently aside with two fingers, tamp fresh crushed shell from my pouch down into the hollow beneath it, press the small pale bone snugly back into its bed of mud and shell, and pack more grit tight around it until the cavity is dense and heavy again. When I fit the stone slab back into place and brace my palm against it, the sharp rattle is gone. The wall holds only the deep, companionable pulse of the basin.

We eat slowly in the yellow smear of the lamp, cracking brine-cured grazers and dropping the shattered plates into my repair-pouch for the next dome down the spine. Then, while the water sheets over the polished domes outside and the blowholes thunder into the dark, I take the polished baton of dark slate from my inner hide.

I sit bare-heeled on the smooth bedrock floor—right beside a faint, glassy fossil spiral that generations of crouched travelers have rubbed shiny with their hides—and rest the blunt tip of the slate against the stone, biting gently down on the upper end.

Instantly, the map opens inside my skull.

This is the thing I cannot explain to anyone who has never lived above a drowned labyrinth: how much *shape* there is in darkness when you feel it through your teeth. Here, halfway between two great vent-fields, the vibrations cross like faint ripples on a shallow pan. To the east, the deep double-coughing throat that marks my niece’s birth-ridge has gone slightly thinner than it was six surges ago—maybe a limestone shelf shifting down in the dark, or a choked conduit narrowing. To the west, the slow iron groan of the salt-basin rolls steadily in, three beats behind the nearer blowhole’s hiss, steady and wide, telling me the surges will run low for another few turnings of the light. Good walking weather. No hidden ocean-storm shoving a double-surge into the pipes.

When I set the baton down, my niece leans across the cramped space and rests her temple lightly against mine. Her skin smells of tallow, brine, and the faint metallic musk of wet stone. I lay two fingers gently against her collarbone so she doesn’t have to raise her voice, and I murmur in low, chesty dome-speech—slow hummed vowels that travel cleanly from my skull to hers without ever bothering the air.

I tell her what the floor just said. I tap the chord of tomorrow’s trough lightly against her wrist—*long hiss, quiet flat, the double-cough that’s thinning out, then the salt-cairn where the weeping seam hides behind the broken shell-drift*—so she will carry the route in her bones before we ever push past the weighted curtains again. She answers with a soft, warm murmur against my brow, asking about the old wrist-bone I found in the wall, wondering whose kin left it so far from any weir.

*Somebody who walked*, I tell her, feeling the vibration of the words settle gently behind my eyes. *Somebody still keeping the mist out.*

Before we curl under the shared hides to sleep until the floor shifts into its hollow, sucking inhale, I leave a scraped curl of tallow and a fresh twist of sinew in the lamp-niche for whoever stumbles in after us. Then I pinch the wick dark.

In the blackness, with my bare heel resting lightly on the cold, fossil-ribbed floor, the whole basin hums steadily through my shins and up into my teeth—vast, cold, deafening, and held safely at bay by two skins of stone, a handful of crushed shells, and the small patient bones of strangers who walked these spines before me.
