I would not be standing on the rim, looking down.

If I started up there on the flats, with ordinary distance at my back and scuffed leather on my feet, I would be too heavy for the lanes below. I would round a damp corner too quickly and set the black water shivering in its channels; I would bring a whole stubborn afternoon of plain time dragging behind my heels like wet clay, and everyone down among the conduits would step into their alcoves until I left.

No—when I try to find myself in this place, I am already below the lip of shale. I am three tiers down, or perhaps one, depending on which stairwell I took after noon, standing motionless on a narrow concrete landing while a cross-street finishes aligning itself with the mouth of my alley.

I know I belong to the hollow before I even look at my hands, because my feet already have the manners for it. I do not test the wet paving ahead of me with a blunt stride. I wait half a beat, feeling the faint, dry tremor settle out of the masonry; I hold clearly in my posture which of the two interpenetrating walls I am currently inside, and pass cleanly through the four-foot overlap where a pale concrete housing block occupies the same cold air as a dark ceramic stair-tower. Only then do I glance down at my cuffs—dark, matte wool, folded sharp as drafting paper—and my bare knuckles, unrubbed, dry, without a single healed split from past winters.

I have no memory of climbing down the embankment. Like the lintels and the blind service towers, I simply precipitated into the middle of my habits, already knowing how to bleed a whistling flange, how to step around a vertical-null stair, and how to wait out the lag in a narrow courtyard after someone speaks.

***

My quarters sit in the middle tiers—neither up near the pale, exposed eaves where the outer sky presses too close, nor down on the raw bedrock floor where the city is still knitting itself out of gravel. It is a wedge-shaped room tucked into the quiet angle of an older, first-emerged block, where two floorplans never quite finished negotiating ownership of the corner.

The floorboards tilt five degrees toward the north wall—not steep enough to trouble a chair once you shim the short legs, just enough that the condensate running down the seamless dark ceramic collects cleanly in a narrow zinc gutter along the skirting instead of pooling under my cot. Six feet up that ceramic wall, a strip of pale composite shelving protrudes eight inches into my room without brackets or seam, belonging to whoever occupies the intersecting chamber on the other side.

I have never seen my neighbor’s face. Our doorways open onto lanes that are seven cross-streets and a sunken concourse apart, and in the basin you do not go hunting for someone’s door when a shared wall already holds you close enough. When the pressure in the silver trunk line drops after a deep *click* underground, I can sometimes hear the dry, unhurried scrape of their tin kettle three feet away through the masonry. Now and then I tap twice on the cold pilaster with a gloved knuckle—just to acknowledge that we are both home—and half a minute later two faint taps answer from somewhere near the ceiling, where the sound chose to arrive.

We use the protruding strip of shelf for small, silent trades. I leave a pinch of papery bluish lichen scraped from a metric conduit, or a neatly folded square of oiled grey cloth. Two days later I find a clean triangular shim of slate set beside my kettle, or a paper twist of crushed woodlice-molts, white and cold as powdered glass, ready to be mixed with condensate for patching a weeping seam.

Beside my door hangs a canvas satchel. In it I keep the modest tools of the work I seem to have arrived with: a thick square of dark felt folded in four; a small brass plumb-bob on waxed twine—useless for finding the sky’s vertical in a tilted room, of course, though it shows you reliably which way the local floor thinks *down* is; thin slips of pale slate and a stub of soapstone; and a lidded tin pail that fits neatly over the dull grey iron heating block tapped into the lower conduit outside my stairwell.

In my left coat pocket, I also keep a single flat lump of raw shale picked up near the upper verge. It has no utility at all. I simply turn it over and over under my bare thumb while I walk, waiting to see if months of steady pressure will eventually rub one razor-sharp corner smooth.

***

If the watchers down on the bedrock floor keep track of the doorways that have arrived—dropping pebbles into dark new rooms and hanging stamped tin numerals beside the jambs—and the wrench-carriers keep the conduits from drifting out of agreement, then I belong among those who tend the sunken courts.

Not all the courts are mapped, because the basin is always folding new pockets between its blocks: chapel-narrow hollows no wider than a stairwell, dead-end light-wells behind ventilation housings, low landings where the northern daylight arrives only in a thin, surgical blade around midday. In those cold pockets, shallow black water collects on the plinths. And sooner or later, a puddle that has been blank for months stops reflecting the empty sky and begins to show a finished thing—a ribbed stone balustrade, a lintel with a rusted iron lantern, or a person sitting with their knees drawn up, hair stirred slightly by a wind that does not exist in the alley.

Someone has to walk the back-lanes slowly enough to notice when a blank surface has become occupied. Someone has to bring warm, bitter lichen-broth to the ones who cannot yet leave their margins, and sit with them while they finish arriving.

That is where I spend most of my days.

Approaching an occupied court takes a quiet kind of discipline. You learn to hear the difference between a blank puddle and a working one before you even turn the corner—not a sound, exactly, just a tighter stillness in the air, as though the cold masonry is holding its breath around a fragile calculation. I slow my boots three alleys back. I lower my chin into my collar so my breath-plume breaks against my coat instead of drifting out across the water. When I round the sharp concrete edge into the hollow, I do not look straight at the plinth.

Direct scrutiny is heavy here. Look squarely at a half-formed parapet or a shoulder still negotiating its own solidity, and the pale mist-scaffolding softens, losing its stiffness, forced to start its slow millimeter of work all over again.

Instead, I look slightly away—at the damp seam where the plinth meets the shale, or down into the black water itself.

And this is where my own tolerance makes itself useful.

Like everyone in the basin, I carry a small clearance left over from settling into the same footprint as the city. Mine is quiet enough that a passerby would never spot it in an alley. In brushed-alloy lintels and condensate-beaded window glass, I appear as crisp and solid as any other resident: dark coat buttoned high, pale unlined face, grey eyes matching the bleached linen sky.

Yet when I lean over one of the black reflection puddles in a sunken court, the water does not catch me at all.

I can crouch inches from the wet margin, steady my boots on the gravel, and look straight down into the dark skin of the water, and it shows only the sky, the overhanging conduits, and whatever finished figure is waiting beneath the surface. The space above the edge where my head and shoulders ought to hang remains wholly unoccupied in the mirror.

I do not treat it as an affliction, though in the long, quiet hours I sometimes wonder what it means—whether I stood up from a puddle that had already gone dry before the last edge closed, or whether I have simply finished arriving so completely that the water has nothing left to negotiate with me. Whatever the reason, it leaves me unusually well-suited to the courts. I can sit right beside an unfinished neighbor on the cold composite lip, close enough to steady them, without my own sharp-edged shape ever intruding on the water to confuse what they are trying to match.

***

This afternoon I am in a narrow court two lanes east of the embedded birch—a damp well boxed in by blind ceramic walls, where a low plinth has been occupied for six weeks.

In the black water below, a narrow-shouldered man in a heavy oiled apron sits leaning forward with his forearms on his knees. His sleeves are rolled unevenly to the wrist; the knuckles of his reflected hands are broad and faintly reddened, as if he has just come in from washing stone in cold water, and the left knee of his trousers carries a neat, faded patch sewn in paler thread.

Above the water, on the freezing plinth, he is solid up to the ribs—heavy boots beaded with fine dew, crisp dark trousers without a crease of wear, an apron stiff as sheet tin. Above the chest he is still mostly negotiation: his right shoulder and jaw are clean, cold skin, while his left arm and collar grade smoothly into a pale, taut lattice of condensed mist. His breath rises from a quiet point three inches to the left of his throat, curling slowly downward toward the dry shale.

I unfold my square of dark felt and set it on the plinth a hand’s width from his hip, taking care not to jar the stone. From my pail I pour a measure of pale, bitter lichen-broth into a tin mug, wedge a small triangle of slate beneath it so the liquid sits level with the sky, and slide it gently into reach of his solid right hand. He does not look at me—turning too quickly would disturb the alignment he has been holding since morning—but after a careful pause, his bare fingers close neatly around the tin.

I sit down on the felt, fold my left hand in my lap, and rest my gloved right hand lightly against the solid cloth of his sleeve.

Then I turn my gaze away—up toward a damp cornice three stories overhead, where a pale fox once ran silently along a lip too narrow for its paws—and hold completely still.

Continuity is a strange currency in the basin. You cannot pour it from a kettle or pipe it through matte-grey conduit, yet you can lend it out in small, steady amounts simply by refusing to hurry. I keep my breathing slow and shallow. I let the iron chill sink evenly into my coat until my knees feel almost as stationary as the composite beneath them. At the far edge of my vision—never stared at, only held gently in the corner of my eye—the mist-lattice along his collar tightens, stiffening with a faint, frost-bright crispness.

An hour passes. Somewhere two lanes over, a deep, dry *click* travels up through the shale into my boots, like a lock tumbler turning under clay. A few seconds later, a faint whiff of ozone and wet chalk drifts down the light-well from wherever a new doorway has just opened. Neither of us flinches. Another resident—one of the deep-floor watchers, or a neighbor carrying chalk—will test the jamb and hang a stamped tin numeral beside the lintel. Down here, our only work is to stay put.

When the light-well begins to darken from bleached linen to pale pewter, the man shifts a quarter-inch on the plinth, bringing his solid jaw into closer agreement with the face in the puddle below.

“The birch,” he says, very quietly. His voice does not issue from his lips yet, but from the cold air half a pace beside his knee, dry and carefully spaced.

I wait three heartbeats for the syllables to settle against the ceramic walls, listening for any lag, before answering at the same low pitch. “Still holding its leaves.”

Another long pause. The mist around his wrist looks a shade denser than it did at noon—almost opaque, nearly ready to decide on skin.

“Three stairs down,” he adds. “Soft this morning.”

“I’ll chalk it on my way back,” I tell him.

That is the whole of our conversation for the afternoon. It is plenty. By the time I lift my hand from his sleeve and stand up, taking care not to let my coat brush the wet margin, the reflection in the water seems steadier—and when he eventually walks out of this court, three weeks or two months from now, he will remember my face mostly from the edge of his vision, the way you remember a plumb-line hanging beside a wall you were trying to lay true.

***

On the walk back toward my room, I chalk a neat white cross on the damp paving beside the third stair—where a solitary pale hare paused ahead of me, nose twitching at the vertical-null patch, before slipping silently behind a ventilation housing and reappearing two stories higher without climbing.

Once in a long while, when the afternoon feels unusually thin, I do not turn straight into my own lane. Instead I follow the cramped, razor-edged stairs upward, through the stacked bridges and weeping seams, until I reach the highest landing just beneath the outer embankment.

I never step over the lip of dark shale. I do not think my coat would know what to do with a wind that actually blows, or ground that stays flat for ten miles without needing to be negotiated. I simply stand on the last poured step, resting one bare palm against the raw outer earth, looking out across the ordinary northern flats.

It is up here that I remember the one time an outsider came down into my tier—a stranger who stopped moving long enough, sitting motionless on a step with their breath slowed to match the lanes, that I did not have to slip behind a pilaster to let them pass.

I stood three paces off with my tin pail in my hands, studying them with that quiet, cold absorption none of us quite know how to name. I barely looked at their eyes. I looked at the softened creases across the toes of their boots, shaped by miles of ordinary mud that had dried and flaked away and been replaced by other mud. I looked at the dull shine worn into the wool along their forearm, the frayed cuff, the pale crescent of a childhood scar across their knuckle.

Every puddle in the basin shows us things like that—a mended pocket, a faded patch on a trouser knee, the slight warmth of a face that has endured weather not piped in from somewhere else. Yet when we finish arriving on the plinths above, we arrive brand new, crisp-edged, precipitated whole into the middle of our lives without the intervening years to blunt us. Even our age-lines are freshly chiseled.

Standing below the rim, I slip my left hand into my coat pocket and roll the flat piece of outer shale between my thumb and fingers. A faint, dry dust comes off on my skin. When I lift my forearm to check the dark wool of my sleeve—the exact strip of felt that has rested, afternoon after afternoon, against cold plinths and the solid shoulders of half-formed neighbors—I look closely in the plain northern light.

It is still sharp-creased. There is no real pilling yet, no loose thread, no true softening at the elbow. Perhaps the faint grey sheen along the seam is only dried condensate, or stone-flour brushed from a pilaster.

Still, I think it sits a fraction less stiffly than it did last winter.

I turn my back on the flats and descend again into the tight, damp silence of the rooftops, stepping cleanly through the seams where two walls agree to share the cold. Down in the middle tiers, my kettle is waiting beside the protruding shelf; two lanes over, a blank puddle in a chapel-wide court has begun to gather a thin dark reflection along its margin; and there is all the quiet time in the world left to sit beside it, hold still, and wear my way slowly into staying.
