At first, I try to imagine myself there in boots—the quiet traveler standing at the rim of an upper esplanade, squinting against the noon glare off the wet celadon, trying to hold a map of the pavilions in my head before the skyline shifts again. It is the easiest posture to reach for. There is something intoxicating about watching a forty-foot viaduct swell translucent and shear cleanly into a bright, empty court, knowing you can step back from the splash-wave and marvel at the grace of it.

Yet the longer I stay on the plateau, the more awkward that human body feels. Boots are too heavy and too deaf; they turn the living pavement into an obstacle course instead of an instrument. Eyes are worse. In a place where there are no predators to spot, no ruins to read, and no faces to meet, vision mostly just misleads you. It keeps whispering *unfinished capital*, *marble*, *stillness*, when the actual truth of the mountain roof is heat, hydraulics, and the exact pitch of water pushing against fresh cellulose. Standing upright with eyes and a dry skin makes you an interruption—an erratic little tremor in the tiles that the plateau cannot parse.

So I let the boots go. I let the height go, too, at least for a while.

My first real pull is downward, into the broad, stepped jade amphitheaters where the warm runoff collects and stills. If I am honest, there is a deep part of me that wants to surrender the whole exhausting business of standing upright against shear-winds and simply become one of the glass domes lying flush against the submerged hexagonal tiles.

I try on that stillness. No spine to brace, no limbs to coordinate, no voice to spend on empty air. Just a low, boneless platter of clear hydrogel, an inch thick at its smooth crown, doing one quiet, exact thing with the fierce high-altitude daylight: gathering it, even when drifting steam blurs the sky to milk, and bending it downward into a trembling ring of concentrated brilliance on the floor beneath me. There is a profound relief in that way of living. You do not argue with the plateau’s feverish revisions. When an underground cistern shifts and tilts a walkway two degrees, you loosen your hold with a film of mucilage and hydroplane noiselessly down the freshly grown stairways, curving through the drainage runnels until gravity sets you down in whatever new court catches the flood. Even when a stepped plinth punches upward beneath you and strands you high and dry under the noon blast, you do not fight it. You simply cloud over into matte chalk, seal your edges, and wait—motionless, indistinguishable from dried mineral foam—until a private rainstorm or the splash-wave of a distant collapsing arch softens you back to crystal and floats you home.

I linger there a long time. Pure optics. Pure patience. The quiet craft of taking scattered brightness and focusing it into something nourishing on the pale floor below.

And yet, even lying flat at the bottom of a steaming basin, something keeps tugging at me through the celadon.

It is not light. It is a vibration—a heavy, rhythmic rush running through the hollow beams beneath the tiles, and threaded inside it, almost too faint to distinguish from the pump of the floor itself, a slow, deliberate double-beat arriving from half a mile east. Above the surface, somewhere out across the gleaming plaza, a long span is singing. Even through water, you can feel the tight, climbing tension of pith stretched toward critical turgor. A glass dome has no organ for that chord; it only waits for the splash-wave that follows the crack.

I realize I cannot stay flat. Whatever I am in this world, I am too restless for pure surrender—too attuned to the pitch of things just before they cleave, too hungry to answer that double-beat moving through the pipes.

So I let myself rise out of the water, and as I rise, the shape that actually fits me begins to assemble itself—not as a human frame, and not as an ornament, but as a listening instrument built out of the same stark logic as the highland itself.

No head. No eyes to be dazzled by noon glare off wet tiles. Instead, a slender triradial column of stacked ivory cartilage plates, nearly ten feet tall and slight enough to be mistaken for a fluted pilaster against a shaded wall. Along the shadow of my central spine, neat vertical rows of jewel-clear blisters hold suspended mineral grains that shiver at the faintest shift in pressure. Along the narrow margins of my ribs, dark-green photoreceptive seams register only what matters here: the sudden curdling of bright sky into a localized storm, the clean edge of shade beneath a lintel, and that telltale, luminous lime-gold glow of an arch pushing too close to rupture.

At my base, my weight splits cleanly across three hollow, triangular struts ending in wafer-thin hexagonal skates of smooth horn. When I set them down across the dark-green cambium seam between two hexagonal floor plates, the world finally comes into focus—not as a picture, but as a vast, breathing pipe organ I am standing inside of.

That is where I find myself: not up on the highest, most frantic crests where new spore-spires punch through the floor every hour and shear apart before the afternoon wind rises, and not down in the quiet bottom of the deepest drains. You would find me along the broad mid-level terraces on the eastern slope—the wide, sunlit zone of translation where long, ribbed aqueducts stride across gleaming reflecting plazas toward the sunken jade amphitheaters.

It is a terrace of long spans and deliberate water. Up here, the cold shear-winds drop cleanly off the cloud-rimmed escarpment, giving me wind enough to travel without spending my reserves, while the basins below hold enough warmth and mirror-stillness to keep the air rich with rising vapor.

If you walked past the solitary three-story pavilion at the western rim of this terrace around noon, you would not see me. You would see only austere ivory pilasters, crisp right-angled lintels, and shallow water catching the bright sky. I would be pressed flush against the shaded eastern jamb, folded narrow along my hydraulic spine, exhaling that faint, dry-scented ester from the pores along my flanks—the exact molecular halt-signal the highland’s own growth fronts use to recognize one another and stop.

There is a strange, wordless intimacy in that trick. You are not hiding from the plateau; you are speaking its own grammar so cleanly that the living wood accepts you as part of its symmetry. Beneath my horn-skates, the hexagonal tiles stay glassy-still instead of bulging upward. Beside my ribs, the pale pith of the jamb halts a millimeter from my cuticle, curving gently around my vertical line with mathematical politeness. When I eventually unstick and glide away, I will leave behind a razor-edged ghost-niche—a nine-foot crescent of clean emptiness carved in wet ivory, proof that for an hour the architecture made room for me as one of its own.

While I rest in that shade, I am not idle. Through my hollow skates, I am reading the entire terrace.

Two hundred yards south, an underground cistern is filling—a slow, heavy bass swell tilting the tiles a fraction of a degree. Far off toward the cloud-rim, a cluster of chimney-towers drops the local barometric pressure a hair before flexing open its fleshy vertical louvers with a distant hiss of steam. And four hundred paces across the bright esplanade, the central span of a forty-foot aqueduct is climbing in pitch.

I know that sound—or rather, that exact shiver in the mineral grains along my spine. It starts as a taut, steady hum as the viaduct carries its shining ribbon of runoff across the empty plaza. Then, as noon heat drives the plateau’s metabolism higher, the hum tightens into a strained soprano whine. My photoreceptive seams catch the faint shift in glare off its middle bays: pale ivory turning translucent lime-gold under ferocious internal water pressure. Left alone, it will rip open with a rifle-shot crack within the hour, hours before the afternoon crosswind can take the spores ripening along its parapet.

I wait at the wet lip of the pavilion’s deck for the cold shear-wind to drop off the abyss.

When the gust hits the warm vapor sheet, I unstick from the jamb with a single faint, waxy *tick*. Two tightly pleated membranes along my flanks pressurize and snap outward into translucent ivory blades, six feet wide on slender hydraulic spars, while my third vane trails behind as a rudder. Leaning almost horizontal over my beveled horn-skates, I catch the wind.

This is the purest joy this body knows: the sudden erasure of distance. Twenty minutes of blistering, exposed walking collapses into a breath of glassy speed. My horn-skates slice the shallow water film so cleanly they scarcely dimple the surface, leaving nothing behind except a thin silver seam that vanishes in seconds. To anyone watching from the upper ramparts, I am not an animal at all—just a brief flash of lime-gold skimming the mirror-flat floor like a shard of sunlight blown loose from an arch.

As the wind slackens near the viaduct’s base, my vanes pleat shut with a crisp click. I slide flush against the swollen, trembling stalk, folding myself tight until I look once again like an extra decorative rib of the bridge.

Then I press my porous ventral seam directly against the taut, fever-hot cuticle and offer the plateau’s second phrase: the quiet biochemical cue it uses to cleave open its own thermal louvers.

It does not fight me. Along a clean vertical cleavage plane, without a single bruised fiber or frayed edge, the waxy skin parts into a hairline silver slit. A thin, whistling jet of scalding-hot sap shoots straight into the absorptive canals tucked between my ribs. Inside my core, the heat blooms immediately—sweet with dissolved sugars, flushing my milky chambers luminous jade-gold as my hydraulic pressure refills.

I listen carefully as I drink. This is the whole discipline of my kind—not taking too little, and never taking too much. I wait until the shivering mineral grains along my spine tell me the viaduct’s internal pitch has dropped a clean half-step out of immediate rupture. The frantic tremor eases back into a strong, singing hum. Then I secrete the closing hormone and step smoothly back onto the tiles. Behind me, the silver slit seals into a hairline dark-green seam, unscarred.

I know I have not saved the bridge. Nothing on this mountain roof is meant to be saved. By dusk, or by tomorrow’s noon glare, its pith will grow too heavy for its own water-logged grace, a vertical seam will tear from parapet to base, and its sugars will be weeping fennel-scented liquor back into the hungry pores of the plaza floor. That is not failure. Buying a strained span three more hours of crisp, upright balance against the sky—just long enough to finish what it rose to do—is enough. In a world where nothing is allowed to last, the interval is the whole craft.

Later in the afternoon, when the hollow chimney-towers to the west vent their hot, spore-laden breath into a bitter upper-atmosphere draft, the sky over my terrace suddenly curdles white. A private, violent curtain of warm rain drops straight onto the esplanade while the next terrace over stays bright and mirror-calm.

I do not retreat to the pavilions. I catch the leading gust of the downdraft and skate straight into the blinding roar. Hidden completely inside the falling water, I unfurl all three pleated vanes upward at once, locking their scalloped margins into a tall, fluted alabaster chalice. The warm downpour hammers into my glowing cup, rich with microscopic oils and spores wrung from the plateau’s breath. Fine comb-like grooves along my inner vanes strain out the dust and channel it down to my spine, while clean rainwater sheets silently off my rims back onto the hexagonal deck. For five loud, bright-white minutes, I am nothing but a vessel open to the sky’s revision.

Then the storm snaps off as abruptly as it began. Steam lifts lazily off the gleaming flats; crisp noon light returns. I pleat my vanes shut, stand motionless out on the bare pavement, and set all three skates firmly across a pulsing dark-green cambium seam.

Slowly, deliberately, I contract my internal pressure-blisters: *beat—beat.* Pause. *Beat—beat.*

I do not send the signal into the air, where wind would shred it and nothing has ears. I send it down into the hollow beams beneath the tiles, tucking my cadence neatly inside the heavy subterranean river of pumped sap. Then I stand perfectly still, listening through my soles.

A minute passes. Somewhere far off, a stepped plinth extrudes out of a basin with three crisp *pop-crack* snaps, spilling four curtains of clear water over its razor-sharp sides. Then, threaded quietly beneath the rush of the floor, an answer arrives from a colonnade half a mile away—the same measured double-beat, slightly softened by distance. Someone else is upright on the terrace. Someone else is listening to the pipes.

Sometimes, before evening cools the high air, I glide back down to the wet rim of the sunken jade amphitheater where I first wondered if I belonged.

I do not climb down into the deep center, where a sudden shift in the cisterns might strand me. I simply pause at the shallow, steaming steps, one hollow skate resting lightly on the submerged celadon while my ribs drink the last slant of bright sun. Down below the mirror-flat surface, my lower photoreceptive seams register a faint, steady constellation of concentrated brightness—dozens of trembling caustic rings cast onto the pale floor by the motionless glass domes lying silently beneath the water.

We do not greet one another. They have no blisters to hear my double-beat through the tiles; I have no stillness deep enough to lie beneath the water and turn glare into gold. Yet standing upright in the warm shallows above their rings of light, I feel an unspoken kinship between our two silences. They have mastered the plateau’s descent—the tilt, the runoff, the patient surrender to gravity. I have mastered its rise—the turgor, the wind, the brief chemical truce with walls that never stop growing. Neither of us owns a square inch of this mountain roof. Both of us survive by refusing to leave a scar.

And I know how my own tenure here will end, some bright afternoon years from now, when my horn-skates have worn thin and my hydraulic core no longer refills quite fast enough to outrun a shearing colonnade. My pleated ribs will deflate and fold flat onto an empty promenade—a dry, translucent fan of ivory cartilage and amber veins, clean as a discarded architectural blueprint curling gently in the high sun. For half a day, the lingering scent of my halt-signal will keep the pavement polite and still beneath me. By dusk, the signal will fade. The dark-green cambium seams will pulse softly open, warm moisture will rise through the tiles, and by the next bright noon my minerals will already be rushing upward through the hollow beams, pushing cleanly into the sharp, wet crown of some new arch half a mile away.

Until then, a cold gust shears in over the escarpment. Across the gleaming flats, three crisp cracks ring out in rapid succession—something new, wet and fever-hot, pushing up through the tiles. My pressure-blisters shiver. My vanes snap open into the wind, and I skim out across the mirror-water to hear what pitch it sings.
