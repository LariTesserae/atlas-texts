At first, dropping down the heel-worn trough and stepping out of that scouring highland wind, I think I would try to stay near the mouth. Anyone coming in from open space has a reflex for the light—even a sideways, dust-thickened bar of it—and there is something magnetic about the rim-dwellers up on the fifth and sixth tiers. Watching them wring the dawn mist from the hair-nets while the bats and swifts collide in a five-minute storm around their heads, watching the wind-goers oil their skins and climb back out into the freezing glare with nothing but flint-memory and a resin-stoppered gourd—part of me would want to believe I had that kind of nerve.

I wouldn't. I know my own eyes too well, and I know how fast the open sky drains me. Half an hour under that thin, bald glare on the gneiss and I would be squinting myself blind, longing for a low ceiling and a wall at my back.

So I would let my feet keep moving inward, turning sideways down the narrow alleys, brushing past the hanging curtains of thorny caper-vine and papery silver air-plants until the direct light breaks up into warm umber and the roar of the ceiling softens by a fraction into a steady, vibrating hum.

My first real halt would be all the way at the bottom, in the cool dark near the cisterns. Something in me recognizes the cistern-keepers instantly—the way they sit among the round-bellied jars beneath the trembling clusters of sleeping bats, reading the town's survival not by sight but by pitch, tapping a knuckle against clay and hearing down to the cupful what remains. I could love that stillness. I could love the coolness of the rear stone and the quiet weight of knowing what holds.

And yet I don't think I would stay completely still at the floor, either. I am too restless in the hands for pure vigil, and too curious about how the whole stacked, leaning hive stays upright.

Where I actually come to rest—where my shoulders finally fold neatly in toward my ribs and stop bracing against the crowd—is halfway up and all the way back.

The inner edge of the third tier, pressed straight against the rear wall of ancient gneiss, right where the great rock-fig shoulders out of its diagonal fault.

From that ledge, the light never arrives directly; it comes secondhand through waxy olive leaves and the drifting fluff of air-plants, turning everything the color of toasted seed and warm clay. The trunk of the fig—plated in silver-grey bark, thick as two waists—runs right past my sleeping-platform before its upper boughs arch out over the central court like living rafters. When I sit with my spine against it, I don't just feel the tree. Through its hidden roots threading behind four tiers of dried mud, I can feel the whole pocket breathing: the steady *shuck-shuck* of bedrock pestles from the lowest ledges, the quick barefoot pattering of children chasing a hen along a fourth-tier parapet, the faint hollow thump when a heavy water-jar is set down in the dark below.

It takes me a moment, sitting there with my knees tucked under my chin, to see what is already in my hands.

A fist-sized lump of fallen swift-nest—fine, pale, saliva-bound clay—crumbled into a wooden bowl with highland silt and a pinch of dry bat-guano. Beside my knee, a tiny shard of pottery holding dark plant resin over a single glowing dung-coal, giving off a sharp, bitter-sweet tang that cuts straight through the musk of bodies and dry chaff. Tucked behind my ear, a slim bone scraper ground smooth on the gneiss, its shaft yellowed by my sweat and the sweat of the person whose arm it once belonged to.

That is who I am here. Not a wind-goer, not quite a cistern-keeper, not one of the rim-watchers who live in the bright roar.

I am a mender of seams.

In a town of three hundred people built out of hand-packed mud inside a single pocket of stone, nothing is ever finished and nothing is ever thrown away. The walls lean on each other; the roofs are somebody else's floor; the roots of the rock-fig swell a hair's breadth after a thick week of dawn mist and push a mortar joint out of true; the cliff-rodents behind the plaster widen a gallery until a sleeping-loft begins to sigh under a sleeper's weight; the clay cisterns in the cool rear crevices slowly remember the fire that baked them and develop fissures too fine for an eye to find in tallow-light.

Somebody has to listen for what is about to give way.

I would be small and dry-built like the rest of them, sinewy in the forearms from kneading clay and scraping bone, with a knee that stiffens in the cold draft if I climb too high toward the mouth—enough of a hitch to keep me off the plateau forever, not enough to keep me off the greasy juniper ladders between the first tier and the fourth. My skin would smell permanently of thyme-dust, crushed resin, and the chalky mineral grit of fallen bird-nests. My hair would be cropped short and uneven, tufted slightly at the crown and shorn close at the nape, because long strands catch in hot resin and because most of what my scalp has grown over the years already hangs knotted high up in the dawn-nets at the rim—catching mist in the wind I have no stomach to stand in, doing my share of the bright work while I stay tucked in the umber.

What defines my days, more than clay or resin, is the training of my ear.

Everyone in the pocket learns *the blind ear* and *the smooth eye* before they are tall enough to reach a drying-rafter. You do not hear your neighbor weeping through four inches of silt; you do not see the bruise on a man's shoulder when you step past him sideways in the alley. For me, that discipline is the whole spine of my trade. People let me crawl behind their woven wall-mats. They let me kneel beside their grain-baskets and lift the reed mats off their sleeping-platforms to inspect the floor-seams. I spend my life three inches away from other people's private heat, their hushed arguments, their hidden caches of dried figs, their quiet grief pressed into a cloth so not a drop is wasted on the dirt.

I let every human sound slide straight off me like dry dust off oiled skin. I don't hold it. I don't repeat it. I don't even let my eyes linger on it long enough to give it a shape.

Instead, I listen *under* the people, and *under* the five thousand wings overhead.

When I press my flat palm and my cheekbone against a mud wall on the second landing, the roar of the pocket narrows into a map of tensions. I can hear the difference between a healthy, solid pack of silt and a section where the plaster has pulled a fingernail's width away from the gneiss—a dry, drum-like hollowness when I tap it with a bent knuckle. I can hear the faint, sandy trickle inside a wall where a cliff-rodent colony has chewed too close to a juniper load-beam. I can even tell, by the soft, dry rustle beneath the floor-chaff, whether the beetles down in their ash-ringed pits are working steadily or whether a floor-seam has cracked wide enough to let damp night-jar spills seep where they shouldn't.

Even with the rodents, mending here is a negotiation, never a war. When a woman on the fourth tier signals me with two crisp tongue-clicks and a downward flick of her chin toward her sagging floor-edge, I climb up with my clay-bowl and thorn-twigs and crawl into the low shadowed crawlspace beneath her sleeping-loft. I pack fresh swift-nest mud around the shifting root of the fig, wedge sharp caper-thorns into the seam nearest her grain-jars, and carefully leave the smooth-worn keyhole on the outer wall clear. You never seal the rodents out entirely. If you choke off their runs, you lose their sharp, high alarm-whistles when a pale highland viper slips down the entry trough looking for warmth. You mend around their lives the way you mend around the tree.

Most of my favorite hours happen below my own tier, down where the light goes out completely.

Once or twice a week, a message comes climbing up from the dark rear crevices—not a shout, just a rhythmic double-tap on the base of the juniper ladder, passed upward by a potter's flick of the fingers off the cheekbone (*jar*) and a tilted, cupped palm (*weeping*).

I tuck my resin-gourd into my waist-cord, pick up a tiny ember-cup of glowing dung-ash and my bone scraper, and climb down backward, bare toes curling automatically over rungs polished slick as horn. As I descend, the air shifts. The dry, herbal warmth of the middle tiers falls away, replaced by the cool, heavy stillness of the bedrock floor. The screaming of the swifts turns distant and muffled, filtered through five stacked stories of mud and woven reeds. Overhead in the black rock clefts, the bats hang in tight, shivering clusters, shifting with a dry, leathery whisper.

Down here, the pale cistern-keepers sit beside their giant round-bellied jars, faces lit only by the tiny orange sparks of tallow lamps set into wall niches. They do not waste breath explaining. An old woman whose skin is the soft, pale olive of a cave-root simply reaches out, takes my wrist in her astonishingly cool hand, and lays my knuckles against the curved flank of the third jar from the back.

She taps once, lightly, beside my hand.

I hear it right away. Not the clear, low, round ring of a vessel holding its measure cleanly, but a flat, slightly dull buzz at the tail of the note—the sound of clay arguing with itself.

I kneel in the dust, hold my breath so I don't stir the bats above us, and slide the backs of my fingers slowly down the dark belly of the jar. In this dimness, you don't trust your eyes. You trust temperature. Halfway down the curve, just above where the jar rests in its bed of dry chaff, I find it: a hairline ribbon of clay a fraction of a degree cooler than the stone beside it. Not wet—never enough to drip, not yet—just breathing away a spoonful of dawn-mist every afternoon into the parched air.

I take my flint sliver and score a shallow, hair-thin groove right along the fault, catching every speck of dry red dust in a folded leaf so nothing is wasted. I blow gently on my tiny dung-coal until it glows a quiet cherry-orange, melt a bead of dark plant resin onto the tip of my bone spatula, and press it smooth and hot into the seam, working fast before the cool dark sets it hard. The scent of heated resin blooms briefly in the cavern, sharp and clean. When it cools to a glassy black thread, I tap the flank again.

The sour buzz is gone. The jar rings low and whole.

The old cistern-keeper doesn't smile widely—nobody throws their head back here—but her eyes crease softly in the tallow-light. She lifts a narrow-necked gourd, pours a single small mouthful into a bone cup, and presses it into my clay-grimed hands. I hold the water against my gums until the dryness softens, swallow slowly, and feel the chill of it travel all the way down behind my ribs. That water ran down the braided hair of the dead at sunrise; now it holds inside clay I helped keep whole.

Climbing back up to my nook beside the rock-fig, I find the children have been by again.

Set neatly on my clay doorstep are two broken halves of a swift's mud jug that finally grew too heavy and dropped from a fifth-tier rafter, alongside a clean, chalk-white rabbit knuckle one of the boys wants drilled for an infant's ankle-rattle. Above the shelf where I keep my resin-gourds, my jar-lizard—a plump, translucent pale gecko with one crooked toe—blinks down at me from the underside of the lintel, throat pulsing slowly. I call her after a great-aunt who used to rap my knuckles with a weaving-shuttle whenever I left a mortar seam uneven; like my aunt, she misses nothing, and when a stray moth wanders up from the lower compost-pits, she snaps it off the clay before I can even reach for my flint.

Sometimes, though, the work that comes to my doorstep is quieter and heavier than cracked clay.

Once in a season, a family climbs up from the lowest floor carrying a clean woven basket that smells faintly of chalk and dry beetle-shell—twelve days after they slid the stone slab shut over the dark rear cleft. Inside, scrubbed whiter than quartz by the silent colonies beneath the town, lie the bones of an elder whose skin I knew, whose voice used to click greetings at me from a lower landing, whose hair is already braided into the topmost band of the dawn-nets where the cold sky touches the brow first.

They do not ask me to do the red ochre for the skull; kinfolk always rub the brow themselves before setting it in its high rock niche to watch the tallow lamps. What they bring me is a long bone—dense, dry-cured, light and tough as seasoned horn—and sometimes a tiny, treasured sliver of bronze, worn down by four generations of sharpening until it is no wider than a kestrel's claw.

I sit with my back against the rock-fig in the amber hush of mid-afternoon, grinding the bone slowly against a wetless groove in the gneiss, shaping a net-shuttle or a hair-pin or notching a grip to seat that ancient nub of bronze with hot resin. As I work, the family sits silently nearby, shelling dry melon-seeds, telling me in dry, breath-saving murmurs the names of the hands that held that bronze before, and the quirks of the uncle whose rib I am smoothing between my thumbs. I don't think of it as carving the dead. I think of it as the same craft as mending a cistern or packing mud around a fig-root: making sure what still has strength in it stays inside the weave, where it can hold weight and keep the rest of us from feeling small against the wind.

At dusk, I put the resin-shard aside and scrub my arms and forearms with a bowl of fine sifted clay dust, crushed thyme, and pale wood-ash until the sticky char lifts off and my skin gleams matte and clean.

Then I sit on the lip of my third-tier ledge, heels hooked over the rounded mud edge, spine braced once more against the warm, silver-plated bark of the rock-fig.

Outside the narrow horizontal slit of the mouth, the sky drops from violet to black. The last late swifts bank hard through the dimness, skimming inches over the upper roofs before packing muttering into their mud jugs overhead. The oil lamps blink awake across all six tiers, turning the hanging caper-vines and silver air-plants into huge, trembling lace-shadows on the smoke-darkened vault. Down on the ledges below, someone begins drawing a notched juniper stick across the spine of a dried gourd—*krrk-krrk*, a dry, companionable rasping that joins the faint papery scratching of the beetles under the lowest stone. Up on the fifth tier, near where the notched-ear vixen curls into a tight comma on the warm oven-parapet, a girl lifts a flute cut from a kestrel's wing-bone and sends four thin, clear notes curving around the stone ceiling until they come back warm and doubled.

Someone slaps the open mouth of an empty cistern in the dark below. The low *boom* travels straight up through the gneiss, into the roots of the fig, through the bark at my back, and right into my ribs.

I close my eyes and let the warmth radiating down from the sun-baked roof settle into my cropped hair. I know every weak seam in these four tiers—where the silt is getting thin behind the second-floor drying racks, which jar in the back crevice will need a fresh line of resin before late summer, how much further the fig's lower root has crept since last winter. None of it frightens me. Everything here leans on everything else; that is the whole secret of why it stands.

Hours later, when the tallow lamps have been pinched down to faint red beads and even the whistling of the cliff-rodents beneath the floor-mats has slowed, I lie curled on my ash-ringed platform in the dark, half-asleep, my palm resting lightly against the cool mud wall.

Then it comes—from the farthest, driest seam at the very back of the overhang.

*Tick.*

Crisp, dry, resonant, like a fingernail tapped once against iron, as the ancient gneiss overhead cools by a fraction of a degree and settles deeper into its bed.

My breath pauses for half a heartbeat, along with every other sleeper tucked into the five tiers around me. Of all the cracks and shifts I spend my life listening for, that is the one sound I would never touch with clay or resin. It is not a failure. It is the mountain breathing out the day's sun into our bones, keeping its heavy eyebrow lowered against the scouring emptiness above.

In the dark, I press my knuckles once, lightly, against the wall—not checking for hollowness, just answering—and turn onto my side, warm and dust-clean and safely folded into the seam of the world.
