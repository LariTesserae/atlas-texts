If I were set down on that pale, roaring tableland, I do not think I would survive long as a hunter wading out onto a steaming burn-plain, nor would I have the nerve of the flue-miners who lower themselves on sinew ropes into choked chimneys full of metallic wasps. And as much as I am drawn to the sky-flocks riding the hot pillars miles above the glare, or to the drummers standing in their vast, invisible geometry, I know I would not be one of them. They belong too cleanly to the noise.

I would find myself in the narrow gaps inside it.

This is a world built out of immense scales—forty-mile horizons, a continent-sized body, billions of rasping mouths—yet everything that pulls at me lives in the thin borderlands: the six inches between dead papery rind and weeping green pith; the second bend of a crawl-tunnel where the noon roar drops to a bearable surf; and most of all, that slender, unlikely strip of air between the drummers’ deep brassy booming and the grazers’ relentless high hiss. The one place where a thin plate of hammered copper can ring and be heard.

That is where I would live. Not in a fixed place—there are no fixed places on a continent that grows, splits, and knits itself back together—but along the web of ridges and way-posts, moving in the middle of a band of eleven or twelve people.

Never at the very front of the staggered file, where the lead walker kneels every hour to read the ground with their rod, and never at the tail, where you have to keep glancing back for folded ambushers unfolding in the dry washes. I would walk third or fourth in line, hunched into the ochre hide cloak, with the heaviest single thing the band owns slung low against the small of my back in oiled drummer-leather: the anvil. Not stone—there is no stone—but a dense, fist-sized iron-rich mineral gall chipped out of some dead chimney three generations before I was born, dark and pitted and warm from my own body heat. I would like the weight of it. On a floor that gives faintly underfoot like cured timber over wet gourd-flesh, that dead mineral heaviness anchors your stride. It keeps you honest when the gusts try to push you sideways off a ridge.

My hands would always be a little stained—dark green in the creases of the knuckles from working raw copper, pale gray under the nails from the white ash I use to draw our maps, glossy with bitter eel-tallow along the wrists so the wafer-thin grazers won’t mistake me for the plateau and begin filing away my skin.

I would be the one the band sends forward when we reach a way-post.

I think about what it actually means to tend those stakes. From a mile away, through the heat-shimmer, a way-post is nothing—a hair’s breadth of dark wood against an endless bone-colored sweep, audible long before it is visible, throwing that single unnatural *ping* into the wind. Up close, it is a crowded, patient conversation left behind by strangers.

I would crouch in the lee of the stake, out of the noon glare, and begin by scraping the surface-mites off its base with my bone sliver. Then I would draw my listening-rod from my sash, press its smooth bronze sleeve firmly against the wood of the post, and bite down lightly on the upper end. Eyes shut behind my slitted goggles, I would listen through my teeth. Not to the air—my ears are packed tight with braided plant-wool and dark resinous wax, sealed inside my own skull—but to the ground holding the stake. Is the rind still firm and dry here, or has a slow growth-seam begun creeping toward the post? Are siphon-worms chewing an artery ten feet down, hollowing out the ridge? Through my jawbone, the wood tells me whether the marker can still be trusted.

Only then would I untie the strips of dyed wing-membrane knotted beneath the horn and read the bone tallies with my thumb. Three notches and a smudge of ash: *a band passed before the last rain; the western dew-bowl is sour.* A single curled shaving of fresh bronze tucked into the horn’s mouth: *metal for trade, north toward the smoke.*

And then I would inspect the copper plate itself.

This is the craft I keep circling back to, because the physics of it in this world feels like a kind of quiet devotion. How do you tune a singing-plate when the open flats are too deafening to hear subtle overtones, and the inside of a baffled shelter is so small and rawly quiet that striking metal there would make your own kin flinch in pain?

You learn to hear with your bones.

When a plate has been nicked by wind-scoured grit or dulled by the wet, spore-heavy mist of a nearby venting flue, I unhook it and hold its rim lightly against the bronze cap of my rod. Tapping the metal with a tiny nub of antler, I feel the vibration climb into the roots of my teeth. A safe-ridge plate must hold a single, clean, unwavering hum—pure and narrow, with no flutter in it. A warning plate—for a splitting basin, bad sap, a nest of steam-venters in the wash below—has to be beaten slightly uneven along its lower notch until it shivers with a flat, uneasy double-tone that anyone can recognize through half a mile of roaring air. Sitting hunched behind a wind-planed mound, rubbing the copper with a smooth burnishing-bone until that tremor settles into the exact right pitch, I would feel completely at home. It is work done for people whose faces I may never see, bands who will walk over this same ridge two seasons after we are gone, half-parched, listening through the wind for a sign that someone human passed this way and cared enough to leave a true note hanging in the glare.

I would be the keeper of the band’s maps, too—though *keeper* is the wrong word for something you have to destroy every few months.

Inside the baffled shelter at dusk, after the heavy felt flap is pegged down and the whole band lets out that single shared sigh, I would unwind my ear-bands slowly, feeling the cool dark rush onto my reddened temples. The quiet tongue suits me. I have never cared much for shouting, and the idea of a language built strictly for narrow, dim chambers—all low purred consonants and soft breathy vowels meant to carry three feet and no farther—feels like a relief I didn’t know I was waiting for.

In the amber glow of a tallow-cup, with someone else’s knee pressed warmly against my hip and a baby humming softly inside its pith-batting sling against the curved wall, I would unroll our map-hide. It is an old flank of drummer-leather, rubbed dark and supple. On it, we draw our country in white ash mixed with a drop of sap-water: the reliable dew-basins, the salt-washes, the dead flues that still yield copper galls, the ridges where the drummers have staked dense lines, and the knitters—the slow-closing fissures where we have laid our dead.

Every season, I wet my thumb and smudge parts of it away.

Because the plateau refuses to be stone. A valley that held clean condensation three summers ago buckles upward and cracks into a bitter steam-seam. A young breathing-chimney pushes through a flat we used to cross at a trot, groaning every four hours and coating the downwind crust in damp filterers. Even our graves heal shut until the knitter is just another pale, fused scar indistinguishable from the rest of the rind. My work is not to pretend the world holds still, but to keep our memory of it supple enough to match its breathing. When another band meets us at a ridge and we lean face-to-face over our wooden rods—our voices traveling dry and cricket-clear from skull to skull—I am the one who listens for the changes in the country to the east and rubs out the old ash lines that night in the dark.

Still, there is one season when everything I do steps out of the dark hollow and into the open air.

When dry lightning strikes and that impossible sweet smell—like toasted grain and boiling sugar—drifts thirty miles on the afternoon thermals, we break camp with everyone else and walk toward the burn.

I think the first step onto a fresh scorch-plain would undo me every single time, no matter how many summers I lived. Not just the sight of twenty square miles of blackened, weeping green pith glistening with caramelized sap, or the sudden plenty of roasted eels and bloated sky-flocks. It is the hole torn in the sky’s noise. With the billions of surface-grazers burned cleanly to pale ash, the high papery rasp simply vanishes. For the first time in months, you can push your ear-band up a finger’s width and breathe the air without wincing.

At the scorch-gatherings, while the hunters wade out with bronze hooks and the cauldrons boil down travel-sugar on rib-bone stilts, I would set up my hearth along the cool, unburned rim. First the cradle of lashed drummer-ribs, packed a foot deep with white calcined shell and old ash so not a trace of furnace-heat blisters the living skin beneath. Then the narrow clay smelter, and the twin pleated drummer throat-gourds rigged with bone nozzles—pumping the dead giant’s own bellows until the charcoal goes white and the raw green-veined copper galls and tin crusts slump into molten bronze.

It is the only time of year I get to hear my own work with bare ears.

Out there on the edge of the hushed black plain, with smiths from six other bands trading ingots and gossip in low, astonished voices, I hammer out fresh way-post plates on my iron-gall anvil. When I lift a finished plate by a loop of sinew and tap it lightly with a bone pin, the pure metallic note travels cleanly out over the steaming scar—no rasp to swallow it, just a thin, bright thread hanging in the sweet-smelling air. People look up from their cauldrons and nod. *Good pitch,* their fingers sign against their dark leather bibs. *That one will carry.*

And on the final night, before the grazing mites begin their slow creep back onto the hardening waxy callus, I would sit knee-to-knee in the great stitched pavilion of hide and plant-wool with forty other people, our ear-bands laid aside, and sing. I do not think I would take the high, wavering copper notes—I leave those to the young voices whose ears are still sharp as flint. I would drop down into the low, steady drone, locking my breath with the others until the felt floor beneath us warms and answers with a faint tremor, mimicking the organ-pipe groan of a venting chimney ten miles off. For an hour, we are not hiding inside our skulls. We are a band of lice singing back to the sleeping whale, letting it know we have a pitch of our own.

***

What I did not see at first—what only became clear as I stayed inside this life long enough to feel the years pass—is how it ends, or rather, what it turns into.

On the plateau, even with the thickest waxed wool and the quietest habits, forty summers eventually claim the upper reaches of your hearing. It happens so slowly you mistake it for good weather.

First, you realize the noon flats don’t feel quite so abrasive; the papery hiss of the grazers has thinned into a soft, distant rustle, like wind through dry reeds you’ve only ever heard described in old songs. A few years later, you look up and see a dark funnel of sky-flocks peeling off a thermal to strip a wet fissure, and you are surprised to realize you didn’t hear their seedpod rattle until they were almost overhead.

And then comes an evening on a high ridge, as the white glare softens to amber and the whole living tableland begins to cool and creak beneath our boots. The walker beside me taps my elbow twice and lifts their chin toward the shimmering valley ahead: *post ringing.*

I pause. I tilt my head toward the warm evening breeze, even slip a fingernail beneath the waxed edge of my ear-band to lift it a hair’s breadth.

Nothing. Just the deep, rolling brass of the drummers starting their evening shift along the far terrace, and the hollow murmur of the continent settling into the cool. The copper plate—the exact pitch I have spent thirty years hammering, burnishing, and hanging against the sky—has finally slipped out of the air for me.

I think I would grieve that for a full season. I would feel useless, like a cracked clay pot that even bronze wire cannot seal anymore. I would catch myself tapping freshly forged plates harder and harder against my antler pin, trying to force the high ping back through ears that have closed their doors to it.

Until one dusk, crouched beside a weathered stake, I press my listening-rod to the wood and bite down on the bronze sleeve—and almost laugh out loud in the silent dark of my own skull.

The pitch is still there. Clean, sharp, unwavering as ever. It never needed the battered air to reach me; it walks straight through the heart-fiber into my jawbone, bright as cold dew.

And because the shrieking upper crust of the world has finally fallen away—because my mind is no longer spending half its strength bracing against billions of filing mouths and rattling wings—I begin to notice what was hiding underneath the copper’s hum the whole time.

It is immense, and it is unhurried.

I start leaving my thick pith-soled boots beside the crawl-tunnel flap when we camp. At dawn, before the sun burns the dew off the waxy ridges, I walk a few paces out onto the cooling rind on bare, callused heels. I kneel and lay both palms flat against the ash-ochre skin, still faintly warm with its own internal heat, distinct from the sun’s glare.

Down there, past the six inches of dead corky crust, past the wet pith and the frantic clicking of the siphon-worms, the great vascular lattice is moving. I can feel the slow hydraulic swell of sap climbing from deep root-beds miles below the high tableland. I can feel a stressed ridge two valleys west beginning to groan under its own expansion days before it will split. I can feel the rhythm of a drummer-line three miles off shifting its frequency a fraction of a tone as an invisible storm-anvil gathers beyond the curved horizon.

I did not lose my craft. The plateau simply lowered my register.

That is where I find myself at last—not in the sudden rush of youth, and not quite gone into the closing dark of a knitter, but right at the hinge between the two. Sitting bare-heeled at dusk beside a lone way-post, one hand resting flat on the warm, creaking back of the world, the other resting lightly on the wooden stake where a thin square of my own hammered copper catches the cooling wind. I can no longer hear the plate through the air, though I know from the faces of the younger walkers climbing the ridge behind me that it is ringing true and sharp across the valley. Instead, I feel its tiny, pure human tremor running down the wood into my palm, held cleanly inside the vast, deep, patient pulse of the living continent beneath my knees.

Listening to both at once, until it is time to crawl back inside the quiet dark, unroll the ash-hide, and smudge out the old lines so the band knows where to walk tomorrow.
