At first, I try to enter the way anyone from above would: upright, blinking in the dimness beneath the eastern cleft, knees already aching against the wet travertine, listening for an echo that never comes.

It doesn’t hold. That posture is too loud for this room. Even standing still, a body built for frost and wind makes a commotion here—chest heaving too fast in the bath-warm air, weight planted awkwardly on two narrow heels so the rimstone basin beneath me slops over its scalloped lip and sends a clumsy pulse rushing across three alcoves. I try sitting by the three cold tallow dishes at the edge of the gray sheen, folding my hands, waiting for the surface glare to settle. I try imagining myself as the one who brought a chisel down here once, pecking footholds into the slick columns, cutting seventeen short vertical marks into the broad belly of the central pillar before climbing back out into the weather.

But when I look at those marks—or imagine climbing back up through the boulder-choked cleft into open sky and moving air—something in me refuses the climb. I don't want to be a rare weather passing through. I don't want to smell of woodsmoke and wool and leave behind only cold saucers and a stone-crusted scoop.

So I stop trying to keep my head up. I let the hurried breath go. I let the room have my weight.

And slowly, as the water flattens back to glass around me, what I actually am down here begins to come clear—not all at once, the way a face appears in lamplight, but the way a shape emerges in this chamber: first as a slight thickening of the water-skin over a rim, then as a warmth pressed flush against a wet column, then as a quiet *snap* where last night’s mineral veil parts from my flank.

---

I am not near the eastern cleft, where the olive-brown stain clings to the wet pillar and the midday brightening feels almost sharp. Nor am I in the deepest velvet alcoves at the far end of the thicket, where the oldest ones go when their shoulders grow too heavy to unhinge.

I live in the middle ash-gray—close enough to the central pillar that its broad, smooth belly is only three sideways turns away, close enough to the narrow corridor of niches to know the cadence of almost every clay bowl by the ring it sends through the floor when it overflows.

I am not ancient, and not newly hatched. Across my shoulders and the ridge of my spine, the mineral water has already painted me in bands of wet ochre and pewter gray, fractured into shingled plates that flex when I turn between two swollen columns. There is a scatter of pale sinter roughening my brow, enough that if I press flat against a stalagmite and fold my arms inward, even the midday gleam slides right over me as if I were part of the bedrock. Yet underneath—along my throat, my belly, the insides of my long limbs, the broad flat-tipped pads of my hands—I am still bare as tallow, slick and pale and thin-skinned, kept always against water or wet limestone.

If I had to say what my place is among the ten or twenty of us scattered through the stone forest, I would not know how to name it. We have no names, and no center to gather in. Still, each of us falls into a habit of the chamber—the alcoves we favor, the drips we recognize, the pace at which we move.

My habit is the seams. Everything that has not quite been sealed shut yet.

---

I remember—not in pictures, only as a texture held in my palms—the rough curve of unglazed clay.

I hatched in one of the wide-mouthed bowls tucked into a waist-high niche four columns inward from the skylight’s reach. For my first months, that curved wall of coarse clay was the entire edge of the world, and time was a single muted *plip* every long half-minute, striking the center of the water over my head and sending a smooth ring out to the rim and back. When I finally hooked my tiny, translucent hands over the lip and slid down the slick flank of the pillar into the warm pools below, the limestone felt startlingly smooth after the grit of the bowl—almost slippery, almost too finished.

Maybe that is why I still make a slow circuit of the niches, taking days to drift from one to the next.

Two of the bowls—wedged too tightly in high crevices, or sitting beneath drips that taste bitter and thin—have already been taken by the room. Thick white collars of travertine have grown up around their bases, welding them cleanly to their shelves. When I pass them, I sometimes lay the back of my knuckles against the cold clay anyway, feeling the steady beat of the drop travel through the fused base into the pillar. They are stone now; they no longer have any give in them.

The others are still loose. Every few days, I stretch my long neck up to a niche, rest my soft snout on a rough rim, and drink. The ceiling water is nothing like the warm mineral seepage rising beneath our bellies; it is a fraction cooler, filtered down through roots and soil from a sky I have only ever felt as a dull silver lift on the eastern columns. After heavy rains above, it carries a faint, sweet sharpness that wakes the gill-fronds behind my jaw.

When I finish drinking, I do not simply pull away. I curl my broad fingers around the clay belly and nudge the vessel a hair’s breadth sideways against the wet shelf—just enough to break whatever invisible hairline of calcite began to bridge the gap since my last visit. A tiny, grainy rasp passes through my pads, softer than sand settling, and then the bowl sits flat again, brimming, unsealed, still its own small thing inside the chamber.

It takes almost no effort. It is only a way of keeping a promise to a room that wants to turn everything into itself.

---

On my way back toward the deeper basins, I almost always pass the central pillar.

Turning sideways through the pinch where long-ago chisels pecked shallow footholds—half-glazed over now by fresh lime—I pause with my bare cheek and throat pressed lightly against the column’s broad belly. There they are: seventeen short vertical cuts, neat and evenly spaced, shallower every few years as the mineral skin fills them from within.

I do not know what seventeen is. To my skin, it is not a number; it is a cadence. The water never carves straight lines like that, never stops and starts with such deliberate, dry-edged intervals. Sliding my palm slowly from the first groove to the last feels like listening to a very old message tapped into the rock by someone who did not know our tempo—too quick, seventeen beats packed side by side with no long silence between them, as if whoever made them was afraid of running out of light.

Sometimes, when my cheek is resting there, I think—in the slow, wordless way we think down here—of the tall, warm shapes that used to descend from the eastern cleft. I never felt their clumsy splashing myself, only inherited the memory of it in the shape of the pools they deepened and the bowls they left behind. Yet I like that they sat still at the end. I like that whatever hurried them down here with iron points and burning fat eventually let go of them, long enough for the water to go glass-flat again and for one of our elders to come out from behind a pleated curtain and rest a pale hand beside theirs on the scalloped lip.

Someday, in another span of years, the lime will finish filling the seventeen cuts. The pillar will be smooth as tallow from floor to ceiling, and no palm passing in the dark will guess that anything other than water ever touched it. Until then, I press my throat to them when I pass, and remember the shape of their haste without wanting any of it for myself.

---

What truly wakes me, though, is not the bowls or the pillar. It is the bedrock.

I might be lying draped across two stepped pools in the dimness, half-submerged, my heart beating three times a minute, my gill-fronds drifting loosely in the warm water while the sensory pits along my fingers read the intersecting webs of six familiar ceiling drips. Nothing moves. A blind snail inches along the underside of the calcite lip beside my ribs. Somewhere far across the room, a bead of condensation swells and lets go with a muffled *plip*.

Then, through the rimstone shelf beneath my jaw, comes a sound that never enters the air.

*Tick.*

A long silence—ten heartbeats, maybe more, long enough for a drop to gather and fall somewhere behind a stone curtain.

*Tick. Tick.*

My eyes do not open wider—they are lidless beads of milky silver, useful only for telling midday from dusk—but every sensory pit along my throat sharpens. I press my cheekbone firmer against the wet lip and listen into the stone. The signal comes from four or five columns deeper in, toward the dark alcoves where the iron-brown flowstone hangs thick as folded hide. The pitch of it is slightly dull, heavy, muffled by a thick coat of crust.

*My shoulder is stiffening. Come.*

I lift my brow an inch, let the calcified crown touch the column beside me, and send a single clean *tick* back through the floor. *I hear. I am moving.*

There is no hurry. Hurry only wakes the whole chamber with needless waves. I draw one slow, quiet sip of humid air through the surface film, tuck my gill-fronds neatly behind my jaw, and slip out of the basin—letting the overflow lip thicken by scarcely a hair as my weight leaves it.

It takes me twenty minutes to cross thirty feet of crowded stone. I pass the ankle-deep pool where the stone-crusted wooden scoop lies submerged, brushing its brittle outline with the edge of my foot as a familiar turn. I slide sideways between two swollen ribs where a human chest would wedge fast, feeling my banded back scrape softly against the wet calcite, leaving a faint, harmless smear of slime on the travertine.

I find the elder already waiting in one of the deepened basins—a pool whose natural rim was raised a palm’s breadth long ago with flat shards of dark limestone, now cemented seamlessly into place. Only the domed, mottled ridge of its back breaks the surface, looking for all the world like a half-submerged boss of flowstone. Around the crease of its left shoulder and down along the hinge of its hip, fresh pale sinter has crept in a thick collar, bridging the natural cracks in its plates, beginning to bind the crust to the bare skin underneath.

I slip into the water beside it without breaking a ring. The pool is deep enough here to hold us both to the collarbone, bath-warm and completely still.

For a long while, neither of us does anything. I simply lay my naked palm across the elder’s banded back, feeling the sluggish, steady thrum of its heart beneath a quarter-inch of layered stone—slower than mine, two beats a minute, patient as seepage, but still clearly, stubbornly alive.

Then I begin.

I cup my broad, webbed hands and sluice warm water slowly over the stiffened seam, working it into the hairline crease where the pale lime has bitten into the soft fold behind the joint. There is no pulling, no tearing. You wait until the seam softens, until the elder’s shoulder gives a fraction of an inch beneath your fingers. Then I slide the hardened, calcified outer edge of my thumb gently beneath the lip of the encroaching crust and press—just enough.

Under the water, the sound is small, crisp, and clean: *snap*.

A curved, wafer-thin flake of ochre-and-pewter stone lifts free and drifts lazily down through the clear warmth to settle on the silt, indistinguishable from a shard shed by the ceiling drapes. Beneath it, the newly bared skin gleams faintly pearlescent in the dark, impossibly tender, pulsing once with dark blood before the warm water folds over it.

I work my way along the shoulder, down the flank, around the rigid hinge of the hip. Flake by flake. *Snap*, pause, sluice, wait. Sometimes the elder shifts its jaw against my collarbone, a quiet pressure that means *there—just below the rib*, and I follow the seam with my fingertips until the collar gives way in a single curved half-moon of brittle stone.

When it is done, the elder draws a deep, unhurried breath, lifting its whole banded back an inch out of the water and flexing its freed shoulder in a slow, wide arc. For a moment it looks almost young again—narrow, supple, loose-jointed, its fresh-bared flank pale against the iron-brown gloom. It presses its bare brow briefly against mine beneath the surface, our gill-fronds brushing like drifting silt, and then turns silently back toward its favored alcove.

I stay in the deepened pool for a while after the ripples die, watching the curved flakes of flowstone resting on the floor around my knees. Already, invisible grains of silt are settling into their hollows. In a few decades, the floor will weld them smooth.

---

I know what happens at the end of this, of course. We all know it, without needing words for it.

Every year my own dorsal plates grow a fraction thicker. Every season the overnight film that binds my hip to a sleeping shelf breaks with a slightly louder *snap* when I peel away. One day—long after the seventeen cuts on the central pillar have vanished beneath smooth lime, long after the last unglazed bowl I kept nudging loose has finally grown a white collar around its base—my own heart will slow until the water feels heavier than my limbs. I will drift into a quiet alcove far from the eastern sheen, lay my chin across a scalloped lip, and stop answering the ticks in the bedrock. Someone younger will come, brush a bare palm across my banded back, feel the stillness beneath the crust, and leave me to finish turning into the room.

I don't dread that. Down here, becoming stone is not a violence; it is only the last, longest stillness.

Still, I am not ready for it yet.

Not while the autumn rains still wash down the eastern cleft and turn the ceiling drips cool and sweet. Not while there is a wide-mouthed clay bowl sitting clear and brimming beneath a steady stone tongue, waiting to hold three or four faint gray-gold dots under its dark mirror while the drop above keeps patient time. Not while someone four columns away still taps twice into the floor, asking for a hand to crack the limestone back from their shoulder so they can bend again.

So I slip out of the deepened pool, turn sideways between the wet columns, and make my way slowly back toward the niches. Somewhere ahead in the ash-gray gloom, a bead of condensation swells for a long half-minute and lets go—*plip*—sending a single smooth ring outward against rough clay. I reach up, drink a slow mouthful of the outside world, shift the bowl by the width of a hair against its shelf, and settle my bare belly flush against the warm, unchanging stone.
