At first, standing on the narrow landing at the base of the spiral stair, you think the birds and lizards and climbers account for everything alive in the well. The place is already so full of motion and bright dust that there seems no room left for anything larger or quieter.

Only after an hour—once your ears begin to sort the clapping clouds of doves from the hum of crickets and the steady *tock* of the drip-basins—do the small impossibilities begin to show themselves.

Brick and fired clay will hold their shape for centuries, and adze-hewn cedar will last almost as long in warm, dry air. Rope will not. Neither will stiffened reed, nor plank bridges hung over weeping stone. Yet the lashings along the catwalks are tight and pale, wrapped in neat, flat braids that smell faintly of crushed fig-bark rather than hemp. The hanging cradles high beneath the roof-flues have been patched with fresh stalks and bound tight with fine, dark sinew. Along the middle galleries, dozens of the embedded earthenware jars are partly stopped from within by smooth, chalk-grey discs of chewed lime and dry plant-pulp, each pierced with a clean round hole—some wide as a thumb, some narrow as a reed-stem. And the rungs of the vertical ladders, worn smooth to the sheen of old bone, are not merely polished by the memory of absent builders; they carry a faint, dry film of warm oil and powdered mortar, fresh enough to smear across a fingertip.

Something still keeps the well. Not the hands that fired its bricks and carved its pulleys, and not anything that walks in from the flat world above, but a quiet, narrow-bodied household that has grown wholly into the shape of this buried drum.

You will not spot them by looking into shadows, because there are few true shadows here. Whatever lives in a well of bounced daylight hides in brightness itself.

Look long enough at a freestanding tower of stacked clay drums, where ancient lime-wash has peeled back in dry, curving sheets, and one of those peeling patches will shift by half a finger's width. What seemed to be a crust of flaking terracotta plaster lifts slightly from the masonry, resolves into a lean, hunched back, and slides smoothly around the curve of the column without dislodging a single crumb of mortar.

They are slighter than a human—scarcely longer than a tall man's reach if stretched straight, though they almost never stand straight—and astonishingly light, built for swaying planks and narrow brick ledges. They have no fur, for fur traps baking heat and bird-lice in a crowded roost. Instead, their skin is dry, fine-grained, and pebbled like unglazed earthenware, the warm, dusty buff of sun-struck brick, always powdered with lime-dust from the walls. Down the ridge of the spine, across the narrow shoulders, and along the outer edges of their long forearms, that hide thickens into overlapping plates no larger than broken potsherds, dull ochre-red and curled slightly at the margins like old paint lifting from damp stucco. The corners of those plates are worn smooth and blunt by decades of squeezing into jar-mouths and brushing past masonry. Pressed flat against a flaking brick pillar, they vanish completely into the patina of the wall.

All four of their limbs end in dry, long-fingered gripping hands, the nails neither claws nor thin human shells, but thick, flat, spoon-shaped plates of pale horn—tools meant for scraping calcified bird-lime, twisting bark-fiber, prying loose sherds, and seating mud-paste into crumbling seams. At the base of the spine sits a short, stiffened wedge of fused plates, ridged and calloused on its underside. When one of them climbs a sheer face of horizontal jars, it braces this hard prop firmly against a lower rim like a mason locking a scaffold, hanging effortlessly by its hind-grip and wedge while both long arms work free in the bright air.

Their faces are narrow, smooth-browed, and unhurried. Two neat slits serve for nostrils, pinching tight whenever a cloud of doves beats up mortar-dust and chaff. Their eyes sit forward beneath dry, wrinkled lids that close with a slow, dusty blink—irises the pale, milky gold of limestone in noon sun, with narrow horizontal pupils that shrink to bright threads in the direct shafts from the oculus and open wide inside the shaded mouths of the pots. They have no outer ears. Behind each cheekbone lies a shallow, oval dish of taut, parchment-pale skin, faint as a drumhead, that shivers subtly with every tremor in the masonry. Beneath the jaw, down the length of the lean throat and upper breastbone, the skin hangs in neat, vertical pleats like folded linen, pale chalk-grey and soft.

They are, before anything else, creatures of sound.

In a pit forty paces across and twice as deep, stuffed so thick with towers, catwalks, and hanging cradles that no eye can draw a clean line from one wall to the next, sight is only half a sense. A newcomer hears a wall of chaotic noise; the dwellers hear a vast, finely ruled map.

Every horizontal jar embedded in the seven stepped galleries is an acoustic horn, and over generations they have tuned hundreds of them. By packing chewed fig-pulp and damp lime-paste into the rims, or wedging chipped potsherds diagonally across a jar's throat, they alter the pitch at which each vessel answers the wind. When a breeze crosses the angled chimneys overhead and sends its low, hollow note rolling down through the pit, those tuned jars wake in faint, sympathetic resonance—a quiet, shifting lattice of tones humming just beneath the shrill chatter of the swifts and the metallic trill of the crickets.

To the dwellers, that lattice is as legible as sunlight on stone. If a heavy bronze snake crawls into a tuned amphora on the second tier, its body damps the note; a single quiet hole opens in the hum of the wall. If a russet climber leaps onto a loose plank bridge four galleries up, the flex of the cedar sends a faint shudder through the lashed beam into the clay drums, and every parchment ear-dish in the well turns toward the tremor. They move *inside* the noises of the pit rather than against them—stepping across creaking timber on the exact *tock* of a nearby catch-basin, slipping out from beneath a catwalk at the instant a startled flock of doves bursts clapping from the upper pots, freezing motionless the moment the wind dies in the roof-flues.

They speak the same way. They almost never raise their voices into the open air, where a call would only scatter the birds and betray their place to every climber in the middle tiers. Instead, one will crouch beside an embedded jar, press its pleated throat-pouch gently against the flared clay lip, and inflate the pale folds with a slow breath. The sound that leaves its chest is scarcely louder than a sigh, yet the curved pottery catches it, rounds it, and sends a warm, hollow tone sliding cleanly across the drum of the pit, pitching straight beneath the bird-noise to strike the taut ear-dishes of another dweller seventy feet away. A slight shift of a finger across the pot's rim bends the note upward or down; a dry tap of a horn-nail against the brickwork breaks it into short, crisp syllables. Two of them, hidden completely from one another behind towers and drifting feathers, will hold long, unhurried conversations across the bright afternoon by murmuring into the mouths of the walls.

They do not know how to make fire, nor how to shape raw earth and bake it hard. Whatever fired clay, carved stone, and hewn cedar exists inside the octagon is all there will ever be, and they treat it with a thrift that looks almost like devotion.

Nothing in the pit is allowed to rot unmended, yet nothing is remade from scratch. Where old lime-mortar crumbles between bricks, they press in damp wads of lime-dust kneaded with crushed maidenhair stems and sticky white fig-sap—soft at first, drying to a tough, pale crust not unlike the miniature clay tubes of the mud-dauber wasps. Where a bridge-lashing frays, they climb to the stunted fig saplings clinging to the middle walls and peel away a single thin vertical ribbon of inner bark, never girdling a stem, leaving the tree enough pale root and sap to heal cleanly. Those pale bast-strips they chew supple, twist against their dry thighs, and braid together with shed flight-feathers and dried sinew until they have cord strong enough to hold a hanging cradle or re-bind a sagging plank.

They prune the spiny caper bushes along the bright upper ledges so their spreading roots do not pry loose the brickwork of the topmost galleries, eating the tangy pale blossoms and swollen buds as they work. Twice each season, in the cool dimness just before sunrise when the air sinks heavy down the flues and the bronze snakes lie sluggish in their lower pots, two adults descend all the way to the pale flagstones at the bottom. Working swiftly with curved hooks of pale bone, they clear packed husks and calcified bird-lime from the iron-colored sump-grate at the center of the floor, keeping the shallow thumb-wide drainage channel open so the weeping lower walls never pool and drown the floor.

For they depend on the floor-world as surely as they depend on the birds above.

The matte-black beetles, pale ribbon-centipedes, and flat drainage-spiders churning across the flagstones are the pit's furnace—a cold, clicking fire that strips flesh clean without smoke. The dwellers use the ancient carved stone pulleys, whose grooves have never lost their polish, to deal with it. When they snare one of the lean, russet-furred climbers along the middle bridges—which they do whenever the bold raiders grow too numerous or creep too near the high cradles—nothing of the animal is wasted. Its meat is sliced thin with the sharp edge of a freshly chipped potsherd and laid out on flat bricks directly beneath the hottest roof-flues, where the baking-house heat cures it dry and dark before the flies can claim it. Its long tendons are stripped and dried for bridge-twine; its soft russet pelt is scraped clean with lime-dust and tucked into the nursery cradles. Then the picked-over frame—skull, ribs, and narrow leg-bones—is sealed inside a tight cage of woven reed and lowered slowly on a braided fig-bast cord down the stone pulley until it rests on the flagstones near the sump.

By late afternoon, the beetles have finished their work. When the sun narrows into steep gold bars, a dweller hauls the reed cage smoothly back up through the drifting dust. Inside lie clean, dry, gleaming bones, rattling softly against the reeds. From these they shape awls, basket-needles, flat toggles for the plank bridges, and short hollow chimes hung beneath certain catwalks—warning-strings that clatter softly whenever a climber sets a padded paw on a forbidden span.

Toward the rest of the pit's crowded neighborhoods they keep an unsentimental, careful balance. They take eggs from the rock doves and swifts with astonishing delicacy, sliding a warm, chalk-dusted hand sideways into a horizontal jar at the slow pace of a shifting shadow, lifting one egg from a clutch of two without waking the clapping panic that brings down showers of loose sherds. Now and then they brush calcified droppings from overcrowded upper roosts or scatter crushed fig-seeds along the brightest ledges to hold the flocks steady. Against the bronze snakes of the lower galleries they carry no grudge; a heavy snake coiled in a cracked amphora beside a drip-basin keeps the climbers from descending to the quiet lower tiers. Only if a bold snake works its way up into the middle towers do two dwellers converge—one tapping a jar-lip to draw the reptile's flickering tongue toward the vibration, the other slipping a stiff loop of braided fig-root smoothly behind its head and lowering it unharmed back down toward the weeping stone.

At dawn, before the shafts of daylight steepen, they wake with the condensation. Because the bronze snakes favor the catch-basins, the dwellers rarely stoop to drink directly from the open water. Instead, they hang upside down beneath the plastered undersides of the catwalks, bracing their stiffened lower props against the cedar beams, and draw dry, spongy skeins of maidenhair moss gently across the beaded plaster. When the moss grows heavy and dark, they squeeze thin streams of cold, mineral-sweet water straight into their open mouths. Sometimes one will descend to a quiet basin anyway, resting its long, pale knuckles lightly on the wet terracotta rim while the tiny translucent frogs—no larger than thumbnails—continue their rhythmic clicking an inch from its horn-nails, sensing no hunter in that dry, lime-smelling stillness.

There are never many of them—perhaps eleven or thirteen at any time, a single household stretched across three or four generations, held in tight proportion to what the closed well can bear. Their lives unfold vertically, following the heat and gravity of the pit from roof to flagstones.

High beneath the adze-hewn cedar beams, where the lime-washed chimneys angle downward and the air stays warm as a baking-house even past dusk, hang the deepest cradles of stiffened reed, lined thick with dove-down and cured russet fur. Here the young are born—one at a time, rarely two—small, wrinkled, and pale salmon-pink as unfired clay, their backs smooth and soft as wet leather. For their first season they never leave the cradles. When hungry or startled, they make no cry that might draw a climber's ear, only a dry, rapid ticking in the back of the throat, indistinguishable from the contracting cedar beams cooling above them.

In the noon hour, when the adults retreat beneath the cool undersides of the bridges to escape the direct blaze of the oculus, the half-grown young creep out along the brightest upper ledges. There they lie flattened beside the coppery skinks and sand-colored geckos, pulsing their pale pleated throats in the scorching shafts of sun while the dry heat slowly thickens and hardens the first overlapping plates along their spines. They learn to hunt by watching the lizards, darting in sudden, silent bursts to snap up mud-dauber wasps and sunlit gnats, and they learn to speak by pressing their small faces to the upper jar-mouths, testing faint, uneven hums until an adult three galleries down answers with a patient, steadying note.

In their prime decades—lithe, dry-limbed, and sharp-eared—they claim the roaring middle tiers. These are the bridge-menders, the jar-tuners, the snare-setters, the haulers of the stone pulleys, moving constantly through the crisscrossing shafts of dust and feathers, keeping the whole precarious vertical tangle in tension.

And as they grow truly old—fifty, sixty, perhaps eighty years inside the unweathered stillness of the well—their dorsal plates thicken with layers of hardened skin and calcified mineral dust until they turn pale, heavy, and chalk-crusted as ancient plaster. Their joints stiffen; the swaying leaps between freestanding towers grow too steep. Slowly, without ceremony, an elder descends gallery by gallery, leaving the bright clamor of the middle tiers for the cool, humid, weeping brickwork near the bottom.

There, in the lowest two rings where the salmon-red masonry stays perpetually damp and dark ribbons of velvet moss trace the condensation drips, the elders settle into the widest cracked amphorae beside the catch-basins. They sit for whole afternoons with their dry hands folded over their knees, their milky limestone eyes half-shut, barely stirring as the drops fall *tock—tock—tock* beside them. The heavy bronze snakes coil undisturbed against their shins, taking them for warm, lime-crusted stone. Yet the elders are not idle. From the very bottom of the acoustic drum, where every vibration from the seven galleries settles and gathers, they listen. They know by a faint sourness in the hum which upper timber is beginning to split, which tower's mortar is softening, which chimney-flue has caught a blown thicket of dry brush from the world above. At dawn and dusk, their slow, deep throat-hums rise from the damp amphorae like notes from the bottom of a well, guiding the lighter hands far overhead.

When an elder at last grows wholly still and cold inside its jar, the younger ones wait until the morning light reaches the second gallery. Then three or four descend in silence, lift the brittle, chalk-pale body gently from the amphora, and lay it neatly upon the foot-hollowed flagstones beside the grated sump. They do not linger to watch. By dusk, the armored floor-world has done its clean work, leaving only smooth, dry white bones and the curled, sherd-like plates of the spine rattling faintly on the limestone.

Before night thickens, the adults come down once more and gather every piece. The long arm-bones and smooth ribs are carried up into the middle galleries to become bridge-toggles, net-hooks, and hanging chimes; the curved dorsal plates are carried to cracked seams in the masonry and pressed firmly into wet lime-paste, edge to edge with the ancient bricks. In this way, nothing of an elder is lost to the pit, and over centuries the walls themselves have slowly absorbed the skins of those who kept them.

They never climb the spiral stair to the surface. To them, the world beyond the latticed oculus is not a place of freedom, only an incomprehensible, unwalled emptiness where sound runs off into nothing and never returns an answer. They know it only by its leakings—the sharp mineral smell of distant rain drawn down the roof-flues, the sudden slant of a hawk's shadow slicing across the bright dust-shafts, the autumn chaff and dried grass-seeds spiraling down through the reed slats, and the seasonal shift in the low, hollow note the wind blows across the chimneys.

Yet late in the afternoon, when the sun tilts steep and deep gold and the trapped heat begins to lift from the baked clay walls in spiraling drafts, they climb toward that unseen sky as far as the pit allows.

While the needle-winged swifts corkscrew upward without beating a wing, rising in a tight, chattering vortex toward the oculus, the dwellers emerge from behind the pillars and from beneath the planks. Young and prime alike perch along the upper jar-rims, on the swaying reed cradles, and astride the sun-warmed cedar beams, letting the rising thermal wash up across their dry bellies and lift fine trails of white lime-dust from their shoulders. Down in the damp lower galleries, the heavy elders lean their narrow chins against the lips of their amphorae.

One by one, as the upper cedar begins to cool with tiny, dry ticks, they press their pleated throats to the mouths of the tuned jars.

No melody emerges—nothing shaped for a stranger's ear—only a slow, overlapping chord of warm, hollow pitches, each held for the length of a single long breath and then passed gently to another jar across the octagon. It slips cleanly under the ruffled muttering of the settling doves and winds itself around the rising evening chorus of the crickets and wall-frogs. The whole forty-pace vessel of fired clay and hard plaster takes the note into its ribs and answers back, humming steadily, deeply, alive from its weeping flagstones to its angled chimneys, breathing warmth and low resonance up into the cooling air above.

If you are standing on the hand-struck brick of the spiral stair when that chord begins, you will think at first it is only the evening wind in the roof-flues.

Then a catch-basin three tiers down misses a single beat of its *tock—tock—tock*, damped by the light brush of a horn-nailed hand, and resumes.

The low chord does not break in alarm, yet its pitch shifts by a hair—a subtle, rippling adjustment running down the stepped galleries from rim to floor. Slowly, your eyes untangle themselves from the bright clutter of ochre brick and peeling plaster. Ten paces away, a curved patch of flaking terracotta wash on a freestanding clay drum blinks once, milky-gold eyes narrowing calmly in a shaft of late sun. Beneath the nearest plank bridge, four long, chalk-dusted fingers slide unhurriedly back behind a lashed timber until only the braided fig-bast remains in view. Far below, in the damp shadow beside a weeping basin, a pale, lime-crusted elder tilts its narrow wedge of a head a fraction of an inch upward, parchment ear-dishes turned squarely toward the spiral stair.

They do not threaten you, and they do not scatter like the birds. They simply hold their places across the seven crowded tiers, measuring the strange weight of your boots on the brick, listening to the quick, loud beat of your breath echoing off the pottery, waiting in the warm, bright hum of their buried vessel to learn whether you are merely another passing shadow across the oculus—or something that has come to disturb the jars.
