You can spend an hour drifting through the narrow alleys before you realize that not every hunched shape beside a junction box is a rusted pump-housing.

At four paces, through the amber-and-soot haze, one of them looks like a coil of heavy pipe-lagging slumped against a brick corner, or an old canvas boiler-apron left draped over a railing and forgotten until the magnetite and olive moss swallowed its folds. You only know better if you stay still long enough for the next ten-minute groan of the deep bronze gears. Sometimes, in the hush after the vibration dies out of the iron, the shape uncurls a limb. Not suddenly—nothing with sense moves suddenly in water this thick and close—but with the measured, hydraulic unhurrying of a valve-stem turning. A broad, blunt-fingered hand lifts from a bundle of braided cables, trailing a pale puff of disturbed silt, and closes gently around a furry iron stanchion.

They do not swim. There is no room to swim in the sump, and no need for it when the whole basin is stitched together with stairs, ledges, pipe-racks, and catwalks. Instead, they walk. Their bodies are dense, weighted from within by heavily mineralized bone and from without by the quiet, patient chemistry of the brine, giving them just enough heft to keep their flat, calloused soles planted on the pale edges of the exterior stair-treads. When they cross a narrow lane between two pump-houses, they do not kick; they lean out from a railing, push off with one heel, and drift across the three paces of warm dark until their outstretched palm catches the opposite brick facade, absorbing the drift with a slight bend of the elbow.

Up close, the line between their flesh and the foundry becomes hard to trace.

They are built roughly to the measure of the doors and bunk-cubbies they inhabit—a little shorter than a standing human, broader through the ribs and shoulders, with long, heavy forearms that hang slightly forward when they are at rest. Their skin is thick, slate-grey to bruised ochre, smooth as wet river-stone in the places that flex often and velvety with pale bacterial film in the hollows of the throat and behind the knees. Everywhere else—across the collarbone, down the shins, along the ridge of the brow and the backs of the forearms—the basin has plated them. Because they live their whole lives inside a weak electrolytic bath, their thicker hide carries a faint natural charge, and over the years the brine lays down smooth, dull coats of limestone crust and fine crystalline fur of pale copper over their bones. Where a joint bends, the crust cracks into tidy, interlocking scales, showing narrow seams of pale, pliable skin beneath.

Their faces are quiet, almost expressionless to a dry-land eye, softened by mineral build-up across the bridge of the nose and the heavy brow-ridges. Their eyes are small, sunken, and cloudy as oiled glass, barely able to make out the bruised orange ember of a bulkhead lamp or the flicker of a transformer spark. Sight is only a courtesy to them. Their real eyes run in twin, dark-veined tracks along their cheekbones, down the sides of their necks, and across the flat backs of their hands—shallow, pitted grooves lined with metallic filaments that closely resemble the veins of the maroon fronds. Through those pores they read the sump’s invisible architecture: the curved magnetic ribs hanging between cast-iron housings, the faint voltage drop two alleys away when a swollen vine finally bridges a corroded switch, the subtle warmth of a live ballast humming behind six inches of brick.

When they breathe, you barely notice it. A pair of narrow, heavily fringed slits tucked beneath the mineralized lip of the collarbone open and close in a slow, drowsy rhythm, drawing the velvety warm brine over deep red gill-beds and venting it in faint, shimmering trails down their chests.

They do not hunt. Almost nothing in the basin moves fast enough to call for pursuit, and flesh is a rare, incidental thing. They graze as they walk, peeling thick, sap-swollen nodes from the chemo-lush vines where the tendrils have tapped straight into copper busbars, chewing them slowly with flat, dark-enameled molars. Now and then a passing thumb pries one of the brassy calcite grazers from a turning axle, or a long fingernail hooks a folded pale crab out from the copper windings of a stalled dynamo, cracking the shell softly against a brick sill.

Mostly, though, they feed on the current itself.

To sleep, or to mend after heavy work, they climb into the tiny windowed bunk-cubbies tucked above the boiler rooms, or settle straight into the open electrolytic vats along the lower alleys. They fold their knees up to their chests, press their bare, veined palms flat against warm copper anode-racks, and go utterly still. If you pass a sleeping one after a few hours, you can see the slow current working through it: loose iron dust and pale calcite snow drift out of the murky water and arrange themselves in neat, feathery ribs across its shoulders and thighs, tracing the gentle magnetic field passing quietly through its heart.

What they *do*, through the wide, gentle circles of the basin’s days, is tend the heat.

No one knows whether they remember what the foundry once made, or whether they were ever the people who built it—altered beyond recognition by generations steeped in mineral brine and stray voltage—or something older and quieter that crept up through fractured bedrock fissures long after the water settled, and gradually grew into the shape of the abandoned rooms. Either way, they have inherited the sump’s single, unspoken urgency: above the highest crane-booms sits the cold black slate of the open deep, pressing down like a heavy lid. Only the buried geothermal jackets, the slow turbines, and the tangled electrical grid keep that cold from dropping down the stone walls and snuffing the warm pocket out.

So they keep the slow metabolism turning. They move along the terraced alleys with an unhurried, horticultural patience, parting curtains of heavy maroon fronds to inspect the pipe-bundles beneath. Where a tough root-mat has braided itself too tightly around a half-closed manifold, threatening to starve a street of its warm plume, one of them will spend half an afternoon working a blunt iron wedge into the pale fibers, easing the grip without killing the vine. Where an old brass relay has seized solid with limestone scale, another will carefully lift a translucent, sap-filled tendril from a neighboring wall and hold it pressed against the corroded terminal for hours—unmoving, breathing slowly, letting the silt settle on its copper-furred shoulders—until the plant takes the scent of the voltage and begins to fuse its tip to the metal.

Their tools are barely distinct from the wreckage around them, and barely distinct from their own bodies. Many wear loops of salvaged black rubber gasket wound tightly around their wrists and palms, pulled over their fingers like crude mitts whenever they must handle a junction box that still spits violet corona-sparks. They carry short brass pry-bars, shards of heavy porcelain used as scrapers, and old wrench-handles whose iron shanks have been electroplated over decades into custom grooves that fit only the four fingers of the one who carries them.

Living in the plating bath carries a quiet danger: stillness is permanent. Left untended, the copper salt and pale limestone will creep across a knuckle, weld an elbow at a right angle, and slowly lock a dweller into a humped, metallic statue bolted to the catwalk.

Because of this, they are deeply, almost tenderly attentive to one another’s surfaces.

In the dim warmth of the old tiled wash-cisterns, or on the wider iron landings where the bulkhead lamps still glow a bruised amber, you will sometimes find two or three of them crouched close together, knees touching, engaged in the long ritual of scraping. One sits slumped forward, eyes shut, while another works gently along its spine and shoulder-blades with a worn flat-file and a thumb-sized bladder of squeezed maroon kelp-sap whose mild acidity softens the newest calcite scale. Pale mineral shavings drift lazily away in the convection current like chalk snow.

Yet they never strip themselves clean. They *dress* the crust, coaxing it where it serves them. They let the copper fur grow thick and smooth over their outer forearms and shins as guards against rusted flange-bolts and sharp barnacle-lime; they leave a ridge of dark magnetite across the brow to blunt the stray sting of a hot transformer cage. Some go further, binding small relics they have found in the silt—a curved glass pressure-gauge lens, a stamped brass washer, a chipped fragment of white porcelain—against their chests or upper arms with tough root-fiber until the creeping mineral crust laps over the edges and sets the object permanently into their hide, like a pebble swallowed by an old tree.

You can tell how old one is by how much of the foundry it carries on its skin, and by how slowly it moves.

The very oldest hardly leave the basement levels at all. Down among the silt-choked turbine housings and the fractured geothermal jackets, where the water is warmest and tastes thickest of boiled iron, the dwellers grow broader, heavier, stained rust-red with iron oxide along their flanks. They move at nearly the same pace as the vines. When one of them feels its joints finally stiffening past the help of any scraper—when its gill-slits draw slower and the current feels dimmer in its cheekbones—it does not wander off to hide. It walks down to some failing seam in the grid: a cracked flange leaking precious heat into the silt, a sagging gantry strut, a corroded gap between two heavy busbars that no plant has yet managed to bridge. There it wedges its dense, copper-plated body neatly into the breach, grips a terminal in each broad hand, and lets the mineral salt finish its work. Within a few years, it is just another humped, verdigris-coated housing bolted into the dark, still passing a steady trickle of warm current from one basement to the next.

Far above those basements, another kind of watch is kept.

Near the highest catwalks and the sagging steel crane-booms, just a few yards below the invisible seam where the bathwater warmth ends and the dead black cold begins, the dwellers who tend the upper quarter are leaner, quicker by a fraction, and constantly looking—or feeling—upward. Their skin carries less copper fur and more pale, chalky calcite. They spend their days tending the great clusters of melon-sized gas-bladders that buoy up the rotting conduits and tiered walkways, tying fresh root-ropes around swollen bladders so a warming street does not float its own catwalk up into the freezing dark, and puncturing dead, waterlogged sacs before their weight can drag a gantry down. Sometimes a pale, blind scavenger from the empty black water above drifts too close to the warm thermal lid and tumbles over the seam, stunned by the sudden heat and the strange mineral smell. The upper tenders meet it quietly, guiding it back over the rim with long wooden poles or, if it dies in the shock of the brine, dragging it down to the roots of the thickest maroon beds so nothing rots open in the narrow streets.

Between the deep silt-minders and the high catwalk-tenders, messages travel the way everything else does here—without hurry, and without breaking the muffled hush.

Across a distance of several streets, they speak through the iron. A knuckle tapped slowly against a vertical steam-riser sends a muted, metallic cadence ringing down through the pump-houses—three slow knocks, a long pause, two soft ones—felt in the palms and soles long before it reaches any ear. Closer in, within four or five paces, they talk directly through the brine. By flexing a dense pad of conductive, vein-laced tissue beneath the skin of their chests and throats, they send soft, deliberate ripples through the ambient electrical field. To an outsider, it would register only as a faint, shifting tightness in the jaw, a drowsy tingling behind the teeth. To another dweller, those swells, drops, and tiny resistances form an intimate, silent vocabulary—entire conversations conducted face-to-face in the dim amber haze without a single bubble leaving either mouth.

Only in one place do they use voices of air.

Under the overhanging brick eaves, beneath inverted iron wash-tubs they have propped on masonry brackets, and inside the domed bells of flooded stairwells, the tiny silver pearls of oxygen and electrolysis gas gather into flat, trembling puddles of trapped air. The dwellers cultivate these pockets carefully, tilting rusted sheets of corrugated iron to funnel rising bubbles into the best eaves, keeping the silver ceilings broad and clear.

Now and then, two or three of them will climb up a flaking brick facade and push the tops of their heads straight through the quivering, mirror-bright underside of a gas-puddle—just far enough for their mouths and nostrils to break into the shallow pocket of air while their eyes and gill-slits stay submerged in the warm brine below.

The trapped gas is sharp, metallic, rich with oxygen from the fronds and faintly biting on the tongue. Breathing it through a small, dry-walled sac tucked behind their throats gives them a sudden, brief wakefulness—a bright, clear-headed rush that cuts through the sleepy trance of the sump. Up there, with their chins resting on the trembling silver meniscus, they speak aloud in soft, wet clicks, low buzzing hums, and dry breathy murmurs that bounce gently inside the tiny pocket and never carry out into the water. It is where they settle matters that cannot wait for the ten-minute groan of the gears: which alley’s heat-plume is fading, where a heavy hydraulic press is nearing the end of a three-day stroke and must be cleared of stray vines, which bunk-cubby is warm enough for the new young.

The young cannot bear the bare current yet.

Freshly emerged—whether hatched from leathery, copper-flecked capsules tucked behind warm boiler jackets, or borne soft and limp into the quietest wash-cisterns—they are pale as wet parchment, small enough to fit in a cupped palm, and startlingly quick compared to their elders. They dart like translucent minnows or cling like wet burrs to the thick maroon fronds, twitching sharply if they brush against even a mild trickle of voltage. Their elders build insulated nests for them inside the tiny bunk-cubbies or within the empty glass housings of dead floodlamps, lining the cavities with torn rubber gaskets and thick beds of soft, non-conductive olive moss.

There the young stay, peeking out through clouded glass at the drifting amber haze, fed on mashed kelp-sap and tiny scraped slivers of warm biofilm, until their skin begins to thicken from translucent white to pale slate. When an elder judges a young one ready, it lifts it gently from its rubber-lined hollow and carries it down to a dead cast-iron pipe, holding the small, four-fingered hand flat against the cold metal until the next distant groan of the deep gears rolls up through the iron. Days later, it guides a single fingertip onto a low-voltage copper wire, just long enough for the child to feel the drowsy hum settle into its jaw without flinching. Weeks after that, the first pale fleck of verdigris blooms across its knuckle, and it climbs down from the bunk-cubby to walk the stairs on its own.

They keep no monuments, and if they have stories of a dry world above the slate ceiling, they leave no trace of them in the alleys. Yet they move through the tight urban grammar with a quiet, ingrained reverence that feels older than mere habit. They never smash the small glass windows of the bunk-cubbies; when a pane cracks, they seal the seam with thick, resinous sap from the gas-bladders. They coil salvaged cable neatly around old iron hooks when they are done with it. And as they walk past flooded doorways on their slow rounds between the vats and the valve-wheels, a broad, copper-dusted thumb will often reach out without thinking to wipe the ochre slime from a bolted porcelain number-plaque—leaving the white glaze and its dark, forgotten numerals shining faintly in the turbid amber dark, clean for another turn of the slow bronze gears.
