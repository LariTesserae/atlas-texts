You only think the plain is empty because you are looking for something that breathes.

Nothing here pants in the damp heat, nothing flickers at the corner of your eye, nothing flees your boots with a clatter of claws or wings. The air stays wool-thick and quiet, and the miles of wet pavement stretch out unbroken save for those lonely, raw-edged works rising from the mist. So when you see the ripples still widening in a pan of gray slip, or a cast-lead plumb-bob dying down on its knotted cord, you assume a laborer just stepped behind the kiln dome—and when you walk around the kiln and find only ankle-deep pewter water, you assume the place is haunted by a ghost of hands.

Wait by a curving retaining wall instead. Stand still long enough for your boots to sink a hair’s breadth into the weeping mortar, and watch the row of pale timber props bracing the brickwork every ten yards.

Do not look at the first one, or the second. Look at the fourth.

The water around its foot is not lapping against wood. It is wicking upward in a dark, thirsty collar, turning pale chalk-gray to the color of damp river-clay. Follow its long, slightly bowed back up into the dimness where it meets the bulging middle courses of the wall. There is no iron bracket pinning it to the masonry, no mortise cut to receive it. At the top, the beam divides into two broad, flattened palms, lime-whitened and square-fingered, pressed firmly against the wet bricks. Between the two long arms, tucked slightly under the curve of a shoulder that has taken the exact cant of the wall, is a narrow, tilted head.

If you stay quiet—and if the retaining wall finally settles its load, the mortar along its seams giving a faint, satisfied *shuck*—the fourth prop will ease its weight back onto its heels.

Its palms part from the brick with the soft, polite suck of wet mud leaving a board. Two pale handprints remain behind in the damp plaster, crisp down to the cross-hatch of the skin, already beginning to crust with feathery white salt. The figure steps back into the shallow water, straightens—never quite all the way, carrying a permanent stoop from whatever it has been holding up—and bends to retrieve a wooden float lying half-submerged beside a heap of lime.

Once you have seen one, the plain is never empty again.

They are not animals, and they are not statues stirred to a clumsy mimicry of life. They belong to the masonry the way salt-bloom belongs to damp brick, only further along the threshold—as though the endless, headless labor of this place condensed here and there into something tall enough to carry a load and patient enough to wait for mortar to set.

Up close, they have the silhouette of laborers who have spent a lifetime bent over low foundations: broad-hipped, long-armed, knuckles thick and square from pressing clay into molds. Yet there is no skin over their ribs, no blood warming them against the tallow dimness. Their outer substance is damp, dense, and raw-edged, sharing the exact palette of the flats—streaked pale marl, wet ochre terracotta, patches of slick pewter-gray where hot condensation runs down their flanks. Like the long parapets out on the plain, their bodies refuse to cure evenly. One of them may walk past you with a left forearm still soft as greenware, weeping gray slip from the elbow so that it leaves a faint smear whenever its wrist brushes its hip, while its right hand is vitrified dark purple—fired hard as kiln-slag by some forgotten pocket of heat, ringing with a dull metallic *tink* when it taps a flagstone to test the bedding.

Their joints are not concealed beneath smooth flesh. Where a shoulder turns, or where a knee bends into a crouch beside a drainage channel, the clay parts slightly to show what holds them together: damp courses of lapped tile, lengths of coarse hemp cordage lashed in knots that loop straight through the body of the clay without tearing it, and hammered copper staples bridging old drying cracks, already furred with verdigris. Along the creases of their elbows and the backs of their knees, feathery white efflorescence blooms whenever they stand still too long, flaking away in quiet powdery drifts the moment they move.

They have no faces to read, though you are always half-convinced you just missed one. The front of the head is a narrow, slightly canted plane, floated smooth as though a wooden blade drew across it while the clay was still wet, leaving only the faint, pale ridge of the tool’s edge. On some, a shallow hollow sits just above the brow where a thumb once pressed to test the firmness of the mix; on others, two fine hairline fractures run upward from the jaw, weeping warm beads of condensation that climb toward the crown before breaking free as mist.

They do not look at you with eyes. When one becomes aware of your boots on the pavement, it turns that flat, troweled front toward you, and you feel its attention not as a gaze, but as a slight, inexplicable drift in your own balance—the sensation of a heavy load shifting on a deck underfoot, or a floor pitching two degrees off true.

If one bends low beside a cistern and its chest-courses gap a finger’s width at the sternum, you can glimpse why. There are no lungs in there, no tangled guts. The torso is a dark, vaulted cavity, slick with warm moisture, arched inside like the underside of a small kiln. Hanging down the center of that hollow, suspended from the collarbones on a knotted linen string, is a single teardrop of cast lead. Even when the figure stands utterly still, you can feel—rather than hear—the faint, patient swing of that internal plumb-bob settling toward whatever direction counts as *down* in this acre of the flats.

That hanging weight is their heart, their inner ear, and their compass. It is also their sharpest vulnerability.

On a plain where gravity drifts sideways toward windowless turrets and water pools thick on level slabs, staying upright is not a habit; it is a continuous, delicate craft. Leave one of these dwellers unattended in a pocket of crooked geometry, and its own body begins to obey the local error. Its spine stacks along a twenty-degree lean. One hip swells downward into wet slurry while the opposite shoulder dries too fast and spalls off in brittle flakes. If it rests against a warm retaining wall for more than a few dozen breaths, the plain tries to reclaim it—pale mortar beads silently along the seam where its shoulder blade touches the brick, fine threads of lime creep across its calves, and within minutes it is being incorporated into the buttress. You sometimes find a shallow, body-shaped niche set into an otherwise clean wall, rimmed with torn mortar and damp fingerprints, where one of them lingered too long and had to wrench itself loose before the seam cured.

So their entire waking existence is a quiet, unending discipline of maintenance.

You see them wading out into the wide, sunken pans of hot water—never the scalding pockets where the oily film trembles, always the ankle-deep pewter sheets of middling warmth—and standing there motionless while moisture wicks up through their heels, darkening their chalky shins back to supple ochre. They do not eat; they replenish their temper. When a fissure opens along a thigh, they kneel beside a trough of gray slip, scoop a palmful, and pack it smoothly into the crack, drawing the edge of a wooden float down the leg until the seam vanishes. When salt-crust thickens under their arms and threatens to wedge a copper staple loose, they scrape it away gently with a sherd of broken tile, leaving pale dust drifting across the puddles.

And because no one can check the line of their own back, they do not live entirely alone.

They travel the flats in loose, silent pairings or clusters of three and four, drifting between the open water and the lee of half-finished walls. When two meet on a wide flagstone courtyard, there is no greeting you can hear. Instead, they stop a pace apart and *true* each other.

One plants its feet wide, lets its long arms hang loose, and settles its internal weight. The other steps in close, draws a length of knotted hemp from its own hip—sometimes untying a spare loop straight from its own ribs—and hangs a small lead bob from the first one’s collarbone. It waits until the swing dies. Then, with the broad heel of a lime-pale hand, it leans gently against a drifting shoulder, coaxing the damp courses back into square. It scrapes down a fresh bulge of wet clay along the other’s ribs with a flat stick, taps a loose copper staple flush into the nape of the neck (*tick, tick*, polite as cooling terracotta), and presses a warm thumb into the hollow behind the wrist where the clay is still soft enough to take a mark.

There is an astonishing tenderness in it. It is grooming, medicine, and conversation folded into a single act of handwork. When they part, each carries the other’s fresh thumbprints along its arms, and sometimes a small column of four strokes crossed by a fifth, scratched neatly into the leather-hard clay of the forearm.

Those tallies are the closest thing they have to a tongue, and the way they bargain with the plain’s restless arithmetic.

They do not command the landscape—nothing commands the building-squalls that bruise the horizon soot-gray and unmake whole square miles of pavement at a stroke. When that distant mallet-thrum begins to vibrate up through the flagstones, the dwellers read it through their bare soles long before the warm, clay-smelling draft rolls out across the basins. You will see them hurrying then, with a strange, rolling, weight-forward gait, splashing noiselessly through the shallow pans toward the lee of deep-footed kilns or low, broad plinths.

As they move, they touch the walls. A long finger drags across a wet lintel—four strokes, crossed by a fifth—and a drainage trench unzipping across their path pauses, sprouts a single stout herringbone bridge just wide enough for their heels, and holds it steady until the last of them crosses. One steps through a lonely doorway standing out on the flats and pauses with its hand pressed flat against the jamb, scratching a quick, deep notch into the damp plaster so the threshold will not brick itself shut behind its companion. They are not builders in the grand sense; the plain builds itself in blind, dreaming convulsions. They are more like tenders of the small exceptions—coaxing an arch to stay upright for twenty breaths, keeping a single courtyard level enough to stand in, holding a corner of the world square against the tide of its own unmaking.

Yet they do not merely flee the squalls. As soon as a distant storm turns inward and collapses, letting a terraced district slump back into steaming heaps of broken tile and liquid clay, the dwellers turn and wade straight into the warm haze of its aftermath.

They carry shuttering-boards, damp laths, and lengths of knotted cord. Moving knee-deep through the fresh slurry where the plain is drinking a ruined monument back into pavement, they walk slowly, dragging the flat of their palms across the hot mud, feeling for *stubbornness*.

Most of the wreckage flattens out and vanishes without a sound. Once in a while, though, a knot of wet clay refuses to lie down. Something in the violent folding of a collapsing cistern—the accidental meeting of a thumb-pressed brick, a trapped cord, a scrap of copper—leaves a clot of marl that keeps the curve of a knee, or the pitch of a hip, or a hollow that remembers the weight of a plumb-line. It trembles there in the steaming muck, barely taller than a boot, slumping and gathering itself again, unable to hold its own shape for more than three breaths.

The dwellers kneel around it in the warm slurry. They set pale wooden shuttering-boards against its sides to keep it from weeping flat. They pack crushed bits of fired tile—grog, broken from the ruins around them—into its base so it will not shrink and crack as it cools. One of them unhooks a tiny lead weight from its own belt and suspends it from a lath braced over the center of the lump, letting the little plumb-bob hang motionless a hair’s breadth above the wet clay, teaching the new mass which way is down.

Most never take. By the time the steam clears, the boards are holding back nothing but flat gray water, and the dwellers lift their timber and walk away without looking back.

Every few squalls, one holds. It stiffens from slip to greenware, draws its edges crisp enough to nick skin, and begins to carry a faint, answering sway inside its hollow chest. When the shuttering-boards come away, it does not slump. It pulls its raw, shining feet out of the pavement with two soft, wet *pops*, wobbles against the damp flank of the elder that braced it, and takes its first crooked step—smelling sharply of slaked lime and boiled iron, carrying the grain of the timber boards imprinted all down its pale back.

If the slurry is their beginning, the kilns and the dry pockets are their end.

No matter how carefully they soak in the pewter basins or patch their seams with wet clay, time cures them—unevenly, stubbornly, past leather-hard and toward stone. A knee loses its give and locks at a slight bend. A shoulder turns dark, glazed purple, smooth as bottle-glass, ringing faintly when hot rain strikes it. Every stride now risks a deep, irreparable fracture—a sharp *tick* up the shin that no dab of gray slip can mend.

When a dweller has vitrified past the point of bending, it does not crumble in a heap. It goes looking for a difficult place.

It walks—slowly, stiffly, leaning on a companion’s shoulder or bracing itself from pier to pier—until it finds a stretch of the flats where the rules are coming apart worst: a lip where a basin keeps tilting, a junction where water insists on running uphill, an open stretch where no doorway will stay open for more than a minute. There, the old one sets its purple-glazed feet squarely across a fresh mortar seam. It lets the wet lime creep up its ankles without pulling free. Slowly, with a faint creaking of fired clay against copper staples, it raises its arms—either braced outward like a pair of lintels, or bowed forward in the clean curve of a half-arch—and stops.

The mortar cures around its shins. Efflorescence blooms along its vitrified ribs and hardens into a permanent chalk crust. Within a day, or an hour, it is indistinguishable from the plain’s own works: one more leaning pier stepping out into the fog, one more windowless buttress holding up an aqueduct trough that hasn’t yet arrived, one more crooked doorway standing alone on the wet tiles.

Yet they are not quite dead—not in the way a snapped beam is dead. Long after the plain has built and unbuilt whole courtyards around them, younger dwellers still detour miles through the mist to visit a vitrified elder. They step under the stiff, dark-glazed arch of its arms, lay their smooth foreheads against its cool, glassy flank, and stand very still. Deep inside the vitrified stone, sealed forever behind courses that will never part again, the old lead weight still hangs true. Against that buried steadiness, the living ones check their own drift, adjust their cords, and walk on.

There is one other thing they do, though you will only see it if you are walking near one when the ground beneath you pulls apart.

Most of the time, the pavement seems seamless and whole. Then two wide sections ease away from each other without a sound, opening a raw, steaming slot no wider than a handspan. You peer down and feel your stomach turn at the sight of inverted bricks, wet laths, and knotted cords hanging in a tallow dimness that mirrors the sky above.

The dwellers do not recoil from the seam. They kneel right at its weeping lip, bracing their palms on the hot stone so they do not slide in, and tilt their smooth heads down over the crack.

From the dimness underneath, other hands are already reaching up.

They look like the hands up here—broad-palmed, square-fingered, lime-bleached—except that they emerge from the underside of the slabs, wrists bending upward out of the soffit, knuckles crusted with sulfur-yellow salt from whatever hot mist pools down there. Their thumbs are worn slick from smoothing mortar upward against gravity; water drips off their elbows toward the ceiling they stand on.

No sound passes between them, only a brief, patient exchange across the narrow mouth of the floor. A dweller from above lowers a knotted hemp cord into the seam; fingers from below catch it, pull four slow tugs and a fifth sharp draw across the lip, and send back a hammered copper staple, or a wedge of dressed mudstone cut to an angle that makes no sense on this side of the tiles. Sometimes a hand from above simply clasps a hand from below—wet clay pressing against wet clay, two raw thumbs checking the temper of the other’s grip—as though confirming that the underside has not given way, that someone is still walking upside-down along the ceilings of the abyss, pinning the endless masonry together from beneath so the whole continental floor does not unzip and spill into whatever lies beyond the lime-scented dark.

Then the slabs begin to drift back. The hands below withdraw into the inverted haze. The seam closes with a quiet, wet kiss, and a fresh bead of pale mortar squeezes up along the join, smoothing itself flat—though whether stroked by a thumb from above or by one working patiently along the underside, there is no way to tell.

The dweller rises from its knees, brushes a trace of white salt from its shins, and steps back toward the shadow of a kiln.

If you blink—if you look down for a moment at your own damp footprints, or glance toward a distant squall bruising the ochre fog—the courtyard seems empty again. There is only the low retaining wall, its row of crooked props, a shallow pan of gray slip whose surface carries a fresh ring of ripples, and a lead plumb-bob hanging from a linen cord, swinging in a tight, dying circle.

Yet if you step closer to the damp retaining wall before you walk on, you may notice a single new mark pressed into the wet clay at the height of your chest: four vertical strokes, crossed neatly by a fifth, still glistening with warm brine.

You have been tallied. And behind you, out in the pewter mist, the fourth timber prop shifts its broad, flat palms half an inch along the brick, holding the wall steady until you pass.
