Anyone who climbs down through the sandstone throat and stands for an hour on one of the swept balconies will leave convinced the hollow is empty.

They look, as wet creatures always do, for wet signs: the dart of a shadow across a sunlit flagstone, the glint of an eye in a dark doorway, a smear of droppings beneath an eave, a footprint pressed into the rippled grit of the plazas. They listen to the furnace-roar climbing from the sunken floor and hear only weather. They watch the wooden louvers flip in ragged bursts and the heavy copper baffles ring against their stone jambs, and assume the city is a carcass left rattling in the draft.

They are looking at the wrong rhythm, and they are looking for the wrong kind of flesh.

Stay through three lulls. Stop scanning the bare floors—nothing that lives here ever sets foot on the open sand—and fix your eyes instead on the undersides of the timber bridges, the tangled crowns of the wind-towers, and the dense, thirty-foot curtains of soot-green vines. Wait until the air drops into its tense, whining quiet. Everything hangs slack and dead.

Then the deep thermal pocket breaks loose from the bedrock below. The heat spikes. The roar swells up the terraced walls.

Watch that dry, crooked tangle of oil-dark stalks wedged beneath the lintel of a fourth-story gallery. During the lull it was indistinguishable from old rigging and dead brush. As the superheated wave strikes it, two small, parchment-thin vanes set along its upper ribs catch the rush and spin—a blur too small to separate from the shivering fronds around it. Inside its jointed frame, you can almost hear a crisp, rapid ratcheting, lost beneath the general clatter of the city. Its center softens in the sudden heat. Three slender, segmented booms unhook from the masonry, unfold across the narrow width of the stepped street, and brace effortlessly from wall to wall.

One long limb reaches up and catches a heavy copper baffle-plate *just* before the gale can slam it into the stone. It holds the plate still for half a beat, lets it go on a different rhythm—*ting, pause, tang*—and then folds seamlessly back into the hanging vines as the surge begins to fade.

That is who lives here.

---

They are not animals. There is not a drop of water in them, no blood to boil, no lung to scorch, no gut to leave a trace in the corners of the clay rooms. If you broke one open, no sap would run, no marrow would spill; only the pungent, turpentine scent of warm resin, a puff of fragrant amber dust, and the snap of dry wood and taut fiber.

They have nothing of the human shape, though they inhabit a city built by hands that once were. A mature one is as wide across as a person is tall, yet weighs no more than a bundle of seasoned cane. At its core sits a dense, dark knot of pitch—hard as amber in the cooler moments of a lull, turning warm and supple as beeswax whenever the furnace-draft surges upward. Nested inside that pitch-knot is a hollow, gourd-like chamber baked from pale, fiber-tempered clay and wrapped in layers of waxy bark, pierced with narrow, slit-like vents that widen and pinch with the heat.

Radiating unevenly from this center are four, five, or six jointed limbs made from the same hollow, segmented stalks that crowd the avenues. They have no muscles. Instead, each segment is strung through with twisted skeins of horn-hard vine-cord, anchored to tiny counter-ratchets of dense wood and spun taut by small parchment-frond vanes whenever the hot wind blows. The wind literally winds them up. During a strong thermal surge, their internal cords coil tight with stored tension; during the lull, they spend that tension sparingly, moving with a slow, clicking economy—or locking themselves rigid into the timberwork to wait out the quiet.

Where their limbs meet the world, they are tipped not with claws or fingers, but with patient craft: narrow wedges of dark slate bound into their grips, fine tufts of the bristling bronze-wire moss, and scavenged slivers of hand-hammered copper, curved like calipers and worn silver-bright along their inner edges.

They have no eyes. The weak blades of ochre light falling through the basalt roof-slits mean almost nothing to them, except as shifting patches of warmth along the upper ledges. They know their world through vibration, through draft, through chemistry, and through spark.

Every hollow chamber in their cores is tuned like the body of a dry drum. Through their braced tips they feel the deep geological boom of the sunken floor, the exact pitch of the air whistling up a clay flue three terraces away, the tension of a timber bridge groaning under its own vine-weight. Their bronze-wire crests read the drifting haze—tasting the turpentine sting of a split root, the sweet yellow fog of lichen spores, the sharp, dangerous acridity of a leaf beginning to smolder over a hot bedrock crack.

And for the quickness of their thoughts—for the sharp, sudden impulses that let them catch a swinging plate or dodge a falling stone—they drink from the sand-chutes.

Watch them along the eaves when the surge peaks and the shallow stone channels hiss with pouring pumice-dust. Three or four of them will hang down from the timber beams like gaunt, wooden spiders, dipping the tips of their limbs into the dry, rushing grit. In the bone-dry inferno of the hollow, the friction of fine pumice pouring over stone raises a fierce, invisible crackle—the kind of dry static that lifts hair and snaps between knuckles in winter air. They trail fine threads of charred, blackened vine-fiber and bright copper points through the hissing stream, gathering that dry charge into themselves with tiny, almost invisible ticks of pale spark. It runs along their blackened inner fibers in an instant, waking their joints, sharpening their sense of the wind, lighting them from within like struck flint before bleeding gently away into the stone.

Water would kill them in an afternoon. Dampness would swell their horn-hard cords until their frames split, turn the pumice-chutes to choking mud, and steal every spark from the air. Dryness is their breath; friction is their blood; the roaring updraft is the beat of their hearts.

---

Because their motion rides inside the motion of the hollow, an outsider hears chaos where there is, in fact, an old and intricate conversation.

The wind-works of the city do not merely rattle at random. When the thermal wave rises, the louvers along the tall wind-towers want to flip in a predictable sequence, and the copper plates strung across the stone jambs want to hammer in a steady, mechanical din. The dwellers perch among the slatted towers and timber galleries, letting the gale do the heavy work while they lightly edit it—damping a copper plate with a pitch-coated pad, propping a wooden shutter closed for two heartbeats, plucking a taut vine-rope so it thrums against the masonry.

Across the three-hundred-pace void of shimmering air, the districts on the opposite rim feel those tiny deviations through their hollow ribs and vibrating clay drums. In the next surge, minutes later, they answer.

A single greeting, a warning about a loosening retaining wall, or a quiet argument over the angle of a gallery's reed screens may unfold across half an hour of pulsing gusts—unhurried, layered, braided so tightly into the furnace-roar that no wet ear could ever separate speech from storm.

And it is not idle talk. The balance of the city—the strange, settled endurance that keeps the ancient dry-laid stone from collapsing into rubble—is their continuous, collective labor.

Left to itself, the vigorous resin-flora would pry the terraces apart and spill them into the central abyss. Left to itself, drifting pumice would choke the settling pits and blind the chimneys, suffocating the very drafts that keep the hollow alive. So the dwellers tend the bowl with a patience measured in decades.

They do not build new houses; they have no use for flat floors or closed rooms, though they carefully angle the reed screens so the drafts keep the bare interiors scoured clean and singing at the right note. Instead, they maintain the bones of the place. When a stone block loosens under the heave of a growing root, two of them will wedge themselves across the narrow alley during a surge, tapping thin slate shims back into the seam and pressing warm beads of freshly nicked pitch along the joint to seal it tight. When a vine grows too thick and threatens to pull a timber balcony down, they do not hack at it—they have no heavy blades—but braid a younger, tougher cord from an opposing stem to pull the load back into balance, or pack a pinch of caustic amber-spore into the offending fissure to turn the root aside.

They are the reason the copper rubbing-edges gleam bright amid centuries of brown patina: they polish them with grit and wire-moss so the plates ring clean and hold a keen spark.

One of their strangest and most delicate crafts happens only at the lowest terraces, overhanging the cracked bedrock floor. They never step onto the flat sand plazas—there is nothing for them there, and too close to the hottest floor-fissures, even their pitch-joints soften past holding and their parchment vanes curl to ruin. Instead, they lean out from the lowermost stone ledges on braided harnesses and lower long, thin strands of raw vine-fiber into the superheated air shimmering just above the cracks. They hold the fibers right at the threshold of heat where fallen leaves smolder without flame, listening intently to the faint hiss, drawing the strands back up the exact instant they turn to supple black lace—scorched just enough to carry a dry spark cleanly, not so far that they crumble to ash. Those charred threads are prized above almost anything else; they weave them carefully into their own cores, and into the cores of their young.

---

They are not born as animals are born, nor built as machines are built. They are grown, and then tuned.

Look inside one of the cistern-shaped silos built into the terraced rock—vessels that have never held a cup of water in all the long history of this place. Peer down through the slatted hatch at the top during a surge, and you will see why they exist. Each silo is tapped at its base by a narrow stone flue from the floor and ringed near its lip by angled pumice-chutes. Inside, the dark cylindrical chamber is a controlled cyclone of warm air, swirling mineral dust, and crisp static crackle.

This is where the dwellers bring the choicest winged seeds caught in mid-air from detonating pods, along with clumps of bright amber lichen, curls of charred fiber, and a single tiny sliver of copper or slate. They drop them into the dry vortex.

In the quiet dark of the silo's wall-niches, fed by warm bituminous vapors seeping through the stone and jostled endlessly by the charged dust, a few of those seeds do not grow into ordinary creeping vines. Guided by the elders who reach down periodically to twist a sprouting stem, press a pinch of warm pitch around a fork, or bind a fledgling clay shell onto a swelling node, they grow into **kernels**—small, hollow, three-spoked things no wider than an open hand, all parchment frond and airy cane.

When a kernel's cords tighten enough to pull itself free of its silo-niche, it climbs to the upper wind-towers and lets go.

For their first long season of life, the young do not live on the walls at all. They live in the empty center of the bowl. They are the ones you might mistake, in a dim shaft of ochre light, for a particularly stubborn flurry of torn leaves or a drift of ash-grey seeds. Light as paper, unfurling translucent fronds veined in dull copper, they ride the roaring central column of superheated air—soaring up toward the sagging basalt roof, peeling outward along the cool stone ceiling, tumbling through glowing ribbons of yellow spores, and catching the rising draft again before they drop too low.

In that bright-edged, roaring emptiness, they learn the invisible architecture of the hollow: every shear-line where a cold pocket drops from a roof-slit, every fierce eddy spinning off a promontory, every booming thermal column rising from the deep cracks.

Many do not survive this drifting youth. A sudden collapse of the draft between cycles can drag a slow or misshapen young one down toward the baking bedrock. There is no cry, no struggle that leaves a mark on the sand; the heat catches its thin vanes over a floor-fissure, it curls inward with a faint, dry hiss into delicate black lace, and the next gust scatters it completely into the grit.

Those that endure grow heavier. Their pitch-knots thicken; their segmented spokes harden and darken with resin; their parchment sails shred away to fringes as they begin catching hold of the outer vine-curtains, hauling themselves into the terraced quarters to receive their first wedged slate tips and copper hooks from the tenders of the wall.

---

And when they grow old—when decades of kiln-heat have baked their pitch-joints too stiff to flex smoothly, and their hollow stalks have turned dense and brittle as horn—they do not fall to the floor to leave a bone or a husk behind.

They go into the walls.

An aging dweller climbs slowly down to the retaining terraces, to the deep clay flues built inside the masonry, or to a cracked archway where two promontories meet. There, during a long surge when its pitch is as soft as it will ever be again, it presses its angular frame flush against the warm stone. Slowly, over weeks of cycles, it unbraids the lower cords of its limbs and drives tough, woody tendrils straight into the dry-laid joints and deep down into the bituminous fissures beneath the foundations. Its outer parchment flakes away into dust. Its hollow, clay-and-bark core fuses seamlessly with the brick and timber around it, sealed in place by a final, heavy weeping of dark resin that hardens to stone.

To an outsider, it is now nothing more than a gnarled knot of root clasping a retaining wall, or a strange, fluted swell inside a chimney shaft.

Yet it is not dead.

Rooted directly into the mineral chemistry of the bedrock and bathed continuously in the superheated rush of air climbing up the wall-flues, these anchored elders become the slow, immovable memory of the city. They no longer wind themselves to walk or leap; they have no need of spark-quick reflexes. Instead, their hollow bodies become permanent acoustic chambers built into the very ribs of the architecture. The roaring heat passes straight through them, turning them into low, brooding resonators that hum at a depth no human ear could distinguish from the furnace-boom of the earth itself.

They feel everything that moves too slowly for the nimble wall-tenders to notice: the hairline widening of a basalt fissure fifty feet overhead, the century-long sag of a sandstone terrace, the gradual cooling of one floor-crack and the awakening of another, the slow seasonal drift of the ochre sunbeams across the bowl.

During the quiet lulls, you may sometimes see a mobile dweller—one of the swift, copper-tipped tenders—flatten itself motionless against the smooth stone face of a retaining wall or thrust two of its long cane limbs deep into the mouth of a warm clay flue. It is not resting. It is listening. Through the steady vibration of the stone, the rooted elder passes up its slow counsel: *the bridge on the north rim is pulling heavy; the east chute is silting fine; the fissure beneath the third silo is warming.*

And perhaps, deepest in the oldest retaining walls—where the masonry is smoothed by long-vanished footsteps and the pitch has turned almost to coal—the most ancient elders still hold a dim, vibrating memory of the wet ones who first dressed these blocks with hammer and chisel. They remember them not as masters or gods, only as strange, heavy, leaking shapes that once moved clumsily across the flat plazas without waiting for the wind, leaving damp salt on the flagstones and mud in the seams, until the basin dried, the ground warmed, and the soft ones either crumbled or climbed back up through the sandstone throat into the glare.

To the dry ones, it is an old, faint shiver in the stone—hardly more than a cautionary note about wetness and silence.

---

They have their own pleasures, too, though they are as alien as their bodies.

Now and again, when the dim day above the basalt ceiling shifts just right and a single, unusually bright blade of ochre light cuts slantwise down through the empty center of the hollow, a ripple of quick, deliberate clacks runs from tower to tower around the entire rim. In the next surge, every district adjusts its louvers in concert.

They bend the gale.

Instead of rushing straight up the walls to vanish through the roof-slits, the superheated currents catch each other sideways, curling inward across the three-hundred-pace gulf until the empty middle of the bowl begins to turn. A vast, slow vortex forms right around the dusty shaft of ochre light. High along the balconies, the dwellers shake the taut vine-curtains and tap the swollen pods; the air fills with a spinning column of silver-grey winged seeds, glowing ribbons of yellow amber spores, and dozens of papery young drifters spiraling effortlessly around the lit pillar, flashing copper and ochre as they bank through the dimness.

Every reed screen around the bowl thrums in a single deep chord. Every copper baffle rings in slow, staggered unison, sending waves of metallic shimmer chasing each other around the curved basalt roof. For three or four minutes, the entire city sings to itself, bathing its dry ribs in a storm of its own tuning, before the thermal pocket breaks apart, the vortex unravels toward the ceiling vents, and the hollow drops back into its whining lull.

If you ever do step down into that scorching dimness—sweating, blinking, your lips cracking in the fierce dry air, your boots printing heavy, clumsy hollows into the rippled grit of a plaza—you will never see that dance.

The instant the strange, heavy rhythm of your footsteps vibrates up through the flagstones, a tiny, dry whisper of muted baffles will ripple out ahead of you faster than you can walk. The wall-tenders will fold their segmented booms flat against the oil-dark beams and lock their ratchets tight. The copper hooks will tuck behind soot-green bark. The papery young will scatter high into the dark roof-shadows, indistinguishable from drifting chaff. Even the louvers will be left wholly to the wind, clacking in bare, unedited disorder.

You will stand on an empty balcony, looking across an empty gulf, listening to the roaring heat and marveling at a forgotten city that runs on without witnesses. Above your head, wedged tight into the dry-laid eaves, six taut, resin-blooded frames will hang completely still, smelling the dangerous dampness evaporating off your skin, waiting patiently for the strange, leaking ghost to climb back up into the sun—so that on the very next surge, they can unwind their cords and go back to keeping the fire in tune.
