You can walk for weeks across the crown of the world, passing braided hollows and knotted lines and sagging canopy cradles, and remain entirely convinced that whoever shaped them vanished long ago—that you are wandering through the slow ruin of an extinct patience.

Then one dim afternoon, beside a mirror-pool where white feeder-roots dangle into dark tea, a curve of the mossy arch twenty paces away shifts its weight. A drop falls from what you took to be a knot of shed stem-skin. Two dark eyes, wide and unblinking as the pool itself, regard you from beneath a heavy brow that sheds condensation cleanly to either side.

They have been watching you since you stepped off the firmer root-ridge a quarter of an hour ago. They did not hide; they simply did not move, and in a landscape where stillness is the native grammar, you looked straight through them.

If they ever stood at full height, they would be as tall as a lowland human, perhaps taller, yet you rarely see one fully unbent. Their spines hold a natural, easy forward curve, like the living bridges they walk, balanced low over long, narrow hips. Their ribcages are deep and remarkably long, built to draw huge, unhurried breaths from the thin, warm air of the plateau; when one breathes, the whole torso expands and settles over ten quiet seconds, matching the sluggish rhythm of the fermenting peat beneath their soles.

Their skin is pale as cellar-wax or wet limestone, faintly translucent at the inner wrists and along the hollow of the throat, where slow, dark veins show through like the ribs of a young leaf. There is almost no hair on their heads or limbs—thick hair holds sour water and catches in hanging bladder-moss—only a sparse, velvety down along the shoulders, forearms, and spine that beads the mist into tiny silver droplets, much like the cilia of the pillar-trunks. A faint, clean sheen covers them from brow to heel, partly their own skin-oil and partly a clear resin pressed from pale wax-shoots, rubbed in daily to keep the perpetual damp from softening their hide to ruin.

It is their hands and feet that tell you most clearly what sort of place made them. Their soles are broad, thick-padded, and unusually supple, the toes long and widely splayed so their weight spreads over the floating peat-mat without breaking the moss. Their palms are similarly cushioned, unscarred by sharp edges, ending in long fingers whose middle joints are broad and powerfully muscled. Their nails are neither claws nor thin plates, but thick, blunt, pale spoons—tools grown directly from the body for prizing loose lime-mud, scraping algae, and coaxing stubborn shoots without tearing the rind.

Over their shoulders, most wear loose mantles felted from dried moss and overlapping strips of shed stem-skin, stained the same smoky plum and wet slate as the arches around them. The mantles do not keep them dry—nothing stays dry here—they merely channel the slow drip away from the hollow of the throat and offer warm pockets against the chest for carrying small, necessary things.

To see one move is to feel your own pulse drop in involuntary imitation. They do not stride. They advance with the same liquid, testing delicacy as the felt-walkers, lifting one broad foot, pausing while the peat-tea clears from its print, and setting it down heel-to-toe along the firmer root-ridges before committing their weight. Through the thick pads of their soles, they listen constantly to the floor—feeling the subtle, hollow tremble of a dark water-vein beneath thin crust, or the distant, muffled thump of another traveler thirty yards away along the same fused root. Climbing is no different from walking to them; they simply tilt forward, lay their long palms flat against the ribbed, barkless hide of an arching trunk, and flow upward into the tiers of dripping crimson membranes, never jerking, never slipping, never dislodging so much as a hanging veil-drifter.

They do not have a spoken name for themselves that travels across open air. Sound dies quickly in this wet, cushioned density, and a shout would feel to them like a wound—an ugly tearing of the skin of the world. Their small, close-folded ears are tuned instead to low, intimate frequencies: the deep subterranean sigh of gas venting from a peat-chimney, the slow groan of an ancient arch settling half an inch closer to the floor, the slight change in the pitch of dripping water when a large body stands nearby.

When two of them meet—which may happen only once in a long stretch of weeks—they do not call out. They pause several paces apart, letting the slow drift of mist carry scent and stillness between them. Then they step close, bow their heads so that forehead touches shoulder or palm rests flat against collarbone, and begin to speak.

Their language is made of low, warm resonance felt bone-to-bone: deep, vowel-less hums that rise in the long chest and vibrate straight through the other’s ribs, punctuated by faint breath-clicks at the lips and deliberate, shifting pressures of the fingertips along the forearm. Standing ten yards away, an observer would hear nothing at all—only see two pale, cloaked figures leaning together in the pewter twilight like a pair of trunks in the slow act of fusing, condensation ticking quietly off their elbows.

Yet their true conversation spans the entire continent, and takes place across decades.

Follow one as they approach a stretched, rot-resistant vine hung between two pillar-trunks. They do not glance at it; they raise one damp hand, close their wide dark eyes, and walk slowly alongside the cord, letting the swollen knots and threaded beads slip through their fingers. Every change in texture is a syllable. A tight double-knot followed by two bleached seed-husks means an aerial bowl forty paces northeast is deep and sweet, or a braided hollow lies dry and sound beyond the next basin. A broad, loose twist beside a naturally perforated river-pebble warns that the peat-crust has thinned over a deep water-vein, or that a great canopy bridge is nearing the end of its centuries and beginning to sag. A tiny ring of bone, slipped carefully between two knots, records a death—and the subtle shaping of its edges, worn smooth against wet shale by a particular hand, tells anyone who knew that person whose breath has returned to the buried hearth.

Because nothing rots quickly here, and nothing blows away, a single vine-line may hold a hundred years of passing travelers. Now and then a person stops beside a line that has grown furry with fine moss, pinches the green velvet gently away between thumb and forefinger, and reads a message tied before they were born. If a boggy crossing has thickened again over the intervening decades, they will patiently work the old warning knot loose, slide the pebble further down the cord, and leave the line honest for whoever comes next.

They are few—perhaps a few thousand scattered across a living crown the size of a lowland sea—and they do not build villages. To stay in one place for years would sour the moss beneath their feet and strip the nearby trunks of their tenderest shoots. Instead, they move in vast, unhurried circuits that may take a decade or half a lifetime to complete, walking alone, in quiet pairs, or in shifting little clusters of three or four that join for a season and part without ceremony at a fork in the knotted lines.

That is why the braided hollows always look half-tended and half-forgotten. No one weaves a shelter for tonight. When a traveler comes upon a promising warm peat-vent beside a mirror-pool, they may spend two dim afternoons coaxing down a ring of young, pale aerial roots from the overhead arch, warming each supple shoot between their palms until it bends without bruising, and binding the first crossings with strips of soaked stem-skin. Then they walk on. Five years later, another traveler—someone who may never meet the first—passes the same pool, sees the intent in those bound shoots, bends in the new side-growth, and packs a first thin floor of pale calcareous mud gathered from the chimneys of the blind peat-burrowers. Ten years after that, a third hands-on circuit thickens the walls. By the time the braided ribs swell and fuse into a muscular, crouching-height dome with a dry lime-packed floor, three generations of hands have shaped it, and any traveler who arrives wet and tired may fold their long limbs inside its tear-shaped cleft and sleep in dry warmth.

Everything they make obeys this same refusal to hurry or to sever. They have no metal, no fire, and no blades—not because some ancient law forbade them, though perhaps once there was a reason, but because they live inside a single continuous body. You do not strike sparks on a continent made of fermenting peat. You do not hack open the skin of a living arch when an open wound invites black weeping rot that can bring down a vault a century later. Even the tiny bone-rings strung upon the guide-lines are never sawn or chipped; they are shaped over months of quiet travel, rubbed patiently against flat slabs of wet shale and limestone grit until a weathered, hollow bone—gathered from an ancient felt-walker long after it lay down to sink into the moss—separates cleanly into smooth, pale circles.

The felt-walkers know them well. Nothing here is penned or driven, yet when a herd of the tall, algae-furred grazers stands motionless beside a black pool, a traveler can walk straight through their midst without a single splayed foot lifting in alarm. Sometimes a person pauses beside an older grazer, reaches up with unhurried tenderness, and plucks a tangled knot of heavy thread-fern from behind its smooth ear-hollow, or offers a pinch of pale burrower-lime from a curled palm for the beast to lip away with its pleated muzzle. The grazers are fellow walkers of the same yielding floor; the people never kill them, and only take their bones after the damp and the dark peat have finished with them.

They eat the way the plateau itself eats: by warmth, water, and patient transformation. In the dim aisles they gather the pale, crisp wax-shoots that push up around the roots, swollen subterranean tubers prized cleanly out of the moss with a blunt bone rib, and the translucent, mild-tasting flesh of certain fungi that bloom like pale ears along older arches. Now and then they lift a slow, gill-laced amphibian from an aerial pond, or peel a handful of sheet-slugs from the underside of a crimson membrane, always leaving the greater part of the cluster undisturbed so its faint green flicker still ripples overhead.

Most of all, they use the buried hearth beneath their feet. A traveler will mash fallen gourd-flesh, bitter moss-berries, and sweet stem-pith inside a broad, folded leaf-skin, tie the bundle neatly with root-fiber, and tuck it two feet down into the fever-warm peat beside a fermenting vent, marking the spot with a single bruised, knotted tendril. Weeks or months later, whoever passes that mark—the maker or another—reaches down into the dark warmth and draws up a steaming, sour-sweet cake transformed entirely without flame. They wash it down with clear, pale condensate dipped from the high leaf-axils in hollowed gourd-shells, saving the dark, bitter tea of the floor-pools for when their joints ache or when they wish to sit motionless for a whole dim cycle and think long thoughts.

Because their bodies burn so slowly, and because neither winter nor famine nor harsh sun ever wears them down, they live for an immense stretch of time—a century and a half, sometimes two—measuring their age not by years, which have no meaning beneath an unchanging cloud-deck, but by growth. An elder remembers when a massive fused pillar was still three separate saplings braided by their grandmother; they know which mirror-pools have narrowed by two paces since their youth, and which knotted lines have had to be moved thrice as a subterranean water-vein slowly shifted course.

Children are rare and extraordinarily quiet. They are born high above the floor in the sagging bog-clay cradles tucked inside the oldest canopy forks, well above the heavy floor-vapors and within arm’s reach of the cleanest aerial cisterns. For their first years they ride slung inside a parent’s stem-skin mantle, pressed against the steady, slow drum of the chest-hum, their small broad hands reaching out to touch the silver cilia of passing trunks and the cool, swollen knots of the vine-lines. They do not run or shout as lowland children might; their play is a patient absorption—coaxing tiny pale mosses into spiral mats, floating bleached seed-husks across a leaf-bowl to watch the miniature tides of falling drops, or pressing their ears flat against a great arch to count the passing footsteps of felt-walkers forty feet below.

Once in a great while—not on any calendar, for there are none, but perhaps when a colossal ancient vault finally completes its centuries-long slump to the floor, opening a rare well of brighter pewter light, or when some slow tremor in the buried peat shifts a dozen familiar basins—small bands begin to converge. Word moves ahead of them along the knotted lines, pebble added beside husk, until thirty or forty of them gather around a wide, stacked shale ledge beside one of the oldest mirror-pools.

Even then, there is no clamor. They sit shoulder to shoulder along the velvet-creeping stones, knees drawn up, passing hollowed gourds of thick, sweet fermented sap that smells faintly of bruised plums and wet chalk. Together they slip into a long, shared hum—a low chord built from dozens of deep, unhurried chests that carries poorly through the damp air yet travels cleanly through the shale and into the black water, setting the entire surface of the mirror-pool trembling in tiny, concentric rings. Between those long chords, they mend one another’s felted mantles, exchange river-pebbles and shaped bone-rings, tie the first knots of new infants onto the central lines, and share the slow histories of the crown: which arches are weakening, where a new herd of felt-walkers has drifted, whose hands finished the hollow by the double-cistern, and who has recently gone down into the moss.

For death, too, is simply a yielding. When one of them grows very old—their pale skin grown thick and faintly mottled with harmless silver-green flecks, their long fingers stiffened until they can no longer coax a tender shoot without snapping it—they feel their breath begin to shorten and cool. They do not linger in the dry braided hollows. Guided by companions, or walking alone on their last circuit, they seek out a quiet hollow where the moss-crust is deep and pale, or the soft edge of a still basin fringed with white feeder-roots.

There they lie down on their back, folding their long hands cleanly over their deep ribs. If companions are with them, they press their foreheads one last time to the cooling collarbone, wait until the slow tide of the breath ceases altogether, and then draw a loose blanket of living velvet moss across the body. Nothing is dug, nothing is burned. Over seasons, the warm damp claims them; the pale roots thread gently through the mantle, and the body sinks millimeter by millimeter into the dark, fermenting strata below, becoming part of the ancient buried hearth that keeps the whole sky-high world fever-warm.

Only twice in a long lifetime do most of them leave the dim, weeping aisles altogether.

Some make the long pilgrimage outward toward the unseen rims of the plateau—walking for months until the pillar-trunks stunt and thicken, the living peat thins to a ragged hem, and the ancient dissolved limestone finally shows its pale, cold teeth above a sheer drop into churning white cloud. That is the only place where the smooth, naturally perforated river-pebbles are found, pried gently from dead stone channels carved epochs ago by water that still hurried. They never linger at the rim. Out there, the wind pushes rudely against their mantles, the light is raw and glaring, and the air smells sharply of distance and cold. They fill a small pouch with worn stones—relics of a world that had edges—and turn back with quiet relief into the warm, shadowless shelter of the vaults.

More often, and for no practical reason at all, a solitary traveler will spend a dim afternoon ascending the broad, sloped backs of the oldest fused trunks, climbing tier after tier through hanging bat-wing membranes until they breach the upper roof of the canopy.

Up there, standing alone on the rolling sea of velvet-dark domes where the horizon dissolves into warm white cloud, they unfasten their wet mantle and lie flat upon their back, spreading their long limbs wide over the breathing skin of the continent. They do not look for a sun they have no word for, nor scan for stars they have never imagined. They simply let the afternoon mist rise warm past their ribs, feel the heavy pewter cloud gather overhead, and wait through the ash-violet dusk as the whole vast sky settles gently back down upon their face—listening, with every quiet bone, to the single ancient organism of which they are the smallest, gentlest, and most patient part.
