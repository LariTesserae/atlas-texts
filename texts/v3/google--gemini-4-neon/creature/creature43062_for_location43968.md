You can walk half a kilometer down a cork-paved causeway before you realize you are already being watched.

It is not that the people of the basin hide. They simply belong to the same pale, matte register as everything else here. Against a half-grown refinery wrapped in cream-colored bark, a crew of four framers bolted to the upper ironwork reads at first glance like a cluster of dry seed-pods or folded canvas shrouds. Only when one of them shifts—reaching out with a long, remarkably light arm to guide a climbing vine-shoot into a ceramic collar—does the geometry resolve into living posture.

They are tall and spare, built with the same thrifty engineering as their architecture. Beneath loose tunics of pressed reed-cloth and felt sheared from the meadow-grazers, their frames are narrow-chested and buoyant, supported by hollow, honeycombed bone that is rigid under load and nearly weightless. A full-grown surveyor carries the height of a tall human and less than half the mass. When they step off an elevated viaduct onto the springy turf, they land with a faint, dry rebound and almost no sound at all—their soft cork-soled boots fitted at the heel with a flat tongue of braided copper that trails lightly against the mat, bleeding static cleanly into the substrate with every stride.

Up close, the first thing that unsettles an outsider is the utter absence of moisture about them.

There is no sheen on their skin, no mist on their breath in the cool valley air, no wet gleam along the rim of an eye. Their skin is continuous, matte, and fine-grained—like heavy, unbleached vellum or dry birch-skin sanded smooth—ranging from chalk-pale in the young to the warm, seasoned ochre of old cedar in the elders. Along the wrists, ankles, and throat, where the body must bend most often, it folds into neat, permanent pleats rather than stretching. If a framer slips on raw basalt and slices a forearm open, no red blood wells out; instead, a slow bead of clear, pale-amber resin rises to the edge of the cut, smelling sharply of crushed juniper and warm pine-pitch, and cures into a hard, glossy seal within minutes.

Their biology runs on the same dry chemistry as the basin itself. No water circulates through them. Beneath the papery hide, a network of fine, pressurized vessels carries a thin, volatile terpene oil that never freezes, never evaporates, and never spoils. Their breathing is quiet and unhurried—a slow lateral expansion of pleated thoracic ribs that draws cool air across dry, honeycombed chambers to burn compressed starches and dense seed-oils, releasing a faint, warm exhalation scented like toasted anise and dry chalk.

Their faces are long, calm, and sparely featured. Where wet-bodied creatures carry soft, blinking lids and tear ducts, the basin-folk have wide, dark-amber eyes sealed beneath hard, transparent lenses like polished quartz. Every few minutes, a dry, translucent inner membrane sweeps swiftly sideways across the lens to clear drifting pollen or gypsum dust, leaving the surface immaculate. They have no mammalian hair; instead, dense fringes of fine, silver-gray keratin filaments grow along the temples, the nape of the neck, and the backs of the forearms.

Those filaments are never entirely still. In the bone-dry air of the cavern, they act as living electroscopes. Long before a driverless freight bogie glides into view along a viaduct, or before a distant transformer begins to leak charge across its ceramic ribs, the pale tufts along a worker’s cheekbones lift and lean toward the current. Two people standing near a live conduit can tell the mood and health of the grid simply by watching the quiet halo of raised filaments around each other’s wrists.

---

Because the porous floor and fibrous walls swallow sound so greedily, the inhabitants have grown into a people of two languages: one intimate, and one metallic.

Up close, they speak in low, dry registers that barely carry ten paces. They have no wet tongues or mucous-lined throats; sound is made by forcing air past taut, parchment-thin vocal reeds set high in the collarbone, shaped by clicking ridges along the palate. To listen to two maintenance workers talking over a parked electric scooter is to hear a brisk, musical rustle—something between wind combing dry sedge-grass and the soft, rapid tap of wooden knitting needles, punctuated by warm, resonant hums drawn up from their hollow chests.

Across distance, voices are useless. Shouting into the cork-lined hollow only exhausts the chest and dies in the turf twenty meters away. Instead, the basin converses through its bones. Every worker carries a small ceramic striker tucked into a belt-loop. To call a partner down from the fourth tier of a bare iron armature, a framer taps a crisp rhythm against a bolted girder; the blackened steel carries the dry *tink-tink-clack* cleanly through the entire skeleton, vibrating right into the palms and boot-soles of everyone clipped to the frame. Out along the rail viaducts, signalers tap coded messages directly into the dark-gray rails, and whole construction districts sometimes chatter for minutes in bright, crystalline percussion while the air above them remains completely hushed.

There is another kind of silence they obey even more strictly.

Every few hours, when the colossal fans recessed into the cavern walls shift their pitch and send a slow wind rolling across the open meadows, the *plume-sedges* lean in silver waves and loose their clouds of buoyant spores. Fine, dry organic dust suspended in static-charged air is the one true terror of an anhydrous city: a single careless spark can turn a bright afternoon harvest into a rolling flash of fire.

Just before the air-shift breaks, the sixty-cycle hum rising from the geothermal mains drops briefly to a deep, unmistakable forty-cycle thrum felt directly through the soles of the feet. Across kilometers of open basin, work stops cleanly. Electric scooters coast to a halt and engage their grounding-pins. Automated pruning gantries lock their ceramic shears. Framemen on the high scaffolding reach out and lay a bare, copper-strapped palm flat against a grounded iron rib. Even the children crossing the silver-turf meadows pause and drop to one knee, touching both knuckles to the cork-mat until the static needles in their forearms lie flat again. For ten long minutes, while the overhead electrostatic nets darken from white to pale ochre and drag the drifting yellow harvest safely out of the sky, an entire industrial city stands motionless in the artificial breeze, breathing slowly in the chalky light.

---

Their days are woven tightly into the quiet machinery and the dry wildlife that shares the floor with them.

Along the power avenues and substation yards, the brush-crews ride their silent electric scooters in slow, methodical circuits, carrying long poles of seasoned pith-wood tipped with horsehair-fine bundles of stripped sedge. When they sweep the luminous silicate webs of the *coil-spinners* clear of high-voltage contacts, tiny blue sparks pop and hiss across the wood, making the filaments along their forearms stand straight on end. They never waste the catch. With a practiced twist of the wrist, a tender spools the fireproof, sparkling lace directly onto notched cedar bobbins slung at the hip. Back in the workshops, that harvested silk is spun into heat-proof gloves, glowing translucent curtains for residential skylights, and feather-light sleeping hammocks. Many older technicians keep a single tame coil-spinner in a perforated clay jar beside their workbenches, feeding it tiny chaff-beetles in exchange for a neat daily patch of fresh insulating thread to mend frayed cables.

Out on the wide avenues separating the wards, the *spindle-grazers* are treated with the calm familiarity of slow, eccentric neighbors. Their grazing keeps the cork-turf trimmed back from the rail-beds, and they pay almost no mind to bipedal traffic. On quiet shifts, you will often see a pair of basin-born youths walking alongside a tall, six-legged grazer, gently raking loose tufts of pale gray-brown felt from its shedding flanks with wide bone combs, stuffing the dry wool into canvas sacks to line winter tunics and boot-insoles. If a herd wanders too close to a newly seeded foundation where young sclerophyte shoots are still tender, a trainer simply taps a small brass wand against a capacitor-pack at their belt; the sudden, invisible snap of static makes the grazers’ broad padded feet shuffle awkwardly backward until they drift off toward older turf.

It is only out along the raw perimeter—where the silver mat breaks into newborn crust-lichen and jagged, unweathered scree—that the inhabitants move with visible wariness.

The surveyors who snap chalk-lines across the fractured slate and test mineral seams for new tap-shafts never work alone. They wear stiff, flared shin-guards of layered cork-bark and carry long, weighted ceramic staves wired to small belt-batteries. They are watching for *husk-stalkers*. A stalker pressed flat into a pile of dried bark-shavings and braided hemp is virtually invisible even to sharp, dry-lensed eyes, and its burst across the sound-eating felt gives almost no warning. The basin-folk do not try to exterminate them; the stalkers keep the rim clear of stray grazers and overgrown beetle swarms. Instead, a lead surveyor periodically touches the tip of their stave to a bare iron stake or stone outcrop and lets off a sharp, cracking arc of blue static. If a husk-stalker is crouched within twenty paces, the sudden pulse of charge makes its segmented dry plates twitch involuntarily—a brief, brittle rustle against the stone that breaks its stillness and lets the crew step wide around its lair.

---

Because the valley is barely past its first generation of growth, you can tell almost at a glance where a person was born simply by watching how they cross an open meadow.

The elders—the *Seeders*, who arrived when the cavern was still nothing more than a raw, roaring shaft of blasted basalt and bare scaffolding—came from older, deeper hollows far beneath this one. Those ancient basins are centuries thick with growth: dense, dim labyrinths where living timber roofs have knit together five stories deep, where every avenue is narrow as a hallway, and the air smells heavily of old resin and dark, darkened cork. The Seeders still carry the posture of those cramped vaults. Their skin is darker, thicker, deeply creased around the joints; their shoulders hold a slight inward curve; and even after twenty years in this vast new basin, they instinctively walk near the edges of causeways, reaching out now and then to brush a palm against a porcelain pylon or a growing trunk just to confirm where the wall is. The sheer, uninterrupted kilometer-wide horizons of the new valley still make them faintly dizzy.

Their children—the *Open-Born*, raised entirely beneath these wide, papery light-canopies—are a subtly different shape already.

Grown tall under unobstructed arc-light with room to run, they are lanker, paler, and lighter-boned than their parents. They have no fear of empty space. During off-shifts, bands of them race stripped-down electric scooters along the uncluttered perimeter rails, leaning hard into the turns with their copper heel-straps throwing tiny, dry sparks against the graphite tracks. They climb bare forty-meter iron armatures before the pale climbing wood has even reached the second story, perching on the highest crossbeams just to watch the translucent *vellum-gliders* spiral lazily beneath the ceiling gantries—and sometimes reaching out with quick, papery fingers to snag a shed, onion-skin wing drifting down on a thermal, pressing it flat between sheets of cedar to mount later in a bedroom skylight.

Between the two generations runs a quiet, unspoken tenderness. The elders watch the young ones sprint straight across a kilometer of bright silver turf without once checking for a ceiling overhead, and feel a strange mixture of pride and vertigo—as if they have planted a familiar seed in new stone and watched it sprout into a species they only half recognize.

---

When the ceiling arc-ribbons begin their slow turn toward steel-blue twilight, and the papery canopies far overhead curl inward to loose their thin, silent snow of chalky chaff, the basin-folk retreat into the low, terraced cloisters.

Inside the hexagonal dwellings, there is no clatter of wet crockery, no hiss of steam, no drainpipes murmuring behind the walls. The rooms are dry, resonant chambers lined in pale, honeycombed bark, warmed from beneath by gentle geothermal radiant-ducts buried under the cork floor. Light comes from small, low-voltage filaments encased in pressed amber-resin panes, casting a calm honey glow that contrasts softly with the blue dusk outside the hexagonal windows.

They gather around low tables of compressed pith inlaid with electric induction plates. Supper is slow, aromatic, and entirely dry: thin, brittle wafers pressed from harvested spore-flour and ground sedge-seed, warmed until they crisp and perfume the room with toasted grain; crumbly cakes of compressed starch spiced with crushed juniper husk and white mineral-salt; translucent amber lozenges of refined seed-oil held under the palate until they dissolve cleanly, replenishing the body’s slow internal circulation. Afterward, dishes are not washed—they are brushed spotless with stiff tufts of dried moss and stacked neatly in cedar racks.

Then comes the quietest, most intimate hour of the twenty-eight-hour cycle.

In a world with zero moisture, the body’s hardest struggle is stiffness. Over a long shift in the cool, chalky air, the fine pleats of papery hide at the elbows, knees, neck, and knuckles grow taut and prone to tiny, painful splits. So as the twilight deepens and the living timber ribs of the cloister begin to knock and flex in the cooling air, small stoneware flasks of clear, fresh-pressed seed-oil are passed from hand to hand.

Partners sit cross-legged on woven silicate-silk mats, warming drops of oil between their dry palms and rubbing it patiently into the creases of one another’s shoulders, wrists, and heels. Parents oil the slender knees and thin ankles of their children before lifting them into suspended hammocks. It is grooming, medicine, and affection collapsed into a single quiet rite; in the blue dimness, the entire residential district smells faintly of warm cedar, fresh seed-oil, and the clean, crackling stillness of settling wood.

Every household knows the voices of its own walls. As the temperature drops and the hollow-celled bark contracts around its iron bones, a sharp *tok* from the north rib or a low creak along the skylight frame draws an effortless nod from an elder sitting half-asleep in a hammock—*that one is tight; that seam will need a smear of acid-dust and a fresh shoot come morning*.

---

There is one last thing that shapes who they are, though in a valley this young it is only just beginning to show itself.

Nothing here ever rots.

When one of the basin-folk grows very old, they do not soften, swell, or grow wetly ill. They *season*. Over decades, their internal oils run thicker and slower; their skin deepens to the color of pale walnut and grows stiff as cured parchment; their reed-like voices thin into dry, papery whispers that can barely stir the air. They spend their last cycles sitting in warm patches beside the geothermal vents or out near the edge of the cloister terraces, wrapped in grazer-felt, letting their grandchildren oil their stiffening fingers and listening to the quiet sixty-cycle hum of the valley floor.

When the breath finally stops, the body does not spoil. Within a few days of cool, dry air, it cures completely—becoming extraordinarily light, clean, intact, and faintly fragrant of seasoned cedar and dry resin.

In the ancient, deep caverns where the Seeders came from, the dead are everywhere: standing in dry wall-niches along the oldest avenues, or laid gently into the junctions of structural armatures so that climbing sclerophytes can knit their hollow bones directly into the pillars of the city, letting their mineral salts and conductive copper-traces feed the living architecture for centuries. A mature hollow is quite literally held upright by its ancestors.

Here, in the newly opened valley, the central plaza’s tall iron framing-pillars are lined with smooth, hexagonal alcoves—and almost all of them are still empty. Only three hold quiet, light forms wrapped in luminous silicate silk, pale cream-colored vines just beginning to send their thin tendrils across the cured ribs, knitting the very first elders of the basin into the timber of the roof.

Stand near the rim as the steel-blue twilight reaches its deepest hush, and you may see one of the remaining First-Seeders sitting alone on a stack of seasoned conduit, knees drawn stiffly to her chest, her silver filaments lifting slightly in the cool night breeze. Behind her lies raw, sharp basalt that has never known a root or a touch of warmth. Ahead stretches miles of bright-ribbed industry, humming copper roots, grazing felt-backed herds, and open-born children sleeping in hammocks woven of fireproof light. She sits very still, feeling the slow, dry stiffening in her own joints—neither afraid nor hurried—waiting for the arc-ribbons overhead to brighten toward another morning, and knowing which unfinished iron rib down in the plaza has been left open for her.
