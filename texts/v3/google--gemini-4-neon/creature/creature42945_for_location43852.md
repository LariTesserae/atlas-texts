If you stay past the waking of the chimney—long enough for your eyes to give up on the slivers of sky overhead, long enough for the dry convection to feel less like an oven and more like a steady, invisible weather—you begin to notice that the chamber does not quite add up.

A patch of shingle-backs on the western rib detaches from the vertical tide and moves *sideways*, crossing the grain of ten thousand grazing neighbors without a single shell tilting in alarm. High in the nave, where the swirling blizzard of drifters rises in unbroken spirals, a clean, narrow lane opens through the papery drift and closes again behind nothing you can hear. And then, faintly, up through the soles of your boots—not through the thin air, which refuses to carry it, but straight through the basalt beneath the flour—comes a measured, ringing tremor: *tick—tick—pause—scrape*.

Too deliberate for a splitting carapace. Too rhythmic for salt snapping free of a ledge.

You were wrong about the stonework being abandoned. You were only looking at it on a clock that runs too fast.

There are seven of them in the cathedral at present, though the count changes over spans longer than a lowland tree’s life. During the stalled, stifling hours of the mountain day, you could run your hand directly across one of them and swear you had touched a boss of basalt tiled in sleeping grazers. They lodge themselves in the deep pockets where the vaulted ribs intersect, folding their long limbs flush against their narrow sides, clamping a pair of broad, hinged dorsal shields shut over their flanks like the shutters of a kiln. Those shields are deeply grooved and pitted, dusted with gray mineral flour, and colonized two layers deep by living shingle-backs that ride them willingly. Their breathing slows to a single dry tide every two or three minutes. In the midday hush, they are neither warmer nor cooler than the stone. They simply become part of the vault’s architecture.

When sunset strips the heat off the outer peak and the chimney catches, the stone comes unhinged.

The first thing to detach is a limb—long, slender, and matte-dark as charred willow, double-jointed at knee and ankle so it can reach across wide spans of wall without disturbing the grazing mosaic beneath. It ends not in claws, which would crack shells and scatter the grazers into a panic, but in a broad, splayed pad of matted, soot-fine bristles that settles over three or four shingle-backs at once, distributing weight so evenly the tiles beneath barely press harder against the rock. Three more such limbs follow, unfolding from the arch-pocket with a dry whisper like stiff leather unrolling.

The whole body stretches nearly the span of a tall person from brow to counterbalancing tail, yet it can hardly weigh more than a bundle of seasoned driftwood. Inside, its frame is a lattice of honeycombed, pumice-light bone built for thin air and vertical stone; outside, not a millimeter of wet tissue is anywhere exposed. Every plate is horn-hard, kiln-cured, and matte with sulfur-dust.

Only when one pauses on a rib ten feet above the floor, angling its head down toward the ember-smudge of the fissures, do you see—if light existed here to see by—the strangest parts of it.

Its head is long, flattened, and wholly blind to the thin sky above. Where eyes might have been sit two broad, concave mirrors of black, glassy scale recessed beneath the brow-ridge, angled slightly outward to catch the faintest shift in radiant heat across forty feet of dark. Between them runs a tall, fluted crest along the snout, packed internally with paper-thin scrolls of mineral-crusted bone. Every breath it draws passes through that labyrinth; every breath it exhales is stripped of moisture on the way back out, leaving the air that quits its nostrils as bone-dry as the vault itself. To exhale wet breath here would be madness—not merely a waste of water, but a cold flare in the dark that would summon every velvet-footed stalker within twenty paces.

And tucked neatly beneath its narrow breast-keel, folded out of sight while it climbs, lies a second pair of limbs altogether unlike the dark walking legs. They are shorter, pale as unbleached bone, and exquisitely jointed, ending in five slim, spatulate prongs with hardened tips and supple inner pads.

Hands. Not human hands—longer in the palm, drier in the skin, built for precision rather than strength—yet unmistakably the hands of makers, menders, and keepers.

***

They do not merely survive the chamber. They tend it as an orchardist tends a terraced hill.

Once the night-pulse hits full draw, the household disperses along the vertical axis of the dome, each moving to its own altitude and labor. Two of the leanest climb high into the wind-sheared arches where the colonies of gourd-skinned bladders hang pulsing out of phase. Balancing upside down from the stone ribs by their four dark walking limbs, they free their pale inner hands and work among the tethered sacs with terrifying delicacy.

Where bitter salt-crust has begun to choke the narrow wall-cracks feeding a bladder, they chip the rime clear with a sliver of struck obsidian carried wedged beneath a dorsal plate. Where dead drifter-husks have snagged around a sinewy stalk and threatened to strangle its draw, they tease the smoky parchment free and tuck it away. And when a mature bladder swells taut at the peak of its slow inhalation—its catalytic iron-dust having wrung a fraction of chemical moisture and dark, mineral-heavy liquor from the volcanic gas—one of the keepers slides a hollow, beveled spine (harvested long ago from a giant stalker) cleanly through the gourd-thick wall, draws a guarded swallow straight into its sealed beak-seam, and withdraws the needle in the same breath. Before the punctured skin can weep a single cooling drop into the oven-air, the keeper smears the pinhole shut with a dab of dry gray paste carried in a folded pouch at its hip.

The bladder scarcely misses a beat in its long, soundless collapse.

Others work the open convection itself, perched on the highest projecting bosses of the dome where spent drifter-hulls spiral upward toward the ceiling flues. They snatch the empty membranes out of the rising column with brisk, silent flicks of their pale fingers, flattening them against their chests until they hold a thick sheaf of pale, soot-fringed parchment. Later, crouched in the lee of a rib where the draft slackens, they press the membranes together, edge overlapping edge, and stitch them with dry sinew stripped from dead bladder-stalks.

In a world without cloth, bark, or wet clay, drifter-parchment is everything. They fold it into tight-lipped sleeves, layered shrouds, and water-proofed pouches—not to keep rain out, for rain is a rumor from another planet, but to keep vapor *in*.

Down in the basin, you can see what those pouches are for.

One of the older keepers—heavy-shouldered, its dorsal shields tiled so densely with generations of shingle-backs that its outline blurs like a moving cairn—descends the western rib until its bristled pads meet the curved rim of the dry-stone ring jutting from the flour. It does not wade into the shifting ash, where its weight would sink and send clumsy vibrations rolling out to warn the floor-dwellers. Instead, it crouches low on the ring, uncoils a long length of braided bladder-sinew weighted at the tip with a thumb-sized chip of basalt, and lowers the stone gently into the heaving dust.

Then it lifts and drops the pebble in a small, irregular rhythm—the exact dry pattering of a fallen grazer struggling to right itself on the flour.

Ten feet away, a ridge swells across the ash and drives straight toward the ring. A horn-segmented burrower breaches just enough to clamp its flaring, grinding collar around the pebble. Before the blind tube can register stone instead of shell, the keeper hauls upward in a single, whip-fast heave of both pale hands, snapping the thrashing, wrist-thick length clear of the dust and directly into the mouth of a wide, multi-layered drifter-pouch held open between its knees.

The pouch snaps shut.

Inside that sealed dark, no evaporative chill escapes to the oven-air. No velvet-footed stalker comes racing down the walls toward a sudden drop in temperature. Through the taut parchment, the keeper cracks the burrower’s horn-segments with steady, practiced pressure, slides its narrow snout through a tight overlap in the membrane, and drinks the creature dry behind closed doors. Only when nothing remains inside the pouch but chalk-dry chitin and pulverized mineral does it shake the brittle husks out into the hollowed tuff mortar beside the stone ring.

There, with a smooth, fist-sized cobble of basalt worn glossy by centuries of turning, it grinds the husks together with bitter ochre salt-bloom scraped from the lower fissures and fine iron-powder shaken from spent bladders. What fills the mortar is not meal for eating; it is dry-caulk—an astringent, waterless salve they pack into every cracked plate, every bruised joint, every punctured bladder-skin, and every seam where a shedding hide threatens to betray them.

***

They never make a sound with their throats. In this thin, parching air, a voice would be both useless and ruinous, bleeding moisture for a whisper that dies six feet away.

Yet the vault is full of their conversation.

Down by the low pillar of hard, glassy rock—the one where the salt-bloom refuses to take hold—a keeper pauses on its way back toward the walls. It draws the hardened heel-spur of a walking limb down one of the ancient vertical grooves scraped into the stone. Not hard, just with a measured, rising pressure. A dry, glassy *chrrr—tik* runs straight down the pillar, spreads across the bedrock beneath the knee-deep flour, and climbs every arch of the cathedral at the speed of stone.

Up on the forty-foot ribs, through bristled footpads pressed to the rock, the other six feel it as cleanly as a spoken name. Each groove on the pillar holds a slightly different pitch; each arch in the vault answers with its own resonance. By striking, dragging, and damping the rock with their spurs or with flat chips of struck basalt, they pass news across the pitch-black nave: a fresh gas-crack opening along the eastern floor; a cluster of bladders going dry near the northern flue; a storm shearing cold across the outer ridge; a danger in the entrance crawlway.

When two of them meet face to face on a ledge, they have a quieter, more intimate tongue.

Along the sides of their throats and beneath the forward edges of their dorsal shields sit thin, finely pleated louvers of sealed skin, flushed with dry blood vessels just beneath a translucent horn film. During work and travel, those louvers stay folded flat and hidden. When kin greet kin, they draw the shields slightly apart and pump warm blood through the pleats in shifting, deliberate pulses. Not a drop of moisture crosses the horn; not a glimmer of visible light enters the dark. Yet to their concave black brow-pits, the pitch-black air between them suddenly blooms with crisp, silent geometries of radiant heat—ripples of brightness, sharp bands of shadow, slow swells of deep ember-warmth.

It is a language of pure temperature, unreadable to any creature that relies on sun or ear. They argue in sharp, flickering grids of warmth; they comfort one another by holding a broad, steady bloom along the collarbone; they teach the youngest where the scalding cracks lie by flashing tight, fierce bars along the jawline until the young one mirrors the shape back.

***

Nothing in the vault is more dangerous to them than growing.

Like the tiled grazers on the walls, their horn-plates do not stretch forever; every few years—more often in youth, once a decade in old age—a keeper must shed its outer armor. And to shed in this chamber is to become a beacon of cold. The instant fresh, damp keratin meets the kiln-air, evaporation drops its surface temperature by a fraction of a degree, and across the vaulted dark, dozens of needle-legged stalkers tilt their concave heads in unison and begin to glide.

No single keeper could survive a molt alone.

When one of them feels its old plates loosening and turning brittle along the spine, it signals the household through the pillar’s lowest groove and climbs to a shallow recess along the middle ribs, where the stone holds a steady, forgiving warmth. The others gather around it with armfuls of stitched drifter-parchment and bowls of ground caulk from the mortar. They swaddle the molting one completely—limbs, torso, dorsal plates, even the breathing-crest wrapped loosely in triple-layered membranes dusted thick with warm ash and iron-powder, leaving only a narrow, baffled vent to breathe through.

For three full turns of day and night, while the old hide splits and the pale new plates harden beneath the shroud, the others do not leave it unattended. Two stay pressed tightly against the wrapped bundle at all times, spreading their own heated dorsal shields over it like dry wings to smother any leak of evaporative chill. A third patrols the surrounding stone with pale hands ready, plucking any inquisitive velvet-footed stalker off the living wall-tiles before it can probe a seam, cracking it quietly between spatulate fingers and drinking its stolen water on the spot.

The same fierce vigilance surrounds their young. A clutch is never more than one or two—leathery, salt-dusted eggs laid deep inside a woven ball of drifter-parchment and tucked into the warm flour beside the stone ring, where the elders take turns lying coiled around the nest to keep the horn-burrowers at bay. When the hatchling breaks free—blind, spindly, pale as bone-ash, its dorsal plates still bare of grazers—its parents do not feed it solid food. They seal their beaks tightly to its mouth-seam and pass dark, concentrated fluid straight from throat to throat, while an elder gently pries a dozen small, calm shingle-backs from its own ancient shoulders and sets them onto the hatchling’s grooved back—the first tiles of a cloak that will grow with it for a century.

And when a century or two has worn a keeper down—when the delicate bone-scrolls inside its snout grow calcified and begin to leak faint wisps of precious vapor, when its bristled pads lose their purchase on the high ribs and its movements turn stiff even in the rush of the night-pulse—there is no panic, and no waste.

It climbs the thumb-worn footholds up the western wall one final time, aided from behind by two of the younger ones who steady its weight against the shifting carpet of shells. At the top lies the narrow shelf where the four knee-high cairns keep their rough, stacked outlines beneath a three-inch crust of living tiles.

There, on the bare basalt beside the oldest mound, the elder folds its walking limbs neatly against its ribs and opens its neck-louvers in one long, fading bloom of warmth—a slow goodbye read silently by the gathered household. Then it tilts its narrow head back and unseals its mouth-seam for the youngest of them.

Water, here, is locked strictly inside flesh. None of it belongs to the oven-air.

One by one, gently, without haste or violence, the living draw out what remains—the last stored currency of a long life returning directly into the vessels of its kin. By the time the chimney stalls at dawn, the elder is lighter than dry parchment, hollow as a winter reed, emptied of everything the parching mountain could ever steal. The others fold its pale hands across its chest, wrap it loosely in a spent drifter-shroud, and gather loose basalt chunks from the shelf’s edge to build a fifth rough, knee-high cairn over its bones.

Within a season, the migrating shingle-backs have crawled across the fresh stone and beveled their edges together over the cracks. Within a decade, no outsider climbing those footholds could tell a fresh grave from an ancient pile of rubble.

***

Perhaps, long ago, other hands truly did come through the narrow mountain cleft—storm-driven wanderers from the outer ridges who wedged the first basalt slabs in the crawlway to break the freezing gale, who chipped the first footholds and stared into the stifling dark beside the breath of the earth. Perhaps they met the quiet tenants of the vault in silence, or perhaps they left before they ever understood that the shadows on the ribs were watching them with black heat-mirrors. Either way, those outer hands are gone, and the windbreaks still stand straight—because whenever decades of winter gales nudge one of the three entrance slabs a fraction out of true, the keepers come down in the dead of a calm night, brace their long dark limbs against the stone, and lever it gently back into place. They know in their dry, hollow bones what the chimney requires. If cold mountain air spills straight through the throat instead of shearing cleanly across the ceiling slots, the draft breaks, the salt-bloom dies, and the waterless city starves.

And now, standing in the middle of the stifling dark, you feel the sudden change in the room.

To you, you are merely a visitor holding your breath in the pitch black. To the seven pairs of concave heat-mirrors turned down toward you from the high basalt arches, you are an astonishing, terrifying prodigy. Every breath you draw pours a billowing plume of wet, cooling vapor into the kiln-air. Your skin weeps moisture from a million open pores, blazing in the infrared dark with the furious chill of rapid evaporation—a massive, clumsy lighthouse of cold that has already set every velvet-footed stalker on the lower walls sliding quietly toward your ankles. Your boots crush the powdered flour with loud, blind thuds that scatter the burrowers beneath.

From the low glassy pillar ten paces to your left comes a single, sharp vibration up through the soles of your feet: *scrape—pause—scrape*.

High overhead, the work along the ribs stops. A dozen paces up the western arch, a patch of living wall-tiles shifts, freezes, and clamps its beveled margins shut. One of the elders hangs motionless directly above the stone ring, its dark limbs spanning the basalt, its pale working hands tucked safely beneath its chest, its neck-louvers sealed tight so it gives nothing of itself away.

They do not rush you. They do not strike. They have outlived storms that buried the ridge in ten feet of ice, and droughts that turned the lower valleys to alkali. They know what the kiln-air does to anything soft, loud, and profligate with its water.

They simply wait in the dry, rustling dark, reading the frantic, cooling flare of your face from forty feet up, listening through the stone to the uneven hammer of your heart—patient as basalt, thrifty as salt, watching to see whether you will disturb their wind-slabs on your way back out into the freezing sky.
