If you stand beside one of the slow porcelain belts long enough—long enough for the forty-second rhythm of the press to stop feeling like a measurement and start feeling like a pulse—you may eventually notice a single disc that is not quite like the others.

Where the worn steel die leaves only that faint, thumb-sized dimple in the clay, this disc carries something else: three hairline grooves pressed cleanly across its centre before it entered the kiln, baked now into pale grey permanence. It glides past you on its metal slat, crosses the dark viaduct, rides the unmanned tram for three silent blocks, and disappears into the throat of the grinding mill. If you walk down to the far side of the mill, nothing remains of it. The granite rollers have returned it to the same blank, warm talc as everything else.

Yet someone put those marks there. And someone else, standing in the black lee of the mill hopper with dry fingertips hovering just above the moving belt, read them while the clay was still warm, before letting the disc drop into the crushers.

You almost never see them first. In a place where the oxides and vitrified stone drink lantern-light, they are made of the same matte silences as the architecture. You might pass within ten paces of one seated beside a rail-tram track and take it for a slumped coil of braided cable, a low transformer casing, or a stack of porcelain blanks left by an forgotten shift. Only when it shifts—unhurriedly, with no sudden break in posture—does the geometry separate itself from the masonry.

They are roughly of human height, though narrower through the hips and shoulders, with a slight, listening tilt to the upper spine. Their bodies are sheathed in dry, pale grey hide that looks and feels like kiln-cured vellum stretched tightly over a dense, stoneware-hard frame. At the elbows, knees, and along the flanks, the hide folds into crisp, narrow pleats—the same dry bellows-work you see on the tall walkers, though finer, more supple, dusted permanently in the creases with a faint bloom of grey porcelain talc. They wear no cloth for warmth or modesty; the air is already held at an even oven-heat. Instead, they carry slung across one shoulder a single loop of braided, oxidised copper wire, and at the waist a narrow strip of petrified linen holding two or three small tools of ground horn and darkened brass.

Where a face should be, there is only a smooth, eyeless slope of bone and tight grey skin, slightly concave at the temples so that the skull acts as a sounding-board. They have no lips, no wet mouths, no nostrils that flare or draw breath. Beneath the smooth jaw sits a recessed cowl of thin, overlapping mineral plates—delicate as the cooling fins on a small rectifier—which part by a hair’s breadth when they have been working near a furnace, venting excess warmth into the dark with a dry whisper too faint to hear from a pace away.

Their hands are the most finished thing about them: long, lean, four-fingered, ending in tips that are dark, smooth, and subtly flattened like tiny burnishing stones. Centuries of touching rails, copper ribbons, hot clay, and carbon brushes have impregnated the dry callouses with graphite and metallic dust. They move with a slow, sparing precision that makes a human gesture look frantic, almost wasteful.

To understand how they live here, you have to let go of the idea that the plateau is silent or dark to them.

To their bare, thick-soled feet, the hexagonal slag tiles are an open map of vibration. The forty-cycle mains hum is not background noise; it is the weather, the sky, and the compass all at once. Every hall, every buried busbar, every flywheel turning in its oil bath throws a distinct tremor into the stone—a slight drift in pitch, a granular bass rumble from a grinding mill four avenues away, the crisp tremor of a relay dropping half a kilometre off. They know when a bearing three blocks north has worn down by a fraction of a millimetre because the floor beneath their heels begins to taste slightly rough.

And where you see uniform blackness, they move through a vivid topography of heat and charge. The muffled kilns stand out to the sensitive skin along their forearms and temples like bright, heavy hills; the wide avenues are flat, cool valleys; the buried conduits glow beneath the pavement in long, straight rivers of faint induction. Even a walker passing two hundred paces away across a yard registers to them as a tall, drifting pocket of low-grade static, moving unhurriedly between substations.

They do not eat, as wet creatures eat, and they do not drink. When one needs to replenish itself, it slips through the ajar iron door of a windowless substation, finds a transformer humming quietly in the dark, and sits with its bare back pressed directly against the warm cast-iron ribs. Along its spine, two narrow rows of dorsal pleats part slightly, exposing a dry, matte-black membrane veined with fine filaments of copper-salt. It remains there for hours, motionless as a plinth, drawing radiant heat and stray induced current straight out of the casing. Once in a long while—perhaps once in a span you would call a month—it reaches into a tiny horn vial at its belt, takes a pinch of fine powder mixed from porcelain dust, iron oxide, and dried mineral salt, and tucks it carefully beneath the recessed plates of its jaw. Not for energy, which the city gives freely in its humming equilibrium, but to replace the microscopic grit worn away from its own heels and bone-plates over years of quiet travel.

There are not many of them—perhaps a few hundred scattered across the whole kilometres-wide tableland. You never see a crowd, never hear a commotion. Yet they are not solitary.

If you walk down one of the dead-level tram avenues at the right hour—though all hours here are identical—you may come upon two of them seated twenty paces apart along the same iron rail. Neither looks toward the other. Neither makes a sound into the thin air. Each has simply unslung the braided copper cord from its shoulder and laid one end across the steel track, holding the other gently between graphite-darkened fingertips.

To your eyes, they are two statues resting in the dark. Only if you knelt and pressed your ear directly to the warm iron rail between them would you catch it: a dry, scarcely audible ticking, interspersed with tiny, shifting purrs of modulated current—a conversation conducted entirely in micro-volts and piezo-clicks carried through the steel. They can speak this way across half a block without disturbing the stillness of the air, exchanging news of the grid: a transformer flange sweating a hair faster than it did ten cycles ago; a kiln vent losing half a degree; a slight, unusual slackness in one of the overhead steel cables spanning the southern viaduct.

For longer thoughts, or messages meant for whoever tends the next hall along a loop, they use the porcelain discs. A tender waiting by a press lifts a freshly stamped blank from the belt, uses a horn stylus to score a brief pattern of grooves or dots into the soft, unglazed clay, and sets it back in line. The kiln bakes the message hard; the unmanned tram carries it across the dark avenues; the tender waiting at the far end lifts the warm disc, reads the faint geometry with four dark fingertips, and drops it into the granite rollers. Within minutes, the thought is powder again, drawn back under the street to be pressed into a blank disc once more. Nothing piles up. There are no libraries here, no archives, no permanent walls scrawled with history. Even speech obeys the closed loop, borrowing a cupful of clay for a single circuit and returning it clean.

What they actually *do* is almost invisible until you have watched the city long enough to see how near-equilibrium differs from true permanence.

Friction here is vanishingly small, not absent. Over decades, a nut backs off a quarter-turn under the forty-cycle hum; a pinch of porcelain dust drifts off a belt and settles into a slag seam; a fine skin of dielectric oil sweats past a transformer gasket and cakes the dust at its foot into dark putty. Left alone for ten thousand years, those microscopic leaks would eventually tip the plateau into silence.

They are the margin that keeps the account balanced.

With a patience that feels geological, they walk the avenues settling small discrepancies. One kneels for an hour beside a grinding mill with a brush of stiff mineral bristle, sweeping a thimbleful of escaped talc from the rounded curb-grooves and tipping it back into the return hopper. Another scrapes the oil-darkened putty from the base of a transformer flange, presses it inside a small brass hand-screw until a single clear, heavy bead of oil wells up, and carries that drop in a sealed quartz tube to an oil-bath flywheel whose meniscus has dipped a fraction of a hair below its ancient mark. They do not alter the machines. They do not improve them, or ask what the porcelain discs were once meant to be, or wonder where the copper ribbons used to go. To them, the loop is not a ruin of an older purpose; it is simply the metabolism of the ground they walk on, and keeping it level is no more strange than keeping one’s own balance while walking.

They move among the other sparse life of the plateau with the same unhurried courtesy. When they find the papery, empty husk of an insulator-clinger resting on the hexagonal tiles—dropped there with its tiny metallic clink after a moult—they pick it up carefully between two fingers. Dry, pure, and non-conductive, the husks make ideal shims for a chattering relay housing, or, crushed between thumb and forefinger, a dry lubricant to rub into their own pleated knees when the joints begin to whisper.

With the tall walkers, their kinship is quieter and stranger. The walkers are heavier, simpler, slower-minded creatures—wandering capacitors that drift along the boulevards without pattern, drawn wherever a substation builds up a faint surplus of static. Usually they pass the plateau’s people without a flicker of notice, hooves clicking lightly on the slag. Yet once in a long while, a walker’s mineral hoof slips into the narrow gap of a tram switch-point, or its long, stiff knees lock near a live third rail. When that happens, a tender comes quietly out of the dark, draws its braided copper cord across the walker’s pleated flank, and feeds a calm, steady pulse into its hide—matching the exact forty-cycle hum of the earth beneath them. Under that familiar rhythm, the tall creature bows its dish-snouted head and goes completely still while the tender patiently wedges a horn lever into the iron point and eases the hoof free. Neither hurries. Neither expects thanks. The walker simply lifts its leg, pauses for a long minute to listen to the ground, and steps away into the wide blackness between the halls.

Because nothing enters the plateau and nothing leaves it, the people themselves are held to the same strict arithmetic as the clay and the oil.

They do not multiply freely, and they do not rot.

As the centuries pass, the mineral salts a tender takes in gradually accumulate in its frame. Its grey, talc-dusted hide grows thicker, denser, and smoother, shifting slowly from the pale ash of unglazed porcelain to the deep, velvety maroon-brown of stabilized iron oxide—the same permanent rust that seals the leaning lamp standards along the boulevards. Movement grows stiff; the pleated knees lose their silence, beginning to emit a faint, dry creak that feels indecent in the hushed avenues.

When an elder reaches that threshold, it does not fall ill or collapse. It simply chooses a place to stop.

Often it is a quiet recess inside a brick switch-house, or the broad stone plinth beneath one of the house-sized copper toroids at the centre of a wide roundabout, where the stray magnetic field is rich and steady. There the elder sits, folds its long limbs neatly into its lowest, tightest posture, lays its copper cord across its knees, and closes the thin cooling-plates beneath its jaw to a hairline slit. Over the course of years, its joints vitrify completely, fusing into seamless mineral permanence.

A stranger crossing the roundabout with a dim lantern would walk right past it, seeing only a weathered, maroon-brown bollard or a slumped pillar of slag set beside the plinth.

Yet the settled ones are not dead.

Inside the fused, motionless shell, the induced current still turns, and thought continues—slower now, stripped of the small urgencies of walking and mending, stretching out until a decade passes like a single quiet breath. They become the deep memory of the grid. Every so often, one of the younger, grey-hided tenders will walk across the empty plaza, kneel beside a settled elder, and press two graphite-tipped fingers directly against its smooth, warm collarbone.

For an hour, neither moves. Through that single point of contact, the elder passes along the longest rhythms of the plateau: the exact pitch the buried conduits beneath the western boulevards held before a slow mineral creep shifted them three centuries ago; the cycle of a deep flywheel whose oil seal only needs tending once in a hundred years; faint, half-worn recollections of things that happened so far back the details have rounded down like the emblem on the porcelain dies—a season when the ambient air cooled by half a degree, or a single, inexplicable tremor that once rose all the way up through the bedrock from far below.

Only after centuries of stillness does the inner hum of a settled elder finally thin out and stop. A visiting tender touches the smooth collarbone and feels nothing answering—only the passive, neutral warmth of ordinary stone.

There is no grief in it, only exactitude. Over the next several cycles, two or three tenders come quietly with fine horn chisels, brushes, and stone mortars. With infinite care, wasting not a flake of oxide or a grain of bone-salt, they dismantle the empty shell. Every gram is weighed and accounted for. The tiny, sealed ampoule of heavy mineral oil resting deep inside the elder’s chest—the same ancient fluid that has passed through generations of bodies, never once spilled to the dry air—is drawn out intact. The ground mineral salts and dense clay-ash are carried into a low brick hall where one of the muffled kilns glows a sub-visible cherry red through its vents.

There, in a quiet alcove behind the furnace wall, the tenders press the reclaimed salts and ash together around that single sealed thimble of oil, shaping a smooth, dense, ovoid vessel about the size of a kneeling torso, sealed in a matte grey skin. They tuck it into a warm brick niche beside the kiln flues, where the heat is steady and the stray current from the furnace coils hums steadily through the wall.

It sits there untouched for years—sometimes decades—absorbing warmth and charge in the dark, until one quiet cycle, with a faint, papery *crack* no louder than a relay dropping blocks away, the shell splits cleanly down its seam.

Out steps a new one: smaller than the tenders, darker, almost the colour of freshly annealed copper, with smooth, un-dusted pleats and restless, quick-stepping feet. Its mass matches, down to the last pinch of dust, the elder who finished before it. The broken halves of its kiln-shell are swept carefully back into the porcelain hoppers. Within a few cycles, the young one is already climbing the leaning lamp standards and high pylons where the brief blue sparks snap between ceramic discs, or crawling down into the narrow pneumatic screw-tunnels beneath the streets where the older, stiffer tenders can no longer fit, learning the vast, humming chord of the city from the inside out.

There is only one thing on the entire plateau that unsettles them, and it is not darkness, nor silence, nor time. It is the edge.

Out where the wide, straight boulevards end against the smooth, bevelled lip of bedrock, the closed account breaks open. Anything dropped beyond that rounded rim—a brass gauge, a porcelain disc, even a handful of swept dust—falls away into the immense, featureless drop and never comes back. It is the one true theft the plateau can suffer. When they work on the outermost substations near the perimeter, they tether every tool to their wrists with braided copper, and move with an extra, measured deliberation.

Yet once in a long while, if you walk all the way out to the margins of the grid, you may find one of them sitting there alone.

It has left its tools and its copper cord neatly coiled twenty paces back along the hexagonal pavement, safe from any slip. It sits folded quietly on the smooth, bevelled bedrock right above the drop, bare heels tucked against its thighs, its eyeless, concave face turned straight out into the unpressurized, bottomless black.

It is not looking for light, and it is not waiting for anything to rise from below. It has come here for the one thing the city cannot give it inside the avenues: the thinning of the hum. Out on that rounded stone lip, with nothing behind it but miles of quiet equilibrium and nothing ahead but empty air, the forty-cycle vibration finally fades from its bones until there is almost nothing left at all—only dry warmth, and the immense, unmeasured hush beyond the loop.

It stays there for an hour, or a week, utterly still against the dark. Then, without haste, it rises, walks twenty paces back to collect its coiled copper wire, feels the steady, familiar hum return up through its soles, and steps quietly back toward the distant ember-line of the halls.
