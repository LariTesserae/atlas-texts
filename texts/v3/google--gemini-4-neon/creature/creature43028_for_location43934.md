They begin inside a muffled drum.

A child born on the basin does not know silence—silence is an idea from some other world, like trees or a blue sky. The first thing a newborn feels is the low, steady tremor coming up through the bedrock floor of the dome, answering the mother’s heartbeat in a slower, deeper meter. For the first turning of the light, and for many surges after, the infant stays tucked inside the double stone walls, wrapped in soft belly-hide scraped thin with chert, breathing the warm smell of rendered tallow, brine, and damp wool-less skin. When they are finally carried out through the weighted entrance flaps for the first time, their ears are already packed carefully with warm wads of tallow-soaked skimmer down and bound snug under oiled leather ear-flaps. Even so, the roar hits them like a slap of cold water. Their whole body stiffens; their mouth opens to cry, and no one can hear it—not even the mother, who only knows the child is wailing by the sudden, fierce vibration against her ribs.

It is the first lesson the plain teaches, and it never stops teaching it: out in the open, the air belongs to the stone and the water. If you want to be known, you must use your hands, your weight, and your bones.

---

They are not tall people, nor are they slender. Generations of walking oiled biofilm in perpetual damp cold have favored compact, thick-shouldered frames and low centers of gravity. They move with a slight, habitual bend at the knee and a wide, testing placement of the foot, toes gripping inside layered hide boots whose soles are stitched with rough ridges of thick grazer-plate to bite into the greasy microbial slick. You can tell a person of the flats even inside a dry shelter by the way they stand—never quite locking their knees, always subtly balanced as if waiting for the floor to heel over or shudder.

Their skin is pale as wet limestone beneath the weathered, brine-chapped sheen of their faces. Every exposed inch—cheeks, brow, knuckles, the backs of the wrists—is rubbed daily with pale bellower fat to keep the cold mineral mist from cracking the flesh open. It gives them a faint, waxy luster in the yellow smear of the stone lamps, and it carries the basin’s own smell: iron, brine, cold stone, and the dense, musky oil of marine-slick hide. Their hair is kept short, hack-trimmed with slate knives, or braided tight with sinew cord so it never whips wet across the eyes in a blowhole draft.

On the open flats, wrapped in overlapping capes of dark, greased hide, they nearly vanish into the leaden gleam of the whalebacks—until they raise their hands. Sewn across the palms and outer forearms of their heavy mittens are broad strips of bleached, bone-white bellower belly-skin, sometimes dusted further with chalky mineral salt scraped from the high domes. In a dimness where a dark sleeve disappears at thirty paces, a raised hand snaps cleanly out of the mist like a marker stone.

Their outdoor language is built for that distance and that gloom. It is wide-armed, angular, spare of wasted motion—less like conversation and more like navigation. A flat, pale palm pushed slowly downward means *the trough is holding; keep low*. Two fingers hooked and dragged across the collar means *a bull bellower holds the runnel ahead*. A quick double flick of the wrist near the temple warns that skimmers are stooping over the next ridge, where a dropped grazer-shell falling out of the cloud belly can split a skull open.

When the blowhole spray thickens into a white wall and even the pale palms vanish, they borrow the language of the grazers. Everyone carries a heavy knuckle-mallet or a grooved striking-stone lashed to their belt. Kneeling on the slick bedrock, a hunter will rap sharply against the stone—sharp, staggered bursts that travel straight through the wet basement of the plain, tingling up through a companion’s soles fifty paces away. Three quick knocks: *I am on the spine.* Two slow, heavy thuds: *wait, the floor is changing.* They have even learned to mimic the grazers’ own alarm-drum, hammering a fast, rippling rattle into a drying dome to spook a dense drift into lifting on their stiff legs and clattering toward waiting nets, or to make them clamp down tight so a pair of gatherers can walk past without sending a noisy cascade of rattling shells echoing toward a lurking bellower.

---

Life here does not turn on suns or seasons, for the pewter cloud-ceiling scarcely changes from one long stretch of dampness to the next. Time is counted in *surges* and *troughs*—the double breath of the drowned labyrinth underneath.

A working trough is a race conducted without haste. Hurrying on biofilm means a broken wrist or a cracked hip, and a person who cannot crawl back to a high spine before the pipes fill will drown in knee-deep rushing brine or be dragged under by a throat-whirl. So they move with an unhurried, watchful precision, always in pairs or trios, never alone.

Watch three of them work a wide drainage pan just after the hollow, gulping inhale of the vents signals the end of a surge.

Two wade straight into the draining runnel where their curved bone-and-stone weir angles across the shallow depression. The water is numbingly cold, sliding fast over their hide-wrapped shins as it retreats toward the sink-throat. Wedged behind the pale ribs of the weir, their braided gut-traps bulge and writhe with pale, blind swimmers caught on the ebb, feathery gills flaring frantically against the mesh. The two gatherers work without looking at each other, hands moving in a practiced, wet rhythm—hauling the slick, thrashing weight into stiff horn-and-tendon baskets, clearing wedged grazer shells from the rib-stakes, resetting a stone that the surge rolled loose.

The third stands ten paces upslope on the dry whaleback, back to the wind, watching the mist and feeling the rock through their boots. In one hand they hold a heavy bone harpoon bound with gut-line to a perforated drag-stone; in the other, a slate scraper. They are not looking at the traps. They are watching the dark shadow crouched in the lee of the next stone hump—a broad-backed bellower, mottled slate and bone-white, watching the same weir with wet, dark eyes.

They do not always fight the bellowers. The two species share the same cold table and keep a wary, loud truce across most of the basin. An old bull who holds a drainage pan drives off younger, unpredictable rivals and tears loose thick, stubborn crusts of old grazers; often, a huddle of people will simply leave him his favorite channel and work the next saddle over. When the bellower in the shadow braces its splayed forefeet and inflates its vast, veined throat-sac to let out a wet, brassy horn-blast, the lookout on the ridge does not flinch. They feel the note in their sternum, judging the beast’s weight and temper by the depth of the buzz in their ribs. If the horn-blast is long and steady, it is only a claim of ground; the lookout flashes a slow, low palm to the two in the runnel—*he holds his side, finish up*—and nobody wastes a harpoon.

Only when a huddle needs fresh oil for the lamps, heavy hide for a failing door-curtain, or dense knuckle-joints for mallets do they hunt one. Then five or six go out together in the slack water just before the drain, encircling a younger beast cut off from the main sink-throats, waiting for the moment its throat-sac swells and its head lifts. A bellower is most anchored when it calls; that is when the bone harpoons fly, their stone-weighted lines dragging against the wet rock so the wounded animal cannot pull a hunter down into the flooding conduits. Nothing of it is wasted. The thick subcutaneous fat is rendered slowly in hollowed stone troughs behind cobble windbreaks over smoky oil-wick fires; the tough flank-hide is scraped, salted, and rubbed with brain and oil until it sheds water like wet slate; even the vast, pleated throat-sac is carefully detached, washed, cured, and oiled into a supple, translucent bladder.

Those throat-bladders carry the basin’s rarest treasure. The surges are brackish, sharp with salt and dissolved limestone; drinking them will shrivel a person from the inside out. Fresh water comes only from the hairline seams that weep clear and ice-cold along the flanks of the older whalebacks. During quiet troughs, children and elders walk the high ridges with small chisels of cold-hammered copper and grooved splinters of skimmer-bill. Where a seam weeps steadily, they wedge a narrow horn-spout into the fissure and hang a cured bellower throat-sac underneath. Drop by slow drop, the veined pouch swells back into its living shape, heavy and clear, until it is tied off with sinew and slung gently over a shoulder back to the domes.

---

When the pressure shifts before the next surge—a faint, sudden heaviness in the wet air, followed by the abrupt quieting of ten thousand rasping grazers clamping flush to the rock—the people vanish from the open plain.

They follow the pale worn tracks along the highest spines, stepping past the bleached rib-and-quartz cairns until the low, hunched stone shells of their huddle rise scarcely higher than boulders out of the grey flats. One by one, they bend low and crawl through the angled stone tunnel, pushing past the first heavy, stone-weighted curtain of oiled bellower-skin, then the second.

The change is physical, almost shocking. The crushing, ear-numbing thunder of the plain shears away behind the double walls packed with crushed shell and mud, collapsing into a deep, steady, companionable hum felt mostly in the soles of the feet and the base of the skull.

Inside, they become different creatures.

The greased outer capes are shaken off in the narrow entry-niche and hung from bone pegs wedged between the dry-fitted slabs. The sinew-bound leather ear-flaps are untied; the tallow-soaked down plugs are drawn out and tucked carefully into tiny hollowed skimmer-bone cases worn on cords around the neck. In the cramped space—no higher than a bent shoulder, perhaps four paces across—five to eight people settle onto layered hides laid over the bare, polished bedrock floor. A carved stone lamp burns pale swimmer-oil along a twisted sinew wick, throwing a small, buttery smear of yellow light across the arching stone ribs overhead and catching the grease-darkened sheen of bundled harpoons, copper awls, and horn baskets.

Because the world outside is vast, cold, and deafening, the world inside the dome is radically close, warm, and soft-spoken.

They do not shout indoors; a sharp, loud voice bouncing off bare stone feels ugly, too much like the screech of a skimmer or the crack of a falling shell. Instead, they speak in *dome-speech*—a low, resonant murmur pitched deliberately beneath the faint whine of the distant vents. It is a language heavy on hummed vowels, soft nasal stops, and slow chesty cadences that carry poorly in wind but bloom warmly in a small stone room.

And they speak with touch. Even with tallow-down plugs, thirty years of living under the basin’s bass pressure wears away the high registers of human hearing. By middle age, most people hear airborne voices only as a dim, watery murmur. Nobody treats this as a failing; the language itself is shaped around it. When one person tells another about a crack widening in a weir, or argues over whether to walk to the eastern seams for copper nodules, they lean close and lay two fingers lightly against the listener’s wrist, collarbone, or the side of the jaw. Between lovers, or between a grandparent and a child learning the names of the vents, foreheads or temples rest together as they murmur, so the words travel cleanly from skull to skull through bone.

While the plain outside drowns under sheets of grey water and the blowholes thunder into the clouds, the dome becomes a quiet workshop. Someone chips a fresh edge onto a chert scraper with a dense antler-like splinter of old bellower jaw, catching the sharp flakes on a scrap of hide so none cut bare feet. Another pounds a rare green-stained copper nodule cold against a flat anvil-stone, patiently coaxing it into a thin awl. A third cracks boiled or brine-cured grazers with a small stone, scooping out the salty, firm muscle and dropping the shattered plates into a hide sack—eventually, those shells will be crushed fine and tamped into a settling hollow in the outer wall to keep the roar at bay.

They sleep in segments, matching the basin rather than the hidden sun. A long surge is time enough for a meal of brine-cured swimmers and bitter, fatty grazer meat, a few hours of mending and low-voiced stories, and a deep, curled sleep beneath shared hides while the water sheets past the lower stones outside. When the floor’s vibration shifts from a steady booming rumble to a hollow, sucking shudder, everyone wakes almost at once. The water is going back down into the dark. Time to plug the ears, smear fresh tallow across the cheekbones, and crawl back into the roar.

---

In every cluster of domes—three to six shelters crouched together on a high whaleback, sharing a single windbreak rendering-ring and a string of weirs—the most valued person is usually the oldest, whose ears have gone entirely deaf to the air.

They are called the *listeners*, though they hear nothing with their eardrums at all.

When an elder can no longer safely wade the fast runnels or brace against a harpooned bellower, they stay close to the domes and give their attention to the floor. Sitting bare-heeled on the smooth bedrock inside the shelter, palms laid flat on the cold stone—or sometimes biting gently onto a thin, polished baton of slate whose blunt end rests directly against the rock—they read the hidden labyrinth beneath the plain.

To a stranger, the basin is a single wall of chaos. To a listener, it is a map made of chords. Every blowhole and sink-throat within half a day’s walk has its own unmistakable voice, shaped by the curves and hollows of its buried pipe: the slow, wet cougher two saddles north; the double-hissing throat by the salt-flat; the deep, iron-toned groan that runs right beneath their own ridge. They do not map the flats by hills—there are no hills—but by where one vent’s tremor fades and the next takes hold in the shins.

The listeners feel what no eye can see through the pewter fog. They feel when a distant storm out over whatever unseen ocean feeds the drowned conduits is shoving extra water into the pipes, building a double-surge that will crest higher than the usual spine-marks; hours before the mist thickens, the listener taps a warning rhythm onto the floor, and the huddle hauls its gut-traps onto the dome roofs and wedges extra stones against the lower door-curtains. They feel when a subterranean shelf fractures deep in the dark, silencing an old vent and opening a new sink-throat a mile away, altering the whole drainage of a pan. They can even tell, by the subtle, prickly quiver running through the polished stone, when a massive drift of tens of thousands of grazers is shifting across the neighboring basin, or when an unusually large gathering of bellowers is contesting a channel two pans to the west.

When a young person asks *where are we going?* before a journey across the plain, the listener does not point toward a landmark. They take the traveler’s hand, press it flat to the stone, and tap out the sequence of vents they will pass—*long boom, quick hiss, double-cough, then the quiet flat where the chalk-cairn stands*—so the traveler carries the sound of the route in their wrists before they ever step outside.

---

No huddle can survive forever on a single whaleback. The basin doles out its gifts sparingly and unevenly across vast stretches of barren rock. The rare green-stained copper nodules weather out only along certain ancient, iron-red seams far toward the rim of the flats. Good flinty chert washes up only from a handful of deep volcanic pipes where the surge spits out sharp dark cobbles. Thick, pure salt crusts form best on the broadest, driest western domes where the mist thins slightly.

So there are *walkings*.

During periods when the surges run low and shallow, small groups of two or three set out along the pale, boot-worn tracks that link one high spine to the next. They travel light—a hide sling of cured swimmer-strips, a bellower-bladder of seam-water, trade bundles of copper, sinew, salt, or fine bone needles, and spare plugs of tallow-down. Along the longest, emptiest stretches, where miles of gleaming, featureless rock separate one vent-field from the next, stand lone way-domes: tiny, cramped stone shells built generations ago and maintained by everyone who passes. No one owns them. Custom demands only that if you crawl into a way-dome to wait out a surge, you leave a scraped curl of tallow and a dry twist of sinew in the lamp-niche for the next pair who stumble in out of the spray.

When travelers approach a strange huddle, they never walk straight up to the shelters—in the roaring fog, a sudden figure looming beside a dome looks too much like a threat or a stray bull bellower. They stop by the outermost rib-cairn, raise both pale-stitched hands high, and trace the sign of their home-ridge and their kin-line slowly in the dim air. Someone watching from the rendering-ring answers with the huddle’s sign. Only then do they close the distance, dropping their heavy packs, pressing shoulder to shoulder and brow to brow, letting the warmth and the tremor of their chests say what their voices cannot carry over the wind.

It is through these walkings that blood and craft stay strong. Young adults rarely spend their whole lives in the dome where they were born; after a gathering or a trade-walk, a youth will often choose to follow another huddle back along the pale spines, bringing with them a different way of lashing a weir, a new pattern for pleating a horn basket, or the quiet memory of another vent-field’s chord. Every huddle has cousins, former partners, and old friends scattered three or four basins away, linked across the dim grey mirror by lines of bleached ribs and worn stone.

---

Ask them—in the warm yellow hum inside a dome, fingers resting against a collarbone—where the world comes from, and they will not look up.

Above them hangs only a sodden pewter lid that has never broken open, that sends down nothing but cold drizzle and harsh-voiced birds. Everything that feeds them, warms their lamps, shapes their tools, and shakes their bones rises from underneath. To them, the drowned labyrinth is not a pit; it is a vast, cold, breathing body, and they live on the skin of its back, much like the plated grazers and the dark films of olive slick.

They know they are not the first things to crawl across it. Everywhere the water and scraping shells have polished the whalebacks glassy, the pale spirals and curved stone ribs of ancient fossils lie flush with the surface. Children trace those stone shells with bare fingers while waiting by the weeping seams, noting how closely the dead rings locked in the bedrock resemble the living grazers rasping an inch away. Nothing here disappears entirely; it is only ground smooth, soaked in brine, and pressed back into the floor.

That is how they treat their dead.

There is no soil in which to dig a grave, and no wood to build a pyre. When a person’s breath finally stills inside a dome—whether from cold joints and old age, a bad fall on slick rock, or a bellower’s blunt teeth—the kin do not oil the body. They wash it carefully with precious clear water from the throat-bladders and rub salt across the brow and palms. On the next quiet trough, they carry the wrapped body far off the marked trails to a high, lonely salt-dome where no one gathers grazers and no weirs are set. They lay them bare on the pale bedrock, weighted gently with a low ring of cobbles so the rising drafts and splashing spray will not scatter them, and walk away without looking back.

The skimmers do the rest, wheeling down through the drizzle to return the soft flesh to the mist, while salt and cold brine scrub the bones clean until they shine as pale as the fossil spirals beneath them.

Many surges later, the kin return with a clean hide sling. Most of the bleached skeleton is bundled tight, lashed to a heavy grooved stone, and carried to the lip of a deep, quiet sink-throat just as a surge peaks and turns. They stand braced against the wet stone, hands linked, waiting for the water to stop welling upward and begin its long, hollow inhale. As the throat gulps and pulls the grey sheet back down into the dark, they lower the bundle in and let go. The labyrinth takes the dead down into the cold conduits where the pale swimmers breed and the thunder is born—back into the lungs of the world.

Yet they never give away all of a person.

Sometimes a long, pale rib is kept back, scrubbed bright, and wedged firmly into a new cairn along a treacherous ridge, so the dead still stand watch in the fog and guide the living home along the dry spines. And almost always, a single small bone—a finger-joint, a wrist-bone, worn smooth in the pocket of a grief-heavy hide—is carried back to the home dome. On the next trough, a slab is pried carefully loose from the outer skin of the shelter wall, and the small pale bone is pressed deep into the cavity among the crushed grazer-shells, scraped mineral mud, and matted strips of cured hide before the stone is fitted tight again.

An old dome’s walls are thick with generations of them—shells of the grazers that fed them, hides of the bellowers that warmed them, and the small bones of the ancestors who murmured in the yellow lamplight before them—all packed tightly together around the living, holding back the cold mist and softening the basin’s endless roar into a low, steady hum you feel safely in your teeth.
