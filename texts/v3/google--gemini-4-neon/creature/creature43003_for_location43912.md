It takes a long time—sometimes an entire bleached morning after your first blistering dusk—before you realize the roar is not all of it.

Once your ears stop flinching from the bass hum of the kilns and the cannon-crack of distant filament arcs, they begin to sort the thinner layers braided underneath. Somewhere above the grinding bone-gears, tucked neatly into a narrow, bright band of high air right beside the static hiss, there is a small, deliberate sound that has nothing to do with grazing or crushing bedrock.

*Tck.* A pause. *Tck-tck.*

It is crisp and intimate, no louder than a dry fingernail tapped against an unglazed saucer. It lands cleanly beside your left temple, close enough to make you flinch and look over your shoulder. There is nothing on the ledge behind you—only miles of planed gypsum and a distant smelter creeping along the far basin floor.

Then your gaze drops into the ten-foot groove worn through the basalt fifty yards below, and you see the standing stone.

At first it looks like a weathered chimney of white dolomite, six feet tall and lean as a stripped post, leaning slightly against the thermal wind. You would walk past it without a second thought, just as you almost mistook the grazing colossus for an ironstone outcrop—except for its shadow.

While the pale column remains motionless, its shadow kneels on the dark stone, reaches out a long, sharp-angled arm, and lifts a loose sliver of fractured quartz. Nearly twenty seconds later, the pale figure itself bends cleanly at the hip, extends a slender arm, and rises holding the exact shard its shadow already gathered.

If you climb down into the groove and approach—slowly, because the thin air punishes haste—it does not startle or retreat. Nothing up here wastes energy on panic. Even from ten paces off, you can feel the odd, dry stillness radiating from it, a small pocket of composure inside the deafening factory of the plateau.

Up close, you see it belongs to the same mineral kingdom as the titans, built on the same hollow, waterless logic, though worked to a quiet, startling fineness.

Its frame is matte, chalk-white bone—vitreous on the outer plates, porous as pumice within, so light that the scalding updrafts seem to tug gently at its ribs. Like the colossi, it is mostly vacant air. Its chest is an open basket of thin, curved struts with plain white daylight shining straight through from back to front; suspended inside that lattice, held by taut threads of carbonized sinew, hangs a fist-sized knot of layered mica and pale, dry salt. As the figure draws in the blistering wind, the mica-knot shifts subtly in color from ash-gray to a faint, glowing tea-brown, storing warmth and static with a quiet, papery hum.

There is no wet flesh anywhere on it, no oil, no breath-vapor, no soft tissue for the heat to boil. Its face is a smooth, wind-planed shield of pale chalcedony, featureless save for a narrow horizontal slit across the upper third, like the peephole of a kiln. Inside the shade of that slit, tiny faceted grains of dark tourmaline click and resettle as it takes your measure—reading the shimmering thermal blur rising off your clothes, the faint static snapping around your boots, and the strange, heavy, water-logged density of your body.

Look at its hands. Between the tapered wrist-bone and the palm hangs a clean half-inch of scorching daylight. The ankles float the same distance above the flat, wide plates of the feet. Nothing bridges those gaps—no tendon, no wire, no sleeve—only a faint shimmer of ozone and the stubborn local agreement that the hand still belongs to the arm. The fingers flex across the void with a delicate, unhurried precision, turning the shard of quartz over and over against the light.

You notice it carries nothing. There is no satchel, no pouch, no staff, no strip of woven cloth, no mark of a tool anywhere on its body. Up here, dead objects that do not belong to a living circuit cannot last—not because they rot, for there is nothing to rot them, but because the plateau gradually loses track of unattached matter, the way a colossus’s slag ribbons thin out and vanish over an afternoon. If anyone stacked stones into a wall or chipped a bowl from basalt and set it down, the stone would drift out of sequence with its own weight within a season and quietly evaporate into hot haze. So the inhabitants do not build, and they do not own. Whatever they need, they either grow into their own hollow frames or borrow briefly from the loose laws around them.

Watch this one—match its long, gliding stride as it moves down the grooved basalt—and you begin to see how a person makes a living out of heat, grit, and weathered physics.

Half a mile down-basin, it reaches a fresh ribbon of slag laid down an hour ago by a passing bellows-grazer. The copper filament running through the white-hot crust is already beginning to blur at the edges, thinning out as the world starts to forget it owes the ribbon mass. The pale figure crouches beside it and holds its disconnected palms two inches above the humming thread. Tiny blue hairlines of static leap cleanly across the gap into its finger-bones; inside its hollow chest, the mica-knot brightens and sings a low, dry chord. It takes no solid matter from the stone—only skims the voltage and the thermal gradient before the ribbon ghosts away into nothing.

Farther on, it pauses beside a shallow saucer ground into the pale dolomite, where a colossus’s detached flywheel-shadow is still turning lazily on its own, three days after the giant itself crossed the eastern shelf. The figure sits cross-legged at the shadow’s rim—its shins floating an inch clear of its knees—and with a bare mineral toe nudges the shard of quartz it carried earlier into the spinning dark edge. As the phantom wheel catches the stone with a dry, gritty hiss and grinds it to powder, a sharp snap of piezoelectric light jumps out of the dust. The figure leans its open ribcage forward into the flash, letting the crisp jolt settle into its salt-core.

Look closely at its collarbones while it sits. Growing upward from each shoulder is a delicate, branched sprig of pale calcified bone, no thicker than a quill, curving slightly outward like a spare pair of tuning forks. Now and then, over the course of a week, a walker will lean against a flat shelf of abrasive basalt and slowly draw one of those prongs across the rock, wearing a microscopic notch into its edge until the dry wind blowing across its shoulder sings at a cleaner pitch. That is the closest thing to a tool it possesses: its own skeleton, patiently tuned against the plateau.

Then it lifts one floating hand and flicks its porcelain thumb lightly against its upper sternum.

*Tck.*

The sound is tiny—barely louder than a dry seed cracking—yet because the thin air refuses to soften distance, the click rides cleanly out across the basin without fading, threading the narrow acoustic seam above the titans’ roar. Ten miles, twenty miles, thirty miles—it lands as sharply on the far dolomite rim as it did beside your boot.

Four seconds later—or perhaps four seconds *before* the thumb actually moved—an answer arrives right beside your ear: a deeper, darker double-click, carried on a faint rasp of ozone. That is a neighbor, twelve miles south on the ironstone flats.

Slowly, the true scale of their society comes into focus.

Across six hundred square miles of blinding, stepped tableland, there may be only a few hundred of them. You could walk for a week without seeing two figures within the same basin; to them, three people within sight of one another is a crowded hall. Yet they are never alone. They inhabit a single vast, unroofed room where every whisper reaches every corner intact.

Their manners are built entirely out of restraint. To strike rock carelessly, or to raise a loud noise, would be an act of violence—a sharp blow laid straight inside the ear-cavities of everyone from horizon to horizon. So they converse in tiny, arid percussions: a knuckle tapped on a collarbone, two dry pebbles rolled gently inside a hollow palate, a sliver of wind drawn across the mica-core in a brief, papery reed-note. They know one another without needing to meet for years. Someone whose bones precipitated over pale gypsum rings chalky and light; someone raised near the basalt shelves strikes sharp and dark as slate. And the older a walker grows, the wider the daylight gaps between its joints become, weaving a faint, unmistakable hiss of ozone into every syllable it taps.

Without roads, walls, or writing, they still leave signs for one another—made out of the one thing the plateau never sweeps cleanly away on the first try.

If a walker discovers a fresh quartz vein exposed by stamp-beetles, or a grazing colossus whose rear axle is beginning to lock, it steps onto an open shelf of pale dolomite, plants its wide feet, raises one detached arm to point steadily down-basin, and goes utterly still. It stands like that through the pitiless white noon, through the dusty-gold dusk, and through the scalding, heat-flowing night, letting its internal charge bleed slowly downward into the rock until its shadow cures into the flatstone like a dark mineral stain.

By morning, it lowers its arm and walks away.

Its shadow stays behind. For the next three or four weeks, anyone crossing that shelf will find a crisp, solitary silhouette standing on the bare rock, extending a thin black arm toward the seam or the ailing titan. It turns slowly over the days, always slightly out of step with the real sun, until the plateau finishes forgetting it and the dark stain thins back into plain dolomite. No cairn, no road, no clutter—just a polite, temporary darkness left on loan.

Every so often, a dry, urgent cadence of clicks passes across the basins and draws four or five of them into the same valley—a rare gathering that takes two full days of unhurried walking to assemble.

Down on the flats, a grazing colossus has slipped badly out of balance. A wedge of fused silica has jammed inside one of its vast bone-and-nickel reduction gears, and its graphite glands have baked dry; the agonizing shriek coming off its haunch tears straight across forty miles of lossless air, setting every walker’s open ribs humming in sympathetic discomfort. Worse, a knot of three-legged transformer-parasites has latched onto its rear tendons and fed until they have grown impossibly heavy without growing larger, pressing the titan’s massive back hooves inch by slow inch into solid basalt as if the bedrock were warm wax.

The pale walkers do not carry levers or hammers. Two of them stride straight under the beast’s glowing, cherry-red kiln-belly and begin to climb the hollow cathedral of its hind leg. Watching them ascend is quietly unsettling: where the colossus’s knee floats half an inch clear of its socket inside a crackling halo of electrostatic repulsion, the walkers simply step up onto the empty, snapping air as though it were a rung.

One reaches the screaming gear and does not pry at the fused silica. Instead, it presses its hollow porcelain chest flat against the vibrating axle and listens, adjusting the taut sinews around its mica-core until its own frame matches the exact jagged frequency of the jam—and then deliberately drags its resonance half a beat late. The lag bleeds outward from its ribs into the nickel-bone. For ten quiet seconds, the friction and the torque fall politely out of sequence with each other; the silica wedge slips free into the bright air as harmless dust, and the massive gear settles back onto its floating cushion with a deep, relieved *thrum*.

Below, two other walkers stand beside the swollen transformer-parasites and gently tap their own collarbone-prongs with their floating fingertips—not striking the parasites, only mimicking the crisp, crackling prelude of a descending filament-swarm discharge. Startled in their dull, heavy way, the parasites vent their stored current in a long, shimmering sigh, shedding tons of phantom weight in an instant, and winch themselves lazily back down the voltage-fields toward the boulders below.

The colossus does not thank them—it barely registers beings no larger than its own axle-pins—but as its spine settles back into clean current, a broad, singing overflow of voltage washes down its oxidized ribs. The walkers cling to the open struts for an hour, drinking the spare charge until their tourmaline eyes warm to a quiet, smoky amber, before stepping back down onto the flatstone and drifting miles apart again.

Watching their dry, un-fleshed frames scatter across the glare, you wonder how a people without water, blood, or wombs keeps its sparse numbers from wearing down to nothing.

It happens rarely, and always where the sky and the leftover dark cross paths. Now and then, when a drifting filament-swarm throws down a blinding white lightning-arc across a basin, the bolt strikes the exact patch of dolomite where two abandoned signpost-shadows happen to overlap. The silica dust fuses instantly into a fist-sized, hollow knot of pale glass—a fulgurite with a sharp little shadow of its own that refuses to match the sun’s angle.

Most of those knots cool, crack loudly under the afternoon glare, and crumble back into abrasive grit. Once in a great while, one holds. It traps a tiny, stubborn remainder of the bolt’s charge inside its hollow core and begins to emit a faint, clumsy, irregular *tick… tick-tick* into the lossless air.

Thirty miles off, every walker on the plateau hears it—a bright, unpolished sound with no mineral depth, stumbling out of rhythm with the stamp-beetles and the bellows. Whoever has wandered longest without a task turns aside from its path and walks two days across the shimmering basins to find the spot.

It kneels beside the tiny glass-knot and stays there. Not for an hour, not for a day, but for a season. It positions its own body between the fused glass and the bedrock tremors of passing stamp-beetles, taking the shock up into its floating ankles so the fragile shell will not fracture. It nudges microscopic flakes of gypsum and cooling copper-dust close to the knot’s rim. Every few minutes, it flicks a single, calm, unhurried click against its collarbone, giving the raw spark a steady pulse to lean against.

Glacially, week by blistering week, the knot begins to precipitate thin struts of pale chalk-bone around its humming core, borrowing stray static from its keeper’s open hands to grow a lattice of ribs, then slender, clumsy limbs that have not yet learned how to stay disconnected. By the time it can lift itself off the flatstone—still small, barely knee-high, its joints tight and heavy, its voice a bright, glassy chirp—it already knows the mineral pitch of every neighbor scattered across six hundred miles of stone.

And when they grow truly old, they do not rot or wither. Up here, aging is simply a slow widening.

Over centuries of drinking current and leaning into broken laws, the clean daylight gaps in a walker’s body broaden. An elder’s hands may hover four inches beyond its wrists; its wind-planed head floats half a foot above its collarbones, turning thoughtfully atop a quiet pillar of shimmering ozone. Its ribs open wider to the wind until it is nearly as vacant as the titans, and the static hiss woven through its clicks grows louder than the tap of bone. Eventually, the scalding updrafts begin to tug at its loose parts. It takes too much quiet concentration just to keep a foot walking in the same minute as its knee.

When that hour comes, the elder makes no long speech—only draws a single, soft, papery chord through its open mica-core, heard cleanly in every basin from shelf to shelf. Across miles of empty stone, walkers pause beside stray shadows and cooling slag, tapping one low, polite reply against their chests. Then the elder turns toward the far rim of the plateau.

Out where the tableland breaks off into a hazy drop of terraced stone and bright empty air—where the endless screaming labor of ten thousand living engines has rubbed local space so thin that the jagged edge appears twice, once in solid rock and once hovering faintly thirty yards past it, slightly more eroded, its shadows leaning at a different hour’s angle—the elder does not stop at the cliff.

It waits at the brink until the real sun and the phantom ridge’s shadows settle into an angle only its tourmaline eyes can read. Then it steps cleanly out across the thirty yards of scorching emptiness.

It does not fall. Its floating foot lands smoothly on the fainter echo of stone.

If you raise binoculars from the solid rim and squint into the bleached glare, you can see them there—a sparse, unhurried row of elders spaced hundreds of yards apart along the hovering lip of rock, seated facing outward over the vast drop toward whatever lower world once held the plateau up. They no longer feed. They no longer tend jammed axles or leave their shadows on the dolomite. Their disconnected hands rest in mid-air above their knees; their smooth, pale heads float calmly above their shoulders, tilted slightly downward, as if listening for a sound from miles below that never arrives.

Over decades, the plateau gently does to them what it does to cooling slag. Their mass thins out long before their shape does. One season you make out a chalk-white lattice of hollow bone; the next, mostly bright daylight and a crisp, patient shadow leaning at yesterday’s angle on rock that is barely there. And even after the last plate of bone has been cleanly forgotten by the air, if you stand very still at the rim at dusty-gold dusk—just as the stone gives back its heat and the dry breeze begins flowing downhill into the hollows where the great kilns settle—you can still catch, right beside your ear, a tiny, polite, dry *tck*, keeping unhurried time above the roar.
