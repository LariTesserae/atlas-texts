Anyone who steps down behind the berm and stays for an hour will leave certain that the hollow is empty. The eyes adjust to the pearlescent dimness, sweep the open silt floor, trace the six flat slabs along the eastern curve, note the undisturbed clay beneath the rear travertine, and find nothing out of place. Even the sound confirms it: the room holds only the weight of its own quiet, punctuated twice a minute by falling water.

The mistake is looking for life at the speed of the plain outside.

Along the curved rear wall, where the terraced travertine steps down toward the narrow trough of damp clay, one of the broad, rounded ledges is not stone. At first glance—and at second, and third—it reads as a slumped lobe of flowstone, or the pale crown of a roof boulder long since absorbed into the shelf. It is roughly the bulk of a curled sheep, or a grown person folded tight to the rock, though lower and broader through the flank, with no sharp silhouette to catch the flat wash of reflected light. Its upper back is dull ivory and dusty ochre, faintly rippled where a slow ceiling seep has traced the same vertical tracks down its ribs for years.

Only after the eyes have grown thoroughly used to the low contrast does the boundary separate itself. Beneath the pale, matte crest of the back, where the body tucks inward out of reach of the drip, the mineral surface stops. What lies underneath is hide—bare, smooth, and faintly creased, the color of damp calcareous clay along the sheltered flanks and softening to weathered cream across the throat and belly. There is no fur, no scale, no bristle. Like everything else in the hollow, every edge of it has been blunted.

It rests entirely on stone, propped in the smooth saddle where a travertine terrace meets the upper face of a half-sunk boulder, never letting its weight down into the damp clay trough below. Its four limbs are heavy-boned and roughly equal in length, folded cleanly beneath its ribs so that it sits low and settled, like water held flush with a rim. The limbs do not end in claws or fingers, but in broad, flattened, four-lobed pads—leathery and pale underneath, faintly crusted on top. When it shifts, those pads lift and press against bare limestone without scraping, without digging in. It leaves no tracks because it never crosses the central silt, and it never sets foot in the clay.

Its head does not rise on a distinct neck; instead, the rounded slope of its shoulders narrows smoothly forward into a broad, blunt muzzle, creased along the sides like old folded cloth. Its mouth is a long, quiet seam that stays shut, echoing the horizontal bedding planes of the wall behind it. Above the seam sit two narrow vertical nostrils that barely widen when it breathes.

And its eyes—if they are open—are the single dark thing about it. Set wide and flush with the pale brow, they are round, flat, and glossed like oiled slate, covered by a clear, thickened film that keeps them slick in the dead air without needing to blink. They do not dart or scan. There is nothing moving in the hollow to follow. Instead, they hold the whole chamber at once in pale, low-contrast stillness: the dim mouth under the overhang, the three rubbed hollows on the central boulder, the faint red iron-smudges high on the western cheek of rock, the exact waterline of the basins beside its shoulder.

If you were to lay a palm against its flank—past the thin chalky crust of its upper back, where the bare hide stays sheltered—it would feel neither cold like a lizard nor warm like a mammal. It carries the exact neutral temperature of the room. Thirty feet of sun-baked limestone do the work of keeping it warm; the creature spends almost no energy manufacturing heat of its own. Its blood moves with the patience of the roof percolation. If you pressed an ear to its pale ribs, you might count twenty seconds, or thirty, before a single deep, muffled thud answered against the bone—an interval close enough to the ceiling drips that the two rhythms occasionally fall into step, and then drift apart again over an hour.

It draws a breath every four or five minutes. There is no heave of the chest, only a slow, almost imperceptible widening of the lower ribs, a long pause while the heavy, mineral-scented air rests inside its lungs, and a quiet release too slight to tremble the nearest frond of maidenhair fern yards away.

The crust along its back and shoulders is not a shell it was born with. It is simply what happens to anything that holds still long enough beneath a limestone vault. Over months and years, the lime-saturated seep lays down a skin of calcite thinner than paper across the creature’s upper hide, coating the ridges of its spine and the folded tops of its haunches in the same dull ivory and wet pewter as the travertine shelves. It does not bind the creature fast. Whenever it finally shifts—once in a fortnight, or once in a season, turning an inch toward a basin or adjusting the angle of a folded shoulder—the dry mineral rime fractures along the creases of its joints with a tiny, crisp *tick*, softer than a fingernail tapping chalk. A pinch of fine pale dust sifts down into the crevices of the rock, indistinguishable from the calcareous silt of the floor. Over the following months, the seep quietly seals the hairline seam again.

Its life runs on almost nothing.

To drink, it lowers its blunt muzzle with glacial deliberation until the lower edge of its mouth meets the exact surface of one of the shallow rear basins. It never leans against the delicate calcite rim, never breaks the crusted lip; it draws the lime-heavy water in so gently that the meniscus barely dimples, and no ripple crosses to the far edge of the pool.

To feed, it waits until the dimness turns to full night and then extends a broad, soft, pale tongue—rough as worn pumice—across the wettest travertine lips beside its resting place. It gathers the thin smear of dark olive moss, the flat, leathery scales of liverwort, the invisible film of mineral-fed slick clinging to the damp stone, and the dissolved lime itself. It takes no more than a palm’s breadth of stone in a month, moving slowly along the wet flank so that the faint dark velvet has years to close back over the rock before it returns. What little waste its body produces is chalky and sparse, released directly onto the wet travertine where the slow seep dissolves it and plates it over as fresh mineral crust.

In this way, it belongs to the hollow’s accounting rather than the plain’s. Nothing arrives to feed it, and nothing rots because of it.

Though it never leaves the shelter of the berm, it is not cut off from the outer world; it simply receives it delayed and smoothed out, the way the rock does. The seasons beyond the escarpment do not reach it as wind or glare, but as intervals. A long summer drought on the coastal plain arrives inside the vault six months later as a slight lengthening between drops—the *plip* into the high basin stretching from thirty seconds to thirty-eight, the dull tap on the clay thinning to a whisper. A wet autumn arrives in spring as a faint brightening of the damp film along the rear wall, a fuller curve to the meniscus of the pools. The creature reads the sky through thirty feet of limestone, knowing rain only after the rock has finished digesting it.

Its hearing is built for this. Behind the wide, slate-dark eyes, two shallow, skin-covered hollows sit flush with the sides of its skull, tuned to the curve of the vault. It knows every seep in the chamber by pitch and weight: the clear drop that strikes the deepest pool, the flatter note of the shallow basin farther along, the slow, irregular seep along the western flank that takes four minutes to swell and fall. It hears the basins themselves change over decades, the tone rising by a hair’s breadth as calcite thickens on their floors.

It does not sleep the way creatures of the daylight do, dropping into darkness every evening and waking with the sun. It watches the pearlescent grey at the entrance slope warm slightly toward midday and then drain down to black, and often its wide dark eyes remain open through all of it. Other times—for weeks at a stretch, especially when the delayed drought thins the ceiling seeps—its pale, thick lower lids rise smoothly up until they meet the brow. With its eyes shut, the last dark marks on its body disappear. Its face becomes as seamless as a water-worn boulder, and its breath slows to once every ten minutes, hovering just inside the margin of wakefulness, waiting out the dry spell the way the maidenhair ferns hold near dormancy.

Very rarely—perhaps once in a year, or once in several—it leaves the rear travertine.

It does not cross the open floor. Instead, it slides slowly off the wet shelf onto the bare bedrock skirting the eastern curve, moving with an unhurried, heavy grace—one broad pad lifting, hovering a fraction of an inch above the stone, and settling forward without sound. Step by slow step, taking the better part of a night to travel ten yards, it makes its way to the arc of six flat limestone slabs laid end to end along the drier flank. There it folds its limbs beneath itself and rests across two of the slabs, clear of the silt that has crept halfway up their sides.

The rock there is drier, smoother, and a fraction of a degree warmer than the rear wall, closer to the reflected light sliding under the overhang. For a week or two, the damp pewter crust along its back dries to pale cream and flakes thinly along its ribs, adding a dusting of chalk to the silt around the slabs. Its slate eyes face outward toward the pale brow of the berm, watching the slow, flat tide of overcast afternoon brighten and recede. Then, during some quiet night when the air inside is indistinguishable from the still air outside, it lifts its weight, turns back along the stone curve, and returns to the travertine shelves before the crust on its flanks can dry too deep.

It was already in the hollow when those six slabs were carried in and laid flat.

It was smaller then—its hide thinner, its back almost free of mineral rime—and it lay pressed deep into the rear curve while the fast, warm-blooded visitors came down the slope behind the berm. They did not bring torches; the air stayed clear of smoke. They sat on the flat stones they had arranged, rubbed pale dust in the three oval hollows of the central boulder, pressed two quiet smudges of red ochre high onto the western wall, and sat for long stretches without speaking, letting their breathing fall closer to the tempo of the drips.

Before they stopped coming, someone laid seven smooth, dark river pebbles—basalt carried from a streambed far across the plain—in a loose cluster on the dry ledge beside the rear travertine. When they were first set down, they were loose against the pale limestone, clicking faintly if a hand brushed them. Now a translucent skin of calcite has crept up their lower curves and cemented them fast to the rock.

The creature often rests with its blunt cheek or the edge of one pale fore-pad lying along that dry ledge, an inch from the cluster. It does not move them or try to pry them free. In the dimness, the seven dark, wet-looking stones are the exact color and gloss of its own unblinking eyes, and it has stayed beside them long enough to watch the limestone slowly claim them, skin by paper-thin skin, at the same pace it claims its own quiet back.

If someone descends the worn entrance slope today, their boots grinding faintly on the settled rockfall of the berm, the disturbance arrives inside the vault as a small violence. The air shifts; a loose grain of silt rolls; the quiet breaks.

The creature does not flee, and it does not stir to defend itself. Speed is not in its body, nor is alarm. It simply pauses its breath, holding the heavy, mineral-scented air inside its broad chest for ten minutes, fifteen minutes, twenty. If its eyes are open, the pale lower lids may slide halfway up, narrowing the dark gloss of its gaze to a hairline shadow indistinguishable from a horizontal seam in the travertine. It offers nothing to catch the eye—no sudden twitch, no flare of color, no sharp shadow against the rounded wall.

The visitor walks out into the open silt, pauses beside the knee-high boulder, listens to the water drop *plip* into the rear basin, admires the stillness of the maidenhair ferns, and climbs back out into the wind on the plain, carrying away the impression of a place wholly untouched by living things.

Only if someone stepped off the silt onto the six flat eastern slabs, sat down with their back to the pale stone, and stayed utterly still—not for an hour, not until their patience ran thin, but through the whole flat sweep of afternoon until the lip of the berm began to drain of light—might the hollow finally give itself up.

First, after long silence, would come a tiny, dry *tick* from the rear wall, softer than a drop striking damp clay, as a hairline seam of calcite gave way across a shifting shoulder. Then a slow, almost imperceptible exhalation—not a wind, only a faint thickening of the mineral scent in the still air. And looking again at the rounded travertine shelves in the last pearlescent grey before nightfall, the watcher would see two flat circles of oiled slate resting flush against the pale stone, steady as the basins beside them, holding the dimness as patiently as the hollow holds its shape.
