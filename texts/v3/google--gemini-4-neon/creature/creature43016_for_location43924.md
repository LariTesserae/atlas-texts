Nothing that belongs here lingers in the open.

The bright, stretched middle of the pith floor is an exposure—too wide for the eyes to measure honestly, too loud with echoes that arrive before their causes, lit by an afternoon sun that has no sky behind it and never softens toward evening. Anything that tries to make a life out in the glare gets caught between a pressure-surge and a stray shadow, or simply dries down to husk in the hot, salt-lifting draft.

Instead, life in this buried immensity is built like a splinter. It lives in the hairline tears, the sliding sheaths, the wet margins where one colossal layer of living tissue grinds past another.

The smallest residents are barely thicker than paper. The translucent white sap-crawlers that slip out beneath the wooden wedges have no true thickness to them at all; their guts and pale nerve-cords are laid out side by side in a single flat plane, like leaves pressed inside a book, so that a closing seam can squeeze down to the width of a fingernail without popping them. They are deaf to the booming roar of the walls and blind to the crisp daylight, navigating strictly by the sweet metallic scent of fresh ruptures and the microscopic salt-trails left by their own kind. When they scuttle across the pith, their tiny, brittle claws strike loud as dropped pins because the floor beneath them is hollow and taut as a drumhead.

That resonance is what feeds the ambushers.

They cling to the underside of the humming bast-cords, or flatten themselves along the razor-sharp flutes of the upper ribs where their pale-green, dry-shelled carapaces pass for curled shavings of cambium. They do not hunt by sight—their eyes are tiny, milky beads crusted over with dried salt. They hunt through their feet. Each of their six long, hinged legs ends in a forked bristle that grips the vibrating cord and reads the whole chamber through its hum. When a sap-crawler clicks across the pith three paces below, the ambusher feels the impact through the cord a fraction of a second *before* the sound even reaches the air. It releases its hold on that pre-echo, dropping stiff-legged like a snapped twig, folding its serrated forelimbs shut on the crawler at the exact instant the victim's own steps catch up with it.

Then there are the things that only exist for sixty seconds at a time.

When the hydraulic surge doubles and the walls flush dark jade, the stomata along the upper ceiling flare open with their collective *shhhhk*—and out of those microscopic green mouths spill dust-fine mites, pale as chalk, pouring down the silver streaks of sweat to graze on the dissolving mineral crust along the troughs. They work in a silent frenzy while the green steam hangs thick. The instant the deep thump behind the walls slackens, they race back up the flutes toward the closing pores. Half of them never make it; the stomata snap flush like tiny shears, slicing the laggards clean in half and leaving minute white flecks dried into the next cycle's salt.

And deeper still—never truly inside the chamber, only glimpsed as dark, whipping blurs behind the translucent jade walls during a heavy surge—move the vein-runners. They are long, eyeless, silver-pale ribbons of muscle and fringe that ride the staggering subterranean currents, clinging to the interior of the vascular pipes with rings of soft hooks so they aren't torn apart by the flow. Once in a hundred pressure-cycles, when a seam cracks open with a rifle-shot report and tears straight through a swollen vessel, one of them gets blown out onto the dry pith floor in a spray of clear sap. It thrashes violently for ten heartbeats, completely silent until a beat *after* its tail slaps the floor, and then the warm greenhouse draft drinks the moisture straight out of its skin. Within a minute it stiffens into a flat strip of pale, salty leather—which is a windfall, if one of the wedge-owners happens to be listening from the other side of the rib.

***

The ones who own the wedges are not monsters, and if they were ever ordinary people, the buried green has been remaking them for too many generations to tell.

Up close, in the brief window between surges when one peels back a vertical rib and squeezes into the daylight, you see immediately that their bodies are built for compression. They are short-waisted, hunched, and heavily braced through the shoulders, with thick collarbones and deep, springy ribcages that can bow slightly under lateral weight without snapping. They are built to serve as wedges themselves. When a wooden billet splits under the slow, tons-heavy healing of a wall, one of them can plant bare heels against the opposite curve, brace its broad back against the groaning rib, and hold a pocket open by sheer skeletal tension for twenty seconds while a kin-mate pounds a fresh pin home.

Their skin is the color of freshly peeled willow—pale ivory with a faint, cold undertone of green in the thinner places over the wrists and throat—and it never browns under the sourceless afternoon light. Instead, it toughens into a waxy, parchment-dry hide dusted white in every crease with mineral salt. Because every edge in their world is sharp enough to slice flesh, their forearms and shins are cross-hatched with fine, straight scars. They do not bleed for long; they keep lumps of thick, glistening amber sap tucked behind their ears or inside folded leaf-pouches, and smear it straight into fresh cuts. The sap sets hard and glossy within two pressure-cycles, sealing the wound into a smooth brown ridge. The oldest among them carry a dense lattice of these amber seams across their knuckles and shoulders, giving them the look of pale wood that has been split and mended with resin.

Their hands are their truest tools—huge for their frames, with flattened, heavily calloused knuckles and broad, spatulate thumbs tough enough to pluck a vibrating bast-cord or pry a razor-edged rib without drawing blood. The outer heel of each palm carries a thick, horn-hard pad of built-up callus, used for setting wooden pegs before the river-pebble comes out. Their nails are thick, ridged, and stained permanently greenish-amber from scraping cambium and digging salt.

Everything about their heads is shaped to survive the roar.

Their ears are small, thick-rimmed, and pinned nearly flat against the skull, backed by dense little knots of muscle that pull the outer ridge down tight over the canal whenever a seam begins to groan toward a gunshot split. In the open pockets, airborne sound is too loud and too treacherous to trust anyway—half of it echoing out of order, arriving from the wrong wall or half a beat ahead of the event. So they listen with bone instead. When a wedge-keeper steps out of a crease and pauses by the humming bast-cord, it isn't merely testing the knot; sometimes it will lean in and touch its calloused thumb, or the flat edge of its front teeth, lightly against the trembling fiber.

Through that single low bass note, carried straight into the jaw and skull, the entire chamber lays itself bare. They can feel the exact lateral load pressing on every wooden wedge in the walls. They can feel the fullness of the hidden hydraulic surge building ten paces back in the green mass. Most importantly, they can feel whether the room is *settled* or *slipping*. A healthy pocket hums on one clean, steady pitch. When the pitch splits into a faint, wavering double-note, they know the room's time is coming unglued—that shadows are about to lag, that cracks will start jumping across the ribs, that objects left on the floor may drift out of reach.

Their eyes stay narrowed to wet, glossy slits whenever they step into a lit cavity. Thick, heavy lids and a constant film of clear fluid protect them from both the unrelenting, sourceless glare and the stinging chalk of wind-lifted salts. They rarely look directly across the stretched center of the floor; it makes even them dizzy, watching six paces of bare pith turn into thirty beneath their stride. They hug the curved perimeter, moving with a low, crabwise economy, never lifting their feet higher than necessary.

And their reflexes are tuned half a second out of joint with the rest of creation.

Watch one work in the dry trough of the cycle and it looks almost like sleight of hand. It flinches its shoulder sideways a heartbeat *before* a sharp crack splits the rib beside its head and sprays hot clear sap across the spot where its ear just was. It swings its smooth river-pebble down onto a wooden peg, and its wrist checks the rebound *before* the stone even touches the wood, reacting to the sharp pre-echo that rang back from the opposite wall half a beat early. They do not think of this as foresight. To them, sound simply arrives before impact the way lightning arrives before thunder in a world with a sky. You learn to move on the warning, or the walls take your fingers.

***

They do not live in the bright, roaring pockets. The pockets are only workshops, salt-pans, and traps—temporary blisters pried open inside the living mass and held apart against its will.

When a wedge-keeper peels back a vertical rib and slips backward through the wet crease, letting the edges snap flush with a pop behind it, it is returning to the *sleeves*.

Beyond the seamless walls of the cavity, there is no open ground and no sourceless daylight. The afternoon sun only exists where an air-pocket has been torn wide; the moment the living fibers close together, the world goes pitch dark, muffled to a deep, visceral thrum, and impossibly tight. The sleeves are narrow interstitial gaps between the vast, overlapping sheaths of the buried plant—warm, slick, dark corridors no wider than a bent elbow, constantly flexing as the outer layers slide past one another with a slow, wet sigh.

Here, the wedge-folk are entirely at ease. They travel in small, close-knit kin-bands of four to seven, moving through the dark seams by touch, warmth, and the low vibration of the vascular tides. Where an open room forces them to shout into a drum, the sleeves carry the smallest tremors straight through the pith. They do not use words in the open air, but inside the dark creases—pressed shoulder to shoulder, jawbones resting against the same warm stalk—they communicate in a soft, rhythmic language of hums, throat-clicks, and knuckle-taps. Across longer distances, through whole thicknesses of living tissue, they strike deliberate *thocks* with a stone or a hardwood billet, carefully synced *off* the hydraulic pulse. A double-knock followed by a dampened scrape means a rich salt-pocket has just been pried; three sharp, evenly spaced blows mean a sheath is shifting fast and the sleeve ahead is about to shear shut.

They sleep wedged tightly into the warmest folds, curled around short, thick bars of heartwood—breath-pegs—held braced between their knees and breastbones. Without a breath-peg, a sudden surge during sleep could press a sleeper's ribs flat before they woke. Their young are born small, pale, and remarkably quiet, with ears already folded tight and fists that clamp instinctively onto reed-cords and hair. A child learns to read the warning tremor of a surge against its cheek and set its heels against a closing seam long before it ever sees the blinding afternoon light of an open chamber.

To survive in a world made entirely of soft, wet, relentless growth, you need one thing above all else: hardness.

Nothing in the sleeves is hard. Everything yields, swells, slides, or heals. And so the wedge-folk are obsessed, unsentimental curators of foreign matter.

The open pockets are how they mine it. When they pry a cavity wide and stake it open with wedges, they disrupt the smooth hydraulic flow of the plant around the hollow, creating a tight, straining eddy in the living tissue. Over dozens of pressure-cycles, the colossal buried growth pushes upward and outward around the obstruction—and whatever ancient, indigestible debris the plant swallowed centuries ago as it consumed rivers, ridges, and forgotten valleys gets slowly worked toward the strained seams of the pocket, like splinters migrating out of a swollen palm.

That is where the smooth river-pebbles come from. That is where the dense knots of dead heartwood come from, and the occasional flake of pale grey flint.

A river-pebble is never abandoned; it is slung in knotted reed-cord against the collarbone, worn slicker by generations of calloused palms. When they need to harden fresh wooden billets to drive into a seam, they cannot build a fire—there is no dry tinder to spare, and the sixty-second sweat would drown a blaze anyway. Instead, they find a hairline crack opening onto one of the great outer sheaths that slides past at terrifying speed during a pressure-surge. Bracing their feet, they jam the raw tip of a heartwood peg just a finger's width into the rushing friction. The living outer wall screams against the dead wood; a sharp puff of bitter white smoke hisses back into the sleeve, and when they yank the billet free three seconds later, its tip is charred glossy black, fire-hardened without a flame.

And the pale bone wedges—the thick splinters battered flat on their butts, the slender awls tucked into every reed-sling—do not come from buried rivers.

Nothing rots in this place. There is no soil to dig a grave into, and if a body is left in a sleeve, the plant simply seals around it, digests the soft parts in warm, acidic sap, and sweeps the rest away into the endless green pulse. When an elder dies—crushed by a missed surge, or worn down until their joints lock stiff with salt—the kin-band does not leave them to be swallowed whole. They strip the body cleanly in the dry trough of a quiet pocket, giving the flesh back to the wet seams, and keeping the femurs, the shins, the thick forearms. Dried in the crisp afternoon light, scraped smooth with a sharp flake, and lashed with bast-sinew, an ancestor's bones go straight back into the splits—groaning under the weight of the walls, holding open just enough hollow air for their children to scrape salt and braid cord.

***

Even with all their craft, they live on borrowed space, and they know it.

A wedged pocket serves them well for a while. It gives them the hot dry draft they need to cure bast-fiber, dry out salt-stiff strips of blown vein-runner, and evaporate the stomata-sweat down to thick chalky crusts of mineral salt in the floor-troughs. It draws out the sap-crawlers and lets them harvest the brittle, protein-rich ambushers off the humming cords—snatched cleanly off the fiber with a quick pinch before the creature's pre-echo can warn it.

But the plant does not like being held open, and the longer a pocket stays wedged against its will, the more badly the rules inside it begin to fray.

The wedge-folk watch the signs carefully.

At first it is just the normal lag—the half-beat offset between a droplet's *tok* and its landing, the double-ring of a hammer blow. Then the shadows begin to lose their manners. A wedge-keeper working along the eastern rib notices its own shadow stretching toward the center, raising its stone pebble a full second late, or flexing a wrist at a sharp, impossible middle joint. The pale shadow of that tall, wind-tossed stalk appears on the smooth green wall—swaying lazily in a breeze that belongs to some sun-struck meadow miles above, or centuries gone.

They never step on that swaying shadow. Not out of reverence, and not out of fear of spirits, but from hard physical experience: put your bare heel across a shadow that doesn't belong to the room, and your own foot goes numb and heavy for three pressure-cycles, dragging a beat behind the rest of your stride.

And then things begin to go *unseated*.

A tool set down too far from the walls—like a leaf-shaped flake of pale grey chert, lashed with glossy resin to a curved root-grip—begins to lose its purchase on the current version of the chamber. The pure-plant world hates the idea of stone; left sitting on bare pith without a warm hand to insist on what it is, the flint starts to smudge at its edges, softening visually as the room tries to edit it back into thorn or shell. The pale ribbon of freshly shaved cambium curled beside it stops wilting, pinned permanently three seconds after the cut.

When a wedge-keeper pauses near that knife and chooses not to pick it up, it is not confusion. It is discipline. Grab an unseated tool before its two overlapping versions of the room finish locking together, and your fingers may close on empty air while the blade opens a clean wet gash across your shadow's palm—a cut that only begins bleeding on your real skin a full pressure-cycle later. So they test the humming bast-cord with a thumb, listen to the wavering split in its bass note, and leave the knife where it lies. Sometimes, ten cycles later, the overlap snaps flush, the cambium shaving finally curls dry, and the knife can be retrieved. More often, one quiet trough, the knife is simply gone—reclaimed by whatever adjacent pocket dropped it there.

When the pitch of the bast-cord splits too wide to hold a steady note—when cracks begin zipping shut in reverse and jumping two feet along the wall, and the afternoon light starts throwing shadows at two conflicting angles at once—the kin-band knows the pocket has grown too old.

They do not fight it.

In the quietest trough between surges, three or four of them peel back the vertical ribs around the perimeter at once. Moving fast, never crossing the stretched center, they untie the humming bast-cord and coil it cleanly—the moment the bass drone cuts off, the roar of the hidden rivers seems to swell twice as loud. They scrape the last chalky salt from the gutters into folded leaf-pouches. With braced backs and bone levers, they work loose every intact bone pin and every undamaged charred billet they can pry free without letting the walls slam shut on their wrists. Whatever wedges the plant has already bitten too deep into—splintered heartwood pinched half-flat by tons of jade-green muscle—they leave behind.

One by one, they slip backward into the dark vertical tears. The last to go pauses for a fraction of a second in the bright, steady, impossible afternoon glare, eyes narrowed to wet slits, glancing once at the smudged stone knife still lying isolated in the middle of the floor.

Then the rib snaps flush behind it with a loud, wet pop.

Inside the empty, blazing chamber, the pressure-surge doubles. Without the tension of the bast-cord or the fresh pins to check them, the curved walls bow inward with a deep, woody groan. The leftover wedges crack like rifle shots—their snapping heard cleanly from the opposite wall half a beat before the wood actually shears—and the living green closes seamlessly over the splinters, swallowing the light, the stretched space, and the swaying phantom stalk until there is not even a seam left to mark where the room had been.

And twelve paces away, deep in the pitch-dark, sliding quiet of a fresh sleeve, a smooth river-pebble rises in a broad, salt-dusted hand.

*Thock—thock—thock.*

Steady, deliberate, striking cleanly out of time with the pulse, prying a brand-new sliver of air open against a whole buried world trying to heal.
