If you walk the high spans with the brisk, forward-leaning stride of someone used to flat ground, you can cross three weeping belts and a dry belt without ever laying eyes on the people who keep this world from coming undone. You will see only what they leave in their wake—or in their prelude: a freshly pared shoot weeping pale milk, a coil of green reed-cord lying tight and damp beside an ancient splice, the face of a thornwood mallet still faintly warm in its dry hollow beneath a joint.

You miss them for three reasons, and only the first is the size of the lattice.

The second is that you keep looking along the top of the deck. A braided trunk is round, and its gentle pull belongs to its own heartwood, not to any buried earth. While your boots press the shallow trough along the cane walkway, someone may be walking twenty paces away along the underside of the very same beam, soles planted easily against the curving bark, tools slung tightly to the hip so nothing drops into the open air below them—which, from where they stand, is above.

The third reason takes longer to learn. To see them, you have to sit down on the wet cushion-moss, stop bracing yourself against a fall that has nowhere to end, and let your own tight sense of the present go slack.

Wait quietly in a weeping belt while the bruised twilight pools around your knees. Watch the shadows of the passing gliders soak slowly into the cane. After an hour, or what feels like an hour, the gnarled olive-grey burl resting in the fork of the next stanchion shifts its weight by half an inch, and you realize it has shoulders, a narrow ribcage, and two long, patient hands resting flat against the wet timber.

They are built the way the lattice is built: slender, long-limbed, light-boned, made for tension rather than compression. Their skin is matte and pale—the color of inner willow bark just after it has been stripped and left in the damp air—with faint, dark veins showing cleanly at the wrists and collarbone. Because the warm twilight never burns and never freezes, they wear little: a belted apron of cured glider-vellum oiled to a soft translucence, cross-slings of retted nettle-cord, loops of braided bast hung with wedges, fids, and tightly stoppered resin-gourds. They have no body hair to trap cold sweat in the dry-belt drafts, and the coarse, dark hair of their heads is kept tightly plaited with living sprigs of pale bog-moss tucked behind the ears. The moss drinks from the air before human skin can feel the shift, swelling and darkening along their temples to warn them of a weeping belt twenty minutes before the vapor arrives—or twenty minutes after they have already felt the dampness on their palms.

Look first at their hands and feet, for those are the true instruments of the place. Their soles are broad, supple, and deeply grooved, permanently stained a dark tannin-brown from walking bare on wet cedar and moss-chinked cane; the great toe stands slightly apart, strong enough to pinch a swaying vine-cable while both hands work overhead. Their fingers are long and faintly spatulate, tipped with thick, horn-flat nails worn smooth from pressing cushion-moss into seams and scraping soft rot out of dowel-holes. Their eyes are large and tea-colored like the mist, slow to blink, and when one finally turns to look at you, their gaze rests for a heartbeat slightly behind your shoulder, or slightly ahead of where your chin is tilting—the habit of a mind raised to read lagging shadows and branches that lean before they are pulled.

They call themselves simply the Hands, or the Binders, when they trouble to name themselves at all, though they rarely waste breath on the air. The void swallows voices; a shout dies ten paces from the mouth, smothered by wet lichen and miles of empty vapor. Instead, they speak through the wood.

Watch two of them meet at a narrow crossing where a lateral tension-cable joins a main braided rib. Neither opens their mouth to greet the other. They stand three yards apart, press the heel of a bare foot or the flat of a knuckle against the same damp pole, and hum low in the chest, clicking the tongue softly against the palate. The air between them stays completely dead and hushed, yet if you lay your own palm on the cane beside your knee, you feel the wood come alive—a clean, clear vibration running along the wet phloem like a plucked string, carrying news of a swelling arch, a dry-belt shrink, or a stranger sitting quietly on the causeway. That distant, muted *thock* of wood striking wood that drifts across two miles of warm haze is seldom just a mallet driving a peg; a double tap, a pause, and three soft blows travel miles along the continuous grain, telling every binder on the span what is coming due.

For that is their real labor—not merely mending what has rotted, but paying what is owed.

Stay with the binder who emerged from the fork of the stanchion, and watch them work. Half a bowshot down the causeway, a thick young sucker-limb growing out of the main trunk has begun to groan and arch smoothly downward into a parabolic strut, straining against nothing, its bark stretching tight and pale as though bent by a heavy winch. No wind blows. No weight hangs from it.

To a ground-born eye, it looks like a haunting. To the binder, it is simply a task that has arrived ahead of its maker.

They walk calmly down the glistening trough, kneel beside the straining wood, and draw a fire-hardened thornwood wedge and a coil of pale green nettle-cord from their sling. With unhurried, exact taps of a concave mallet, they drive the wedge into the fork and bind the curved limb fast to the lower rail. The moment the final hitch pulls tight, the eerie strain leaves the bark; the limb settles cleanly into the tension it had already been bearing for ten minutes. The debt is paid.

If no hand comes when a branch bends early, or when a shoot begins to weep sap before any blade has touched it, the binders say the grain goes *loose*. A loose joint does not merely rot; it begins to lose its grip on sequence and direction. The wood grows back through its own bindings without turning. The local pull of the beam tilts sideways until rainwater drifts off the edge of the deck in sluggish arcs. Shadows gather around the knot and refuse to fade, pooling dark and cold across the moss. Left untended for a century, an unpaid splice can unravel a mile of causeway into a tangle of overlapping weather and twisted distance. So the binders walk endlessly, listening through their soles for the faint, tight tremor of an effect waiting for its cause, arriving with horn fid and flint scraper to close the loop before the world can fray.

Even their tools obey that patient thrift. There is no stone here, yet you have seen their flint scrapers bound in forked willow—dark, glassy blades no bigger than a thumb, chipped to a keen edge. They do not come from any quarry. Deep inside the hollow joints of the oldest cane-culms, where centuries of mineral-rich mist have pooled and dried inside sealed pith, pale silica slowly hardens into fist-sized nodules of plant-flint. When a thousand-year beam is finally opened and replaced, the binders crack the dead culms like nuts and share out the dark hearts, hafting them in resin-blackened willow to be passed down for twenty lifetimes. Nobody owns a blade or a mallet; tools belong to the spans, tucked into dry hollows beneath the joints where the next pair of hands will already have needed them.

Follow a binder off the long, solitary causeways, down a swaying ladder of knotted vine where the walkways swap top for bottom halfway through the climb, and eventually the arches thicken and converge into a living junction—not one of the vast, ancient cathedrals where sense itself wears thin, but a working hollow the size of a broad barn, woven where three continental trunks were braided together centuries ago.

Inside, the greenhouse warmth is thick and sweet, smelling of steamed fern-root, cedar-oil, and fermenting sap. Dim greyish light filters through narrow wickerwork slits in the curved timber walls. There is no floor, and no ceiling. Gravity pulls gently outward toward whichever colossal curved trunk lies nearest the soles of your feet, so the life of the hollow wraps all the way around the interior like moss inside a log. Hammocks of bleached nettle-mesh are slung radially across the open center; drying racks of pale ribbon-flesh hang from the upper curve, dripping gently *upward* into the bark above them.

Directly overhead, walking upside down along the inverted arch of the highest beam, two half-grown children are learning to splice damp reed-cordage, their bare toes gripping the ribs while an older binder taps a steady correcting rhythm into the wood beside their knees. On the curving wall to your left, a woman leans out over a woven basket watertight with blackened resin, dropping heated nodules of cane-flint into a broth of crushed fern-starch and the savory, insect-rich liquor ladled from the hanging pitcher-pods.

In the very center of the hollow, suspended from six tension-vines so it touches no dry wood, hangs the hearth—a thing of dread and reverence. It is a colossal, thick-rinded gourd, cradled inside three nested baskets of constantly misted moss and lined four inches deep with wet peat-ash. Inside that damp belly, a tiny, smokeless coal of dense thornwood heart-char has been kept alive for forty years, fed one sliver of dried pith at a time. Here they harden their dowels, melt their pitch, and char the tips of their digging-pins, never letting an open flame breathe near the dry-belt timber. When a binder sets out on a long journey to a distant junction, they carry a single living ember inside a pierced horn tube packed tight with smoldering punk-fungus, tucked close to the ribs where its warmth beats against their skin like a second heart.

Their harvest comes from the air around them and the timber beneath them. They tap the slow-breathing phloem of the great trunks for sweet, milky sap, fermenting it in stoppered gourds into a pale, frothy mash whose rising-bread scent drifts down through the slatted walkways for miles. They farm crisp, ghostly canopy-tubers in slung cradles of rotting moss-compost. In damp moss-pouches tied to their belts, they carry tame bark-amphibians—the little many-fingered knot-dwellers—releasing them into freshly opened splices to lick out deep pockets of hidden rot where even a binder's slender fingers cannot reach. And when a forty-yard ribbon-grazer drifts silently within arm's reach of a junction rail, a binder leans out with a cane-flint knife and makes one swift, clean slice across the creature's frayed tail-edge, hauling in six yards of pale, pleated flesh to salt with wood-ash and dry in the sinking air of a dry belt. The ribbon-grazer never hurries or flinches; often its ragged edge has already sealed cleanly with translucent new growth a heartbeat before the knife draws away.

Yet not everything in their craft is grown from plants. The horn fids polished smooth by handling, the notched bone struts propping open the hanging cisterns—those come from two far stranger harvests.

The first arrives once in a generation, rising slowly out of the bottomless ochre murk far beneath the lowest spans. Down in the blind, bottomless depths where no timber grows and the mist thickens into warm, unanchored murk, live vast, sluggish, gas-buoyant creatures that never see even the bruised twilight of the upper world while they live. They are eyeless, soft-flanked drifters shaped like flattened bells a hundred paces wide, braced internally by honeycomb-light struts of pale bone and ringed around their intake-slits with heavy, curved plates of dense keratinous horn. Only in death, swollen with the warm fermentation-gas of the abyss, do their carcasses rise slowly up through leagues of vapor like sunken hulls surfacing from a deep sea, snagging at last against the lowest tension-cables of the lattice.

When the dry-belt watchers feel that immense, soggy tug reverberating miles up the braided strands, binders gather from half a continent away. For weeks they hang in harnesses above the murk, flensing the drifting giant in total hush, rendering its pale oil to waterproof their bast-cord, hauling up curved plates of horn and hollow ribs of bone before the carcass breaks apart and sinks back into the grey.

The second source of bone is closer to home.

In a world with no ground, you cannot bury your dead, and you cannot toss them over the edge. Everything dropped into the void comes back. Just as a loose thornwood peg loops through the unseen depths and tumbles back out of the ceiling-cloud minutes later, swollen with tepid water and three wet seasons of black liverwort, a body cast into the mist will return—perhaps an hour later, perhaps three spans over, bloated with warm vapor, draped in blind white abyss-fungi and marked with tiny, circular bites.

Because the void is not empty, even down where the arches end. In the endless looping currents of the bottomless murk live creatures that never touch solid wood in their entire lives: pale, soft, radial colonies the size of millwheels that tumble eternally through the warm fog, trailing fine sticky threads to snag falling spores and dropped splinters. Riding their flanks are swarms of blind, translucent wood-borers—the tiny things whose bite-marks freckle every peg that returns from a fall—spending their whole lives grazing on scraps of timber that never stop falling.

So the binders never give their dead to the mist. When one of the walking hands dies, their kin lay them inside a hollowed pocket carved into the dead heartwood of a thick trunk, leaving the opening unsealed for a season so the hanging crustaceans and the bark-amphibians can work in the quiet dark. Afterward, the living cambium is encouraged to swell shut over the hollow, swallowing the rest just as it swallows old wedges. A few clean bones—a collarbone, a curved rib—are kept out, rubbed with cedar-oil and notched with a flint blade to prop open a pitcher-cistern or toggle a young binder's sling, so that even the dead remain useful, holding the world open to catch the rain.

And even up on the warm, familiar walkways, the binders are not the only quiet presences sharing the moss.

Walk carefully when a ribbon-grazer glides past a span, or when a flock of pale vellum gliders wheels overhead, leaving their murky grey-green shadows sprawled across the wet cane for a full minute afterward. Watch the edges of one of those lingering blotches. Now and then, a patch of darkness does not fade cleanly into the wood; it slides an inch sideways, ripples smoothly over a dowel-head, and slips beneath the lip of a split-bamboo gutter.

The binders call them *lag-leeches*, though they draw no blood. They are flat, nearly paper-thin creatures wide as a dinner platter, velvety matte charcoal-green on top and damp pale grey beneath, without eyes or distinct heads. They live entirely inside the tardy shadows of other things, flattening themselves into the cool, lingering dimness left behind on the moss, drinking the slight chill and the microscopic spores that settle only where light has been held back. Step on one with a bare sole and it neither bites nor flees; it feels merely cool and slick, like wet lichen, and slips away between the slats. Yet for the next hour, your own shadow drags heavily across the walkway three paces behind your heels, stretched thin and reluctant, as if something flat and patient were still clinging to its hem.

More unsettling still are the *echo-weavers*—pale, spindly, six-limbed climbers the size of a child's forearm, with translucent, dew-beaded skin and long, delicate mandibles, that dwell inside the hollow pith of neglected beams. They are harmless to living flesh, shy as moths, vanishing into dowel-holes at the faintest vibration of a footstep. Yet at night—or during the dimmer troughs of the endless twilight, when the binders sleep in their hammocks—the echo-weavers slip out along the rails and imitate what they have felt through the wood. They chew fine filaments of bast and tie tiny, maddeningly intricate knots around twigs, tool-handles, and loose cane-ends: braids within braids, doubled back without turning, copied blindly from the ancient splices around them. A binder waking to find their mallet-handle bound to a stanchion with gossamer knots that pass cleanly through solid wood does not curse them; they simply pare the impossible cord away with a sigh, knowing that where the echo-weavers gather, the timber nearby is beginning to forget its own hardness.

But if you want to understand what truly anchors this continental scaffold—what keeps the whole vast, weathered filigree from slipping entirely loose of time and drifting apart into warm fog—you must walk until four or five colossal trunks converge into one of the oldest cathedral-junctions, where the scar-tissue of petrified knots rises like dark ribs into the ceiling-cloud and the silence feels thick enough to press against your eardrums.

At the warm, dim heart of such a chamber, nested high in the fused cradle where five continental beams knit into a single pulsing core of sap and cambium, sit the oldest of the Hands.

Time does not wear evenly on people who live where seasons drift sideways and a short walk can cross a loop of slack water. Some binders grow stiff-jointed in sixty years; others walk the spans for two centuries, their skin growing dry and papery, their knees slow, until their sense of the slack widens so far that they can no longer safely balance on a narrow tension-cable. They begin stepping onto rails that rotted away ten years ago, or flinching from dry-belt creaks that will not sound until next week.

When that widening comes, they do not stop tending the lattice. They climb into the heart of a cathedral-junction and take their seat in a hollow backrest woven long ago out of living shoots.

They are called the Rooted, though they never truly turn to wood. Over years of sitting nearly motionless in the warm, breathable hush, their younger kin pack living cushion-moss around their shins and hips to keep the damp from souring their skin, bring them warm sap-broth in horn spoons, and wipe the pale resin-salt from the corners of their closed eyes. Slowly, gently, the colossal junction accepts them the way it accepts a wooden wedge or a thousand-year hemp knot. Pale new cambium swells neatly over the wooden armrests cradling their forearms, wraps around their calves in smooth, warm bolsters of bark, and seals their spines fast against the fused heartwood of five trunks at once.

Their breathing slows to two or three shallow rises an hour. Their eyes rarely open; their pale hair, unbraided now, hangs down into the hanging moss-curtains, braided through with fine white fungal threads that drink straight from the mist. They live on for an unmeasured stretch of wet belts and dry belts, warm and faintly humming in the dark, half-swallowed by the cathedral they helped build.

They are the memory of the lattice, and its ear.

Pressed spine-deep into the convergence of five continental spans, a Rooted elder feels everything the walking hands are too small and too hurried to catch. Through the continuous damp grain, they hear the slow, deep creak of a dry-belt rib shrinking forty miles away; they feel the distant, sluggish tug of a dead abyss-drifter snagging a lower cable three leagues down; they taste in the rising phloem the faint, trapped rustle of a weeping belt that will not condense for another month, grafted ahead of itself into the living sap.

When a walking binder climbs into the cathedral-junction, dusty with bark-meal and uncertain which span needs them next, they do not speak. They step quietly across the knotted floor—or hang calmly upside down from the arched rib above—and lay one bare, tannin-stained palm flat against the warm bark directly beside the elder's ribcage, or against the dry, papery skin of the elder's shoulder.

For a long, hushed minute, nothing happens. You hear only the faint *tick* of a droplet sliding off a gourd-lip somewhere far across the hollow.

Then the elder's throat clicks—once, twice, a slow, woody vibration that barely stirs the air yet runs cleanly up the walker's wrist and into their collarbone. In the wake of that quiet buzz, the walker knows without words: three days out along the curving western arch, a splice is already pulling tight against an unlaid cord; two leagues beneath the sixth crossing, a hollow culm has cracked open to show dark flint; a stranger in hard boots is kneeling on the wet moss miles away, staring down into the tea-colored haze and wondering where all the hands have gone.

And sometimes, if you sit very still in the deepest cathedral-junctions and press your own ear against the warm, fused bark beside a Rooted elder, you catch something older even than their slow breathing—something the elders themselves only murmur about in the faintest, lowest clicks through the wood. Deep inside the petrified core of the oldest trunks, where dry-belt splits occasionally expose bindings millennia old, there are knots no human hand knows how to tie, spliced in loops that require three palms, or six fingers, or a straight line that no longer exists in this curving air. The elders say a faint vibration still runs through that deepest heartwood, thinner than a thread, older than the earliest remembered mist: the last, unspent shudder of a mallet-blow struck when there was still something heavy beneath the world to strike against, still traveling patiently along the grain because there is nowhere left for it to end.

Out beyond the junction, the walking binder lifts their palm from the bark, shoulders their coil of fresh green reed-cord, and steps back out onto the narrow, hand-lashed ribbon of living cane. Behind them, their shadow lingers on the wet cushion-moss for a few quiet breaths before soaking slowly into the wood. Ahead of them, two miles out across the bruised, breathable twilight, a pale arch is already beginning to bend smoothly into place, waiting patiently for the hands that are on their way to bind it.
