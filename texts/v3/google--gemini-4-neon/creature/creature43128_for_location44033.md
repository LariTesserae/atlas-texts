At first, you think the builders have only just stepped out of sight.

The mortar ridges are too sharp, the bamboo lashings too green, the wet sheen on the lime-plaster too fresh for the valley to be abandoned; it feels like a courtyard emptied five minutes ago for a noon meal. You walk half a mile along the straight limestone causeways without seeing anyone, only the chalk-white herons and the jewel-bright frogs holding their breath in the glare. Then you round the corner of a bamboo trellis—or rather, the trellis turns quietly across your path and puts itself behind your shoulder—and three of them are sitting on the low brick curb of a sunken water-pan, their bare ankles submerged in the inch-deep warmth.

You cannot tell whether they arrived while you blinked or whether they were sitting there all along, blended into the pale, damp geometry of the plaster.

They are built along human lines, though at second glance they seem stretched or pared down, as if drawn from the same unhurried proportion as the pavilions. Some are tall and narrow-ribbed as peeled roof-poles; others are no taller than the waist-high wicker troughs, compact and wide-palmed. None of them look young in the soft, unfinished way of children, and none of them look old. There are no crow’s-feet beside their eyes, no knotted knuckles, no stooped spines, no scars or sun-darkened blotches on their shoulders. They come in different scales, the way water-jars or paving tiles come in different molds, every one of them equally fresh.

Their skin is the color of pale river-slip—warm cream, wet chalk, raw beeswax—smooth as unglazed stoneware rinsed in clean water. In the incandescent white glare, they do not tan or burn, and they do not sweat salt; instead, the same fine, clear condensation that beads on the mudbrick balustrades collects along their collarbones, the backs of their wrists, and the long ridges of their shins, giving them a faint, pearly sheen. Look closely at a forearm resting against a woven reed mat and you will see that the skin takes impressions the way fresh lime-plaster does: crisp cross-hatchings from the sedge, the neat indent of a hemp cord around a wrist, the smooth track of a thumb pressed lightly against a cheek. The marks stay sharp-edged and pale while you watch them. Look away toward the distant mist and look back, and the skin is unblemished again, ready for the next touch.

They wear single lengths of unhemmed, stiff-woven flax or pale sedge-cloth, wrapped and pinned at the shoulder with smooth slivers of bone. The cloth hangs in clean, damp folds that never wrinkle into creases and never fray at the edges. Where the hems trail through the shallow runnels, the water darkens the weave up to a neat, level line and stops there, refusing to creep any higher up the threads.

Look into their faces and the strangeness deepens without ever turning cold. Their eyes are dark, wide, and entirely calm. They do not squint against the sourceless glare of the sky; the white dome simply sits reflected on the wet curve of each iris, bright as a porcelain dish. When they turn their heads, the fierceness of the light passes faintly through the thinner parts of them—their ears, the webs between their long fingers, the rims of their nostrils—turning them a soft, luminous coral, the exact shade of the wading birds’ bills. Their hands are the most unmistakable thing about them: broad-palmed, immaculate, fingernails translucent as thin mica, the pads of their thumbs etched with the exact fine parallel whorls pressed into every mortar seam in the valley.

They are never quite idle, yet they never appear to hurry.

Watch one kneeling beside a notched timber planter. She parts the shield-broad taro leaves with both palms—carefully, so the fat drop poised at each leaf-tip is not shaken loose—and reaches down among the clean, soil-less roots. With a flat bone spatula, she nudges a hand-chipped pebble a hair’s breadth deeper into the lattice of terracotta tubes. Another sits cross-legged on a raised plaster platform, a skein of raw hemp across his knees, braiding curtains of fleshy green moss onto a knotted net. His fingers move with a steady, hypnotic grace: over, under, draw tight, over, under. You can stand ten paces off and watch his hands travel through the same quiet sequence for five minutes, and the braid will not grow a finger’s length longer. Glance away for a breath—at a turquoise lizard lifting one foot above a tile—and glance back, and the finished moss-curtain is already hanging heavy and gleaming from a bamboo rack, while the weaver sits three yards further down the terrace with a fresh bundle of unknotted hemp, beginning again.

They do not fight the valley’s refusal to move forward; they live inside the width of it.

Because sound dies a few strides from the throat in this thick, cottony air, they never call across the open courts. You will never hear a shout, a laugh flung over a roof, or the clatter of dropped tools. Instead, they live in close, drifting clusters—twos, threes, sevens—constantly stepping within arm’s reach of one another. When they speak, they lean in with an intimacy that startles an outsider, bringing their mouths near a companion’s coral-lit ear, or resting forehead gently against forehead. What passes between them is barely voice at all: low, breathy hums that vibrate against the bone, small dry clicks of the tongue, soft whispered syllables that vanish completely a handbreadth past the listener’s shoulder.

Across a wider court, they converse in quieter currencies. A tall figure standing by a wicker trough will press two damp fingers gently against the bulging pillow of water trapped between the willow slats; the surface tension shivers without breaking, and twenty paces away, a companion kneeling by a connected runnel tilts her head, reading a tremor too fine for your eyes to catch. Sometimes they communicate simply by shifting a stone, or stepping aside so a detached, pale-grey shadow slides politely across a walkway to point toward a doorway.

And distance itself does not keep them apart. They know every quiet fold in the basin’s geometry the way a fish knows the eddies of a pond. A short, broad-shouldered tender will pick up a stack of unglazed bricks at the edge of the eastern plaza, turn down a dead-straight causeway that seems to lead toward the far parapet, walk nine deliberate paces, and step out calmly beside a thatch pavilion half a valley away, facing back the way he came. They use the twin flights of four brick steps—the eastern stair and the western stair that open into the same courtyard—as a kind of silent shorthand, descending one side to fetch a wooden mold and returning up the other without ever having to cross the blinding expanse of damp tile between. To watch them from a raised causeway is to see them appear and vanish across the courts like white herons moving between blinks, never breaking a sweat, never out of breath.

Their shadows follow them with the same courteous disobedience that governs the shadows of the cisterns and ferns. Walk behind one of the tall plasterers and you will notice his milky-slate shadow trailing a full stride behind his heels, pausing a moment at each corner to keep from brushing against a wet wall. When two of them sit side by side beneath a pavilion’s eaves to rest, their shadows do not overlap; they lean slightly away from one another, leaving a neat ribbon of luminous plaster between them, as though even their darkness respects the clean seam of each body.

They share the valley with the silent creatures not as masters, nor even quite as keepers, but as neighbors made from the same clear stillness.

A tall figure wading ankle-deep through a mirror-smooth basin will brush his damp flax hem right past the chalk-white legs of a heron; the bird does not flinch, only tilts its coral beak an inch to let the linen pass. Beneath the overhanging palm-thatch, you may catch one of the smaller figures standing on a low limestone block, lifting papery white moths one by one from the flat of her palm and pressing them gently against the underside of the roof-poles. Each moth folds its wings tight and stays where it is placed, overlapping its neighbor like a fresh shingle. Elsewhere, a pair of them crouch beside a narrow bamboo runnel, lifting porcelain-shelled freshwater crabs from the algal slick to inspect the undersides of their legs with grave, quiet satisfaction before setting them back down on the exact herringbone brick from which they were lifted.

Even the frozen pockets of heat-shimmer are woven into their daily habits. They know where every stationary distortion hangs above the sun-baked causeways, rigid as blown glass. A joiner will carry a newly lashed cane pole over to a fixed wave in the air and sight along its length through the ripple, tilting it until the bent reflection straightens out, checking some trueness of line that only makes sense inside this valley. Now and again, you see a moment of quiet play: two of them standing on opposite sides of a broad, frozen shimmer, leaning their faces close to the invisible pane so their features stretch into wavering ribbons, watching each other with slow, closed-mouth smiles before stepping back into clear air.

When they gather beneath the wide pavilions to eat, there is no fire, no smoke, no grease, and no scraps left afterward. Nothing here rots, and nothing they take into themselves leaves dark waste behind. They sit in loose circles on the stitched reed mats and pass around shallow bowls of unglazed clay, smooth-rimmed and cool. Inside lie pale, glistening cubes pressed in wooden molds—steamed reed-pith, clean water-chestnut curd, translucent seeds peeled until they shine like wet quartz—tasting faintly of green cane, lime-water, and warm rain. They lift each piece with bone pins, chewing slowly, cleanly, leaving not a single crumb on their lips or on the damp weave beneath them.

To drink, they rarely use cups. More often, one will pause beside a waist-high wicker trough along a terrace edge, lean down, and press her lips directly to one of the smooth, trembling pillows of water bulging through the open willow lattice. She draws in a long, slow mouthful. The taut glassy skin dimples inward under her mouth, never tearing, never spilling a single drop onto the tiles below; when she pulls back, wiping her wet chin with the back of a pale wrist, the trough is already brimming full again, the meniscus smooth and round as if nothing had been taken.

Wait long enough, and you begin to discover that the valley you can see is only one floor of their household.

If you stay quietly near the edge of a dry stair, you may see two of them kneel over a seam in the woven reed matting. Neither breaks a brick or chips a line of mortar. Instead, one draws a slender bone needle from his coiled hair and unpicks three neat loops of raw hemp. Together they fold back a square corner of the mat. Blinding, sourceless white light wells immediately up out of the floor, washing their calm faces from below and erasing the milky shadows beneath their chins.

They lean over the bright gap without shielding their eyes. One lowers a tightly lashed bundle of freshly washed straw—or a flat wooden tray carrying six newly molded bricks, still damp from the wooden form—down into the glare. From the inverted plaster ceiling hanging askew beneath the gap, another pair of pale, wet-gleaming hands reaches upward, fingers splayed, thumb-whorls crisp. There is no word spoken, only a brief pause as fingertips touch fingertips across the bright air between the terraces. The inverted hands take the bundle; sometimes they press something back up in return—a coil of green cane, a clay ring glazed with clean condensation, a scarlet wax-flower whose petals have never known a bruise. Then the hands withdraw into the askew rooms below, the reed mat is laid flat, and the bone needle stitches the three loops of hemp shut again, tight and unfrayed.

They are everywhere down there, too, tending terraces turned sideways and upside down in the endless white light, passing craft from floor to floor like buckets along a chain, holding up a world that has no bedrock by the simple, tireless fact of continuing to build it.

What they cannot bear—though even then, there is no anger in them, only a sudden, breathtaking concentration—is anything that tries to begin *ending*.

If a visitor from outside stumbles along a terrace and scuffs the sharp bevel of a sun-baked brick into pale powder, or tracks a smear of real, dark, soil-born dirt across the herringbone tile, or brushes too hard against a wicker trough and breaks the surface tension so that a real, heavy stream of water begins to spill and pool and threaten to *drain*, the valley responds at once. You do not hear running footsteps. You simply look up and find four or five of them already kneeling around the flaw, having stepped out of the crooked stairs and folded causeways before the first splash could settle.

They do not scold you or push you back. One gently takes your elbow with cool, damp fingers and guides you two paces aside, out of the way of the polite shadows. Another kneels by the weeping wicker slats, pressing both broad palms gently against the willow strips while a companion draws a wet bone trowel along the rim, murmuring those low, breathy clicks an inch from the trembling water. A third lifts the dark speck of outside dirt onto a clean reed leaf with exquisite delicacy, carries it to a lifted seam in the matting—or to the far parapet—and drops it out into the white vapor where it vanishes before it can stain the lime-wash. A fourth wets his thumb against his tongue and draws it slowly along the chipped brick edge.

Watch his thumb and nothing seems to heal; the little pale scar remains. Blink once in the hot, stifling glare, and the bevel is sharp again as pulled wax, the wicker trough is bulging round and dry-rimmed, and the tenders are already rising, smoothing their unwrinkled flax, offering you a calm, unblinking look that holds neither blame nor memory—only the quiet relief of a seam closed cleanly.

They do rest, though it is hard to call what they do *sleep*.

In the broadest, quietest pavilions, where the thick palm-thatch throws wide pools of milky-slate shade across the reed mats, you will sometimes find three or four of them lying flat on their backs, spaced with the same patient geometry as the gardens. Their arms rest neatly at their sides, palms turned upward, eyes shut. Their chests rise and fall so slowly that you have to watch for a full minute to be certain they are breathing. Condensation gathers in tiny, bright beads along their closed eyelids and the hollows of their throats.

While they lie there, the stillness of the basin closes right over them. Bronze and turquoise lizards climb unhurriedly onto their flat stomachs and pale shins, pressing their bellies down to drink the damp warmth. Papery white moths drift down from the eaves and fold themselves neatly along the sleepers’ wrists and collarbones. Watching them in the pale shade, with their smooth, unscarred limbs and faint internal glow, you feel a sudden, quiet vertigo: you cannot tell whether they are living artisans resting between tasks, or freshly cast figures of damp clay laid out beneath the thatch to dry, waiting for the moment of firing that will never come.

Then a bead of water drops—or disappears—from a fern frond outside. You glance toward the soundless splash, look back beneath the eaves, and the reed mats are empty. Not a dent remains in the woven sedge. Three paces out in the blinding open court, a tall figure is tightening the hemp lashing on a trellis of scarlet wax-flowers, damp flax clinging cleanly to his shoulder blades, a single white moth still perched calmly on the bone pin in his hair.

Only at the far rim of the valley do you ever see them stop working entirely.

Now and again, a pair of them—or sometimes a small, quiet line of five or six—will climb the three shallow steps at the edge of the basin and come to rest against the waist-high parapet of hand-fitted limestone. They lean their pale forearms on the warm, chisel-pocked stone and look out past the terrace edge, into the seamless wall of luminous white vapor rising gently from nowhere to meet the linen-textured dome of the sky.

They stand there for a long time without speaking, without even tapping the stone. Their wide, dark eyes do not search the mist for a horizon, nor do they look weary of the endless noon behind them. Their posture is unmistakable once you recognize it: it is the quiet, attentive stance of hosts who have swept every tile, washed every root clean of dirt, filled every trough to the very tremble of its brim, and walked out to the low wall at the edge of the yard to see if the first arrival is coming up the path.

There is no path out there—only warm, bright mist, and the soft pressure of humidity against the ears. After a while, one of them turns back toward the shining courts, touches a companion lightly on the damp wrist, and together they step calmly down the limestone stairs to check a mortar seam, straighten a clean pebble, and keep the house ready on the bright side of beginning.
