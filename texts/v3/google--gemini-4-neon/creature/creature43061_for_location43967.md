If you stay past half a day—stay until your knees stiffen on the wet stone, until your own breathing thins to match the damp stillness and the pale gray sheen on the eastern pillars begins to fade back toward ash—the chamber starts to loosen its hold on what it hides.

The first sign is never a shape. It is the water.

You might be watching the brim of a knee-deep rimstone pool a pace away, where the overflow slides across the scalloped lip in a clear, silent skin. Without any breeze, without any splash behind the columns, that skin thickens by the width of a hair. For three heartbeats the sheet runs slightly fuller, catching a faint line of gray light along the tallow-smooth rim, and then thins back to what it was. Somewhere deeper in the crowded thicket of pillars, something with weight has lowered itself into a basin—slowly enough that no wave broke, no ring survived the journey across five intervening pools, only a quiet, polite displacement passed down from lip to lip.

Wait longer, and you hear a *tick* against the rock—dry, crisp, no louder than a heavy ceiling drop hitting a glazed ledge. Ten seconds later, from somewhere behind a pleated stone curtain to your left, an identical *tick* answers it. Not a drip. No bead of condensation falls with that much dry bone in its voice, nor waits so cleanly for a reply.

And then, if you stop looking for movement and look instead at the stone itself, you notice that one of the low, bulbous ribs swelling along the flank of a nearby pillar is not quite welded to it.

Along its curved edge, tucked into the velvety shadow where it meets the column, runs a hairline crease that is paler than bone-white calcite—wet, faintly translucent, with the dull, warm blush of living tissue beneath. The swollen back facing you is banded in muted flowstone: pewter gray, wet ochre, thin streaks of iron-brown, beaded with condensation like every inch of travertine around it. Yet its surface is divided into quiet, overlapping plates, cracked along natural hinges where a shoulder might shift or a ribcage might expand. Once, very slowly, over the span of a long breath, the whole pale swelling rises by half a finger's width and settles again.

When it finally decides to move, there is a small, wet *snap*—softer than a dry twig, barely louder than a fingernail catching on linen—as the fresh mineral film that has grown along its flank overnight parts from the pillar.

It peels itself down from the column without hurry.

What unfolds is neither beast nor person, though for a blurred instant in the dimness it borrows quiet echoes from both. It is long—easily the length of a lean human from blunt snout to the tip of its ribbon-flat tail—yet so narrow across the ribs and so supple along the spine that it fits sideways through gaps where a man's shoulders would wedge fast. Its four limbs are long and curiously jointed, ending in broad, pale, five-fingered hands and feet with flattened, soft-padded tips and thin, silky webbing between the digits. When it climbs a pillar, the webbing folds cleanly out of sight; when it steps into a pool, the fingers splay wide against the silt.

Its body belongs to two different worlds at once.

Everything turned outward to the chamber—its broad back, the crown of its skull, the outer ridges of its forearms and thighs, the pleated crest along its tail—is rough-textured, matte, and built to take the stone. Over seasons and decades, the warm mineral seepage and heavy humid air coat those surfaces in thin, layered laminae of travertine. It does not grow solid rock; beneath the pale, banded crust lies a tough, slowly flexing hide that fractures the calcite into shingled, interlocking scales, mirroring the rimstone pools on the floor. A very old one carries a quarter-inch of flowstone across its shoulders, mottled in the exact ochre and pewter of the alcoves it favors, with tiny beads of sinter roughening its brow.

Everything turned inward—its belly, its throat, the insides of its long limbs, its palms—is entirely bare. That skin is slick, naked, the color of bleached tallow, so thin over the ribs and throat that in the faint midday gleam from the eastern cleft you could trace the slow, sluggish pulse of dark blood moving underneath. It keeps this underside pressed always to water or wet stone, shielded from the drying air that never comes and from any rough touch.

Its head is broad, flat, and solemn, narrowing slightly toward a soft, wide mouth without visible teeth. Where eyes should be sit two tiny, lidless beads of milky silver-gray, sunk beneath calcified brows. They cannot make out a face or the edge of a ledge; they register only the faint brightening on the slickest eastern columns at midday, and the dull return of dusk—or, once in a very long memory, the warm orange quiver of a tallow flame near the skylight's reach.

Once it slips down into the ankle-deep pool beside the pillar, its posture changes completely. The stiffness of the vertical climb leaves it. It sinks until only the banded ridge of its back breaks the surface like a half-submerged shelf of sinter, and from two smooth slits tucked just behind its jaw, pale, feathery gill-fronds unfurl slowly into the warm water—translucent as the wheat-grain shrimp, swaying faintly in no current at all.

Because you can never see across the chamber, you never see how many there are.

Move twenty paces deeper into the thicket—turning sideways between swollen columns, stepping carefully over scalloped lips—and you begin to feel them rather than count them. One lies draped horizontally across three stepped pools, its flexible spine following the curves of the rimstone basins like a fallen stalactite, its long ribbon-tail trailing into a fourth. Another stands upright in the dark niche behind a flowstone curtain, arms wrapped patiently around the waist of a column, chin resting on its own collarbone, motionless as the stone it embraces. High on a waist-high shelf beyond the ash-gray gloom, two smaller ones rest shoulder to shoulder, their bare bellies pressed together for warmth that neither truly needs, simply because touch is familiar.

There may be ten of them down here. There may be twenty. None of them ever gather in one place, because the chamber has no center to gather in.

They live at the tempo of the water.

Down here, where the air never cools by a single degree from one year to the next, they spend almost no life keeping themselves warm. A resting adult's heart beats three or four times a minute. It may remain folded against a pillar for two days without shifting a finger, rising only once every quarter-hour when submerged to touch the tip of its snout noiselessly to the surface film and draw a single slow sip of heavy, humid air.

Because they burn so little, the chamber's sparse larder is enough to sustain them for a century or more. Every few days, one glides unhurriedly toward the eastern side of the room, where the pale, flat light from the crooked cleft nourishes that single dull smudge of olive-brown film on the wet pillar flank. Pressing its wide, soft mouth flat against the stone, it grazes the slick stain with fine, comb-like ridges inside its lips, never stripping the patch bare—taking only a few slow sweeps before drifting back into the dimness. Elsewhere in the deeper alcoves, they nose gently through the ochre and iron-brown silt at the bottoms of the pools, straining the invisible velvet of mineral bacteria, sweeping up the tiny blind paper-shelled snails from the undersides of the rims and whatever translucent shrimp drift too slowly past their parted jaws. After heavy rains on the surface—seasons they know only by a slight cooling in the ceiling drips and a faint, sweet sharpness in the water—the eastern cleft occasionally washes down drowned beetles, fragments of moss, or the dark rot of oak leaves. Then the pools directly below the shaft hold three or four pale shapes moving slowly through the murk, their broad fingers sifting the submerged floor with delicate care, leaving only the pallid harvestman untouched high in its dry pocket between the fused stalactites.

They do not need sight to navigate the crowded forest of columns. Their world is drawn in water-tension and vibration.

Along the sides of their throats and the fine, pale edges of their webbed fingers run rows of tiny sensory pits that read the surface of every pool like an open page. Each ceiling drip in the chamber has its own steady weight and rhythm; its rings cross the brim-full basins in familiar, intersecting webs. A creature resting with its chin on a rimstone lip knows if a neighbor shifts two alcoves away by the faint alteration in the overflow sheet. It knows if an unfamiliar foot steps into the water thirty feet across the room—even though the columns swallow the splash—by the sudden, heavy pulse that swells over the scalloped rims.

And through the continuous limestone of pillars and floor, they speak.

Sound dies almost where it starts in this wet air, so they do not waste breath on voices. Instead, they press their jaws or cheekbones lightly against a travertine column and listen through bone, answering by tapping the hardened, calcified outer knuckle of a wrist—or the mineral-crusted crown of the brow—against the rock. A single *tick*, spaced ten or twenty seconds apart. Two ticks in closer succession, followed by a long silence. To any human passing through the room, it is only condensation falling onto glazed shelves. To the ones pressed against the columns in the warm velvety dark, it is a slow, unhurried exchange carried through the bedrock: *I am awake. The eastern drip runs sweet. My shoulder is stiffening. Come.*

For the stone that shelters them is also their quiet danger.

Nothing in this chamber stays still for long without the mineral water claiming it. A clay bowl left untouched grows a white collar around its base; a wooden scoop becomes a brittle gray husk; a fallen boulder merges with the floor. The creatures that live here are subject to the same patient geology. If one sleeps too deeply for too many days, fresh calcite creeps down from its crusted back across the naked folds of its hips and shoulders, bridging the hinges, beginning to weld its ribs to the wet shelf where it rests.

That is what keeps them bound to one another.

When an older one's joints grow thick with pale sinter, or when the mineral collar along its flank begins to bite into the soft skin beneath, it taps a low, patient rhythm into the column beside it. Slowly, sometimes taking half an hour to cross twenty feet of crowded stone, another slips sideways between the pillars to find it. There is no hurry in their tending. The newcomer settles beside it in the nearest deep pool—most often one of the basins that long-ago human hands deepened by wedging flat dark limestone shards atop the natural rims, making the water just deep enough for a full-grown body to submerge completely.

There, in the bath-warm water, the younger one cups its broad, webbed hands to sluice the stiffened joints, softening the mineral seam, then uses the hardened, stone-edged ridge of its own thumb to gently worry and pry at the encroaching crust. Tiny, crisp snaps sound under the water as the brittle laminae give way. Wafer-thin curved flakes of banded flowstone peel free and drift to the pool floor—pale, scalloped shards that any visitor would mistake for chips fallen from the ceiling drapes. Once every few years, a healthy adult sheds almost its entire dorsal coat this way in large, hollow plates, emerging pale, vulnerable, and pearlescent until the mineral water slowly paints it back into the colors of the cave.

Sometimes, though, an elder grows too heavy, its heart too slow, its skin too weary to crack free of the crust. It sinks into a deep alcove far from the eastern light, rests its jaw on a low rimstone lip, and stops answering the quiet taps in the stone. The others come once or twice to brush their pale palms across its banded shoulders, feel the stillness beneath the plate, and leave it alone. Within a season, its soft underside returns quietly to the shrimp and the warm silt. Within a decade, fresh sinter welds the domed, banded shell of its back cleanly into the floor. Many of the smooth, rounded bosses rising from the deeper basins were never fallen boulders at all.

Because they live so long and move so gently, the scattered traces left by people have folded seamlessly into their habits—neither feared nor worshipped, only accepted as useful changes in the landscape.

They remember the visitors—not in stories, not in names, only as a rare, strange weather that used to descend from the eastern cleft. It smelled sharp and startling: woodsmoke, sweat, wet wool, and the rich animal fat of the tallow burning in the three shallow stone dishes near the skylight's edge. Those heavy, two-legged shapes moved with an astonishing, clumsy haste, splashing across the scalloped rims so that every pool in the chamber trembled at once, scraping chisels along the slick travertine to peck footholds, wedging dark slabs to deepen basins, setting coarse clay vessels beneath the steadiest drips.

Back then, the chamber's true tenants pressed flat against the shadowed sides of the deeper columns, watching the small yellow flames with their dull silver eyes until the visitors grew quiet. And some of the visitors did grow quiet. Some sat motionless for hours in the bath-warm gloom, letting the heat soak into their bones, dipping their hands into the pools without stirring them. When one stayed still long enough—long enough for the water to settle glass-flat again—an older resident would sometimes drift silently out from behind a pleated stone curtain, resting its broad, bare knuckles on the same scalloped rim a hand's breadth from the human's fingers, or tapping once, gently, against the broad belly of the central pillar.

Now the tallow dishes are cold and frosted with damp lime, and the people have not come down for a long while. Yet the things they left remain alive in the creatures' quiet routines.

The calcified wooden scoop lying in the ankle-deep pool is a familiar landmark brushed by passing palms in the dark. Against the central pillar where someone once scored seventeen faint vertical cuts, the residents sometimes press their bare throats or cheeks as they pass, feeling the neat, half-filled grooves—perhaps because the texture is unlike any natural water-channel, perhaps because the tall, warm shapes used to linger there longest.

And the coarse unglazed clay bowls tucked into narrow niches beneath the ceiling drips are tended more faithfully than anything else in the room.

Only two or three—the ones wedged into crevices too tight to reach, or set under bitter drips—have sat undisturbed long enough to grow white travertine collars that fuse them to the shelves. The rest stay loose because the creatures visit them. Water dripping from the roof is different from the warm mineral seepage rising through the floor; it is rainwater filtered slowly through living soil and root-beds far above, a fraction cooler, carrying the faint, sweet taste of the outside world. Every few days, a long, pale neck stretches up into a niche, a broad snout rests on the rough clay lip, and a creature drinks quietly from the brimming cup, shifting it by a fraction of an inch against the shelf so the sinter never quite seals it fast.

Once in a long span of years—perhaps once in a decade, when heavy autumn rains above fill the ceiling drips with sweet, oxygen-rich water—the widest clay bowls serve a rarer purpose.

In the deepest quiet, two of the chamber's dwellers wind together in a warm, deepened basin, their pale undersides pressed flush, their banded crusts grating softly like wet stones settling. Days later, one climbs slowly up to a waist-high niche where a wide-mouthed unglazed bowl sits directly beneath a steady stone tongue. Curling its long limbs around the narrow column for balance, it lowers its bare belly over the rim and leaves three or four soft, pearl-sized eggs resting in the clear water at the bottom.

They are completely translucent, faint gray-gold dots visible at their centers, hidden beneath the dark mirror of the brimming bowl. No blind snail climbs the dryish clay to disturb them; no overflow from the floor pools sweeps them away. Every long half-minute, a bead of condensation swells on the ceiling tongue above and lets go, striking the center of the bowl with a muted *plip* that sends a single smooth ring rolling outward to the clay rim, stirring the water just enough to keep the eggs breathing.

When they hatch, the young are no longer than a knuckle, wholly naked of stone, as clear and fragile as the wheat-grain shrimp. For their first months they never leave the clay basin, gliding slowly in small circles around its rough interior, grazing the invisible micro-film on the submerged clay while the drop above keeps steady time over their heads. Only when they have grown the length of a finger—still pale as tallow, their backs not yet touched by a single grain of ochre or pewter—do they hook their tiny, flat-tipped hands over the wet clay lip, pause in the heavy, unchanging warmth, and slip silently down the slick flank of the pillar into the overlapping pools below.

Then the bowl sits clear and flat again, brimming under its drop. Around it, behind the swollen columns and pleated curtains of wet limestone, the chamber settles back into its dense, warm dusk. A pale hand folds inward against a pillar. A gill-frond tucks away beneath a smooth jaw. Somewhere deep in the velvety shadow, a crusted knuckle taps once against the bedrock—just a faint *tick*, lost among the falling water—and after a long, unhurried silence, another pillar answers.
