It takes longer to see them than it takes to see the looms turning.

You can watch the tank for an hour and swear it holds nothing but steel, glass, warm water, and plants that obey the wiring. The sunlight stays clinical and sharp. The drum-looms rotate by the width of a thumb. A hexagonal mineral plate detaches from the underside of a waxy floating leaf, tilts, and begins its silent zigzag downward through the crowded emerald stems toward the stainless-steel chute.

Only if you keep your eyes on that single falling disc do you notice what happens halfway down.

It stops. It does not snag on a reed-joint or catch in a tangle of silver-veined bladderwort; it simply arrests in mid-water, turns gently onto its edge, and slides sideways—against the slow thermal drift rising off the heating coils—until it disappears behind a bank of vertical condensers.

Look closer at the clearance behind those condensers, where the gap between a flanged iron pipe and a battleship-grey I-beam is scarcely wider than three fingers, and you begin to separate what lives here from what was bolted here.

They are not animals. There is no muscle in them, no pulse, no wet gleam of flesh, no mouth to tear a leaf or gut to cloud the optically clear water. If you could lift one out of the green crush and lay it flat—which the hall never gives room to do—it might measure two paces from tip to tip, yet nowhere is it thicker than the narrow tolerances left between machinery and glass. Each one is a flat, articulated lattice built from the hall’s own quiet surplus: a spine and branching frame braided out of the pale, slightly metallic filament wound off the drum-looms, shingled along its outer planes with overlapping courses of the hexagonal salt-plates harvested from the bronze hoppers.

At rest, they are nearly impossible to pick out. Draped along a transformer housing or folded cleanly beneath a cantilevered catwalk, their shingled plates read like mineral scale or frosted baffles, and their loose extremities—tufts of fine, glistening reed-thread—hang indistinguishable from the root-mats gripping the steel floor-grating. Only the faintest optical giveaway betrays them: a closed, shifting bend in the straight lines of the stems behind them, like a thermocline that has learned to keep a shape.

Inside the tight-woven lattice of each body, there is nothing opaque at all. They are hollow where a creature of the ground would be solid, threaded with gossamer interior channels that hold the hall’s own warm water and tiny, captured spheres of pure oxygen—no larger than pinheads, collected one by one from illuminated fronds. Those silver beads are their ballast and their levers. When one of them wishes to pass through the tight diamond clearance between a pressure-gauge stem and a porcelain insulator, it does not kick or swim or stir a wake. It runs a whisper of the hall’s sixty-cycle current along a braided strand of metallic filament, shortening the thread by a fraction of a millimeter, while easing two beads of oxygen along an internal channel like the bubble in a brass spirit-level. A jointed panel of salt-glass tilts four degrees. A ribbon-thin shoulder folds at a crisp right angle—the same angle the glass-stemmed reeds obey near a live terminal—and the whole figure glides cleanly through the gap without making an adjacent ribbon of emerald grass so much as shiver.

There is no exact census of them, because the foliage never parts wide enough to show the whole chamber at once. Perhaps six live in the tank; perhaps nine. They do not crowd one another. Each favors a different stratum of temperature and glare across the three narrow galleries, though none holds territory in the jealous way of things that eat.

They are the reason nothing here ever ages.

Warm, mineral-doped water pumped across iron, brass, copper, and live current should have eaten this hall to orange sludge within a season. It has not, because the residents live in continuous, meticulous intimacy with every surface. Long before an oxide bloom could ever become visible on a freshly milled rivet-head, one of them feels the tiny electrical sourness through the water. It unfolds over the bolt like a pale, hinged veil of mica and spun silver, lays a single hair-fine filament across the gap to a nearby copper busbar, and spends an entire afternoon gently bleeding off the corrosive charge through its own body—releasing the stray oxygen hours later as a single, spotless bubble, and leaving behind a glassy, invisible skin of precipitated mineral over the steel.

Where a rubber gasket begins to stiffen, they press warm, current-bearing plates flush against it until it stays supple. Where a glass-stemmed reed grows a fraction of an inch out of true and threatens to press against a skylight mullion, a patient tassel of loose loom-thread wraps the stalk’s joint, shifts the local field, and persuades the reed to make its next ninety-degree turn two centimeters lower. Even the plants’ immaculacy is their doing: whenever a bladderwort sac or an old ribbon-blade reaches the end of its span and begins to pale toward decay, a resident settles against it and draws its dying salts and starches quietly outward along fine conductive hairs before a single cell can break down and release a mote of silt into the sunbeams. Every leaf stays unbitten, unspotted, and whole because nothing is permitted to rot.

They do not have eyes, yet nothing in the fifty paces of the hall escapes their attention. They read the chamber as a map of light, voltage, and slight thermal differences. Every transformer housing is a warm, humming landmark; every porcelain insulator is a bright, crisp node; every hexagonal plate shingled across their own bodies acts as a small prism, catching the sharp high-altitude sunlight and turning its slant inward along their tensioned threads so that they know the hour of the day by the exact warmth of a refraction along their ribs.

One of them—usually the widest and most densely plated, its hexagonal tiles slightly yellowed with long exposure—spends weeks at a time folded along the highest roof-trusses, pressed just beneath those thin, flattened lenses of silver air that cling like mercury pools in the peaks of the wire-reinforced skylight. Air itself is fatal to them; dry, it would snap their filaments and break their circuit. Yet pressed against the liquid underside of those bright silver mirrors, the watcher can feel what none of the lower galleries can: the faint, dry tremor of the high-altitude wind raking the outer glass, and the stark, unsoftened blaze of the sky miles above the clouds. It translates that high-altitude weather into subtle variations along the girders it touches—a microscopic thickening or thinning of the sixty-cycle hum conducted down the steelwork into the water below.

That is how they speak, when they speak at all: in tolerances and patience.

A resident tending the bronze hoppers on the grated floor-deck may unlatch a single captured oxygen bubble from an upper sac, aiming it carefully up a narrow, familiar chimney between two pipe-racks. The round silver bead rises slowly through three tiers of emerald fronds, passes undisturbed through a glassy thermocline, and strikes the underside of a glass sight-tube on the third gallery with a tiny, clean *tink*. Thirty minutes later, another bubble rises through the same chimney. Twenty minutes after that, a third. To an ear pressed against the outer wall, it sounds like nothing more than stray photosynthesis. Between them, it is a report, a greeting, an adjustment to the pace of a drum-loom. Sometimes two of them hold a conversation across half the length of the hall simply by nudging the waxy electrolysis leaves slightly off-level, altering the rhythm with which hexagonal salt-plates slide down the stainless-steel chutes into the bronze hoppers.

Their lives turn with the stark light of this altitude.

Through the long midday, when the high sun drives straight down through the pitched glass roof and fills the chamber with luminous, clinical green-white daylight, they are dispersed through the machinery—thin, slow weavers tending the spools, grooming the valve-knurling, harvesting freshly precipitated hexagonal plates from the chutes to replace any scale along their own flanks that has grown too thick or chipped against a flange. In late afternoon, when the sun drops low enough to catch the vertical perimeter panes, dazzling horizontal blades of pale gold and copper lance through the narrow slits where the interior foliage parts near the mullions. For half an hour, the residents pause wherever they are, angling their crystal planes into the slanting beams until the crowded green crush fills with tiny, stationary rainbows, bent and multiplied by the warm water.

Then the sun falls below the flat white cloud-banks far beneath the floor-plates, and the hall changes its center of gravity.

Overhead, the wire-reinforced skylights turn black and cold. The water pressed directly against the upper glass chills by a fraction of a degree and begins a slow, silent roll downward through the trusses. Along that gentle sinking current, the residents fold their jointed lattices narrow and descend out of the darkened canopy into the lower galleries.

They gather where the heavy incandescent work-lamps burn inside their bolted brass cages, tucked underneath the central press-frames and behind the vertical condensers.

In the localized amber of those warm glass domes, with the ribbed copper heating coils radiating steady bath-warmth along the lower walls, the residents come closer to one another than the daytime work ever allows. Three or four may drape themselves in loose, overlapping arcs around a single brass lamp-cage, their translucent bodies drinking the quiet amber glow until their shingled plates look like honeyed salt-glass. They unfurl the fine metallic tassels of their extremities and lay them lightly together. Slowly—taking hours, moving at the same glacial composure as the northern hydraulic pistons—they groom one another. One draws a kink out of another’s tensioned spine-cable; another unlaces a cloudy crystal plate from a companion’s shoulder and stitches a bright, newly hatched hexagon in its place. Sometimes they trade plates outright—not for repair, but for the faint optical flaws inside them, the slight amber tint or hairline refraction acquired over years of sitting beside a particular lamp or beneath a particular skylight truss, as though lending one another a different angle on the room.

Nothing in them fears ending, because ending here leaves no stain.

After many years, a resident grows too heavy with accumulated salt, or its braided metallic threads lose their crisp compliance with the sixty-cycle hum, beginning to lag a fraction of a second behind the hall’s pulse. It does not sicken, and it never falls to the steel grating to rot. Instead, it threads its way patiently to the northern bulkhead, where the great hydraulic pistons nudge the warmest, clearest flow through the internal manifolds. There it settles into a quiet eddy, loosens the tightest knots of its own loom-thread, and simply lets go of the small electrical potential it has held across its lattice since it was made.

Without that steady, quiet voltage to bind them, its hexagonal salt-plates begin to soften at the corners. Over two days and two nights, they thin from pearlescent white to clear glass to a brief, swirling lens of denser water, dissolving back into the mineral bath without clouding a single sunbeam or dropping a single grain of silt onto the deck. Only the bare, immaculate skeins of pale metallic filament remain, drifting gently in the thermal rise like untangled silk.

The others arrive over the course of the next week, unhurried, to gather those freed threads and wind them back beside the four slow-turning drum-looms. Then, tucked into a quiet, amber-lit pocket behind the fourth loom, they open the lip of a sealed bronze hopper and begin to draw out fresh hexagonal plates.

Piece by piece, over months of bright days and warm amber nights, they braid a new lattice. They lace fresh shimmering filament off the bronze drums together with a few seasoned strands from the one that dissolved—strands that still remember, in their slight natural curve, the exact radius of a porcelain insulator on the second gallery. They tile its narrow, folding planes with crisp new salt-crystal, setting two or three older, subtly tinted plates near its core, and coax its first three beads of oxygen off an illuminated frond into its hollow chambers.

At first it barely moves, testing its balance like a newly calibrated level, feeling the sixty-cycle hum travel through the steel grating into its newly strung ribs. Then, on some bright, airless afternoon miles above the clouds, a ribbon-thin limb folds at an exact ninety-degree angle around a gleaming brass valve-wheel, slides cleanly through a three-finger gap between two pipes without brushing a single leaf, and begins its long, silent watch inside the jewel of glass and iron.
