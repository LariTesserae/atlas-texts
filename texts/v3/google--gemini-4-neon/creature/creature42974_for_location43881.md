Stand still on that high lip a little longer, and let your eyes stop searching for walls.

Walls belong to places where the air sits still. Here, anything raised upright that is not built to spin or bleed charge is torn down to splinters in a season, and any track cut into the pale crust foams over with resin before sunset or is planed blank by the quartz-dust wind before the year turns. That is why you looked across ten kilometers of bright, empty plateau and saw no mark of any hand.

Look again—not at the horizon, but at the oxidized shadow tucked under the lip of the canyon below you, where a hedge of copper-veined scrub bristles against the shear.

One of the gnarled, tarnished shapes hunched beside the hedge shifts its weight. A pale spark snaps from its heel into the fluted pavement with a dry *tick*, and then a second shape rises beside it, and a third, moving low and unhurried along the wind-line like pieces of the crust come loose.

From fifty paces away they could pass for lean, crooked snags of dead bark wrapped in cured hide. Closer, the illusion breaks all at once.

They are built the way everything on this highland is built: to stay down, to stay sealed, and to bear pressure without cracking. They are shorter than you expect at first, until you realize they are only carrying their shoulders rolled forward and their knees flexed against the cross-gusts; straightened out in a sheltered hollow, they are tall, narrow-hipped, and remarkably dense. Their ribcages are deep, barrel-curved cages that swell in slow, deliberate draws—pulling thin highland air deep into double-chambered lungs that hold the breath a long time before releasing it through narrow, baffled nostrils lined with dry, intricate membranes. Not a whisper of fog leaves their faces in the freezing wind.

Their skin is the color of weathered birch and oiled parchment, dusted white in the creases with pulverized chalk and smeared dark at the elbows, knees, and neck with the slick graphite-wax scraped from spire bearings. It has no pores that weep, no softness for the quartz-grit to tear. Their faces are narrow and hard-planed, their eyes pale amber or slate-gray beneath heavy, flared shelves of brow-bone that cut the razor-white glare; whenever a gust lifts a sheet of yellow chaff, a dry, glassy second lid flicks horizontally across the eye with a faint clicking sound.

Nothing loose flutters about them. Loose cloth would shred to threads in an hour; loose hair would stand upright in the electrostatic field and draw a bolt straight through the skull. Instead, their scalps grow stiff, dark, bristled ridges of keratin that they lacquer with warm resin and plait tightly with stripped filaments of copper-vein. The braids hang down their backs and past their hips, braided together at the tips into a single heavy cord that drags behind them across the bone-white floor. Every few steps, a needle-thin blue arc spits from the trailing copper tail into the crust, bleeding off the voltage their bodies gather from the rushing air.

To walk the open flats, they strap dense slabs of petrified pith weighted with deep-mined iron beneath their broad, calloused soles, giving them the low, stubborn ballast of the canyon grazers. Their hands are bare—squared fingers tipped with thick, translucent nails as hard as silica-horn, dusted yellow with pollen and scarred white where old resin-burns healed without bleeding.

You do not hear them speak to one another as they cross the ridge. Open air here eats a human voice ten feet from the teeth. Instead, the leader halts beside a bronze-streaked swell of crust, drops to one knee, and presses the flat of her bare palm—and then the bare hollow just behind her jaw—tight against the pavement.

There, behind the jawbone and mirrored in two tight, pale ovals just beneath the collarbones, their skin thins to parchment-dry drums stretched over hollow bone. They do not trust the screaming air for news; they listen through their skeletons. When the second figure crouches beside her, the two of them touch their foreheads together, brow-ridge to brow-ridge, and you hear only the faintest dry *clack-hum-clack*—a rapid, intricate burst of tongue-snaps and low chest-vibrations passed straight from skull to skull, completely untouched by the jet stream roaring three inches over their backs.

Then they rise and turn toward the nearest rotor-spire, whose crown is spinning into a shimmering bronze blur two kilometers downwind.

*

They are not the masters of the plateau. Nothing can master an engine the size of a hemisphere. Nor are they merely scavengers picking at its edges. If you watch them work a spire, you begin to see what they actually are: the small, quick, stubborn intelligence that lives inside the tolerances of the machine—tuning what drifts, harvesting what vents, and turning the blind physics of wind and charge to quiet, deliberate ends.

Approaching a spinning spire is like walking under the blade of a waterfall made of thin air and lightning. The mechanical whine vibrates in your teeth; the ground beneath your boots kicks with the steady *thump—hiss—thump* of the subterranean bellows. Violet arcs crack ten feet from the iron-banded trunk to the surrounding collector scrub, smelling sharp as struck flint.

The crew—five of them now, tethered to one another by braided copper-core lines—moves straight into that roaring shadow without flinching.

Two of them anchor themselves to the base of the trunk, driving long, grooved wedges of cured iron-lignin into the seams between the hollow chimneys to test the vibration. Another three climb. They do not use ladders; they hook curved spurs of silica-horn into the banded ridges of the trunk and ascend smoothly up the screaming pillar, their grounding-tails sparking continuously against the dark bark so their bodies stay at the same charge as the spire itself.

Eighty meters up, just beneath the deafening blur of the spinning crown, the bearing-collar sits hot from friction, weeping slow beads of slick, dark graphite-wax. Hanging sideways in the violent eddy that spills off the fronds, a climber pries loose sheets of the warm wax with a horn scraper and packs them into a sealed pouch of cured grazer-hide—lubricant for their joints, their tools, and the dark tunnels below. Beside her, a second climber listens with her jaw pressed hard to the vibrating chimney, reading the pitch of the internal ratchets. When she hears the thin, dry screech of a worn cork brake-pad slipping against the central tap-shaft, she taps three rapid rhythms onto the hollow trunk with the butt of her scraper.

Down at the base, the anchor-crew feels the message through their palms instantly. They uncoil a heavy spool of braided root-fiber and haul up a fresh slab of compressed cork-tissue, cut from the deep canyon strata and soaked in fire-resistant mineral salts. Working inside the shriek of the collar, timing their hands to the staggered beat of the ratchets, they slip the fresh pad into the inspection-slit that their own grandfathers first coaxed the spire to grow around a hardwood plug fifty years ago.

The tortured whine drops a half-step into a smooth, steady hum. A ten-foot violet arc leaps off a bronze frond tip not four feet from the climber’s shoulder, washing her pale amber eyes in stark white light; her grounding-braid spits an answering blue tongue down the trunk, and she does not even blink her inner lids.

That is how they shape the continent without leaving a monument on its face. They do not build; they *divert*. Drive a line of copper-veined stakes along the lip of a gully, and over three seasons the lightning shifts twenty yards west, starving one subterranean nitrogen-furnace until it cools and shrinks while overfeeding another until the living crust above it bloats with heat. Jam a tuned wooden reed into a venting pressure-valve, and the back-pressure ripples half a kilometer underground, forcing a dead stretch of vascular pipe to swell, split its resin-glands, and push fresh, rich sap-blisters up through a canyon wall right where a herd of heavy grazers—and the hunters who shadow them—will find them.

They are surgeons of air pressure and current. Every terraced swell that looks wholly wild to your eyes is quietly rigged with hidden plugs, shunts, and grounding pins—invisible beneath a skin of regrown bark and yellow chaff.

*

If you wait until the sun drops low and the horizontal glare turns the sky the color of heated brass, you will see where they vanish.

Near the base of a wind-scoured terrace, one of the living floor’s ordinary pressure-valves blows its periodic plume of mineral flour and snaps shut with a wooden clap. Before the dust has even blown clear, the crew is on it. One of them thrusts a heavy forked lever of petrified pith under the wooden lip, locking the valve half-open while a second slides a thick leather baffle-cap down the throat to choke the upward blast. The deafening pneumatic shriek drops to a muffled hiss.

One by one, they slip feet-first into the opening, folding their lean bodies neatly into the torso-wide vascular conduit, and pull the valve shut behind them. The lip claps closed. Five seconds later, a small puff of chalky dust drifts across the pale pavement, and the highland looks utterly uninhabited once more.

Follow them down, and the world turns inside out.

For the first forty feet you descend through a dry, dizzying labyrinth of honeycombed air-pipes, sliding along braided root-lines while pressurized gusts slam past adjacent conduits with the muffled thunder of distant trains. The walls are smooth, pale, faintly warm, polished slick by centuries of rushing mineral grit and the greased shells of the black segmented pipe-runners that skitter away into side-branches at your approach.

Then you reach a heavy, circular diaphragm stitched from double-layered bellows-leather and rimmed with tacky resin-felt. One of the crew works a wooden latch-wheel, equalizing the air with a sharp *hiss* that makes your ears pop violently, and hauls the hatch open.

You step through, the diaphragm seals behind you, and suddenly the screaming continent is gone.

The silence hits almost as hard as the noise did above. Not true silence—never that—but a deep, warm, cradling thrum that feels less like sound and more like standing inside the chest of a sleeping giant. The steady *thump—hiss—thump* of the bellows is now slow and heavy, vibrating comfortably in the soles of your feet. And for the first time since you arrived on the plateau, you take a breath that actually fills you.

The air down here is *thick*. Tapped off the pressurized exhaust of the deep bellows and held behind sealed diaphragms, the chambers sit at nearly sea-level density—warmed by the slow, steady glow of the subterranean nitrogen-furnaces buried in the lower strata. It smells dry and intensely alive: hot pine-resin, crushed chalk, toasted mineral meal, ozone, and the faintly sweet, waxy tang of the emulsions they live on.

You are standing in a vaulted, bone-smooth hall forty feet across and twenty feet high, its ceiling ribbed like the inside of a dry gourd. No pickaxe ever hollowed it out. Generations ago, their ancestors plugged a junction of three wide vascular conduits, bled a trickle of current from a copper-hedge overhead to warm the surrounding corky tissue, and let the bellows slowly inflate the softened living cavity over years until the resin-glands cured the walls hard as seasoned oak.

There is no flame anywhere—a single stray spark in dry cork-dust would turn a warren into a blast furnace in three heartbeats. Instead, the hall is lit by cold, humming threads of violet-green light. Along the ribbed arches hang delicate loops of stripped copper-vein encrusted with crystalline mineral salts harvested from the bedrock-furnaces. Fed a steady, carefully throttled trickle of static from the collector ridges sixty feet above, the salt-filaments glow with a constant, eerie corona—bright enough to work fine bone-needles by, soft enough to soothe eyes seared by the white highland glare.

In that pale, humming emerald-violet twilight, fifty or sixty people are moving through the warm, dense air.

Down here they shed the weighted iron-pith boots and the stiff bellows-leather coats, moving barefoot on floors woven from flattened root-fiber. Without the roar of the wind, they speak aloud—not in long, flowing vowels that waste breath and moisture, but in a swift, dry, musical patter of soft clicks, aspirated whispers, and resonant chest-tones that bounce cleanly off the smooth cork domes. When two people stand close in conversation, they still rest a hand casually on each other’s collarbone or forearm, feeling the unspoken undertone of the other’s pulse and chest-hum alongside the words.

Near the center of the chamber, where a blistered dome of dark vascular tissue radiates the dry, steady heat of a buried nitrogen-furnace beneath the floor, elders and children sit together working the harvest.

Watch what they eat and drink, and you understand how life endures in a place without rain.

Wide, shallow troughs of carved pith hold thick, pale emulsions whipped from the pressurized sap of the canyon resin-blisters—rich in dense waxes, fats, and concentrated sugars, tasting faintly of sharp cedar and bitter almond. Beside them sit flat, dark, crisp cakes baked directly on the warm furnace-dome from sieved mineral flour, ground spire-seeds snatched before the leathery gliders could claim them, and the roasted, nutty meal of black conduit-runners trapped in wire baskets at the pipe-junctions. Their metabolisms wring every drop of chemical water out of those dense fats and resins; their kidneys and sealed hides lose almost nothing to the air.

Yet there is one rarer thing kept in a locked rack of braided root-fiber near the warmest wall: a dozen small, stoppered flasks of fused silica-glass, no larger than a thumb, each holding a clear, sharp, intensely metallic liquid. Deep beneath the continental crust, where the pressurized air-blasts mill ancient bedrock into dust and the humming electrical furnaces crack raw stone, certain deep minerals still hold ancient, locked-away hydrates. When the furnaces cook that dust at peak voltage, microscopic mists of vapor bleed off through fine, cooled copper coils that the people thread into the furnace-vents. It takes a whole season for a single coil to weep enough bitter, iron-tasting condensation to fill one thumb-sized vial.

They do not drink it from cups. A mother wets her fingertip to touch the dry lips of a newborn, or a healer drips two drops onto the tongue of a climber hauled back parched and scorched from a resin-flare. To waste a drop on the dry floor would be like throwing away a year of light.

Along the shadowed edges of the hall, children who are barely old enough to walk are already learning the first and most vital craft of the highland. An old man whose knuckles are swollen and stiff with decades of accumulated silica sits beside a thick, pulsing root-pipe exposed through the chamber wall. He presses a small child’s bare palms and flat chest-drums against the pale bark.

*Thump—hiss—thump.*

To you, it is still just the mechanical beat of the plateau. The old man taps a single fingertip against the child’s collarbone in time with a subtle, staggered flutter hidden *between* the main strokes: *thump—tap-tap—hiss—thump—tap.*

He is teaching the child to read the continent.

*

Far down the corridor, tucked into a small side-alcove where three main vascular pipes converge, sits the *Listener’s Seat*—a narrow hammock of braided copper and hide suspended directly against the humming conduits.

The woman curled there has her braids clipped directly to grounding-pins in the floor, her eyes half-shut behind her translucent inner lids, both palms and both bare heels pressed against different branches of the root-network. Beside her knee, protruding from the living bark, are six carved wooden toggles—*reed-valves* of petrified pith fitted snugly into small side-ports tapped directly into the pressurized arteries.

When she pulls a toggle half-open for a fraction of a second, a sharp puff of air vents into a tuned horn of iron-lignin, sending a clean, unmistakable pressure-drop racing down ten kilometers of subterranean pipe at the speed of sound.

Half of the rhythmic hammering you felt beneath your boots on the open flats is not the blind pulsing of the bellows at all. It is a hemisphere-wide conversation.

Because the spires are spaced ten kilometers apart across the endless pale crust, the people are scattered thin—bands of thirty to eighty tucked beneath each cluster of spinning chimneys, separated by miles of howling shear and crackling dust. No band could survive alone for long. If a spire’s tap-shaft snaps, the bellows beneath it fail, the warren’s air grows thin and cold, and the people must relocate before their furnaces go dead. If a dust-front strips the copper hedges of a western ridge, they need fresh wire from the eastern canyons.

So they play the roots like a continental pipe-organ.

Every warren keeps a Listener on the pipes day and night. Through the *thump—hiss—thump* of the plateau travel weather warnings hours ahead of an amber dust-front, calls for a skilled brake-wright or a healer, news of a calf born among the heavy canyon grazers, bargains over graphite-wax and roasted seed-meal, marriages arranged between distant bands, and long, slow songs—rhythmic pressure-poems tapped out during the quiet hours of steady wind, echoing simultaneously under the feet of a thousand people spread across two hundred kilometers of cold, bright wilderness.

And when a message is not enough—when a bundle of copper-braid, a flask of bedrock-dew, a freshly grafted seed-pod, or a person themselves must cross twenty kilometers of howling flatland without risking a sudden cross-gust—they summon the *Riders*.

You see them preparing in the lower launching-bay, where a smooth, torso-wide main conduit runs dead straight toward the eastern spires.

They are mostly young—lean, narrow-shouldered, their skin gleaming black from head to toe with a thick coat of graphite-wax. Over their chests, backs, and shins they buckle overlapping, articulated plates of polished silica-horn borrowed from the design of the black segmented pipe-runners, leaving their arms free only enough to tuck tight against their ribs. At their elbows and heels jut short, hinged spurs of dark iron-lignin, filed sharp as chisels.

A Rider checks her pack—a torpedo-shaped tube of cured hide strapped flat between her shoulder blades—then bites down on the mouthpiece of a small, pressurized leather breathing-bladder tied to her throat. She does not speak; she presses her brow briefly against the Listener’s, checks the rhythm of the approaching bellows-stroke through the floor, and crawls head-first into the dark loading-breech.

The crew seals the breech behind her, locking the heavy diaphragm. The Listener watches the pressure-gauge—a thin, taut membrane of translucent resin-skin bulging steadily outward as the subterranean bellows ten meters away draws back for its stroke.

*Thump.*

The Listener yanks the release-lever.

A thunderous pneumatic kick slams through the pipe, and the Rider is gone—fired like a seed-pod down the pitch-black arterial tunnel on a roaring cushion of compressed air.

Inside that dark throat, rushing at speeds that would snap an unbraced bone, she glides centimeters from the slick, pale walls, riding the pressurized surge in total darkness. She cannot see the junctions coming; she hears them through her helmet of silica-horn and feels them in the pressure-shifts against her cheeks—each side-branch whistling past with its own distinct note, a pitch memorized since childhood. Two kilometers. Five. Ten. When she hears the triple harmonic of the eastern spire’s junction rising ahead in the dark, she shifts her weight, flares her iron-lignin elbow-spurs, and bites into the tough vascular bark.

Dry sparks shower blinding orange in the pitch-black pipe. The smell of scorched lignin floods her nose around the mouthpiece. Her brakes scream, juddering violently through her shoulders until her speed drops just enough to kick herself sideways into the receiving-branch—where a thick net of braided root-fiber and a waiting crew catch her tumbling, smoking, breathless, and laughing in dry, gasping clicks, twelve kilometers from home in less than fifteen minutes.

*

They are never more fully themselves than when the sky turns amber.

Up on the surface, the clear cold blue begins to bruise along the western horizon—a towering, sunlit wall of pulverized pollen, quartz-flour, and crackling static rolling across the plateau at a hundred kilometers an hour. Long before the dust reaches the local spires, the reeds in the Listener’s alcove begin to shriek an urgent, jagged stutter from the western bands: *Shear rising. High charge. Crown slipping on the third northern shaft.*

Down in the warren, nobody bolts the hatches to wait it out. Instead, the chamber explodes into swift, disciplined motion.

A falling spire is both a threat and a miracle. If its decapitated trunk blows open unchecked, the sudden pressure-drop can collapse the air-pressure in neighboring conduits for miles, choking the warrens and smothering the furnaces. Yet a shattered crown also rains eighty meters of prime bronze-edged fronds, fresh iron-lignin, exposed bearing-wax, and a brief, violent fountain of hot, pressurized deep-resin onto the open flats—a harvest that can feed and equip a band for three seasons, if you reach it before the storm scatters it and the crust foams shut.

Twelve of them haul on their heaviest iron-pith ballast boots, cinch their copper-core safety tethers to a single central harness, and climb straight out through the valve into the blinding, metallic amber glare.

The world above has gone ferocious. Visibility has dropped to fifty yards of swirling gold dust. Jagged white lightning stitches the advancing wall continuously, snapping with the deafening crack of cannon-fire; ozone burns bitter in the throat. Every copper-veined hedge along the canyon lip is humming loudly, tipped with dancing crowns of violet fire.

Half a kilometer upwind, the third spire is dying.

You hear it before you see it through the amber haze—a long, agonizing, woody groan that shakes the teeth in your skull. Its internal cork brakes have jammed and caught fire from friction, pouring a black ribbon of smoking dust sideways into the gale. The spinning disc of its crown wobbles wildly against the sky, shrieking like a tearing sheet of tin.

The crew drops to all fours against the wind, hauling themselves forward hand over hand along a permanent anchor-cable pegged low into the pale crust years ago for just such a day. Sparks dance continuously along their knuckles and copper braids.

Above them, the spire gives one final, ear-splitting crack.

The eighty-meter crown shears clean off its shaft in a blinding explosion of splinters and whistling bronze fronds, tumbling end over end into the golden gloom like a broken wheel. Instantly, the decapitated trunk screams—a monstrous, roaring whistle that flattens the crew against the pavement as a forty-foot geyser of pressurized chaff, ground mineral dust, and hot pale resin blasts straight into the amber sky.

A stray white arc leaps through the dust-wall and strikes the rising plume of resin-vapor. For two furious heartbeats, a tower of bright orange flame erupts fifty feet into the freezing wind, lighting the white bark like a forge—before the gale rips the fire flat, tears it into streaming rags, and snuffs it out against the empty flats.

Before the scorched bark has even stopped smoking, the crew is already scrambling into the blackened ring—racing the plateau’s own pale, coiled tendrils that are already unspooling from the neighboring crust to swallow the liberated ash and copper salts.

They move with terrifying grace inside the roar. Two of them sprint downwind with hooked grappling-lines to snare a sheared frond the size of a boat before the wind carries it into the canyons; its bronze-veined edge will yield knives, scrapers, and traded metal for a decade. Four more rush straight to the screaming stump, shielding their faces behind silica-horn bucklers as they heave a heavy, hinged cap of cured hide and petrified pith over the roaring throat.

It takes all four of them leaning into the blast, their ballast-boots grinding sparks off the crust, to force the cap down and twist its locking-pins into the jagged lignin. The deafening shriek drops to a manageable, muffled roar. The pressure in the subterranean pipes stabilizes; two kilometers away, the warren’s lamps stop flickering and settle back into a steady violet-green hum.

Meanwhile, the remaining crew works frantically around the split collar of the stump, jamming hollow bone-spigots into the foaming emergency resin-glands before the sap cures glass-hard, filling leather bladders with warm, golden emulsion until their gloves are coated thick as wax candles.

When they finally retreat down the anchor-line into the lee of the canyon, blackened with soot, dusted gold with pollen, their copper braids still spitting dying blue sparks, they carry a season’s wealth slung across their backs—and behind them, the wounded spire is already sealed, quiet, and beginning the slow, decade-long work of pushing a fresh central bud up through the plug they left in its throat.

*

They do not see themselves as strangers clinging to a hostile rock. If you could sit beside one of their warm furnace-domes and learn the dry clicks and collarbone-hums of their language, you would find they have no word for *empty*, and no word for *wilderness*.

To them, the quiet, wet lowlands far below the rim of the plateau—if they even remember rumors of such places in their oldest pressure-songs—sound suffocating and rotten, drowned in heavy rain, choked with soft green things that rot into mud and cannot hold a charge or carry a voice ten paces.

Here, everything is clean, dry, bright, and awake. Wind turns to pressure; pressure turns to dust and stone-flour; friction turns to spark; spark turns to warmth and salt and light. They are simply the part of the cycle that remembers.

Even at the end of a life, nothing steps outside the engine.

After sixty or seventy years of eating mineral-flour, breathing charged air, and living on the waxy emulsions of the crust, the plateau begins to settle visibly inside a person’s bones. Their skeletons grow extraordinarily heavy, veined faintly with leached copper and hardened silica; their joints stiffen until they can no longer climb a spire or fold into a launching-breech. In their final seasons, the elders sit mostly beside the warm furnace-domes or in the Listener’s hammock, reading the farthest, faintest pulses of the continent with palms grown sensitive as tuned reeds.

When an elder’s breath finally stops—quietly, in the warm pressurized dark, with their kin holding their palms against the cooling collarbone until the last faint tremor fades—there is no burning, and no hole dug into earth that does not exist.

First, their grandchildren unplait the long, resin-stiffened copper braids from their scalp, carefully saving every tarnished strand to weave into the hair of the young; a family’s grounding-wire is passed down for centuries, carrying the patina of ten generations of lightning. Their iron-pith boots and horn scrapers are laid beside the door for whoever next grows into them.

Then, at low sun, when the wind drops a degree and the plateau turns fierce white-gold, the band carries the body out to the quiet lip of a deep canyon—or to the base of a young spire whose trunk is still thickening against the gale.

With horn chisels and a wedged valve, they open a deep, clean hollow in the pressurized outer bark, cutting just deep enough to wake the living tissue without piercing the main air-pipes. They lay the elder gently inside the pale, fibrous cleft, facing upwind toward the glare, hands folded flat over the hollow drums of the chest.

The pressure-wedge is pulled.

Instantly, the wounded crust responds. Pale, warm resin foams from the severed glands with a soft, dry hiss, surging smoothly over the body, sealing every crease of parchment skin, filling the hollow of the bark, and curing within minutes into hard, translucent, bone-white wood. The wind sweeps a veil of yellow chaff across the fresh scar. By the next morning, the canyon wall looks unbroken.

Over centuries, the quartz-dust gales slowly plane those terraced walls back, inch by dry inch. If you walk far down in the sheltered verdigris grooves of the oldest gorges, looking closely at the stratified bands of ancient pith and cork, you will sometimes pass a pale, mineralized curve embedded seamlessly in the living wood—a rib, a dense femur, a narrow brow-ridge gleaming faintly like tarnished bronze inside the silica-hardened bark.

They are still conducting charge. Still bracing the canyon wall against the shear. Still feeling the steady, tireless throb of the subterranean bellows passing straight through their bones.

*

Climb back up to the high lip of the wind-planed terrace before the sun drops behind the rim of the world.

The light is fierce and horizontal now, throwing razor-edged shadows kilometers long across the fluted white floor. Out on the open flats, a dust-devil of bright golden chaff spins upward and shreds to nothing. Ten kilometers away, a lone spire blurs and sings in the cold jet stream, trailing its pale plume downwind, while a flock of narrow-winged gliders knives silently through its wake.

Nothing has changed to the casual eye. There is still no wall, no road, no tower of stacked stone.

Yet stand still now, press your heels down hard against the bone-dry pavement, and listen past the screaming wind. Beneath the great, tireless hammering of the subterranean bellows, you can hear it at last—quick, dry, deliberate, slipping neatly into the gaps between the strokes:

*thump—tap-tap—hiss—thump—tap.*

Answering itself from spire to spire across the bright curve of the highland. You are not standing on an empty engine. You are standing on a roof—and down in the warm, humming, violet-lit dark beneath your boots, the whole continent is talking.
