At first, even if you carried a lantern through the warm gloom, you would mistake them for the masonry.

Draped across a sunken bridge or tucked lengthwise into the pillowy curve of a stairwell, they have the same pale, dissolved look as the limestone around them—the same absence of hard angles, the same patient willingness to stay where the current lays them. Only when the slow convective wheel shifts a fraction, or when a blind catfish brushes too persistently against a rib, does a long pale shape uncoil from the travertine curtain beside an octagonal pillar and drift half an arm’s-length sideways, leaving behind a faint, milky puff of stirred lime.

There are never many of them. Fewer than a dozen, perhaps seven or nine, spaced loosely through the crowded galleries so that no single basin is ever taxed beyond its quiet yield. They are of a size with the place—the same rough proportion as the chest-high bridges and the worn stair treads—though none of them has stood upright in a thousand years. Their spines have lengthened and softened in the lifelong buoyancy, curving easily around the tight ranks of columns; their limbs are long, slender, and curiously weightless, with joints that bend a little further backward and inward than dry bone allows. If they were ever kin to the hands that chiseled these blocks and wedged the clay jars into the vaulting, the water has melted away the crispness of that kinship just as it has melted the chisel marks from the stone.

Their skin is pale as lard in the deeper creases—behind the knees, along the inner arms, down the narrow flanks—and slick with a thin, pearlescent film that lets them glide past rough limestone without scraping. That is the quiet whisper you hear in the dark when nothing else is moving. Elsewhere, along the broad flats of their backs, the ridges of their shoulders, and the outer planes of their shins, their stillness invites the cistern to claim them. Fine, matte blooms of chalky travertine dust their shoulder blades; clusters of minute glassy bivalves and thread-thin calcified worm-tubes anchor themselves along their collarbones and forearms, opening papery fans into the thermal drift as readily as if they were rooted to an arch. They do not scrub themselves clean. A coating of stone and shell is merely the ordinary thickening of age, a sign of how well one has learned to keep still. Only when a mussel settles in the hinge of an elbow, or too close to a nostril, will one reach across to another in the dark and pry it loose with a thickened, mineral-hardened thumbnail—the tiny, crisp *tick* of the shell snapping shut ringing faintly down the water-lanes.

They have no hair. Their heads are smooth and rounded, the skulls slightly broadened above recessed ear-openings that have lost their outer shells; underwater, sound does not need cups of cartilage to catch it. It enters cleanly through the jawbone and the crown. Their eyes are large, lidless or veiled in thin, translucent folds, and clouded to the color of pale river-milk. They cannot make out a pillar from a ledge by sight alone, nor see the blind loach hanging a finger’s-breadth from their knees. Yet they are not wholly blind. Those broad, milky orbs register the faintest shifts in light—the seasonal lengthening and thinning of the three gray dust-needles descending from the dome’s vent-slits, and the brief, cold flares of pale greenish-white light that bloom along the columns whenever something disturbs a grazing limpet.

What they lack in sight is repaid tenfold in their hands. Their fingers are long, slightly broadened at the tips, and patterned with deep, sensitive whorls. A single palm laid flat against a column tells them the temperature of the water to a fraction of a degree, the direction of the half-day turn, and whether the limestone beneath is slowly dissolving or slowly building its glassy skin. Moving through the cramped galleries, they do not swim with sweeping strokes; kicking wastes warmth and stirs the silt. Instead, they drift and pull—two fingertips hooked lightly around a rounded balustrade, a heel braced against a swollen bronze pin, letting the slow upward weep from the floor-seams carry them effortlessly from the lower settling-tanks toward the blackened crown.

Twice a day—sometimes only once, in the deepest, most miserly stillness of summer—they rise to breathe.

They share the upward lanes with the ancient soft-shelled turtles, ascending in the same straight, unhurried line, their hearts beating three or four times a minute. Near the ceiling, where the water grows dim gray beneath the vent-slits and cooler where it meets the outer vaulting, they slip between the crossing ribs and press their upturned faces into the flat, silver-bellied lenses of trapped gas. Their nostrils are wide and soft-valved, opening cleanly as they break the mirror of the meniscus. The air up there is thin, stale, and pressed tight by the weight of the water and the altitude; it tastes of wet chalk, faint sulfur, and the dry, biting chill of the mountain outside. They draw it in slowly, filling chests that seem almost too narrow to hold so much, and also puffing a small reserve into the pleated, elastic pouches along the sides of their throats—folds of thin, faintly pink skin that pulse slowly in the dark and let them draw a trickle of oxygen straight from the warm water between ascents. Then, without a splash, they let their lungs compress, tuck their chins, and drift sideways toward the outer perimeter walls, where the chill bleeding through three paces of limestone makes the water heavy enough to carry them gently back down to the floor without the twitch of a single muscle.

In that narrow, drowned world, they have no use for speech in air; the silver pockets are too small and too precious to waste on breathy words. Their language belongs entirely to the water—slow, sparse, and built out of pressure, resonance, and dying light.

When two meet in the pitch black between the pillars, they do not hurry past. They drift close until their jawbones nearly touch, and one hums a low, closed-mouth note—less a sound than a warm, steady vibration felt directly in the teeth and cheekbones. For greater distances across the cathedral-width of the cistern, they use the architecture itself. A knuckle tapped cleanly against the thickened travertine rim of a horizontal clay amphora mortared into the vaulting sends a dull, earthen thrum through the water. Every jar in the ceiling has its own pitch, altered by the depth of its mortar and whether one of the lard-pale eels lies coiled inside it like a stopper. By tapping two or three jars in slow sequence—separated by long pauses in which the water settles back to silence—they can tell one another where a rich hatch of amphipods has gathered on the lower steps, or warn that a loose flake of lime plaster has drifted down into a favored sleeping-niche.

And then there are the limpets.

They have learned the patience of the small glowing shells that rasp at the sulfur-crusted pillars. When one wishes to be seen—to show a companion a freshly cracked crayfish held in cupped palms, or the tilt of a head, or merely the comfort of nearness—they draw two fingertips slowly down the flank of an octagonal column. A cold, greenish-white smear flares behind the hand, lingering for four or five seconds before the warm dark swallows it whole. In that brief, ghostly wash of light, a pale face emerges: broad, calm, smooth-lipped, speckled with tiny white snail-shells along the temple, looking back with wide milky eyes. Sometimes, in the deep of alpine winter when the cold outside thickens the downward slide along the outer walls and nudges the whole convective wheel a fraction faster, one of the younger ones will drift leisurely through the middle galleries with both palms trailing over the arches, waking long, fading ribbons of greenish-white light across the drowned stonework—illuminating the rounded columns and sunken bridges one piece at a time, watching the old handwork appear and vanish in the dark for no reason more urgent than the quiet pleasure of seeing the shape of their world.

They live on the cistern’s strict budget of heat and flesh, neither above the rest of its creatures nor wholly apart from them. They graze with the same unhurried thrift as the cone-shelled snails. A hand hangs motionless near a stair tread for twenty minutes before closing softly around a blind, spindly crayfish; a thumbnail slips beneath the thin edge of a glassy bivalve; occasionally, one will wedge themselves upside down beneath the vaulting beside a clay amphora’s mouth, waiting half a turn of the water until a thick pale eel lunges a hand’s-breadth out for a passing loach, and seizing it smoothly behind the gills before it can retract. They never strip a pillar bare. They leave the salamanders undisturbed in their empty sluice-grooves, and will even share a groove with them, lying shoulder to shoulder while the small wrinkled creatures’ feathery gills pulse rhythmically against their ribs. The blind catfish drift beneath their chins without starting; flat pale ribbon-leeches loop calmly across their calves and move on. With the ancient soft-shelled turtles they keep a particular, quiet courtesy, pausing a body-length below the ceiling whenever a turtle rises straight through the dark, letting its pointed nostrils pierce the silver gas-pocket first before taking their own turn at the crown.

Their thoughts move at the same pace as a grain of lime rising from the floor-grates and sinking back down the chilled outer walls. Nothing in their memory is sharp or hurried. What they know of history is entirely tactile: they drift along the upper friezes and lay their broad, striated fingertips over the bands of old carving buried beneath translucent mineral glaze, tracing the worn ribs of tally marks and geometric braids as a dry-world sleeper might trace the grain of a familiar wooden wall. They do not know what a chisel was, or a mason, or a mountain pass, or a sky. Yet something in them still answers the impulse of the builders who kept subdividing and adding inward. Now and then, one of them will gather a few tiny, loose objects—a shed turtle scute, a perfectly whole cone-shell, a detached pebble of mortar—and tuck them carefully into a recessed joint or along the smooth lip of a low baffle-wall. Over decades, the mineral-heavy water lays its glassy skin across the arrangement, fusing it permanently into the stone. In this way, over centuries, they add their own faint, pillowy reliefs to the cistern—arrangements made to be read only by the fingertips of those who come after them.

They are born beneath the silver pockets and they end against the warm floor-seams, and between those two points stretches a life as long and unhurried as the turtles’. A child comes into the cistern only rarely—once in a long generation, when the warmth and quiet yield of the water can spare another mouth. When the time arrives, the mother wedges herself high between two horizontal amphorae directly beneath the widest lens of trapped gas, clinging inverted to the blackened ribs of the dome while two others steady her ankles and ribs in the slow upward drift. As the newborn slips free—tiny, wrinkled, startlingly pink beneath its pale glaze—she lifts it instantly so its face breaks the flat silver mirror of air. Its first breath is a thin, muffled mewl pressed into the shallow pressurized pocket between stone and water, heard by nothing outside the masonry. Then she draws it down against her chest, tucking its slick limbs beneath her chin, and rides the cool perimeter current down into the warm dark, where the blind fish gather curiously around the unfamiliar scent of birth.

And when one of them grows very old—after two centuries, or three, when their joints have stiffened with internal lime and the long ascent to the dome begins to cost more warmth than their slow hearts can easily pay—they descend for the last time into the warmest, narrowest settling-tanks beneath the lowest arches, right where the geothermal spring weeps up through the flagstone seams.

There they fold themselves comfortably against the sulfur-crusted floor, tucking their knees to their chests, resting a cheek against the rounded waist of a column base, and simply stop moving.

The cistern accepts them with the same quiet patience it gave the fallen bricks. Year by year, decade by decade, the mineral-rich water settles out over their motionless limbs. The chalky blooms on their shins and hips thicken into smooth, translucent curtains of travertine, fusing their calves, their elbows, and eventually their ribs seamlessly into the flagstones. Bivalves pave their shoulders; white snails crawl unhurriedly across the backs of their hands. For many years while they are still alive inside that growing glaze, the younger ones tend them. Twice a day, a pale shape descends from the blackened crown with lungs full and throat-pouches swollen with thin, limestone-tasting air, drifts softly into the warm settling-tank, and presses its broad, soft mouth gently against the elder’s lips—passing a long, slow breath directly from chest to chest, and sometimes a mouthful of crushed crayfish or eel-flesh, before resting a forehead briefly against the elder’s calcifying temple.

Gradually, over decades, the elder’s need shrinks. Their pleated throat-folds pulse more and more faintly; they take fewer breaths, then none at all, until one day a younger one drifts down from the ceiling, lays a palm against the glazed ribcage, and feels only the steady, metallic warmth of the geothermal spring rising through stone. Nothing is mourned with noise or commotion. In time, the translucent mineral skin finishes smoothing over the folded face, softening the cheekbones and lips until no outsider could tell the sleeper from a pillowy outcrop of travertine—yet the living always know where they lie. Drifting through the lowest arches in the pitch dark, they still trail their fingertips lightly across those particular rounded swellings beside the floor-seams, greeting the stillness of their predecessors as easily as they read the blurred friezes overhead.

Of the vast, frozen alpine world beyond three paces of limestone, they hold only the dimmest, strangest notions—known entirely through the four slender messages that still pass the walls. They know the outside as the cold weight that leans harder against the perimeter masonry in winter and eases in summer; they know it as the heavy, sub-audible pressure of mountain gales humming faintly in their jawbones while the water around them remains utterly still; they know it as the three pale needles of gray dust tilting through the dome’s crown.

And now and then, drifting near the upper galleries, one of them feels a tiny, alien tickle against an upturned palm and closes long fingers around a drowned mountain midge that has blown through a vent-slit and sunk slowly into the warm gloom. They do not eat it immediately. Suspended motionless between two octagonal pillars, they roll the microscopic body gently between thumb and forefinger, their ultra-sensitive pads marveling at the brittle legs, the impossibly thin, crisp wings—a fleeting, sharp-edged scrap from somewhere dry and violent beyond the stone, where things have not yet been softened into permanence.

The oldest among them—the ones whose own knees are just beginning to stiffen, who remember when the three gray dust-needles reached two treads farther down the upper mezzanine stairs—can feel the vent-slits narrowing by the width of a fingernail over the turning of decades. They feel the silver gas-pockets at the crown thinning by a hair’s-breadth, the winter gales pressing a fraction more faintly against their teeth. They do not claw at the dripstone in the blackened roof, nor panic at the slow sealing of their sky. Everything in this place settles, thickens, and fuses in its own time. They simply fold their limbs a little closer, let their hearts slow by half a beat, and drift on along the warm, crowded lanes between the pillars—soft, patient, and utterly at home inside their drowned cathedral in the clouds.
