If you stand still at the bottom of the heel-worn trough and let your eyes adjust to the amber dusk, the first thing you realize is that the people here do not move like people who grew up under an open sky.

They move like things that know the exact geometry of a crack.

A woman carrying a clay jar balanced on her hip climbs a near-vertical juniper ladder without once looking at her feet, her bare toes curling over rungs polished slick as old horn, her free hand brushing aside a curtain of thorny caper-vine before her face even reaches it. Two boys chasing a loose speckled hen along a fourth-tier parapet turn their shoulders sideways in perfect unison to slip past a slung bundle of drying squash, neither breaking stride nor losing a syllable of their argument. Nobody swings their arms wide here. Nobody stretches lazily or throws their head back to laugh. Every gesture is folded close to the ribs, neat and frugal, shaped by five hundred years of low lintels, leaning walls, and neighbors breathing three feet away.

They are small, dry-built people—narrow in the hip, sinewy in the calf and forearm, with broad, hard-soled feet and collarbones sharp as bird-keels beneath dusty ochre skin. On the plateau outside, the wind would try to drink them dry; inside the pocket, the heat and thin highland air have cured them like strips of fruit hung in the rafters. Even the stoutest grandmother has flesh that feels taut and dense as sun-dried clay, and the children are all knobby knees, dark watchful eyes, and ribs visible beneath a fine bloom of silt and wood-ash.

They never wash with water. To pour a gourd of water over your own head would be seen the way a lowland town might see someone setting fire to a granary. Instead, they bathe in dust and scent. In the warm slack of midday, you will see people sitting on the roof-edges rubbing their arms and necks with bowls of ultra-fine, sifted clay dust mixed with crushed thyme, bitter-melon oil, and pale wood-ash, scrubbing away dried sweat and swift-mites until their skin gleams with a soft, matte luster. They smell unmistakably of the room they live in: dry herbs, resin, warm terracotta, tallow smoke, and the faintly sweet, clean musk of dry-kept bodies.

Look closely at their heads, and you begin to understand the oldest rule of the pocket.

Almost no one past childhood has hair hanging loose below the ears, and half the heads in sight are cropped uneven—tufted at the crown, shorn close at the nape, or bound into tight, oiled ridges that hug the skull. Hair here is not vanity; it is infrastructure. A human head is the one crop in the hollow that grows steadily through drought and lean years alike, drawing nothing extra from the cisterns, and fine human hair catches dawn mist better than any twisted leaf-fiber alone.

Every person here wears their civic history on their scalp. Infants keep their soft down until their third year, when a grandmother shears the first locks with a sliver of flint and presses them into the hands of the net-menders. Young men and women grow heavy, carefully greased braids through adolescence—not to flirt, though a thick glossy braid is still admired, but as a debt waiting to be paid. When they claim a sleeping-loft of their own, or join a hearth, they sit on the high lip of the mouth at dusk and let the elders cut the braids off at the collarbone. Within a week, their hair is knotted into the wide mist-curtains stretched across the stone brow.

When someone dies, they are shorn down to pale stubble before the body goes cold. That final harvest is braided into the topmost band of the outer nets—the edge closest to the biting highland sky—so that the dead are the first to catch the morning fog and send it dripping down into the gourds of the living. Every sip a child takes at sunrise has run down the hair of their great-grandmothers.

Because every drop of water is combed out of the air one bead at a time, moisture carries a weight of etiquette an outsider could spend a year breaking by accident. Nobody spits; to spit on the floor is an obscenity worse than striking a neighbor. Nobody drinks in long, noisy gulps; you take a mouthful from a narrow-necked gourd, hold it against your gums until the dryness softens, and swallow slowly. Even grief is dry-eyed if it can be managed, or wept silently into a cloth that is pressed back against the face. And nothing wet leaves the body unused: night-jars are carefully emptied into tall, stoppered clay vessels tucked behind the outer ledges, where aged urine softens tough leaf-fibers for cordage and feeds the stubborn roots of the bitter-melons and rock-figs clinging to the masonry.

*

In an acoustic bowl where five thousand wings and a hundred grinding stones never stop churning the air, a lowland voice would be worn raw by noon. The people of the pocket have adapted their speech to the stone.

When they stand face to face in the narrow alleys, they speak in a dry, breath-saving murmur tucked close to the collarbone, threaded through with sharp tongue-clicks, crisp glottal stops, and high, sibilant consonants. Those tiny percussive sounds cut cleanly *between* the metallic trilling of the swifts and the low rumble of the wind outside, traveling six paces through the roar without anyone ever raising their chest or wasting throat-wetness.

Across the vertical tiers, they hardly speak at all. Instead, they talk with their hands, chins, and shoulders. A potter on the second landing can hold a swift, sharp-tempered negotiation with a vine-tender on the fifth balcony without opening his mouth: a flick of two fingers off the cheekbone for *gourd*, a cupped palm tilted downward for *cracked*, a sharp double bob of the chin for *wait until dusk*. Watching them from the shadows of the courtyard, they look uncannily like the rusty-throated agamas perched on the lintels beside them, signaling silently across the sunlit shafts while the air screams with birds.

That quietness is more than acoustics; it is how three hundred people survive living inside a single room without tearing one another apart.

The walls between houses are scarcely thicker than a spread hand—hand-packed silt and chopped stems, riddled with rodent-runs and lizard-cracks. You hear your neighbor's child coughing through the plaster. You hear the dry rustle of lovers shifting on a woven reed mat overhead. You know by the cadence of the stone pestle downstairs whether the woman beneath your floor is angry, weary, or pregnant. There is no architectural privacy anywhere in the pocket, so privacy has had to be built entirely out of manners.

Children are taught almost before they can climb a ladder how to practice *the blind ear* and *the smooth eye*. You do not look through an open lintel as you pass on the stairs. You do not mention a quarrel you overheard through the bedroom clay, even if dust shook down onto your face while they shouted. If a neighbor steps into a shadowed corner to weep or nurse a burn, the alley magically widens around them; people step past as softly as geckos, refusing to register their presence until they step back into the light.

Still, heat and closeness breed grudges. When two people have poisoned the air between them so badly that even the blind ear cannot cover it, the town does not beat them, nor does it cast them out—exile onto the plateau is simply murder with a slower clock. Instead, the elders send both parties up the heel-worn trough, past the baffle of boulders, and out onto the bald lip of the highland in the late afternoon, with nothing between them but a single thin woven cloak and no water-gourd.

They are not allowed back down until the sky turns violet.

Out there, there is no ceiling to hold your warmth, no wall to lean your spine against, and the wind peels the heat straight off your ribs in ten minutes. By the time the first stars prick the thin cold blue, the bitterest enemies are almost always sitting shoulder-to-shoulder behind a rock, sharing the single cloak, their faces tucked out of the gale. When the watchers finally tap a gourd to call them down, they tumble back into the dim, stinking, roaring warmth of the pocket with a gratitude so sharp it washes clean through the bone. Out on the crown of the world, you remember very quickly why you tolerate your neighbors.

*

Though the town is no wider than a courtyard, its six stacked tiers have sorted themselves into distinct climates—and with them, distinct breeds of people.

Up on the fifth and sixth tiers, pressed close to the overhanging stone brow and the bright horizontal slit of sky, live the rim-dwellers. They are the leanest, darkest-skinned, and quickest-handed people in the pocket, permanently dusted with bird-lime and silver air-plant fluff. They live directly beneath the roar of the incoming swifts and share their parapets with the kestrels' stray feathers and the nightly patrols of the notched-ear cliff-fox. Here live the mist-watchers, the net-menders, and the *wind-goers*—the few dozen men and women tough enough to climb regularly out onto the plateau.

The wind-goers are looked at with a strange blend of reliance and quiet pity. Two or three mornings a month, before the sun climbs high enough to bake the gneiss, they oil their skins heavily with melon-seed grease, wrap their faces in dampened hair-cloth, sling resin-stoppered water-gourds across their chests, and climb out into the scoured emptiness. They range for miles across the barren swells, following ancient, barely visible cairns to the rare leeward folds where stunted juniper still dies slowly enough to leave dry wood, where nodules of grey flint weather out of seams, where dried dung of wandering highland sheep can be scraped from the lee of boulders, or where crusted mineral salt gathers in wind-scoured pans.

Sometimes—once in a few years, or once in a generation—a party of wind-goers makes the long, brutal descent off the rim of the highland toward the distant grey valleys below, carrying bundles of cured aromatic herbs, finely knotted hair-cordage, carved bone needles, and tiny jars of concentrated caper-buds to trade with valley-folk who barely believe the plateau is inhabited. That is where the town's few scraps of metal come from: the hammered copper awls, the thumb-length bronze blades. No single person owns a piece of metal. Each blade belongs to a lineage of hands, carried in a leather sheath around a craftsman's neck until their fingers stiffen with age, passed down along with the names of everyone who ever sharpened it. When a bronze knife wears down over decades to a dull sliver no wider than a bird's claw, it is still not discarded; it is wedged with heated plant resin into a notched goat-bone and becomes an engraving pin for marking clay cisterns.

When the wind-goers drop back down the entry trough at twilight, their eyes are bloodshot from the glare of the open sky and their voices are cracked dry. They sit on the upper ledges for an hour without speaking, letting the dim amber light and the wall of bird-noise close safely back over their heads.

Below the rim-dwellers, packed into the warm middle tiers—the second, third, and fourth landings—lies the dense, golden-brown heart of the town. This is the realm of the makers and growers: the potters who blend fallen swift-nest clay with Highland silt; the women who prune and pollinate the hanging bitter-melons with bird-feathers tied to twigs; the gourd-splitters, basket-weavers, resin-boilers, and snare-makers. Here the light arrives entirely secondhand, filtered through hanging curtains of waxy rock-fig leaves and silver air-plants, and the air smells thickest of toasted seeds and drying clay. Every roof here is a workbench; every windowsill is a tiny farm. The middle-tier people rarely climb out onto the plateau at all. Many of them have not seen the full dome of the sky since childhood, and speak of the outside with the mild, shuddering distaste a sailor might feel for the bottom of the sea.

Down at the very bottom, where the uneven bedrock floor slopes back into the darkest, coolest recess of the hollow, live the pale ones—the cistern-keepers, the elder seed-grinders, the heavily pregnant, the bone-carvers, and the very old whose knees can no longer manage the greasy juniper ladders. Sunlight never touches them. Their faces are lit all day by the trembling orange sparks of tallow lamps set into wall niches, and their skins fade to the soft, pale olive of cave-roots.

The cistern-keepers are the quietest people in the pocket, and the most respected. They sit among the giant, round-bellied clay water-jars tucked into the cool rear crevices, right beneath the hanging grapes of sleeping bats. They know the water not by sight—it is too dark back there to peer into the necks—but by sound. Tapping a knuckle lightly against the curved flank of a jar, an old cistern-keeper can tell by the pitch of the clay ring, down to the cupful, how much dawn-mist remains, whether a hairline crack is weeping against the stone, and whether the town must tighten its rations for the dry week ahead. They speak softly so as not to wake the bats overhead, and their palms are always cool to the touch, prized by feverish children who are carried down from the hot upper tiers to sleep beside the jars.

*

Yet if you ask someone sitting on a clay doorstep *who lives here*, they will not count only the humans. To count only the two-legged tenants of the pocket would seem as foolish as describing a tree and leaving out its bark and sap.

The town is a parliament of dynasties, most of them older than the mud walls.

Overhead, the swifts and rock-martins are the landlords of the daytime air. The people know the geography of the mud-jug ceiling as well as their own alleys: which clusters hold aggressive elders that dive-bomb anyone carrying a bright basket up the fifth-tier ladder, which old nests have grown heavy and cracked and are about to drop a prized lump of fine, saliva-bound clay onto the drying mats below. Every spring, when the young birds fledge and the weakest tumbles into the courtyard dust, a child or a speckled hen gets to it before the beetles do. No one climbs up to raid the active nests—not out of piety, though the birds are spoken of with gruff affection, but because the swifts are the wall that keeps the midges and biting flies from turning the pocket into a plague-pit, and their chalky droppings are the secret strength of the town's mortar and soil.

In the dark rear clefts, the bats hold the night shift. Their soft, dry rustling is the backdrop to every birth and every council held near the cisterns. Once a season, young men tie cloths over their mouths and scrape the dry, crystalline guano from the rear ledges into woven panniers—black gold, light as ash, traded by the spoonful to the women who tend the roof-edge bean troughs and thorny capers.

Along the walls and beneath the eaves live the pale, translucent geckos and bronze skinks—the town's unspoken watchmen. Almost every household has a *jar-lizard*, a plump, pale gecko that has claimed the warm overhang directly above the family's grain-baskets and drying fruit. These lizards are never shooed away; elders name them after stubborn uncles or sharp-eyed aunts, and children gently flick half-stunned moths toward their favorite mortar-cracks. If a jar-lizard suddenly flattens itself and darts deep into the wall-chaff, every woman grinding seeds on that ledge stops and looks up, searching the rafters for the quiet, pencil-thin glide of a rock-snake.

Behind the plaster and inside the hollow spaces beneath the woven floor-mats live the cliff-rodents—blunt-eared, silky-grey, and maddeningly clever. They have lived inside human walls for so many generations that they are no longer quite wild animals. They have learned the rhythm of the town's labor: they sit quiet while the bedrock pestles go *shuck-shuck-shuck*, knowing the women are crouched beside the grain-baskets, and pour out of their smooth-worn keyholes the very instant the rhythm breaks and the footsteps move toward the ladder. The people trap them with clever hair-nooses and thorn-baffles, and eat the fattest ones spit-roasted over tiny dung-fires when winter wind starves the plateau—yet they never quite wish them gone, for the rodents' sharp alarm-whistles beneath the floorboards are the earliest warning in the town when a venomous highland viper slips down the entry trough looking for warmth.

And out on the highest parapets, patrolling the seam between the green overhang and the barren world, moves the lineage of cliff-foxes. The lean, long-eared vixen with the triangular notch in her left ear is the fourth of her line the elders remember. Her grandmother had a white tip to her tail; her mother walked with a slight hitch in one hind paw after a tussle with a kestrel. The notched-ear vixen does not beg, and she will snap a child's fingers to the bone if cornered, yet she curls openly in the afternoon sun on the sixth-tier drying roof while women pound leaf-fibers ten paces away. She keeps the rodents from overrunning the upper rafters, and in return, no one throws stones when she drops silently into a vine-tangle to claim a slow-moving kit or a stray rabbit.

Beneath all of them—beneath the humans, the fowl, the rodents, and the clay floors themselves—live the true foundations of the pocket: the matte-black beetles.

They are everywhere, yet cleanly kept in their place. In a warm, closed room packed with living things and almost no water to wash away filth, rot would kill the town in a single season. The beetles are why the air smells of warm clay and dry chaff instead of sickness. They swarm silently in dry, ash-ringed pits built beneath the lowest ledges, where kitchen scraps, plucked feathers, broken eggshells, and spoiled skins are dropped each evening. Listening closely with your ear to the lower stonework, you can hear them working—a faint, dry, papery scratching, like thousands of tiny fingernails turning pages in the dark.

And it is the beetles who take care of the dead.

You cannot dig a grave into iron-dark gneiss, and wood is far too precious to spend on a pyre. So when an elder's breath finally goes thin and stops in the cool dark near the cisterns, the family washes their skin one last time with thyme-dust, shears their hair for the top edge of the dawn-nets, and wraps them in a worn reed mat. At twilight, four kinfolk carry the light, dry bundle into a low, dry cleft tucked far behind the deepest wall—a narrow chamber screened by stacked slabs, sealed from the birds and rodents, where the oldest colony of beetles lives in absolute darkness.

No one watches. They slide the mat in, set the slab back, and leave a pinch of dry resin burning on the sill.

Twelve days later, when the resin-crust has gone cold and dust has settled over the threshold, the kinfolk return with a clean woven basket. There is no stench at all—only the sharp, dry mineral odor of chalk and beetle-shell. Inside the mat lies a skeleton scrubbed cleaner and whiter than river-quartz.

Nothing in the pocket is ever thrown away if it can still hold warmth or weight. The skull is rubbed with red ochre clay and tucked into a small, high niche in the rear rock wall, overlooking the family's tier, where dozens of smooth ancestral brows watch the tallow sparks flicker in the dark. The long bones—tough, dense, cured by a lifetime of dry living—are rarely left idle. A grandmother's forearm bone may be wedged horizontally into a mud-plaster wall as a peg for hanging water-gourds, or split and ground down on gneiss into awls, net-shuttles, and hair-pins. The small finger-bones are drilled with copper pins and strung onto infants' ankles as dry, clicking rattles. To an outsider it might feel eerie; to the people of the pocket, it is simply the refusal to be lonely. You mend your mist-net with your uncle's rib, hang your shawl on your mother's wrist, and drink mist combed through your grandfather's hair. Nobody truly leaves the overhang.

*

Even the oldest things alive in the pocket are not animal at all.

Wedged into a deep, diagonal fault that splits the rear wall and runs upward toward the lip of the brow grows a single gnarled rock-fig whose trunk is thick as two men's waists, barked in silver-grey plates like crocodile hide. Nobody remembers when it sprouted; the oldest wall-plaster wraps cleanly around its lower roots, meaning the tree was already gripping the stone when the first mud walls were patted upright. Its roots thread invisibly behind four tiers of houses, holding the dried silt together like sinew; its twisted upper boughs arch out through the third and fourth landings like living rafters, polished shiny where generations of children have swung from them and where mothers tie woven cradles so the passing drafts rock their babies to sleep.

Every year it puts out small, hard, dusty-green figs that taste fiercely sweet, like dry honey and pepper. People do not climb into its high crown to strip it bare; they take what falls and what hangs within easy arm's reach, leaving the rest for the bats at night and the rock-martins by day. In the driest spells of late summer, when the morning mist thins to almost nothing, the cistern-keepers still pour a single cupped handful of grey water from the bottom of the dregs into the root-crack at the tree's base. If the fig ever dies and its roots shrink back inside the mortar, half the middle tiers will crack and slump. They know it; the tree seems to know it too, clinging to the gneiss with a stubborn, muscular calm.

*

It is at dusk, after the bats have poured out into the violet slit of sky and the swifts have packed themselves muttering into their ceiling jugs, that you can feel what this place truly is.

The sun is gone from the plateau above, and outside the baffle the highland wind drops swiftly toward freezing. But the ancient gneiss overhead has been drinking sunlight all afternoon, and now it begins to exhale. A gentle, dry, steady warmth radiates downward from the curving stone vault, wrapping all six tiers like a palm cupped over a spark.

Up and down the honeycombed walls, tiny orange beads of tallow light blink awake. The hanging curtains of caper-vine and silver air-plants throw huge, trembling lace-shadows across the smoke-darkened ceiling. Pale geckos creep out to the edges of the lamplight, throats pulsing, waiting for moths. Down in the narrow court, the speckled hens tuck their heads beneath their wings inside woven reed coops, and the notched-ear fox curls into a tight comma on the warm top slab of the fifth-tier oven-parapet, nose buried under her brush.

This is when the town makes its music.

Not with shouting or heavy drums—the bowl of stone would turn loud noise into a headache—but with small, bone-dry instruments built for an intimate room. On the third landing, an old man draws a notched juniper stick across the ridged spine of a dried gourd, making a soft, insectile *krrk-krrk* that blends seamlessly with the beetles beneath the floor. Across the dark air, a girl on the fifth tier answers on a flute cut from a kestrel's wing-bone, four thin, piercing notes that bend around the curving stone and come back warm and doubled. Someone else slaps the mouth of an empty clay cistern with a flat palm, sending a deep, hollow *boom* vibrating right up through the bedrock and into the bare heels of every listener.

People sit shoulder-to-shoulder on the roof-edges, knees tucked under chins, shelling dry melon-seeds, twisting cordage between thumb and thigh, letting the heat of the stone roof settle into their hair. Babies sleep strapped to their mothers' chests. Down in the dark floor-cracks, the cliff-rodents whistle their high, thin evening chorus, answered by the clicking of geckos in the eaves.

Then, once or twice in the deep hours after the lamps are pinched down to embers, a sound comes from the farthest, driest seam at the back of the overhang—a single, crisp, resonant *tick*, like a fingernail tapped against iron, as the ancient sun-baked gneiss cools by a fraction of a degree and settles in its bed.

Every human voice in the pocket pauses for half a heartbeat. Even the children curling on their ash-ringed sleeping platforms know that sound. They do not believe in gods perched up in the bright, empty, freezing sky; what lives up there only scours and strips and takes. Whatever keeps them alive is down here—in the fold, in the shadow, in the warm iron spine of the rock holding its heat through the dark.

When the stone ticks, an old cistern-keeper in the back crevice murmurs a dry, clicking word of thanks beneath her breath, turns onto her side beside the cool clay jars, and goes back to sleep. Above her, five tiers of packed, breathing, chattering life—human, bird, lizard, root, and beetle—huddle together under the heavy stone eyebrow, warm and stubborn and completely invisible to the empty roof of the world.
