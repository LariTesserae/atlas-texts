To live here, you have to stop thinking of the ground as a floor and start thinking of it as a skin.

Everything that breathes in the hollow knows this before it knows anything else. You can see it first in the smallest, most stubborn tenants—the ones you almost never lay eyes on, because they have made a pact with the green warmth just beneath the frost.

If you slice open one of the raised, winding welts that cross the waxy terraces, you will find a clean, damp bore the width of a man’s forearm, its walls scored with fine parallel grooves and glistening with a thin, sweetish glaze. The creature that made it is rarely far ahead, though it hears your boots through the fibrous strata long before you hear it. People here call them *pith-moles*, or simply *warm-backs*, though they have little in common with the moles of any soil-bound world. A full-grown one is heavy as a small pig, built like a blunt wedge of muscle wrapped in dense, silver-white wool that sheds sap the way a duck sheds rain. It has no eyes—only smooth, scarred bosses of pale hide where sockets ought to be—and its ears are tight, muscular valves it can pinch shut when the cambium floods. Its forelimbs are wide, flat paddles tipped with translucent horn-blades that do not dig so much as plane and shear, shaving curls of living pulp straight into its mouth as it moves.

Because the plant-crust insulates what lies beneath it, the cambium layer stays a few degrees warmer than the air outside, kept fluid by the plant’s own sugary antifreeze. The warm-backs swim through that narrow band of mercy. Their blood runs thick and faintly syrupy; their body heat is high enough that they must vent it every few minutes by nosing up to the waxy outer skin, rasping a pinhole through the chalky armor, and exhaling a single bright bead of steam into the freezing glare.

They live in a world of pure pressure and vibration. Long, stiff guard-hairs along their flanks read the slow hydraulic pulse of the great plates around them. When a shaft of unblunted light crawls onto the terrace overhead, the warm-backs know it within seconds—not by sight, and not even by heat at first, but by the sudden, frantic thrumming in the vascular tubes as dormant sap slams upward through the veins. That sound is their terror. When a shelf begins to blister, the welts on the surface suddenly writhe and fork as the burrowers race furiously inward, away from the cliff edge, trying to outrun the shear. Sometimes they misjudge. When a half-mile slab of white-green earth peels away into the void, you can occasionally see, if your eyes are sharp behind your goggles, two or three dark, furry specks tumbling silently alongside the shattered fronds, clawing at empty air.

And waiting for them in that air are the gliders.

Up close, a glider is less like a bird than a kite assembled from bone, parchment, and dry spite. Its wingspan can clear five meters, yet the whole animal weighs scarcely more than a winter cloak. The membranes are hairless, frosted cream, thin enough that the noon glare shines straight through them, turning the creature’s hollow struts and fine branching veins into a faint, ghostly filigree against the brightness. It has no chest muscle to speak of; it cannot flap itself off a flat plain. If a glider ever touches down on a level terrace, it dies there, awkward and grounded, dragging itself on its elbows until the cold or a hunter takes it.

Instead, they live permanently on the lip of the drop. They roost by hooking their long, curved hind-talons into the raw edges of newly sheared overhangs, folding their wings like pleated fans and leaning out over two kilometers of nothing so that launch requires no effort at all—only the decision to let go. Their heads are narrow, wedge-shaped, and nearly naked, the skin stained dark charcoal around the eyes to drink up the glare. Over each eye sits a thick, leathery nictitating shield pierced by a single horizontal slit, held half-shut even in flight.

They are the scavengers of geometry. A glider can hang motionless for hours in the cold updraft peeling off a sun-warmed wall, scarcely tilting a wingtip, waiting for the dull *whump* of a distant collapse. The sound brings them from ten kilometers around. They slide silently across the gulf toward the rising veil of diamond-dust, banking through the swirling mist to pluck stunned burrowers off fractured ledges, or clinging precariously to the dripping violet-black wounds of a freshly split trunk to rasp out the soft, sweet amber pith with their serrated tongues. They make no call. When two gliders dispute a fresh wound, they do it in utter silence save for the dry, papery *clack* of their jaws and the brittle snap of wing-tips slapping together like stiffened linen.

Closer to the sheer, jagged sails and vertical ribs live the clamberers—nervous, slender things that look as if someone stretched a pale marten to twice its proper length and gave it hands instead of paws. Their fur is hollow-shafted and faintly greasy, refusing to hold rime even when a dry squall of ice-crystals flashes over them. Their toes end in black, recurved hooks of keratin that can find purchase in a millimeter-deep seam of waxy bark.

Unlike the blind burrowers or the austere gliders, the clamberers are cursed with curiosity. They are solitary and fiercely jealous of the narrow, shaded crevices where meltwater and thin sap collect in dark puddles, yet they cannot leave a strangeness alone. A lone traveler crossing a ribbed ridge will sometimes catch a flicker of movement forty feet up a vertical blade of foliage—a pale, triangular face peeking around a serrated edge, amber eyes blinking behind translucent double lids, long ears twitching at the soft crunch of boots. They will shadow a human for half a day across terrain no person could dream of climbing, leaping effortlessly across bottomless ten-foot gaps with their long tufted tails trailing straight behind them. They have learned that where people pass, salt follows. Leave a braided phloem-rope lashed to a cliff overnight, and by morning a clamberer will have chewed half the fibers to fuzzy pulp just for the trace of human sweat dried into the cord.

In the dark pockets where those clamberers drink—the deep, cold wells inside split stems and overlapping bracts—there is smaller life still: thread-thin, glassy worms that knot together in slow, pulsing tangles at the bottom of freezing sap-puddles, and pale, flat-bodied beetles no bigger than a fingernail that skate across the briny surface, feeding on the faint, sour bloom of cold-fermenting sugars. When a beam of light boils a puddle dry, the beetles simply freeze into hard white beads and wait for the next thaw, or ride the diamond-dust updrafts across the gulf to fall on some distant cliff and wake again.

***

And then there are the people.

If you do not know how to look for them, you can cross thirty kilometers of bright, ringing emptiness and swear the hollow holds no human life at all. They do not build roads, because a road here is a joke the floor tells for a week before tearing it in half. They do not build fires in the open; smoke is a thin grey smudge easily lost in the mist, and fuel is too precious to burn where the dry air steals the heat before it reaches your hands. Even their bodies are dressed to vanish: heavy, oddly light coats stitched from the silver-white pelts of warm-backs, trousers reinforced at the knees and shins with cured, waxy outer-bark that matches the celadon ridges underfoot.

What gives them away, from miles off, is a single stubborn speck of rust-red—a rag tied to a walking-staff, a strip stitched across the shoulder of a parka, or a marker-flag snapping softly above a low dark hump tucked into the crook of an older heartwood knuckle.

Up close, the place is written directly onto their faces.

Even in youth, the skin around their eyes is creased into deep, permanent fans from squinting through the narrow horizontal slots of their smoked-pith goggles. When a traveler ducks inside a dark bark dome and pushes those goggles up onto their forehead, the face beneath looks briefly masked: a pale, almost translucent band across the eyes and bridge of the nose, framed by cheeks, chin, and brow burned a harsh, weathered copper-brown by the reflected glare. It is a strange sight—people who have lived their entire lives four kilometers underground, carrying the sunburn of high-altitude mountaineers.

Their hands tell the rest. The knuckles are thick and stiffened by cold; the creases of their palms are stained an indelible greenish-black from handling raw pith, dark curing resins, and the bitter juices of wounded stems. Nearly every adult bears the pale, puckered scars of frost-nip, or a shallow, razor-clean white line across a wrist or forearm where a tensioned tendril snapped loose like a wire. Their feet are rarely still, even when they stand talking; they shift their weight subtly from heel to ball inside soft-soled burrower-hide boots, listening downward.

For they are a listening people, though their world barely speaks.

Sound in the open hollow is treacherous—deadened by distance, swallowed by the spongy vascular walls, arriving seconds too late from across the void. So they do not trust their ears to the air. They listen through their shins, their knees, and the butts of their long, bone-shod staves. Tap a stave against a waxy plate and rest your bare palm against the shaft, and you can feel what the ground is thinking: a dry, hollow *tock* means older, deadened timber, safe to cross and slow to shift; a dense, wet hum means active sap rising beneath the crust, warning you that the ridge will tilt or buckle before the next midday glare. A tight, high vibration—like a bowed string held just below audibility—means the shelf is under shearing stress, held in place only by frost and habit, waiting for a single degree of warmth to let go.

Children here learn that vocabulary before they learn to count. A four-year-old tethered to a heartwood peg by a braided phloem-cord will lay an ear flat against the cold horn of the floor and tell you whether the ground is *sleeping*, *drinking*, or *reaching*.

They live by a clock no surface-dweller would recognize immediately, though the unseen sun above still rules it. The hollow has no true night, only the *glare* and the *dim*. At the height of the glare, when sunlight drives nearly straight down through the fractured kilometers of glacial ice, the white blades from the ceiling rifts rake steadily across the cliffs and terraces, waking the walls into silent, blistering violence. That is when the people stay still, or work only in the deep, permanent shadows beneath the muddiest, thickest veins of the glacier overhead.

It is during the *dim*—when the unseen sun drops low and the light through the ice turns a heavy, luminous indigo, then a ghostly, cold silver—that the basin truly belongs to them. In the dim, the shafts vanish. The heated faces stop sweating; the diamond-dust squalls settle; the rushing sap thickens and locks into a rigid, crystalline stillness. The whole vast landscape cools with a soft, ceaseless chorus of tiny *ticks* and *pings*, like a miles-wide furnace shutting down. For six or eight hours, the cliffs stop falling. The trails hold their shape. That is when the small knots of travelers—rarely more than five or six people together, pulling light sledges bent from dried curved bracts—step out into the wide blue quiet and cover ground.

They do not speak much while walking. In the cold air, shouting wastes moisture and carries barely fifty paces anyway. Instead, they talk with their staves and their red rags—a slow sweep overhead for a newly opened seam, two sharp taps of a bone-ferrule for a warm-back welt, a held vertical blade of dark bark flashed against a white wall to hail a distant party across a three-kilometer gulf. When they do speak, inside the dim hush of a dome or crouched in the lee of a rib, their voices are quiet, clipped, and curiously flat, pitched low in the throat so they do not bruise ears accustomed to silence.

***

No one here owns land, because land does not stay where you leave it. Instead, they divide themselves loosely by how closely they dare to live beside the moving light.

Along the far, curving rim of the basin—where the colossal flora roots itself against the oldest, thickest perimeter ice and the glare arrives diffused and mild—sit the *knuckle-keepers*. Here, the botanical plates have piled up over centuries into gnarled, slow-moving buttresses of dense, darkened heartwood that shift by mere inches a season rather than yards a day. In the deep, windless crooks of these knuckles, extended families pitch clusters of three or four domed shelters, their bent-rib frames shingled tight with overlapping plates of cured, blackened bark that drink up the stray brightness and hold in the heat of a single burrower-fat lamp.

Even here, nothing is permanent. Every week, someone walks the perimeter of the domes with a bone mallet, tapping back the copper pegs that the living plant is patiently trying to push out with pale, irritated calluses. Every year or two, a knuckle begins to groan in a new pitch, or a seam opens beneath a drying-rack, and the whole cluster calmly unlashes its ribs, rolls up its bark shingles, packs its salted pith and grease-bladders onto sledges, and shifts half a kilometer along the rim without a backward glance.

The knuckle-keepers are the crafters, the keepers of children, and the tenders of sap-wells. They tap the slow, deep vascular veins with hollow bone spiles, collecting dark, mineral-rich syrups that they freeze-concentrate in open troughs—skimming away the pure water-ice again and again until what remains is a thick, amber cordial that keeps a body from scurvy and madness through the coldest stretches. They strip the long inner fibers of dead fronds, soaking them in bitter sap-lye and chewing or hammering them soft to braid the phloem-cord that binds everything they own. They cure the warm-back pelts by rubbing them with cold wood-ash and brain-fat, and carve the dark, smoky inner-pith from ancient deadwood into the slit-goggles without which a human goes snow-blind in an afternoon.

Then there are the *seam-runners*—smaller, leaner pairs, or solitary walkers who spend months out on the restless central shelves and the sweeping buttresses that arch out over the void.

They go where the light-blades cut. A fresh botanical avalanche is a horror if you stand on it, and a harvest if you arrive an hour after the dust clears. Where a half-mile face has sheared clean away, the raw, steaming wall exposes things you cannot reach anywhere else: deep heartwood ribs dense as oak for shelter-frames; huge blocks of sweet, unweathered amber pith; bruised violet veins whose oxidized juice, mixed with iron-dust, yields the fierce rust-red dye that keeps them visible to one another in the endless white. Most of all, the fresh collapses expose the severed tunnels of the warm-backs. A skilled seam-runner will crouch motionless beside a warming shelf just outside a moving beam of light, bone-tipped lance resting across their knees, waiting for the floor to twitch and the panicked burrowers to break out of the swelling crust. A single fat warm-back yields enough tallow to light a dome for a month and keep four travelers fed on the march.

And the seam-runners watch the ice overhead for the rarest gift the hollow knows: *ice-drop*.

There is no rock beneath their boots, no ore, no clay, no gravel anywhere in the reachable basin. Yet their guy-lines are pinned with cold-worked copper, their skinning-knives are edged with hammered meteoric iron or splintered flint, and their red dye holds the unmistakable bite of rust. All of it falls from the sky.

Three or four kilometers overhead, locked inside the crushing weight of the glacial ceiling, ride the scraps of an unseen upper world—boulders scraped from vanished mountains, pockets of iron-stained moraine silt, nodules of native copper and quartz dragged along by ancient ice. Once in a great while, a deep ceiling rift melts just enough to loose its grip on one of those prisoners.

You hear it before you see it: a thin, descending whistle that does not belong to the muffled hush of the hollow, swelling in three seconds to a sharp, tearing scream, followed by a gunshot *crack* as a stone the size of a fist or a loaf of bread punches straight through the waxy floor like a cannonball, vanishing ten feet down into the living green pith in a puff of frost and splintered horn.

A fall like that is an event remembered for a decade. Whoever hears it marks the angle, watches for the pale bruise of shattered plates, and runs for it before the plant heals over the hole. Digging out a fallen stone is frantic, bloody work with bone wedges and pith-saws, racing against the plant’s own swelling callus and the creeping shift of the light. Most drops are useless grey granite or cracked shale, prized only as whetstones or boiling-rocks. Once in a hundred falls, a lump of raw, dull-green copper or a streak of red ochre-clay comes down out of the glacier. A fist-sized nugget of copper, pounded flat and cold-worked between two older fallen stones, will trade for three winter sledges, twenty cured pelts, and a season’s worth of salted cambium. The people have no clear memory of the surface world—only a quiet, unsentimental awareness of the *heavy place* pressing down on the ice above them, a dead world of stone and dark dirt that occasionally drops its bones into their bright, living bowl.

***

If you are fortunate—and if you remember to stop two hundred paces off, lower your staff, and show your empty palms so you are not mistaken for a shadow or a clamberer—you may be invited inside a dome during the noon glare.

Ducking through the low, double-curtained flap of hide is like stepping into another planet. The white blindness cuts off instantly. For a full minute you can see nothing at all, only floating purple afterimages swimming across your retinas as your eyes relax for the first time in hours. Then the small, steady orange flame of a burrower-fat lamp resolves in the center of the cramped circle, set in a hollowed stone cup—a prized heirloom from some forgotten ice-drop.

The air inside is startlingly close and rich after the razor-dry void outside. It smells of melted tallow, warm wool, dried turnip-sweet cambium, sour sap-ferment, and human sweat. The curved walls of bent white ribs and dark overlapping bark creak gently as the living floor beneath shifts a hair’s breadth, reminding you that the dome is lashed to something that is awake.

Sitting cross-legged on layered mats of dried, braided fronds, the people push their goggles up onto their foreheads and pass you a horn cup of warmed sap-water—faintly sweet, sharp with pine, steaming softly. The talk begins as it always begins when two travelers meet: not with names, which are small and private things here, but with the state of the floor.

*The east shelf past the Three-Split tilted ten fingers since the last dim,* a woman will say softly, scraping frost from a copper needle with her thumbnail. *The long rift widened above the Amber Well. Its knife cuts the lower stair now by midday.*

*We saw it,* the visitor answers, warming stiff fingers over the little flame. *The stair went clean yesterday. Lower half hanging over the drop. No use mending it; the whole lip is drinking fast.*

They trade maps not on paper, which would be obsolete before the ink dried, but in gestures—hands tilted to show the cant of a ridge, fingers tracing the slow arc of a crawling light-shaft across the air between them, voices dropping to a murmur when they speak of the deep, hollow groans that have been rolling through the northern ceiling for the past three days.

They do not mourn the severed staircase, any more than a sailor mourns a wake that closes behind a boat. Everything they make is built with its own surrender braided into the knots. Their shelters unlash in twenty minutes. Their drying-racks knock down into six straight poles. Even their graves are borrowed.

When someone dies in the hollow, you cannot bury them in earth there is none of, and fuel is far too scarce to burn a body to ash. Instead, the knot carries their dead out during the dim, wrapped tightly in old white pelts stitched shut with phloem-cord, and seeks a *closing seam*—one of the deep, narrow fissures where two massive plates have recently buckled apart and are now slowly swelling back together under the pressure of new growth. They lower the wrapped body into the bruised, cold-scented cleft, wedge a single small copper peg or a strip of red cloth beside the shoulder, and leave. Within a month, the pale shoots and waxy calluses bridge the gap and seal it smooth.

Years later—sometimes generations later—a beam of light will shear away a cliff face kilometers down-slope, and a seam-runner scanning the freshly broken wall from across a chasm will spot a dark bundle embedded high in the translucent green timber, preserved perfectly in cold resin and frozen sap, jutting briefly into the blinding glare before the next collapse carries it away into the unseen depths.

***

No one knows what lives at the very bottom of the hollow, two kilometers down where the light never quite reaches and every fallen shelf eventually arrives.

The people rarely speak of it, except as the *Soft*, or the *Warm Dark*. You can lean over the sheer lip of a terrace during a calm dim and peer down until your stomach turns over, seeing only layers of pale mist thickening to a bruised, impenetrable grey-green miles below. Yet the abyss is not dead. Every few days, when the air pressure shifts under a groan of the overhead glacier, a slow, strange updraft rises from the center of the void—warmer than the air above, damp, and heavy with an alien, cellar-sweet stench of wet compost, vinegar, and ancient rot.

Sometimes that rising breath carries messengers.

Out of the grey depths drift pale, heavy-bodied moths as wide as a child’s hand, their wings soft and damp, entirely blind, patterned in muddy purples and ochre. They are creatures of the crushing, steaming leaf-litter far below, where centuries of shattered fronds mulch down into a dark, sunless heat that nothing on the bright terraces could endure. Caught by accident in an upward eddy, they rise helplessly out of their dark world into the surgical white glare of the upper basin. The freezing air takes them within seconds. Their damp wings flash into brittle white glass; their bodies lock solid; they tumble down onto the waxy terraces like strange, ornate snowflakes, shattering when they hit the frost.

The knuckle-keepers’ children gather them when they can, slipping the frozen, translucent wings between plates of clear ice-rind to hold them up against the lamp-light inside the dark domes, marveling at colors that do not belong to the glare.

Then the dim settles in again. The blinding white ceiling cools to indigo. Across the vast gulf, the distant walls fall entirely quiet, their sweating faces sealing shut under a fresh skin of rime. Outside a lone dome tucked into an ancient heartwood knuckle, a figure in white fur steps out into the blue chill, lifts their goggles for a rare, brief moment to look up at the miles of fractured ice overhead, and feels through the soles of their boots the slow, steady pulse of the living ground freezing hard enough to walk on.

Tomorrow, the light-blades will return, and the cliff a mile east will blister and let go. For tonight, the floor holds. The traveler unlashes the rust-red rag from the tall pole, folds it carefully inside their coat, and begins to pull the copper pegs before the ground decides to keep them.
