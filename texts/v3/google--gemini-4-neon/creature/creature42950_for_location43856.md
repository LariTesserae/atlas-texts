You do not know they are there until the static goes slack.

It happens in the thick middle country, half a wind-tide out from the nearest engine-tower, where the grounding-trunks stand close enough to keep the damp hair on your wrists lifted in a steady prickle. One moment the air around you tastes sharp and tinny, buzzing faintly against your damp collar; the next, a broad pocket of charge simply drains away three paces to your left, as though a hidden root has grounded itself in midair.

Then comes the smell—and it belongs to neither the jungle nor the iron. Beneath the perpetual rot of spongy mats and the metallic tang of wet foliage, you catch a warm, layered scent: boiled tuber-tallow rubbed thick with crushed copper-bark, the sweet-sour musk of living hide, and, unmistakable beneath it all, a thin smear of scorched mineral grease stolen from a weeping flange.

No silence falls around it. The multi-limbed climbers overhead do not freeze; the raspers keep scraping the pleated fronds. Whatever is standing in the wet understory beside you is not an ambush.

Then it speaks to the room.

*Tock—tock.* Two dry, measured knocks, hard bone against a wet limestone rib, followed immediately by a soft, double-pitched exhalation—not a roar, not a hiss, but two distinct tones braided together like breath across paired reeds. You feel the sound brush your face and bounce back off your chest, your sodden clothes, the dense hollow of your ribcage. It is measuring you. A heartbeat later, a small, wet click snaps from the exact same spot, sharper and higher, testing the edge of your shoulder, the angle of your chin, the strange, clumsy stillness of your boots on the rock.

If you stay still and hold out an open palm—wet, unarmed, palm-up so the rain drums plainly into it—you hear the faint squelch of broad, springy weight shifting through the root-mat. A wave of heat reaches your knuckles before skin does. They run hotter than the plateau air, a steady, furnace-like warmth radiating through the damp.

Then fingers touch yours—not timidly, not roughly, with the brisk, thorough curiosity of a craftsman inspecting an unfamiliar stone.

The hand is wider than your own, leathery and strangely supple, with three thick, blunt-tipped fingers and two opposing thumbs—one set high on each side of the palm. Its inner skin is deeply grooved in concentric whorls, slick with tallow-salve and faintly warm. As those ridged pads sweep across your wrist and knuckles, you feel a faint, rapid tremor in them: they are listening through their palms, reading the beat of your pulse, the calluses on your joints, the unfamiliar weight and weave of your wet cloth.

They are stooped and compact, built for the three-dimensional tangle of slick limestone and hanging root-curtains rather than open flats. Their shoulders are broad and densely muscled, tapering to a deep, barrelled chest that rises and falls with a slow, heavy rhythm. Below the throat, tucked along the sternum, lies a pleated, supple sac of cartilage and skin—a living barometer that swells taut as a drumhead when the sky’s weight climbs and softens to a warm, loose fold during the dripping lulls. Their legs are shorter than their long, double-jointed arms, bowed and powerful, ending in wide, flat feet padded with thick, grippy cartilage that holds wet river-glass stone without slipping. At each heel juts a blunt, dense spur of horn, polished smooth from a lifetime of wedging into cliff-seams and rapping bare bedrock to listen to what rings beneath.

If one trusts you enough to let you lay a flat hand against its back or brow, you understand at once how completely they belong to this world.

There is no face as you know a face. Where eyes would sit, the front of the skull rises into a smooth, rounded dome of thick, warm bone—slightly yielding to pressure beneath the slick hide, filled with dense, resonant fluid. When they click or hum, you feel that entire domed brow vibrate under your palm like the belly of a struck gourd, focusing their voice into narrow beams of sound and gathering the returning echoes off every raindrop, leaf-vein, and cliff-rib within a hundred paces. Beneath the dome sit twin vertical nasal slits that flare and seal with every breath, continuously tasting the wet chemistry of the wind, and a wide, lipless mouth lined with hard cartilaginous ridges. Behind the jaw, no outer ears protrude to snag on hanging vines; instead, recessed drumheads sit beneath heavy, hinged flaps of leathery cartilage that snap shut automatically before a steam-valve cracks open or a static front discharges across the canopy.

And along the back of the skull, down the ridged spine, and fringing the outer edges of the long forearms grow dense crests of stiff, damp bristles. Brush them and they feel slick as oiled graphite, heavy with absorbed mineral salts. They are never still. Even as the creature stands before you, the crest along its forearms leans gently away from the nearest grounding-trunk and fans outward toward you, drinking the faint static field of your own body, reading the invisible architecture of charge that hangs in every cubic yard of the highland air.

They have no single name for themselves that can be spoken in one voice. A person’s name is a short phrase of sound—a click, a hummed interval, a rhythm tapped against the breastbone—and the name of a band is a chord that takes four or five throats to hold at once.

They do not build towns. The plateau is too vast, the acidic rain too patient, the wet root-mats too hungry to let walls stand for long. Instead, they live in tight, wandering choirs of fifteen or twenty, moving along the ancient ribs of the highlands in long loops that match the turning of the planetary weather.

You might think a traveler could lose themselves forever in thousands of leagues of unmarked, rain-hammered jungle. They never do. Every child among them learns to walk the buried lines before their forearm-bristles have even sprouted.

Watch—no, *listen*—as a band travels across the wet tableland. They move in a loose, undulating chain through the crowded understory, never more than two arm-lengths apart, one hand trailing a wet aerial root or resting lightly on the warm, humming flank of the walker ahead. Every twenty paces, the lead walker pauses, plants a heeled foot squarely against a patch of exposed bedrock or a taut, warm root-web, and goes utterly still for three heartbeats. Up through the heel-spur, through the dense, mineral-heavy shinbone, straight into the resonant dome of the skull comes the faint, stubborn vibration buried forty feet beneath the mud: the hum of a continental trunk-line carrying its unseen cargo toward an unimaginable terminus.

To them, the buried conduits are the bones of the world, and the solitary engine-towers bolted to the cliff edges are its hearts.

They do not worship the towers, nor do they fear them. A tower is simply older than any memory-song—older than the limestone ledges, older than the canyon-chords—and infinitely useful, like a hot spring or a reliable updraft. Every tower across five hundred leagues has its own acoustic signature, and every adult carries a sung map of dozens of them in their chest: the one whose flywheel bearing whines a flat third above the canyon wind; the one perched on a double-fluted escarpment where its ninety-second steam-blast makes the whole gorge bellow like an injured grazer; the rusted giant forty wind-tides west whose reciprocating piston stutters half a beat every seventh stroke. When two bands meet in the dripping lull after a surge, they do not exchange greetings first; they sit knee to knee on a rounded stone rib, press their grooved palms to one another’s collarbones, and sing the current state of the towers they have passed—whose bearings are running hotter, whose trunk-line has dropped in pitch, which cableway across a mist-chasm has snapped a humming strand.

Yet they never make their homes right against the riveted iron. A tower is too fierce a neighbor. Linger too many barometric tides within its hot radius and the relentless static dries out your breathing-slits, makes your bristles brittle, and dulls your sense of the quieter weather beyond. The scalding exhaust blisters the soft-skinned young; the armored grazers and blind web-hunters crowd too thick around the warm heat-exchangers.

So they visit the towers the way a coastal folk visits a tidal reef—arriving with woven baskets, ceramic scrapers, and bone chisels, working cleanly and quickly within the machine’s blind rhythms, and retreating to the cooler, quieter country a mile or two back when their packs are heavy.

There is no fire anywhere on the plateau. The air is too wet, the wood too sodden and mineral-choked, and flame belongs to a physics the highland does not trade in. Yet the folk work horn, seasoned reed, thick hide, and scavenged metal with astonishing subtlety—because the towers give them heat, acid, and pressure.

Stand thirty paces off from an engine-tower during a steady mist, and you may smell tuber-tallow mixing suddenly with the harsh reek of hot iron and ozone. Listen between the four-second sledgehammer blows of the pistons—*Thump—chug—hiss*—and you will catch a fainter, brisker tapping: bone hammers cold-shaping a fallen sliver of bronze-alloy against a flat iron bolt-flange, or ceramic blades carefully slicing a weeping ribbon of vulcanized rubber off a cracked gasket.

Nothing is prized more than rubber and ceramic. In a highland saturated with silent, skin-pricking voltage, insulators are wealth and armor. A worker wrapping their forearms in scavenged rubber strips can press straight up against a humming copper collector-mast during a rising surge without being thrown backward by the charge. Strands of broken ceramic insulators, drilled patiently with grit and wet bone drills, are braided into the cords slung across their shoulders—both to dampen stray static and to clink softly as they move, letting every member of the band track their neighbors’ stride and load through the densest rain.

And then there are the steam-benders.

They are usually the lithest among the band—slick from brow to heel with a thick coat of tallow and stolen bearing-grease, their ear-flaps latched tight, their wrists bound in double layers of vulcanized rubber. One will crouch directly on the riveted iron lip beneath a pressure-valve, heel-spurs wedged into dripstone seams, holding a bound bundle of soaked reed-staves or green grazer-horn out into the dark air on a long bone tong. They do not count seconds the way you would. They count the piston strokes trembling straight up through their shins: twenty, twenty-one, twenty-two. At the top of the twenty-second stroke, they lean their domed head sharply back behind an iron baffle and press their palms flat against the hot casing.

*CRACK.* The weighted valve snaps open. The scalding plume roars out into the canopy, thrashing nearby fronds and sending flocks of clicking gliders scattering in frantic curtains of sonar. For four ferocious heartbeats, the steam hammers the held bundle, softening the horn and opening the pores of the reed until it smells sharply of wet hair and boiled sap. The instant the valve slams shut, the bender pulls the blistering bundle down into the cool rain, twisting, bending, and lashing it into curved harvest-hooks, resonant horn-flutes, or sprung basket-ribs before it cools stiff.

While they work, a small, heavy-shelled maintenance mechanism may clatter out through its iron flap, smelling sharply of cold solder and oil, tapping its articulated legs along the weeping flange toward a loose seam. The folk neither flee it nor strike it. They simply lift their knees out of its path and click softly at it in amused, respectful cadence—the same gentle throat-ticks they use for a stubborn elder who has grown hard of hearing. Sometimes a young bender will scrape a dense crust of shelled raspers off a bolt-head just ahead of the mechanism’s tapping feelers, or pull a stubborn heat-loving tendril clear of an inspection hatch. They know a well-tended tower keeps the whole valley warm, rhythmic, and predictable. A broken tower goes cold and quiet, and silence in the highland draws things nobody wants near their sleeping-hollows.

When the work is done and their netted satchels bulge with raw iron-oxide salt, hot grease, rubber strips, and buckets of metallic tubers steamed tender over the exhaust-grates, the band slips back out into the wet green dark, returning to their roost.

You would walk right past a roost and never know a living soul was ten feet above your head—until you noticed the harmony.

They choose high, overhanging ribs of rounded limestone, tucked halfway down the rim of a perforated gorge where the endless warm updrafts rise from the unseen ocean below. Beneath the stone lip, out of the direct hammer of the rain, they sling deep, wide hammocks woven from braided aerial roots and roofed with stitched, overlapping fronds that shed the constant drips smoothly out over the drop. Every line anchored to the rock is taut as a bowstring. Around the perimeter of the hollow, stretching twenty yards out into the tangled canopy and down the nearby grounding-trunks, they run hair-thin filaments of stretched sinew, each one knotted back to a cluster of hollow ceramic shards and dried rasper-shells hanging beside the mouth of the roost.

At all times, one member of the band sits cross-legged at the threshold, forearms resting loosely on their knees, ten wide-padded fingers laid lightly across the taut lines. Through those strings, the sentinel reads the entire hillside: the steady, heavy drumming of rain on pleated leaves; the tiny, rhythmic shudder of a climber crossing a vine; the fat, drifting brush of a rising spore-sac; and—if it comes—the sudden, taut drag of a slender web-hunter stepping onto a root-line, followed by that telltale pocket of freezing quiet rolling through the dark.

At a single sharp double-knock of the sentinel’s heel against the limestone floor, the whole roost wakes in silence. No one shouts into the air. Instead, they speak in bone-speech: pressing their smooth brows briefly to a neighbor’s shoulder, laying warm palms flat against one another’s ribs, sending low, vibrating instructions straight from skeleton to skeleton while bronze-tipped striking staves are lifted silently from their hooks.

Yet it is not the trip-wires that give a roost its soul. It is the wall.

Wherever they settle for a season of tides, the folk seek out a limestone rib already honeycombed with bellow-colonies—those bloated, muscular sacs wedged permanently into the weeping rock crevices. Wild colonies groan in deep, drifting swells that answer the canyons at random. The highland folk prune, feed, and tune them.

They wedge carved bone-baffles and notched copper tubes into the stone flutes beside the fleshy sacs, altering the shape of the air-channels until the colony’s booming exhale locks into a clean, steady chord. They feed the sacs crushed sweet-spores to keep them plump and vigorous. In return, the tuned wall becomes the hearth and clock of the roost. Its warm, muscular bulk radiates a steady, damp heat into the rock. Better still, the sacs are far more sensitive to the sky’s weight than even a person’s own chest-barometer. Six hours before a great barometric surge rolls in off the unseen sea—long before the rain thickens or the canyon-chords drop—the tuned bellows shift by a quarter-tone, turning their steady lullaby drone into a slow, throbbing beat that hums straight through the woven hammock-ropes and wakes every sleeper in the hollow.

That is when you hear the roost at its richest: in the dense, feverishly close hours before a surge, or in the long, dripping lull after one has passed.

They gather under the limestone overhang, slicking fresh tallow into one another’s grooved hides, cracking open sweet, salty rasper-shells with their elbows, and passing around waxed spore-bladders filled with fermented grounding-trunk sap. The drink burns sharp on the tongue—copper, bitter salt, and a bright, prickly fizz that makes the drinker’s dorsal bristles lift and shiver with phantom currents.

As the drink warms them, they talk—and to an outsider standing down on the wet ledge below, it sounds at first as though the stone itself has begun to dream.

Their air-speech is a layered marvel, forged to live inside a world that never stops roaring. Because each throat holds paired cartilaginous reeds, a single speaker carries both a low, resonant drone that slips cleanly beneath the hiss of the rain and a weaving upper line of dry tongue-clicks, wet pops, and clear, whistling pitches. They do not wait neatly for one another to finish. Three or four voices braid together at once, answering, disagreeing, teasing, and telling stories in shifting intervals, using the boom of the tuned bellow-wall as their bass-line and the distant four-second *thump—chug—hiss* of the nearest engine-tower as their measure.

They tell of the great cliff-harvests, when the boldest among them lash braided root-harnesses around their hips and lean far out over the dizzying, bottomless abyss, listening through the howling updraft for the soft, hollow *whuff* of giant spore-sacs rising miles up from the unseen ocean, sweeping wide, slack nets through the rushing mist to snatch them before the leathery gliders can dive. They argue passionately over the taste of different marshes—which terraced basin grows the sweetest, least caustic metallic tubers, which armored grazer-herd has grown aggressive since the last surge. They mimic the clumsy, rigid gait of the maintenance mechanisms for the delight of the young, clicking their heels in stiff mechanical cadence until the whole hollow shakes with a wheezing, rhythmic barking—their laughter, felt as much in the trembling hammock-ropes as heard in the air.

And the young—born soft-skinned, small-domed, and entirely bristleless—spend their first seasons slung tightly against a parent’s warm, grooved back, learning the geography of the plateau through the vibration of their carrier’s spine. Only when their acoustic brow hardens and their first damp fringe of carbon-rich bristles pushes through along their forearms are they set down on bare, wet limestone for the first time. When that happens, the whole choir draws in a breath and holds utterly still for three full heartbeats—even the sentinel lifting their fingers from the strings—so the child can strike its own small heel against the rock and hear, unobstructed by any adult voice, the true shape of its own echo coming back off the cliff.

They age slowly, and age changes their bodies in a way the band treasures. Over decades of drinking mineral-sharp sap, eating copper-rich tubers, and soaking in the slightly acidic rain, the bones of an elder grow extraordinarily dense—heavy with deposited iron and calcium salts, ringing faintly duller and deeper than a youth’s when tapped. Their joints stiffen in the endless damp, and they can no longer hang out over the cliff edges or crouch beneath scalding steam-valves. Instead, they become the long-listeners.

Because their mineral-heavy skeletons conduct vibration and static far better than young bone, an elder can lie flat on a bare rib of bedrock during a quiet lull, press their smooth brow and broad palms flush to the weeping stone, and read distances that leave the young bewildered. They can hear a buried trunk-line surge fifty miles away before the local tower’s relays even clack to adjust load. They can feel the faint, sickened shudder of a failing bearing two canyons over. They know, by the subtle souring of the mist’s chemistry and the tiny tilt of their stiff white-crusted bristles, whether an incoming barometric tide will bring a gentle soak or a canyon-scouring torrent.

And when an elder’s heat finally begins to ebb—when their barometric sac no longer tightens smoothly with the sky, and their forearm-bristles lie permanently flat against their slick skin—the band does not bury them in the hungry, root-choked mud, nor cast them into the bottomless updraft without mark.

Instead, the finest reed-carver and steam-bender in the choir sit beside the dying one for a full wind-tide, holding palms to their chest, memorizing the exact double-toned interval of their resting hum. From a thick length of seasoned hollow reed—or, for a beloved long-listener, a prized pipe of scavenged copper—they drill, notch, and steam-shape a wind-flute tuned to that exact chord.

When the elder’s warmth has gone altogether, the body is given over to the roaring warm updrafts off a high escarpment, returned to the vast, unseen below that breathes life up onto the plateau. Then the band climbs along the sheer, perforated rock lip beside the roost—a gallery worn slick by hundreds of generations of broad, gripping feet—and wedges the newly carved pipe firmly into a weeping cliff-vent facing the endless wind.

Walk along that ledge with your hand trailing the rounded stone, and you pass through a dense, humming forest of the dead. Hundreds of weathered tubes bristle from the cliff-pores: ancient reeds long since petrified by mineral dripstone, older copper pipes green with salty crust and half-choked with moss, fresh lengths of smooth, warm horn still smelling faintly of tallow and steam. As the great warm air rivers climb out of the abyss and rush across the perforated stone, every tube drinks the wind and gives back the voice of someone who once walked the buried lines. Some are clear and piercing; some have cracked over centuries into a soft, breathy wheeze; some sing only when the barometric wind doubles during a storm. Together, they never stop—weaving the dead directly into the permanent chord of the canyons, so that no one who lived here ever truly falls silent.

Sometimes—not often, perhaps once in a generation—a walker among them grows restless with their own valley’s music.

Perhaps their bristles itch with a strange, unquiet hunger, or perhaps they lay their brow against a buried trunk-line one lull and become possessed by the slow, steady pulse travelling infinitely away beneath the roots. They pack a light net of dried tubers, a full bladder of copper-sap, a coil of rubber strips, and a single notched chime-rod bearing the chord of their home choir. They press their brow one last time to every rib and shoulder in the hollow. And then they step out past the familiar canyon-chords, planting their heels on the humming vein beneath the mud, and begin to walk.

They walk for twenty wind-tides, fifty, a hundred—following the buried line from tower to tower across thousands of leagues of wet, blind tableland. They trade news, rare ceramic beads, and unfamiliar steam-bending knots with distant bands whose speech-clicks have drifted into strange accents over centuries of isolation. They learn the names of towers their home choir has never heard of, bolted to cliffs above canyons deeper and hotter than any they knew. None of them has ever reached the far terminus where the continental trunk-lines end—or if one did, they never walked back to sing what they found. Yet the bands along the lines listen eagerly for the solitary, steady *tock—tock* of a lone traveler’s bone staff tapping wet stone in the dark, welcoming them into the warmth of the bellow-wall to taste the dust of unimaginably distant iron on their skin.

Then the next great barometric surge begins to build over the unseen ocean far below, and everything on the plateau turns to face it.

Hours before the storm arrives, the tuned bellow-wall drops half an octave and begins to throb. Across the roost, inner ears pop; chests tighten as the air grows thick, hot, and suffocatingly heavy. The sentinel draws in the sinew-lines before the wind can snap them. Young ones are tucked deep into the high, dry pockets of the limestone overhang, pressed cheek-to-chest against the heavy, mineral-dense warmth of the elders.

Outside, the upward wind doubles until the hollow reeds shriek and the gallery of dead voices along the cliff swells into a vast, chest-rattling chord. Warm rain hits the pleated canopy like a solid wall of gravel. Half a mile away, the nearest engine-tower senses the climbing atmospheric charge and spins up—its hidden flywheel rising from a greased whine to a ferocious howl, its reciprocating pistons hammering twice as fast, its buried trunk-line thrumming so hard beneath the roots that you can feel it rattling your back teeth.

And the folk do not hide from it.

Out along the sheltered lip of the overhang, wedging their strong heel-spurs firmly into the slick stone seams and locking elbows together in a braced, swaying row, the choir leans straight into the wet, roaring dark. Their pleated chest-sacs swell full with the heavy, saturated air. Along their necks, spines, and long forearms, every mineral-heavy bristle lifts and fans wide, drinking the fierce, silent voltage pouring off the storm-front until their whole bodies hum with a harmless, intoxicating prickle and the air around their shoulders tastes wildly of rain on hot copper.

Then, as the distant towers howl in chorus across hundreds of unseen miles and the canyons boom beneath them, they open their ridged mouths and sing.

It is not a hymn of supplication, nor a cry of fear. It is a huge, layered, double-toned roar—sharp bone-clicks cutting cleanly through the hammering rain, deep chest-drones locking right into the rhythmic gaps between the engine-tower’s pounding pistons and the furious bellowing of the limestone cliffs. Warm, slick, heavy, and alive, they weave themselves straight into the rising voltage and the roaring wind, answering the blind machines and the endless boiling ocean below with a voice of their own—utterly at home in a world that has never once needed a sun to know where it is.
