What lives in the basin does not belong to a single world. That is the first thing you notice once your eyes adjust to the chalky glare and your ears stop trying to sort the noise into sense: the pit is a collision. When the limestone crust gave way and dragged four hundred feet of the plain down with it, it did not merely open a hole in the dry upper flats—it tore the roof off a warm, drowned labyrinth of deep karst caverns and thermal vents that had never known daylight. Everything now crowding the floor is either a refugee pushed up from the dark beneath, a scavenger that poured in over the rim from the hungry scrub above, or a human being stubborn enough to climb down between the two.

You can see the seam between those worlds most clearly in the deepest, warmest hollows along the center of the floor, where the air carries that sweetish mineral tang that sets human molars humming.

Down there, half-folded into the blue-grey clay, live the largest things in the basin—creatures the rim-workers call *slump-backs*, though from fifty paces away they look like nothing more than low, rounded ridges of freshly heaved mud. They are broad, flat-bodied amphibians—or something older and stranger that never bothered to decide between lungfish and salamander—eight to twelve feet from snout to blunt tail, wide across the ribs as a skin-boat. They came up through the cracked fissures when the subterranean aquifers ruptured, and daylight is an insult to them. Their hides are naturally the pale, bluish pink of raw lard, slick with a thick, bitter mucus that keeps sharp shale from slicing them open; left bare in the midday glare, they blister in an hour. So they wallow deliberately in the wet clay until they wear a crust of the basin floor two inches thick, leaving only a pair of wide, fluttering nostrils and the faint twitch of translucent sensory filaments along their flanks to betray them.

They are nearly blind—their eyes are milky crescents tucked under thick, lidless skin—and they hunt by listening through their bellies. When a clay ridge cracks open or a bank of burrowers surges up to graze a yellow bacterial film, a mound of mud twenty feet away will suddenly shudder, heave forward on short, paddle-footed legs with surprising, fluid speed, and open a mouth as wide as a wicker basket. It scoops up gallons of wet silt, clicking burrowers, and squabbling scavengers all at once, snaps its blunt jaws shut, and forces the slurry back out through heavy, fringed gill-slits behind its head, swallowing whatever stays tangled behind its horny plates.

The workers who venture near the central seam have a rule they teach on a child’s first descent: never step onto a smooth clay hump that has no breathing-holes dimpling its skin. The fist-sized burrowers will tunnel into almost anything damp, even the mortar of their own dead chimneys, but they will not pierce the clay crust on a sleeping slump-back. Even the leather-winged gliders know them; at midday, you can sometimes spot three or four of the dark, oily-winged fliers perched motionless along a half-buried ridge of clay, letting the warmth rising from the hidden body dry their membranes while they snap at the papery flyers drawn to its breath.

Underneath the overturned slabs and in the wet, splintered shale around the warm seeps live quieter castaways from the dark below: hand-long, translucent crevice-crawlers with whip-thin antennae twice the length of their bodies, fleeing in frantic, glassy ripples whenever a rock shifts and lets the sun in; and clusters of pale, soft-bodied tube-worms that bloom overnight in any warm pool that manages to survive more than six hours, waving tiny rust-red plumes in the mineral water until a fissure opens beneath them and leaves them as shriveled grey threads in the mud.

Pressing in from above are the opportunists of the open sky, and none of them have adapted more shamelessly than the pale-feathered cliff-hunters. From the floor they look clean and swift, wheeling in white arcs against the glare, but up close on their nesting ledges along the sheared gypsum walls they are heavy-shouldered, foul-tempered bullies the size of geese, with thick, chalk-caked ruffs, hooked slate beaks, and scaly yellow toes scarred from wrestling claw-scavengers. Their rookeries are messy shelves of droppings, stripped wing-ribs, unbroken pearly carapace plates, and whatever bright or notched junk they have pilfered from the people below—bits of sinew, lost bone route-markers, even a flint scraper wedged into a nest rim.

They have learned the sound of hammered copper. When a human crew pries at a newly cracked seam on the flats, two or three cliff-hunters will drop out of the updraft and settle twenty paces away on the talus, wings half-cocked, watching with pale, unblinking eyes. The moment a slab rolls and exposes a knot of fat, blind mud-grubs, the birds rush in with a dry, rattling hiss, snatching the grubs almost out from beneath the workers’ pry-bars. The people rarely kill them—their meat tastes sharply of sulfur and iron—and instead toss them crushed burrowers to keep the heavy beaks away from their baskets and their faces.

The sleek, leather-winged gliders keep a cooler distance. They are thermal-riders, narrow-bodied and almost weightless, with dark, soot-colored wings that soak up the morning heat so quickly they are aloft an hour before the air even warms enough for the cliff-hunters to launch. They have small, hooked thumbs midway along their wing-edges, and when the glare is fiercest or the wind turns erratic, they vanish into the narrow vertical fissures just below the northern rim, hanging packed shoulder-to-shoulder like damp leather pouches. Anyone riding the hemp line past those cracks hears them rustling and clicking softly in the shade, smelling faintly of musk and crushed fly-wings.

Down on the floor itself, the true proprietors are the squat, bristled scavengers. Every loose boulder hides three or four; every shadow behind a gypsum slab holds a pair of stalked eyes and one oversized crushing claw lifted in perpetual irritation. They are the reason nothing in the basin stays intact for an hour. They have no fear of noise, heat, or vibration—only of the slump-backs’ wide jaws and the sudden, whistling drop of a cliff-hunter—and they will test anything that ceases to move. Drop a hide strap, leave a basket resting on the clay, fall asleep with your heel pressed against the oiled wall of a lean-to, and within ten minutes you will hear the dry, insistent *tick-tick-tick* of their smaller pincers probing the leather, trying to decide whether you have finally become food.

And yet, woven right through this deafening, jostling hunger—never ruling it, never safe from it, just fast enough and stubborn enough to snatch a living from its teeth—are the people.

They do not live on the floor; no one sane tries to raise a roof where the earth refuses to keep the same shape from Tuesday to Thursday. They live along the wind-scoured lip of the northern and western rims, in low, hunched clusters of dry-laid limestone huts whose hide roofs are lashed down with heavy stones against the flat plain’s wind. Up on the rim, the world is dry, flinty, and stingy—thin gray scrub, harsh wind, water hauled from distant sink-wells. Down in the four-hundred-foot bowl there is no grass and no peace, but there is endless animal fat, tough pearly shell that can be boiled soft and stitched into armor or traded for grain across the plains, and—most precious of all—the heavy, dark mineral nodules that the central seam squeezes up from the deep strata, dense with native copper and iron-rich ore that can be hammered cold or sweated out in a dung-fed hearth.

Three generations ago, the oldest grandmothers say, the plain was unbroken here until a dry summer when the ground groaned for twelve days and fell away in dust. Their fathers came to the edge thinking the earth had opened a mouth to swallow them; by the next spring, someone had braided a rope long enough to go down and see what was shining in the wet shale. Now the basin has shaped the people who work it as thoroughly as it has shaped the birds.

Up around the northern timber derrick, the camp runs on watchfulness. Timber is scarce on the limestone flats—the derrick’s thick beam was dragged twenty miles by ox-team and patched three times with spliced ribs and rawhide lashings—and the people who tend it treat the rope the way sailors treat a hull. The winch-hands are usually the older ones whose knees have given out on the wet clay, or workers nursing a bruised rib or a fresh gypsum burn. You can tell a retired pit-runner even in the dark by the dry, chalky cough that rattles in their chest from years of limestone dust, and by the faint, milky haze at the corners of their eyes from the shadowless glare. They spend their days greasing the braided hemp line with rendered mud-grub tallow so the mineral damp won’t rot it, boiling carapace plates in clay vats until they can be punched with bone awls, and roasting trays of dried papery flyers and pressed larvae into dense, dark travel-cakes that smell faintly of wet iron and keep all winter.

Beside the winch, bellies flat on the warm limestone lip with safety-ropes knotted around their waists, lie the watchers—often children between seven and twelve, whose eyes are sharpest and who know every wrinkle of the six-mile bowl before they are tall enough to lift a pry-bar. From four hundred feet straight up, the frantic confusion of the floor resolves into readable geometry. A child lying on the northern rim can spot the pale, greasy sheen where clay is about to heave half a mile away, or notice a cliff-wall turning suddenly bare as ten thousand flat-shelled grazers peel sideways off a loosening slab. Beside each watcher sit two wide paddles of hide stained dead-black with grease and soot, and a polished slab of pale selenite. Against the blinding glare of the rim sky, black reads clearer than bright color. A steady dip of the left paddle tells a crew far below that the cliff above them is about to shed; two paddles held wide and pumping slowly mean a fresh slump has split open on the eastern apron, exposing new mud and probably new nodules; paddles crossed low over the derrick beam mean the hide-wrapped basket is coming down to the ledge, and whoever wants their load hauled up had better be there before the rope hangs still too long.

Going down is an art of grease, leather, and listening.

The floor-crews work in tight teams of three or four—never alone, because a twisted ankle in a slumping clay hollow gives the bristled scavengers an invitation you cannot afford to answer. Before they step into the swaying hide basket, they smear their forearms, shins, throats, and cheeks with a thick yellow paste of grub-tallow and cold wood-ash. Without it, the iridescent bacterial slimes raise weeping red rashes by noon, and the fine airborne spray from the warm seeps dries into a salt-crust that splits the skin raw. Over their eyes they tie narrow visors carved from curved carapace plates, blackened on the inside with hearth-soot and pierced with two hair-thin horizontal slits; looking at a crew waiting on the lower ledge, with their marl-caked hides and pale, slit-eyed masks, they look less like visitors from the upper plain than another breed of armored crustacean that learned to walk upright.

Their footwear is thin, supple rawhide soaked in oil—never heavy soles, because thick leather clogs with twenty pounds of blue clay in fifty paces and blinds your feet to the ground. A good runner walks with knees slightly bent and weight balanced over the balls of their feet, stopping every few dozen yards just long enough to feel whether the low, constant tremor through the clay has changed its pitch.

At every worker’s hip hangs a small, perforated cylinder of clay-stiffened wicker, plugged at both ends with twisted grass and kept damp with a splash of seep-water. Inside, cushioned in wet mud, live two or three captive chalky burrowers. The workers feed them scraps of scraped bacterial film and replace them every few days when they grow sluggish, because no human ear can catch the first warning of a gas-vent or a sliding slab over the racket of the pit. You walk with one palm resting lightly on the wicker tube. So long as the creatures inside merely shift and murmur, the ground is keeping its temper. The instant they erupt into a frantic, dry clatter against the wicker right against your thigh, you do not look back for your copper wedge or your basket—you drop what is heavy and sprint sideways toward the nearest solid ridge.

Because shouting dies twenty paces from your mouth in the crowded, percussive din of the pit, the crews on the floor speak mostly with their hands and bones. Each carries a short whistle carved from the hollow wing-bone of a cliff-hunter, pitched piercingly high so a quick double-chirp slices clean above the static of rasping grazers and tumbling scree. Up close, they barely waste breath at all: a sharp tap of two knuckles against a partner’s collarbone means *look up at the rim*; a flat hand pressed briefly against the ribs means *hold still, slump-back ahead*; a tug at the elbow points toward a hairline seam where dark, heavy nodules have just been pushed up through the wet shale like plums through dough.

They work fast, greedily, and with a strange, jittery grace. While one runner jams notched bone markers into cracks to flag a dryish ridge back toward the western talus, another levers a fresh boulder aside with the dull hammered-copper bar, ignoring the cliff-hunters hopping impatiently at their heels, while the third scoops out the heavy dark stones and wipes the slick ochre slime from pearly molted shells before dropping them into clay-stiffened wicker baskets. Every ten minutes someone glances up at the tiny timber tooth of the derrick on the northern sky, checking the black paddles. Every half-hour they chew fresh knots of bitter juniper-root brought down from the flats, spitting dark streams into the grey mud to cut the sweet, metallic ache building behind their teeth.

When they rest, they do not rest well. At the three low lean-tos crouched against the least-unstable western talus—nothing driven into the earth, only lashed driftwood-pale ribs and oiled hides pinned down under heavy stacks of raw gypsum—everything of value hangs off the ground. Baskets dangle from the ridge-poles; water-bladders swing from sinew loops; even the copper pry-bars are tucked high into the roof-lashings, because an hour on the clay is enough for a scavenger to chew the leather grip away or for a slow heave of mud to swallow the tool whole. One person always stays awake beside the shallow, grease-blackened fire pit, feeding it hard cakes of dried dung and rendered fat lowered from the rim, tossing pebbles whenever a squat, bristled shadow creeps too close to the weighted hides. The others sleep in shallow, uneasy bouts, curled on folded hides with their wicker warning-tubes tucked inside their elbows, waking instantly if the clatter at their ribs speeds up or if the ground gives a wet, deeper groan.

Most crews endure three days below, maybe four, before the relentless vibration, the glare, the tooth-ache, and the sense of being climbed over by an impatient earth wear their reflexes thin. Then they haul their loaded baskets to the northern ledge, wait for the hide sling to drop from the derrick, and ride the spinning hemp line back into the clean, dry wind of the flats, where they sleep twelve hours straight and spend the next morning blinking at how weirdly quiet a motionless world can be.

Yet there are always a few who stop coming up on schedule.

Every rim-camp has two or three of them—usually solitary men or women who have worked the floor since they were teenagers, or someone whose hearing was taken early by a rockfall and who claims the silence of the upper plain makes their head ring. They stay down for two weeks, sometimes a whole moon, living out of the western lean-tos or curling in dry overhangs along the upper talus where the wind carries off the worst of the damp. The rim-folk lower them fresh water-bladders, salt, and bitter root in exchange for the richest hauls from the central seam—places ordinary crews will not linger for more than ten minutes—and watch them with an uneasy mixture of reliance and quiet grief.

The basin leaves its mark on those long-stayers deeper than grease and chalk. The blue-grey clay works permanently into the creases of their knuckles, the rims of their fingernails, the dry folds behind their ears, giving their skin a faint, slate-colored undertone that never scrubs completely clean. Their front teeth carry pale chalky bands from the sweet mineral air; they stop chewing juniper-root altogether, claiming that after six days in the deep hollows the ache dulls into a clean, warm buzz in the jaw that lets you taste when a fresh seam of ore-heavy rock is about to break the crust. Even when they do climb up to the rim for winter storms or family funerals, they rarely speak in full sentences anymore. They tap your forearm instead of calling your name, flinch when someone sets a basket directly on the dirt, and stare down at the floorboards while you talk, listening through their heels for a tremor that never comes. They crack pale burrowers raw between thumb and palm and eat them without breaking stride, and they walk across shifting blue clay with an eerie, drifting unhurriedness, stepping aside three seconds before a mud blister pops or a bank sloughs away—not checking their wicker tubes at all, as if the floor had finally begun talking directly to their bones.

And when the sun drops behind the four-hundred-foot western collar—cutting the basin abruptly into deep blue shadow two hours before dusk reaches the plain above—everyone who stays down there sees the pit turn over and show its other face.

With the glare extinguished, the rising heat falters and dies. Within twenty minutes, the vast tornadic curtains of papery flyers lose their lift and rain down onto the wet clay like damp ash. The cliff-hunters fold their pale wings into the chalky ledges, muttering softly among their stolen shells; the leather-winged gliders pack themselves tight into the upper fissures, a soft rustle of oily skin fading into silence.

Then the floor belongs entirely to what came up from beneath.

Freed from the burning sun, the great, blind slump-backs haul themselves completely out of the central hollows, shedding sheets of blue clay as they drag their broad, glistening pink-white bellies across the cooling flats, leaving wide, slick trails that look eerily like the tracks of wooden sledges. Out from beneath every tilted slab pour the bristled scavengers in dark, clicking rivers, harvesting the fallen flyers and testing every rope, stake, and weighted hide skirt in the dark. In the deepest, freshest cracks where warm iron-rich water still sweats out of the sheared shale, faint, cold films of pale sulfur-green luminescence flicker awake along the undisturbed bacterial mats—never bright enough to light a path, only a dim, ghostly outline tracing the edges of the newest wounds in the earth, winking out abruptly whenever a tremor shakes the mud and tears the living skin apart.

Down at the foot of the western talus, a single small grease-fire burns orange beside the three hunched lean-tos, barely holding back the dark while claws tick patiently against the stretched hide just beyond the coals. And four hundred feet straight above, tiny against the first cold stars of the upper plain, a lone tallow lantern hangs from the projecting timber of the northern derrick—swaying gently on the dry night wind, keeping watch over the dangling hemp line, waiting for the bright, deafening rush of morning to begin all over again.
