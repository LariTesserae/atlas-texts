At first, you think the courtyard is deserted because you are still looking for the quick, wet signs of occupancy—a damp gleam on an eye, the sudden jerk of a breath, the hurried clatter of heels across stone. Here, anyone who moves like that would knock clay urns from every ledge and tear the vellum canopies down around their ears. It takes a long while, standing still in the baker’s warmth, before your eyes separate the tenants from the masonry.

The first one you notice is sitting beneath a covered loggia not ten paces away, so still and closely toned to the limewash behind her that you mistook her shoulders for a pair of stacked amphorae draped in raw linen. Only when a shingle-lizard creeps off the plaster onto her collarbone—flattening itself seamlessly into the skin of her throat before gliding down beneath the surface like a dark brushstroke toward her wrist—do you realize you are looking at a person.

She turns her head slowly to regard you. There is no damp click of eyelids; thin lids of matte, chamois-like skin draw down and lift again with a faint papery whisper over eyes that resemble dark, oil-rubbed slate. They are entirely dry, yet unmistakably warm with attention. Her skin has the fine, powdery grain of unglazed bisqueware heated all afternoon in the sun, though where the linen sleeve falls back from her forearm, the curve of her ulna runs a little too straight and clean, showing the pale, tight grain of planed cedar before softening back into supple, warm flesh at the wrist. Her hair is pinned up in a dense coil the color of unspun flax, held in place by three hammered copper nails; from the head of the uppermost nail, a tiny wafer-thin blossom has sprouted, dusting her collar with ochre.

She does not startle at a stranger. She simply rests one dry palm flat against the bare brick beside her knee, and when she lifts it a minute later to smooth her apron, the fresh crescent-sweep of a wooden trowel remains pressed into the warm mortar where her hand had been.

Once you have seen one of them, the crowded alleys resolve into a quiet village.

They are everywhere, moving with the unhurried, shoulder-turned grace of people who have spent their whole lives threading between low lintels and brittle vines. They are lean, somewhat narrow through the ribs, dressed in layered wraps of stiff, unwashed flax, reed-woven aprons, and broad terracotta-dyed shawls whose hems have sometimes loosened into raw cane or dry fringe that catches gently on the flagstones. When they breathe, their chests do not heave; instead, there is a soft, pleated expansion beneath their linen—like a small leather bellows opening—and a faint exhale scented of warm chalk and toasted cedar shavings.

They are not statues animated by a trick, nor clockwork dolls. If you sit close to one on a stepped parapet, you feel the unmistakable, steady heat of a living body—only it is a dry heat, held in porous bone and hollow, reed-like vessels filled with warm air and fine ochre silt rather than blood. Their joints are fitted with exquisite, quiet precision. An older man mending a trellis nearby bends his elbow and you catch the clean silhouette of a wooden toggle beneath the taut parchment of his skin; when a ceramic-shelled beetle trundles up his bare instep and pauses, tapping its mandibles against his little toe, the man patiently reaches down, pops a worn bone pin from his own knuckle, and trades it for a smooth bead of kiln-fired clay the beetle unglues from its back. He presses the clay bead neatly into his joint, flexes his fingers twice to test the fit, and returns to his vines while the beetle ambles off wearing his old knuckle like a shield.

Because nothing here bleeds or rots, they do not know sickness the way wet creatures do. Instead, they loosen, or warp, or forget what material they are supposed to be. On warm afternoons, you will find neighbors sitting knee-to-knee in the narrow shaded courts, tending to one another’s seams with bone awls, strips of oiled vellum, and small saucers of dry resin-paste. If a woman’s heel has begun to harden back into raw terracotta after too many hours standing on the sunny tiles, a friend kneels beside her with a fine wooden rasp, gently dressing the edge until it sits flush and supple again, sweeping the pink clay-flour into a small bowl to save for later.

It is watching them at work that finally explains why no builder is ever seen, though the courtyard never stops raising new walls and balconies.

They do not haul hods of brick or swing iron hammers. Force is too abrupt for a place this tightly pleated; a hard blow would only leave its sound rattling loose in the rafters for days. Instead, they coax the architecture with shadows and patience. Watch a man standing on a bare terrace in the full parchment light: he holds up a small frame of woven twigs and stiffened leaf-vellum, tilting it carefully until a crisp, dark polygon falls across the hexagonal floor tiles. Slowly, kneeling, he drives four tiny gypsum pegs into the corners of the shadow, pinning the darkness down onto the clay, and then he folds his frame and walks away. The shadow stays behind without him. Over the next handful of slow afternoon cycles, dry vines will find its edges, the dust inside the pinned shape will thicken and grow fibrous, and a low cedar-and-plaster bench will stand solidly where he left the dark.

Elsewhere, a pair of sisters sit side by side leaning their backs against a low perimeter wall, sharing a quiet hour while their shoulder blades press into the sun-warmed brick. They appear to be merely resting—and they are—yet by the time they rise and brush the kiln-flour from their shawls, the wall behind them is a course taller, the new bricks still sharp-edged and breathing out a dry, fresh heat. Here, labor and rest are not opposites. To sit thoughtfully in the right corner, to lean a palm against a sagging lintel until its dry roots remember how to square themselves into a joist, to nudge a stray seed into a mortar seam with the tip of a sandal—this is how the place is kept.

Their speech obeys the same unhurried drift. Because cause and sound have come slightly unglued in the warm hush, they rarely talk face to face in quick exchanges; a sentence spoken straight into a friend’s ear might not arrive until the friend has walked into another courtyard, only to spill unexpectedly out of a hanging basket behind them. Instead, they converse *spatially*, planting their words where they know someone will be.

You see a reed-weaver pause beside a shady alcove where the air smells richly of cinnamon, tilt her head toward an empty clay pitcher on the sill, and murmur three dry, rhythmic phrases into its mouth—a voice like a thumb drawn across warm, stretched sheepskin. Then she moves on to her loom. Five minutes later, her husband rounds the corner carrying a bundle of pale hollow reeds. He stops beside the pitcher without breaking stride, bows his ear to the lip just as her voice floats cleanly out of the terracotta dark, and smiles to himself. To answer, he taps two brisk knuckles and one lingering thumb-press against a mortised cedar beam overhead. He knows the gentle *tock-tock-hush* will surface inside her wooden needle-box halfway through the next turn of light.

Because stray sounds can pile up in the tight alleys like dry leaves—dropped shards clacking minutes late, forgotten sighs pooling under the benches, old arguments still clicking faintly inside the eaves—a few keen-eared residents take on the quiet chore of sweeping the noise. They walk the galleries during the warm, straw-tinted hours carrying wide-mouthed terracotta jars lined with unraveled hemp. Pausing beneath a thick trellis or inside a stairwell pleat, they unstopper a jar and hold it still until the lingering *clack-tink* of a morning’s broken pot or a stray puff of dry laughter drifts into the clay belly, then plug it briskly with a cedar bung. Most of these jars they carry to the central well-head and unstopper over the rim, letting the old clutter tumble down into the pale sky below. A rare few—the crisp settling of a new roof-beam, the soft chorus of a household dining together, a particularly bright remark left behind by a grandparent—they label with painted ochre bands and shelve in cool alcoves like preserves, opening them for a moment whenever the afternoon feels too still.

If you are invited inside one of their dwellings, you learn how effortlessly they inhabit the folded geometry that bewilders an outsider. A house here does not have a front door in the ordinary sense; it has a *turn*. Your host steps between a limewashed pillar and a copper-leafed trellis where there seems to be scarcely a hand’s breadth of gap, turns her shoulder at a practiced angle while resting two fingers on a carved peg, and slips sideways into a broad, sunlit room that cannot possibly fit inside the brick perimeter.

Inside, the walls are paneled in dry, sweet-smelling cane and hung with woven mats that whisper softly in the thermal drifts. There is no hearth—everything is already warm—and no basin for washing. Instead, at the center of the room sits a low table of planed pine, its legs still sprouting tiny, mathematically neat joinery twigs that the family trims back every few days with bone knives.

Here they gather to eat. They have no use for broth or wet fruit; dampness is something they cannot quite picture, a faint, uneasy word preserved only in the crescent marks their own thumbs strangely leave in the dry plaster. They pass around shallow terracotta dishes heaped with toasted flax-husks, brittle wafers pressed from ochre blossom-dust, and small translucent drops of pale cedar resin that dissolve on a dry tongue into a clean, sharp warmth of pine smoke. For refreshment, the head of the household brings out a flat slate tray bearing thin, cool ribbons of *shade*—harvested that morning with a bone scraper from the deep, untouched dark beneath the rafters, folded neatly like dark silk. Laying a strip of shadow across the tongue brings a crisp, dry coolness down through the hollow ribs without a single drop of moisture. Afterward, they break open bundles of the bone-pale reeds that grow between the flagstones and draw the warm, cinnamon-scented air deep into their chests, passing the stems from hand to hand until their faces flush a shade richer terracotta and small, pale rings of chalk dust drift lazily up toward the rafters.

They are deeply affectionate, though their closeness looks quiet to wet eyes. Lovers sit hip to hip on the narrow brick steps, letting the sheepskin browsers creep near to nibble the ragged edge where their two shadows overlap, stitching them together into a single dark shape on the clay tiles. Sometimes they trade small parts for the comfort of carrying one another’s craft—a wooden toggle from a left wrist, a strip of vellum binding, a coil of flaxen hair braided into a belt.

Even the way new lives begin among them is an act of patient, shared fitting. When a household—a pair, or three or four who share a pleated suite of rooms—feels a quiet absence at their table, a space shaped like someone who has not yet arrived, they throw a fresh, thick-walled amphora on a dry wooden wheel. Over the course of a long season, each member adds what belongs to them: a pinch of warm kiln-flour brushed from their own palms, a handful of wedge-shaped wooden seeds from the courtyard vines, braided flax, a miniature cedar toggle carved by an elder, and—last of all, tipped carefully from a stoppered sound-jar—a single preserved breath of household laughter. They seal the mouth with gypsum paste and set the vessel along the sunniest brick bench.

They do not hover over it. They let the warm afternoon do its slow persuasion. Weeks pass as the light shifts from parchment-white to toasted straw and back again. Gradually, the terracotta lip softens; ribs appear down the clay sides as the amphora remembers how to be a dry-skinned gourd, swelling slightly in the heat until one quiet noon it splits cleanly down its wheel-seam with no sound at all.

Out climbs a child, knee-high and powdered head to toe in fine pale dust, with glossy pebble-dark eyes and joints that click softly like new bamboo. Four minutes later, from inside a nearby cedar chest, comes the crisp, joyous *pop* of the gourd splitting open.

The young ones are the closest thing to haste the courtyard knows. Their bodies are still freshly fitted, light as hollow reed and coiled flax, and they move slightly ahead of their own physics—so that you hear the dry *pat-pat-pat* of small bare heels crossing a terrace half a second before a dusty child darts around the corner, chasing a rolling clay bead. They are terrible teases. They lift the pinned corners of newly laid shadows so an uncle sits down on empty air; they coax the chalk-breathing finches down from the mortised rafters to exhale tiny white rings around their fingers; they dare one another to lean far out over the central well-head and drop wooden spoons straight down into the inverted sky below, giggling silently until the spoons drop back up out of the shaft from the annoyed neighbor living upside down on the other side.

For the inverted courtyard beneath the well is inhabited too—or rather, it is simply the far side of the same cramped, neighborly village. In the middle of the afternoon, you may see the hand-twisted flax rope hanging down the brick throat suddenly draw taut. Moments later, a pair of reed-sandaled feet rises calmly out of the bright parchment light at the bottom of the shaft, followed by a man climbing *upward* from the underside, gripping the rope hand over hand. Halfway up the ten-foot throat, where the descending copper vines tangle seamlessly with the ascending ones, his linen tunic gives a gentle rustle as his weight flips cleanly around; by the time his head clears the curved brick rim, he is upright, stepping lightly onto the flagstones with a woven basket of fresh mineral petals tucked under his arm, greeting his cousins with a dry nod before ducking into a nearby pleat.

And on the highest parapets, where the brick stairs narrow beneath canopies so dense with paper-leaves that the warmth feels almost thick as oil, the residents keep an easy, companionable peace with the outer copy of their own world. A woman who has mislaid her bone awl climbs the steep steps, ducks under the vines, and leans her elbows on the warm perimeter wall, gazing out across the brick rim at the identical, quarter-turned courtyard beyond. She watches the small figure standing on the far parapet five minutes behind her—herself, facing the wrong way, just turning down the stairs—and tracks her earlier self’s movements through the alleys until she sees which bench she set the awl beside. Sometimes, when no tool is lost at all, an old man climbs to the wall simply to rest his chin on his hands and watch himself tending the vines a few minutes earlier, offering a small, dry wave across the rim that his recent self does not see, yet somehow feels as a quiet warmth between the shoulder blades.

Nothing here dies into ruin, and neither do the people.

When a resident has lived through more breathings of the parchment sky than anyone cares to count—when their limbs have been patched and re-pegged a dozen times with cedar, olive-wood, and fine bisque, and their movements have slowed until even the shingle-lizards seem brisk beside them—they do not take to a sickbed. They choose a corner of the courtyard they are fond of: a sunny bend in a staircase, the warm shoulder of a loggia column, the brick ledge beside a well-used bench.

There, over the span of years, they settle.

At first they simply sit longer each day, letting the morning dust gather in the folds of their shawls while younger folk pause to leave murmured questions in the wicker baskets at their feet. Gradually, their categories soften back into the house. The curve of an old grandfather’s back broadens and hardens into a warm course of limewashed brick; his outstretched forearm lengthens, squares its corners cleanly, and mortises itself into the overhead trellis to bear the weight of the heavy copper vines. His lap loosens into a bed of fine gypsum sand where pale jointed reeds sprout straight upward. Yet for decades after his knees have become part of the terrace step—worn smooth by the sandals of children trading pins with beetles—his face remains tucked calmly into the shadow of the cornice, matte and sun-warmed, eyes half-lidded beneath thin parchment brows. Every so often, when a dispute over a sagging beam or a tangled pleat lingers too long in the court below, his dry lips part, and a single crisp ring of white chalk dust drifts downward through the sunbeams, settling onto the exact joint that needs a peg.

Eventually, even the face smooths back into the wall, leaving only a patch of unusually warm plaster that retains the clean, sweeping thumbprint of someone who loved the afternoon.

If you stay long enough in that bone-dry, crowded hush, you stop trying to draw a line between the courtyard and the people who inhabit it. Every brick was coaxed upward by the warmth of someone’s back; every person is stitched together from the cedar, flax, clay, and kept shade of the courtyards that raised them. They live inside one another’s craft, folded tightly against an emptiness that never reaches them, endlessly loosening into cane and dust and patiently re-fitting themselves by hand in the steady, golden warmth.
