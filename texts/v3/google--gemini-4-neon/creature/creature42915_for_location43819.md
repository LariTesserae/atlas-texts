You can walk through three districts without ever suspecting they are here, because they share the city’s palette and its habit of stillness.

The first signs are easy to mistake for accidents of erosion. A narrow ribbon of dark, dry vellum knotted tightly around the impost of a freestanding arch—too neat to be wind-caught debris, yet utterly motionless in the quiet air. A line of pale ochre mineral-crusts scraped clean off a cistern wall, except for a single deliberate band left untouched to seed the next season’s creep. A shallow hollow worn into the lee side of a tilted stairwell, polished slightly smoother than the chalk around it, as though something dry and narrow has rested its shoulder there for a century.

Then, on a high promenade where the masonry breaks off over a mile of pale haze, a weathered abutment beside a colonnade shifts its weight and stands up.

At a glance, they look tall, spare, and strangely pleated—not draped in cloth, for nothing fibrous grows here to spin, but wrapped in loose, overlapping layers of chalk-pale grazer hide stitched with dry sinew. Where skin shows at the wrists, throat, and ankles, it has the matte, unglazed finish of bisque porcelain or old vellum, fine gypsum-dust settled permanently into the creases of the joints. They carry no warmth. If you were to lay a palm against their forearm, it would feel no different from a shaded limestone balustrade—cool, dry, slightly powdery, drawing no heat and offering none. Nothing in them sweats, flushes, or weeps. Their eyes are large and set deep under bone-pale brows, the irises the flat pewter of the overcast sky; every few breaths, a thin, translucent inner lid wipes horizontally across the surface to clear drifting grit without a drop of moisture.

They do not hurry. In thin air where every wasted motion costs more than the body can easily pay, economy has become grace. Their ribcages are unusually deep and long beneath their layered hides, rising and falling in slow, measured cycles—one drawn-out intake, held effortlessly while they cross an entire plaza, then released in a faint, papery sigh. They walk unshod on soles hardened to the texture of fine pumice, placing each narrow foot flat and quiet so the settled dust barely lifts around their ankles. Along a knife-edge cornice with half a league of empty air dropping away beneath their toes, they move with the same unhurried attentiveness you might give to a hallway at home.

They have no wet voices. In air this thin and dry, a soft throat would crack and fail, and sound dies twenty paces from the mouth anyway. Up close, sheltered in an alcove where two of them bend over a scraped crust or a knotted cord, you can hear them speaking in a quick, intricate rustle—dry clicks, hollow pops, and fine rasps produced by pleated cartilage plates along the sides of their throats and behind their jaws. It sounds less like speech than like dry shale shifting, or brittle leaves turning in a place where no leaves have ever existed.

Their real conversation happens through the floor.

Kneel in the middle of an apparently deserted six-sided square and press your bare palm—better yet, your cheekbone—flat against the cold flagstones. At first there is only the hush, heavy against your ear, and the tiny *tick* of contracting stone as a pressure-front moves through the valley. Wait longer, and you begin to hear it: a faint, dry lattice of knocks traveling up through the limestone from miles away. Two short taps, a long pause, a clustered triple-tap that answers from a different depth of grain.

Just as the long-limbed grazers knock once or twice to test a floor or call across a folded threshold, the city’s people have turned the bedrock itself into a vast, silent instrument. They tap with the horn-hardened pads of their knuckles, or with short, dense pins carved from grazer cannon-bone, striking specific dished flagstones they know to be acoustically live. A rhythm tapped into a courtyard on an eastern needle travels down the continuous stone, slips across the invisible seam of a freestanding arch, picks up the hollow resonance of an empty cistern, and surfaces a league west beneath someone’s bare heel. Through the floor they pass the news that matters here: which viaducts are holding steady, which district’s edges have begun to blur and double, where a thick sheet of ochre-crust has crept out of reach of the light, and who has gone silent on the far side of a closed fold.

Because the city does not hold distance firmly, neither do their lives.

They do not build towns within the city, nor do they cluster in crowded quarters where too much movement might draw the dark, silent attention of the shadow-gliders. Instead, they live in small, unhurried households—three to seven individuals, rarely more—whose rooms are not next to one another in any ordinary sense. A sleeping-niche may lie tucked inside a narrow stair-shaft on a northern spire, chosen specifically because gravity there tilts fifteen degrees into the wall, holding a resting body snugly in a dished corner where a gliding predator cannot bank or drop. Ten paces away, through a blind-looking stone arch, lies their sifting-cistern on a sunny-less terrace two miles south, where an upward dust-tide pours cleanly over a lip and deposits its finest mineral silt in graded bands. Another doorway off that terrace leads to a wide, sheltered gallery on an isolated mesa where the household gathers to scrape crusts, mend hide-wraps, and listen to the floor.

To a stranger, it looks like a scattering of empty rooms separated by unbridged gulfs. To them, it is a compact, familiar house—until a slow barometric cycle shifts the city’s adjacencies and carries the sifting-cistern away for three months, leaving the doorway opening onto a blank shale wall or a sheer drop into slate haze.

They do not panic when a room drifts. They do not own enough to be ruined by it, and they have never believed stone ought to stay put. Every adult carries a bundle of narrow strips sliced from dark glider-vellum and knotted with dry tendon—not maps of where places are, for no map survives a year here, but tallies of *sequence and interval*. A double knot means an arch that holds through low pressure; a split bead of grazer-horn marks a threshold that only opens after the gypsum-tides reverse; a frayed tail warns of a courtyard prone to sudden doubling. By feeling the knots with their fingertips in the dimness, they remember the city’s long, slow breathing—knowing that the cistern lost today will likely align again with the lower amphitheater before a colony of crust has crept the width of a hand.

They leave no tool marks on the city, because they have no tools that could bite into stone, and no wish to fight it. Yet they are not wholly passive tenants. Over decades, they coax the bedrock’s own architectural habit. Knowing that the limestone fractures cleanly along municipal lines when stressed by shadow and cool pressure-shifts, they stretch broad panels of dark vellum across a terrace lip to create a sharp thermal boundary, or stack small wedges of loose shale in a dry gutter to divert an upward dust-tide against a specific riser. You would see nothing happening in a week, or a year. Come back in a generation, and the stone has parted quietly along the line they shaded, opening a new low bench, a wind-break, or a fresh cistern-lip without a single chisel ever touching rock.

Much of their quiet industry centers on the orphaned shadows.

Where a long, crisp wedge of darkness lies across an empty plaza—cast by a tower three streets away, or by one that fell out of existence before memory—the stone beneath it is subtly cooler than the pale pavement beside it. Along that razor-clean border, where the shadow-chill meets the drifting gypsum-dust, the papery mineral-crusts grow thickest and richest in ochre salt. The inhabitants tend these shadow-edges the way farmers elsewhere tend irrigated rows. Kneeling beside the dark geometry of an absent spire, they use flat bone blades to lift the mature, eggshell-thin fans of crust without breaking the calcified rim that keeps the colony creeping forward. Pressed between flat slabs of pumice and ground with trace salts sifted from the rising dust-tides, these crusts form the bulk of their dry sustenance—chewed slowly until chalky enzymes in their mouths break the minerals down, feeding a cool, low-burning chemistry that requires no water and leaves no waste but fine white grit.

Tending the shadows is also where the danger lives.

A shadow-glider flattened against a tower wall or spread across an amphitheater floor looks almost indistinguishable from the shade of a vanished building. Almost. The inhabitants learn to read shadows before they are tall enough to reach a lintel. A true orphaned shadow has a hard, dry edge that never softens; gypsum-dust skates across it in unbroken sheets. Where a glider lies flat, the border carries the faintest slow heave of breathing, and tiny eddies curl off its taut vellum rim like smoke off a hidden sill. When one detaches silently from a high parapet and banks into an intersection, the people below do not run—running kicks up dust and burns air too fast. They drop instantly flat against the pale flagstones, pulling their chalk-colored hides tight over their heads and limbs until they read as weathered stone, or they slip sideways into the tilted stair-shafts where the skewed gravity ruins a glider’s flat, effortless descent.

Even so, they hunt the gliders when they must, because nothing else in this elevation yields a membrane as light, tough, and responsive as glider-vellum. A small strip of it stitched to the cuff of a sleeve flutters silently in pressure-fronts no skin can feel, warning of a coming dust-surge or a spatial tremor minutes before the stone begins to tick. Larger sheets make wind-screens, shadow-casters, and knotted route-tallies. To take a glider without bows or metal, three or four of them work in utter silence over days, setting out dried sinew-snares weighted with chamfered shale slabs along a narrow window-slot the predator uses to ride an updraft. When it stalls and folds in on itself with no more sound than a slate tile slipping from a roof, they wait until its slow struggling stops, then take it apart with bone-splinters—saving every fine, hollow articulated rib for pins, needles, and tally-frames.

Because nothing rots in this dry chill, death has no stench and no urgency. When one of the tall, many-jointed grazers grows too stiff to clear a closing arch and folds down quietly inside a sunken courtyard, it simply dries into parchment and clean bone. The inhabitants never hunt them; the grazers are too useful as unwitting guides, sensing an unstable fold long before a person’s eye catches the faint double-edge of the flagstones. Instead, they follow a dying grazer from a respectful distance, wait until its slow toe-tapping ceases altogether, and only then harvest the wide, chalk-matte hide and the dense leg-bones—leaving the skull upright in the corner of a cistern, where the tiny fingernail-sized grit-scavengers soon fill its eye-sockets with neat, stepped spirals of white stone-powder.

Those tiny scavengers are the smallest neighbors, and the most carefully watched. Whenever a district’s spatial revision resolves—one ghosted pavement hardening into solid stone while the other puffs away into pale powder—the scavengers emerge from hair-thin fissures to build their minute cones. An inhabitant stepping onto newly settled flagstones will pause and look down at the threshold corners first. Fresh spirals mean the stone has truly chosen its form and will hold; an empty, swept corner means the revision is not yet finished, and a foot placed there might find itself stepping into a courtyard that is about to cease being.

Most people stay well clear of a doubling square. It is deeply unsettling to stand where two versions of a plaza occupy the same air—one smoothly dished by centuries of heels, one freshly cleaved and razor-sharp—feeling the grain shift ambiguously beneath your soles as the air goes dry with unmade grit.

Yet every household has at least one member—usually older, with steady eyes and horn-thick palms—who seeks the doublings out.

When the pressure shifts and the parapets go faintly translucent at the edges, these watchers step right up to the border of the overlap and stand utterly still, holding a single slow breath. For three or four minutes, before one version of the city dissolves into white powder, the veil between adjacencies wears thin enough to touch. Sometimes they see only an older version of the same square: an arch still whole that collapsed long ago, or an unfamiliar shadow cast by a spire that no longer stands. Sometimes they reach quickly into the ghosted niche of an overlapping wall and pull back a rare slab of black salt-crust or a smooth bone pin that belongs to a lineage whose district has not touched theirs in fifty years.

And once in a long while, looking across the doubled flagstones, they see another pale figure standing on the opposite threshold—similarly wrapped in chalk-hide, similarly still, fingers resting on a bundle of knotted vellum. Neither speaks, because air does not carry across the overlap, and tapping the unsettled floor would scatter the resonance. They simply raise one long, dusty hand, palm open, showing the pattern of calluses and scars—a silent greeting across a branch of the city that drifted out of alignment generations before—and watch each other steadily until one of them resolves quietly into a rising puff of fine white dust and drifts away on the cool draught.

They keep no calendars, because there is neither night nor winter to count. Age is reckoned instead by the slow migrations of the mineral-crusts and the number of times a household’s familiar arches have cycled shut and opened again.

Their young begin quietly, kept through their earliest, softest years in the deep shelter of dry, dust-buffered cisterns where the wind cannot reach them and the orphaned shadows keep the stone steady. They are born small, pale, and extraordinarily thin-skinned, wrapped at first in overlapping swaddles of soft inner-hide rubbed with fine white grit from abandoned scavenger-cones to shield them from the desiccating air. Before a child is allowed to walk an open plaza alone, it must sit for long hours with its palms pressed to a quiet gallery floor, learning to tell the hollow tick of cooling limestone from the measured double-knock of an elder three spires away, and learning to tell the dead, razor-sharp edge of a vanished tower’s shadow from the faint breathing rim of a glider waiting to drop.

And as they grow very old, the city slowly reclaims the mineral they borrowed from it.

Over long decades of chewing salt-crusts and breathing chalk-dust, their dry bodies calcify from the inside out. Their joints stiffen into pale ridges; their skin thickens into something nearly indistinguishable from compacted pumice; their breathing stretches until a single quiet intake lasts several minutes. They lose the nimbleness needed to catch a closing arch or drop flat in a plaza before a shadow falls.

When an elder feels the last flexibility leaving their knees, they do not wait to fail on an exposed viaduct. Accompanied by two or three of their household, they walk—taking days, resting often—to an anchored, deeply weathered ledge overlooking one of the vast interior gulfs, or to the sheltered corner of a permanent orphaned shadow where the stone has not doubled in living memory. They sit down with their backs flush against the masonry, knees drawn close, long hands folded neatly over their deep ribs, and pull their chalk-pale hides tight around their shoulders.

Their kin do not weep; they have no tears to shed. One by one, they kneel beside the elder, press their bare palms to the flagstones on either side of the sitting figure, and tap out a long, slow cadence—the full sequence of every arch, stair, and cistern they have shared, spoken one final time into the bedrock. Then they rise and leave without looking back.

Nothing decays. In the cool, sterile hush, the elder’s breathing slows until it stops altogether, and the body dries cleanly into solid salt and lime. Over months, the rising gypsum-tides bank neatly around the folded knees; papery ochre-crusts creep softly across the motionless shoulders; millennia of dry abrasion round the sharp angles of elbow and crown. In ten years, a traveler walking that promenade would see only a low, deeply weathered abutment projecting from the wall—another seamless quirk of a bedrock that fractures along architectural lines.

Only the people who live here know better. Passing that ledge generations later, on their way through a newly aligned arch toward a distant cistern, a narrow, chalk-dusted figure will pause for a breath, lay a horn-hard knuckle against the weathered stone corner, and give it two soft, dry taps—listening for the faint, hollow resonance of an ancestor’s ribcage still answering from inside the city’s bones.
