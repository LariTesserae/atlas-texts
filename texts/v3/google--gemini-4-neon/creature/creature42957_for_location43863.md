You could cross four basins and walk the length of two green troughs before realizing the plateau is not uninhabited.

Even then, you do not see anyone at first. You only begin to notice small improbabilities—things a fraction too deliberate to belong to wind or geology. A broad malachite frond whose wind-split central rib has been drawn closed with three neat stitches of translucent silk and sealed with a glossy drop of amber resin. A finger-length sliver of clear selenite wedged vertically into a hairline fissure on a bare limestone swell, catching the midday sun at an angle no natural fracture would hold. Most startling of all, if you kneel on open stone in the glass-cold silence just after dawn, you may feel something through the bones of your shins that never reaches your ears: a dry, crisp sequence of pulses—*tock, tock, pause, tock*—traveling straight through the unbroken dolomitic bedrock from somewhere miles beyond the next low terrace.

When you finally meet one of them, it is usually because you almost walked past them.

Deep inside a shaded trough, where the arched fronds hold back the glare and the air smells sharply of cedar-resin and dry sage, a pale shape resting among the coiled rosette-cushions will shift by an inch and cease to be an outcrop of rose-veined gypsum.

They are tall, spare, and long-limbed, built with the same severe economy as everything else on the high continent. Their chests are deep and narrow, the ribs broad and slightly overlapping like roof-tiles over huge, slow-working lungs; even after a long walk, their sides barely move—four measured breaths to a lowland visitor’s twelve. Their skin is completely dry, matte ivory tinted with faint chalk-rose, carrying a fine, powdery bloom of gypsum flour rubbed deliberately over the forearms, neck, and cheeks, with a thin sheen of clear leaf-oil worked into the creases of the knuckles and elbows so the thirsty air cannot steal a drop of moisture. They have no hair to tangle in mineral dust, only stiff, pale, crystalline-looking lashes beneath heavy, overhanging brow-ridges that shade their eyes like stone eaves. Their irises are dark amber, almost black, and when they step from a shaded aisle into the raw white glare of the stone flats, a second, smoky translucent lid draws smoothly across each eye from the inner corner.

Their mouths stay closed in a neat, dry seam. In air this thin and parched, an open mouth is an open wound.

They do not speak to you across a clearing. They wait until you are within arm’s reach, sheltered beneath the oiled vaults of the canopy, and even then their voice is scarcely louder than the papery shift of the leaves around you—a hushed, remarkably articulated fabric of dry clicks, soft palatal taps, and low, closed-throat hums that spend almost no breath and vanish completely three paces down the aisle.

Distance belongs to the rock and the light.

Every adult carries a slender rod of dense, mineralized root-wood, as long as a forearm and tipped with a honed point of pale enamel scavenged from a fallen grazer. When one group needs to hail another across a basin, a listener drops to one knee on an unbroken slab of dolomitic pavement, sets the enamel tip flush against the stone, and strikes the butt of the rod with a knuckle or a small, flat pebble. The thin air swallows the sound instantly, yet the continuous bedrock carries the vibration clean and sharp for leagues. To answer or receive, they press a bare cheekbone and the bony heel of one hand directly against the cold limestone, eyes half-shut, reading the cadence of distant steps and tapped phrases through their own skulls.

Across longer spans—basin to basin, forty miles over the dazzling curve of the tableland—they use the sun itself. Stitched to the backs of their wrists and along the stiff collars of their garments are flat, hand-ground plates of silver-backed mica and polished enamel. Tilted into the unfiltered glare, a single flick of the wrist throws a needle-sharp pinprick of blinding light across the pale flats. If you know where to look toward late morning, before the dust-veils thicken, you can watch whole conversations glittering silently between the dark green ribbons ten or twenty miles apart: crisp little stars blinking on and off along the edge of the emerald haze, telling of a fresh mineral seam, a glider’s nesting ridge, or a traveler setting out on the wind.

***

They wear nothing spun from wet fibers. Their garments are layered, pleated cloaks made from the shed outer cuticles of the giant trough-fronds, cured flat and scraped supple until they feel like waxed parchment—deep oiled green on the inside, burnished reflective silver on the outside, bound together with fine silken filaments combed from the afternoon spore-bladders. At rest, the cloaks hang in stiff, neat folds that shed mineral flour and trap a quiet layer of still air against the body.

Around late morning, when the sun-warmed stone sets the colossal rivers of wind sliding down from the high domes, those same cloaks become sails.

This is why there are no roads on the plateau. A traveler bound for a trough sixty miles away does not trudge through the drifting gypsum smoke. At the edge of the open flats, they unlatch a bundle of slender, hollow spars—wing-bones gleaned from long-dead gliders, light as dry reeds and springy as horn—and thread them through sewn sleeves along the pleated skirts of their cloak. Onto their wide, flat feet they strap upturned runners shaped from curved grazer-enamel, polished glass-smooth on the underside. Then they step out of the wind-shadow of the canopy, open their arms to the cool, steady push of the continental air, and lean back.

There is no roar of wind in canvas, only a taut, low hum like a thumb drawn across dry silk. The traveler accelerates without effort, skimming across the fluted, whale-backed limestone at twenty or thirty miles an hour, banking gently down the mile-wide terraces and riding the pale, horizontal veils of glittering mica dust. Behind them, the enameled runners leave twin faint ribbons in the silica flour—lines so shallow that the wind erases them completely ten seconds later.

Crossing the bare rock is never careless, even at speed. Three thousand feet up in the indigo zenith, the pale cruciform gliders turn on the morning thermals, and from that height a solitary figure moving over white limestone looks dangerously close to a stray grazer. So a wind-traveler keeps one wrist turned steadily skyward as they glide, throwing a sharp, measured strobe of reflected sunlight into the upper air—three quick flashes, a pause, three more. It is an unnatural, blinding pulse that the gliders have known for a thousand generations means *barbed, hard-boned, and not worth the drop*.

Sometimes a real grazer, forced by a depleted thicket to risk a narrow neck of open rock, will emerge quietly from the fronds as a small band prepares to cross and pace slowly twenty yards behind them, borrowing the safety of their flashing wrists. The people do not drive the grazers, pen them, or lay hands on them in haste. Nothing on the plateau has the spare oxygen for domination. Only occasionally, in the dim afternoon heat beneath a trough’s lower tiers, an elder will sit motionless beside a boulder-still grazer and patiently pick stubborn crusts of sulfate salt from the narrow seams between its chalk-white dorsal plates, keeping the mineral scrapings in a small cuticle pouch while the beast lets its slow, heavy flanks relax into the leaf-litter.

***

They build no walls because the plateau builds their houses every evening and takes them down every morning.

Toward dusk, as the amber light stretches mile-long shadows across the polished flats and the air begins its plunge toward freezing, each small family cluster—rarely more than four or five individuals, for the thin margins of the troughs forbid crowds—retreats deep into a mature stand of the towering emerald fronds. Between the stout, resin-filled stalks of the canopy, they sling narrow, springy hammocks braided from spore-silk, hanging a few feet above the dry mattress of papery husks. They draw their silver cloaks tightly over their knees and shoulders; if there is a child—children are rare, slow-growing, and fiercely guarded—it is tucked completely inside a wax-lined pouch against a parent’s ribs, breathing the warm, slightly humidified air inside the cloak.

Then the groves begin their nightly furl.

With a long, whispering rustle, the great silver-backed leaves curl inward overhead, overlapping layer upon tight layer until the entire forty-mile trough has sealed itself into rows of insulated green-lined cylinders. Inside that living vault, the day’s warmth lingers for hours, thick with the clean scent of cedar-oil and chalk. The killing frost passes harmlessly across the silver exterior. In the pitch dark of the sealed stems, the people murmur in their low, breath-sparing clicks, mend frayed silk-stitching by touch, and sink into a shallow, slow-pulsed torpor until the first white sunlight strikes the eastern rim and the rippling, wax-loosening crackle wakes the canopy, unrolling their ceiling back to the dark blue sky.

That dawn hour, before the thermal wind wakes, is the busiest time of their day. Slipping out of the opening fronds onto the still, glass-cold rim of the trough, they sweep the feathery overnight blooms of white salt-rime from the bare stone pans using broad, flat scoops of cured cuticle, filling light oiled sacks before the wind can shave the basins clean. Others kneel to press their cheeks to the bedrock, taking the morning’s stone-tapped news from neighboring clusters along the seam.

What they eat and drink comes entirely from the dry, sealed loop of the groves. They never pour liquid water down their throats; their bodies reclaim almost every molecule they metabolize, yielding nothing damp back to the air. Instead, they chew the thick, pale ribs of young lower leaves slowly in sealed cheeks, extracting the dense amber fluid; or they tap the swollen morning joints of deep-trough stalks with hair-fine needles of hollow bone, sipping the slow, pine-sharp glycerine sap and immediately sealing the tiny puncture with a smear of warm wax from their thumb so the plant does not lose a drop to the dry wind. They pry small, dense, chalk-skinned tubers from where the wire-fine roots fuse into buried clay seams, and mix ground mineral flour with thickened sap into hard, bitter-sweet cakes that never spoil.

In the bright afternoons, when the crimson pods split deep in the bronze-veined tiers and thousands of iridescent, gas-buoyed spore-bladders lift into the sky, the people string near-invisible veils of silk between a few chosen aisles, taking a small harvest of the translucent spheres. Pierced carefully with a bone thorn, each bladder yields a single warm drop of rich, golden yolk. Meat is rarer still—never hunted in wasteful chases, only gleaned with startling speed at twilight when a glider drops a grazer on an open terrace and rises with the oily organs. Racing the flat, copper-shelled twilight runners in absolute silence, the people slice thin ribbons of lean flank before the frost sets, pressing them flat under warm limestone slabs until they dry into brittle, salt-cured strips that keep in an oiled pouch for years.

In return, they are quiet, almost invisible gardeners of the seams they inhabit. As they move along a trough, they strip away dead, suffocating husks from young rosette-cushions, gently untangle wind-locked fronds, and brush heavy drifts of gypsum flour off fledgling shoots too small to digest it. They leave no cleared fields and no straight rows—only groves that grow a little thicker, a little cleaner, and a little longer-lived where their small bands pass.

***

Because they have never lit a fire—the thin air starves flame, the dry oils are too precious to waste as smoke, and a spark in a resin-rich trough would unmake a thousand years of stone-born growth—their entire craft belongs to sunlight and slow acid.

If you climb a low, wind-planed terrace at high noon, you may see an artisan sitting completely still in the blazing glare, eyes veiled behind their smoky second lids, tending a shallow parabolic dish no wider than a wagon wheel. Its frame is bent glider-rib; its lining is the mirror-bright silver underside of cured canopy-leaf, rubbed smooth with fine talc until it shines like burnished pewter. Tilted cleanly into the unfiltered zenith light, the dish gathers the fierce sky into a single silent, blinding point of white heat suspended in midair.

Holding tiny bone tongs with steady, slow-breathing patience, the artisan works inside that smokeless bead of pure sun. There they fuse pinched grains of white silica flour into clear, opalescent beads and tiny magnifying lenses; they melt hardened nodules of amber sap into glass-clear lacquer and waterproof seals; they gently soften flat strips of scavenged grazer-enamel until they can be bent into needles, hooks, combs, and the sleek upturned toes of wind-runners. When the midday wind rises too strong to hold the focus, they unhook two bone ribs, fold the silver dish flat against their back, and walk away. Not even a scorch mark remains on the pale limestone where they worked.

Everything else they make is carved by patience and chemistry.

Down among the dark root-mats, where the plants pry bound water straight out of the bedrock, the people gather tiny drops of the milky mineral acid that weeps from young root-tips, storing it in thumb-sized vials of hardened wax-resin. With a single stiff plant-bristle, they paint that slow acid onto palm-sized plates of pale rose gypsum or thin, flattened slabs of enamel. Over days and weeks, the acid bites cleanly into the surface, etching lines fine as spider-silk.

These thin, feather-light stone tablets are their books, their maps, and their memory. Every adult carries a small bundle of them wrapped in oiled silk inside their cloak: maps of buried alkaline veins and fresh fissures sprouting green fifty miles away; wind-charts showing how the seasonal currents bend around the high domes; intricate genealogies keeping track of distant clusters so the small, scattered bands do not grow too close in blood; and older tablets, worn smooth at the corners by ten generations of dry thumbs, etched with records of slow geological shifts—a trough that dried and faded three centuries ago, a new ridge of talc exposed by a century of blowing grit.

They leave libraries, too, tucked into dry, shaded crevices beneath the root-mats at the ends of major troughs, marked only by a single wedged sliver of clear selenite—caches any traveler may open, read, copy, or add a fresh etched slip to before sealing the hollow again.

***

Across eighty or a hundred miles of pale tableland, there may be no more than two or three thousand of them in total. Yet they do not feel isolated.

Twice a year, near the solstices when the thermal winds slacken into a gentler rhythm, small bands sail in from three or four neighboring troughs to meet at certain shallow, circular stone basins. Over centuries, using nothing but dry silica grit and flat stones rubbed patiently by hand, generations have ground the centers of these basins into wide, almost imperceptibly smooth concave pans. At windless dawn, a person lying face-down in the heart of such a pan can hear the faintest bedrock-tapping from thirty miles off, focused cleanly by the dish of rock.

A gathering makes almost no sound at all. Seen from a ridge half a mile away, twenty or thirty people seated in a wide ring on the bare dolomitic pavement look at first like a scatter of pale, wind-planed boulders catching the morning glare. There are no drums, no raised voices, no campfires sending smoke into the indigo sky. Instead, etched gypsum tablets pass steadily from palm to palm; resin-lenses and rare red clay-minerals from distant seams are laid out on folded silver cloaks; young adults quietly trade places between clusters, stepping into new lineages to walk new green ribbons; and children sit cheek-to-stone at the center of the pan, learning from the elders how to distinguish the crisp tap of a storm-warning from the long, rolling cadence of a greeting.

Even between gatherings, they stay lightly stitched together by the afternoon air. When the crimson pods split and the iridescent spore-bladders lift lazily out of the dark green aisles into the violet zenith, elders and children alike will sometimes catch a rising sphere by its trailing filament, tie a microscopic flake of acid-etched mica or a single knotted thread of resin-dyed silk to the strand, and let it go. It drifts upward, catches the fierce sun, and joins the glittering constellations riding the high air for fifty or a hundred miles. Most vanish into empty rock. Now and then, years later, a walker in a distant fissure finds a tiny, sun-flashing sliver caught in a young canopy and reads, in three microscopic grooves, the sign of a far band: *the seam still holds; we are still here.*

***

When one of them grows very old—when their ribs no longer draw enough from the thin air and the cold inside the shadows begins to linger even in full sun—there is no burial mound and no pyre.

Their cluster walks with them, slowly, to the quiet head of a young trough where the pale, wire-fine root-mats are pushing freshly into exposed gypsum. They take the elder’s etched tablets, their tapping-rod, and their silver cloak, folding the empty pleats neatly to be remade for a child. The old one lies down unsealed among the dry papery husks, cheek resting one last time against the bedrock, listening as their kin tap a long, slow farewell into the stone nearby—a cadence felt through the bones rather than heard in the air.

Then the others step back into the green aisles. Before frost seals the plateau, the flat, copper-shelled runners emerge at twilight and strip away everything soft. Over the seasons that follow, the slow mineral acids of the roots pry the calcium, phosphorus, and bound water straight out of the dry white bones and draw them upward into the stems. Within a few years, nothing remains of the body except a brief surge of darker, thicker malachite fronds arching cleanly over the seam, and a few new lines etched into the carried stone.

They have no myth of conquering the plateau, and no desire to leave it. On the rarest mornings of a lifetime—once in a decade, perhaps, when the continental wind stalls completely overnight and the white-gold veil of mineral dust settles out of the horizon—a lone traveler standing on the outermost wind-planed terrace at the rim of the crown can look past the bleached curve of stone and see, miles below, a dim, heavy sea of bruised grey vapor that sits motionless in the dark.

Their oldest, most worn gypsum tablets speak of that lower world in spare, cautious grooves. They call it the Heavy Air—a suffocating, sun-starved abyss where water falls loose from the sky like gravel, where rock dissolves into black mud, and where nothing can stay dry or clean for a single day. They stand at the lip for a few slow breaths, watching the thick grey haze beneath their feet, feeling the crisp, pure cold bite quietly at their nostrils.

Then they turn their backs on the drop, step out onto the blinding limestone, unfold their silver-backed cloaks into the first rising breath of the morning wind, and slide silently home toward the dark emerald ribbons—just one more quiet, shining motion in the vast, settled instrument of stone and light.
