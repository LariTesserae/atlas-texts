You do not meet the bowl-folk face-first; you meet their shoulders, their elbows, and the flat of a guiding palm between your shoulder blades.

Nobody walks straight down here, and nobody walks alone in their own pocket of air. The people who live inside the hollow move with a low, springy, crabwise grace, knees slightly bent, heads tipped just enough to keep an overhead drip out of their collars. When two of them pass on a three-plank bridge, neither stops; they simply turn their hips in unison, catch each other’s forearms for half a heartbeat to share their balance over the sixty-foot drop, and flow past like water parting around a cobble. To an outsider, they seem almost startlingly handsy—always steadying a neighbor’s belt, steering a stranger’s elbow clear of a swinging pulley-hook, resting two fingers on your collarbone so you can feel them coming up behind you in the racket. Up on the bare mountain passes, space is cheap and you keep your hands to yourself. Down in the six-story tangle, a hand on your ribs is just good manners.

The wet and the climb have shaped their bodies into something compact, knotty, and hard to bruise. Most of them are built on the shorter side, wide through the ribcage from hauling loads up ladder-stairs in thin high-country air, with calves like knotted hemp and a slight forward stoop that settles into their backs by forty—not from frailty, but from a lifetime of ducking under mossy lintels and leaning into wet wind. Their faces carry the permanent wind-burn of high altitude: bright-cheeked, weather-creased around the eyes, with a habit of glancing upward mid-sentence whenever a shadow crosses the light or a pulley changes pitch.

Look at their hands and you can read their whole history. Thick-knuckled, scarred across the backs from adze-slips and alder thorns, they are stained down into the creases with a stubborn dark patina of oak-bark tannin, bog-peat, walnut-husk, and pine resin. Nails are pared back to the quick so nothing snags on wet rawhide or splits in the cold. Many of the older folk have a stiffness in the fingers that flares when a cloud-bank sits heavy on the rim; they treat it by whipping their knuckles lightly with fresh stinging nettles until the blood rushes hot, then rubbing the skin down with warm mutton tallow and holding their palms close to the peat-braziers until they steam.

They dress as if they expect to be rained on from both above and below, which they are. Their outer wool is not the soft, loose weave of the lowland valleys, but thick cloth beaten under the water-driven fulling-hammers down in the mire until it is nearly as dense as boiled leather. Over their tunics everyone wears a *drip-cape*—a short, stiff shoulder-mantle of waxed felt or greased calfskin with a high-peaked hood that throws water cleanly off the back while leaving the arms free to haul ropes, swing a billhook, or steady a basket. Sleeves are bound tight at the wrist with leather thongs so damp cloth cannot wick cold water up to the armpit. On their feet are heavy bog-oak soles lashed over thick woollen foot-wrappings, cross-grooved underneath to bite into the adzed floorboards, and almost every belt carries the same three bare essentials: a short, fat-bellied pruning knife kept razor-keen, a horn pot of tallow-and-resin salve, and a coil of dry hemp cord tucked safely under the oiled wool against the chest.

Every person in the bowl carries the same underlying scent—woodsmoke, wet lanolin, crushed green bark, and a faint sour-sweet breath of barley and river-silt—though anyone born here can tell from two paces away which tier you work on by what rides on top of it: sharp cedar-pitch from the third-tier saw-pits, sour oak-liquor and lime from the bottom tanning vats, or dry dusty bird-down from the upper lofts.

Because the town never stops roaring, they have two entirely different ways of talking.

Close up, they speak mouth-to-ear, leaning in with teeth bared slightly to make the words crisp, punctuating their sentences with quick, economical flicks of the wrist. Across the open well, where the *thud-clack* of the grain-stamps and the rush of forty hollowed troughs swallow ordinary speech whole, they use a high, pitched, ringing call—clipped syllables thrown straight out of the nose and palate so the sound slices cleanly above the bass rumble of the water without getting lost in the harsh scolding of the jays. It sounds almost like singing to a stranger, a sharp, carrying yodel of warnings, curses, and bargains ricocheting off the wet slate walls.

Underneath the voices runs a quieter language of wood and cord. Two sharp tugs on a hoist-rope, a three-finger tap against a soaked lintel, a dry knuckle-rap against a living cedar trunk that travels straight up the sapwood to tell the workshop two tiers above that the flume-gate is about to open. They listen as much with the soles of their feet as with their ears. A woman scraping sheepskins on the fourth deck does not even have to look up to know when the miller’s chute jams two stories below; the steady vibration under her oak-soled boots misses a beat, and she mutters a curse before the water even begins to back up.

There are no lords in the bowl, and no flat square to build a guildhall on, yet the town is fiercely, intricately divided—not cleanly into rich and poor, but vertically, by light, damp, and what you have to breathe all day.

Up near the rim and the high sunlit boughs live the *Dry-Folk*, or the *Perchers*, as the lower decks call them behind their backs. These are the elders whose knees no longer forgive the slick lower ladders, the fine-spinners and wool-graders, the seed-keepers, the feather-sorters, and the sharp-eyed families who tend the tar-greased grain-staddles where the town’s winter barley sleeps out of reach of damp and voles. Up here the wind slams hard across the lip, the clouds roll right through the door-curtains, and on bright breaks the sun actually dries your hair. The Perchers are leaner, sharper-tongued, and famously stingy with their dry loft-space. Because they sit beside the upper flume-heads where the rim-falls are first caught and split into wooden troughs, they hold a quiet, stubborn leverage over the whole basin. If a Dry-Loft grandmother says the frost is coming early because the starlings are packing deep into the sod eaves, half the town starts salting fish before dusk.

Through the churning middle tiers—three, four, and five, where the cedar branches spread widest and the alleys knit tightest—live the great, noisy bulk of the settlement. Carpenters, sawyers, basket-braiders, hide-scrapers, nettle-retting women, rope-twisters, cooks, and goat-boys. They are impatient, loud, fiercely gossipy, and endlessly practical. They live with water dripping off the bridges above them and wood-shavings raining down on the heads below them, and their tempers are quick and shallow as mountain squalls: a screaming match over a backed-up gutter or a goat eating half a bundle of dried fiber will rattle the eaves for three minutes, and five minutes later the same two neighbors will be leaning their backs together to lever a rotted spruce joist out of a shared wall.

And down at the bottom of the bowl, in the cold green gloom where the peat-pools braid between cottage-thick buttress roots, live the *Sump-Folk*. Here are the tanners standing knee-deep in oak-bark and lime pits, the fullers working the deafening water-hammers, the charcoal-burners, the silt-dredgers, and the trout-reeves. Sunlight barely reaches them for two hours at midday; their aprons are perpetually crusted with mud, fish-scales, and fat, and their hands stay blue-chilled half the year. Outsiders might take them for the lowest caste, shoved down into the stench and shadow. Anyone who actually lives in the bowl knows better. The Sump-Folk carry the heaviest forearms, the bleakest humor, and the final word in almost every real crisis—because the entire seven-story nest rests on the charred pilings and peat-footings they keep from washing away. If the bottom screens clog, the whole basin floods; if a footing softens, three tiers of workshops sag like a wet basket. Nobody mocks a sump-dredger to his face, and when a tanner climbs up to the sunlit fifth tier for a cup of hot barley-beer, people make room near the brazier without being asked.

All of them—Percher, mid-bough carpenter, and sump-man alike—live by a strange, supple kind of law that makes no sense to flatland magistrates.

In a place where roots heave stone, damp eats dead timber in four seasons, and every roof is somebody else’s floor, nobody truly owns a piece of dead ground. What you hold is a *claim of tending*. A young couple who decide to tie their lives together are not given a parcel of earth; they are granted a *crotch*—a sturdy fork between two living cedar or spruce limbs, vetted by the older carpenters—and for three loud, mud-spattered days both families swarm the branches with adzes, wet rawhide, and green hazel saplings to sling a new sleeping-loft into the canopy. The house is never finished. It grows, twists, leaks, and tries to become a tree again every spring.

So your right to your doorway lasts only as long as your knife stays sharp and your flume runs clear. If a man grows lazy and lets wild alder suckers thicken until they crack a neighbor’s dry-stone corner, or fails to clear the liverwort choking his scupper so cold water spills onto the loom-shed underneath, there is no long court trial. The **Flume-Reeves**—four rotating wardens chosen every thaw for their stubbornness and loud voices—simply climb up with three neighbors, hack back the growth, re-wedge the gutter, and walk away with a winter ham, a bundle of tanned pelts, or a young kid-goat as *rot-fine*. Nobody argues the fine for long. To let rot spread in the bowl is like letting a spark sit in dry thatch; it is an offense against gravity itself.

That constant battle against decay has bred a few strange, revered trades that could not exist anywhere else.

There are the **Timber-Listeners**—usually old carpenters whose ears have been half-ruined for ordinary conversation by decades of fulling-hammers and rushing water. What they have lost in voices, they have gained in wood. A Timber-Listener moves slowly along the creaking tiers with a polished sheep-bone rod and a short mallet, tapping the underside of wet pilings and pressing the bone between the beam and his own jaw. Through his teeth and skull he can hear the difference between sound, sap-heavy cedar and the soft, papery sigh of inner heart-rot or the faint grinding of beetle-grubs hidden under healthy bark. When a Listener scratches a white chalk cross onto your main girder, you do not wait until Monday; you empty your loft before nightfall and call for the rope-crews.

And up in the dizzying outer reaches of the canopy work the **Tallow-Walkers**—mostly wiry, half-feral adolescents and light-boned women who seem to have been born without whatever part of the brain fears a hundred-foot drop. Barefoot or in thin, resin-rubbed felt slippers, carrying clay pots of warm pine-tar and mutton-grease slung from their waists, they scramble out along slick roof-ridges, overhanging gable-ends, and the narrow cross-poles under the grain-staddles. They grease the exposed beam-ends against the rain, re-tar the vole-guards, clear dead crows and storm-snapped twigs from the high catch-troughs, and tighten weathered willow bindings while a mountain gust whips the cedar crowns sideways around them. Down below, people glance up at a dark silhouette hanging one-handed over the mire to smear pitch on a pulley-arm, shake their heads, and go back to their work. Every Tallower eventually grows too heavy or breaks an ankle and drops down to the mid-tiers to become a rope-maker or a sawyer, carrying for the rest of their life a restless habit of perching on railings instead of sitting on stools.

Even the animals are woven into the town’s politics, not as livestock neatly penned behind fences, but as noisy, opinionated co-tenants with whom the humans maintain a dozen uneasy treaties.

The birds are both roof-thatch and calendar. Every morning in late spring, small, nimble children are cinched into hemp waist-harnesses and lowered six feet beneath the overhanging walkways to reach into the hanging clay pots where the cliff-swifts and starlings nest. They are taught the rule before they can count to ten: *take two, leave two, never break the mud-lip*. Break that rule, and the parents abandon the pot; keep it, and the flock stays thick enough to keep the midge-clouds bearable all summer and fill every quilt in the bowl with warm shed down. Down on the lower ledges, boys with flat wooden paddles scrape the thick white crust of guano off the slate overhangs into woven baskets—nothing is wasted here, for bird-dung is prized in the bottom tanning vats to soften stiff mountain hides before they hit the oak-bark liquor. And when a goshawk slices across the sky-hole and fifty thousand wings detonate off the eaves in a roaring dark storm, the bowl-folk don’t even break stride; they just pull their waxed hoods forward, hunch their shoulders against the sudden hail of droppings and shaken water, and keep hauling. Only the oldest Perchers stand still and squint up through the feathers, reading the twist of the flock to judge whether the wind above the rim is turning north.

The short-legged mountain goats have practically achieved citizenship. Each wears a notched ear or a tuft of dyed red or yellow wool braided into its beard to show whose hearth owns its milk, though they roam the slatted ramps with the arrogant calm of landlords. The people tolerate them because goats are tireless living shears, nibbling back the tender shoots of the braided hazel-railings before they grow wild enough to choke the narrow lanes. Only when an old billy develops a taste for oiled-parchment window-flaps or steals dried nettle-fiber off a weaver’s peg does someone wrestle a triangular wooden yoke around his neck so he can no longer poke his thick head through doorways.

With the darker, quicker beasts, the rules turn half-practical, half-superstitious. The sleek river-martens that patrol the wet water-flumes in broad daylight are treated like dangerous mercenaries. Nobody tries to pet a bowl-marten—they are all teeth and wet muscle and will lay a finger open to the bone—yet people quietly leave trout-heads and chicken-necks on the outer corners of the grain-lofts to keep them near, because one hungry marten clears out the bold, fat rats faster than four hearth-cats combined. The mottled cloud-frogs and cold-skinned highland salamanders that spill out of moss-chinks whenever a shutter is unlatched are never harmed; children are cuffed sharply if they poke one with a stick. The bowl-folk call them *spring-keepers*, believing that if you crush a salamander inside your walls, the cold seepage will turn spiteful, shift its channel beneath the slate, and wash out your hearth-footing before the year is out.

Snails, though, enjoy no such mercy. After a heavy afternoon squall brings the walnut-sized grey shells plastering the wet undersides of every bridge, small children swarm the lower decks with reed baskets, plucking them off the damp cedar by the hundred. Purged for two days in a crock of dry barley-meal and then simmered in iron kettles with wild mountain sorrel, thyme from the sod roofs, and smoked fish-bones, they make the thick, dark, peppery broth that keeps the damp out of everybody’s chest through the cold months. And down in the moss-rimmed catch-pools at the bottom of the bowl, where the town’s washed kitchen scraps drift through reed screens, the shoulder-to-shoulder cold-water trout belong to no single family. They are the town’s living larder. Taking a trout out of season without the Sump-Reeve’s nod is one of the few things that will get a person hauled up the trail and shoved out over the slate lip for good; come late winter, when the passes are drifted shut with snow and the smoked racks grow thin, the reeves dip the pools with willow-creels and share the fat, dark-backed fish out evenly by chimney-count.

A small, restless breed of bowl-folk leaves the hollow regularly: the **Pass-Packers**. Lean, long-legged, and quieter than their cousins, they are the ones who drive strings of shaggy pack-goats—or haul towering wicker back-frames themselves—up the moss-furry slate trail and out across the naked high passes to trade with the dry valley towns below. They carry out what the wet mountain engine makes best: dense fulled wool that shrugs off sleet, supple oak-tanned hides, barrels of smoked trout, bales of swift-down, and finely carved cedar bowls seasoned over peat-smoke. They come back sweating under loads of coarse valley salt, bars of bog-iron, flint, dried peas, and winter barley.

You can always spot a Pass-Packer on the first evening back in the bowl. After ten days under an empty, flat sky where you can walk twenty paces without brushing a neighbor’s sleeve or ducking a branch, the sudden green press of the hollow hits them sideways. They blink too much in the dim amber glow, flinch when a duck flaps past their ear, and stand awkwardly in the middle of the plank-alleys until someone grabs their shoulder and steers them out of the way of a wheelbarrow. By the second morning, once the cold drizzle has soaked back into their wool and the *thud-clack* of the water-hammers has settled back into their ribs, they stop flinching and shrink happily back into the swarm.

Even in death, the bowl’s rules hold firm. There is nowhere in the hollow to bury a body—every foot of floor is either living root-knot, bare slate, or running spring-water that feeds somebody’s kettle twenty yards downstream. So when someone’s breath finally thins out beneath the sod roofs, their kin wash them in warm thyme-water, wrap them tightly in a shroud of un-oiled wool, and carry them in a long, panting relay up the steep switchbacks, past the last dripping cedars, all the way over the jagged rim into the cold wind above the tree-line. There, on flat, sun-bleached ledges of bare grey slate where the snow melts first, they lay the body out under stones weighted just enough to hold the cloth against the gales, and leave it to the ravens, the mountain wind, and the clean high frost. Nothing heavy is buried to sour the springs; the bones are picked bright and white under the open sky, and sometimes a passing Pass-Packer will leave a twist of green cedar or a dry barley-cake on the ledge as they climb past.

What holds these people together, more than their laws or their shared troughs, is what happens when the weather closes in.

When a true mountain fog settles right down over the rim and plugs the bowl shut like a cork in a bottle, the frantic anthill energy of midday draws inward. Daylight flattens into a wet silver twilight; the clouds curl lazily right down the alleys between the cedar trunks; the thousands of birds pack themselves shoulder to shoulder under the mossy eaves, muttering softly like simmering kettles. One by one, the oiled-parchment flaps drop shut and the greased-hide windows catch fire with a deep, smoky amber as the tallow lamps and peat-braziers come alive behind them.

Inside those low, cedar-ribbed rooms, people pack in tight as the trout in the bottom pools—three generations, two wet sheepdogs, and maybe a newborn kid-goat shivering in a basket behind the hearth. The air turns thick with hot barley-mash, dried thyme, wet wool steaming off shoulders, and sharp alder-smoke. Nobody’s hands ever sit idle while they talk; an empty lap in the evening looks strange to them, almost sickly. While stories and bitter little jokes pass around the coals, knives are whittling wooden pegs, fingers are twisting soaked willow withes into repair-loops, awls are punching fresh holes in boot-leather, and spindles are whirring dry wool off distaffs.

When they make music, it is nothing like the slow, airy ballads of the valleys. A soft voice or a delicate string would be drowned instantly by the water outside, so their music locks teeth with the bowl’s own engine. Someone taps a tight-skinned frame-drum or stamps a bog-oak heel squarely onto the downbeat of the distant fulling-hammers—*thud-clack, thud-clack*—while a sharp bone flute cuts bright and shrill over the floorboards like a mountain jay, and five or six voices weave into a close, harsh, droning round that vibrates right through the cedar walls into the neighbor’s loft next door.

If you listen closely to those songs, and to the quiet superstitions murmured when the wind groans through the ancient trunks overhead, you begin to understand the only two things the bowl-folk truly dread.

The first is high fire—a stray chimney-spark riding a dry gust up into the windy sixth-tier grain-lofts, where old cedar shakes and stacked dry wool sit far above the reach of any flume. For that reason, the chimney-sweeps are treated like priests, and a child caught playing with embers gets a thrashing they remember into grey hair.

The second is silence.

A quiet bowl means something has gone terribly wrong. It means an iron-hard freeze has locked the rim-falls into hanging cliffs of ice and stopped every wheel and hammer in the dark, or a slab of wet slate has sheared off the cliff above and choked the spring-head. When the grinding, dripping, clacking uproar falters—even for ten seconds—every knife pauses over its peg, every head tilts toward the greased-hide window, and a cold prickle runs down three hundred spines at once.

So when the thaw drips steady, when the pulleys groan under crates of squealing piglets, when a neighbor shouts curses down a light-well because a goat just ate their nettle-string, and the living beams creak gently under seven stories of wet, warm, crowded life—the bowl-folk take a deep breath of cold fern and peat-smoke, adjust their waxed hoods against the next drip from above, and get back to work. They do not think of the mountain as something they have conquered, nor as a wilderness they merely pass through. They are the busy, stubborn, bark-stained thing growing inside its knuckles—lashed fast with rawhide and green willow, forever leaking, forever mending, and utterly unwilling to live anywhere dry or flat.
