To find what lives on the plateau, you have to stop looking across the flagstones and begin looking at the edges where stone meets iron, and then further down, beneath the soles of your boots.

The people who belong to this place do not inhabit the bright, sunlit expanse of the city. They inhabit its thickness—the vaulted brick galleries, dry baffle-chambers, and cork-lined cells threaded between the drainage culverts and the sunken machine wells. There are never very many of them, perhaps three or four hundred scattered across miles of tableland in small, widely separated households called bays. Because the towers above must stand far apart to keep from shaking one another to rubble, the households below are stretched thin as well, an archipelago of warm, dim rooms joined by long brick corridors and heavy double doors.

From above, you can pass within three paces of a door into their world and never know it. Their hatches sit flush in the leeward flanks of basalt buttresses or tucked beneath the aprons of viaduct piers, painted in the same dull, crusted oxide as the surrounding ironwork. Drop through one, pull the wheel-bar tight overhead, and step through the second felt-gasketed door of the acoustic lock, and the glassy singing snaps off at once. The dry roar drops away a beat later, leaving only a steady, warm, chest-deep murmur—the sound of a great ship’s hold heard from inside a blanket.

The rooms inside are low-vaulted, brick-arched, and lined from floor to spring-line with thick blocks of dark lowland cork pinned to the masonry with wooden pegs. Daylight still reaches them, though never directly; it falls through thick salt-clouded bullseyes set flush into the plazas overhead, or pours down narrow, whitewashed chutes lined with angled tin plates that bounce the broad pale sky around three baffled corners—letting light descend while breaking the back of the sound. The air smells of warm damp masonry, beeswax, dried cork, lanolin, bitter leaf-tea, and the clean, sharp scent of black graphite grease.

Everything in a bay is built against the floor’s slow, tireless tremor. Nothing hard rests directly on wood or stone. Bowls are thick-walled, flat-bottomed stoneware glazed in matte ochre, set on rings of braided felt; chair legs wear stitched rawhide boots; metal spoons are never left bare on a tabletop, because anything unanchored will slowly shudder across the grain over the course of an hour and walk itself over the edge. Children learn this before they learn to tie their own slippers, and their favorite rainy-day game—though rain barely matters here—is setting smooth travertine pebbles on an unmatted threshold slab to watch them creep and gather along the invisible lines where the stone pulls them.

In every bay, near the inner threshold, a shallow square dish of black slate sits cemented directly onto a load-bearing basalt pier. Each morning, someone pours into it a finger’s depth of clean condensate darkened with a pinch of soot. The surface of the water never sleeps; locked into the vibration of the bedrock beneath, it holds a crisp, silvered honeycomb lattice no larger than a spread hand. People glance at the slate dish when they wake the way people elsewhere look out a window at the weather. As long as the six-sided peaks stand sharp and even on the black water, the great slow engines above are in phase, the mortar in the arches overhead is safe, and the day may proceed. If the lattice begins to smear into ragged chevrons, or a slow diagonal twitch crawls across the dish, nobody finishes breakfast. Someone fetches the grease-tin, the ashwood sounding-rod, and the heavy boots.

They do not keep clocks. They have no need of them. Living inside the plateau’s masonry, their bodies tell the hour by the slow thermal drift of the city’s chord. In the cool dark before dawn—which they call the *high*—the miles of steel gantries above contract slightly, pulling the floor’s hum upward into a tight, keen tension you can feel behind the knees. As morning sun warms the iron, the pitch begins its long, gentle *giving*, sinking a quarter-tone down by mid-afternoon into the warm, heavy *slack*, before creeping upward again in the *tightening* of evening. Even in a windowless storeroom forty feet below the flagstones, a woman mending felt gaskets knows it is past noon because the note in the soles of her bare feet has gone broad and low.

The plateau shapes their bodies as patiently as it shapes its travertine. They walk with a slight, cushioned bend in the knee, weight carried forward on the balls of the feet, a silent gait learned in infancy so the stone’s tremor does not jar the spine. Indoors they wear cork-stuffed felt slippers or nothing at all. Their skin holds a warm, slightly damp sheen from the mineral air; the creases of their palms and knuckles are darkened permanently with graphite grease and pale lime-chalk, and the outer edges of their hands grow thick calluses from testing pipes, struts, and housings by touch.

They guard their children’s ears fiercely. Infants sleep in deep woollen cradles slung from braided rubber cords bolted to cork-wrapped beams, suspended free of the floor so the bass passes around them rather than through their small bones. In the quiet of the inner vaults, children grow up speaking a quick, clipped dialect pitched high enough to ride cleanly above the cellar-thrum—full of dry consonants and crisp pauses—while learning, at the very same time, the broad, unmistakable hand-speech used in the louder corridors and out on the sunlit stone. No one is allowed topside onto the open plateau until they are nearly grown, and even then only after years of learning to read metal and stone indoors.

Still, anyone who spends thirty or forty years going up onto the gantries eventually feels their outer hearing soften and draw inward. They do not call this going deaf; they say a person’s ears have *gone to bone*. It is not a misfortune, and in truth an elder whose ears have gone to bone is the finest diagnostician a bay possesses. Freed from the pain and glare of airborne noise, they listen through their palms, their soles, their ribs, and the hinge of their jaw. When a grandchild tells them a story in the evening, the child climbs onto their knee and speaks with their throat resting lightly against the elder’s bare fingers, signing lazily with one hand while the elder feels every vowel hum clean through the skin. And when a cylinder three stories underground begins to bind, it is the bone-hearing elder who walks straight into the roaring valve-well with nothing but tallow-soaked wool in their ears, presses the brass ferrule of an ashwood sounding-rod against the greased cast iron, cups the hollowed wooden butt against their cheekbone, and listens straight into the skull. A clean bronze aperture feels smooth as drawn silk; a dry worm-gear feels like fine grit caught between the molars; a travertine-choked sluice feels thick and wet, like a congested lung.

Because of this, their work looks nothing like the frantic tending of an ordinary factory. The plateau moves on the timetable of tides and seasons, and its people have learned to move with it. They do not think of themselves as masters of the great towers, nor as worshippers of them; they keep the plateau the way mountain people keep stone terraces, knowing that if the balance slips, the terraces fall on their heads. If two condenser blocks drift far enough out of phase, their overlapping waves will not merely blur the slate dishes—they will tear the mortar out of the basalt vaults, crack the cisterns, and drown the under-city in lukewarm water. Nobody alive can remember how the first stones were laid, or what grand accounting first demanded that the plateau distill its slow chalky cakes from mist and deep-ground pressure. The old linen drawings rolled in beeswaxed tin tubes show drowned galleries beneath the third level that no one has entered in five generations. That hardly matters. The city was humming when their grandmothers were swung in wool cradles; their task is simply to keep it from going sour.

This is why a stranger walking the elevated iron walkways from the upper intake basins to the southern rim can travel for hours and see no one at all. Most of the tending happens out of sight, beneath the flagstones, where the slow counterweighted cylinders descend in greased silence. When a pair of workers does need to go topside—to wipe a salt-clouded pressure gauge, pack fresh black grease onto an exterior worm-gear, chisel an overgrown travertine lip off a bronze louver, or string a new length of white porcelain between rusted arms—they prefer the cool dimness of the *high*, just before sunrise, or the fading light of evening. Dressed in heavy waxed-wool coats the exact brown of weathered iron oxide, padded hoods tied tight over beeswaxed ear-plugs, and thick-soled felt-and-rubber boots that cut them off from the stone, they move along the sheltered leeward bases of the walls rather than the open walkways. Seen from three hundred yards away across bright, hazy flagstones, two motionless figures crouched beside a valve-housing look like stacked tarpaulins or bolted iron casings.

And if an outsider does climb the old basalt road from the lowlands—which happens rarely, for the sound makes most lowland travelers dizzy and weeping within half an hour—the bays know it before the stranger has crossed the first plaza. A silhouette passes over a glass-brick light-well; an unfamiliar, hard-heeled stride sends an uneven *tap-tap-tap* down through a stone pier into the bare soles of someone sweeping a cellar floor. Nobody goes up to greet them. You cannot speak to a stranger on the open stone, and bringing an unacclimated visitor down through the locks is an exhausting business: outsiders shout without meaning to, slam felt-edged doors, drop metal on bare tables, and ask endless questions about what the noise is for. It is kinder to step behind a buttress, wait for the footsteps to drift away toward the southern spillway, and return to the worm-gear once the walkway is empty again.

Yet the people of the vaults are not wholly creatures of the dark. They have a deep, quiet hunger for the broad, pale sky above them, and for the one strange gift the plateau’s geometry offers in return for their care.

Out in the middle of the flooded plazas, where the sustained harmonics of spaced-out towers cross in exact destructive interference, lie the rare ovals of quiet. To a passing walker, they are simply odd pockets where the water goes mirror-smooth and pale ferns creep onto the wet flagstones inside a faded ring of yellow paint. To the inhabitants, those paint-rings—often drawn in double ovals, an inner line and an outer one—are old maps of a living drift. Because the plateau’s pitch drops a quarter-tone as the afternoon sun warms the steel, its wavelengths lengthen by a fraction, and every null zone creeps silently across the flagstones by two or three paces between dawn and the *slack*, then creeps back again as evening cools.

On warm afternoons, when the sun stands bright and milky overhead, people slip up from the hatches solely to sit inside those moving islands of muffled air. A pair of workers coming off a long shift beneath Condenser Four will wade across a roaring courtyard, step across the faded yellow line, and pull back their heavy hoods in unison, exhaling as the crushing weight lifts off their ribs and the sound of their own wet breathing rushes back with startling clarity. Young people from distant bays—who rarely meet in the long brick corridors—court one another inside the null zones, perched on overturned grease-buckets among the damp lichen, speaking in low, unhurried voices while ten feet away an invisible cliff of sound holds the plaza puddles in furious silver honeycombs. It is the only place on the open plateau where direct sunlight and bare human speech occupy the same air. They never leave anything behind when they go, not a cup or a wooden bench; if they linger too long as the afternoon begins its *tightening*, the quiet oval slips patiently out from under their boots, and the wall of sound nudges them back toward the hatches.

Down in the vaults, the people do not live alone. A whole quiet domestic ecology has settled into the warm, damp masonry alongside them, shaped by the same vibration and mineral water.

In the retired brick cisterns—wide, dark chambers disconnected from the main drainage grid centuries ago—the bays cultivate terraced beds of thick-fleshed, pale bracket-fungi and velvety grey mushrooms. Fed on shredded cork-dust, lowland straw, and damp sedge stalks, watered by a single calibrated copper drip-pipe tapping the warm condensate overhead, the fungus-vaults produce a dense, earthy harvest year-round. Beneath those same vaults, in the warm flooded culverts where the bedrock muffles the worst of the drone, the bays set narrow baskets of woven brass wire to trap the blind, translucent crustaceans that sift the warm silt. Dried in trays along the warm flue-pipes, they turn crisp as glass and taste faintly of chalk and rain.

Even the chalk-white, blunt-shelled snails gliding along the weeping exterior terraces have a quiet truce with the households. The inhabitants leave the snails undisturbed on the upper stone gutters, where their endless grazing keeps the amber biofilms thin and slick before they can mat into clogs; only in the lower settling basins are the largest ones gathered, simmered for hours into a rich, mineral-sweet broth, while their curiously dense shells are baked and ground into bright white lime for patching damp mortar.

Inside the cork-lined rooms themselves live the *seam-keepers*—small, pale, velvety-skinned lizards with wide, pad-tipped toes and no external ears at all, descendants of creatures that rode up generations ago in baskets of lowland cork. Entirely silent, impervious to the floor’s bass, they spend their lives patrolling the warm joints between cork blocks and brick arches, snapping up the tiny wood-boring beetles and damp-silverfish that would otherwise honey-comb the soundproofing to dust. Every household knows its own seam-keepers by the faint chalky mottling on their backs and leaves shallow brass bottle-caps of fresh water tucked into the corners of the dry storerooms for them.

And whenever a household must chisel away a decade’s growth of fluted, ear-like travertine from a valve housing so it will not choke a louver, they never throw the stone away. The cleanest brackets are sliced thin with a wet wire and filed on wooden racks in the ledger-vaults—frozen archives whose microscopic ripples record every cold winter, every storm, and every slow shift of frequency the plateau has endured. The rest—along with the dense, pale chalky cake scraped from the slow-dripping flatcars on the spur lines—makes its way to the one place on the plateau that no surface walker ever sees.

At the southern and western rims, where the tableland ends in a stepped basalt precipice, the volcanic shelf juts outward in a deep, natural overhang before plunging into the mist-choked lowlands. Tuck yourself through a low iron portal at the foot of the spillway galleries, and you step out onto narrow stone ledges hanging *beneath* the lip of the plateau.

Here, thirty feet of solid volcanic rock overhead deflects the roaring acoustic ceiling straight out into the bright sky, carving a long, curving pocket of astonishing hush directly beneath the edge of the world. Warm, saturated mist rises past the ledges all day, smelling of wet fern, distant woodsmoke, and lowland earth—moving air, real wind, soft enough to stir hair against a cheek.

In this hidden green ribbon under the stone lip, the people of the plateau tend their cliff-gardens. Packed into stone-walled troughs fed by slow trickles of spillway mist grow everything whose broad leaves would shred in an hour on the flagstones above: tangled runner-beans, bitter pepper-cress, dark cabbages, hardy herbs, and gnarled, stunted lemon trees trained flat against the warm basalt wall. At three points along the overhang stand heavy, hand-cranked timber winches bolted into the rock. Every change of season, when the lowland mists thin slightly in the morning, braided hemp cables carry iron-bound sledges loaded with mineral chalk-cake and pale, fluted travertine ears down into the unseen valley below. A day later, a sharp double tug travels up the long hemp line, and the workers lean into the windlass to haul the sledges back up into the mist—packed tight with bales of raw cork bark, unwashed wool, beeswax, rendered tallow, dried lentils, salt, and dense sticks of ashwood. Few of the plateau’s people have ever seen the hands that tie those knots far below, and they rarely wonder about them. The exchange is as slow and steady as the turning of a sluice-wheel, and needs no conversation.

It is here, too, beneath the overhang, that the great dark sky-birds finally come down to rest. High above the plateau they refuse to drop through the invisible wall of sound, circling like flecks of ash in the pale glare—but from below, rising effortlessly out of the mist-choked valleys, they glide straight in under the basalt lip on wide, flat, silent wings to roost on the dry rock spurs just past the garden ledges. They have no fear of the quiet figures pruning lemon branches or greasing the winch-drums; sometimes a gardener tosses them scraps of tough snail-foot or trimmings from the culvert-traps, which the birds catch out of the rising mist without a single flap.

When a person of the bays reaches the end of their years—their hands stained dark to the wrist with grease, their ears long since gone to bone—they are not buried in the vibrating rock. In the cool hour of the *high*, before the first warmth softens the pitch of the stone, their household washes them in warm condensate, wraps them tightly in clean, unbleached wool, and carries them down through the cork locks and out along the under-rim galleries to a wide spur of dark basalt jutting clear of the gardens, open to the silent air below the sound-wall.

There is very little singing at a parting, and no loud lament. For people who have spent their whole lives cradled inside an endless chord, music is something woven quietly *within* the plateau’s hum—two or three voices drifting in on the exact harmonic of the hour, braiding softly above the bass, and ending not on a swell, but on a shared, sudden holding of breath that lets the bare stone rush in. Out on the rim-ledge, where the basalt shields them from the roar overhead, they stand together for a long while in the soft hiss of the rising mist and the faint, distant whisper of the spillways falling past them into the clouds. Then they turn back through the low iron door, pull the felt-edged lock tight behind them, and return to the warm dark vaults—where the black water in the slate dish holds its silver lattice steady, and the floor beneath their bare feet is just beginning its long, patient bend toward noon.
