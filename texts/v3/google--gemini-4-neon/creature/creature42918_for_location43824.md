You have to stand still much longer than ten seconds to see the largest thing in the room.

At first, your eyes keep sliding right past it, the way they slide past any pale blister of wet calcite along the north seam. There is no darkness to outline a silhouette down here, no change in temperature to warn you that one smooth white hump near the floor is not stone. You only catch it because of a small wrongness in the spacing of the room: the mound beside the three thumb-sized niches looks six paces off, yet when you turn your head a fraction to watch a skip-moth blink across the ceiling, that mound is suddenly half a pace closer to the mud-brick threshold, and one of its edges has separated into five pale, blunt-tipped fingers.

It is crouching, as it almost always crouches—not out of fear, but because a body raised beneath a two-foot clearance learns to fold itself into the geometry of the dome. If it ever stood straight, it would be no taller than a slight adolescent, though nothing about it reads as young. Its spine carries the permanent, patient curve of an arch. Its skin is the matte, unglazed cream of wet chalk and drying lime across the shoulders, ribs, and shins, turning subtly glassy and half-translucent where the tendons pull tight at the wrists, throat, and ankles. In the creases behind its knees and along the hollows of its collarbones, fine mineral rime gathers like dried salt.

It wears nothing. There is nothing down here to weave, and nothing to hide from. Even its face has the stark, unweathered spareness of the walls—high, flat-planed cheekbones sharp as cleaved slate, a small mouth that almost never parts, and no hair at all, only a faint dusting of silver-white bloom along the crown of its skull that catches the sourceless glare like frost. Where its ears should cup outward to catch air, there are only tight, shallow whorls pressed flat against the bone. Air in this pocket carries almost nothing worth hearing. Instead, every few minutes, it lowers its bare heel or the flat of its jaw briefly to the wet floor, listening through its teeth to the faint mineral ticks and pressure shifts moving through the buried limestone.

Its eyes are wide-set and pale as wet chert, ringed with fine, salt-crusted lashes. Most of the time they are veiled by translucent milky inner lids that slide sideways whenever the glare sharpens before a rain, letting it watch the room through a soft opalescent haze—the exact filter needed to spot the brief yellow afterimages of skip-moths, the hairline seam about to split upward, and the faint sliding smudge of a loose shadow.

And its hands—the hands whose prints you noticed before you ever noticed their owner—are almost human. Four slender fingers and an opposable thumb, long-palmed, knuckle-heavy. Only when it lifts one to smooth a wet edge of stone do you see that it has no fingernails. In their place, each fingertip terminates in a smooth, seamless cap of glossy calcite, worn pearlescent as river glass from a lifetime of pressing unhardened rock.

It is not a prisoner of the pocket, and it is not quite a master of it either. It is its mason.

Watch it for a quarter of an hour and the little half-formed ruins scattered across the bare floor stop looking like accidents or failed mimicry. They begin to read like maintenance. The pocket does not know how to stay open. Left to itself, it extrudes inward—blister after blister of waxy chert, ridge after ridge of white quartz—slowly forgetting that it was ever hollow until it would cure shut into solid buried stone. Worse, it cannot keep track of its own inches; left unpinned, the floor bunches and stretches like wet dough, throwing distances out of joint.

So the dweller keeps reminding the room what an interior is.

When a fresh seam sweats open and the rock along it softens to the yield of wet soap, it kneels beside it, dipping its glossy fingertips into the cold mineral paste. It works with a slow, economical tenderness, pinching up a low line of six mud-bricks before the floor commits to solidity. Those bricks frame no doorway because they are not meant to be walked through; they are pins driven into the wandering floor, insisting *here is a boundary, here is an inside and an outside*. For as long as the threshold holds, the inches around it behave a little more honestly.

The shin-high arches of balanced fresh slate serve a sharper purpose. Watching the dweller build one is like watching someone set a hair-trigger snare. It selects wet, razor-edged flakes that have peeled from the ribs of the wall and leans them together at angles that ought to fail under their own weight, holding its breath—not that its breath would carry—until the last wafer settles into improbable balance. The arch is an argument for hollowness, a small dome answering the larger dome overhead. It is also an instrument. In a silent room where stone shifts without warning, that trembling balance of slate is a barometer; a heartbeat before a new ridge splits out of the floor, the arch shivers, then collapses cleanly into loose flakes, telling the dweller where the rock is about to move.

And the three thumb-sized niches pressed into the vertical lip of stone—each cradling a single glossy wet pebble—are the closest thing down here to a clock, or a song. Time under the unchanging glare loses its edges, pooling and thinning without dusk or dawn. After every downpour, when the ceiling dries and the room settles back into waiting, the dweller crouches before the north seam, lifts one wet pebble between thumb and forefinger, rolls it once across its chalky palm, and sets it gently against the rim of the next niche. A tiny, crisp *tick*—too sharp for the room to swallow—travels through the limestone and rings faintly in your back teeth. Three pebbles, three hollows, cycled in a patient tally against hours that refuse to keep their own shape.

Yet for all its stillness and craft, the dweller shares the same vulnerability as the smallest grazer crawling across the bright stone: its shadow will not stay attached.

Look a handspan to the left of its crouched hip, and you will see it—a broad, washed-out gray smudge pooled loosely on the white floor, thinned by years of raw, sourceless glare until it looks like a spill of dirty water. It lags when the dweller shifts. It drifts slightly toward wet seams when the floor tightens. And the twig-hunters know what it is.

Even a predator the length of a finger can ruin a much larger creature if it catches an unguarded edge. You see it happen while the dweller is busy burnishing the lip of a mud-brick: a slender, backward-jointed hunter streaks noiselessly out from beneath a slate flake, sensory filaments flaring, aiming straight for the ragged corner of the dweller's drifting shadow. The dweller doesn't even raise its head. It simply shifts its bare left heel half an inch along a hairline crack—folding a tiny tuck into the room's frayed inches—and its shadow snaps cleanly sideways like a wet cloth yanked out of reach. The hunter's hooked foreleg strikes bare calcite, and the predator darts away into a seam.

What is a peril while awake, however, the dweller has turned into a mercy when it needs to sleep.

There is no night in this pocket, and no eyelid—even doubled—can entirely shut out noon sealed underground. When exhaustion gathers in its long forearms, the dweller curls tight against the damp curve of the north wall, knees tucked under its chin, and begins a slow, subtle dance of posture, tilting a shoulder, shifting a hip, coaxing its loose gray shadow across the stone the way one might herd a puddle with a flat hand. Once the smudge slides smoothly across its own curled face and chest, or settles squarely beneath a flat flake of slate beside it, the dweller lays one smooth calcite fingertip gently down upon the shadow's center.

Not the sharp pierce of a hunter's hook—just a quiet, deliberate weight.

Instantly, the locking stillness takes it. Its faint pulse drops to almost nothing. Its skin cools to the exact cellar chill of the surrounding limestone, and the chalky bloom across its ribs thickens until it is indistinguishable from a fresh mineral blister. While it rests inside that self-pinned quiet, the border between its body and the unfinished room softens. Its lower hip sinks a fraction of an inch into the soap-soft floor, committing gently to the rock. Hair-thin segmented stone-worms glide effortlessly up through the milky calcite, slipping across the boundary from mineral to skin to strip loose salt-crust and dried flakes from its sleeping shins before retreating back into solid stone without leaving a pinhole behind.

Only when the air tightens before the next rain does its fingertip lift from the shadow's edge. The stillness breaks. The dweller peels its hip loose from the damp floor with that faint, sticky whisper—the same sound your boot sole makes lifting from wet stone—leaving a shallow impression that the limestone immediately smooths away.

When the rain finally comes, the slow mason vanishes, and you see how thoroughly this creature belongs to the pocket's frantic, soundless pulse.

As the glare sharpens by a fraction of a degree and the sweating ribs of the ceiling swell with hanging drops, the dweller's milky inner lids slide open, revealing pale, sharp-focused irises. It does not scramble across the blinding open floor. It reads the room's fractured distances the way a fish reads eddies—stepping once along a wet seam where six paces collapse into two, arriving cleanly beneath the narrow localized downpour while the rock a yard away remains merely clammy.

It tilts its bare face upward, mouth parting just enough to catch the slow, dandelion-drift drops before they can vanish mid-air. It moves with astonishing, silent economy through the five-minute bloom. With a thin wafer of slate, it skims a glistening ribbon of translucent grazers from an extruding seam—never taking the whole seam, always leaving a silver thread behind to breed back. It plucks the clear jelly beads from the tips of needle-thin stalks, and reaches out between its own mud-bricks to gather the hovering skip-moth eggs right out of empty air, rolling the suspended beads against its palate until they burst cold and bright; for a heartbeat afterward, a faint afterimage of cool yellow light glows through the translucent skin of its cheeks.

With the milk-pale amphibians it keeps a quiet, unhurried truce. It never steps on the cool wet spots where one has folded itself flat against the stone. Once, you watch it lift a freshly unfurled amphibian gently onto its smooth palm and carry it three paces—which take only a single step—to set it beside a newly weeping crack, letting the creature exhale its coin-sized patch of bait-vapor onto the bare floor while the dweller waits patiently a handspan back to share whatever grazers come to drink.

Then the ceiling dries. Somewhere beneath the floor, pressure gathers, and a fresh blister of white calcite pushes cleanly upward—*tick*—tilting the balanced slate arch until it dissolves into loose flakes across the wet rock. The grazers pour back into seams too narrow to hold them. The amphibians crease neatly down their spines and vanish into puddles.

The dweller shows no dismay at its ruined arch. It simply kneels beside the new white bulge while the stone is still cold and yielding as soap, wipes a swipe of fresh lime across its lower lip to feed the chalk in its own bones, and begins pinching the next brick before the floor remembers how to harden.

It has known you were here since the moment you dropped through the split in the clay.

To a creature shaped by this sealed, selective quiet, you are an astonishing, clumsy storm. Even though the pocket swallows your breath an inch past your lips, your boots peel loudly from the damp stone every time you shift your weight; your blood moves hot and heavy enough to tremble faintly through the limestone beneath your heels; you smell sharply of surface dirt, iron, and green things that grow under a real sun.

It does not bristle, and it does not flee. There is nowhere to flee to in a cellar that holds all its own inches tightly inside itself. Instead, it watches you from across the glare—three paces away, then six, then four without ever appearing to walk—its smooth head tilted slightly, pale eyes fixed not on your face, nor on your hands, but on the bare white stone behind your boots.

Because you have brought something down through the clay that this room has not seen in a very long time.

Pooled clumsily half a pace behind your heels, sliding loose across the wet calcite whenever you turn, lies a real shadow. Not one of the pale, bleached-out smudges native to the white glare, but a thick, heavy spill of genuine dark, forged by sunlight and open sky, utterly untended.

You don't even notice when a twig-hunter spots it—a pale, peeled-willow streak shooting noiselessly out from behind the mud-brick threshold, hooked forelegs raised to pin the dark fringe trailing off your left calf. You have no idea you are half a second from feeling your leg lock rigid as limestone.

The dweller moves. One smooth step along a damp seam collapses the distance between you without stirring a whisper of air. It does not touch you—you are far too warm, far too loud, too solidly committed to your own shape. It simply crouches a foot behind your boot, extends one pearlescent, calcite-tipped finger, and flicks a single bead of condensation directly across the hunter's path, scattering the predator back into a crack.

Then it lingers there for two slow heartbeats, hovering its smooth, chalk-dusted palm an inch above the spilled pool of your shadow—marveling at the weight of it, the dense, unbleached richness of a darkness that came from somewhere with a sky. Slowly, cautiously, it lifts one small wet flake of slate from the floor and touches it with feather-light curiosity against the outermost rim of your shadow's sleeve.

Up in your left hand, two feet away and completely untouched, your little finger goes suddenly, inexplicably cold and still as wet chalk—a quiet measure of your weight, a brief, wordless greeting.

Then the dweller lifts the slate away. Your finger warms and loosens. Without looking back at your face, the pale figure rises to its crouched half-height, steps once across a hairline seam, and is suddenly six paces off beside the north wall, setting a glossy wet pebble back into its thumb-sized niche with a tiny mineral *tick*, before folding down over the wet stone to begin building its room all over again.
