If you stand on the bolted steel catwalk for ten minutes, you will leave convinced that nothing larger than a palm lives here. The hum of the oil-cooled transformers presses against your skull, the steam curtain keeps shifting in the shafts of sunlight, and every lump of moss-draped stone or corroded iron looks thoroughly dead.

You have to stay past the third or fourth stroke of the reciprocating pumps—long enough for your own footsteps to fade from the metal—before one of the rounded, rust-streaked mounds wedged beneath the copper manifolds decides to move.

Even then, it does not dart or startle. It waits for the top of the stroke, when the hydraulic whine peaks and the iron latch throws with its cavernous *clank*. As the brief shower of droplets shakes loose from every fern frond in the cleft, the mound lifts two inches off the wet concrete, rotates slightly with the unhurried weight of a submerged boulder, and settles a hand's-breadth closer to the sweating flange of a pipe.

There are three of them living in the nave right now, moving among seven or eight older shapes that no longer move at all.

***

At first glance they seem made of the same materials as the machines and the fractured rock, and in a literal sense they are.

Each one has a broad, hunched body—the largest nearly the bulk of a crouching human, though flattened and low-slung, with no proper neck and limbs far too long and thick-jointed for anything that walks on open ground. Their undersides are pale, scaleless, and perpetually slick, thin enough at the throat and flanks that a dark, sluggish pulse shows through when they climb. Beneath the overhanging eaves of their ribs sit pleated, pinkish gill-frills that stay wet in the steam and flush deeper red whenever they venture high toward the thinner air near the rim.

Their backs are another matter entirely.

The skin across their shoulders, spines, and blunt skulls is pitted and porous, secreting a faint, binding slime that traps whatever precipitates out of the geothermal mist. They do not shed it. Season by season, by fractions of a millimeter, the basin builds itself onto them: pale terraces of lime-chalk, ridges of orange iron-rime, and broad, vivid washes of copper verdigris where they have slept beneath the dripping manifolds. Rubbery mountain liverworts and cushions of lime-green sphagnum anchor into the crevices along their backs. To touch one from above would feel like laying a palm on wet, upholstered slag—cold-edged with moss on top, blood-warm underneath where the living hide meets the crust.

They have no external ears; in a stone pocket this small and loud, open ears would be an injury. Their eyes are dark, lidless, and set deep beneath the chalky overhang of their brows, covered by thick clear lenses that shed falling drops like oiled glass. Their mouths are wide and blunt, housing a heavy, ribbon-like tongue blackened with iron deposits and rough as a worn file. When they climb the sheer basalt or haul themselves along the steel tendons of the pressure vessel, they grip with wide, splayed pads soft as wet chamois, tipped with blunt nails hardened to dull chisels.

***

Because the steam hiss and the slap of runoff swallow anything spoken into the air, they do not use voices. They live inside the iron.

Every bolted frame, copper manifold, concrete plinth, and steel grating in the cleft carries a continuous map of vibration, and their flat lower jaws and calcified ribs are built to read it. When one presses its chin or a broad palm against the riveted flank of the pressure vessel, it does not merely feel warmth; it feels the slow rising stress inside the tank, the dry whisper of a valve seat thirty feet away, the exact cadence of the grease-dark piston rising through its collar under the overhang.

Their own lives are braided into that two-minute cycle. They rarely shift during the quiet, straining climb of the pump; they time their heaviest movements to the echoing *clank* at the top of the stroke, and they speak to one another in the warm, exhaling trough that follows.

Just as the sigh of exhaust rolls out across the sphagnum, one of them will tap a measured phrase against a flanged copper joint with a mineral-hardened knuckle, or press its throat-sac straight against the ribbed casing of a transformer and send a slow, humming pulse down into the plinth. Twenty feet away, tucked under the overhang, another feels the message rise through the damp bolts into its palms. It is a patient, sparse language—mostly markers of presence, of territory, of shifts in the heat—spaced across minutes the way birds in the valleys below space notes across seconds.

***

The three residents divide the cathedral-sized hollow less by aggression than by weight.

The middle one is the climber—broad-shouldered, still agile under a neat, tight armor of fresh lime and ochre rust not yet burdened by ferns. Without knowing what pressure, current, or corrosion mean, it is the reason the machines have not choked and died. Its body craves the exact things that would otherwise seal the basin solid.

Every few days it hauls itself through the waxy fern fronds to the brass bleed-valve weeping its slow, rusty trickle. When the chalky crust thickens enough to narrow the flow, the creature hooks a blunt chisel-nail into the rim and worries the mineral plug loose with its iron-black tongue, lapping at the warm, metallic water until the trickle runs clear and steady again. It follows the slow cycles of the reciprocating pumps, waiting with geological patience for the piston to pause at the bottom of its descent so it can rasp away the dark, oil-fed fungal film blooming around the collar—polishing the steel gleaming and grease-dark while it feeds. Up along the bolted steel ribs and porcelain insulators, it browses the pale, tender tips of aerial roots and the crisp young fern crosiers that grow richest in the dry electrical warmth near the coils, trimming back the heavy woody runners that would otherwise pry apart the flanges while leaving the soft, wet moss untouched.

Sometimes it lingers for an hour beside one of the heavy drab moths resting on a warm junction box, both of them vibrating at the exact frequency of the copper windings inside, before a pale, broad tongue flicks out and the moth is gone without breaking the stillness.

The oldest one rarely climbs above the level of the catwalk anymore. Its carapace is an archive of decades: thick basal plates of bone-white chalk from the lower catch-basins, layered bands of orange iron-oxide from long years near the weep-valves, and a whole left shoulder glazed brilliant blue-green from sleeping under the sweating manifolds. A small cluster of dark-emerald baby ferns has taken root along its spine, bobbing faintly with each slow heave of its flanks. It moves at nearly the pace of the pumps themselves, taking an entire afternoon to shift from one simmering stone pan to the next. In the lukewarm water beneath the condensers, it settles until only its mossy ridge breaks the surface, letting the slow bubbles drift across its brow while tiny metallic beetles wander over its dry upper crust as though it were just another shelf of basalt.

The youngest is barely the size of a curled hound. Its back carries only a thin, frosted rime of pale chalk along the spine, leaving its skin too soft and translucent to bear the harsh high-altitude daylight. It keeps to the crowded green shadow beneath the steel grating and the dripping curtains of aerial roots. Unlike its elders, it still has curiosity to spend. Now and then it creeps up the wet rock ribs to the steel I-beam and taps a fingernail against the motionless, rusted links of the chain hoist, sending an erratic metallic clatter through the overhang until a sharp, flat double-knock rings up through the copper pipes from the elder below. More often it perches on a sodden peat ledge beside the solitary analog pressure gauge, watching the tiny red-tipped needle tremble behind its half-moon of condensed water—the only quick, nervous movement in the entire basin—while its own pale gill-frills flutter in faint, unconscious sympathy.

***

Down in the quietest, lukewarm catch-basins beneath the condenser manifolds, those small pale amphibians with translucent skin cling motionless to the undersides of wet stones, their throats pulsing slowly in the mineral water.

They are not another species. They are the waiting ones.

Once every twenty or thirty years, when a slight surge in the geothermal fissures shifts the heat and salt of the deepest pans, two of the adults settle side by side in the simmering water, locking their mossy backs together like neighboring boulders while pressing their soft, un-crusted undersides close. The tough, mineral-tinted eggs they anchor beneath the submerged rock hatch into those tiny, translucent swimmers—and there the young stay.

A pocket barely the size of a cathedral nave can feed only so much weight. There are only so many yards of sweating copper, so much oily film, so many warm pools. So the hatchlings do not grow. They hold themselves suspended under the stones for years, sometimes for decades, feeding on yellow algae film and microscopic grit, grazing gently along the sheltered gill-frills of the oldest resident whenever it lowers itself into their basin to soak.

They are waiting for a silence.

Because accumulation here never stops. Season after season, the mineral steam adds another pale skin of lime and rust to the oldest one's back, thickening the terraces, stiffening the joints where shell meets shoulder. Eventually a season comes when it can no longer hoist its armored bulk over the lip of a stone pan. It works itself slowly into a familiar recess—wedged between the steel tendons of the pressure vessel, or tucked against the split concrete plinth beneath the transformers—and tucks its pale limbs tightly underneath the overhanging eaves of its shell.

It stops knocking on the copper pipes. Its breath slows, stretches across two pump strokes, then five, then ceases altogether.

Nothing scavenges it; there is nothing large enough to break the crust. Over the following years, the dripping mist seals its edges seamlessly to the basalt, burying the tucked chamois pads under pale chalk and orange iron-rime while the ferns on its spine keep growing right out of its ridge. That is what those seven or eight rounded, oddly symmetrical humps tucked around the bases of the plinths and manifolds are—not spilled concrete, not rounded boulders in a cleft that has none, but the previous tenants, turned slowly into part of the engine-room's architecture.

Down beneath the wet stones of the catch-basins, the waiting amphibians register the change within weeks. One heavy, familiar rhythm has vanished from the floor; an ungrazed stretch of pipe begins to bloom thick with sweet iron-slime and chalk crust, altering the faint taste of the runoff trickling into their pans. Under one dark basalt slab, a translucent throat begins to pulse faster. Its jaw broadens; its soft pads thicken; iron darkens the tips of its toes. On a misty morning, it climbs out of the bath-warm water onto the razor-edged stone, turns its bare back to the tepid steam, and begins collecting its first white film of lime.

***

They know the edges of their world intimately, and they treat each boundary with its own quiet gravity.

When the crisp, hard shafts of high-altitude daylight slice past the serrated rim and drag bright geometric bands down one wet wall and up the other, the two older ones crawl deliberately into the beam. They tuck their pale limbs, blunt noses, and vulnerable gill-frills completely under the wide stone eaves of their backs and hold motionless for an hour. The thin-air UV would blister their undersides in minutes, yet it feeds the thick coats of sphagnum and liverwort anchored to their carapaces; under the hard light their moss turns a fierce, glowing lime-green, and tiny threads of steam curl off their heated iron-rimmed backs until the band of sun moves on and leaves them dripping in the green shade.

When the mountain wind occasionally tears across the peak hard enough to shear away the steam blanket for an hour—making the rusted chain hoist swing and clink in a wild, broken rhythm—all three retreat into the deepest simmering pans and wait underwater, unsettled by the sudden cold and the disorder in the sound, until the mist settles back into the hollow and the two-minute heartbeat of the pumps reclaims the air.

On clear, windless afternoons, the middle one sometimes hauls itself up onto the upholstered moss lip right behind the jagged natural parapet. It never crosses the edge; a step beyond the trailing hem of warm vapor lies air thin enough to dry its gills and starve its lungs, and a silence so vast it feels like falling. Safely anchored inside the mist, it rests its blunt chin on the sphagnum and gazes out with wet, lidless eyes over the blinding white deck of cloud-tops miles below, moving so slowly they look like an endless, frozen floor of pale chalk. If a dark wing-shadow slices across the mist overhead, it does not scramble back—it simply flattens its legs beneath its overhang and becomes a ledge of mossy stone until the shadow is gone.

And at night, when the narrow slot of sky turns black and sharp with stars and the mist thins overhead, the oldest one sometimes drags itself across the damp concrete beneath the overhang to where the thick, moss-wrapped power cables lift off their porcelain insulators and vanish into the neatly bored circular tunnel in the back cliff wall.

None of them has ever entered that round, dry dark. The faint draft moving through the bore smells cold and mineral-dead, stripped of steam and living green. Yet the oldest one will rest a single wide, soft palm flat against the wet armor of a cable and hold still there for an hour, feeling the steady, relentless charge pouring down into the mountain's dark interior—answering the hum in its ribs, listening to whatever far-down stillness drinks the power they keep alive, while behind it the piston rises, throws its latch with an echoing *clank*, sighs warmly into the ferns, and begins its slow descent all over again.
