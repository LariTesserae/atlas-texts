At a distance, standing on an upper residential terrace or leaning over the cast-iron rail of a cable-dock, they are easy to mistake for ordinary humans—upright, slender, clothed in pale layered garments that catch the clean afternoon light. It is only when you pass one in the narrow shade of a vitrified-brick alley, close enough to hear the faint papery brush of their sleeves and smell the warm, crisp scent of chalk and hot ozone that clings to them, that you realize they belong to the cliffs as completely as the drifters and the grazers do.

They have no wet skin, no sweat, no pulse of liquid blood. Their bodies are dry, warm, and intricately jointed—built of smooth, close-fitting dermal plates that have the matte, fine-grained look of unglazed stoneware or sun-burnished travertine. Along the shoulders, forearms, and cheekbones the plates overlap cleanly, leaving narrow, supple pleats of mineral-fiber membrane at the elbows, wrists, and throat that fold and unfold with a whisper so soft you can barely hear it over the substation hum. Their coloring is the quiet palette of the escarpment itself, though no two wards share quite the same shade. A worker who has spent forty years in the lower copper-refining terraces carries a faint, dignified sheen of verdigris along the edges of their collar-plates and knuckles; a glass-annealer from the upper alcoves is pale as dry gypsum, with faint amber seams; the iron-sifters are dark as oiled shale, their fingers permanently dusted with metallic soot that buffs their outer plates to a dull pewter gleam.

Their faces are sculpted and calm, mobile in ways that take a moment to read. Their eyes are deep-set lenses of dark smoky amber or polished quartz, shielded from the dry wind by thin, translucent shutters that blink sideways with the faintest dry rustle. Where hair might grow on a creature of a wetter world, they have fine, sparse fringes of translucent silica-filaments along the brow, the backs of the wrists, and the nape of the neck. These filaments are exquisitely sensitive to the invisible geography of the cities. Walk beside a citizen down a narrow foundry lane and you will see the delicate threads along their temples lift and lean gently toward a live conduit buried behind the brick, or settle flat when they step into the insulated quiet of a shaded courtyard.

They breathe slowly—once every twelve or fifteen seconds—drawing warm, dry air into pressurized chambers within their ribs to feed the slow electrochemical reactions that keep their resins supple and their mineral tendons taut. When they speak, there is no wet slur of tongue or saliva. Their voices come from dry lamellar reeds tucked inside resonant chambers of hollow silica in the throat, shaped by articulated lip-plates. It is a quiet, luminous sound: papery consonants, clear ceramic clicks, and low, flute-like vowels that carry effortlessly through the still air without ever needing to be raised. To hear two neighbors conversing across a shaded alley is like listening to dry reeds stirring in a warm breeze, punctuated by the soft tap of porcelain cups.

They are never in a hurry.

To them, haste is neither admirable nor sensible; it is simply bad engineering. Move too quickly in this dry air and erratic static gathers along your joints until it arcs painfully against the nearest iron rail. Rush the cooling of a sintered glass beam and four days of labor shatter into worthless grit. Lean hastily against a shadowed parapet without first tapping the stone with a copper-tipped staff, and you may wake a motionless stalker tucked into the fluted overhang. Their gait is long-strided, gliding, and wonderfully economical—a rolling step on thick ceramic-soled sandals that makes only a muted, rhythmic *clack* along the fused-slag paving.

Their hands, long-fingered and tipped with slightly denser pads of quartz-hardened horn, move with the same unhurried certainty. You see it in the way they greet one another. Because different wards sit at different electrical potentials—the low smelting yards humming with raw leakage from the strain-seams, the high residential galleries heavily insulated in dry gypsum—two people meeting after a morning apart rarely share the same charge. So they greet by touching fingertips, or pressing the backs of their knuckles lightly together. A tiny, bright *snap* jumps cleanly between them, balancing the voltage in both bodies. Among strangers it is a brisk, polite touch at wrist’s length; among close friends or kin it lingers, palm against palm, until the fine filaments along their forearms settle flat in quiet equilibrium. Lovers walking the high promenades at the end of a shift will often twine their fingers for half an hour at a time, letting their bodies share a single steady potential as they watch the amber kites wheel over the three-mile drop.

---

They do not drink, and they have no word for thirst as a creature of water would understand it. Instead, they know two distinct hungers: the dry, hollow lightness of depleted mineral salts, and the dull, stiffening fatigue of a low charge.

To feed the first, the towns cultivate the cliffs.

Clinging to the sheer, shaded flanks of the escarpment below the residential wards are the bloom-terraces—narrow, stone-walled ledges strung with delicate lattices of braided copper wire and dark porous slate. Here, the salt-farmers work in absolute quiet, tuning the weak, steady voltage bled from the city’s secondary transformers. Where the tuned current meets the drifting mineral haze of the chasm, crystalline crusts grow out of the dry air itself: crisp fronds of sweet gypsum-frost, tart verdigris curls of copper salt, rich crusts of iron oxide, and sharp, pungent needles of canary-yellow sulfur. The farmers move slowly along the ledges on counterweighted harnesses, scraping the ripe crusts into shallow glazed pans with broad horn knives, careful never to strip a slate completely bare so the seed-crystals remain to draw the next week’s growth.

In the sunlit markets of the upper promenades, those harvests are pressed and baked on quiet resistive griddles into dry, layered cakes—pale chalky wafers veined with green and gold, brittle savory tiles of roasted shale-salt, and small sealed jars of clarified amber-resin tapped from domesticated drifters or rendered cleanly from the fallen. A citizen takes a meal unhurriedly, breaking off dry fragments and grinding them thoroughly with hardened molar-plates before swallowing, often pausing to inhale a slow breath of warm air that makes the salts crackle faintly with mild internal heat.

To answer the second hunger, every neighborhood has its grounding parlors.

They are the warm, quiet hearts of the residential wards—vaulted chambers of smooth soapstone and vitrified brick tucked deep into the wind-carved alcoves, far from the dust of the thoroughfares. Down the center of each parlor runs a thick, polished pillar of forged copper driven straight down into the live quartz seam beneath the floor. Radiating outward from the pillar are low stone benches inlaid with braided brass strips and smooth ceramic hand-rests.

At the end of a working shift, citizens slip off their outer dust-cloaks and unlace their insulated sandals, stepping barefoot onto the warm stone. They sit quietly along the benches, resting their bare heels on the brass inlays and cupping their palms around the warm porcelain-sheathed conduits. A gentle, regulated trickle of the mesa’s geological voltage flows up through the soles of their feet and along their spines. You can watch the weariness leave them: the tiny dry creak in their pleated shoulders eases, their posture lengthens, the fine filaments along their brows lift in a soft, rhythmic halo, and their eyes half-close behind their translucent shutters. Nobody speaks loudly in a grounding parlor. A few citizens share trays of spiced sulfur-crumbs; two elders sit in silence over a slate board, moving heavy pieces of fused glass and copper once every ten minutes; from a corner alcove, someone draws a slow, resonant hum from a set of tuned metallic-shale bars, letting the notes beat gently against the steady subterranean drone of the pillar.

---

Their work is shaped by the same vast, patient physics that shapes the ledges around them.

Down in the sintering yards and transformer houses, the industrial workers wear heavy, knee-length aprons woven from spun glass-fiber and sewn with overlapping tiles of white ceramic—both to shed stray arcs and to deflect the scorching radiant heat of the resistive kilns. Fine braided copper chains trail from the heels of their sandals, dragging lightly across the slag paving with a soft metallic whisper to bleed off any excess charge before it can build to a dangerous snap. Pinned to their collars are tiny quartz tuning-prongs no longer than a finger; when a worker steps too close to an unshielded busbar or a spiking rock-seam, the prong begins to sing in a clear, high mosquito-tone, warning them back long before the air smells scorched.

Higher up, along the wind-planed lips of the mesas, work the sail-minders and cable-keepers. They are leaner and more nimble than the foundry folk, dressed in tight, pleated tunics of cured drifter-membrane that shed the ceaseless updraft without snagging. They climb the colossal thirty-yard torsion towers with slow, deliberate grace, greasing the iron gear-houses with dry powdered graphite, checking the canvas-and-slate vanes for grit abrasion, and leaning far out over miles of sunlit emptiness with long, ceramic-tipped gaffs to gently nudge away any overly curious drifter whose charged trailing filaments threaten to foul the slow cable-wheels.

They do not view the dry fauna of the cliffs as pests, nor as wild beasts to be conquered, but as fellow tenants of the same spare mineral economy.

Nearly every courtyard and terraced roof has its resident grazers—two or three broad-backed creatures the size of serving platters, their mica-and-horn shells polished smooth by generations of domestic care. Children paint tiny ochre spirals on their carapaces so they can tell them apart, and leave them to creep slowly across the brick walls and slag alleys, where their quartz-tipped mouths rasp away stray static-crusts that would otherwise short out a doorway conduit or foul a shutter track. On the narrow windowsills of the residential alcoves, families scatter pinches of fine iron filings for the delicate, porcelain-like locusts that live beneath the ceramic insulators; in return, the locusts weave their gossamer conductive webs across the louvered vents, catching drifting dust-fleas and bleeding harmless sparks before they can enter the rooms. Even the drifters are harvested with a patient courtesy. Along the windward balustrades, membrane-cutters wait with woven nets not to slaughter the soaring flocks, but to catch the older, heavier kites whose metabolic bladders have begun to lose buoyancy at the end of their natural drift, or simply to trim a yard of trailing silk-filament as a broad amber kite glides peacefully past a public landing.

Only the stalkers—the motionless, hollow-boned predators tucked into the high fluted shadows—are met with steady vigilance. Children are taught from their first steps on the terraces to watch for the faint, wrong angle of a shadowed bracket, to tap any dark overhang with their staff, and never to linger near the parapet when a drifter sails close enough to draw a hunter out of the stone.

---

All of life in the wards breathes in time with the seven-day cycle of the air.

During the four rising days, when the deep invisible basins warm and the great thermal updraft climbs past the ledges, the towns open outward. Shutters are folded back into their stone recesses. The great wind-wheels turn their slow, forty-second arcs; the cable-gondolas creep out across the golden haze toward the sister cities on the opposite escarpment; the bloom-farmers descend on their ropes to tend the vertical cliff-gardens. The broad sunlit promenades fill with quiet movement—citizens carrying rolled bolts of mineral-cloth, trays of annealed optical quartz, and baskets of freshly harvested salt-cakes, while pale-amber kites and chalk-white bladders drift lazily beneath the iron railings just an arm’s length away.

Then the gradient slackens, and the three settling days begin.

The wind-wheels ease to a standstill with a final, dry *tock* of their weighted ratchets. The great flocks of drifters sink slowly out of sight into the warmer lower bands of the abyss. A fine, luminous veil of ochre grit begins to drift straight downward through the clear midday light, dusting every horizontal rail, insulator, and parapet with a soft mineral frost.

Across every ward, wooden-framed louvers of laminated mineral fiber are drawn neatly shut. Citizens wrap fine filigreed veils of spun glass-silk around their lower faces and tuck their amber eye-shutters half-closed as they finish their errands, retreating from the open cliff-ledges into the vaulted interior galleries and covered stairways. The transformers hum a shade louder in the sudden, thick calm.

These three quiet days belong to indoor precision and to one another. In sealed, dust-free workshops lit by cool recessed arc-lamps, the opticians grind quartz lenses, the coil-winders braid gossamer copper wire around ceramic cores, and the scribes incise records onto thin slabs of dark shale or ink them in iron-gall—no, iron-oxide and thin resin—onto sheets of cured drifter-parchment. In the residential alleys, lanterns of glowing resistive filament hang beneath the brick arches, casting a steady amber warmth across the shuttered doors.

It is during the settling days that the towns make music. They gather in the high, echoing chambers carved deep into the ancient alcoves, where the acoustics are bone-dry and astonishingly pure. Players strike suspended bars of tuned metallic shale and vitrified glass with padded horn mallets; others pluck taut wires of phosphor-bronze stretched over hollow resonance boxes of cured silica. Above the ringing mineral notes rises the chorus—dozens of citizens singing without words, sustaining long, papery, flute-like tones that twine together into slow harmonies. The notes do not swell dramatically or crash to a climax; they shift by tiny, patient intervals, rubbing gently against the deep, unceasing bass drone of the continental shelf humming through the stone floor beneath their bare heels.

---

They come into the world as cleanly and dryly as everything else upon the shelves.

A child begins as a dense, palm-sized ovoid of pale calcified shell, nourished for months inside a warm, sheltered hollow beneath a parent’s lower rib-plates before being laid in a household warming-niche—a small, brick-lined alcove beside the home’s grounding transformer, kept at a gentle, unwavering resistive heat. Both parents, and often older siblings and aunts, rest their hands against the warm clay wall of the niche each evening, letting a soft trickle of personal charge pass through the brickwork into the developing shell.

When the shell finally parts with a crisp, dry *tock*, the newborn that climbs out is astonishingly light—all long, nimble fingers, wide amber eyes, and translucent, porcelain-thin plates that show the faint glowing geometry of its internal resin-chambers in direct sunlight. Children are the only creatures in the settlements that move quickly. For their first decade they are pale, jittery, and bright with excess static, darting up the covered stairways and scrambling along the mesh-screened roofs like the little porcelain locusts, their silica brow-filaments perpetually tufted upright with curiosity. Elders watch them from the shaded benches with dry, clicking amusement, warning them gently not to ground themselves carelessly on the Foundry Ward’s return rails.

As the decades pass, a citizen does not wrinkle or sag. They accrete.

With each year of mineral salts and quiet piezoelectric current, their dermal plates thicken microscopically, gaining richer mineral color and a denser, more resonant weight. Fine hairline rings—almost invisible except in raking afternoon light—mark the edges of their shoulder-plates and forearms, recording seasons of lean dust or rich copper harvests the way stone strata record an age. Their movements slow even further; their voices drop into deeper, warmer reed-tones; their internal resins grow richer and darker, like honey thickening toward solid amber.

And their sensitivity to the rock deepens into something extraordinary.

The oldest citizens of any ward—those who have lived a century or more and whose joints have begun to stiffen with accumulated mineral—often retire from the foundries and looms to become Listeners. Wrapped in heavy, pale cloaks of pleated membrane, they sit for days at a time in small, bare chambers cut directly into the raw escarpment where the great copper grounding spikes enter the cliff. They press their bare heels, palms, and foreheads against the smooth, sun-warmed stone and close their translucent eye-shutters.

A human hand pressed to the mesa feels only motionless rock. A Listener feels the continent moving.

Through the dense quartz-rich plates of their own aging bones, they feel the immense, silent weight of miles of ancient sandstone pressing downward, slumping a fraction of a millimeter every ten years. They feel the vast, slow tide of piezoelectric pressure building along a buried fault forty miles away, long before any brass dial in the substation twitches. They can tell a ward’s engineers when a strain-seam is quietly shifting north, when a new copper spike must be driven before an old junction starves, or when a deep tremor—so slow it takes three weeks to pass—is rolling down the continental shelf from the unseen higher horizons. To sit beside a Listener in one of the rock-cut alcoves is to feel how little separates the builders of the towns from the cliffs that hold them.

When a citizen’s long life reaches its close, there is no sickness of rot, no fever, no dreadful loosening of the flesh. Simply, over a season of settling days, the internal charge refuses to hold as long as it used to. The warm elastic pull of the mineral tendons relaxes. The thickened resins inside their hollow silica frames cool and set into clear, solid dark amber. One quiet afternoon, resting on a stone bench in the family gallery with kin holding their hands to share the last tiny balance of potential, the slow breath ceases.

What remains is light, clean, dry, and immaculate—an intricate figure of warm stone-colored plate and gleaming amber seams, weighing barely more than an armful of dry chalk.

For a generation or two, many families keep their dead close. Seated respectfully in a quiet, shaded niche of the upper residential alcove, dressed in a fresh folded mantle of chalk-white membrane, an ancestor remains part of the household’s stillness. When the four-day updraft blows gently through the louvered shutters, air passes softly through their hollow chest-chambers, producing a faint, recognizable chord—a breath of dry music that tells the grandchildren in the next room that the wind has turned and the cableways are moving again.

Only after decades, when the oldest outer plates begin to craze softly into fine, clean flakes, does the family carry the ancestor up to the high Wind-Galleries—long, open colonnades carved into the very lip of the topmost overhang, facing the sunlit abyss. There, beneath the smooth fluted rock, hundreds of silent figures sit in serene rows overlooking the stack of horizons, slowly surrendering their thin mineral plates and gleaming silica dust to the warm rising air. Nothing is wasted, and nothing is mourned with horror. What is densest and most precious—a small knuckle of polished quartz-bone, a collar-plate richly veined with copper from a lifetime in the yards—is sometimes gently removed beforehand and ground into the glaze of a household lamp or set into the foundation brick of a new warming-niche. The rest lifts, flake by clean flake, into the golden haze, where the drifting amber kites sail past and the ionized dust settles once more upon the sunlit stone.

---

Stand on an upper balcony at the close of the third settling day, when the ochre veil finally thins and the clear air brightens over the gulf, and you can see them emerging onto the terraces by the hundreds.

They brush the fine red-gold grit cleanly from the iron railings with flat palms. Children crouch by the parapets, whispering dryly to the first chalk-white bladders rising from the warm depths below. Along the cliff-edge, the sail-minders release the weighted ratchets, and with a soft, distant sigh of fabric, the colossal wind-wheels begin to turn once more. Two old foundry workers meet at the head of a fused-slag stair, touch knuckles with a tiny, bright spark of greeting, and pause together by the balustrade, looking out across ten miles of open, sunlit sky toward the white insulator towers of the sister city clinging to the opposite cliff.

They do not speak of what lies at the bottom of the warm emptiness, nor of what might wait beyond the highest stack of horizons. Their fine brow-filaments lift slightly as the fresh updraft catches the ledge; the stone beneath their bare heels hums its deep, patient note; and they stand in the generous midday brightness, unhurried and entirely at home in the slow, luminous settling of the world.
