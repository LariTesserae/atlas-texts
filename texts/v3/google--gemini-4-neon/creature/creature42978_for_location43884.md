You do not see them until a single bass note fails to come around.

You have already learned the pace of the circling roar—how it swells behind your shoulders, drops into the wide jars near the waterline, and climbs again along the far curve—so when a narrow gap opens in the sound, three paces wide and lasting no longer than a blink, your ear catches it before your eyes do. You turn toward the silence.

At first there is only the weeping river-brick, the mouths of the terracotta jars, and the dark mirror of the floor. Then one of the low shapes you took for a weathered bollard uncurls.

It lifts slowly out of its crouch, shedding water in quiet, greasy sheets rather than splashes, and resolves into a person—slight, narrow-shouldered, and draped from collar to shin in a stiff, overlapping cape stitched from the olive-plated hides of the broad lurkers. The plates clack softly once, like wet slate, as they straighten. Below the hem of the coat, their shins are bare, pale as tallow, puckered from cold water and rimed along the ankles with a thin white chalk of dried lime. They are standing on a real bollard, toes curled securely over its mossy rim, one hand resting lightly inside the mouth of a wide-bellied jar at hip height.

They have not hurt the amphibian inside. Their palm is merely cupped gently over the vessel’s lip, dampening its throat-sac for a single turn of the song.

Across the seventy paces of hollow air, something answers—not a voice, which would only smear and shatter against the dome, but a crisp, double *tock* of bone striking oak high in the gloom. The figure on the bollard lifts their hand from the jar. The ash-colored toad inside inflates at once, late by a beat, booming out a low note that jostles against its neighbor’s before the great counterclockwise wave absorbs the stammer and smooths itself out again.

Now that your eyes know what to look for, the ceiling changes.

Up where the darkened oak ribs converge toward the crooked roof-vent, tucked into the shadow behind the dense, musky clusters of sleeping flyers, there are shallow cradles woven from thick braids of rush and slung between the copper-pegged beams. They are so thoroughly stained with damp, soot, and guano that you read them at first as swells of rot or matted nests. Only when a pale forearm slips over the lip of one cradle to pay out a hand’s length of cord do you realize how many eyes have been waiting in the rafters.

There are six of them—perhaps seven, though it is hard to count in the pewter twilight, and harder still to tell their ages apart. The mineral damp and permanent shadow bleach everyone toward the same muted palette: skin the hue of raw putty and bruised tallow, hair cropped uneven and close to the skull with sharp shell-edges so it cannot hold cold water against their necks, knuckles broadened and chalked white from lime-salts and a lifetime of twisting wet fiber. Even their posture belongs to the dome. Their backs carry a slight, permanent curve from crouching under oak ribs and leaning into jar-mouths; their feet are wide and flat, the soles thick as boiled leather, toes splayed to grip slick brick and wet timber without ever risking a splash.

The oldest of them never comes down to the floor anymore. You spot her high along the collar of masonry just beneath a slanted clay flue, where the pale shaft of daylight is brightest and the drafts pull steadily outward. She sits cross-legged on a plank wedged across two ribs, wrapped in felted sedge-matting and greasy hides, her knees drawn up toward a tiny, hand-coiled brazier tucked directly into the mouth of the flue. Inside it, cakes of dried flyer-guano and marsh-peat smolder without flame, the thin thread of smoke sucked immediately out into the unseen sky so it never fouls the cellar-cold air below. Beside her hip rests a wet lump of river-silt salvaged from the central drain, a pile of snapped lurker-ribs, and a coil of pale marsh-rush harvested during moonless nights from whatever drowned ground lies beyond the overflow culverts.

Her fingernails are wide, thick, and worn flat as horn scrapers. As you watch, she rolls a thumb-sized wad of silt between her palms, pierces it cleanly through the center with a spine of bone, and sets it near the warm lip of the brazier to harden. When she leans forward to test the tension of a rush-cord tied to the beam beside her, she does not look at it. She lays the flat of her cheekbone against the damp oak and closes her eyes.

That is how they listen.

The room is too loud for ordinary ears—a constant, bruised pressure that flattens delicate sounds and turns human speech into wet mud. Many of the dwellers keep their ears packed with soft plugs of amphibian tallow and shredded moss, or carry the pale, scarred look of eardrums long since thickened against the bass. Instead, they take the dome into their bones. The younger ones wear short, notched spools of fish-bone on sinew loops around their necks; when one of them wants to hear the fine tremor beneath the booming, they clamp the bone lightly between their back teeth and press its other end against a brick course, a timber rib, or the taut stem of a hanging rush-cord.

Down on the floor, the first figure steps off the bollard.

They do not wade the way you would. Strapped beneath their arches are narrow, high-cleated wooden pattens carved from salvaged oak, raising their bare heels two inches above the snapping seams of the flagstones. They move in a slow, gliding shuffle, never lifting the cleats clear of the surface, parting the shin-deep mirror without a ripple so the broad lurkers tucked in the troughs do not stir and the acoustic sheet stays unbroken.

They stop beside one of the thirty or forty braided rush-cords hanging straight down into the center of the void. The notched bone spool at its tip hovers a bare fingernail above the dark water. Gently, the keeper catches the cord between thumb and forefinger—not pulling, only feeling the faint, strained hum running down the fibers as the bass chorus sweeps past the far wall. They tilt their head, listening through their fingertips. Then they reach up, untie a tiny slip-knot an arm’s length above the spool, and shorten the cord by the thickness of a coin before stepping aside.

You begin to see what the hanging cords really are. They are not ornaments, nor traps, nor relics of some forgotten rite. They are the nerves by which these people read the vessel—and the reins by which they steer it.

Every clay lump and bone spool is weighed and knotted to a deliberate length. When the water rises back after an ebb, the keepers up in the beams watch which weights kiss the surface and which remain dry; the crossing rings of ripples tell them to the millimeter whether the hidden groundwater outside is swelling with distant rain or thinning toward drought. Each taut cord is also tuned by its weight to hum in sympathy with a specific tier of terracotta jars, carrying the health of the walls straight up into the oak ribs where the keepers sleep. If a jar cracks, if lime-crust narrows a neck and sharpens its pitch, if a cluster of pale seam-hunters grows bold enough to thin out the amphibians along the northern curve, the cord wired to that arc goes slack-throated and dull.

And the cords do something more.

Before you have finished tracing the pattern of them, a deep, wet sucking groan rises from the central limestone grate. The water tilts inward. Instantly, the chamber shifts—and the people who live here stop looking slow.

As the mirror drops by the width of a hand and the glossy black ridges of the flagstones break the surface, two more keepers drop lightly down from the wall-pegs onto the perimeter bench, landing on the balls of their wide, calloused feet with barely a whisper. Overhead, the naked-winged flyers peel off the oak ribs in swirling ribbons, filling the middle of the room with metallic clicks and frantic, sweeping wings.

Watch where the flyers go. Two of the keepers up in the rafters have slid along the beams just before the ebb, unhooking three of the long rush-cords and shifting them four paces eastward across the oak crown. The plunging flyers, steering tightly by the sharp return of their clicks off the hanging braids, bank away from the shifted curtain of cords and funnel hard against the western masonry—straight into a weeping patch of brickwork where the pale, long-limbed seam-hunters had thickened overnight. Claws rake terracotta; chitin scrapes and tumbles; the flyers feast, unknowingly weeding the walls for the jar-singers below.

Meanwhile, down on the exposed tiles, the water-keepers move with crisp, unhurried thrift inside the two-minute roar.

The midges rise in smoky veils around their pale faces; they blink slowly through them, mouths shut, eyes wide and steady. One strides along a black flagstone ridge with a flat woven scoop, sweeping up frantic, rattling crawlers before they can reach the deeper troughs, dumping them into a narrow basket slung across their ribs. Another kneels beside the central limestone grate while the ribbon-fish flash past their shins in silver knots, plunging both arms up to the elbows into the sucking throat of the drain to haul out a mat of rotted fern-fronds, shed chitin, and cracked snail-shell that would otherwise choke the siphon.

A third crouches low beside a wall-sluice where a broad, olive-plated lurker has surged halfway out of its thinning trough to snap at a fallen amphibian. There is no struggle, no drama. A heavy bone-tipped prong flashes once through the shallows, pinning the lurker neatly behind its bony head-plate. The keeper hoists it dripping and thrashing into a greased hide sack before the shockwaves have even finished slapping the nearest bollard.

High along the jar-tiers, a slender youth—no more than twelve or thirteen, bare-chested under a smear of pale tallow, gripping the slick rims of the terracotta mouths with spread fingers and curled toes—climbs horizontally across the curved wall while the seam-hunters are busy dodging wings. Hanging by one arm, they use a curved bone scraper to chip away a stubborn beard of white lime that has half-sealed a middle-tier jar, blowing the chalk dust clear while the fat, ash-colored toad inside blinks indignantly and refuses to yield its hollow.

Everything they do is measured against the breath of the floor. None of them looks at a clock or a sun; they do not need to. Long before the perimeter sluices begin to choke and belch air, the scrapers are tucked back into waist-cords, the dripping baskets are hauled hand-over-hand up into the rafters on spare lines, and the youth has climbed back into the timber gloom.

When cold, clear water pours out of the stone mouths and glides back across the floor, burying the black tiles once more, only one keeper remains below—standing again on the low stone bollard twenty paces from you, pattens resting neatly on the dry crest of the stone, wet hide cloak hanging heavy and still.

The flyers spiral back up into the beams. The midges thin. Along the rising sheet of water, the lowest pierced clay weights kiss the surface and send out slow, intersecting circles of silver light that cross and cancel one another across the wide floor. Out along the weeping wall, a single low jar booms once. Its neighbor answers a beat later. The great circling song begins to gather weight again.

The keeper on the bollard turns their head—not quickly, not with fear or hostility—and looks directly at you.

Up close, in the watery pewter shaft falling from an upper flue, you can see the faint pale ring around their wide, dark pupils, and the tiny dried crescents of lime caught in the creases beneath their eyes. They do not speak. They do not gesture for you to leave, nor welcome you to stay. Instead, they raise their small notched spool of bone, press one end lightly against their teeth, and rest the other against the taut rush-cord hanging beside their shoulder.

They listen for three full turns of the bass wave.

You realize, with an odd quiet chill, what they are hearing. You are a tall, soft obstacle standing shin-deep in a tuned vessel—an uneven pillar of cloth and bone soaking up sound where sound ought to glide, bending the counterclockwise wave a hair’s breadth out of true every time it passes behind your knees.

For a long moment, the keeper simply holds your gaze across the trembling water, taking the measure of the dead spot you make in the air.

Then they reach up to the rush-cord nearest you, loosen the slip-knot with two deft, chalk-white fingers, and let the notched bone weight drop half a thumb’s width lower—until its tip hovers just close enough to the water to catch the softened ripple returning from your boots. They test the taut braid once with their thumb. A faint, clean note hums straight up into the dark oak ribs.

Up in the gloom, two soft *tocks* of bone on timber answer in approval.

Satisfied, the keeper lowers their hands inside the olive-plated folds of their cloak, sinks slowly back into a crouch atop the bollard until their outline blurs once more into weathered stone, and lets the room close smoothly around you both—the circling roar steady again, unbroken, turning and turning through the cold gray air.
