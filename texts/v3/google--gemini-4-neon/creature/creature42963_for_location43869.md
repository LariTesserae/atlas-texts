At first, in the dim pewter flicker of the lantern-galls, you mistake them for parts of the rigging—pale knots of tendon, drifting shreds of algae, translucent splinters broken off the quartz. Only when a current turns and a hundred of them wheel together down a sixty-degree thoroughfare do you see that they are not drifting at all.

They are about the length of a human thumb, though their six slender, jointed limbs give them a reach twice that wide. Those limbs are clear as blown glass, revealing thin white threads of muscle working silently inside them, yet every joint is swathed in a cuff of dark, velvety skin so that nothing ever clicks or grates against the stone. Their narrow, flexible bodies hang slung between their legs beneath a supple mantle like wet, oiled vellum. Beneath that mantle, soft organs glow with a faint, cold blush—milk-blue when calm, clouded ochre when startled, flickering in quick, silent tides.

And none of them is ever truly still.

Watch one paused at an intersection, holding onto a braided algal handrail while waiting for a train of pellet-bearing groomers to pass. Even stopped, a faint, ceaseless ripple runs down the length of her mantle, and her two finest forelimbs—slender, brush-tipped appendages tucked near her mouthparts—twitch in a tiny, absent rhythm, whisking invisible dust from her own knees. At first it looks like nervousness, or the shiver of freezing water. It is neither. It is the baseline habit of staying alive in a brine that wants to turn every quiet surface into pearl. Stop shivering for an hour and your mantle stiffens like wet parchment left in frost; stop for a day and your joints fuse shut into solid aragonite.

They are citizens of a city built entirely out of fear of stillness, and they wear that urgency with a brisk, unsentimental grace.

Because the cavity eats every sound, they live inside a world of flavor and tension. Along the sides of their throats sit tiny, pleated vents that open and close with surgical precision, releasing calibrated puffs of chemical taste into the draft-beasts' wind. To speak to a neighbor across a narrow alley, you lean upstream and breathe out a bitter thread of warning, a sweet, oily curl of greeting, a sharp metallic spark of outrage. Downstream citizens taste the mood of a whole district before they ever turn the corner into it. A plaza brawl over a food-bead—one of those dense, greasy pearls of fermented bacterial curd pressed up from the fissure-farms below—makes no noise at all, yet it erupts like a storm: three or four bodies locked in a soundless, six-limbed tangle, kicking up a cloud of dark silt while their throat-vents blast the water with sour, blinding plumes of challenge-musk. Half a minute later, merchants ten stories down pause mid-stride, wrinkling the feathery gill-fringes behind their necks at the foul taste drifting past their balconies, and briskly roll their wares inside.

For anything that must travel faster than the slow, heavy brine, they use the city itself.

The translucent protein-silk cables and taut algal catwalks strung across the void are not merely bridges; they are the pocket's nervous system. Watch a young courier—lean, quick, her glassy limbs still completely clear of mineral clouding—halt halfway across a diagonal viaduct. She presses the velvety pads of four feet flat against the braided tendon and lets her mantle go slack, listening through her soles. Up through the silk comes the entire city's pulse: the deep, staggered triple-thump of the three forearm-thick draft-beasts pumping in the central chimneys; the frantic, rhythmic double-taps of a masonry crew on the eastern overhang demanding more lime-pellets; the faint, sharp twang of a distant span parting as acid-spitting dismantlers chew it free. She answers by flexing her middle legs, sending three crisp, measured plucks back down the line—*message taken, route blocked, rerouting over the quartz arch*—and vaults off into the current before the lantern-gall beside her finishes a single dim breath.

They do not build with hammers or chisels, because they have neither metal nor patience for stone that will not grow. They build by herding.

At the ragged, unfinished fronts of the city, where the blind ribbon-builders creep nose to tail extruding milky lime-paste, you will always find the surveyors. Older citizens, mostly, carrying small bladders of fermented gland-oil strapped to their bellies. They climb ahead of the blind worms along the raw scaffolding, dabbing tiny smears of sharp, bitter paste onto the silk and quartz, leaving narrow corridors of sweet musk between them. The builders recoil from the bitter borders and crowd into the sweet lanes, oozing their rubbery stone along the exact curves the surveyors painted in taste. An archway, a spiral stair, a cantilevered tenement hanging upside down from a crystal tooth—every one of them begins as an invisible diagram of flavor hung in freezing water.

Other guilds tend the city's stranger necessities.

Far at the razor-toothed apex of the pocket, where the spires graze the trembling silver lens of trapped ceiling-gas, cling the sky-reachers. They are the leanest, most daring citizens in the cavity, their velvet knuckles scarred white from the fresh obsidian walls. Working in roped teams anchored to the highest quartz needles, they thrust inverted baskets of tightly woven algal silk upward through the quivering mirror-sky, trapping fat, glistening beads of gas like captured mercury. Wrestling a basket of buoyancy downward against its fierce hunger to rise takes four climbers working in silence, hauling hand over hand down the vertical rigging to sell lift by the bubble to tenement-builders whose fragile balconies are beginning to sag. In property disputes—and the pocket is cramped enough that disputes are constant—the cruelest act of sabotage is to creep beneath a rival's overhang with a sharpened crab-spine and prick their corralled lift-beads loose, watching the silvery puddles shiver free and race back toward the ceiling while the masonry groans and cracks behind them.

At the center of the city, wedged into the ribbed chimneys beside the colossal, pulsing flanks of the draft-beasts, live the chimney-keepers. They are heavy-shouldered, slow-moving folk coated in thick grease to ward off the fierce mineral rush drawn up from the floor. They feed the great bellows-creatures bucket after bucket of nutrient sludge hauled from the grazing-slug troughs, comb parasites from their breathing-slits, and stroke their pale hides with long, brush-tipped poles to coax their rhythms faster or slower. Every citizen respects them, and every citizen quietly fears them. A chimney-keeper who falls negligent—or who chooses to slow a single shaft for half a shift to punish a stingy district—can drop the current in a dozen alleys to a crawl, letting the aragonite frost bloom across doorways and seal sleeping families inside their own walls.

For here, the true divide between fortune and misery has nothing to do with height. In a drowned hollow where districts tilt at sixty-three degrees and whole streets hang inverted from overhangs, "up" and "down" are mere directions—toward the silver mirror, or toward the weeping fissures. What matters is *brisk* and *dead*.

The prosperous guilds, the master surveyors, the successful curd-brokers and gas-harvesters claim the prime diagonals sweeping straight out from the chimneys. There, the water moves clean and fast, scouring the fine mineral dust off the facades and keeping a citizen's translucent legs sleek, dark-jointed, and supple with little effort. Along these avenues, the domed chambers are wide and bright with clustered lantern-galls, their round doorways ringed with manicured curtains of red algae and living wreaths of stinging polyps—cultivated like window-boxes and fed scraps of crushed foundation-crab so they know the scent of the household and lash their poison threads only at prowling scavengers or bold thieves.

In the dead corners—the cramped, crooked tenements jammed beneath raw basalt ledges or wedged deep in the lower slums where the draft-beasts' breath arrives thin and stale—the brine hangs almost still. There, the frost is an unending tax. You can tell a dead-corner dweller the moment they step onto a main thoroughfare by the cloudy, pearlescent rime cuffing their lower joints and dusting the edges of their mantles. They spend half their waking hours scraping themselves with bone-lime combs or trading precious food-beads for the attention of a passing groomer-swarm. In those shadowy alleys, too, the foundation-crabs pry most brazenly at the mortar, and the velvet ribbon-hunters dangle motionless from unfinished silk, waiting for some exhausted laborer whose vigilance has dulled from cold and hunger.

Even sleep must be negotiated with the water.

None of them can lie flat on a floor; to press your side against stone for six hours is to wake glued to it. Instead, they rest suspended in loose hammocks of stretched protein-silk strung directly across the open draft-lanes of their chambers, rocking gently in the silent breeze pumped by the chimneys while their mantles maintain a slow, dreaming shudder. Families and paired partners seldom sleep alone. They curl together in braided, multi-limbed knots, their brush-tipped forelimbs moving even in deep slumber, unconsciously combing the microscopic needles of fresh lime from one another's backs, neck-hinges, and the narrow blind spots behind their shoulders. In a pocket where every citizen is fiercely protective of their space, their food-beads, and their silk lines, to let another creature's forelimbs clean the joints behind your head is the deepest vow of trust they possess.

Yet the brine always wins in the end.

As a citizen ages, the fine oil that keeps their vellum mantle slick begins to thin. The involuntary tremor slows, skipping beats when they are tired, faltering when they concentrate. First the tips of their hindmost claws turn milky; then the translucent sheen along their shins clouds over with a fine, stubborn glaze that no amount of scraping can completely clear. They grow heavier, moving from the steep acrobatic thoroughfares down to the broader ramps, relying more on the current to carry them.

They do not treat this slow petrification as a horror, though they fight it keenly while their limbs still answer. When an elder feels the stiffness climbing past their waist and knows that one more sleep will seal their knees for good, they make a quiet tour of their kin—pressing their cool, clouding cheeks to younger flanks, exhaling long, sweet, complex farewells into the draft—and then they choose their final post.

Often it is a place that needs weight or steadiness: the sagging corner of a family arcade, the lip of a balcony where the lift-beads have thinned, the junction of a busy stairwell where young couriers swing hard around a turn. Aided by their children or guild-mates, the elder climbs into position while the ribbon-builders lay a fresh bed of soft, wet lime-paste beneath their feet. They brace all six legs wide against the stress of the arch, fold their velvety mantles neatly over their fading chest-lights, tilt their heads cleanly into the current, and—for the first time in their lives—let themselves stop shivering.

Within hours, a delicate white rime blooms over their velvet cuffs. Within days, they are smooth, pearlescent ivory, veined with faint needles of smoky quartz.

Walk through any older tier of the pocket—old here meaning a handful of seasons—and you realize the city is held up by its own dead. Great-grandmothers brace the lintels of the curd-markets, their calcified arms locked around the roof-ribs; stubborn old surveyors lean forever out over the central void, pointing down the currents they once mapped, their glassy eyes still dimly visible beneath a pale glaze of stone. Nothing about them feels grim. Children haul themselves up by their ancestors' hardened knees, tying their play-silks to an elder's stone shoulder, reading the faint, frost-preserved ridges along a forefather's flank with their fingertips the way another civilization might read names carved in bronze.

And still, if you watch them long enough—watching the fever of their building, the frantic rerouting of bridges, the relentless scraping and breeding and crowding into every raw inch of the fracture—you begin to sense the older truth behind their hurry.

They did not begin in this root-cellar hollow. No city this practiced, with its domesticated bellows-beasts and groomed mortar-slugs and flavor-guided masonry, invents itself in the few short years since this basalt tore apart.

They know what happens to pockets in the deep crust. They carry the knowledge not in written histories—parchment rots, and stone gets swallowed by more stone—but in the sharp, ancestral ache of their own urgency. Every hollow eventually fills. Millimeter by millimeter, the quartz needles lengthen from the raw obsidian. Year by year, the aragonite frost thickens on the walls, narrowing the alleys, choking the chimneys, slowing the draft-beasts until the last tired currents stall and the whole tiered metropolis freezes into a single solid geode of banded pearl, sealed forever beneath fathoms of cold rock.

Before that end comes in any dying city, the last survivors gather around the final weeping fissures. They seal their youngest—tiny, unformed larvae no larger than a grain of lime—inside glassy, pressure-proof cysts, packing them tightly alongside dormant spores of lantern-gall, algae, and blind worm into the tightest hairline cracks of the surrounding bedrock. Then the walls close behind them, and the cysts wait in crushing, absolute dark. Some wait ten years. Some wait a thousand. Most are ground to powder when the earth shifts the wrong way.

But now and then, the crust tears cleanly open, raw stone bleeds cold virgin brine into a sudden void, and the cysts crack like seeds hitting wet earth.

That is why, when the bedrock beneath the lowest slums shifts by the width of a hair—sending that sudden, crisp shiver thrumming through every protein-silk cable in the cavity and scattering beads of silver gas from beneath the balconies—not a single citizen freezes in panic. They know a hair wider would grind their city into silt. They know the rock is indifferent.

Instead, every foot clamped to a handrail feels the sharp, unmistakable rhythm of a fresh fracture opening below. Moments later, the draft-beasts draw in the new, bitter-clean scent of raw stone and untouched mineral brine weeping up from the ink-black crack in the floor, and pump that electrifying taste straight through the city's maze of alleys.

Across twenty tilted stories, hundreds of translucent mantles flare a bright, hungry milk-blue. Plumes of sweet, sharp euphoria burst silently from throat-vents, mingling in the cold water. On the upper arcades, surveyors abandon half-finished arches without a backward glance, slinging their oil-bladders over their backs and vaulting off the ledges into the downward draft. Couriers pluck urgent new rhythms across the taut rigging. Down in the shadow of the raw basalt, the first pioneers are already gripping the razor-sharp obsidian lip of the new seep, waving their forelimbs in frantic, silent semaphore, calling the blind ribbon-builders forward into the cold dark—racing to fill the open water with streets, and light, and life, before the stone remembers to close.
