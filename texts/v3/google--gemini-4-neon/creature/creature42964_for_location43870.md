If you stood motionless on one of the middle shelves with your eyes shut, you would meet them the way they meet one another: first as a shift in the air's damp weight, then as a faint, greasy warmth—sweat, crushed snail-meat, old chalk, and the sharp mineral musk of wet skin—and finally as a steady, unhurried tapping that rises cleanly underneath the ceiling's roar.

They do not look much like anything that belongs to sunlight.

Stretch one out along a calcite rib and it might measure as tall as a lean youth, yet it weighs far less than its reach suggests, built all of long levers and spare, knotted muscle. The forearms outrun the upper arms, ending in broad, hairless palms crisscrossed with deep, tacky creases that grip wet dolomite the way damp leather grips stone. Four slender fingers curl over a thick, low-set thumb—a thumb shaped less for fine beckoning than for clamping a cobble firmly into the heel of the palm, or holding a fern-rib steady against a crevice while the wrist turns it. The feet mirror the hands closely enough that an outsider watching in lantern-light would struggle to tell where climbing ends and carrying begins; the inner toe swings wide like a caliper to hook a thumb-thin shelf, anchoring the body on three points while the fourth limb probes, cracks, or scrapes.

What fur they carry is thin, patchy, and the dull color of steeped ash—never truly pale, because nothing in this hollow escapes the slow stain of limestone seepage and guano-smoke. It grows thickest along the ridge of the spine, across the shoulders, and down the backs of the forearms, where it channels falling condensation away from the body like sparse thatch. Everywhere else—belly, chest, throat, the insides of the long thighs—the skin lies bare, warm, veined, and lightly oiled, a bruised pinkish-gray that turns slick in the thickest steam. Years of crouching in the mountain's smooth pockets bow their backs into a permanent, easy curve; even when one hangs full-length from an overhang to reach a lower shelf, its spine keeps the faint crescent of the rock it sleeps in.

Their heads belong entirely to the dark.

Each narrows into a blunt, mobile muzzle tipped with wide, flaring nostrils that never stop working, parting the cavern's dense stench into crisp, legible currents. To an intruder from the frozen ridges outside, the hollow smells simply of heat, lime, and droppings; to a climber, the air is an open chart. It carries the dry, chalky tang of the eastern flint seam thirty paces off, the sour iron burst of a freshly fallen bat striking the guano below, the coppery stillness of a tepid salamander basin, the sharp ammoniac spike of a neighbor's irritation two alcoves along the same flue. Long, translucent whiskers spring not only from the muzzle and heavy brow-ridges, but from the chin and the backs of the wrists and ankles. Watch a climber cross a narrow lip in pitch blackness and you will see the forearm lead slightly ahead of the palm, those pale wrist-bristles sweeping the stone a heartbeat before weight shifts, reading dampness, grit, and the exact width of a ledge without a moment's hesitation.

Above the muzzle, the pale, milky eyes do very little work. Set deep under rounded brow-bones, they cannot resolve a face or a finger, only the blunt difference between pitch darkness and the faint, cold ache of the alpine glare that slips down through the upper cleft near midday. Most of the troop turns away from that bruised blade of light when it reaches the upper ledges; it serves them mainly as a slow clock, telling them when the ceiling is about to peel loose and marking, over months, the slight annual tilt of the sky they never see.

It is the ears that hold the cavern whole.

Broad, pleated, and leathery, veined so finely that a beam of daylight would turn their edges dull crimson, they swivel independently on the sides of the skull. When a disturbance ripples through the vault and ten thousand wings erupt in a sudden leathery squall, the climbers fold their ears flat against their skulls like shutters. When the roar settles, they open them wide and drink the room in. They make no echolocation clicks of their own; they do not need to. The bats and cave-swifts already flood the dome with a constant, metallic hiss, and the falling drops keep their separate, ringing clocks across the basins below. In that saturated soundscape, every solid rib throws an acoustic shadow, every hollow flue hums at its own pitch, and a neighbor creeping along a gallery ten paces away dims the ceiling's surf as cleanly as a body stepping across a doorway dims a lamp. They do not grope through darkness. They move through a chamber brightly lit by noise.

---

Hundreds of them share the middle galleries, yet the troop has no center, no chief, and no single gathering-place. Instead, it is sorted by gravity, heat, and the quiet arithmetic of ledges.

Up near the fortieth pace—as high as the galleries climb before the walls pinch into the writhing, guano-slicked dome of the roosts—live the leanest, quickest climbers: young adults newly split from their mothers' clusters, restless juveniles, and wiry loners with the surest grips. The high shelves are the hardest to hold. They sit closest to the deafening roar of the flyers, closest to the pale glare at noon, and closest to the thin, cold drafts that occasionally slip down from the entry cleft when storms hammer the massif outside. Yet the high tiers pay for their discomfort. Whatever falls from the ceiling strikes here first: a half-eaten lowland fig slipped from a bat's jaws, a plump cave-swift stunned by a midair collision, a clutch of brittle eggs tumbled from an overcrowded flue, stray wing-feathers and fresh, clean condensation trickling down from the fern-choked throat above. The climbers up here move faster than those below, their skin tighter, their tempers shorter, quick to snatch a tumbling scrap out of the air with an outstretched hand before it plunges past them into the dark.

Below them, between fifteen and thirty paces above the floor, lies the warm, steady belly of the hollow—the prime alcoves. Here the rock has been cupped by eons of rising vapor into deep, cradling pockets polished to a soapy sheen by ten thousand generations of crouched backs. The air is blood-warm and still, the ceiling's roar muffled to a thick surf, the great eastern flint seam within an easy traverse along the ribs. These galleries belong to the long-settled clusters: five to fifteen individuals bound mostly through mothers, daughters, and siblings who have slept rib-to-rib since infancy.

In these pockets, life slows to the cavern's true pulse. Mothers sit braced against the smooth stone with tiny, hairless infants locked onto their warm bellies by fists and feet already strong enough to bear their own weight. Older juveniles lounge along the lip of the shelf, ankles hooked around calcite knobs, practicing the steady, wrist-turning twist of a fern-rib in an empty crack. Thick-shouldered elders—their sparse spine-fur thinned to silver-gray bristles, their knuckles knotted and dusted white with chalk—hold the driest inner curves of the shelves, half-dozing with their broad ears tilted outward, monitoring every shift along the wall.

Lower still, down near the tenth pace where the scalloped stone begins to slicken with rising vapor and the heavy, sweet-sour stink of the guano floor presses upward, the population thins again. Few sleep here permanently, save the oldest climbers whose joints have stiffened with damp ache and whose hands can no longer trust the sheer upper traverses. Over the last years of their lives they drift downward shelf by shelf, yielding the higher pockets without a fight, settling onto broad, hot ledges where the floor's furnace warms their stiff ribs and slow snails and thick bracket-fungi are easy to reach without long stretches. Down here, the dry rustle of millions of beetles rises like steady rain beneath their dangling feet. And down here, the younger climbers from the middle tiers come only in cautious pairs, pausing at the bottommost stone margins, nostrils flared wide, listening for the heavy, whispering slide of an ivory pit-snake gliding through the steaming troughs below.

To a pit-snake, hunting by heat alone, a bare-bellied climber shines in the dark like a dropped coal. No climber descends onto the yielding guano casually. When a heavy bat drops all the way to the floor, or a giant floor-beetle clicks along the slick boundary where muck meets rock, two climbers will come down together—one hanging low by three limbs with a river-cobble poised in its fist, the other dropping a loose flake of chalk into the dark trough below and waiting with ears pinned forward to hear whether the soft earth shifts in answer. If the trough stays quiet, the lower climber swings down, snatches the prize before the dermestids can swarm it, and hauls back up the stone rib in three fluid pulls, shaking pale blind beetles off its wrists as it climbs. Once in a long while, a bold pair corners a smaller snake trapped against a rock margin and batters its broad head flat with repeated blows of a hammerstone—not chiefly for its stringy, musky flesh, which they chew grudgingly, but for the long, tough sinews running down its spine and a strip of thick ivory hide that can be wrapped around a palm to guard the skin while striking fresh chert.

---

They have never laid one stone upon another. Even the idea of enclosing space would make no sense to a creature born inside a mountain's throat; the dolomite already cups their backs, catches their drinking water, and walls out the sky. Everything they make fits inside a fist, hangs from a bent finger, or tucks neatly into the natural dimple beside a sleeping hip.

Yet within that narrow span of palm and wrist, their craft is astonishingly old and astonishingly exact.

Walk a hand along the eastern gallery where the dark flint seam shows through the softer dolomite, and your fingers will find fresh scars in the rock and a fine crunch of razor-edged debitage littering the lower ledges. Knapping happens in pitch blackness, guided entirely by the angle of the wrist, the weight of the striking cobble, and the crisp, glassy *tink* that tells a climber the core has split clean. There is only one exception to the dark. Now and then, when a dense cobble glances sharply across the chert seam, a tiny cold spark leaps from the stone—a pale, stinging prickle that flashes for a fraction of a heartbeat across their milky eyes and leaves a sharp smell of burnt bone hanging in the damp air. They have no notion of fire; nothing in this dripping hollow could catch even if they did. To a climber, that brief sting of light is simply the seam answering back, the proof of a good, biting edge.

They test a fresh flake against the calloused pad of a thumb, never the tongue, then carry it back to their home alcove tucked in the crook of an elbow or pinched lightly between the lips. With it they shave bitter, gray-white mineral salt from the chalky efflorescence along the dry drafts—essential in this warm incubator where bare skin loses salt constantly—and slice tough, rubbery bracket-fungi into thin, chewy strips, always leaving the thickest root-seam attached to the wet stone so the shelf will bloom again. When a flake dulls, its holder taps the edge casually against a protruding stone rib until a new bite opens, or simply leaves it on the lip of the pocket. Over decades, falling condensation paints the discarded flint in slow skins of translucent calcite. Run your fingers down any worn gallery and you feel them there beneath the slick stone glaze—dozens of forgotten knives, sealed smoothly into the mountain's ribs like fossils of old hands.

Their finest work is quieter and more patient: the grub-hooks. Making one requires pieces gathered from every layer of the cavern's world—and even from the distant valleys kilometers below, though no climber knows that valleys exist.

The shaft is a stripped rib of tough, wiry rock-fern, torn from the cold throat of the entry cleft. The hook is the pale, V-shaped wishbone of a cave-swift, snapped neatly down one arm and ground to a fine barb against gritty dolomite until it catches cleanly on a thumbnail. The binding is dried sinew—pulled from a dead bat's wing or a snake's tail and softened by hours of slow chewing—and the final seal is a smear of dark, tacky resin. That resin is the rarest thing on the ledges. Bats returning at dawn from warm lowland forests carry microscopic beads of conifer pitch tangled in the matted shag of their bellies and wing-joints; whenever a flyer dies and falls into the galleries, climbers groom its cooling fur with extraordinary care, picking off those tiny, pine-scented crumbs with their fingernails and storing them in dry rock dimples until enough gathers to warm in the mouth and press tight over a sinew lashing. Holding a finished hook in the dark, a gaunt elder holds a piece of mountain cleft, a piece of ceiling, and a piece of a forest three thousand meters below, joined by patient teeth and greasy fingers.

Seated hunched before a damp fissure, the elder feeds the flexible fern-rib deep into the stone, feeling with wrist and fingertips for the soft, yielding resistance of a fat pale grub wedged beyond a finger's reach. A quarter-turn to seat the wishbone barb; a slow, steady draw; a soft *pop* as the grub comes loose into the waiting palm. Nearby, two juveniles take turns with a hollowed swift wing-bone, its tips smoothed against chalk so they do not cut the lip, slipping it into a hairline crack to draw up thin, warm mouthfuls of chalky water. Mothers use shorter wing-bones to blow soft puffs of warm breath into narrow dry cracks, startling huge armored cave-crickets into bolting straight out beneath their waiting hands—ignoring the wild whip of those forearm-long antennae to pin the brittle thorax flat against the rock.

And then there are the cobbles.

They are the only things in the cavern that cannot be replaced.

Nothing inside the dolomitic hollow makes a stone like them—dense, dark basalt or heavy quartzite, rolled smooth and oval by rushing meltwater in some deep gorge that none of the living troop has ever heard or smelled. Perhaps their ancestors carried them up out of the valleys generations ago, when the mountain was warmer and the troop first discovered this steaming slit; perhaps, once in a century, some desperate storm-driven climber wandered down the scree and struggled back up with a fist-sized river-stone wedged beneath its chin. No one remembers. There may be fewer than sixty true cobbles in the entire cavern, every one darkened with centuries of palm-oil and pitted deep in the center from cracking ten thousand calcified cave-snails and giant floor-beetles.

A climber does not truly own a ledge—ledges belong to whoever holds them—and a dull chert scraper is abandoned without a backward touch. A river-cobble is different. It passes from hand to hand across lifetimes: from an aging mother whose thumbs have swollen too stiff to strike true, into the broad palm of the daughter who has shared her alcove for twenty years. Each cobble has its own weight, its own worn thumb-hollow, its own faint, layered scent of old hands and crushed shell. When a cobble slips from a tired grip and plunges into the dark guano below, the whole gallery goes still for several heartbeats, listening to the dull, wet *thump* that marks where it sank. Even if the pit-snakes are active, someone will almost always descend during the quiet hours, tracking the memory of that single sound, plunging a long arm wrist-deep into the hot, rustling beetles to feel for the smooth, alien roundness that does not belong to the cave.

Because every cobble also has a voice.

---

Watch—or rather, listen—in the long, heavy lull before dusk.

Up near the vault, the pale blade of alpine light has thinned to bruised indigo and is dying off the wet rim of the overhang. The roosting flyers grow restless; ripples of papery wing-slaps and sharp bickering roll across the dome as tens of thousands of bodies wake and shift toward the entry slit. The air thickens with heat and anticipation.

Down along the middle tiers, the slow *tock* begins.

An elder on the western wall shifts forward on her haunches, lifts her pitted basalt cobble, and strikes it lightly against the hollow edge of a fluted stalactitic curtain beside her alcove. *Tock. Tock.* She pauses, ears spread wide, letting the clean, resonant note roll out across thirty paces of warm darkness.

Across the void on the eastern gallery, another stone answers—higher, drier, ringing off a thinner calcite rib. *Tock. Pause. Tock.* Then another from ten paces higher up; then two soft, overlapping knocks from a broad pocket down near the steaming lower shelves.

It is neither song nor warning, though it carries a trace of both. In a crowded vertical bottle where a single scuffle on a hand-wide lip could send two bodies plummeting thirty meters into the dark, open fighting is a luxury the troop cannot afford. Instead, the cavern takes inventory by ear. Every hollow curtain along the walls has its own pitch; every inherited cobble strikes with its own unmistakable timbre—flat, glassy, woody, or bright. Within a few dozen slow exchanges, every climber curled in the rock knows without moving a foot who still holds the high western flue, how many warm bodies fill the prime pockets along the flint seam, whether the old double-pitted stone has moved two shelves lower for the season, and whether any shelf has gone quiet.

If a familiar curtain stays silent for three dusk-lulls in a row, climbers from neighboring clusters creep cautiously along the wet ribs in the night-quiet, wrist-whiskers grazing the rock ahead of them, nostrils sorting the dark for the sour smell of fever, the stillness of a cooling body, or the clean, vacant scent of an empty bowl ready to be claimed.

Then the indigo blade vanishes altogether, and the ceiling lets go.

It begins as a sudden deepening of the roar—a swirling, upward rush that pulls a thick curtain of warm air straight past the middle ledges. Tens of thousands of swifts and mountain bats peel loose in tight, spiraling ribbons, pouring upward through the greased entry slit into the freezing twilight sky. For twenty minutes the cavern becomes a chimney of beating wings and stinging dust. The climbers flatten their backs into their smooth pockets, mothers curling around clinging infants, ears laid flat, waiting out the wind.

And then, almost all at once, the mountain falls quiet.

Not silent—never silent—but spacious in a way that feels astonishing after the suffocating daylight press. Without the flyers' surf, every small sound steps forward out of the dark: the patient, separate plinks of condensation falling thirty meters into eroded rock cups; the dry, continuous rain of dermestids turning the guano floor; the soft scrape of a giant snail sliding across a glossy trough; the low, comfortable breathing of hundreds of climbers unfolding from their pockets at once.

This is when the troop truly lives.

Clusters loosen and flow along the galleries in an unhurried, three-limbed stream. When two climbers meet on a shelf barely wider than a palm, neither hurries and neither shoves; they exchange a soft, dry palate-click, and the lighter climber flattens its bare belly flush against the wet stone, locking fingers and toes into the rock while the other steps smoothly over its arched back without breaking stride. Along the dampest margins, climbers kneel beside shallow tepid pools, fingers hovering motionless just above the water until a pale, slick cave-salamander rises toward the vibration of a tapped knuckle and is scooped up in one gentle curl of the palm. They rarely strip a basin bare; once two or three salamanders have been taken, they move on, leaving the tangled pale roots at the bottom to thicken again.

Down near the humid edges of the lower ledges, where seeds dropped by lowland bats have sprouted into eerie, bone-white stalks stretching blind toward a sun that never arrives, juveniles snap off the fragile ghost-shoots and chew them whole—a fleeting, watery sweetness that vanishes within days as the starved stems collapse back into muck. Deeper in the seams, where pale mycelium gives off its cold, hesitant ghost-sheen, climbers trace the faint earthy smell—not for the glowing threads themselves, which taste bitter and metallic, but because damp, clean mycelium always marks the best seams for soft grubs and tender bracket-fungi.

And back in the warm pockets, the long grooming begins.

In a hot, damp hollow teeming with pale mites, cave-ticks, and opportunistic skin-fungi, touch is survival. Climbers sit in dense, murmuring knots—back to belly, chin hooked over shoulder, long legs tangled easily over the lip of the shelf. Nimble fingers part the sparse spine-fur to pinch away parasites; blunt teeth gently nip dry crusts from the folded pleats of a neighbor's huge ears; an elder may even draw the flat of a worn chert flake lightly along a companion's calloused heel to lift away hardened mineral scale. Soft chest-hums vibrate through the shared rock, passing from rib to rib until an entire alcove seems to breathe on one slow, steady count.

---

Almost no climber ever leaves the mountain's throat.

To them, the high entry slit is not a doorway; it is a wound. Up near the crown of the vault, where cold alpine air leaks inward to meet the cavern's rising breath, the stone feels wrong—dry, raw, unpolished, biting against bare skin. Worse is what lies beyond the greased stone lips: a terrifying, wall-less emptiness where sounds fly off and never come back, where the wind tears the warmth straight off a climber's ribs, and where the naked sky burns like a needle against milky eyes.

Yet someone must go up there, because the tough, wiry rock-ferns that make the best grub-hooks grow nowhere else.

The raids happen seldom, usually in the thin gray hush just after dusk or on overcast days when the midday blade is weak and dull. A lean, steady climber from the upper tiers—never an elder, never a mother carrying young—works its way up past the last smeared roost-ledges into the wet, narrowing throat of the cleft. Its ears fold flat against its skull; its sparse spine-fur bristles upright in the cold draft; every movement turns unusually quick and jerky, stripped of the cavern's slow ease.

Just inside the lips of the slit, where lichen crusts the stone and freezing mountain wind smells painfully thin and empty—like ice and nothing at all—the climber hooks both feet firmly into a damp crevice, reaches one long arm out toward the edge of the abyss, and yanks three or four tough fern-stems free by the roots. Sometimes it pauses a single breathless second to lick sharp, clean snowmelt from a wet overhang—water so shockingly cold and tasteless, without chalk or lime, that it makes the jaw ache. Then, clutching the wiry green stems tight in its teeth, it drops back down through the narrow flue as fast as three limbs can carry it, plunging back into the dense, sour, blood-warm roar with a shudder of relief that runs from its nostrils to its heels.

Down in the middle galleries, others crowd close to smell the raw, cold odor of the outside clinging to the fern-ribs. They touch the green fronds briefly with the tips of their whiskers, sniff the alien chill, strip away the useless leaves until only the tough brown spine remains, and let the green scraps tumble down into the dark.

---

They do not measure years, because the cavern barely keeps them.

Outside, blizzards shear across the massif's leeward tooth and summers blister bare granite, yet inside the hollow a wet season means only that the falling drops strike the rock cups a fraction faster, and a dry season means the eastern salt-seams crust a little thicker along the drafts. A climber's life is measured instead in shelves and stones: the first year clamped tight to a mother's warm ribs, learning the balance of the void through the tilt of her back; the quick, lean decades up near the loud high ledges; the long, steady seasons in the smooth middle alcoves, cracking snails and grooming kin and striking the hollow curtain before dusk; the slow descent, shelf by shelf, as knees stiffen and wrists lose their easy spring, down toward the hot lower ledges where the guano's furnace eases the ache in old bones.

When an old climber finally stops moving—curled quiet in a warm lower pocket, or slipping gently from a slick shelf during the hushed night hours—there is no wailing and no ceremony. A neighbor creeps along the rib after two silent dusk-lulls, touches the cool, still flank with the backs of its wrist-whiskers, pries the pitted river-cobble gently from the stiffened thumb, and lifts it back up into the middle galleries. The body slides, sooner or later, down into the steaming black muck below. Within two nights the blind dermestids and pale millipedes have reduced it cleanly to white bone; within a month, even the bones have sunk into the warm, yielding earth that heats the cavern from beneath.

Only the impressions remain.

Before dawn breaks over the freezing peaks outside, the flyers funnel back down through the cleft in a rushing torrent of wings, dragging the chill of the sky and the faint damp scent of distant lowland forests into the mountain's dark throat. The ceiling closes up with shifting, chattering curtains of fur and feather. The roar swells back to its full atmospheric weight.

Curled shoulder to shoulder in pockets worn smooth by ten thousand generations of curved spines, the climbers tuck their chins against their neighbors' ribs, fold their broad ears half-shut against the surf, and rest their calloused palms lightly over the ancient river-stones tucked beside their hips. Beneath their hands, sealed under glassy skins of dripping calcite, lie the dulled chert knives and snapped bone straws of climbers dead since before the glacier left the lower gorges. A single drop swells on an eroded rib, lets go, falls thirty meters through the crowded, screaming dark, and adds another invisible layer of mineral over stone and tool alike—holding the hollow, and everything breathing inside it, in the same slow, warm, unbroken hand.
