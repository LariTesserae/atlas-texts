At first, stepping in from the glare of the rim, you do not separate them from the walls.

The brown-gold twilight softens every outline, and the people of the bowl wear the colors of the place so completely that an alley looks empty until a shoulder shifts against a plastered pillar or a hand reaches down from a timber gallery to haul up a basket of millet. They are small, compact, deep-chested people—broad through the ribs from generations of drawing life out of air too thin for lowlanders, with stout legs and bare, splayed feet whose soles have been worked by the concave stone stairs to the dark, cracked toughness of old saddle-leather. Their skin is the shade of oiled bronze or hearth-baked ochre, flushed darkly at the cheeks and lips with thick, high-altitude blood, and it rarely gleams with sweat. Even in the close, bakery heat of the lower tiers, they stay strangely dry. Every morning they rub their forearms, faces, and necks with a faint, sweet-smelling paste of rendered goat-tallow, crushed silver-herb, and fine limestone dust, sealing their pores against the dry air as carefully as a potter burnishes a water-jar.

If a stranger climbs the approach trail and stumbles through a wind-slot gasping, face shining with perspiration, the first people to meet them will look not with suspicion, but with a sharp, flinching distress—as if the newcomer were bleeding onto the flagstones. A grandmother sitting by a mortar may reach out and press a bundle of chalky, cool-skinned succulent leaves against the stranger’s brow, murmuring in a dry, hurried voice to sit down, to close the mouth, to stop giving themselves away to the air.

Because that is the first rule of the bowl, older than any craft or lineage: *what is inside stays inside*.

You see it in how they move. For all the clamor of the city—the hammers, the looms, the birds, the bleating goats—the people themselves have an unhurried, economical grace. Nobody runs on the staircases. Nobody gestures wildly when speaking. Children learn before they can climb a ladder alone to keep their mouths shut when carrying loads, to breathe steadily through the nose, and never, under any provocation, to spit. To spit on the floor is worse than striking a neighbor; it is an act of spite against the whole hollow. Even weeping is a hushed, careful thing. In grief, a mourner presses a woven strip of raw wool under their eyes, and afterward rings the damp cloth out over the soil of the family’s doorway vine, or wipes their wet thumbs across the rim of the hearth-pot so nothing leaves the house uncounted.

Their eyes are large, dark, and slow to blink, accustomed to the perpetual dusk beneath the tangled canopy. In the rare noon shafts that pierce the overhead weave, they turn their faces slightly aside, squinting through long, dust-pale lashes. Only one sort of citizen willingly faces the raw, unfiltered sky: the skimmers, mostly lean youths and sinewy older women with the keenest balance, who climb to the overhanging rim in the freezing hour before sunrise. They wear narrow visors carved from horn or flat bird-bone, pierced with hairline slits to guard their eyes from the grit-storms and the blinding white salt-glare of the plains below. Hanging half out of the wind-slots with braided rawhide looped around their waists, they work with bone spatulas along the stretched waxed hides, scraping off the thin film of frost and night-dew before the first slant of sunlight touches the basalt and steals it back into the sky.

When the skimmers come back down through the upper galleries as the morning warms, their knuckles are split raw from the cold wind, their faces blackened by the fierce upper sun, and their narrow-necked hide flasks swing heavy at their hips. People step aside for them on the narrow stairs—even the goats seem to know to press flat against the plaster—and whisper blessings over their shoulders. They carry the day’s life in their hands.

The water they gather does not belong to any single house. It descends through the tiers in a quiet, ceremonial journey down to the deepest, coolest rock vaults cut into the basalt core beneath the city’s floor. There live the cellar-minders, usually elders whose joints have grown too stiff for the ladders and upper roofs, their skin pale as cave-mushrooms from years in the subterranean dark. They are the keepers of the city’s memory and its strict arithmetic. Sitting beside rows of fat clay amphorae plugged with beeswax and resin-soaked wool, they know the sound of every jar when tapped with a knuckle—which one holds a season’s reserve, which one was filled in a lean winter three years back, which one has developed a hairline weep that must be patched with hot hyrax-gum. Every evening, families send down a child with a clay measure tied to a cord, and the minders ladle out the household share with spoons of polished goat-horn, recording each portion not on parchment—there is no paper here, and no animal skin to waste on scribbles—but by sliding tiny drilled beads of limestone and copper along knotted sinew cords hung from the vault rafters.

Life in the bowl arranges itself around this vertical circulation, yet not in the way lowland cities divide rich from poor. Everyone is too tightly packed for grand distance; a cellar-minder’s grandchild may marry a canopy-mender’s son whose loft sits seven stories above, and the smells of every hearth mingle in the same warm air. Instead, people belong to their altitude, shaped subtly by the light and labor of their tier.

Up in the highest rooms wedged beneath the curving rock lip live the roof-tenders, the snare-setters, and the tree-keepers. They are agile, lean-hipped, with long-calloused fingers stained green-black from pruning succulent shoots and knotting rawhide around the sprawling, gnarled limbs of the canopy-trees. They move along the interlocking branches over the drop with the casual ease of the rock-hyraxes beside them, patching holes in the goat-hide awnings when a fierce wind tears a seam, or harvesting the little burrowing rodents and fat finches that rustle through the roof-gardens. Their voices carry the sharpest pitch in the city.

You have to learn to hear them before you can understand them. Because the bowl is an acoustic drum—filled at all hours with the low bass hum of the wind shearing over the lip, the rattling canvas of swift-wings, and the rhythmic thumping of mortars and looms—ordinary lowland speech gets swallowed within five paces. Shouting wastes breath and dries the throat. So the people speak in two distinct registers. Close up, in the dim lanes and crouched beside the dung-hearths, they murmur just above a whisper, reading the tilt of a chin, the twitch of a brow, and the light pressure of fingers on a forearm. Across the hollow, from balcony to balcony or gallery to floor, they use a thin, penetrating, fluting call-language—bright whistles, dry tongue-clicks, and nasal singing notes pitched deliberately *between* the deep hum of the wind and the shrill chatter of the cliff-swifts. A weaver on the third tier can haggle over a skein of dyed goat-hair with a dyer on the sixth without ever raising her chest or breaking the rhythm of her shuttle, their voices threading cleanly through the city’s roar like bright needles through coarse cloth.

They are deeply uneasy in silence. On the rare, strange afternoons when the high-altitude wind dies completely and the bass hum above the rim drops away, looms falter, hammers pause, and faces turn upward toward the leafy ceiling in quiet dread. Without the ceiling of wind, the bowl feels cracked open, exposed to the vast, listening emptiness of the sky. Mothers hush their babies and wait, hands folded, until the first dry moan returns to the bird-bone chimes in the clefts and the city lets out its breath again.

Down through the middle galleries—the warm, dim belly of the city—live the makers: the potters, the weavers, the tanners, the seed-grinders, and the metal-beaters. Their craft is governed by a fierce, reverent thrift that a stranger might mistake for poverty until they notice the beauty of it. Because nothing new enters the bowl easily, everything here is an heirloom. A potter never digs fresh clay without crushing broken old sherds into dust to temper the new batch; the bowl of a young bride’s cooking pot carries specks of the jars her great-grandmother used. A smith does not forge great ingots; he squats beside a smooth boulder with a stone hammer and cold-works scraps of ancient copper and bronze, coaxing a cracked needle back into a point, beating a dented ear-ornament into a fine fish-scale pendant, annealing worn blades over a tiny dung-fire fanned with hawk-wing feathers.

Pride here is measured not in thickness or shine, but in thinness and endurance. The most honored tool in a butcher’s or carver’s belt is a bronze knife honed across six generations until the blade is narrow and curved as a willow-leaf, its bone handle polished translucent by a hundred thousand hours of palm-oil. Men and women wear their wealth lightly—a single hammered copper ring, a string of polished fruit-pits and pale bird-beads wound through tight, fat-dressed braids—and they know the biography of every object in their room. Every warped roof-beam has a name, remembered by the houses it held up before this one. When a family invites you inside and points to the crooked bottle-wood lintel overhead, scarred with old lash-grooves and shining black with hearth-smoke, they will tell you whose grandfather dragged it from the upper ledges after the great wind-fall of his boyhood, and which two marriages were pledged beneath it.

Waiting for a tree to yield wood is a lesson in geological patience. No one alive has ever swung an axe at a living trunk; to cut green wood from the ancient canopy-trees would be like cutting a tendon from your own heel. Instead, the tree-keepers watch the gnarled limbs across decades, noting where bark splits dry and hollow, where a branch ceases to push out waxy leaves after a rare damp spring. When at last a dead limb groans, loosens, and is lowered down on braided sinew ropes in a shower of ancient dry-moss and startled lizards, the whole city stops. It is called a *Yielding*. Elders arbitrate who has the oldest claim—a leaning wall on the fourth tier, a rotting pulley-axle by the east steps, a weaver whose loom-frame has finally split beyond lashing—and the sawdust and splintered bark are swept up carefully with swift-feather brooms to be mixed with dung-fuel and fragrant resin.

And woven right through all of this human thrift, treated neither as livestock nor as wild strangers, are the other citizens of the bowl.

The people do not imagine themselves as masters of the animals; they think of the bowl as a crowded room in which everyone arrived long ago and no one can afford to leave. The small, blunt-hoofed goats are half-domestic, half-neighbor, known individually by the rasp of their bleat and the pattern of white hairs on their flanks; children sleep curled against their warm, dry-smelling sides on the coldest winter nights, and old women argue with them softly on the staircases as if they were stubborn cousins. The fat, tailless rock-hyraxes sunning on the sunlit ledges are tolerated with an affectionate exasperation. They steal herbs if left unwatched, and their sharp, barking alarm-calls jar the afternoon naps—yet they are the city’s watchmen whenever a rare hawk circles the rim, and more precious still, their ancient communal latrines along the dry cliff-shelves cure over decades into dark, glossy, amber-scented crusts of resin. Boys with obsidian scrapers harvest this hyrax-stone carefully; dissolved in warm tallow, it heals cracked heels, seals leaks in clay jars, soothes high-altitude coughs, and burns in tiny crumbs inside evening lamps to turn the sharp smell of goat-dung sweet and woody.

Even the tiny copper-backed skinks and pale geckos are welcome at the hearth. A potter will flick a slow mealworm or a crushed dust-beetle toward the lizard perched beside his wheel, calling it *little sweeper*, because without the lizards the biting midges and grain-moths would eat the city raw.

Most revered of all are the swifts and the bats. The people cannot leave the bowl easily, and the salt-pans far below are a vast, bleached graveyard to them, yet every day the swifts pour out through the wind-slots into the sunlit upper air, and every dusk the bats stream out into the freezing night, returning hours later with bellies full of stray lowland insects, drifting pollen, and mountain seeds. Their droppings enrich the roof-troughs; their feathers line infant cradles; their mud-gourd nests tucked under every lintel are never knocked down, even when a doorway must be ducked through sideways to avoid them. To the people of the bowl, the birds and bats are the city’s long arms, reaching out into the dead sky to gather stray scraps of life and bring them home to the jar.

Only a very few humans ever imitate them. Once every two or three years, when the winter winds ease slightly and the copper hoards have grown thin or the salt-cakes are nearly spent, a small party of three or four hardened walkers—usually childless adults or restless youths with wide, strong lungs—packs dried thorny-berries, hard goat-cheese stones, finely woven goat-hair shawls, and lumps of dark hyrax-resin into rawhide slings. They slip out through the wind-slots at dawn and vanish down the bleached, spiraling approach trail into the vast flat glare below.

Those who remain watch them shrink to specks from the overhanging rim, then turn back quickly into the dim warmth, feeling a strange, dizzying vertigo at how flat and unheld the world is beyond their stone lip. Months later, if the walkers return—thinner, sun-blackened, smelling strangely of open wind and lowland alkali, carrying bars of dull greenish copper, chunks of volcanic glass, or rough blocks of salt traded at distant, half-remembered desert wells—they sit in the lower courtyards for days, drinking carefully rationed cups of bitter herb-tea and telling quiet stories of places where water falls straight from the sky and houses stand alone with no walls touching. The listeners shake their heads, half-wondering, half-pitying, glancing up at their own crowded, leafy ceiling with deep relief. How lonely it must be, they murmur, to live where the wind can touch all four sides of your house at once.

Nothing leaves the bowl for good—not even the people themselves.

When children reach their naming-day, when a marriage is bound, or when a widow enters mourning, their hair is shorn close with a sharp flake of obsidian. None of it is swept away. It is bundled into woven pouches and traded to the brick-makers and wall-menders, who knead the dark, coarse strands straight into wet ochre mud alongside millet-chaff and goat-hair. Thus a family’s generations are literally braided into the skin of their rooms; when a plaster wall cracks from the slow shifting of the tiers and a daughter smooths a fresh layer over it, she is laying her own years cleanly over her mother’s.

And when an elder grows very old—when their thick blood finally slows, their breath turns shallow even in the sheltered air, and their hands can no longer twist cord or grind roasted seed—they are carried gently up from the busy middle alleys to the quiet, shadowy *Root-Lofts* wedged just beneath the massive, twisted trunks of the ancient canopy-trees. There, in the warm dimness scented with dry moss and resin, kin sit beside them in turns, wiping their dry lips with a wet wool rag, humming the low wind-songs that match the bass note of the rim.

When the breath stops, there is no pyre—wood is sacred—and no grave dug in earth, for there is no open earth to spare. Instead, the family washes the body with a single bowl of water infused with bitter herbs, then carries that bowl out to pour slowly over the coiled taproots of the nearest tree. The dead are wrapped tightly in porous, unbleached goat-hair cloth dusted with dry hearth-ash and limestone powder, and laid inside shallow, vented niches built into the warm back walls behind the communal dung-kilns and hearths. There, over the course of a year, the steady, dry warmth of the bowl draws out the body’s last moisture through the porous plaster and into the clinging mosses and thirsty vines outside, until what remains is light, clean, and hollow as a sun-cured gourd.

When the wall is next due for thickening, the niche is opened once more. The dry bones are bundled neatly with braided sinew, marked with a small dab of ochre, and tucked deep into the stone fissures behind the new mud-brick, or wedged carefully into the hollows of a sagging buttress. No one speaks of it with horror. To the people of the bowl, it is the gentlest kind of faithfulness. An ancestor does not vanish into cold ground or blow away as ash into the empty sky; they drink the last heat of the hearth, feed the leaves overhead, and stay on to hold up the stairs their great-grandchildren climb.

When evening comes, and the sun drops below the flattened ribs of the dead mountains outside, you see them most clearly for what they are.

As the air beyond the rim plunges toward freezing and the wind rises a pitch higher, singing sharply through the dry branches and the strings of hollow bird-bones in the clefts, the city draws inward like an anemone closing its petals. The bats pour out through the wind-slots in a soft, chittering dark river, brushing past the last skimmers coming down from the ledges. Along the seven stacked tiers, small saucers of tallow flicker awake, turning the ochre plaster walls to warm honey and throwing huge, tangled shadows of leaves and hanging gourds across the ceilings.

People gather on the narrow balconies and around the sunken dung-hearths in the lower courts. Clay lids come off the simmering pots—carefully angled so the beaded steam runs back down into a catch-dish—and the rich, dusty smell of roasted millet, mashed tubers, salted goat-cheese, and sweet dried berries rises through the gloom. Someone on the fifth tier begins to tap a slow rhythm on the belly of an empty water-jar; a woman across the chasm answers with a high, reedy flute-call; a child laughs as a bold gecko darts across a warm knee to snatch a moth drawn to the lamp-flame.

Above them, the ancient stone rim hums its deep, unchanging note against the cold emptiness of the sky. Beneath it, the dark basalt walls slowly breathe back the sun they hoarded all afternoon, wrapping the dense, chattering, leafy little crowd in a shared, stubborn warmth—human and goat, swift and lizard, root and bone, all pressed close together inside the earthen jar, holding onto every drop of breath and water until the frost returns to the wind-slots at dawn.
