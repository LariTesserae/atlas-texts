If you stay inside the fissure long enough for the ringing in your ears to settle into a dull pressure, and for the chill to work past your ribs, you stop hearing a wall of noise and begin to hear a city.

It is a city without a single laid brick, without a hearth, without a road—yet it is as densely governed by habit, memory, and quiet politics as any walled town below the clouds. Everything that truly lives here does so on a scale of patience that makes human breath feel like a seizure. To understand the creatures pressed into these wet seams, you first have to let go of sight altogether.

The blue-white quartz sparks that dazzle an outsider mean nothing to the stone-tappers. They live inside a continuous blizzard of cold lightning they have never seen and cannot imagine. When two knuckles of milky quartz kiss in the dark, a brief flash illuminates a pale flank, a curled balancing hook, a wet rib-comb—and none of the bodies caught in that flash know they have been revealed. Their world is built entirely out of return waves. Every *tink* is a lantern thrown into the blackness; every rebound off wet basalt paints the ledge, the distance to the opposite wall, the fullness of a drinking-hollow, the precise curve of a neighbor’s shoulder three inches to the left.

Their bodies are engineered for an existence where falling is the only fast thing left. Watch one strike a seam-clinger in a brief strobe of quartz-light and you notice the strange economy of that extra, backward-bending elbow. A creature with ordinary arms, perched on a shelf three fingers wide, would throw its weight outward just by winding up a blow. A stone-tapper never leaves the rock. Its forearm folds cleanly back along its own flat ribs like a knife blade shutting into horn, drawing tension slowly into thick, pale tendons until the limb locks; then, with a single crisp release, the forearm snaps out strictly parallel to the basalt face. The blow lands hard enough to crack porcelain shell, yet the climber’s sternum never shifts a millimeter off the wet stone.

Beneath the pleated, quivering dish of the face, the vertical mouth almost never opens except to eat or to chew frond-stalks into cordage. They have no voices in their throats. Instead, they speak through their stones—and because a quartz knocker wears down over decades, every climber’s voice ages with them.

A young tapper, newly separated from its parent and gripping a fresh, fist-sized lump quarried from a shattered vein, speaks in a heavy, dull clack that barely carries across the fifteen-pace belly of the cleft. Over twenty, thirty, fifty years of tapping shells and ringing rhyolite, that stone loses flakes, rounds at the edges, and deepens where four horn-tipped fingers sit in its hollows. Its pitch climbs. By the time a climber has lasted half a century on the high tiers, its hammer is no larger than a plum, polished smooth as river glass by skin-oil and grit, and its strike rings out in a thin, silver-sharp chime that cuts straight through the low rumble of the vault. Everyone within forty paces knows who is working, who is resting, who has moved a ledge higher, and whose rhythm has begun to falter—all from the thinning pitch of an old stone.

Because they share so little oxygen and so much proximity, their manners are entirely a language of tempo and pressure. Anger is an extravagance nobody at this altitude can afford; a scuffle burns ten minutes of breath and risks sweeping half a tier down into the black pools. Disputes over a wet seam rich with shellfish are settled across whole afternoons. Two climbers will wedge shoulder to shoulder, pressing their flat flanks harder and harder against one another in absolute silence, their ribcages flexing like slow bellows until one quietly yields an inch of foothold and shifts away. A deliberate insult is a hammer-blow answered half a beat too late, muddying a neighbor’s echo. Intimacy is the opposite: two climbers resting their pleated ear-dishes near enough that their slow breaths stir each other’s skin, tapping in exact, alternating unison so their two stones map the same stretch of dark as a single shared mind.

Even reproduction obeys the narrow ledges. There are no nests and no eggs that could roll into the abyss. A tapper gives birth pressed flat against the vertical rock, catching a single pale, boneless-looking infant in the hollow of its curved tail-hook and immediately sliding it up onto its own chest. For its first three or four years, the young one never touches basalt. It clings flush between its parent’s flat ribs like a cold, leathery barnacle, skin-to-skin, its tiny horn-buds hooked into the tough creases of its parent’s hide. Its ear-dish is not yet open; it lies folded tight against its brow like a damp, unopened mushroom cap, shielding the delicate membranes while it grows. It drinks salty, chewed seam-clinger paste passed slowly from vertical mouth to vertical mouth, and it learns the cathedral entirely through bone-conduction—feeling the resonant hum of the rhyolite travel through its parent’s spine and into its own tiny skull.

Only when the young one grows too heavy to carry without spoiling the parent’s balance do the pleats of its face finally begin to uncurl. Its first independent act is not to walk, but to listen. Still anchored to its parent’s ribs by its hind feet, it stretches one long, triple-jointed arm out into the black, taps a tiny, blunt claw against the back of its parent’s quartz knocker, and shudders as the first true air-borne echo washes back across its raw new dish.

---

Though the cleft looks from below like one undifferentiated mass of clinging gray bodies, it is divided vertically into three distinct worlds—and the creatures who spend their lives in each tier slowly grow to differ from one another, shaped by the thin differences between high light, resonant dark, and dissolving floor.

Up near the crooked seam of sky, where the pale noon blade cuts down through the drifting mist, live the **Canopy-Tenders**. They are the smallest population and the strangest to look upon, though none of them know it. High-altitude ultraviolet pours through the overhead crack for two hours every clear day, and though the tenders have no eyes to be blinded by it, the fierce radiation burns and toughens their hides. Unlike the ghostly, cave-pale climbers of the dark belly, the upper-tier tappers are bruised dark slate-gray, almost purple along the ridges of their shoulders, their pleated ear-dishes thickened and scarred with dry, chalky mineral crusts where the outer wind-drafts brush them.

They live suspended inside the swaying forest of hanging air-sacs, threading their long limbs among the thick, elastic mucus-tethers like spiders in wet rigging. To an outsider, the sacs look like passive plants or blind bladders; to the Canopy-Tenders, they are unruly, vital livestock. Without the sacs pumping heavy, warmed air down into the lower cleft, everyone below the middle ledges would suffocate in their own stale breath within a week.

The tenders spend their slow days grooming the vault’s lungs. Hanging upside down by their tail-hooks from razor overhangs, they use slivers of obsidian to scrape mineral salt and wire-moss spores out of the sacs’ wide, fringed intake mouths so the bellows do not choke. When a pebble blown off the outer spire tumbles through the sky-slit and slices a sac’s veined membrane, the nearest tender creeps along the sticky mucus-cord, chews a wad of bitter, fibrous frond-stalk mixed with its own thick saliva, and presses the sticky patch gently over the hissing tear until the sac’s own tissue seals around it. And when an ancient air-sac finally fails—its sigh turning wet and rattle-throated, its membrane yellowing with rot—two or three tenders patiently saw through its anchor-tether before its corruption can sour the downdraft. The dead bladder drops two hundred feet in a heavy, flapping hush, splashing unseen into the floor-pools below.

In return, the sacs offer the only true comfort in the spire. Between bouts of work, the slate-dark tenders press their flat chests directly against the warm, swelling sides of the healthy bellows, letting themselves sway gently out over the dizzying drop while the huge membrane expands and collapses beneath their ribs—rocked in the dark by a heartbeat that takes a full minute to finish.

---

Below the reach of daylight, occupying the long, sheer middle throat of the cleft where the thickest veins of quartz and glassy rhyolite run like frozen lightning down the walls, lives the great dense body of the community: the **Choir**.

This is where the deafening voice of the cathedral is made, and where the tappers’ strange, unarchitectural culture reaches its deepest complexity. To make the ceiling weep requires more than blind hammering. A single climber scraping a rib-comb across a grooved vein makes only a dry, insect rasp. It takes forty or fifty climbers along both opposing walls working in a staggering, phased counterpoint—striking quartz ribs at intervals calculated to stack one vibration directly atop the peak of the last—before the narrow stone chamber locks into a standing wave.

There is no single conductor. Instead, the Choir leans on its elders—the **Anchored Ones**.

A stone-tapper can live for eighty years if it never loses its grip and never cracks a bone. In its last two decades, its joints stiffen with cold and its long, slow climbs shrink to a single favorite shelf along a resonant vein. Because it scarcely moves, the cleft slowly begins to claim it. Mineral-heavy condensation runs down the basalt, beads along its knees and curled tail-hook, and leaves microscopic skins of milky silica and iron-oxide behind. Over years, thin crusts of stone solder its ankles and elbows into the fractures. Tiny porcelain seam-clingers cement themselves right up against its calves; stubby, bone-pale cartilaginous fronds root in the damp chitin-silt that gathers in the hollow of its collarbones.

An Anchored One cannot hunt shells anymore, and cannot fetch water. Yet its neighbors never nudge it into the dark. Its pleated ear-dish, grown wide and deeply corrugated over decades of listening, is a living archive of the spire’s acoustics. Younger climbers cluster tightly around it, chewing seam-clinger meat soft and pressing it into the elder’s vertical mouth, wiping the wet mineral silt from its pleats with gentle horn-tips. In exchange, the elder listens. When the Choir’s standing wave begins to drift out of tune—dissipating uselessly against the jagged overhangs instead of shaking droplets loose from the roof—the Anchored One raises one stiffening forearm and strikes its tiny, plum-sized heirloom quartz against the vein. One pure, piercing *ting*. The climbers to its left and right adjust their tempo by a fraction of a heartbeat; the wave snaps back into focus, the air turns prickly against the teeth, and a sudden cold silver rain hisses down across a hundred upturned flanks.

Even rarer than the Anchored Ones—and treated with a quiet, watchful awe—are the **Split-Ears**. Now and then, a quartz hammer shatters under a bad strike, sending a razor shard flying across the dark to slice straight through a climber’s tympanic dish. Usually, a climber blinded in sound loses its map of the wall and slips to its death within days. But a few survive by discovering a stranger sense. Unable to read the ringing air, a Split-Ear presses its cheekbones, jaw-plates, and bare sternum flat against the raw basalt and learns to hear *through the stone itself*.

A Split-Ear cannot join the Choir, and cannot hunt far from familiar crevices. Its neighbors tolerate its clumsy groping because the Split-Ears hear what nobody else can: the deep, sub-audible groans traveling up from miles beneath the volcano’s roots. Minutes before the rising mountain builds enough internal shear to crack the spire, a Split-Ear’s body goes rigid against the rock. Its neighbors feel its muscles lock through their touching knees, feel the sudden, sharp *clack-clack* of its warning knock—and the whole tier knows to hook its tails deep and brace before the stone screams.

---

At the very bottom of the cathedral, where the walls pinch slightly inward above the shard-strewn floor and the black mirror-pools gleam with drowned sparks, live the **Dredgers**.

No climber chooses the lowest ledges out of comfort. The air down here is the heaviest and wettest, thick with the bitter, oily reek of decay and the iron-sour stench of the floor-carpet. Worse, the pale, blind ribbon-scavengers that glide across the razor talus do not stop cleanly where flat ground ends; on damp afternoons, their slick, clay-pale front edges ooze three or four feet up the wet basalt, probing cracks for anything too slow or too sick to recoil.

Yet the entire upper city depends on the Dredgers, because everything the cleft loses falls into the dark at their feet.

When a climber dies on an upper ledge—announced first not by silence, but by the sudden, cool tickle of flat, leathery joint-mites migrating off the cooling elbows onto the ribs of its living neighbors—the surrounding tappers patiently pry the dead one’s quartz hammer and obsidian pry-bar from its stiffened fingers. Those tools stay on the wall, inherited by younger hands that fit their horn-tips directly into the smooth hollows worn by three or four generations of the dead. Then the body is nudged gently off the shelf. It tumbles down through the blackness, brushing past hanging hooks and pleated faces with a soft, papery rustle, before striking the porcelain talus below with a wet snap.

The ribbon-scavengers glide immediately toward the impact. They dissolve soft flesh, gristle, and horn in a matter of days, turning a fallen climber into a faint mineral sheen on the wet shards. But the densest bones—the heavy femurs and curved, tough ribs—resist their digestive slime for weeks.

That is what the Dredgers wait for. Hanging head-down from the lowest overhangs, tethered by tail-hooks and back-bent knees just inches above the shifting, clay-slick carpet, a Dredger lowers a long cord of braided frond-fiber weighted with a hooked bone barb into the black pools. Slowly, blindly, tapping a small quartz pebble with its free hand to map the ripples and submerged shapes below, it fishes in the dark. It drags up cleaned leg bones for awls, stripped ribs to be notched into resonant combs, and—most prized of all—dropped quartz knockers and obsidian blades that slipped from clumsy fingers a hundred feet overhead.

Sometimes, a Dredger hooks something still warm.

Not every climber who falls from the middle tiers dies on impact. The thin air slows nothing, but a glancing blow off an angled overhang or a fall into a deep, black pool can leave a tapper alive on the talus with a shattered spine or a broken hip. Down there, surrounded by blades of unweathered rhyolite that slice skin at the slightest twitch, a broken climber cannot climb back to the ledges. All it can do is lie in the cold black water and weakly strike its quartz knocker against the floor shards—*tink… tink…*—sending thin, jagged echoes up into the cathedral, begging for a hook or a ledge it can never reach.

The Dredgers never pull a broken climber up; there is no strength to haul a hound-sized body up vertical glass in air this thin, and no room on the shelves for one who cannot cling. Worse, the ribbon-scavengers have learned that sound. Unlike the steady, rhythmic ringing of the walls above, an erratic, halting knock coming from floor-level means fresh meat. Across the wet talus, dozens of pale, blind ribbons turn silently toward the weak sparks, gliding effortlessly over razor shards like spilled tallow. High above, the Dredgers hang motionless on their hooks, folding their ear-dishes slightly inward to muffle the sound, waiting until the knocking slows, skips a beat, and finally goes quiet beneath the soft, wet sliding of the carpet.

---

Nothing inside the split needle is wasted, and nothing is truly solitary. Even the smaller things woven through the cracks participate in the slow bargain of the vault.

The flat, leathery mites tucked behind every tapper’s extra elbow are not mere bloodsuckers; in a humid fissure saturated with dissolved silica and iron, a creature that sits motionless for forty hours at a stretch would soon have its folding joints glued shut by crusted mineral salt. The mites graze endlessly on that salt and shed skin-oil, keeping the climbers’ hinges supple—and serving, when they abruptly march off a cooling flank in the dark, as the cleft’s only undertakers.

Even the pale, tongue-curled fronds jutting from the wettest dark cracks live off the Choir’s labor. Too deep for sunlight, they catch the microscopic mineral flour knocked free from quartz veins by hundreds of stone hammers, digesting stone-dust and shed chitin-flakes with their sticky hairs, turning the noise of the walls into tough, bitter outer fibers that the climbers strip, chew, and twist back into the lashings that bind bone to stone. The clamor literally grows the rope that holds the hammers together.

And once every few months, the mountain reminds everything inside it that the stone itself is still alive.

Deep beneath the basalt, tectonic pressure finally overcomes friction. First, the Split-Ears go stiff against the walls, freezing mid-breath. A minute later, a sudden, dry crack—loud as a splitting iceberg—slams up through the unweathered core of the peak. Dust puffs from the overhead seam; loose talus clinks like spilled dishes across the floor; every hanging air-sac shudders on its mucus-cord, spilling a sudden cold cascade of sweat.

Instantly, the entire cathedral drops dead silent.

Hundreds of forearms freeze half-cocked. Notched rib-combs stop mid-stroke against their rhyolite grooves. For half an hour, nobody dares strike a stone. It is not merely fear that holds them still—it is recalibration. When a fresh volcanic needle shifts by the width of a fingernail, every acoustic truth inside its two-hundred-foot belly changes. A horizontal crack that held an elder’s weight yesterday may now be widening toward a shear; a resonant quartz vein that sang clear and sharp an hour ago may now be pinched flat by subterranean stress.

So for thirty agonizing, wheezing minutes, the entire packed population of the dark hangs motionless, ear-dishes flared as wide as they can stretch, listening to the mountain settle. They listen to single grains of basalt popping loose from the walls and ticking down into the black mirror-pools. They listen to the faint, altered draft sighing past the mucus-cords overhead. They listen to the breath of the neighbors pressed against their ribs, checking who is still there, and who has vanished into the silence below.

Then, somewhere along the middle throat—usually where an Anchored One sits half-welded to the rock, crusted in silica and pale fronds—a stiff, pale forearm slowly rises.

Its tiny, worn-down heirloom quartz kisses the wet vein.

*Ting.*

A single pinprick of cold blue-white light winks across a wet gray dish nobody can see. The elder holds completely still, reading the return wave as it bounces across eighty paces of shifted glass, measuring the new geometry of a home that is still growing out of the earth. Ten heartbeats pass. Across the dark gap, a second stone answers—slightly lower, slightly delayed, testing the new distance between the walls. Another answers from the high tiers among the sighing bellows; another from the wet dark above the black pools.

Slowly, painfully, with an unhurried persistence older than any single body on the ledges, they find the mountain’s new note. Rib-combs settle back into their grooves. Forearms fold and snap. The air begins to prickle against the teeth, the hanging lungs heave, and in the cold, ringing dark, the city of stone-tappers begins again to make the rock weep.
