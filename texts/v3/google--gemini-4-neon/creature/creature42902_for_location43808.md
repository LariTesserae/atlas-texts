At first, the basin seems empty of anything larger than the stilt-legged waders. The midday sun is too honest, the sightlines across the dark wet floor too wide and unbroken, for anything of real size to hide.

You notice the edits before you notice the editors.

Along a narrow curve connecting the central mirror pool to the rear wall, the razor-sharp edges of the fractured basalt are no longer sharp. Someone has packed the hairline fissures with a pale, dense putty—root-chalk ground together with fine dark silt—and rubbed the glass-keen ridges down until they are smooth as worn river-steps. Beside one of the porous ceramic plinths sit six small, egg-shaped capsules of wet grey clay, arranged in a tidy crescent just inside the zone where nearby fronds grow thick and tightly curled. And high under the western overhang, hanging from the braided curtains of wet moss, are short strings of translucent mica plates—the cast shells of the basin’s limpet-like grazers, pierced cleanly at the center and threaded on stripped plant-sinew. They do not chime in the deadened air, but as the sun crosses the open sky, they throw slow, wandering slivers of pale prism-light across the gleaming black stone.

Then a shadow detaches itself from the broad overhang near the rear ledges, and you realize why you missed them: they move at the tempo of the water.

There are five of them in the basin, though they rarely gather in one place, respecting the same wide, deliberate spacing as the thickets and the plinths.

The one that steps out into the midday glare is roughly the bulk of a large seal, though longer in the back and built for stone rather than deep water. It moves on all fours in a low, unhurried prowl—its belly clearing the blazing puddles by a hand’s width—before rising smoothly onto its hind legs and a thick, blunt, muscular tail to stand waist-high beside a shallow pool. Its hide is the color of wet cinnabar and smoked slate: matte, dense, heavy-folded, and utterly devoid of the faceted armor or glossy scales of the basin’s smaller creatures. Because the last condensation pulse was fifteen minutes ago, its broad shoulders are dusted with faint, chalky tide-lines of dried mineral salt. Along either side of its spine run two long, smooth ridges of taut, paler flesh that swell and settle with a slow, heavy liquid shift beneath the skin.

Those ridges are reservoirs. A creature whose blood and joints depend on near-boiling warmth cannot afford to chill; they drink the scalding pool-water directly and hold it inside thick, pressurized vascular chambers along their backs, carrying the basin’s heat inside their own bodies like living kilns.

When it tilts its broad, tapering head toward the pool, you see how thoroughly it belongs to this quiet, bright oven. Its eyes are small and deep-set beneath smooth, heavy brows, veiled by a second inner lid the color of smoked quartz that filters the unclouded mountain glare. It has no outer ears—high frequencies die within a few paces here anyway. Instead, set flush behind its jaw and running down the pale skin of its throat are pairs of taut, dark-amber membranes, fine as oiled parchment. As the hovering silver ring at the basin’s center turns its imperceptible fraction of a degree, those throat-drums tremble faintly, reading pressure changes too subtle for human skin to register.

Its forelimbs are long and quietly articulated, ending in broad hands with four slender digits and a thick, opposable heel-thumb. Each finger is tipped not with a claw, but with a smooth, porcelain-hard nail shaped like a tiny flat scoop or a narrow chisel—tools grown directly from the bone for lifting mineral crusts, parting oily leaves, and working soft stone.

It kneels beside the mirror pool on thick, grooved pads of pale rubbery gristle that grip wet volcanic glass without slipping. Without haste, it lowers its blunt, lipless mouth to the unboiling surface and drinks. There is no slurp, no splash, barely a disturbance—only a faint, silky dimple pulled inward across the heavy water as it draws the hundred-degree liquid down into its dorsal chambers, replenishing itself before the work of the afternoon.

Across the spacious floor, two others are already moving through their quiet routines.

They are clearly adults, broader in the shoulder and more heavily mapped with pale mineral seams along the creases of their elbows and necks. One crouches beside a newly rooted thicket where the sharp basalt still juts like broken glass; with its flat chisel-nails, it gently scrapes the chalky paste secreted by the bone-white roots, mixing it in its cupped palm with dark mineral mud and pressing it patiently along a jagged fracture to steer the flow of the next runoff. The other stands motionless beside the triangle of pale ceramic plinths, both broad palms pressed flat against the porous stone. Its smoked eye-lids are closed. It stays like that for ten minutes at a time—not praying, or perhaps doing something older and more practical than prayer: listening through its palms and throat-drums to the silent tension holding the lid of air over the basin, checking the health of the invisible pressure that keeps the cold sky at bay.

When these two cross paths on the bare expanse between thickets, they do not call out. The air here refuses bright voices. Instead, they stop a pace apart, lower their broad fronts slightly toward the stone, and let a brief, heavy vibration travel down through their palms and feet into the wet basalt. You would not hear it with your ears; standing near them, you would feel it only in the soles of your boots and the roots of your molars—a clean, warm, bass-deep cadence, like two heavy stones shifted gently underwater. A whole exchange passes in three slow pulses: the state of a seepage line on the eastern wall, a new crack ticking open beneath the sun, the ripeness of the bladder-pods along the northern terrace. Then they part, the heat radiating from their water-filled backs lingering in the dense air for a breath after they have moved on.

The fourth is much smaller—sleek, narrow-shouldered, its hide a brighter, cleaner clay-red not yet weathered by mineral crust. Its dorsal sacs are still modest, so it keeps away from the open cliff rim, staying near the warm heart of the basin where the mirror pools hold their deepest heat. It moves faster than the elders, which still means a fluid, gliding crouch, pausing beneath a low copper-green overhang where a fat, overheated drop is swelling on the tip of an oily frond.

The young one holds out the back of a flattened hand and waits. When the drop detaches and lands with its muted, fleshy *thup*, the small creature tilts its wrist by tiny fractions, coaxing the trembling, heavy lens of scalding water back and forth across its dark skin without letting the surface tension break. Once, carried away by its game, it shifts too quickly and sends an accidental, clumsy double-thump of excitement down through its heels into the basalt. A tiny crisscross of standing ripples shivers across the nearest pool. Almost instantly, one of the working adults pauses twenty paces away and sends back a single, flat, grounding tone through the floor—not harsh, merely heavy, a hand laid gently on a ringing bell. The young one settles its belly flush against the warm wet rock, tucks its chin down, and lets the water go glassy again.

Then the air subtly darkens its clarity. A brief, quiet weight presses against the skin as a warm thermal rising up the outside cliff strikes the basin’s invisible border.

Instantly, the twenty-minute bloom arrives.

Water condenses out of nothing across every dry millimeter of stone, leaf, and hide. In two seconds, the chalky salt-lines vanish from the dwellers’ shoulders; their matte cinnabar-slate skin turns dark, gleaming, almost plum-black, sheathed in a heavy, unmoving coat of hot liquid. None of them flinch or seek cover. The two adults simply pause, lifting their blunt chins slightly to let the sudden sheen gather in the smooth furrows behind their skulls and run down into the creases of their backs, while the young one curls lazily on a freshly slicked ledge, basking in the sudden weight of moisture.

Over the next quarter hour, as that sudden skin of water gathers into silent runnels and begins its slow crawl toward the hairline cracks, they harvest.

They are meticulous, unhurried gatherers, never taking enough to strip a thicket or bare a seepage line. One adult walks to a cluster of chest-high jointed stalks, pierces a translucent bladder-pod with the tip of a chisel-nail, and squeezes the hot, mineral-heavy nectar directly into its mouth, dropping the dark, wet seeds neatly into a damp crevice further along the floor so another island of fronds might take hold in a year’s time. The other wades ankle-deep along the weeping base of the east cliff and pries four mica-shelled grazers from the burning stone, leaving dozens undisturbed in their clean dark trails.

It carries the four limpets to the center of the basin—right to the edge of the mirror pool where the matte-silver ring hovers two finger-widths above the surface.

Moving with calm familiarity, the creature slides the grazers gently into the smooth, permanent dimple directly beneath the ring’s shadow. Here, the invisible pressure on the scalding water is densest, fiercest, and quietest. No bubble rises; no steam escapes. Yet within minutes, steeped under that silent force, the tough crimson flesh inside the mica plates turns pale, tender, and translucent. The adult lifts them clear with its spoon-tipped fingers, peels the bright, clean shells free to save for the hanging strings, and carries two of the steeped grazers away toward the rear wall.

Only then, following its path, do you see the fifth.

It lies half-submerged in a shallow, hand-smoothed hollow near the base of the rear cliff, just beneath the hair-thin violet filaments threaded through the vertical crack. Looking toward it makes the eyes ache slightly, because the rock behind its shoulders seems half a pace closer than the rock beside it, and the water weeping down the cliff slows to a thick, glassy syrup as it glides over its motionless back.

This one is very old.

Unlike the others, its hide no longer sheds the basin’s mineral salts cleanly. Over years—far more years than this freshly sheared, unrounded basalt has existed—a pale, porous calcification has crept across its broad shoulders, its heavy tail, and the stiffened ridges of its dorsal sacs, giving its back the matte, chalky texture of the three ceramic plinths. Its smoked inner lids stay drawn almost constantly now, opening only a sliver when the adult kneels beside its hollow and sets the warm, steeped meat on the smooth stone lip beneath its chin.

You realize, looking at the worn, mineral-weathered curve of this elder against the razor-fresh volcanic fractures around it, that they did not grow up with this basin. They are older than its sharp edges. Wherever they lived before—some earlier terrace lower on the massif, or a sister-pocket whose quiet seal finally cracked open to the freezing wind—the stone there had already been rubbed round by generations of their palms. This cathedral of raw black glass is a new house, still settling, still ticking in the sun, and they are patiently teaching it how to hold them.

The elder eats slowly, its broad throat shifting once, twice. It moves hardly at all now, yet it is the center of the basin’s attention. Because its calcified hide and stiffened drum-membranes lie flush against the rear bedrock—right where the violet seam thickens the water and bends the distance—it feels the mountain itself. Deep inside the newly sheared massif, miles below the cloud deck, rock strata still groan and creep as they adjust to their sudden lift. Days before a fracture widens, hours before the hovering silver ring tilts by a hair’s breadth, the elder feels the slow stress gathering in the dark stone beneath its belly.

Wait long enough, and you will see what happens when it gives warning.

A low, immense pulse—so deep it barely registers as sound, more like a brief heaviness in the chest—rolls out from the rear hollow across the wet basalt floor. Instantly, the working adults set down their root-paste and lift flat, shallow trays woven from braided mossy ribbons and stiff plant-stalks. Their throat-drums flutter rapidly, triangulating the pressure shift before it breaks. They step quietly out to a narrow strip along the eastern pool, submerging their woven trays just beneath the glossy skin of the water, and wait.

Five breaths later, the hovering silver ring tilts by the width of a hair. Across the far terrace, the three pale ceramic plinths darken by a single shade.

Directly above the waiting trays, the invisible lock on the water momentarily loosens. Without a sound, a narrow band of the scalding pool turns pearlescent as millions of microscopic bubbles bloom through the liquid at once, rising in a slow, shimmering curtain of upside-down snow. Buoyant pale spores and shimmering flakes of dissolved mineral lift off the submerged basalt, drifting upward through the woven trays while the low cup-shaped plants along the bank uncurl their thick petals in silence.

The adults lift their trays with steady, unhurried grace just as the ring levels itself and the water snaps back to dead, obsidian stillness. They carry the pale, glittering residue to the smooth work-ledge beside the plinths, folding the spores and mineral dust into fresh grey clay capsules—small, patient stores of seed and salt, set out in the altered physics beside the ceramic stones to cure dense and hard. What they are saving them for—whether for a lean season, a long winter night when the sun drops behind the mountain’s spine, or the distant day when this basin too cracks open and they must walk out into another raw scar of stone—they keep to themselves.

Late in the afternoon, when the sun leans toward the cloud-deck and throws long, razor-clean shadows from the basalt walls across the wet pools, the basin grows quieter still. The young one curls close against the elder’s warm, calcified flank near the rear wall, its smooth red hide bright against the pale crust. One of the adults rests half-submerged in the central pool, its back gleaming like oiled slate, watching the slow prisms cast by the hanging mica plates drift across the dark floor.

The other walks slowly toward the fourth side of the basin—the open edge where the rock simply ends above bright blue air and an endless sunlit floor of clouds.

It stops two paces short of the jagged drop, just inside the invisible thermal wall. To its heat-bodied flesh, the normal mountain air a hand’s width beyond its nose is an abyss of instant frost, a lethal, roaring emptiness whose wind it cannot even hear. Yet it does not turn away. Slowly, rising onto its hind legs and heavy tail, it reaches a single broad, wet forelimb forward—and pushes its flattened hand clean through the boundary.

For one startling heartbeat, the basin’s silence breaks.

Out in the razor-cold, thin altitude air, the film of hundred-degree water coating its dark fingers flashes instantly into a fierce, brief puff of white vapor—and before that mist can even blow away, a delicate lace of crystalline rime snaps across its porcelain-tipped knuckles with a tiny, brittle *tick* that dies cleanly at the wrist, sheared off by the quiet edge of the room.

The creature holds its hand in the freezing light for two slow seconds, watching the wind tear the white plume away into the bright sky. Then it draws its arm smoothly back inside the rim.

The frost vanishes at once—not with a hiss, only softening silently back into heavy, unboiling drops that roll down its dark palm and fall onto the blazing basalt with a soft, muffled *thup*. The creature curls its fingers once, testing the warmth returning to its joints, then drops quietly back to all fours and pads away across the gleaming stone, leaving the cold sky to itself and returning to the bright, silent oven they have made their home.
