They did not leave. If you watch the hunched figure behind the low stone wall for an hour, you will see that the bone needle is not frozen at all; it has simply progressed the width of a hair through the stitched dry hide, drawing a strand of bleached sinew so gradually that the dust along the thread does not even stir.

The makers are still here, scattered thinly through the immense crowd of flanks and wings, living at a cadence worn down almost to the speed of stone.

At first glance they are easy to mistake for just another dry-land species wedged into the crevices of the limestone. They are lean and compact, wrapped from throat to ankle in layered, dust-colored hides patched together with pale cord and rubbed smooth with chalk. Their exposed skin—knuckles, cheekbones, the narrow rims around their clear, slow-blinking eyes—has the matte texture of old horn or sun-cured wood, neither wrinkled with age nor damp with sweat. No breath fogs the cool air before their lips. Like the browsers and the climbers around them, they long ago shed the loud, wet urgencies of eating, drinking, and sleeping. Yet they keep their hands busy, and they keep their seams tight.

Tight seams matter here.

In a range where two dozen wool-coated beasts can stand abreast on a boot-wide cornice by quietly occupying one another’s ribs, an outline is not a guarantee; it is a habit that has to be maintained. A person who sits bare-skinned beside a limestone wall for too many afternoons, or rests too carelessly against the flank of a broad-backed grazer, begins to lose their edge. First the shadow slips loose and attaches itself to a nearby horn. Then a calf or a shoulder softens into the chalky pelt beside it, sharing space without pain or blood, until the body can no longer remember which vertebrae belong to it and which belong to the herd.

So the people of the terraces sew. They bind their wrists with hide strips, lace their ankles snug, and dust their palms from the shallow clay bowls of powdered feldspar before they touch rock or bone. The fine mineral dust acts as a boundary, keeping dry skin from taking hold of dry stone. When they work with a bone needle, they are not preparing for winter—there has been no winter, nor any night, since before their oldest memories—they are simply mending the borders of themselves.

Wait long enough beside one of the shale shelters and you will begin to notice how they read a world that drinks sound.

A spoken word dies three paces from the mouth here, absorbed by the sheer mass of packed hides and spread membranes. The people do not call to one another across the gorges. Instead, they listen through tension and through stone. Those slack woven-fiber snares pegged into the dry alcoves, looped loosely around the ankles of motionless six-legged beasts, are not traps; they are instruments. The small burrowers and crawlers feel the glacial shifts of the bedrock long before larger bodies do—the microscopic settling of a limestone plinth, or the slow tilting of a terrace where gravity is beginning to change its mind. When a six-legged creature adjusts a foot by a millimeter over the course of a day, the dry cord carries a faint, papery shiver up to the wooden peg.

A hide-wrapped figure will kneel beside an alcove for half a week, resting two fingers lightly on the peg, or pressing the tip of a hammered copper awl into a row of pecked tally-marks on the cliff face and bracing the handle against their cheekbone. Through the metal and the bone of the jaw, the quiet mountain speaks—a sparse, bone-dry telegraph of ticks, faint scrapes, and the long, slow exhalations of creatures buried half a ridge away.

Even the cold hearths and the white ash have their purpose, though nothing has burned in them since the world still knew how to be consumed. Ash is dry, separate, and finished; it does not overlap, and it does not drift into anything else. Now and then a person will dip a thumb into the neat white mound beside their doorway and draw a short vertical line down the center of their forehead, or across the lintel of the shale hut, the way travelers elsewhere might check a compass. The cairns on the knife-edge ridges serve the same quiet watchfulness. Because gravity here is only a local agreement, a balanced column of flat stones tells anyone approaching whether *down* still points faithfully into the ridge—or whether the shelf has begun to lean, holding its stones sideways against the clear air like shelves on a wall.

And when they need to travel, they do not fight the cliffs.

Those hand-lashed ladders of bleached poles and hide strips, leaning against sheer vertical faces packed solid with owl-faced climbers, are not meant to lead onto empty ground. They lead to hinges—narrow notches and cornices where perspective has eroded thin. If you follow one of the hide-wrapped walkers up a ladder—a climb that may take the better part of a month, each boot placed with infinite patience between dry-scaled shoulders that neither flinch nor yield—you will see them stop at the lip of the rock and look out across a three-mile gulf.

They wait, unblinking, watching the silhouette of a distant pale massif where it crosses a nearer blade of slate. Slowly, as the eye stops insisting on depth, the far limestone face slips quietly in front of the near spire. The creatures clinging to the distant cliff grow no larger, yet they are suddenly within arm’s reach. With the unhurried care of someone stepping over a threshold, the walker lifts one wrapped foot over the motionless antlers of a nearby grazer and sets it directly down onto the far mountain wall. They do not shrink. They do not cross the chasm between. One moment they are smelling the dry keratin of the near ledge; the next, their dust-coated heel rests cleanly on stone three miles away, while the shoals of hovering winged things hang untouched in the abyss that was never entered.

Not all of them keep themselves in one place at all.

Just as the five long-necked beasts on the terraced promontory share a single translucent spine threaded invisibly between separate ledges, some of the oldest inhabitants have let their households—and even their own bodies—spread across the verticality. A single person may exist in three places at once, stitched together by an unseen coherence across an entire valley. One crouches in a shale shelter on the western rim, drawing sinew through hide; a second stands upside down beneath an overhanging shelf two miles to the south, coarse hair hanging upward toward the ceiling-rock while scraping dry mineral enamel from a lichen crust with a flint blade; a third sits waist-deep among narrow-ribbed browsers on a high basin floor, fingertips resting on a cairn.

They do not speak to one another, because there is no second voice to answer. When the figure beneath the overhang shifts a wrist to angle the flint scraper, the one in the shale shelter adjusts its grip on the bone needle in the exact same instant. When the one on the basin floor closes its amber eyes for a quarter-hour blink, the other two sit in cool darkness behind their lids until the pale afternoon returns to all three faces at once. They have learned to wear distance the way they wear stitched hides—as something loose, dry, and serviceable.

---

Yet the hide-wrapped people are only the most recognizable of the things that have made a true living out of this eroded stillness. Elsewhere, pressed into the folds where space and light have come undone, stranger citizens dwell—creatures that could not survive for a heartbeat in a world whose rules still held tight.

Go close to a narrow cornice where two dozen wool-coated beasts stand abreast, their pale flanks passing cleanly through one another like layered chalk drawings. Place a bare palm against the wool where three bodies occupy the same hand’s breadth of space, and you will notice something unexpected: it is warm there. Not merely the faint residual heat of a single dry hide, but a doubled, tripled, dense warmth, banked like dry embers inside a closed kiln.

That interior overlap is a country of its own, and it is inhabited.

Living entirely within the shared volume of intersecting ribs and pelts are pale, eyeless creatures no longer than a human forearm, flattened like peeled birch-bark and edged with rows of fine, feathery legs. They cannot exist in open air, nor in bare stone, nor even inside a single solitary animal. They require the quiet, impossible thickness created when two or three living bodies occupy one space without crushing each other. Through that warm, chalky interior maze they swim with infinite slowness, threading between ghosts of overlapping scapulae and lungs that take half an hour to rise and fall. They never cause a flinch or draw a drop of blood; to the wool-coated beasts, they are no more noticeable than warmth itself.

Only once in a great while, if a crowded grazer takes an hour to shift its stance by half a step and momentarily pulls its flank clear of its neighbor, will you glimpse the papery, translucent edge of one of these fold-dwellers retreating hastily back into the double-solid dark, like a sliver of mica slipping beneath a slate. The hide-wrapped people know they are there. Sometimes an elder with stiffened fingers will slide a dust-coated hand gently into the overlapping shoulder of two motionless beasts, letting the blind seam-dwellers brush dryly against their knuckles in the layered heat.

Out on the sun-lit limestone floors, where the afternoon light falls at the same sharp angle forever, live creatures thinner still.

Where three folded, insectile beasts huddle in a dry basin, their combined bodies cast one long, branching shadow shaped like an antlered animal that does not stand anywhere on the rock. Look closely across the chalky terraces and you will realize these mismatched shadows are not accidents of the light. They are a whole dry fauna of their own—beasts whose solid flesh and bone finished scouring away alongside the scree slopes epochs ago, leaving behind only their silhouettes, sharp-edged and cool against the matte stone.

These uncast animals are entirely alive, though they possess neither thickness nor weight. There are flat, broad-horned grazers browsing on bare limestone; coiled, ribbon-thin serpents stretched along vertical slate fins; long-legged hounds poised nose-down beside stone cairns, cast by nothing more than a jutting horn, a floating root-clump, and the curved flank of a resting climber aligned just so in the unmoving glare.

Because the sun never travels across the sky, the uncast cannot run as solid beasts once did. Instead, they migrate by patience. When one of their host creatures twitches a leg by half a millimeter over the course of a week, the dark silhouette on the basin floor takes a single, soundless step forward, its branching antlers gliding cleanly over a crack in the limestone. More often, they travel by borrowing the slow passage of the hide-wrapped people. If a walker approaches a basin where an uncast stag lies coiled across the path, the shadow detaches gently from the rock as the walker’s hem passes over it, flowing up across the stitched dry hides without weight or chill, riding the walker’s shoulder across the terrace until it reaches a fresh cluster of perched creatures capable of holding its shape on the stone ahead.

The people honor this with a quiet, wordless courtesy. If a walker feels the subtle, dry absence of an uncast beast sliding across their cloak—a brief darkening of the chalk-dust along their sleeve—they will halt mid-step for an hour or two, standing as still as the horned herds around them, until the flat silhouette has finished crossing onto the next ledge without tearing.

And then there is the air itself.

Out over the three-mile gulfs, where tens of thousands of winged, finned, and ribbon-bodied creatures hang in motionless shoals at every altitude, the sky is not merely a museum of suspended things. It is a meadow.

Stepping across the clear abyss, far from any cliff, move tall, reed-boned striders—pale, featherless creatures built on four slender, multi-jointed legs that end in broad, papery pads like dry seed-wings. They walk from one suspended flyer to the next, placing their weightless feet squarely on spread membranes, dorsal crests, and trailing tails as though the hovering shoals were stepping-stones across a dry marsh. Nothing sags beneath them. No wing beats in protest. Moving with a crisp, stately slowness that takes half a day to cross a hundred yards of open sky, the striders dip their narrow, toothless beaks into the tiny pockets of sun-warmed air cupped beneath the motionless wings, sipping nothing more than stillness and rising heat, their needle-thin shadows falling three thousand feet straight down onto the dry canyon floors.

Far out among those striders live the rarest of the hide-wrapped folk—the unmoored.

Every few generations, or whatever passes for generations in an afternoon that does not turn, one of the people on the cliffs finds that gravity has quietly stopped making agreements with them. It begins gently: a foot lingers an inch above a shale step; a clay bowl drifts a hand’s breadth above the hearth-stones, its mineral dust held neatly inside the rim by nothing at all. Rather than tying themselves down with pegs and cords, these few simply step off the lip of a spire and walk out into the open, windless light.

If you squint out across the central chasm, past the near blades of slate and through the layered drifts of suspended gliders, you may spot one of them half a mile from any rock. One sits cross-legged atop the broad, chalk-colored back of a hovering ribbon-beast, a copper awl tucked behind their ear, calmly stitching a new patch of pale hide onto a sleeve. Another drifts upright beside a floating gnarl of dry thorn-scrub, their wrapped heels resting lightly on nothing, one hand resting on the exposed roots that still grip the shape of an absent cliff.

They are not lost, and they are not trying to climb back. Now and then, when perspective folds cleanly across the valley, a walker on a limestone terrace will reach out a dusted hand, pass a fresh bundle of bleached sinew directly across two miles of clear air to an unmoored cousin sitting in the sky, and receive a flake of dry lichen enamel in return. Neither speaks. Neither hurries.

Back behind the low stone wall on the sun-lit terrace, the hunched figure finally finishes drawing the bone needle clear of the seam. The pale sinew pulls snug with a sound too small to travel past the knee—a tiny, dry whisper, like powdered feldspar shifting in a wooden bowl.

Beside the figure’s hip, the broad-backed creature’s ribs complete their long, glacial descent. Two paces away, a slack fiber cord tied to a wooden peg trembles once, carrying the faint news that a distant ledge has shifted its weight, or that a relative three valleys over has just stepped across a seam in the view. On the matte stone beyond the doorway, the branching shadow of an absent stag settles its dark antlers a hair’s breadth closer to the cold hearth’s ring of ash.

The figure lays the bone needle gently beside the copper awl, lifts two chalk-dusted fingers to the fresh seam along their wrist to make sure the border holds, and looks out across the vast, crowded silence. Millions of clear amber and dark glossy eyes look out beside them—from the cliffs, from the overlapping cornices, from the underside of overhangs, from the mid-air shoals—all warm, all dry, all quietly attending to the endless, unhurried work of remaining exactly where they are.
