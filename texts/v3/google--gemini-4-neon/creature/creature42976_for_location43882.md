At first glance across the bright, amber-lit volume, you might think the room belongs entirely to its fish and crabs and sleek upper-water hunters. The sightlines are wide, the water is bath-clear, and nothing seems large enough to hide.

Then, along the curve of the southern heat-exchangers, a smooth pale swell of travertine uncurls.

A long, translucent arm extends upward from the cushions of emerald velvet-moss—pale as skimmed milk, slender as a reed, ending in five unusually long fingers darkened at the tips by decades of gripping iron. With unhurried precision, the hand intercepts a rising wire-vine gas-bladder before it can reach the skylight, cups it gently in a half-webbed palm, and draws it back down into the swaying shadows of the ferns.

Only then do you see the others.

There are five of them right now—seldom more than six or seven in any generation, seldom fewer than four, their numbers held in quiet balance by the warmth of the copper coils and the yield of thirty paces of water. Stretched full length, a grown one measures roughly from a person’s crown to their knee: lean, supple-spined, built less for frantic speed than for patient holding and effortless coasting through the wide sunlit lanes.

Their skin is smooth and faintly translucent, taking on the dilute gold of the water around them, with the slow dark pulse of their vessels visible beneath the ribs. What first hides them from an untrained eye is the mineral they wear. Drinking and breathing this lime-heavy water from birth, they grow smooth, milky-white plates directly out of their own skin—curved across the brow and cheekbones, overlapping softly over the shoulders, stepping down the ridge of the spine in gentle scallops. Worn to a dull alabaster sheen by the constant eddy, the plates match the room’s porcelain insulators and lime-blinded pressure gauges so closely that a sleeping body tucked into a manifold crook looks like nothing more than old crusted stonework.

The youngest of the five—quick, restless, no longer than a human forearm—has only papery pale seams tracing its temples and knuckles. The oldest, resting almost permanently among the warm southern pipes, carries a heavy, sculpted mantle of white mineral across its back and skull, smoothed into fluid ridges like a carving left for centuries in a riverbed.

Their faces are narrow, serene, and startlingly watchful. They have no snouts and no external ears. Wide-set beneath the milky arch of the brow sit large, forward-facing eyes the color of oiled bronze, fitted with slow, clear inner lids that slide shut whenever mineral dust snows down from the rafters. Behind their jaws lie neat, fluted gill-covers—pale and ribbed like tiny porcelain casings—that part just enough to show a brief flash of warm crimson when they exert themselves, and fold down into tight, waterproof seams when the cold comes.

They do not experience the tank as loud. To them, the mechanical racket is simply the climate, as natural and necessary as air is to creatures below the clouds. Because their hollow brow-plates and calcified ribs carry vibration directly into their bones, they read the chamber the way a blind craftsman reads wood under his fingertips. Press a palm or a white-plated temple to the iron bulkhead, and they can feel the exact tempo of the hidden hydraulic relays ticking down the wall, the faint electrical throb humming through the copper conduits, and—miles below—the slow, resonant groan of the distant hull warming on its sunlit side. The central drive-shaft’s bass *thrum-drag*, turning twice every three seconds, is simply the floor of the world. If it ever missed a beat, they would feel it as a drop in the stomach.

Between the deep hammer of the shaft and the thin, piercing whistle of the brass bleed-valve near the dome, there is a clean, open band of middle sound. That is where their voices live.

They do not speak with lungs. Instead, they harvest the clear, gas-filled bladders from the wire-vines just before they ripen and tear loose. Holding a single glassy berry inside a soft pouch beneath the tongue, they press it gently against the resonant dome of the palate, vibrating it with tiny contractions of the throat and gill-arches. What comes out into the water is a clear, liquid chiming—short bell-notes, dry wooden clicks, and rising trills that sound like fine glassware lightly tapped beneath a warm bath.

When two of them converse across the open middle water, they often pass a single bladder back and forth from palm to palm, letting it drift a pace between them before catching it, speaking in turns with the same borrowed pocket of gas until it shrinks to a silver bead, slips from their fingers, and rises to chime one last, fading note against the glass dome.

Between the mechanical surges, their hours are unhurried and deeply physical.

You will see the three adults gliding along the wide lanes between the manifolds, pushing off a smooth rivet-head with long, gripping feet and coasting ten paces on a single lazy sweep of their slender limbs. They graze delicately on the sweet, buoyant tips of the emerald moss and the tender curled edges of the hanging water-ferns. Along the floor, they pick up walnut-sized valve-crabs without haste, cradling them against a rounded iron seam and tapping open the brass-plated shell with a hardened, magnetite-stained knuckle—never crushing more than they mean to eat, and often tucking the brightest flake of shed brass or verdigris into a fold of skin behind their collarbone to carry away.

They leave the needle-thin filament-fish alone; their coppery flesh is too bitter, too quick. In fact, when one of the older dwellers rests motionless near the humming porcelain insulators, taking a slight charge into its mineral plates, a ribbon of filament-fish will sometimes break from the copper cable and hover in neat geometric alignment along the sleeper’s white spine, grazing gently on the microscopic velvet that gathers in the seams of its shell.

And during the quiet stretches—especially when afternoon fades to violet-black outside the dome and starlight turns the clear water silver-dark—they gather at the center of the room, around the endlessly turning bronze drive-shaft.

This is the secret behind that slight, burnished waist worn into the metal at mid-height. It was not the water alone that narrowed solid bronze. Every day, and through most of the dark hours, the five of them wrap their long forearms and legs around that comfortable band of the axle, resting their smooth lime-plated chins against the oily-warm satin of the turning column. Clinging loosely together in a drowsy knot, they let the shaft carry them around and around—twice every three seconds—their heartbeats slowing until they fall into lockstep with the *thrum-drag* rising from miles below. Generations of mineral-dusted palms, calcified ribs, and resting cheekbones have polished that waist into the bronze, an embrace recorded in metal across a thousand years.

They never need to watch a clock to know when the four-minute surge is coming. Three or four heartbeats before the ballast-governor trips, a tiny preliminary shiver runs up through the floor-plates—too faint for the fish to notice, sharp as a tapped nail to creatures listening through their ribs.

Instantly, the five scatter into practiced postures.

The heavily calcified elder does not even try to outswim the cold anymore; it slips into the warm lee behind the southern copper coils, hooks its dark-tipped fingers securely around a rounded pipe, pulls its fluted gill-covers flush shut, and becomes an alabaster boulder half-buried in green moss.

The three adults angle themselves toward the northern manifold and the open middle lanes, bracing their feet against the pebble-worn rivets. When the deafening *clang* shakes down the snowstorm of mineral dust and the bronze intake louvre grinds open, releasing that violent, frigid jet of meltwater, they do not flee it—they work it.

For thirty electric seconds, as the room fills with shimmering, glass-like thermoclines that twist the afternoon light into writhing lenses, one adult reaches straight into the roaring fringe of the jet, snatching dislodged moss-spores, tumbled crabs, and fresh white flecks of mineral torn from the far walls. The other two rise deftly into the optical chaos of the middle water. While the sleek pale skate-newts drop out of the bright upper glare to snap at the panicked filament-fish, their own stealth briefly broken by the bending curtains of cold and warm water, the two dwellers move in silent coordination—closing from opposite sides of a shimmering distortion to catch a distracted hunter cleanly behind its feather-like electro-receptors. It happens quickly, without fuss, a flash of pale limbs in silver bubbles.

Meanwhile, the thin-plated youngster does none of the practical work at all. Nearly every cycle, it launches itself recklessly into the outer rim of the freezing stream just to be caught and flung tumbling across the sunlit courtyard—spinning head-over-heels through blizzards of loose oxygen bubbles, chiming bright, breathless notes from a mouthful of stolen vine-gas until it catches the sweeping edge of a flattened fern curtain to brake its flight.

Then comes the heavy, shuddering *boom* of the closing louvre.

The violent rush dies back into the broad, lazy clockwise eddy. The sharp sheets of cold melt away into the bath-warm glow of the floor coils. The five drift back together beneath the rising fern curtains, tearing strips of lean white flesh from their catch to share with the elder on the warm pipes, and spending a quiet minute picking stray grains of lime-scale and torn moss out of one another’s gill-seams with gentle, attentive fingers.

They do not build machines—the machine is already the whole of creation—and they have no memory of why the tank was made, or what lies at the bottom of the tower. Yet they are meticulous housekeepers of their small cosmos, driven by an intimate, unspoken sense of how the room ought to feel and sound.

If thick dead mats of velvet-moss choke the lower lip of the brass bleed-valve, warping its steady piercing whistle into a ragged stutter, one of the adults will climb patiently up the curved ribs and pluck the blockage free until the note rings clean and true again. When the hanging water-ferns grow too dense across the exact center of the ribbed dome, dulling the ordinary afternoon brightness, they pinch back the longest fronds with their hardened nails, keeping the window clear. If one of the pale, slow snails creeps onto a smooth gauge-face where they like to rest, they lift it gently by its mathematically exact hexagonal shell and set it back down upon the iron seam.

And tucked into the sheltered pockets of the northern manifold—or anchored around the milky brass bezels of the dead pressure gauges—are the things they make purely for the pleasure of keeping them.

Using tough, severed stems of the left-handed wire-vines, they string together the empty hexagonal shells left behind by dead snails, alternating them with the brightest flakes of tarnished brass and verdigris salvaged from molted crabs, weighted at the bottom with smooth mineral pebbles broken off ancient bolt-heads. Hung just beneath the fern curtains where the clockwise eddy turns them gently without tearing them loose, these tiny mobiles spin in quiet, disciplined circles, catching the clean daylight and throwing wandering flecks of copper-gold across the blind faces of the gauges.

Sometimes, too, you will find them arranging little rows of white lime-beads along the flat flanges of the cast-iron headers—never quite permanent, always swept slightly askew by the next four-minute blast, patiently nudged back into order afterward.

Yet their deepest habit has nothing to do with the floor coils, or the food, or even the turning bronze shaft. It belongs to the ceiling.

At least once every bright afternoon, when the sun stands high enough to pour clean, direct light down through the pitted glass, they rise—one at a time, or two together—into the sparse, cool water just beneath the dome. Turning onto their backs, spreading their narrow arms wide to hold their place against the slow drift, they float inches beneath the polished underside of the glass, dark bronze eyes wide open behind their clear inner lids, staring straight out into the pale, colorless violet-blue sky.

Beside their upturned faces, escaped gas-bladders from the wire-vines strike the glass with soft chimes, flatten into shivering silver coins, and creep slowly uphill along the curved ribs until they reach the brass bleed-valve and vanish with a thin hiss into the brightness beyond.

They know gas. They hold it in their palms and sing with it. Watching their own bubbles flatten and flee through the brass rim, they understand the emptiness outside the dome as one vast, bottomless, freezing bubble—a silent outer ocean of escaped breath pressing invisibly against the other side of the glass.

And every once in a long while, something happens out there that brings all five of them to a complete halt.

Perhaps the upper-atmosphere winds lean hard against the unseen iron hull miles above the earth, and the whole courtyard tilts by a fraction of a degree—just enough for the bath-warm water to shift its weight with a quiet, heavy sigh against the northern curve. Or perhaps, out beyond the pitted glass, the slow white smudge of a cirrus cloud drifts lazily *beneath* the horizon line of the dome’s brass rim.

When that happens, even the heavily mantled elder lifts its alabaster crown from the copper coils. The youngling stops chasing bubbles. The three adults hang motionless in the wide, open lanes of amber water, pale palms turned slightly upward, listening to the faint cant of their universe in the marrow of their bones.

They have no word for *cloud*, or *wind*, or *ground*. They do not know that an entire planet turns far below that passing white smudge, or that anything could ever live down in the cold dry silence outside their glass. They only know that something immense, slow, and untouchable has moved past the lower edge of their sky—and for two or three cycles afterward, even through the next *clang* and freezing surge, their glassy chiming voices stay very low and sparse, answering only the steady *thrum-drag* of the bronze axle.

In a sealed courtyard whose hatches grew shut before memory began, nothing truly leaves, and nothing is ever thrown away.

If you look closely along the curved iron bulkheads behind the copper coils, or high among the smooth ribs near the porcelain insulators, you will notice certain pale, travertine-coated undulations that do not quite match the rhythm of the old rivet-lines. Here is a gentle mound shaped subtly like a tucked shoulder; there, twin shallow hollows like worn brow-plates, and a delicate scalloped ridge melting seamlessly into the velvety magnetite skin of the hull.

That is where their dead rest.

When the oldest among them grows so heavy with living lime-scale that its limbs can no longer hold against the thirty-second surge, it does not fight the weight. It chooses a warm, sheltered seam along the southern curve, hooks its dark-tipped fingers one last time behind a smooth-worn flange, and closes its clear inner lids. Over several bright afternoons and silver-dark nights, the others stay close beside it—stroking the thickening white plates of its crown, taking turns holding harvested gas-bladders directly against its calcified temple so the soft bell-chimes ring straight through the bone. Slowly, peacefully, the elder’s pulse lengthens until it matches the twice-every-three-seconds beat of the bronze shaft, and then slips cleanly into it.

The warm, mineral-rich water finishes the work without rot or disturbance, sealing flesh and alabaster gently into the smooth, rounded geology of the walls.

And months later—once the water grows a fraction heavier with unclaimed lime, and the outer tower shivers through a season of sharper frost—one of the adults will tuck a single pale, leathery egg deep inside the brightest cushion of emerald velvet-moss on the warmest southern coil. Rocked gently by the lazy clockwise eddy, hummed to by the faint alternating current in the copper lines, it waits beneath the clean, ordinary afternoon light—ready to open its bronze eyes onto a thirty-pace sky, and learn the rhythm of the room that made it.
