If you climb to the rim and stand there for an hour, you will swear that nothing lives here except the birds, the spiders, the clicking frogs, and whatever keeps the wind moving. The volume is too bare to hide a large body. Sunlight hits every wet rib; the open center holds nothing but upward drizzle and spinning swifts.

It takes longer than an hour to notice the small refusals of decay.

In a place where moss grows wrist-deep on every damp shelf, where orb-spiders string heavy webs across the leeward notches twice a day, and where mineral drips build pale calcite straws down from every overhang, the bone-pipes wedged into the stone fissures should have choked shut decades ago. The catwalk rotted through. The cedar kindling in the dry-stone shelter turned silver and sodden. Yet when a western gust shears across the crown, the hollowed bird-femurs and clay tubes still speak cleanly, their bores clear of moss and silk, their notched lips sharp. Look closer at the eastern arch—far past the splintered end of the old planks, where no human boot could find purchase without fresh pegs—and you will see a fissure holding a pipe the builders never set. It is a cloud-hawk’s wing-bone, pared down not with a steel blade but ground unevenly against rough schist, packed into the crack with bitter black resin from the stunted cliff-shrubs and tufts of grey-green lichen instead of twisted hair-cord.

Whatever keeps the mountain tuned does not use the shelter, never lights the blackened hearth-stones, and has no need of planks.

You see it first only when it decides to stop being stone.

High under the curve of the northern rib, pressed flush into a vertical fluting where a curtain of ladder-fern breaks the glare, something shifts by half a hand’s breadth. Until that moment it looked like a weathered shelf of pale schist draped in wet lichen—long, narrow, folded in sharp geometric planes. Then a bead of condensed water runs down the stone, strikes its shoulder, and breaks cleanly off a slick, waxed flank instead of soaking in. A milky inner eyelid slides horizontally across an eye the color of pale amber quartz. Behind its jaw, a wide, silvery dish of taut skin shudders visibly, drinking the deep bass thrum of the summit.

Folded against the rock, it is scarcely larger than a hunched lynx, though built on altogether different geometry: too long in the forearms, too spare in the waist, every joint folding back in tight, crane-like angles. Its body is covered along the spine and crown in fine, oiled filaments the exact shifting grey-ochre of damp mountain lichen, combed flat by ten thousand gusts so water beads and runs off them in silver threads. Beneath that sparse crest, its skin is smooth and thin as cured parchment, mottled like pitted slate when wet, paling toward bleached bone when the crisp sun dries it. It has no outer ears—only those wide, recessed acoustic plates behind each cheek, rimmed with stiff, hair-thin sensory bristles that never stop trembling in the wind-shear. Its fingers are long, spare, and startlingly nimble, the first two digits and thumb free and tipped with blunt, dark nails worn into flat chisels from gripping schist seams. Its toes are splayed and pale underneath, ridged with soft, wet-clinging pads that hold slick vertical stone as easily as a frog holds a fern-bract.

It does not belong to the floor of the world, because here there is no floor.

When a bright break opens in the weather and the updraft steadies, it does not leap or flap. It simply unclenches its toes from the fluted groove and steps backward into the fifty-yard void.

For two heartbeats it drops cleanly, the same two or three feet a detached water droplet falls before the rising breath catches it. Then its elongated outer fingers sweep wide, and what looked like tight, leathery pleats along its ribs and flanks unfurl into broad, scalloped sails of translucent membrane, joined to a smaller fan stretched between its ankles. At the same instant, its narrow chest deepens; paired internal air-sacs along its ribs and beneath its chin draw in the rising thermal until the skin between its pale, hollow struts pulls taut as drumhead.

It stalls.

Hanging thirty paces from any wall, with a thousand feet of dark stone throat yawning directly beneath its feet, it weighs almost nothing at all. Where the swifts must carve the air in frantic, snapping gyres and the cloud-hawks must buy height with heavy wings before plunging like stones, this creature simply finds the exact altitude where its weight and the mountain’s breath cancel each other out. In clean mountain sun, its outstretched membranes turn almost luminous—pale amber and parchment-gold, mapped with a fine dark lattice of hollow bones and slender veins, glowing like autumn leaves held against a lantern. It drifts slowly in the bright center, tilting a single wrist to wheel lazily across the sun-shafts, spinning half a turn as a thermal eddy clips its heel, riding the column of air with the unhurried poise of a suspended drop of water.

Even its shadow is strange: a pale, tea-colored smudge gliding across the emerald moss of the opposite rib, so slow and unhurried that the cliff-beasts on the outer ledges scarcely freeze as it passes. The swifts do not scatter from it; their screaming flocks split cleanly around its quiet span like water parting around a reed. Only the broad-winged cloud-hawks at the upper lip regard it with wary, tilted heads, keeping to the bright rim when it rises toward the crown. Near the trailing edge of its left sail sits an old, healed crescent notch—the pale scar of a talon-strike years ago, when a young hawk misjudged the breadth of what lived below. Whenever it banks hard against a western gust, that scarred edge gives off a faint, reedy sigh.

It is not silent by accident. It is silent because it is listening.

To live permanently inside the mouth of a roaring stone bottle is to live inside pressure. Ordinary ears would be hammered numb in a week. This creature does not hear the roar as noise; it reads the entire hollow chamber as a single shifting architecture of vibrations. Through its silvery cheek-plates and the inflated resonance sacs inside its chest, it feels the mountain’s deep organ-hum in its bones—and registers every disturbance pressed into it. It knows when a bladder-moss pod splits on the southern overhang because the tiny wet pop leaves a hair-thin ripple in the eddy behind the third rib. It knows when a cloud-hawk folds its wings high overhead before the shadow even enters the crown, because the sudden dive tears a brief seam in the updraft. It knows, minutes before the sky darkens, that a dense boil of cloud is climbing the dark throat below, because the air thickens against its membranes and drops the mountain’s bass note by a fraction of a tone.

And it knows every pipe in the stone.

When a gust shifts and one of the ancient bird-bones fails to wake—or wakes ragged, flat, choked with a night’s growth of spider silk or a sliver of loosened moss—the creature slips sideways on the draft, folds its sails in a single crisp snap of parchment, and lands weightless and head-down beside the fissure. Braced by its ridged hind toes, it leans one acoustic cheek close to the pitted rock, listening with exquisite patience. One long, chisel-nailed finger probes the bore, hooking out damp cobweb, a drowned midge, a crumb of mineral crust. If the pitch-seal around a clay tube has cracked in the frost-chill of a dry night, it climbs down to the gnarled shrubs wedged in the deeper seams, bites a bead of bitter resin from a split bark-knot, chews it soft between blunt back teeth, and presses it neatly around the tube’s collar with its thumbs.

Then it hangs back from the wall, fills its throat-sacs, and answers.

Its voice has nothing of a bird’s shriek or a beast’s growl. Venting air through narrow, muscular slits tucked beneath its jaw, it produces a thin, pure, reedy tone—less a cry than a sustained interval held steady against the stone’s bass roar. It does not sing melodies. It finds the harmonic fifth of the eastern arch and locks onto it, holding the note until the two sounds—bone-pipe and living throat—interlock so tightly that the air between them begins to throb in slow, chest-felt waves. If its own crude addition, the ground-down hawk-bone in the high fissure, drifts slightly sharp in the dry afternoon sun, it stays beside it for an hour, listening to the sour friction between the pitches, occasionally pulling the bone free to grind its lip three strokes against wet slate before wedging it back into the resin.

There are no others of its kind on this needle. Perhaps there are others on distant peaks across the flat white floor of the clouds—solitary, hollow-boned gliders riding the high barometric troughs from ridge to ridge, crossing the bright upper sky only once in a decade when the storm-walls align. If this one once made those crossings, it has not made them in a long time. Its knuckles are thickened and pale at the joints; the edges of its sails have the weathered, slightly frayed translucence of old paper; and more than age holds it here. Most peaks across the sea of clouds merely howl—raw, ragged wind tearing over dead rock. Somewhere in its long memory lies the morning it drifted out of the western glare, drawn miles off its course by a clean, deliberate chord that no uncarved mountain had ever made, slipped between the ribs of the open crown, and discovered an instrument left unfinished by hands that never came back.

Between the squalls, it lives sparely, taking what the throat gives up.

Drifting slowly through the bright middle with its wide, pale jaws slightly parted or snapping out a single long arm with startling, frog-like speed, it gathers the heavier freight hoisted up from the sunlit basin far below: fat, wind-stunned lowland beetles, glassy moths as broad as a thumb, occasional bruised dragonflies spun dizzy by the long climb through the mountain’s core. Along the wet margins it plucks swollen orb-spiders from behind the ribs, carefully stepping around the anchor lines so the silk will catch again by dusk. It drinks not from puddles—there are none—but by tipping its chin beneath the delicate white calcite straws, catching the mineral-heavy drops on its tongue without snapping the fragile tubes, or nipping open the taut sacs of the bladder-mosses for the cool condensed water inside. The thumbnail frogs sitting inches from its knees do not fear it; their sharp metallic clicks occupy a bright upper register it leaves undisturbed, a small bright stitching along the hem of the mountain’s deeper sound.

Of the human things left behind, it has made its own quiet map.

It never enters the dry-stone shelter. Once, years ago, it poked its narrow head beneath the low slate lintel, smelled the dead, sour mineral reek of cold ash, felt how the low stone ceiling strangled the chamber’s resonance into a dull, boxed hush, and backed out immediately. The hearth stays moss-carpeted and cold; the split cedar kindling rots undisturbed into pale sponge. The rotted catwalk means nothing to it except a convenient perch for drying its sails after a heavy drenching, its toes hooked over the green-oxidized copper wire that still binds the adze-hewn planks.

Two relics, however, hold its attention every day.

The first is the empty woven basket dangling on its frayed hemp line over the central abyss. The creature never touches it, never climbs into it; it watches it. Suspended out in the clear throat where the mountain’s breath runs strongest, the basket is an honest gauge. When it swings in wide, lazy, erratic circles, the lower updraft is thin and broken; when the frayed rope pulls almost straight out toward the open center and the basket steadies like a buoy on a taut tether, the deep core is warming and a rich column of lowland insects is about to surge upward. Often you can find the creature crouched motionless on the spur just above the line, amber eyes tracking the slow pendulum of woven reeds against the dark shaft below.

The second is the small cairn of river-rounded pebbles beside it.

In a chamber where almost everything rises—mist, spores, detached raindrops, torn lichen, midges, even the creature’s own almost weightless body—those smooth, dense stones are impossible objects. They were carried up from a river miles beneath the cloud-floor, and they contain something this hollow summit barely knows: pure, stubborn downwardness. Every few days, usually during the eerie barometric pauses before a great weather change—when the wind-shear slackens, the bone-pipes fall silent one by one, and even the swifts vanish down into the neck—the creature climbs down to the spur. It settles beside the cairn, lifts one smooth grey pebble between its long, flat-nailed fingers, and turns it slowly against its damp palm, feeling the cool, concentrated weight of it. Often it simply taps the stone once against the pitted schist—a clean, hard, dry *tock* that rings oddly sharp in the damp air—and sets it carefully back on the stack.

Only very rarely, perhaps once in a season when the deep throat beneath the peak goes strangely quiet and the rising breath falters, does it lean far out over the inner lip and open its fingers.

The river pebble does not stall. It does not shimmer or drift upward. It drops straight into the dark narrowing neck of the mountain, vanishing instantly out of sight. The creature stretches its long neck over the abyss, sails tucked tight, silvery acoustic plates angled downward, listening with its whole hollow body as the stone strikes once—a bright wet clink—then again, deeper, flatter, then a faint faraway hollow ring from somewhere impossibly far down in the warm core of the earth, followed at last by a soft, indistinguishable silence.

It stays leaned over the throat until the air shifts against its face.

Almost always, within a minute, the mountain answers. A cool, damp surge pushes up from the dark below; the empty woven basket lifts and tilts on its frayed rope; the eastern bone-pipe wakes with a low, breathy moan; and a dense boil of cloud erupts up through the shaft, swallowing the far stone ribs in luminous silver-grey. In three heartbeats the light flattens and cools. The bone-pipes drop a half-tone as heavy mist packs their bores. Along the wet moss walls, the thumbnail frogs burst into a sudden metallic chorus.

On the spur beside the cairn, the creature rises onto its splayed toes, unfolds its slate-darkened sails into the rising rush of cloud, and lets the mountain lift it backward off the rock. For a single breath its pale, ribbed silhouette hangs clear against the swirling silver vapor, a thin reedy fifth trembling softly out of its throat in answer to the high arch overhead—and then the mist closes cleanly around it, leaving nothing in the roaring, empty chapel but wet stone, rising droplets, and the long, steady chord of the wind.
