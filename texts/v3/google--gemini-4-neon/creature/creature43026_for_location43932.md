If you stand still long enough in the middle of the sparse, wet floor—long enough for your ears to stop fighting the roar and for the double-stroke throb to settle into the joints of your knees—you realize you have not been alone in the room since you pushed through the ferns.

You missed them for the same reason you would miss a heron in reeds, except there are no reeds here to hide behind. There is only bright, even green daylight, bare water, and four or five stout pillars. You missed them because nothing in them breaks the room. Their stillness has the same pitch as the calcite aprons; their slow movements sit cleanly inside the mechanical beat.

The first one you see is scarcely twelve paces away, crouched on its heels beside one of the central root-columns with both hands submerged flat beneath the vibrating skin of the water. Until its head turns—a fraction of an inch, timed squarely to the heavy downward stroke of the engines below—you had taken its bowed back for a smooth, pale buttress of root and mineral crust.

Then your eye adjusts, and the others detach themselves from the bright green geometry of the chamber. One is leaning its bare shoulder against the vertical flutes of the weeping moss wall, motionless save for the gentle rise and fall of its throat. A third is stretched half on its side along a shallow channel between moss-cushions, its lower flank sunk into the ankle-deep pool so that the diamond standing waves run straight up against its ribs and ring its hips in crisp, motionless glass.

There are only the three of them grown—no more than the twenty-pace pocket can hold without crowding its open air.

At first glance your mind tries, and fails, to make them human. They are roughly the height of a slight person when they rise onto their long shins, upright-backed, narrow-waisted, and wholly bare, with wet, hairless skin the translucent pale of soaked parchment or peeled willow. Yet the longer you look, the less human they become. Their skulls are smooth and subtly lengthened toward the crown, without a trace of outer ears; in their place, recessed neatly behind the line of the jaw, sit oval patches of taut, iridescent skin that quiver in tiny concentric rings whenever the sub-floor piston strikes. Their eyes are set wide under smooth brows—dark, glossy peat-brown ringed with a metallic sheen of copper, blinked clear every few seconds by pale inner lids that flick across them in lockstep with the rhythm of the room.

Along the nape of each neck, down the outer ridges of their long forearms, and along the backs of their calves grow sparse, orderly rows of fine, colorless filaments, no thicker than wet thread. In the chamber's continuous static field they never plaster down against the slick skin; they stand lifted and faintly splayed, just like the delicate rootlets hanging from the canopy, wrapping each body in a pale, mist-bright halo that catches the leaf-light.

Every surface of them carries the mineral signature of the water they inhabit. Where skin takes the most wear—across their flat knees, along the fronts of their shins, over the knuckles, and at the blunt tips of their four long, slender fingers—the dermis has toughened and darkened over the years into smooth, glossy plates stained pale ochre and faint verdigris, hard as horn and slick with warm condensation. Along the sides of their lower ribs and tucked into the hollows beneath their collarbones lie narrow, pleated grooves, soft and slightly parted. Inside those damp folds thrives the same velvety, rust-orange microbial film that coats the submerged roots of the floor. When one shifts its weight and its chest expands, the pleats open a fraction wider, showing thin, bright seams of living orange against the pale wet flesh.

They do not move the way dry-land creatures move. Watching the one beside the root-column rise and cross toward the far side of the room is like watching something balanced on water rather than striding through it. Its feet are broad, flat, and unarched, with long, partially webbed toes cushioned on thick, translucent pads. It sets each foot down flat on the exact quiet interval between the twin hydraulic blows, lifting it again with a slow, liquid roll. Where your boots smashed the floor's diamond weave into sloppy, widening rings, its wading barely parts the lattice; the sharp geometric ripples close smoothly behind its heels before the next step falls.

Even when it halts beside a solitary sedge, it is never truly still. A faint, elastic ripple climbs continuously up from the soles of its feet through its calves, thighs, and narrow waist, dying out in a soft shimmer along the raised filaments of its arms. If you look closely at the thin, almost transparent skin inside its wrists and ankles, you can see a faint tide of pale blue-green blood pulsing upward beneath the surface—not to the frantic beat of a mammal's heart, but to the slow, heavy double-stroke of the engines deep below the peat. Their own hearts, tucked behind broad, flexible breastbones, beat rarely and lazily; generations in this resonant pocket have handed half the work of their circulation over to the floor itself. Beyond the fern curtain, where the bog goes cold and quiet and still, their blood would thicken and pool in their long limbs within an hour. The machinery buried beneath the mire is not merely their shelter; it is an external organ they cannot unplug from.

Nothing about their days looks idle, yet nothing looks hurried. Each of them works with the same absorbed, timeless regularity as the beetles and the wrens, woven so tightly into the chamber's steady state that it is hard to say where their bodies end and the room's upkeep begins.

The one crouching by the pillar is reading the floor. With both palms pressed flat just beneath the clear water, fingers spread wide, it stays completely still until it catches a tiny snag in the vibration—a loosened scrap of yellow leaf-rib not yet softened by the warm shallows, or a flake of ochre lime broken off a column base. Anything that mars the standing waves scatters the subtle pulse rising through the moss and blurs the room's clear acoustic lines. With absurd gentleness, its long, dark-tipped fingers pinch the stray stem out of the water. It turns it once, strips two tiny ribbed snails from the underside with a hardened thumb, cracks their shells cleanly *on* the heavy hydraulic stroke so the sound vanishes wholly inside the floor's percussion, and slips the pale meat into its wide, soft-lipped mouth. Then it presses the bare stem down into the warm, teeming sphagnum near the wall, where the microbes will break it down fastest. Behind its withdrawn hand, the diamond ripples snap back into glassy perfection.

A few paces off, the oldest of the three—whose shins, elbows, and rounded shoulders are heavily sheathed in smooth, verdigris-veined calcite, so thick in places that its joints move with a slow, deliberate creakless weight—remains half-reclined in its shallow channel. It is feeding and resting at once. Every minute or two, it drags the grooved underside of its hand gently along the submerged root-edge, gathering a glistening ribbon of rust-orange biofilm across its palm. Some it draws slowly across its tongue; the rest it presses carefully into the damp, parted pleats along its lower ribs, tending the warm colonies nested inside its own skin.

While it lies there, the rest of the room's fauna treats it as architecture. A thick-ribbed water-snail glides unhurriedly over its mineralized ankle, laying a faint pearlescent track across the pale crust before the weeping sheen erases it. A damp-feathered wren drops from a high root, alights directly on the ridge of its raised knee, snatches two iridescent midges out of the hovering ribbon drawn upward by the static field of its forearm, lets out its sharp, three-note whistle on the tempo, and flits away without the creature's copper eyes so much as shifting. A pale marsh frog hops neatly from the wet moss into the shallow hollow above its collarbone, settles its glossy belly against the trembling skin, and folds its rattling call straight off the resonance of the creature's chest.

Beside the weeping wall, the third one stands with its forearm lifted toward a heavy, water-beaded fern frond dipping close to the mineral-darkened bark. Its pale filaments are bristling wide, visibly strained by the charge accumulating across its damp skin. Slowly it brings the hardened, conductive pad of its middle finger to within a sliver of an inch of the dark bark.

*Tick.*

A crisp violet needle snaps through the damp air straight into its fingertip. A light shiver travels instantly up its arm and across its shoulders; its lifted filaments flatten for half a heartbeat before buoying outward again in a softer, more even halo. Its clear inner eyelids blink once in unison, and the long, subtle throat-sac running down its neck slackens with something unmistakably like relief.

When the wading one finishes by the sedge, it glides over to join the one at the wall. They do not speak—not with voices meant to fight a turbine hall—and they do not embrace. Instead, they stop facing one another a forearm's length apart and raise their wet wrists until the pale halos of their splayed filaments nearly kiss. You cannot hear it beneath the roar, only see it: a faint, rapid succession of tiny violet pinpricks dancing in the narrow gap between their damp knuckles as the static charge balances evenly between their two bodies. Then both lay a single flat palm against the same fluted root-column, fingers spread wide on the slick bark. Watching the taut skin-drums behind their jaws quiver in sudden, sharp bursts, you realize they are talking through the wood—tapping and flexing fine vibrations from their throats and hardened fingertips straight into the column, riding crisp signals along the carrier wave of the massive hum below.

It is during one of these quiet intervals that a tiny flaw enters the sound of the room.

You scarcely catch it yourself—just a faint, sour shimmer somewhere high in the brassy wall of frog-song, where an aging frog clinging halfway up the moss wall has slipped a hair flat of the electrical overtone and missed its breath in the seamless handoff.

All three of the pale inhabitants register it at once. The taut ovals behind their jaws tighten; the one nearest the wall turns its smooth head smoothly, without a hint of startle, fixing its dark copper gaze on the exact groove of moss where the faltering frog sits. It glides three paces across the shallow water, reaches up one remarkably long arm, and closes its dark-tipped fingers gently around the frog. None of the dozens of frogs clinging inches away break rhythm or leap; the one beside the gap widens its translucent throat-sac and bridges the missing note without a tremor. The tall inhabitant brings the frog down and holds it for two slow strokes of the floor against its own pleated neck—almost as if feeling the crooked pulse directly against its bones—before opening its mouth and swallowing it whole. In two heartbeats, the harmonic wall of the chamber is spotless again.

Now and then—governed by no clock of morning or evening, only by some slow, internal tide of pressure in the charged air—all three gather around the central root-columns. Even the heavily crusted elder rises slowly from its shallow channel, water sheeting cleanly off its calcified thighs. They do not face one another; each leans its bare spine, heels, and the back of its smooth skull directly against a fluted pillar, letting the wood take their weight. Their pale inner lids draw shut. Their pleated throats swell slowly, not with the frantic shimmer of the frogs, but in a deep, steady expansion that draws their narrow ribs wide.

You do not hear what comes out of them so much as feel the entire twenty-pace pocket change density.

A deep, wet, bone-shaking resonance swells up out of their chests and locks directly into the heavy machinery below—not fighting the roar, not rising above it, but supplying the single missing overtone that binds the hydraulic double-stroke, the electrical drone, and the bright frog-clatter into one vast, seamless chord. The air thickens against your chest; your teeth hum cleanly instead of rattling. All around the room, the tall vertical ribbons of iridescent midges draw narrower and straighter along their invisible field lines. On the floor beneath your boots, the diamond lattices sharpen until every intersecting ridge looks cut from blown glass. For several long minutes the three of them hold the room inside that chord, eyes closed, filaments trembling in perfect phase, before letting it ebb back into the steady, roaring baseline without a break.

Nothing in this pocket accumulates, and nothing is wasted—not even them.

If you let your gaze linger along the pale ochre calcite aprons ringing the bases of the pillars, you notice that one of the smooth, rounded buttresses on the far side of the room has a curious symmetry. Its glossy, water-worn mineral slope curls inward at just the quiet angle of a tucked knee, rising into a gentle, bowed ridge like a spine folded forward against the root. Over decades, the mineral salts that glaze their shins and knuckles creep steadily higher across their skin until the eldest of them can no longer lift its weight from the floor; it settles cleanly against a pillar base, draws its long limbs in, and lets the warm, teeming water work from within while the weeping lime seals softly over the outer shell, turning it back into the weathered architecture of the chamber.

And down by your own ankle, in a shallow rust-velvet channel between two moss-cushions where the water runs clearest, something pale and finger-long darts between the copper-shelled beetles and slips beneath the pale stalk of a sedge. If you catch it before it vanishes, you see a translucent, tadpole-slender body tipped with delicate, feathery orange gill-plumes and four tiny, freshly budded hands—moving in tight, rhythmic jerks timed already to the engines underfoot, waiting out its slow years in the shallow water until the room has room for one more upright shape.

Sooner or later, if you stay motionless in the bright green roar, they finish measuring you.

You are not a predator to them, and you are not a threat; nothing here has a memory of panic. You are simply a strange, jagged snag in the chamber's equilibrium. Your boots crush the crisp diamond weave into muddy rings; your dry clothes drink the static unevenly, bending the nearest midge-ribbon out of plumb; the heat and erratic beat of your body sit entirely off-tempo from the floor.

The sleekest of the three detaches itself from the root-column and glides quietly toward you, stepping lightly on the off-beats so its approach scarcely stirs the water. It stops two paces away, tilting its smooth, earless head slightly so one recessed skin-drum turns toward your chest, listening to the hurried, untethered knock of your heart. Its pale filaments lean faintly in your direction, reading the clumsy shape you cut in the charged air.

After a long moment, it steps a pace closer and extends one long, wet arm. Its hardened, mineral-darkened fingertip hovers half an inch from the damp skin on the back of your wrist.

*Tick.*

The crisp violet spark stings lightly against your knuckles, grounding whatever stray charge you carried in from the marsh outside. The creature blinks its translucent inner lids once, slowly, and then sinks gracefully onto its heels right beside your boots. Without touching your leather, it dips both long, flat palms just beneath the surface of the ankle-deep water and draws them in two slow, measured passes around your feet—coaxing the muddled wash back into tidy lines until the diamond standing waves lock cleanly up against the edges of your soles, folding your outline neatly into the floor's geometry as if you were simply another pale root that happened to rise there today.

Then it rises, turns its smooth back, and glides away to the weeping moss wall.

None of them look at you again. The elder lowers itself back into its rust-orange channel, the wren returns to its knee, the frogs keep their seamless braid of brassy song, and the water beads and drops from the leaf-dome in steady percussion. You are left standing in the deafening, bright green warmth, feeling abruptly clumsy, loud, and temporary—like a gust of outside weather that somehow slipped beneath the fern curtain, which the room has already accounted for and is calmly waiting out.
