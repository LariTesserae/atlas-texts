At first, you miss them for the same reason you miss a resting crab on wet shingle: your eyes are trained to look for stillness against motion, or motion against stillness, and down in the hollow nothing is ever still.

You only begin to see them when you notice that something moves half a second *before* the ground does.

Out near the center of the basin, twenty paces past a sagging loop of tarred conduit, a dark grey heap slumped against the ribbed flank of an oil-cooled transformer drum uncurls itself. Until that instant it looked like a discarded bundle of oilcloth or a heavy coil of slick clay-caked cable. It rises smoothly, without a sound, steps off the tilting concrete plinth onto a narrow timber duckboard, and balances there with its knees loose. Half a heartbeat later, the standing ripples in the puddle behind the plinth sharpen into crisp, glistening needles, the marl beneath the concrete gives a greasy sigh, and the plinth tilts four inches to the west. The figure on the duckboard does not even glance back. It had already read the tremor before the water did.

In the plain, shadowless afternoon light, there is nothing ghostly or blurred about them. They are entirely material, damp, and workaday, built to the scale of the cramped passages between standpipes and gantry-legs. If one straightened its spine completely it might reach the height of a lean, stooped person, though they almost never stand straight; their backs hold a long, supple bow, and their arms hang low enough that a palm or a set of knuckles can brush a passing pipe-rail or concrete pier without breaking stride.

Their skin is hairless, thick, and cool-looking, the dull pewter-grey of wet shale mottled faintly toward pale ochre along the belly, the inner wrists, and the throat. Like the sliding iron of the hollow, they carry a thin, matte film of the basin’s own mineral grease, rubbed into their hide by years of contact with the marl and transformer casings. Because of it, mud never cakes on them; when one withdraws an arm from a collapsing clay bank, the grey silt sheets away in clean, glossy ribbons. Clear beads of condensation crowd their shoulders and collarbones just as they crowd the undersides of rusted beams, gathering until they run down the ribs in thin, silent threads.

Their hands and feet are broad, long-fingered, and strangely patient in shape—the toes and fingers slightly webbed only at the roots, ending not in nails but in blunt, dark pads of horn worn velvet-flat from gripping wet steel and probing fine grit. When they walk a newly heaved dome of clay, their feet splay wide so they barely print the surface; when they cross a sagging overhead conduit, the supple arches curl around the wet wrapping like oiled leather straps.

Their faces are quiet, narrow-jawed, and largely unadorned by expression. Their eyes are wide, set slightly forward, the clouded milky-pale of brine or a fogged brass pressure-gauge, blinking seldom in the steady flat light. They have no outer ears at all—nothing to catch the useless drift of air. Instead, tucked behind the hinge of the jaw and repeated in two smaller ovals just below the collarbones, are smooth, pale patches of drum-taut skin. When one wants to read what is happening thirty feet below the silt, it does not cock its head; it leans down and presses the side of its throat directly against the wet flank of an iron piling, closing its eyes while the muffled beat of the buried engines travels straight into its skull.

They wear no cloth. Cloth would rot in the damp, hold grit, and drag heavily if a bank folded over a leg. Instead, every grown one wears ballast: criss-crossed harnesses of tarred hemp and braided copper wire slung around the chest, waist, and sometimes a single forearm, hung with an assortment of smooth-worn hardware salvaged from the turnover—heavy iron washers, brass manifold-nuts, lead plumb-bobs, halves of cracked porcelain insulators, small dense pebbles rolled round by the mud. None of it is hung loosely. Every iron ring and brass nut is whipped individually in thin strips of salvaged rubber hose or greased twine so that metal can never strike metal. They shift these weights from hip to shoulder, or unclip a lead slug and hook it onto an ankle, with the absent-minded precision of a sailor trimming a spar—adjusting their center of gravity for a slippery pontoon, or weighting a lean body against the pull of a rising counterweight.

There are not many of them—perhaps eleven or twelve in the entire two-hundred-pace bowl—yet because the crowded tangle of conduits, switch-shacks, and mud-bars closes the horizon to thirty feet, you never see more than two or three at once. They move through the hollow’s vertical layers with a damped, unhurried economy, never stepping against the tilt of a duckboard when they can ride it, never hauling themselves up a gantry-post when a greased counterweight is already rising beside them on its wire rope.

Above all, they keep the first rule of the basin. Airborne noise—the sharp clack of dropped stone, a ragged scrape of iron, the bark of a voice—is an obscenity to them, something as jarring as grit forced under an eyelid. If an outsider steps onto the shale rim and shouts, or lets a boot-heel clang loudly against a standpipe, the hollow empties in three seconds. They do not flee in panic; they simply lower themselves behind transformer plinths, ease into the dark slats beneath the switch-shacks, or draw their knees up along the undersides of dripping overhead pipes until their wet grey flanks vanish into the clutter.

Among themselves, though, the basin is thick with conversation.

Watch one crouching beside a flooded trench while another works twenty yards away on a tilting timber catwalk. The first lays two blunt fingertips lightly against a taut overhead tension-wire and draws them downward—not plucking it, never startling the air, only loading the steel with a slow, damped pressure and releasing it. A dull *thrum* runs through the length of the wire, felt in the ribs rather than heard. Twenty yards away, the second dweller, whose bare sole is resting against the iron post anchoring that wire, pauses, shifts its weight once to the left and twice to the right, and answers by pressing its heel gently against the edge of a puddle. The crisp geometric lattice of standing waves in the pool breaks into a broad, deliberate ring that crosses the milky water and nudges the opposite mud-bank.

When two of them meet on the narrow floating planks of a catwalk, they do not stop to gesture or block each other’s balance. They match their sway to the hinging pontoons, turning sideways in a slow, counterweighted glide so their oiled ribs and open palms brush for the space of a breath—sharing a quick measure of warmth, checking the steadiness of the other’s footing—before sliding past each other without a whisper.

They live directly off what the churning floor turns up, tuned to the same frantic, mute schedule as the lone wading bird, though with far greater patience. Sometimes a bird and a dweller work opposite sides of the same freshly bulged hummock of black marl, neither paying the other more than a peripheral glance. The dweller squats motionless on a rusted rail submerged an inch below the milky water, watching the geometric peaks in the adjacent pool. At the exact instant the ripples sharpen and a glossy seam begins to unzip down the mound’s crest, the dweller leans forward—ballast swinging lightly to hold its balance—and plunges both long arms elbow-deep into the anaerobic silt. It draws out handfuls of pale, blind brine-worms, plump pinkish amphipods, and occasionally a soft-shelled, mud-dark bivalve or a greasy white knot of deep peat-fungus churned up from the anoxic layers far below. Its arms come clear half a breath before the opposing clay walls slide shut with a soft sigh.

They supplement the catch with thick curtains of black-green algae stripped cleanly from beneath the fixed overhead pipes, and with clear, mineral-sweet condensation. All over the basin, tucked upright into the forks of pilings and balanced on concrete piers, you begin to notice pale porcelain insulators turned upside down like small fluted cups, catching the steady, silent threads of water weeping off the ironwork above.

It takes longer to realize that the basin’s strange, velvet-worn equilibrium is not entirely an accident of geology and oil.

They do not know what the buried machinery was originally built to accomplish, and if they ever knew, the knowledge has long since been kneaded out of them like salt from old mortar. To them, the cycling hydraulic rams, the clicking relays, and the pressurized groundwater are simply the metabolism of the ground—neither tool nor master, just a great, blind warmth they live inside. Yet they tend it, the way an old river-dweller clears snags from a weir without imagining he owns the current.

If a pulley-sheave high on a listing davit begins to wash dry and threaten the hush with even the faintest metallic tick, one of them climbs the slick iron arm carrying a cracked insulator brimming with black sludge skimmed from the transformer catch-pans and thickened with mineral grease from the mud. It packs the bearing slowly with its bare, blunt fingers until the wire rope runs smooth and dead silent again. If a tremor works a sheet of corrugated iron loose on one of the switch-shacks, they wedge the seam tight with a strip of damp willow bark pulled from the upper embankment or a fold of salvaged rubber hose before it can rattle.

Strangest of all is their negotiation with the valves. Now and then you see one kneeling on a slick concrete apron beside a half-sunken manifold, the pale membrane beneath its jaw pressed hard against a corroded iron handwheel. It stays motionless for five minutes, ten minutes, listening through bone to the choked, uneven throb far beneath the marl. Then, with infinite deliberation, it turns the rusted wheel a quarter-span—no more—and waits. Ten yards away, a buried pipe bleeds a silent plume of milky, lukewarm water into a cold trench; a nearby mud-bank softens and slumps gently to the east instead of shearing against the splayed legs of a switch-shack; a black transformer drum emits its tiny, internal relay-click and sends a fresh ribbon of warm mist curling into the chill air. They never force the basin. Force would snap a rusted stem or wake a collapse too large to ride. They merely lean into the immense turnover by inches, coaxing its folding so the warm pockets keep blooming and the catwalks keep flexing without tearing apart.

They even use the churn as a workshop. When the mud spits up a jagged shard of broken cast iron, a rough bolt, or a chipped lump of thick green glass, they do not discard it. One will wrap the piece loosely in a small net of braided copper wire tied to a recognizable tag of pale ceramic, wait for a deep trench to unzip near the center, and lower the bundle gently into the upwelling brine before the banks close over it. Months or years later, after the restless floor has rolled, folded, and kneaded the shard ten thousand times through its fine grey grit, the turnover returns it forty yards away—clean-washed, gleaming, every sharp edge worn velvet-smooth. They recognize their own tags by touch, untie the smoothed piece, wipe it against their greased flanks, and bind it into a ballast-cord or wedge it into a worn pontoon hinge.

Warmth is their quiet luxury, and it organizes their days. The three small corrugated-iron switch-shacks near the center—perched on splayed, sunken legs that lean and recover with the mire—are their sleeping-hollows. Inside the dim, dry-ish chambers, ancient oil-filled switch-drums still hold a steady, lukewarm heat. Between the ribbed casings, the dwellers weave thick, springy nests from dry tufts of wire-tough rush and grey-green couch grass gathered on rare, cautious climbs up the gullied shale rim, bound together with strips of split hydraulic hose. Here they curl together two or three at a time, flanks pressed to the warm metal, tiny dark oil-flies wandering unbothered across their damp shoulders while the shack sways gently on its buried feet.

The young do not touch the mud at all.

If you look up into the dense, dripping canopy of sagging cables, pulley arms, and tilting poles—where the speckled newt waits out the afternoon—you may catch sight of them: small, pale, limber creatures no larger than a hare, skin still translucent enough at the edges to show the faint grey shadow of bone. They cling effortlessly to an adult’s greased back, or curl inside shallow cradles woven from stripped copper wire and suspended beneath the sheltered eaves of the switch-shacks, rocking softly whenever a tension-cable twitches. From up there, high above the clay, they learn the basin’s grammar before their soles ever meet a plank—watching the puddles below sharpen into geometric spikes, feeling the muted cable-signals pass through their small, wide-padded fingers, learning to distinguish the slow *thrum* of a buried ram from the quick recoil of a snapping mud-channel. Only when they are broad-footed enough to sense a shift half a second early does an older one lower them onto a timber pontoon, keeping a blunt, steadying palm pressed gently between their shoulder blades while they learn to walk the hinge of the water.

At the north rim, clinging to the slumped shale embankment where the floor moves least, stands the listing brick pump-house with half its slate roof slid off into the mire. This is the basin’s anchor, and it belongs to the oldest ones. Inside, where mossy brick holds out the chill and the floor is half-silted, low concrete ledges are lined with decades of clean-washed salvage—fogged pressure-gauges, velvet-smooth gears, coiled busbars, pale mineral crusts gathered like salt-cakes. Here the eldest dwellers spend long, still afternoons, their joints stiffened by cold and years until they can no longer keep pace with the quick snap of the mud-bars out near the center. They sit cross-legged along the damp masonry, broad palms and the pale patches of their throats laid flat against the dark brick, reading the deepest, slowest cycles of the basin—the faint seasonal shifts in the groundwater, the long, hours-wide breaths of the deepest engines that the younger ones, busy on the catwalks, are too hurried to notice. Sometimes a younger dweller comes in from the mire, lays a freshly plucked cluster of pale brine-worms on the mossy sill beside an elder’s knee, presses its oiled forehead briefly to the elder’s shoulder to take the slow news from the wall, and slips back out into the wet pewter light.

When one of the elders grows too cold and stiff even to climb onto the brick ledge—when its balance falters and its fingers can no longer read the fine tremor in a wire—there is no panic, and no break in the hush.

On an ordinary cool afternoon like any other, it rises slowly from the pump-house floor and unclips its ballast. Piece by piece, with unhurried care, it slips the rubber-wrapped iron washers, the brass nuts, and the smooth lead weights off its hemp cords and hangs them neatly over the brass cap of a corroded standpipe just inside the doorway, where the younger ones will eventually divide them. Unweighted, looking strangely light and narrow, it walks out onto the floating catwalks one last time, stepping lightly along the slick planks until it reaches a freshly swelled dome of smooth, homogenized grey marl out near the middle of the hollow.

It steps off the timber onto the glistening dome, folds its long limbs neatly beneath itself, and lies down on its side in the plain afternoon light, much the way a pale, uprooted sod of grass from the rim rides the crest of a heaving clay ridge.

It does not wait long. Around the base of the hummock, the standing ripples in the flooded depressions sharpen into tiny, silent peaks. Two or three of the others stand quietly along the nearest catwalks and concrete piers—not close enough to disturb the ground’s work, just close enough to rest their palms flat against the nearest tilted iron pilings. They watch with clouded, unblinking eyes as the smooth grey marl trembles, splits along a clean, glossy seam beneath the elder’s flank, and draws the body gently downward with nothing more than a soft, greasy sigh. Through the wet iron under their palms, they feel the brief, muffled change in the weight of the clay as the surface seals over, clean and smooth as before, adding one small, quiet note to the slow turnover below. Then they lift their hands from the pilings, adjust the brass weights at their hips, and go back to their work.

If you stay out on the flexing timber catwalk long enough—if you stop talking, soften your knees to the swell of the pontoons, and let the dense, uneven vibration rise up through the soles of your boots until the silence stops feeling eerie and starts feeling full—they stop hiding from you.

A heavy iron counterweight eases smoothly downward beside your shoulder on its greased wire rope, cool air trailing off its wet flank before it slips without a splash into a boiling, soundless pool of geometric ripples. Only as it descends do you see that perched lightly astride its rusted upper yoke, broad grey toes hooked around the iron bar, is one of the dwellers, riding the weight down toward the water. Its rubber-wrapped brass washers hang motionless against its greased ribs. It passes three feet from your face, looking straight at you with wide, brine-pale eyes—neither welcoming nor hostile, only measuring the tension in your ankles, waiting with ancient, unhurried patience to see whether you will startle the quiet, or whether you know how to shift your weight before the floor turns over.
