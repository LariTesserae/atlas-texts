You do not see it for the first hour, because you are looking for the wrong kind of life. You are looking for something wet and hurried—something that breathes, leaves footprints in the mineral grit, shivers, or hides behind a stone when your boot scrapes the shale. You are also far too warm. Stepping in from the outer ridge, wrapped in wool and circulating blood, you enter the pocket like a smear of loud weather. Heat leaks off your coat in a thick, messy fog; your breath snaps the fine air out of alignment; your shadow drags heavily across the sorted terraces.

Nothing that belongs here will show itself while you are still so bright with the outside world.

It is only after you sit down against the curved basalt wall and stay there—long enough for the arid freeze to draw the warmth out of your sleeves, for your breathing to thin to a dry thread, for your pulse to drop and your boots to stop shifting the scree—that the pocket begins to resume its ordinary habits.

The first sign is not a shape, but a correction. Ten paces away, a small dark pebble you dislodged on your way in gives a soft, dry scrape and rolls half an inch back into the shallow lip of the terrace where it belonged. Nothing is beside it. Yet the shadow of the nearest rounded boulder looks slightly too broad at its base, and a moment later, that extra sliver of shade detaches itself by the width of a thumb and lingers before thinning out into the pooled light.

If you look then—not toward the boulder, which shows only empty ash, but toward the patch of dark, glassy slag fused to the rock near the rim—you will see it before it arrives.

In the vitreous crust, reflected alongside the dull twilight sky and the jagged lip of the overhang, something low and shingled is already crouched beside the floating pale slab, one long, segmented limb extended into the four-inch gap beneath the stone. When you turn your head to look directly at the slab, there is nothing there but the undisturbed mound of gray dust and the faint sheen of the silica fans. You look back at the slag. The crouched figure is still there, utterly patient, tilted slightly toward the underside of the stone.

Two or three minutes later, the air along the lee side of the courtyard seems to slacken.

You remember how it took fourteen paces to reach the central dish from the cleft, and only nine to walk back. Something lives inside those five missing paces. It does not walk *around* the boulders so much as it folds itself into the uneven grain of the floor, resting in the shallow pocket of distance that only opens when you move against the slope. When it leaves that fold, it does not burst into view; it simply unbends out of the dimness beside the floating slab, in the exact posture the reflection showed you minutes ago, as if the courtyard had quietly handed it back.

At first glance it looks like a low pile of weathered roof-slate and compressed ash—the length of a crouching person, though nothing about its proportions suggests a spine, a ribcage, or a face. Its back is layered in seven or eight broad, overlapping plates of matte, soot-gray mineral, worn as round and smooth along their edges as the riverless boulders scattered across the floor. Where the plates meet, there is no skin and no sinew—only thin seams of that chalk-white rime that burns into copper-smelling vapor at a warm touch, and deeper within the overlapping shells, a light-swallowing darkness like the substance of the three northern spindles.

It has five limbs, arranged with no interest in symmetry. Underneath its body lie two broad, flat runners like worn stone wedges, with which it slides across the terraces without churning the grit. Rising from its flanks and forward edge are three longer, slender appendages jointed like a surveyor’s brass rods—except there are no pins at the joints, and no cartilage.

At every bend, the segments do not touch.

A clean, hairline gap of empty air separates one section from the next. Looking across the courtyard, you can see thin threads of pale ash and gray sky right through its knees and wrists. Yet the disconnected pieces move in flawless, silent concert, held in stubborn relation by the same quiet, invisible geometry that anchors the floating slab four inches above the dust. Only when it reaches too far, or turns sharply, does a microscopic pinch of cold blue-white static snap across one of the gaps, making a single tiny, dry *tick*—the exact sound you took, an hour ago, for a stone settling beneath the grit.

It has no eyes, no mouth, no nostrils, and no breath. At its leading end, where a head might have been, its layered plates widen into a smooth, tilted crescent—half-plow, half-dish—coated in a dark, fused sheen of vitreous slag. That glassy face does not look at the basin so much as it collects it. Because it shares the temper of the slag by the rim, its reflections do not keep honest time; as the creature turns its front toward the floating slab, the dark crescent holds the faint image of a ribbon of high cloud that has not yet cleared the mountain’s lip.

Watching it work beneath the pale stone, you begin to understand what keeps it alive.

Because it does not breathe, it can lean within an inch of the floating slab without shattering the hair-thin silica filaments hanging from its belly. Its longest fore-limb—those three hovering segments sliding forward without a whisper—reaches into the four-inch shadow above the dust mound. It does not eat the crystal webs, at least not in any way a mouth would understand. Slowly, with the steadiness of frost growing across a pane, it detaches a single brittle fan of precipitated silica and draws it back beneath its own shingled rim, pressing the delicate mineral web into the chalky seam along its underside. There, fed by whatever faint, cold charge leaks from its own hollow interior, the crystal takes hold and anchors itself, replacing finer hairs that must have snapped days or weeks ago.

It is an arid creature, built entirely of dry tension, mineral plate, and cold charge. Warmth is its only poison. Even the thin, reluctant twilight of the basin, or the mild friction of sliding across the grit, slowly softens the discipline of its floating joints, letting the gaps between its segments widen by a fraction of a hair until its posture grows loose and dull.

When that happens, it turns northward toward the three knee-high spindles.

It moves without haste, gliding along the contour of the terraces, leaving behind not tracks but a faint trail of straightened grit, as if the dust preferred to settle neatly once it passed. Reaching the nearest matte-black needle, it folds its five limbs tightly under its plates and lowers its bulk over the scoured hollow at the spindle’s base, drawing its hollow core directly into the warped halo of biting, dense air around the tip.

There it stays for a long time, utterly motionless. You can watch the cold sharpen it. As the spindle siphons the stray, invisible warmth out of its shell, the creature seems to grow denser and more exact. One by one, its hovering joints draw back into crisp alignment, each adjustment marked by a faint, dry *tick* that travels cleanly across the pressure-thin silence. Fresh rime blooms chalk-white along the grooves of its back. The thin ring of uphill-creeping dust around the spindle parts smoothly around its grounded plates, waits, and resumes its slow sublimation into nothing once the creature rises.

Everything it does carries that same unhurried, private precision—less like the foraging of an animal and more like the slow rounds of something checking the balance of its own body, because its body and the pocket are no longer entirely separate things.

Along the eastern slope, where gravity has grown absent-minded, it occasionally pauses to press loose flakes of dark shale against the nearly vertical wall, adjusting their angles until they cling without slipping. Once in a long while, it lifts a small, rounded stone with two of its hovering tips, holds it at the height of its shingled shoulder, and lets it go. Its glassy crescent tilts downward. The pebble drops cleanly for a foot, stops completely dead in the thin air for the span of two heartbeats, and finishes falling into the dust without a sound. The creature remains bent over the spot for a full minute afterward, as though verifying that the pause is still two heartbeats wide—that the old error in the mountain has neither healed nor widened since the last time it checked.

At longer intervals—days apart, perhaps—it rears clumsily onto its broad lower wedges against the curved basalt cliff, bracing its disconnected arms against the stone. With the blunt tip of its longest limb, it traces the hairline diagrams etched cleanly into the rock where the mineral grains once stepped aside. It does not read them. Its touch is too slow and repetitive for reading, more like a blind hand following a familiar crease in cold iron, waiting. If it waits on the right day, the hairline seam gives off its single microscopic twitch of cold blue-white static. The spark leaps straight into the hovering joint, runs in an invisible ripple under the overlapping plates, and grounds itself in the grit. For an hour afterward, the creature sits completely still at the base of the wall, its matte back edged with a faint, chalky sheen, casting two diverging shadows that seem unusually dark and slow to agree with the dim sky.

Its longest vigils belong to the center of the pocket.

Whenever the dim light deep inside the pearlescent ceramic basin begins its slow, multi-hour shift from dull pewter toward the faintest bruised violet, the creature abandons the walls and the spindles and glides across the ash toward the dish. Like the stones, it cannot cross the rim; an unseen contour turns its weight gently aside. Instead, it curls its shingled length along the outer lip, matching its curve to the seamless ceramic plates, resting its dark, vitreous front directly against that intangible shelf in the air.

Suspended half an inch from the edge of the milky, bottomless lattice, it stays there while the violet deepens and recedes. Whether it is sleeping, or warming itself on some colder kind of tide rising from those stacked silvery horizons, or simply listening to a continuity that the rest of the mountain has lost, there is no way to tell. From across the courtyard, it looks like nothing more than a weathered lip of basalt that grew up around the basin over centuries of wind.

Sitting against the wall, watching it curl around the rim, you find yourself unable to decide what it is—and gradually realize that the question belongs to the lower valleys, not up here.

Perhaps it was left behind when the immense machinery vanished: a minor gauge, a dry custodian, a loose assembly of pins and plates whose purpose ended long before its motion did, left to weather in the high cold until calibration softened into habit, and habit slowly thickened into a quiet, solitary life. Or perhaps nothing made it at all. Perhaps the pocket—left alone for centuries with its bent light, its leaking cold charge, its static seams, and its sorted mineral dust—simply precipitated a tenant the way the floating slab precipitates silica webs, coaxing loose ash, basalt, and frayed geometry into a shape sturdy enough to keep its seasons.

There is no way to separate the two possibilities, and up here they amount to the same thing.

If you stay still long enough—so cold that your fingers have gone stiff inside your gloves and your coat has stopped steaming away the frost in the air—the creature will eventually finish its turn at the violet dish and come within three paces of where you sit.

It does not approach with curiosity, or caution, or hospitality. You are simply an unusual boulder that has finally stopped radiating enough heat to ruin the neighborhood. As it nears, a sharp drop in temperature moves ahead of it, carrying that dry, sour tang of cold copper. It stops three paces out, broad wedges resting neatly on the ash, and tilts its dark, concave face toward your knees.

For a long moment you look directly into the vitreous crescent. Your own dim silhouette looks back—not as you are now, seated and stiff with cold, but as you were five minutes ago, shoulders drawn slightly higher, half a step to the left of where your boots rest.

One of its disconnected fore-limbs rises smoothly from its side. You can see a sliver of the bruised gray horizon through the hair-wide gap in its wrist. The blunt, chalk-dusted tip hovers two inches from the frost-stiff cuff of your sleeve, motionless, feeling the last faint ghost of warmth still leaking through the wool. Its fine underside filaments tremble imperceptibly, intact because you have remembered to hold your breath.

Then, with a single tiny, dry *tick* from somewhere deep inside its hovering joints, it lowers the limb. It seems satisfied that you are neither breaking anything nor built to stay. It turns away with unhurried dignity, slides silently across the terrace, and steps back into the uneven distance near the northern curve—thinning into the slack between nine paces and fourteen until only the delayed shadow of a boulder remains, and the pocket closes calmly over its own unbroken quiet once more.
