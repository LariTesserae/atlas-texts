It takes almost as long to see the people of the plateau as it does to separate their terraces from the rock. From a high parapet, what first registers as a drift of cloud-shadow or a dark patch of wet peat along a retaining wall will resolve, only after a second glance, into three or four stooping figures in heavy felt, moving so steadily and low to the stone that they seem less like residents than another slow process of the masonry.

The altitude and the cold dampness have shaped them over uncounted generations into compact, deep-chested people with long, unhurried breath. Their faces are weathered early by the thin, clear light and the constant mirror-glare off the pans—skin burnished dark across the cheekbones and forehead, eyes deep-set and ringed with fine squint-lines that look like kindness even when they are only reading the glare on a cistern lip. What marks them most unmistakably, though, are their hands and their way of standing. Their hands are broad, thick-knuckled, perpetually cool to the touch, with nails worn blunt and crescents of pale lime-mud or dark peat permanently worked into the creases around the joints. The heel of the palm and the outer edge of the thumb grow thick, pale calluses from pressing fresh clay into winter-opened seams and wedging heavy slabs of dressed shale. Even at rest, their fingers remain slightly curled, ready to test a stone or pinch a thread of wet sedge.

They walk with their weight held low and slightly forward, knees never quite locked, placing the whole foot flat upon the wet slate rather than striking with the heel. A child learns this step before it learns to run; indeed, almost no one runs here. To run across dished limestone slick with mist-film is to fall, and to fall in a terrace-city is to break bone against stone or pitch into a brimming pan. In the narrow cleft-alleys, two people passing turn their shoulders smoothly inward without slowing, their damp wool brushing with a brief hush, neither looking up unless they mean to speak. Even their stillness is distinctive: when waiting by a sluice or resting on a coping-stone, they squat deep on their heels with their cloaks tented over their knees, shedding mist like boulders.

Their clothing belongs entirely to the palette of the plateau—layered felt in undyed grey, peat-brown, bog-iron slate, and the muted ochre of scraped lichen. The outer wraps are kept thick with natural grease so the endless cloud-mist beads on the surface instead of soaking through; up close, everyone carries the same clean, heavy scent of wet wool, dried heather, and cold limestone. Nothing is fastened with metal. Cloaks are pinned with whittled bone toggles or tied with twisted grass-cord; soles in the colder months are wrapped in folded felt and bound with plaited rush that grips wet rock better than leather, then shed altogether during the brief, clear hours of midday so bare heels can feel the temperature and slickness of the stone directly.

No herds live upon the built tiers—a single cloven hoof would chip the dressed slate lips and foul the drinking pans for twenty terraces below—so the wool, the flat bone tools, the tallow, and the oiled hides used for window-flaps come from outside the water-fabric altogether. Far out to the west and north, beyond the highest fog-nets where the plateau finally breaks into dry scree and short, bitter upland grass, live sparse bands of rim-herders who keep small, shaggy, sure-footed beasts. Twice a season, at broad dry causeways outside the outer dykes, they meet the terrace-folk to lay down bales of raw fleece, cured hides, horn, and bladders of fat in exchange for coiled clay jars, tight-woven sedge mats, and sacks of pale, frost-dried tubers. The herders seldom venture far into the built tiers; the damp hush presses on their ears, and the terrace-folk, for their part, find the rim-people unsettlingly loud, their voices pitched for open wind rather than sunken courts.

For among the terraces, quiet is not merely a preference or a courtesy; it is the medium in which everyone thinks and works. Their spoken language is dry, low in the throat, and economical, full of soft consonants that die within a few paces. A shout here is not treated as anger so much as a kind of incompetence—like letting a channel drop too steeply until the water foams and chews the stone. Much of daily life passes without words at all. At a sluice-gate, two neighbors confer with tiny shifts of the chin and flat-palm gestures that indicate pitch, depth, and rate of release: hand level for brim-full, fingers tilted slightly downward for a slow draw, knuckles tapped once against a wet boulder to mean a seam is leaking beneath the frost-line.

Their ears, unblunted by iron, wheels, or roaring water, are tuned to shifts an outsider would never register. Lying awake inside a low stone room at dusk, a person can tell by the subtle thinning of the drip outside the window-slit the exact hour frost begins to knit across the upper ledges. Standing beside a broad cistern, a seasoned keeper can hear when a pan two tiers above has had its weir lifted simply by the faint rise in pitch as the incoming film thickens over the travertine lip. They listen to the fog-nets on the ridges the way sailors listen to rigging, knowing from the different rustle of dry sedge whether the approaching wall of pewter carries clean mist, sleet, or a dry freeze.

Even more remarkably, they speak to one another through the water itself. Because every tier feeds the one beneath it, a reach of twenty or thirty households shares a slow, continuous thread of news. When someone upstream prepares to scrape a silted basin, they stir a handful of pale marl into the outflow an hour beforehand; seeing the milky ribbon glide into their settling-pan, the households below lift their bypass slates without a word ever being sent down the stairs. A knotted rush floated onto a header-channel—one bend, two bends, a sliver of dried sorrel tucked into the loop—drifts quietly from pan to pan until it catches in the reed-notch of the neighbor meant to read it, announcing a birth, a slumped wall needing four pairs of shoulders, or a roof due to be re-turfed before the next squall. And without intending any message at all, every household discloses its character in what leaves its threshold: clear water, fine even silt, and a steady unbroken film speak of careful hands; muddy runnels or erratic surges bring quiet, watchful eyes from the terraces below.

It is this inescapable physics of gravity, rather than any king, council, or written code, that holds a continental city together. There are no palaces on the plateau, no high towers, no plazas wide enough for crowds or speeches. Instead, every person belongs to two intersecting loyalties: the *shelf* and the *fall*.

The shelf is horizontal—the band of neighbors at one's own elevation, who wake to the same frost-line, endure the same low cloud-belly dragging through their lanes, and share the labor of bracing slumping retaining walls, re-stringing fog-nets, and trading coiled pottery or sun-cured roots along the level walkways. The fall is vertical—the long chain of terraces linked by a single descending sheet of water, stretching from a high catchment-ridge down through dozens of districts toward the unseen eastern sink. Between shelves there is kinship and mutual aid; along a fall there is what they call the *lower debt*, the understanding that everything you neglect becomes the weight on someone else's head.

Disputes are rare, and when they occur, they are settled with the same slow pressure the plateau uses on everything else. There are no blades forged for fighting—cutting tools are small pieces of knapped highland chert or honed green slate bound to bone handles with dried sinew, fit for slicing rushes and peeling roots. If a household hoards water past its notch, lets a seam rot until dirty runoff stains the steps below, or refuses its share of wall-mending, no one raises a voice. Instead, at dawn, four or five elders from the lower tiers simply come up the stairs and sit silently along the offending coping-stone, knees drawn into their cloaks, watching the water move until the fault is set right. If stubbornness persists across a season, the neighboring shelves stop passing up reed-mats, peat-bricks, and dried roots, and the households immediately above quietly shift two shale weirs so the morning film glides around the offending roof instead of across it. On the plateau, to be made a *dry house* is the gravest exile imaginable: the hearth still burns and the walls still stand, yet the silent pulse of the city passes just beyond your sill without touching you, leaving your steps harsh and dusty until you climb up to your neighbors with fresh clay in your hands and ask to be let back into the run.

Inside the low, thick-walled dwellings, away from the cool silver glare of the terraces, life contracts into a dense, amber warmth. You duck low beneath a massive natural slate lintel, lift a flap of oiled hide and a loose screen of bundled sedge, and step down two worn steps into a room sunk half into the slope behind it. There is no chimney to let heat escape, and no open blaze; fuel is too scarce for flames. Instead, dense bricks of bog-peat smolder slowly inside sunken earthen flues channeled beneath the tamped clay floor, keeping the whole room at a steady, dry, barely-scented warmth that seeps up through reed mats into the soles of the feet.

In the quiet dimness—lit by shallow clay saucers of rendered tallow with rush-pith wicks—everything is kept close to the ground. Sleeping ledges are built directly into the thickest masonry along the warm flue-wall, piled with felted wool and stuffed sedge cushions. Against the damp outer wall stand tall, paddle-beaten clay jars—never wheel-thrown, built in slow coils from fine basin-silt and fired in low peat-clamps out on the leeward margins—holding winter stores of pale bitter tubers, dried highland sorrel, compressed cakes of cress, and roasted seeds of upland rush.

Their diet is austere, patient, and deeply learned. The bitter highland tubers grown in the outer silt-beds cannot be eaten raw; they are soaked for days in cold running seep-pans held in wicker cages, then spread across sunlit coping-stones to freeze by night and thaw by day in the thin air, trodden bare-footed to press out the bitter juice until they dry into chalk-white, feather-light nuggets that will keep for years without spoiling. Simmered over the glowing peat-hole with dark water-cress, bruised sorrel, and a scraping of salted rim-fat, they yield a thick, earthy broth that tastes faintly of cold stone and winter smoke. They drink rainwater dipped straight from the upper pans, or pale, astringent infusions steeped from dried heather and the scraped twigs of dwarf willow.

They do not eat the tiny pale snails that graze the cisterns. On the contrary, the snails are regarded with a quiet, practical tenderness as the smallest keepers of the masonry. Where snails thrive, the algae film stays thin and the travertine grows clean and smooth; if a cistern is drained for repair, children are set to gather the snails gently into wet moss and carry them to a neighboring pan until the water returns. Watchers read them like instruments: when the snails climb above the meniscus at mid-afternoon and seal their shells tight against the stone, everyone knows a hard black freeze is coming before dusk, and the upper weirs are lifted early so no shallow pan splits in the night.

The same quiet reverence extends to the few other creatures that cross the stone fabric. When a solitary grey wading bird descends into a broad mirror-pan at the edge of a quarter, mothers lay a finger gently across their children's lips and rafters pause with their poles lifted clear of the surface; a district where the grey bird stays through an afternoon takes it as quiet proof that its water is clean and its lanes are truly still. And the rare wind-twisted dwarf willows hunched in the lee of ancient cistern-walls are treated almost as elders of the quarter—never cut except for dead winter twigs, their exposed roots packed tenderly with fresh highland silt each spring, their crooked bark worn glossy by generations of passing palms coming simply to touch living wood in a world of slate and moss.

A human life on the plateau follows the same gentle descent as the water, moving slowly from the exposed heights into the sheltered seams. Youth belongs to the edges and the cold air: the young climb to the upper catchment-ridges to mend the woven fog-nets in the cutting wind, pole the heavy reed-rafts laden with wet tubers across the wide mirror-pans, haul baskets of dark peat up the broad shallow stairs, and wade knee-deep in the outer settling-basins to cut armfuls of rattling sedge with flint blades. Their games, even as small children, are intent and almost soundless—building miniature weirs of pebbles in the smallest seep-channels, testing how finely a notch can be cut so a sheet of water glides without breaking, racing tiny folded rush-boats down a shallow apron without letting a hull scrape the stone.

In middle age, hands turn to the slower, heavier craft of stewardship: bracing a retaining wall where the bedrock has shifted over forty winters, laying fresh sod-caps so the wind-baffles keep the high gale gliding smooth above the eaves, teaching younger knuckles how to feel the right wetness in lime-mud before pressing it into a seam, and keeping the quiet compacts of the shelf and the fall. No one builds for show, and no one tears down what can still hold weight. When an older wall bows outward, they pack its belly with rounded cobbles and curve the next walkway respectfully around it; they speak of a good life in the same terms—not straight, not sharp, simply braced well enough to carry what leans against it.

As joints stiffen and the damp settles into the bones, the elders move down into the deepest, warmest rooms and take up the work where judgment outweighs strength. Wrapped in four or five layers of felt, they sit beside the main junction-pans during the bright hours, twisting endless yards of grass-cord between slow fingers while keeping an eye on the water-marks cut into the limestone. They are the keepers of memory on a plateau that keeps no written chronicles and raises no statues. They do not count years by kings or wars; they measure time by the slow thickening of a pale travertine rib over a spillway, the year a great three-day freeze split the northern cisterns, or the creeping ring of rust-lichen that has finally reached the center of a south lintel since their grandmother's youth. Their histories are woven straight into the fabric of the lanes: *the bulge that Taro braced*, *the bright pan where Sela's willow leans*, *the low notch cut in the dry summer*.

When someone dies, they are not buried inside the city, for every handful of earth on the built tiers is soaked by the water the living must drink. At first light, before the frost has loosened from the eaves, the dead are washed with clean mist-water, wrapped closely in their oldest, softest felt, and carried on shoulder-poles by neighbors of the shelf—never hurriedly, never with wailing—all the way up and out beyond the highest fog-nets, where the terraced stone ends against the bare northern wind-scarps. There, under the clean blue dome where no runoff ever turns back toward the plateau, the body is laid inside a shallow drystone niche facing east and weighted lightly with river-cobbles so the high, cold air and bright sun can dry it cleanly to lightness and bone. Nothing is carved over them. The mourners walk back down into the mist before noon, and for three days the deceased's household keeps its window-flap half-drawn and lets its roof-pan run clear without work, a brief pause in the fabric before the next pair of hands takes up the sluice.

If they hold any shared belief about the whole vast thing they inhabit, it is never preached and rarely named. It lives instead in the quiet horror they feel toward sudden force, and the deep contentment they take in thinness: water kept thin enough never to scour, wind kept parted above the roofs, fire kept buried in slow peat, speech kept low enough never to bruise the stillness. Most of them will never travel more than a day's walk up or down from the terrace where they were born. They have never seen the far basin days away to the east where all the water of the plateau finally gathers and disappears, nor the distant southern districts where afternoon squalls trail ribbons of sleet across roofs too far off to resolve.

Yet at evening, as the copper light fades from the wet slate and the thin, almost invisible wisps of peat-smoke rise into the freezing twilight, a person pulling a rush-screen across their window-slit listens for a moment before stepping down to the warm floor. Outside, in the gathering dark, the needles of ice begin to knit outward from the moss-edges, locking the highest channels shut while the lower cisterns go on filling, drop by quiet drop. They know that the water sliding past their own threshold tonight was combed from cloud-mist three ridges west before dawn, and will still be gliding softly over someone else's steps long after they are asleep—part of a single, unbroken surface kept level by a million pairs of cool, clay-stained hands, waiting patiently in the dark for the first pale edge of morning to set it moving again.
