You have to stay still for a long time before you notice them, because at first they are indistinguishable from the architecture.

If you drift close to one of the colossal fluted piers and press both palms flat against the velvet moss, you feel the constant bass tremor of the distant waterfalls running straight up through the fused timber. You hear the crisp, dry crackling of the pale crawlers rasping the wood all around your fingers. Then, if you wait—floating motionless in the cold, thin water until your own breathing slows—you may realize that a long, smooth swell in the pier just above your shoulder is not wood at all.

The moss across it is the exact same black-green nap, cropped to the exact same even height. The blind crawlers march steadily up the column, cross right over the swell without pausing, and continue up into the dark, their clicking unbroken. Only when you slide your hand upward does the surface change. Beneath the wet velvet, it does not have the ironwood stiffness of cured trunks. It is cold as the water, dense as compacted peat, yet faintly elastic—thick, oiled skin stretched over heavy muscle and broader, flatter bone. And beneath your palm, far slower than the bass of the falls, something lifts once, holds, and settles.

Then the clicking of the crawlers breaks off in a sudden, quiet ring. The swell detaches itself from the pier.

There is no splash, no startled bolt into the black. It simply peels away from the diagonal flutes with an unhurried, deliberate heaviness, displacing a slow, cool pillow of water against your chest. For a moment, against the faint bruise-grey glow seeping down from the cloud ceiling hundreds of feet overhead, you catch the silhouette of a body ten or twelve feet long, hung vertically in the dark beside the column like a pale, broad-shouldered pendulum.

They do not have faces meant to be seen. Where eyes would sit, the pale, willow-colored skin is seamless and smooth, pulled taut across a wide, gently domed brow and broad, rounded cheeks. Beneath that pale membrane lie twin chambers filled with dense, clear oil—cold, sensitive drums that catch every tremor in the mountain sea. Only high on the crown of the head do two narrow, pale-lidded pits remain, useless for shapes, good for nothing more than reading the faint grey wash far above so they never lose hold of which way is up when the dark closes in. Their mouths sit low and wide beneath the blunt curve of the skull, lipless and firm. Below the jaw, instead of a slender neck, broad, layered folds of leathery slate-grey skin overlap down to the collarbone, lifting and closing in a slow, heavy rhythm. Each time the mantles part, you catch a glimpse of thick, dark-plum fronds inside, drinking the thin, clean snowmelt without ever needing to know what air is.

Their arms are long and heavy at the shoulder, tapering down to wide, four-fingered hands with flattened, curved nails as thick and pale as cured horn—not claws for tearing, but blunt scoops and gauges. Their lower bodies end in long, muscular limbs built less for kicking through open water than for holding on: wide, splayed feet with deeply ridged soles and prehensile toes that hook effortlessly into the diagonal grooves of the fused timber. Down the center of the spine runs a low, thick crest of flesh, soft-edged and dark with the same moss that coats the parapets.

When the one beside you turns its smooth, eyeless brow in your direction, you feel a faint, almost imperceptible vibration brush across your teeth and ribs—not a shout, not a threat, just a brief, low hum pulsed out through the oil of its face, reading the softness of your body against the hardness of the pier.

They are not blind, not in any way that matters here. To them, the valley is fiercely, intricately lit.

In a place that never falls silent, sound is the sun. The endless, low thunder of the distant rim pours through the water and strikes every pier, arch, and stepped terrace at a slightly different angle, throwing long acoustic shadows across the empty basins. To a creature whose whole skull is a listening-box, the drowned plazas are not empty black voids; they are crisp, ringing rooms mapped down to the breadth of a single sedge-blade. When they want a sharper look across a wide stretch of open water, they do not strain toward the pale sky. They lean in, press the dense, bony heel of a palm against a fluted column, and strike it twice—a deep, wooden knock that mimics the pitch of the great blunt-headed fish. Seconds later, out in the sunken avenue, one of the huge pewter-scaled bottom-dwellers answers with a heavy *thrum* from its swim-bladder. The sound rolls across the terrace, rings off the braided undersides of the viaducts, and floods back against the listener's cheek-drums, lighting up three hundred yards of dark water in a single, luminous wash.

They never hunt the big booming fish. A fish that calls out along the causeways is a lantern kept burning in the dark. Only when one grows very old—when its scales turn dull as silt, its swim-bladder falls mute, and it begins to list sideways in the laminar drift—do they follow it down into the deep bowls, guiding it gently until it settles, taking its heavy fat and its dense, ivory ear-bones only once its voice has gone out for good.

Their own hunger is as slow and cold as the water. They live at the temperature of the snowmelt, burning almost nothing, moving with a thrift that makes every gesture look like ritual. Sometimes a pair of them will hang upside down beneath a braided arch, pale toes hooked securely into the grooves among the motionless, red-gilled salamanders, and graze slowly on the small, starch-rich nodules that bud from the living root-stone in the shadow of the piers. Sometimes they sweep a broad palm across a terrace to gather a fistful of pale crawlers, cracking the coin-flat shells quietly in the back of their mouths, leaving the rest of the drifting carpet undisturbed. Along the lowest, blackest embankments, where compacted peat bulges between the ancient woven foundations, they dig with their horn-nailed fingers for pale, brittle tubers that grow nowhere near the faint grey light above.

Moving through the valley, they obey the current rather than fighting it.

To travel downstream toward the outer terraces, one simply climbs to the rounded parapet of a pier, turns its moss-furred back to the slow, steady pull of the water, and pushes off. Legs drawn together, long arms angled slightly outward, dorsal keel catching the even flow, it glides silently across two hundred yards of abyssal black like a heavy, pale kite in an invisible wind. There is no thrashing, no turbulence; they know every seam of the laminar drift by heart, riding the invisible corridors from one colossal column to the next until a single outstretched hand meets the velvet wood on the far side and checks their weight without a jolt.

To go back upstream—toward the high, cloud-fed seeps at the back of the plateau—they never enter the open water at all. They travel by the walls. Hand over hand, splayed feet clamping into the worn flutes, they walk effortlessly along the vertical faces of the retaining embankments and across the sweeping undersides of the submerged viaducts, moving through the sheltered, sluggish film of water that drags close to the moss. If you drift under an arch for long enough, you may see three or four of them passing overhead in single file, pale bellies pressed close to the wood, silent except for the brief hushes where the crawlers stop clicking beneath their palms.

Whoever braided the first living saplings at the top of the mountain is lost past any telling, and these dwellers do not think of themselves as builders. They carry no tools, carve no marks, keep no tally of the centuries. Ask them—if you could ever learn the language of bone and vibration—who raised the piers, and the question would have no edge to catch on. To them, the drowned terraces are simply the bones of the world.

Yet the basin holds its clean, weathered shape because of them.

They are listeners first, and what they cannot bear is a sour note.

In a sea balanced on a mountain spine, where the roar of the rim rings evenly through every column, even the smallest flaw cries out. A single strand of water-willow that sprouts crooked into the laminar flow sets up a thin, ragged hiss that muddies the acoustics for a hundred yards. A hairline gap opening between two ancient fused trunks drinks water with a tight, sour whistle. Inside a hollow overflow conduit, a wad of shed sedge snagged against a porous root-screen shifts the pressure by a fraction of an inch, and immediately the buzzing chorus of blind amphibians wedged inside the pipe drops half a tone flat.

Whenever the valley goes slightly out of tune, one of them comes.

They do not cut or tear. A broken stem bleeds bitter sap into the clean water and leaves a jagged nub that churns the current. Instead, a dweller anchors its wide feet into the wood beside the stray shoot, hooks one long arm around a fluted rib, and takes the pale, living tendril between its broad, cold fingers. Then it simply stays there. For hours—sometimes for two or three uncounted days, motionless enough for the blind crawlers to resume marching across its back—it presses the slow-growing fiber gently back into the ancient diagonal crease, wedging a smooth sliver of cured peat-wood beside it or braiding a single loose sedge-root over its crest until the living vascular wall accepts the curve and holds it on its own.

The youngest among them—leaner, barely half the length of the elders, their skin still translucent enough to show the faint indigo shadows of their heavy ribs—are the ones who tend the conduits. Drawn by a flat buzz in a retaining wall, a young one will glide down to an intake lip, anchor its splayed toes against the suction, and slide its whole long arm deep into the rushing dark of the pipe. Blind fingers feel carefully past the singing, wedged bodies of the tiny amphibians, plucking loose a clot of decayed fiber with delicate horn-nails so the water runs clean again and the chorus rises back to its true pitch.

And far out along the outer rim—miles from the quiet plazas, where the heavy bass roar grows so fierce that it hammers directly against the skull and the laminar pull sharpens toward the unimaginable void of the sky—live the heaviest and most scarred of their kind. Broad-chested, thick-shouldered, their slate-pale hides furrowed by decades of bracing against the draw, they cling belly-down along the submerged roots just back from the titanic woven weirs. Here, where the perched sea pours out over the edge of the world, a single loosened mat could begin an unraveling that would empty the mountain. They live pressed flat into the trembling timber, cheeks glued to the wood, listening straight down into the throat of the waterfalls. They know by the pitch of the thunder whether the lip is wearing evenly, whether the peat beneath the rim is holding firm, whether a submerged bole needs another living runner coaxed across its seam. They never look over the edge—there is nothing to see, and the dryness beyond the water is a terror with no shape—yet now and then, when one of their kind grows sick in a way that cannot be stilled, or simply tires of the cold balance, it swims all the way out to the rim-tenders, presses its brow briefly against their shoulders, and lets go of the flutes, drifting quietly out into the roar until the water drops away beneath it into thin air.

Most of them, however, never leave the piers.

If you follow one of the colossal columns down into the deeper indigo and trace your hand around its leeward side, you will eventually find a narrow, smooth-lipped slit tucked neatly between two fused trunks—grown so cleanly into the curve of the wood that the current slides right past without a ripple. Slip inside, and the roar of the valley transforms.

The hollow, rotted cores of the great piers are their houses, their meeting-places, and their instruments. Inside, the water is utterly still, sheltered from even the slow drift of the terraces, yet the cured timber walls thrum softly with the amplified voice of the whole basin. Here, where no current can snag them, the dwellers practice the only craft they have ever invented.

Stretched from one hollow wall to another, anchored to tiny knots of living rootlet, hang long, thin strands of stripped willow-bast and cured sedge-tendon, each weighted at its center with a smooth pebble of dense peat-wood or the pale ear-bone of a long-dead pewter fish. They do not pluck these strings with their hands. They tune them—drawing a strand a hair tighter, shifting a bone weight half an inch—and then let the mountain play them. Driven by the ceaseless bass vibration of the distant falls, the taut submerged fibers wake into soft, steady overtones that hum through the sheltered dark. Every hollow pier in the valley holds its own chord, shifting faintly whenever a great fish booms outside in the plaza or a dweller climbs past along the outer flutes.

It is here that they gather. Four or five of them will drift inside a spacious column, curling loosely in the still, dark water, pale limbs intertwined, heavy bodies stacked neutrally without weight. They do not speak through the open water, where sound scatters across miles and belongs to everyone. To talk privately, they press the smooth, oil-filled drums of their faces directly against one another's brows, throats, or chests. Through skin and bone passes a dense, intricate stream of clicks, sliding hums, and delicate pauses—entire histories of leaking weirs and shifting seeps, the births of young in the high cloud-fed terraces, the slow aging of a favored fish, the exact tuning of a pier three basins away. When two of them court, they drift together inside a newly strung chamber, pressing cheek to cheek until their slow gill-mantles lift and settle in the exact same count, adding their own low chest-hums to the trembling willow-strings until the whole column rings warm and full in the dark.

To call across the open plazas, they step back outside and use the bones of the valley itself. A slow, rhythmic pattern rapped with the dense knuckles against a specific fluted rib travels straight down the pier, runs through the buried woven foundations of root-stone, and climbs up into the next column half a mile away, faster and clearer than water could ever carry it. What sounds to a stranger like the occasional hollow *knock* of settling wood is often a greeting passing beneath your feet, or a quiet summons toward a sagging terrace wall.

They live so slowly that a century scarcely alters them, except to make them heavier, broader across the shoulders, and darker with moss. When they sleep, they do not hide; they simply flatten themselves against a parapet or the lee of a viaduct, fold their long arms close to their flanks, and let the blind crawlers come. Within an hour they are part of the wall again, indistinguishable by touch or echo, breathing once a minute while the valley hums through their ribs.

And when one of them grows truly ancient—so dense with bone and stiff at the joints that gliding between piers takes too much effort, and the pale skin across its acoustic drums begins to cloud and thicken—it does not wander out toward the rim. It climbs one last time into the hollow heart of the pier it has tended longest.

There, wedged comfortably among the smooth, cold-cured inner ribs above the weighted strings, it stops swimming altogether. The younger ones still come to press their brows against its broad chest, bringing soft peat-tubers and listening to the deep, slow knowledge stored in its bones. Over the years, the pale water-willows and creeping sedges threading the inner wall of the column reach out, slow as stillness, and lay thin, living rootlets across its motionless hips and folded legs, binding it gently into the curved wood. It does not fight the roots. Its gill-mantles lift more and more slowly, keeping time only with the deepest bass pulse of the distant waterfalls. For decades it remains there—half dweller, half soundbox—humming softly in sympathy with the valley, steadying the pitch of its column from within, answering with a faint tremor whenever a young one raps a question against the outer flutes.

Eventually, the gill-mantles settle and do not lift again. The cold, low-oxygen water wastes nothing. Year by year, the living fibers close smoothly over the heavy, pale bones, curing them into the inner wall of the pier until the column itself is a fraction thicker, its hollow voice a fraction deeper, carrying the exact shape of the one who rested there for as long as the mountain holds its sea.

So when you hover out in the cold black between two colossal piers, feeling the whole sunken plateau work around you like a single quiet organ, you are not alone in an abandoned monument. The stillness you feel is not emptiness; it is patience. High up along the shadowed arches, dark with cropped moss, flat against the booming timber, hundreds of wide, cold hands are pressed lightly to the walls—listening to the rim, counting the hum of the conduits, and keeping the long, cold note from ever breaking.
