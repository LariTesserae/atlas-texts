You would swear, at first, that the pocket holds nothing larger than a thumb.

The glare makes liars of the eyes. Where light pours equally from every raw fracture face, nothing can step forward from a background; there is no dark crease to outline a shoulder, no dim hollow to betray a knee. Every wet surface throws back the same cold, surgical white. Even knowing that bare thumbs pinched those dark clay bowls and bare fingers left those damp iron-ochre marks, it is easy to assume their maker only reached in from a crack that has since groaned shut, leaving a few small offerings to be absorbed by the stone.

Stay still, though, and let your eyes learn to read curvature instead of shadow.

Near the heart of the thicket, where three broad blades of milky quartz converge like the ribs of a narrow hull, there is a plane that does not cleave cleanly. Its sheen is a fraction softer than feldspar—closer to soaked bone, or wet tallow—and along its upper edge, the cold light passes slightly *through* it rather than bouncing hard off its face, catching a faint, pale web of silver-blue beneath the surface.

Follow that line downward, and the geometry of the room alters. What looked like a blistered spar-shelf is the curve of a forearm. What looked like a knotted seam of pale horsetails, beaded with microscopic drops of condensation, is fine wet hair plastered across a narrow temple.

Whoever brought the river-clay and the bird-bones did not go back out.

She is still here, braided through the narrowest negative spaces of the stone.

Because the cavity has no floor and no clear span, she neither sits nor lies. She is suspended and held by the crystal lattice itself, folded edgewise along the crooked, hand-wide alleys between the blades. Her torso turns sideways in the vertical cleft between two glowing plates of quartz; one leg is drawn up with the sole of her foot braced against a flat face of feldspar, while the other shin angles downward into a bright cradle where chalk-white crawlers click their mandibles just beneath her ankle. She does not slump under her own weight. Braced at twenty small points of contact—hip, shoulder blade, heel, rib, cheekbone—and packed tight in the acoustic pressure of the air, she hangs in the stone's grip with the same strange weightlessness as the unfalling drops of dew.

She is bare. Anything woven or stitched would have snagged on the newly split rock when she worked her way down through the hair-thin fissures above, and nothing in this pocket needs shelter from wind or chill. Her body is small, spare, and wiry—the lean frame of someone who grew narrow enough to slip where water slips—with soles thickened from old river gravel and incisors worn slightly smooth from holding bone and sinew. Yet it is impossible to tell how old she was when the passage closed behind her, and meaningless to ask how long she has been here since.

The room has already taken her into its temperature and its sheen. No warmth rises from her skin; she does not steam in the sharp, damp air. She is held at the exact cool threshold of condensation, coated in the same shivering film of water as the selenite around her. Along the outer curve of her left forearm and across one collarbone run three or four hair-thin lines where the raw rock grazed her on the way in. They have neither darkened into scabs nor faded into scars. Sealed under the room's wet film, they remain forever at the pale, unbleeding instant of a fresh nick in cold water. Wherever a sharper crystal edge pressed too close to her ribs or her knee, she wedged a flattened pinch of dark river-clay or a folded pad of wet liverwort between blade and skin—tiny soft buffers, still damp, never crumbling.

And the pocket's other residents have long since stopped treating her as an intruder.

Nothing here rots, and nothing hunts in sudden bursts, so her motionless, cool flesh has simply become another ledge of damp, singing architecture. A thumb-sized amphibian clings to the pale flat of her upper arm, its broad translucent toes spread wide against her skin, its glass-bubble throat inflated and humming steadily against the tendon beneath. Down by her heel, a thin sheet of translucent liverwort has rooted in a wet quartz seam and stretched its taut, pale-green drumskin across the narrow gap to meet the hard edge of her calloused foot, vibrating visibly between mineral and bone. Behind her shoulder blade, tucked into the fingernail-wide slot between her spine and a ribbed plate of spar, a cluster of mirror-carapace insects blurs its wings in place, feeding its dry metallic rasp straight into her back.

Most striking of all is the hollow at the base of her throat. Because her head tilts slightly back into the triangle of stone, the shallow basin above her collarbone holds a coin-sized pool of condensation—just like the tiny cradles in the feldspar ledges. Its surface is carved by the chamber's relentless chord into a crisp, motionless six-pointed lattice of standing waves. Ringed tightly around the rim of that vibrating pool sit four microscopic conical snails, their pale, salt-encrusted shells fixed in place along her skin like beads on a white wire.

She is not dead, and she is not sleeping.

Look closely at her face, held a hand's span from one of the broadest, most blinding plates of quartz. Her eyelids rest low, almost closed, leaving only a thin glistening slit; her lashes do not blink, because the tear-film across her eyes evaporates at the exact rate cool vapor settles back onto it. Each lash holds a single bright bead of moisture that trembles in place and never drops onto her cheek. Directly in front of her gaze, pressed onto the blazing stone, sit two sharp, unsmudged prints of red iron-ochre.

Nothing in this room can cast a shadow—light pours out from behind her, beside her, and directly off the stone touching her skin, shining straight through the thin cartilage of her ears and the webs between her fingers—but the ochre refuses to glare back. Those two damp rust-colored marks are the only dullness in her field of vision, two small spots of earth-color placed deliberately where her eyes can rest without drowning in white.

Her jaw hangs slightly parted, her damp teeth gleaming softly like pale spar. She does not breathe in the heave-and-fall rhythm of the upper world; a chest rising and collapsing would break the standing waves in her collarbone pool and fight the dense acoustic gel packed into the room. Instead, her lungs stay half-full, taut as the liverwort skins, held in an eternal, shivering half-exhale.

Listen beneath the glass-rimmed shriek of the fractured rock, beneath the wet hum of the amphibians and the metallic rasp of the insects, and you will find her voice. It is so low and smooth that at first it sounds like a trick of the heavy bedrock pressing in from outside—a steady, dark, velvety undertone, an octave and a fifth beneath the crystal pitch. It has no words and no cadence. It is simply the sound of a wide ribcage, a throat, and a skull hollowed out and tuned to the chamber, turning her entire torso into the pocket's largest wind-instrument. Without it, the chord would be all edge and glass; with it, there is a faint, marrow-deep resonance running through the base of the room, the closest thing to warmth the sound allows.

It was not always Bearable. You can still read the story of her arrival in the position of her arms and the grime that remains beneath her nails.

When she first slipped through the narrowing seam—drawn down, perhaps, by a thread of cold white light or a glass hum rising through a deep spring-hole—she brought with her the only things that belonged to the world of soil and seasons: a lump of dark grey clay dug from a riverbank, a handful of hollow bird-bones and antler splinters, and a wet smear of ground iron-ochre. Even now, dark river-mud sits packed tight under her fingernails, never drying to dust, and faint rust still stains the whorls of her finger pads.

She entered a room of merciless mineral perfection, where newly torn bedrock screamed at a pitch sharp enough to crack a human mind, and where every angle cut like glass. Before her warmth bled out into the cool air and the equilibrium braided her pulse into its closed loop, she worked with fingers, teeth, and patience to make the instant livable.

She wedged the hollow rib-bones and bird-bones into the sharpest-ringing slits between opposing plates of spar, nudging the room's shriek down by a fraction of a hair until her own ribs could ring inside it without tearing apart. She felt through the vibrating gel of the air until her fingertips found a tiny acoustic node where opposing waves cancelled into stillness, and there she balanced three thin mineral flakes upright—a miniature cairn to mark the only quiet point in the stone. She pinched the river-clay into four or five small cups and pressed them onto the jagged ledges, giving the geometric water soft curves shaped by human thumbs instead of fracture planes. And last, as her movements slowed and the chord finished locking around her, she pressed her red fingertips to the blinding crystal faces within reach, leaving small islands of iron and opacity against the surgical light.

Now her two arms lie threaded far out along separate narrow channels of the crystal thicket, and it is in her hands that you can still see how fiercely alive she remains.

Her left hand rests lightly against a broad vertical sheet of selenite, just beside one of the wedged bird-bones. Two ochre-stained fingertips touch the singing mineral face with almost no weight at all—yet she is not merely leaning there. She is listening through the pads of her fingers, feeling the entire four-pace chamber as a single continuous body. Because the sound links every blade, pool, and creature into one vibrating medium, she does not need to turn her head to know the room. Through the tremor in the selenite under her fingertips, she feels the exact pitch of the bird-bone beside it; through her shoulder blade, she feels the mirror-insects' rasp; through her heel, the steady percussion of the crawlers; through the standing-wave star trembling at her throat, the unbroken fifth of a hundred domed amphibians.

Once in a stretch of stillness that has no clock to measure it, she shifts the pressure of those two fingertips by less than the weight of a drop of dew—a motion far too small for any eye to catch. Across the pocket, the selenite plate softens its overtone by a hair's breadth; the six-pointed lattice in the clay bowls tightens, shimmers, and settles back as her pressure eases. It is not a struggle against the equilibrium. It is a greeting—the quietest possible touch, confirming that she is still *someone* inside the chord, and not merely another plate of white stone.

Her right arm extends in the opposite direction, slipped past a sparse tuft of bleached celadon ferns until her hand opens, palm upward, directly beneath a downward-pointing needle of milky quartz.

At the razor-sharp tip of that needle hangs a single clear bead of condensation, swollen and bright, trembling with hidden geometry. It hangs scarcely a hair's breadth above the pale hollow of her palm. The singing air holds it up; vapor condenses onto its skin at the exact rate it evaporates; it will never grow heavier, and it will never drop onto her waiting flesh.

Yet her palm stays open beneath it.

Inside her cool, still skull, there is no hunger, no exhaustion, and no panic—none of the frictions that require a future or a past. Her thoughts do not hurry in sequences; like the ripples frozen in the clay bowls, they hang suspended in crisp, braided clarity, neither fading nor wearing down. And what hangs there, bright and unsmudged, is the one thing no other presence in the bedrock possesses.

The raw quartz only knows the split-second it tore open. The snails and amphibians and silver-scaled worms only know this blinding, unbroken note. She alone keeps the memory of what lies above the water table: the dark, yielding smell of river silt, the weight of a dead bird's wing in the hand, the slow relief of dusk cooling into shadow beneath wet leaves, and the strange, lost sound of water that actually falls.

In the blazing, shadowless glare, ringed by snails and humming amphibians, she holds her low note beneath the crystal shriek, watches her two red marks on the burning white stone, and keeps her open palm waiting a hair's breadth beneath a drop that will never reach her.
