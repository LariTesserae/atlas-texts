If you stay crouched behind that low half-circle of stacked stone long enough—long past the point where your ears have gone numb to the screaming and your back teeth ache from the constant shudder of the rock—you will begin to notice that the valley is not quite as empty as it pretends to be.

You will not hear anyone coming. Nothing with lungs or footsteps can compete with the chimneys. You will see a hand first, emerging over the satin-smooth rim of the quartzite overhang: broad-palmed, blunt-fingered, scarred white across the knuckles, the skin stained pale ochre and faintly glossy with dried sap. It does not snatch at the rock. It settles flat against the warm stone and stays there for two heartbeats, reading the spine of the bridge before the rest of the body follows.

Then the climber rolls smoothly into the leeward pocket behind the wall, moving with a low, compact economy that never once lifts higher than a crouching shoulder.

At first glance, they look almost cobbled together out of the valley’s own debris. Nothing on them hangs loose. Their torso and thighs are wrapped tight in dark, dull-oiled hide—the leathery wing-membrane of the chasm’s gliders, thick and scarred with old flight-creases, stitched close along the ribs with plant-sinew and sealed at every seam with boiled resin so a sudden cross-draft cannot slip underneath a hem and peel them off a ledge like a fern-mat. Around their waist and across their chest runs a heavy web of braided, sap-stiffened vine-cord, knotted flat with copper toggles and looped with small wedges of fire-hardened wood. Their hair is cropped to coarse stubble, or plaited tight against the skull and varnished down with pale latex so the wind cannot whip it across their eyes.

Bound firmly around their head is a wide strip of felted moss-fiber, tied snug over both ears to hold dense, oiled plugs of wool-moss deep inside the canals. Pushed up onto that brow-band sit two small, curved plates of pale grazer-shell, each pierced with a narrow horizontal slit—pulled down over the eyes only when stepping out into gravel-hail or stinging sap-spray. Their feet are bare, broad, and splayed, the soles thick as horn and tacky along the arches from crushed vine-stems, gripping the humming ochre stone with an ease that looks less like balance and more like adhesion.

They do not look out across the chasm when they arrive. First, they check the rawhide lashings on the curved hardwood trough pinned beside the copper pegs, nudging a loose stone shim tighter with the heel of their palm. Then they kneel, lay one bare forearm along the hollowed wood, and rest the angle of their jaw squarely against the notched upper rim. Their eyes close.

To anyone who does not live here, the valley is a roaring, shapeless violence. To the people of the spires, it is not even loud.

Because their ears are plugged almost from the hour they are born into the open air, the screaming wind reaches them only as a thick, wooly surf at the edges of thought. The real world arrives through bone. Pressing a jawbone to a carved trough, a palm to a stone fin, or bare heels to a vibrating ledge strips away the chaotic froth of surface gusts and opens up the deep, slow architecture underneath. Every spire in the valley has its own pitch, its own hollow throat, its own way of shuddering when the unseen haze miles below begins to boil. A climber learns the grammar of that vibration the way a lowland child learns to read clouds or river-current. Through their teeth and knuckles, they feel the valley breathing—a slow, heavy pulse that tells them when a vortex is grinding past the next arch, when a rising column is about to slam wet cloud against the eastern flank, and when the shear across a narrow rib will drop just enough to grant them ten clear breaths of passage.

While the climber kneels at the trough, a torn ribbon of cloud slams headlong out of the bright sky into the stone bridge.

Instantly the pale rock turns dark and slick; the moss-mats swell; water sheets horizontally off the thrashing olive fronds. The climber does not huddle deeper behind the wall. They move with sudden, practiced speed. From a flat pouch strapped to the small of their back comes a thin wedge of hammered soft copper. Leaning just past the shelter of the overhang, keeping one elbow hooked around a copper anchor-peg, they run their free hand along a narrow horizontal stratum seam. The coin-shaped grazers clamped there have lifted their ridged shells a fraction of an inch to rake moisture off the wet stone. Before the shells can snap shut again, the copper wedge flicks in—one, two, three—prizing loose three heavy, dust-colored discs and tucking them into a waist-loop. With the same motion, the climber presses a wad of clean, dried fibrous moss into the brimming cup of a waxy rosette, pulls it back swollen with tepid frothy water, and wrings it hard into the narrow neck of the scraped bladder beneath the wall.

Thirty seconds later, the dry cross-draft slices through, tearing the mist to rags. The rock steams in the bright afternoon sun. Already the climber is back behind the low stones, dry-skinned, chewing slowly on the salty, mineral-tough meat of a cracked grazer, watching the far horizon through narrowed, sun-leathered lids.

They do not live on exposed shelves like this one. The windbreak on the bridge is only a crossing-hip—a place to wait out a turn of the air, mend a frayed cord, or leave water for whoever comes next.

Their real homes are hidden inside the stone itself.

Scattered among the thousands of shrieking chimneys and wind-bored eyelets are a handful of dead flues—vertical shafts whose lower throats collapsed ages ago, choking off the upward blast and turning a howling pipe into a warm, quiet, dimly sunlit well. High above the rim, the shear still tears past like surf across a cave mouth, but thirty feet down inside the smooth, fluted belly of the rock, a stray frond of moss barely trembles. The stone walls stay faintly warm through day and night, humming softly with the sub-bass of the living chimneys nearby.

It is inside these plugged throats that they sleep, raise children, and work the few stubborn materials the valley gives them. Wide, flat stones wedged into old flutes form narrow terraced shelves. Tightly braided vine-hammocks sling across the shafts from hammered copper pegs, keeping bodies off the rattling grit that still sifts down from above. In a deep side-pocket, baffled by stacked flat slabs and stuffed moss-plugs, sits a hearth no wider than two cupped hands. There is never a blazing fire here, and never a trail of smoke visible from across the gulf. They burn only dense, resin-heavy knots of dried copper-brush root over a bed of pebbles; the tiny wisp of smoke is drawn through a thumb-wide side crack straight into an outer thermal, where the wind shreds it into nothingness two feet from the stone.

Over those careful, choked-off coals they boil white vine-latex into tough amber glue, fire-harden wooden wedges, and anneal the malleable nodules of native copper they glean from wind-scoured gravel bowls. Everything they make is small, heavy, and built to survive shaking. Pottery would shatter in a week; instead they use scraped plant-bladders, woven root-baskets sealed with hardened sap, and shallow bowls hollowed from dense driftwood-like knots of old brush-wood. Their finest needles, awls, and water-siphons are carved from the hollow, hinged wing-bones of the chasm’s gliders.

They do not hunt the gliders in the open air—nothing on two legs can match a creature that rides turbulence that would snap an ordinary spine—nor do they treat them as enemies. They watch them the way sailors watch albatrosses, reading the sharp, asymmetrical folds of their dark pinions to locate invisible seams where rising air meets falling air. When a fresh moss-raft slams into a cliff and a glider drops onto it to tear open the peat for bloated pale grubs, a climber nearby will often wait until the glider banks away before moving in to harvest the rest of the grubs and edible tubers. Only when an old glider misjudges a collapsing shear, breaks a wing-hinge against a spire, and tumbles into a leeward crease do they claim it. They strip the body with grave, unhurried thrift—scraping the dark leathery sail for cloaks and harnesses, boiling the sinew for lashings, saving the heavy curved bones—and then cast the picked carcass back out into the rising draft, returning it to the sky so it will not sour the narrow shelter.

Because no single spire can hold enough greenery or catch enough mist-plumes to feed a crowd—and because an entire lush shelf can be peeled bare to gleaming rock in a single afternoon—they do not gather in villages. They live in small, scattered knots: a solitary tender keeping a bridge-hip, a pair sharing a dead chimney, a three- or four-person band holding a broad saddle with two good water-hollows. Miles of bright, howling emptiness separate one household from the next.

Yet they are not isolated. They live inside a quiet, wide-flung web of attention.

When two of them finally meet—perhaps after weeks of seeing only each other’s distant markers across the gulf—they do not pull out their ear-plugs to shout over the gale. Even inside a dead chimney, airborne speech is thin and unreliable, and most elders have lost the high, papery range of air-hearing entirely. Instead, they step close, wrap calloused hands behind each other’s necks, and press forehead squarely to forehead—or set a bare palm flat against the other’s collarbone and throat.

And then they hum.

Their language is made for bone. It has no breathy whispers, no sibilants that the wind could steal; it is a warm, low carpentry of chest-rumbles, gliding nasal tones, sharp glottal stops, and crisp clicks of the teeth, sent directly through skull and sternum. Pressed brow-to-brow behind a windbreak, eyes half-closed, two weathered climbers can hold an entire conversation that makes barely a murmur in the open air: news of a huge crimson-creeper mat that landed rich with grubs on the western arch; a warning that the copper pegs on the north rib are working loose in the quartz; quiet laughter felt as a rapid, trembling ripple in the ribs; grief held as a long, steady, low note that matches the deep thrumming of the stone beneath their knees. With their hands, they add a swift, tactile shorthand pressed into the forearm or shoulder—counting days, tracing the curve of a spire, marking the angle of a shear.

Children learn this bone-voice before they ever see the open sky. Born in the warm, humming gloom of a plugged chimney, a newborn is lulled to sleep with a parent’s jaw resting lightly against the crown of its skull, its tiny ears padded with soft, oiled moss-down. Before a child can walk properly, its fingers and toes know how to lock around a braided vine-cord. In the leeward pockets, toddlers are kept on short, sap-stiffened tether-lines pegged to copper pins, playing with rattling gravel-pebbles and the pale, wind-pitted nodules that whistle when you blow across their holes. The first game every child plays is the flat-freeze: the instant an adult taps a sharp triple-beat against the rock with a knuckle—mimicking the shudder of a collapsing pressure-slap—the child drops nose-to-stone, fingers wedged into a crack, limbs tucked tight, holding completely still until a long, slow palm-rub along the spine gives the release.

Still, a question lingers whenever you look out from that stone bridge toward the leaning ochre needle half a mile away, where a single thin pole of stripped wood stands jammed into an unreachable crown, its three tattered strips of cured hide snapping into a pale horizontal smudge.

There is no rib connecting that needle to anything. No broken arch, no rope-line long enough to span half a mile of howling void. How did anyone reach it?

Not by climbing. By riding the peel.

When a shelf of moss, braided fern, and copper-brush grows too thick and water-heavy on an upwind spire, the people of the valley do not watch it tear loose with dismay. They watch it with calculation. Days before the stone lets go, a climber who needs to cross an unbridged gulf will crawl out onto the groaning mat and begin to groom it—trimming the leeward vines so it will lift cleanly, weaving a tight, springy cage of living brush-stems right into the tangled root-heart at its center. Inside that fibrous hollow they lash their tools, water-bladders, flint flakes, copper pegs, and a stripped wooden pole wrapped in cured hide.

Then they climb back to the bare rock, press their jaw to the listening-trough, and wait.

Sometimes they wait half a day; sometimes three. They wait for the deep sub-bass of the abyss to settle into a particular, unmistakable rolling swell—the pulse that says a rising funnel is hooking around the upwind flank and sweeping hard across the chasm straight toward the target needle. When the pulse builds under their teeth, they crawl into the woven root-heart, lock their arms and legs around the thickest, greenest vines, pull their shell-slits tight over their eyes, and reach down with a flint-edged chopper to sever the last two anchor-tendrils holding the mat to the quartz.

The wet, fibrous screech shakes their whole skeleton. For a breathless instant the world drops out from underneath them, and then the vertical shear gets beneath the cottage-sized raft and hurls it out into the miles of bright ordinary daylight.

There is no steering it—only enduring the geometry they chose. Curled inside six feet of damp peat, springy brush, and snapping vines, the rider tumbles end over end across the sunlit void. Sky and pale abyss trade places five times in ten heartbeats. Shredded leaves and frothy water spin past in a blinding green blizzard. Up inside a rising column they go weightless, stomach climbing into the throat; then a pocket of sinking air slams them downward, bruising shoulders and ribs against the tangled roots. Minutes later—or what feels like an age of roaring green darkness—the raft collides violently with the ochre flank of the leaning needle.

Half the mat explodes on impact and rains down toward the unseen haze. The center—bound tight by the climber’s own weaving—buckles, absorbs the shock like a massive wet cushion, and snags on a weathered stone rib. Its barbed tendrils hook into micro-fissures; its broken stems immediately bleed thick white latex that cures in the warm blast and welds the raft, and its shaken, bruised passenger, to the new cliff.

Not every peel lands. Everyone here knows someone whose mat caught a bad sink-pocket, unraveled mid-chasm, and drifted slowly down into the pale, sun-washed haze below. They have no name for whatever lies at the bottom of that mist, only the quiet certainty that the warm air boils up from it and nothing solid ever climbs back out.

That is why the pole stands on the needle’s crown, whipping furiously against the blue sky.

Since they cannot call across half a mile of hollow air, they write to one another in stripped wood and hide. Every pole jammed into a prominent nose or saddle is a message meant to be read by sharp, narrowed eyes from another spire. The height of the pole, the stain on the strips, the number and cut of the tails—all of it speaks across the riot. A single dark, sap-blackened strip warns that a spire’s ledges have been stripped clean to bare rock. Two broad, knotted strips mark a sheltered dead chimney with full water-cups.

And those three tattered, pale strips blurring horizontally atop the leaning ochre needle tell anyone kneeling at the bridge-trough half a mile away a quiet, complete story: *I made the landing. The new raft holds water and grubs. I am alive on this bone, waiting for the air to turn.*

Down on the bridge, or inside the safest, widest saddles, are the ones who read those poles most faithfully: the old.

When a climber’s knees stiffen with salt and bruises, and their scarred fingers can no longer trust a ten-breath scramble across a knife-edge rib, they do not become a burden to be left behind. They settle into the broad, bridged crossroads—the places with the deepest dead chimneys and the oldest, best-seasoned hardwood troughs. Forty or fifty years of pressing bone to stone leaves a person with an uncanny stillness. An elder can lay two crooked fingers against a rock fin and feel a distant shear shifting three spires away before a young climber notices the fronds changing pitch. They keep the crossing-hips alive: re-twisting frayed vine-ropes, boiling fresh sap, mending torn glider-cloaks, drying strips of grazer-meat, and tending the small, fiercely guarded copper-coals. When a young traveler rolls over the overhang, breathless and spattered with white latex from a hard landing, it is an elder’s steady, humming brow that meets theirs behind the windbreak, and an elder’s careful hands that press a full bladder into their chest.

And when an elder finally reaches the end—when even the hands can no longer braid cord, and the humming of the valley begins to fade even out of their bones—there is no earth here to dig a grave, and no wood to spare for a pyre.

Instead, their kin gather in the quiet lee of a windward shelf where a thick emerald moss-mat has grown heavy and ready to go. They wrap the body snugly in fresh, broad-leaved creepers and damp, sweet-smelling peat, binding the shroud with supple green shoots, and tuck it deep into the fibrous heart of the raft—the same way a living rider tucks in for a crossing, only without the flint chopper, and without the harness. Then they retreat behind the low stone wall, press their bare palms and foreheads to the warm rock, and wait together in silence.

They stay until they feel the deep sub-bass swell through their knees, until the wet, fibrous screech rises even through their plugged ears, and the whole shelf peels cleanly off the satin-smooth stone. They watch the green raft lift out into the bright, ordinary daylight, trailing ribbons of flowering vine and sparkling spray as it climbs a warm thermal, spinning high across the miles of hollow blue—traded back to the valley, carried out toward some distant, sun-bleached spire, or drifting gently down into the warm, pale haze that feeds the wind.

Standing back at the leeward hip of the stone bridge, looking out across ten miles of sunlit emptiness, nothing appears to have changed. The noise still hammers against the ancient rock; the great beards of air-root still thrash upright like green flames; the distant ochre needle still stands solitary beneath its furiously snapping strips of pale hide. Yet once you know how to look—and once you lay your bare palm flat against the warm, vibrating stone—the whole howling void shifts beneath your skin. It is not a wasteland at all. It is a vast, bright, singing country, fiercely held by small, quiet hands that know every pulse of its stone bones, and ride its fury as lightly as leaves.
