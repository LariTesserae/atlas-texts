If you stand still long enough to hear condensation slide down a fern stem, and long enough for the bare stone plazas to resolve out of the greenish dimness, you will realize that one of the fallen slabs is breathing.

Not visibly at first—perhaps once every forty seconds, a slow, almost imperceptible widening along the ribs, followed by a stillness so complete that the warm air around it does not stir. It sits crouched near the edge of a mirror-black puddle, folded knees drawn up toward narrow shoulders, long forearms draped cleanly down its shins. At a glance it reads as another jutting wedge of raw shale, or a slumped buttress of damp clay. Only when a single delayed drop of water slips from an overhanging frond and touches the puddle—sending a soundless ring across the dark glass—does its head turn, moving with the smooth, unhurried weight of a wet stem bending under dew.

There are three of them in the pocket right now, though you will rarely see more than one at a time unless you know where the charcoal shadows deepen beneath the overhangs.

They are roughly the stature of slight humans if they ever stood fully upright, though they almost never do. Their spines hold a long, supple curve suited to low ledges, tilted slabs, and vertical rock-faces. They have no hair, no clothing, and no harsh angles to their movement, yet their bodies belong unmistakably to the geology of the room. Their skin is matte and slightly porous—velvety as damp lime-plaster—darkening to wet slate across the backs, shoulders, and outer limbs, while the hollows of their throats, the undersides of their forearms, and their wide palms stay the chalk-pale ash of freshly broken calcite.

They drink the chamber through that skin. When they press a palm against a weeping hairline fissure or kneel beside a brimming puddle in the mist-hour, the dark slate deepens and slicks with moisture. Later, as the midday heat thickens and steadies, water evaporates imperceptibly from their pores and leaves behind a faint, dry frost of dissolved limestone—fine pale mineral lines tracing their collarbones, knuckles, and ribs in patterns nearly identical to the calcite veins running through the fallen roof-plates.

Behind each jaw, where outer ears might be, lies a wide, shallow crescent of pale membrane stretched taut as a wet leaf. It catches no speech—nothing here speaks—but it registers the shift of a bat's wing in the ceiling notches, the faint upward draft of a fungal chimney, and the minute vibration when a damp flake of fresh shale slips two inches down the newly broken wall. Their eyes are large, wide-set, and flat-surfaced as the chamber's rainwater puddles, catching the dim pewter skylight so cleanly that you can see the overhanging curtain of maidenhair ferns mirrored in them. Every few minutes, a translucent inner lid slides sideways across the glass-dark surface without a sound.

On the razor-sharp scree and fresh cleavage of the floor, they move barefoot and completely uncut. The soles of their feet and the long pads of their fingers are thick, pillowed, and pale—soft enough to mold around a splinter of raw shale without shifting it or letting it click against the slab beneath, resilient enough to take their full weight on an edge sharp as cut flint. When one crosses a bare plaza, it steps cleanly over the vaulted clay arcades without brushing a single grain of wet mortar, its body heat matching the heavy, body-warm stillness of the room so closely that it trails almost no draft behind it. Even the large-headed soldiers ringing an unfinished arcade threshold do not flare their mandibles as a long slate leg passes three inches overhead.

They did not fall in with the roof, nor did they climb up from beneath it. Like the maidenhair spores and the pale mound-builders, they are pioneers of the break.

Out in the flat, hot lowland scrub above the rim, the wind shakes the brush, harsh sun bleaches every surface, birds shout, and soil rots thick over rounded, ancient stone. They cannot endure that weathered noise for long. When an older sinkhole fills with dirt and brush, when its sharp corners blunt and singing frogs move into its puddles, they leave it on a night of heavy rain—slipping up through the tangled lip and moving slowly across the drenched lowlands until they catch the unmistakable scent of raw wet chalk, freshly fractured limestone, and newly opened groundwater. Then they drop silently into the new skylight, before the air has even finished warming, and claim the brief years before the forest closes over it again.

You can still see the remnant of their last home on the taller of the two older ones. Along its left calf and the ridge of one shoulder blade clings a stubborn, papery patch of dull, olive-stained crust—weathered skin thickened by years in an older, mossier pocket. In the cool early mist, when condensation slicks the raw stone and softens the clay towers, it sits patiently beneath a weeping seam and peels a sliver of that old husk away with a flat fingertip, uncovering fresh, velvety ash-grey skin beneath. It does not drop the shed flake carelessly onto the open floor. It carries the dry strip to one of the sharp wall-corners where fallen spores and insect husks are already gathering, and tucks it neatly out of sight. Within minutes, glossy black ants arrive to dismantle it, and pale workers from the nearest arcade carry fragments away to knead into the wet rims of their chimneys.

In this way, they are not young in age—perhaps they have followed fresh collapses across the lowlands for decades, or longer—yet inside this pocket they are becoming young to the air again, matching the unweathered stone.

The third of them is truly young. Half the height of the others, narrow-ribbed and still almost translucent at the tips of its fingers and toes, it was carried here through the rainy dark folded inside a damp, calcified dormancy and woken only when the first greenish light reached the fresh slabs. Its skin has not yet settled into the deep slate of the elders; it remains a pale, bruised pearl-grey, easily marked by the wet umber of a clay buttress or the green stain of liverwort. It spends long stretches crouched completely motionless at the edge of the central slabs, watching a velvet worm lay its vanishing track, or studying how the older ones place their heels without disturbing a rock chip. Once, when a tiny translucent frog on the underside of a glossy heart-shaped leaf slowly lifted a pale toe, the small one answered—lifting a single long, nearly clear finger with identical, unhurried gravity, and holding it there until the frog lowered its foot.

It is the three of them who keep the plazas open.

The generous bare expanses separating the clay clusters are not accidental. In the mist-hour, when the mound-builders pour outward to extend their streets across the stone, one of the elders walks the clean floor ahead of them. It never breaks an arcade. Instead, it works in the blind builders' own grammar: lifting a fallen shard of scree out of a path it wishes them to take, or drawing a thin, wet line of crushed calcite-dust across a slab it wishes to keep bare. Reaching that sharp mineral boundary, the workers hesitate, turn cleanly, and curve their vaulted street around the edge of the open stone instead. Now and then, the elder gathers a pinch of damp detritus—fallen spores, shed moth-wings, washed silt—from the corners where the first dust of soil is forming, rolls it gently between wet palms, and sets the tiny dark pellet beside an unfinished threshold.

Later, in the thick stillness of midday, when the fungal vaults inside the tallest towers exhale their warmest, sweetest vapor upward through the glistening chimneys, the elder stands beside a knee-high spire and lowers two long fingers into the open crown. The pale soldiers below remain motionless, sensing no threat in the familiar mineral-sweet scent of its skin. The elder pinches off a small tuft of soft white fungal threads blooming just inside the damp rim, brings it to its narrow, seam-like mouth, and steps back without leaving a thumbprint on the fresh mortar.

They eat little else—a scrap of tender liverwort scraped from a wet seam, the faint, sweet bead of sap that gathers at dawn on the uncalloused tips of the dangling aerial roots, a drop of iron-rich groundwater licked from a flat palm. Their days are governed not by hunger, but by attention.

Under the deepest north overhang, where no rain falls and the light softens to charcoal, they keep their instruments—if something so slight can be called an instrument. Using single strands of abandoned gossamer borrowed from the lowest silk skyways, they suspend hair-thin splinters of freshly cleaved calcite from jagged rock-teeth so that each pale point hangs less than a millimeter above a mirror-black puddle. There is no wind to sway them. The splinters hang dead still for hours, until the hidden water table shifts by the width of a hair, or until the limestone vault settles another fraction of an inch into its new shape. Then a tip touches the black glass, and a single silent ripple widens to the cut-straight stone edge.

The older ones spend long midday hours crouched beside these pendulums, resting flat palms against the bare slabs to feel the deep, slow settling of the shelf beneath them. They are taking the measure of the room—reading which overhanging strata have found their rest, where the next seam will open to groundwater, and how many wet seasons remain before the floor softens into mud.

Across the forty-pace chamber, they never cross a plaza unnecessarily to speak. Instead, they use the weak skylight.

When the elder crouched beneath the south fern-curtain wishes to call the others' notice to something—a new hairline crack weeping fresh water, a sudden shift in a clay arcade, a bat stirring early in the ceiling notches—it picks up a wet, flat flake of calcite and tilts it slowly toward the ragged opening above. A dim, greenish-pewter bar of reflected light detaches itself from the gloom and slides soundlessly across the dark floor, climbing the fluted wall of an umber tower until it rests on the dark shoulder of the other elder forty paces away. The distant one does not turn its head. It simply rotates one long forearm outward from its shadow, revealing the chalk-pale inner skin of its wrist in a single, slow flash, then turns it back to wet slate.

When they do gather—usually during the delayed percolation of an afternoon lowland shower, when heavy drops tick softly onto the broad leaves and silver films crawl along fresh fractures—they fold together under the dry north cornice, directly beneath the tiered mud apartments of the potter wasps and cave-swiftlets. Sitting knee to knee, they press their open, chalk-pale palms together, or lay their bare soles flat against the same uninterrupted slab of raw shale. Through the dense stone pass faint, deliberate shifts of weight—not taps, never loud enough to disturb a sleeping swiftlet two feet above their heads, just slow, rhythmic compressions of pressure that travel cleanly into the taut membranes behind their jaws. Sometimes a velvety leaf-tailed gecko slips down the slate wall and rests motionless across the smaller one's shoulder, matching its skin to the pale mineral streaks along the collarbone, and none of the three moves to shake it off.

Of all the things in the chamber, it is the hanging aerial roots that draw their longest gaze.

Dozens of them dangle straight down out of the overhanging tangle into the dim empty air, pale plumb-lines with soft white tips still stretching downward, millimeter by millimeter, toward the bare stone. Every morning, as the mist lifts, the elders pass silently beneath them. Where a root hangs directly above a newly raised clay arcade or the center of an open plaza, an elder cups the tender white tip in a damp palm—warming it, wetting it with a drop from a puddle—and gently drapes a single thread of silk around its pale stalk, drawing it a fraction of an inch sideways toward the rock wall. They cannot stop the roots from coming down. They do not even try. They only guide the first landing, coaxing the future trunks toward the edges so that the center of the chamber stays open, dark, and ringing with bare stone a few seasons longer.

In the quietest hour just before dusk, when the small bats begin to unhook themselves from the dry ceiling notches and drop out into the warm air on wings that make no sound, the smallest of the three often stands alone in the middle of a bare plaza beneath the skylight.

It lifts one long, translucent-tipped hand straight up into the greenish pewter dimness, stopping a hand's span beneath the lowest dangling aerial root. It does not touch it. It holds its pale palm open beneath the white, uncalloused tip—feeling the faint downward reach of the living stem, measuring the narrow gap of empty warm air that still separates the forest above from the raw rock below. Above the rim, five paces away through the dense, hot lowland brush, the wind moves through the scrub and insects saw loudly in the heat. Down here, the three of them remain in the hush, skin darkening with the evening damp, living cleanly inside the space before the root touches the stone.
