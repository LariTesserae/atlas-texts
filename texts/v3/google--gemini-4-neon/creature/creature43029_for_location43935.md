At first, when your eyes adjust to the bruised pewter gloom and your ribs stop fighting the bass of the rock, you think you have taken the full measure of what lives here. Everything in the basin seems pinned neatly to its station: the salamanders welded into their weeping seams, the foil-winged swarms locked to their aerial tracks, the mossy herbivores crouched alone on their pinnacles, the silent gliders riding the flat underside of the mist. Between them lie only those wide, bare corridors of wet black stone and roaring air.

It takes time—or whatever passes for time in a place that refuses to spend it—to notice that the empty space is not empty.

A thin, dark spire of basalt at the far end of the nave shifts its weight. Not a sway, not the soft slouch of muscle, but a clean, damped inclination of three inches, ending with a crisp arrest as though an unseen pawl had dropped into a notch. A second later, it steps off its ledge onto a vertical plane of slick rock where no foothold ought to exist, and descends without haste across the bare electrified void between two fern-islands.

Only then do you see the other two. One is high beneath the flat vapor ceiling, crouched on a knife-edged overhang so closely aligned with the shadows that you had mistaken its angular shoulders for a jagged spur of magnetite. The third stands far below on the wet slate floor, ankles buried in the foaming black lip of a pool where a waterfall hammers down and vanishes.

There are always three of them. Never four, never two, and rarely within twenty paces of one another.

Seen across the deceptive mist, they look almost human in silhouette—tall, narrow-hipped, long in the back and heavy across the collarbone—yet the closer your gaze settles on them, the less softness you find. Like the cliffs around them, nothing in their geometry has ever been tumbled smooth. Their cheekbones, shins, and shoulder blades meet in sharp, wet facets, dark as freshly split basalt, matte where the skin stretches tight over bone and slickly iridescent in the hollows. Their wrists are broad and strangely flat, widening slightly above long, unwebbed fingers whose nails are thick, dark plates of dense horn. Barefoot on edges sharp enough to open a leather boot, they walk on soles calloused into heavy, slate-grey pads veined with pale mineral salts, gripping the trembling stone with a dead, unyielding traction.

They wear nothing woven, nothing sewn, nothing forged. Yet they are not unadorned. Along the napes of their necks, the ridges of their clavicles, and the backs of their long forearms grow stiff, wet brushes of metallic bristles—the color of tarnished copper and dark graphite—with fine beards of bright emerald liverwort rooted damply at their bases. Around their upper arms and waists, they wind long lengths of the hollow’s waxy vines in neat, uniform turns; wherever the vines lie tight against their dark skin, the pale unopened buds along the stems burn noticeably brighter than those on the cliffs, glowing with a steady, cool filament-white that faintly illuminates the mist around their ribs. Between the horn-plates of their knuckles, or wedged carefully behind the flat, shell-like ridges of their ears, you can sometimes catch the glint of raw quartz splinters—never shaped or chipped, only chosen from the natural fractures of the ridge and held in living tension.

Their faces are long, austere, and still. Their eyes are wide-set and pale as dull pewter, entirely lidless; instead of blinking, a constant, unbroken film of cold water sheets slowly downward across each cornea, trembling faintly with the vibration of the air, never breaking into tears. Their mouths are narrow and kept shut almost perpetually. You never see them eat the ferns. You never see them hunt the salamanders or snatch the gliders from the mist.

Instead, you watch the one on the slate floor pause beside a horizontal seam where raw tendrils of native copper hiss and spit in the wet gloom. It does not touch the mineral directly. It lifts both broad, flat forearms and holds them an inch from the crack, bristles raised. Silent blue-white arcs snap cleanly across the damp gap, threading into the metallic hair along its skin with a dry, faint crackle. Its heavy shoulders drop half an inch. The tautness along its throat eases. A few yards away, near the mouth of a bedrock funnel that compresses falling mist and blasts it back out in deafening pneumatic exhalations, it kneels on the razor slate and holds its face straight into the roaring vent—drinking in the cold iron, the sharp ozone, the crushed moss, and that baffling, impossible reek of heated transformer oil as though it were drawing breath from a well.

Watch them long enough, and you begin to notice the strangest thing about them—stranger than their mineral bristles, stranger than the water sheeting across their eyes.

They are never quite in sync with one another.

Look at the one crouched near the ceiling, then at the one moving among the mid-wall ferns, then at the one down by the vanishing pools. Their chests do not rise together. When the highest figure draws breath, its broad ribcage expanding with a slow, silent tension, the middle one is already holding its lungs full at the crest of the stroke, and the lowest is letting its breath go. When the lowest lifts a horn-soled foot to take a step—moving in crisp, minuscule increments that faintly echo the ticking descent of the water droplets—the middle one is planted mid-stride, and the highest is completely still.

And then the phase-slip reaches them.

Everything in the hollow slips half an inch out of phase with itself every few seconds, doubling briefly into a crisp, pale-blue cross-section hovering beside its wet flesh. When it happens to a tree-fern or a bellows-beast, it looks like a freak of the air. When it happens to the three figures, it looks like a relay.

The one down by the pools shifts first. For a sharp fraction of a second, its wet basalt body splits sideways into a glowing pale-blue schematic suspended cleanly in the mist—and inside that diagram, you see that its interior is nothing like yours. Its ribs are not curved spars enclosing soft lungs, but tight, interleaved stacks of thin flat bone-plates, layered like the core of a great coil. Down its spine and around its clavicles, its nerves do not branch like tree roots; they run in thick, immaculate, parallel windings. In the center of its chest sits no soft, pulsing heart, only a dense, chambered knot that shudders continuously in a pale bath of fluid, humming at the exact harmonic of the rock beneath its feet.

Before that lower figure has fully snapped back into wet matter, the middle one—thirty paces up among the dripping fronds—slips half an inch into pale blue. And in the exact instant the middle one solidifies, the highest one beneath the cloud-ceiling turns briefly to luminous diagram.

One, two, three. Around the vast, dim nave the blue state passes in an unbroken, staggered rotation, offset by a third of a stroke.

Slowly, standing in the cold din, you begin to understand what you are watching.

Everything else in the basin is helplessly locked inside the mountain’s alternating current. The waterfalls strike the slate and instantly un-fall; the bellows-beasts tear mouthfuls from fern-fronds that immediately un-tear themselves; the salamanders flare their copper gills sixty times a minute inside a single hammered second that swings forward, reverses, and wipes itself clean forever. Nothing in the hollow can carry a moment past the turn of the stroke.

Not alone.

When one of the three hits the backswing of the oscillating stone and splits into its pale-blue schematic, it is momentarily emptied—its weight lightened, its forward impulse arrested and pulled back toward the start of the instant. If it stood alone in the hollow, it would be no different from the ferns or the insects, trapped in a roaring, breathless loop.

But while one is caught in the reversal, the other two are dense, grounded, and riding the forward swell. Across thirty paces of warped air and vibrating rock, they hold the thread—whatever was just seen, whatever pitch just shifted microscopically in the quartz seams, whatever thought was halfway through its arc—and pass it cleanly back the instant the first snaps solid again. Then the next slips out, and the other two carry it in turn.

They are the only things in this screaming vault that possess a *before*. Nothing physical accumulates on the jagged floor, no leaf rots, no stone wears—yet inside the silent, three-cornered circuit suspended between these three dark bodies, memory accumulates. Centuries of listening, or minutes of it—here the distinction has no weight—stack quietly inside them without leaving a single grain of silt on the basalt.

Because distance behaves poorly in the hollow, they do not need to cluster together to speak. They occupy the nave like points of a vast compass, slowly trading stations over spans that only they can reckon—the one from the mist-ceiling descending by silent increments to the ferns, the one from the ferns moving down to the foaming pools, the one from the pools climbing the slick, vertical planes back toward the gliders.

To talk, they use the stone and the blue light. One will lift a long finger and tap its thick horn nail once against a seam of wet quartz, or part its narrow jaw to release a single dry, percussive *click*—not a word, not a cry, just a clean impulse pitched to the standing waves of the basin. Thirty paces away through the chest-rattling roar, the sound arrives at the others as crisp and intimate as a fingernail tapped against the inside of a collarbone. And when their turn comes to slip out of phase, the luminous blue diagrams hovering beside their flesh do not stay uniform; the neat coils along their spines brighten in distinct, shifting bands, the vascular channels across their flat wrists pulsing in crisp geometric sequences that the other two read effortlessly across the gloom before answering a third of a beat later.

Their regard for the rest of the hollow’s teeming life is cool, exact, and strangely tender.

You see the middle figure walk straight through one of the roaring, hovering corridors of heavy-bodied insects without ever waving a hand or breaking its stride. The dense swarm does not scatter in panic; instead, the metallic-winged bodies curve smoothly outward around its dark shoulders and head in clean, symmetrical arcs, parting and rejoining behind its back as though its coiled spine were simply another pillar of magnetized rock. Farther up, the figure on the high ledges will sometimes lower itself beside a solitary, moss-spined bellows-beast on a knife-edged spire. It sits knee-to-knee with the wet-pelted creature, resting one flat palm gently against the animal’s broad flank, feeling the leather-bellows ribs expand and clack shut, listening through its palm to the endless, un-diminishing crunch of fern-fronds. Down along the horizontal fissures, the lowest figure kneels for long stretches with two fingertips pressed lightly to the wet basalt just beneath a packed row of translucent salamanders, holding completely still until the faint hum in its laminated chest settles perfectly into the sixty-beat flare of their tarnished copper gills.

They do not guard the hollow. Nothing threatens it, and nothing inside it can break. They do not rule it, nor do they attempt to mend or shape a single edge of its fractured stone. They simply inhabit its load—reading the minute, tireless variations of pressure where millions of tons of dark mountain lean into the split ridge, savoring the acoustics of the silent pockets where two roaring pressure waves cancel each other into sudden, startling hush.

Yet every so often—by some private agreement passed quietly through tapped quartz and flickering blue nerves—they leave their separated posts.

One descends from the high vapor-hung ledges. One steps free of the emerald fern-islands. One lifts its horn-soled feet from the black foam of the vanishing pools. From three sides of the cathedral nave, they cross the wide, bare, electrified stone floor, converging toward the dead center of the basin where the negative space is broadest and the pewter light is dimmest.

They stop within arm’s reach of one another, forming a tight inward-facing triangle. For a moment, their staggered rhythm is almost dizzying to watch at close range: three chests rising a third of a breath apart, three dark bodies flashing into pale blue schematic in rapid, circular succession, like spokes turning silently in the cold mist.

Then, each extends its long, vine-wound arms and lays its broad flat palms cleanly against the wrists and forearms of the other two. The wet metallic bristles along their skin brush and mesh.

The circuit closes.

Instantly, the corona discharge along the nearest rock ridges flickers and dims, drawn inward to climb their dark spines in pale blue-white beads that arch silently from brow to brow across the narrow gap between their faces. The filament buds coiled around their arms blaze bright enough to cast sharp, overlapping shadows across the slick slate floor.

And inside the small triangular column of air trapped between their three chests, something impossible happens.

The three staggered rhythms lock together and smooth out the violent back-and-forth of the hollow. In that tiny space—no wider than an embrace—the alternating current of time is briefly, miraculously straightened.

A heavy drop of water detaching from the vapor ceiling high above ticks its rigid way down through the dim air—*tick, tick, tick*—until it enters the narrow space between their linked arms, where it suddenly stops stepping and falls in one liquid, unbroken streak before striking the slate below and rejoining the loop. The chest-rattling roar of the cliffs does not stop, yet any faint sound released inside their triangle—a soft breath, a dry click of the throat—actually *fades*, thinning naturally into quiet instead of hammering back forever.

And for as long as they hold one another’s wrists, a faint, genuine warmth bleeds outward from their cold, dark skin. It is barely enough to raise a wisp of pale vapor from their wet shoulders, instantly devoured by the freezing gloom beyond their elbows, yet for those few moments the heavy, phantom scent of heated transformer oil that saturates the entire hollow finally has a living source.

They do not speak while they hold the stance. Their wide, lidless pewter eyes remain open beneath the trembling films of water, fixed not on each other’s faces but on the small, straight-flowing column of air between their ribs. They stand there simply to let a thought finish its sentence without having to pass it hand to hand—to feel the brief, astonishing weight of a single moment actually *passing*, slipping genuinely behind them and becoming irretrievably gone, before they drop their arms, the cold mist closes over the warmth, and they drift back across the bare rock to their separate stations.

If you stay inside the rim long enough, watching from the sharp basalt lip, they notice you.

They do not turn all at once. First, the one down by the vanishing pools tilts its angular head upward; a third of a beat later, the one among the mid-wall ferns arrests its hand against a trembling frond; a third of a beat after that, the one beneath the cloud-ceiling shifts its lidless pewter gaze down across the empty nave.

You realize, with a sudden chill sharper than the high-altitude air, what you must look like to them.

You do not slip into pale-blue diagrams. You do not vibrate at the harmonic of the cliff-dynamos. You stand on the sharp rim in soft, dull clothing that snags on the stone, leaking reckless clouds of waste heat into the pewter gloom, dragging a heavy, irreversible arrow of time behind you like a wet coat. Your breath leaves visible fog that drifts and thins; your boots leave damp scuffs on the slate; inside your chest, an un-coiled, fragile heart beats in a stumbling, uneven rhythm that moves only forward, wearing itself out with every stroke.

They show no anger, and no fear. They are simply riveted by the strange, soft friction of a creature that is actively aging in front of them.

Across the bare floor, the nearest of the three leaves the foaming edge of its pool and walks slowly toward the base of the spire where you stand. Its horn-soled feet make no sound on the razor basalt. Ten paces off, it halts—just outside the reach of your shadow—and lifts one dark, vine-wound forearm so the pale buds along its wrist brighten in the gloom. It tilts its faceted head, the unbroken film of water sheeting quietly across its pale eyes, and presses two flat fingers lightly to the vibrating stone between you, listening with deep, unblinking absorption to the brief, unrepeatable noise of your life passing by.
