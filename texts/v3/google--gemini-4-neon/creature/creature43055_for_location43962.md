You notice them first by an absence of sparks.

Walking along the wet, salt-darkened rims of the terraced brine pans, you grow used to the dry, rapid crackle of the salt bridges—tiny blue-white ticks snapping across the white crust where one shallow pool steps down into the next. The rhythm is as steady as the roar of the ironstone chimneys behind you. So when you round a shoulder of fluted shale and come upon a broad, three-tiered pan where the white crusts sit utterly quiet, your eyes drop to the water to see what has broken the circuit.

Nothing is broken. Something is simply using it instead.

Sprawled across the wet mineral sill between the upper pan and the middle one lies a low, heavy mound that you would pass at fifty paces for a slumped boulder of wet ironstone. It is the same damp galena-grey, the same shouldered, worn-smooth geometry as every other outcrop in the basin. Along the dry crest of its back, powdery turquoise blooms of malachite have crusted into deep longitudinal creases. One side of it gleams with a bright, raw sheet of freshly plated copper, catching the plain afternoon light just like the electrolytic ledges submerged a yard away.

Only when you stand still—feeling the floor’s steady hum vibrate up through your calves—do you see the shallow brine around its lower edges trembling with a faint, independent pulse. Deep vertical pleats along its flanks widen by half an inch, hold, and slowly draw shut again. Two of the palm-sized bronze crawlers are clinging motionless to the dull, chalk-crusted ridge of its spine, their hooked legs tucked beneath them, grazing quietly on the slick dark film that gathers in the damp folds of its hide.

Then, without warning, the boulder shifts its weight and stands up.

For a startled second, watching it rise against the pale, hazy sky, your mind snaps back to the conviction you have been trying to shake all afternoon: *someone works here.* It lifts itself onto two thick, wide-set legs, unfolding to a height of perhaps seven feet, though it never truly straightens. Its shoulders are broad, heavy, and permanently stooped forward; its arms hang past its knees. Coated half in bright metal and half in pale mineral rime, it has the unmistakable, heavy-shouldered silhouette of a foundry hand in a stiffened leather apron, climbing out of a sump to walk a shift.

Then it turns slightly in the clear daylight, and every trace of the human falls away.

It has no face. Above the rounded shelf of its shoulders sits only a low, blunt keel of bone-dense cartilage, smoothly weathered like river basalt, with no eyes, no nostrils, and no mouth. Along the lower ridge of that eyeless crown, where a jaw might have been, runs a dense fringe of short, dark, copper-tipped filaments that twitch faintly in the warm updraft. Its arms do not end in hands, but in broad, flat, heavily calloused paddles as wide as shovel blades, webbed with dark, raised veins of dense magnetite-rich tissue.

Water pours off those paddles in murky, rusted threads as it lifts them clear of the upper and lower pans. Behind it, the moment its weight leaves the sill, the white salt bridges along the terrace lip flare back to life—*tick-tick-tick-tick*—the blue-white sparks resuming their dry crackle as if a switch had been thrown.

The creature itself has been the bridge.

Stand close enough as it steps onto the slick mud apron—it pays you no more mind than it pays the steam—and you begin to read its body the way you read the strata of the valley. Its hide is not shell like the little crawlers, nor ordinary skin, but a thick, leathery, deeply pleated rind impregnated with salt and trace iron, damp around the joints and stiff as cured rawhide across the broad flats of its back. And the two halves of it do not match.

Down its entire left side—from the blunt crest of its shoulder, along the ribbed flank, and all the way down the long left arm to the broad paddle—it is armored in bright, unoxidized copper. The metal is not worn; it is grown directly into the outer calloused layer of its hide, plated out of the warm brine during the hours it spent holding that limb in the lower pan while its right limb rested in the strongly acidic upper terrace. Current has been running continuously through its deep, fluid-filled chest, driving its slow internal chemistry the way a galvanic cell drives an industrial bath. Wherever the metal has thickened around its left elbow and knee, thin, gleaming curls have already begun to lift and peel at the crease. Every stiff stride it takes shears off a bright, hair-thin shaving of pure copper that drops onto the buzzing gravel behind it.

Its right side tells the opposite half of the equation. That flank and arm are dull, chalky, slightly pitted, and stripped bare of sheen—washed pale with white salt rime and faint mustard streaks of sulfur, slowly etched away at the anodic end of its own circuit.

If it stayed bridged in one direction too long, the copper would weld its left joints solid into a statue while the brine ate its right side down to the sinew. It survives only by leaving before the plating locks, and by turning itself around the next time it settles into the pans—left side where the right had been, stripping down what it built up, rebuilding what was stripped away.

You watch it step out from the wet margin onto the wide, vibrating flat.

Its gait is slow, heavy, and remarkably clean. Its feet are broad, splayed, thick-soled pads with no toes, planted flat on the hardpan at each step. On the flats, where the constant high-volume tremor from the deep chambers keeps the loose grit dancing downslope, the creature’s posture subtly relaxes. Its pleated chest ceases that slow, deliberate widening and narrowing; it no longer needs to pump its own internal fluids. The roaring vibration of the basin floor travels straight up through those wide, cushioned soles, rattling the dense brine inside its torso, keeping its circulation moving the way a running shaker-table keeps slurry suspended.

It does not wander. It moves along a straight, unhurried diagonal across the open grey ground, heading directly toward the nearest of the basalt rockers—the four-beat slab hammering half a mile out in its wet, graphite-slick socket.

Halfway there, it crosses within twenty yards of where you stand.

Without eyes or ordinary ears, it still knows you are on the floor long before you could raise a hand. To anything with feet tuned to the basin’s bedrock, the entire valley is mapped in sharp relief. The dozen interlocking rockers send continuous, overlapping shockwaves across the hardpan; every ironstone chimney’s hollow suction, every wet terrace, every slumped shale ridge bends and dampens those ripples into a permanent seismic grid. Your two boots—soft, narrow, shifting unevenly, carrying a warm heartbeat completely out of phase with the stone—cut a small, erratic smudge into the hum of the floor.

Fifteen paces out, the creature slows and comes to a halt.

It does not turn its blunt head toward you. Instead, it settles both wide feet firmly into the buzzing grit, leans slightly forward, and lowers its copper-bright left arm until the paddle hangs an inch above the sorted windrows of quartz and galena. The fine dark filaments along its brow-ridge and wrist stir, tasting the dry static halo clinging to your clothes and the unfamiliar thermal wake rising off your skin. From the damp, pleated shadow behind its right knee, one of the bronze crawlers peeks briefly out, tests the humming air with its static-bleeding spines, and retreats back into the quiet fold.

For several heartbeats—measured by the distant *shriek-thud* of the basalt slabs—it simply registers you: something soft, leaky, and asymmetrical, carrying the faint, unmineral smell of things that grow, burn, and rot away.

Then, with neither haste nor caution, it lifts its bright arm, steps three paces off its line, curves around you in a smooth, wide arc, and rejoins its exact diagonal ten yards behind your back. Already, the vibrating floor is erasing both your narrow tracks and its broad, flat prints, sorting the disturbed grit cleanly back into neat, straight bands.

Follow it out to the basalt rocker, and you see why it came.

The great lens-shaped slab is working on its steady four-beat cycle—lifting a hand’s breadth off its polished bed with a sharp pneumatic shriek, firing a sideways jet of scalding vapor through the worn notch in its rim, and dropping back with an iron-heavy *thud* that rattles your teeth. The creature does not hesitate at the edge of the wet, silt-rimmed apron. It walks right up to the outer lip of the bedrock socket, timing its approach not by sight, but by the deep, swelling rise of pressure underfoot in the thermal flue below.

On the third beat, it drops into a heavy crouch and presses its gleaming, copper-stiffened left flank and arm firmly against the wet, graphite-slick rim of the stone socket—not inside the crush zone, but right against the resonant lip where the shockwave breaks hardest.

The multi-ton slab lifts, shrieks, and slams home.

A clean, dry snap runs through the creature’s plated side—inaudible beneath the roar, but plain to see. A network of bright fissures spiderwebs instantly across the copper shell along its shoulder and arm. It shifts an inch, turning its stiffened elbow into the stone for the next beat, and the next. With each hammering *thud*, curved plates and long, gleaming ribbons of pure metal shear cleanly away from its flexed hide, sliding down the wet, slick rim onto the vibrating gravel apron below. There, the shaker-table floor immediately claims them—nudging the bright curls downslope to join the neat windrows of filings, where the hard quartz sand will grind them fine and wash them down into the lower acidic sumps, to dissolve back into the brine and rise once more at the upper seeps.

Not an ounce of metal leaves the basin’s loop. The creature is only a slow, walking detour in the copper’s circuit.

Once the heaviest plating has cracked free, leaving its left flank raw, dark, and pliable again, it rises and steps around the socket toward the worn exhaust notch. Timing the fourth beat again, it turns its chalky, salt-encrusted right side into the wide, cool outer fringe of the sideways steam jet—far enough back that the vapor is only scalding-warm rather than flesh-stripping. The wet mineral plume washes over its pale, etched creases, melting away the white salt rime and leaving its pleated hide dark, slick, and supple.

From the rockers, its round takes it toward the roaring chimneys.

Around the slumped, blackened bases of those thirty-foot ironstone stacks, coolish damp air slides steadily inward across the gravel, tugging at your ankles, while the grooved stone flanks radiate a fierce, dry, furnace warmth. Here you often find two or three of them standing motionless at a time, spaced twenty yards apart in the acoustic lee of the great roaring mouths. They stand half-turned to the heated stone, spreading the deep vertical pleats of their torsos slightly open to the steady inward draft, venting excess moisture and balancing the salinity of the warm brine locked inside their chests.

While they dry, the bronze crawlers come and go freely—climbing off the damp backs to slip into cool crevices behind nearby shale banks, while fresh ones creep out along the gravel to climb the broad ankles and resume their slow grazing among the salty folds. The tall ones never brush them away. The crawlers keep the dead microbial skins pared back from the sensitive pleats, and their tiny bristle-like spines help bleed off stray static before it can arc across a drying joint.

Walk further across the miles of flat hardpan, and you begin to spot them everywhere you had missed them before—scattered widely across the open glare, rarely more than three or four visible from any single vantage. One half-submerged in a distant brine terrace; another trudging a clean diagonal between a seven-beat rocker and a chimney; a third leaning its dark, grooved shoulders into the shimmering heat of a flue.

Once in a while, down in the largest of the stepped terrace systems, you come upon two or three of them bridged across the same sequence of pools at once.

They do not crowd each other or contest a ledge. One settles its broad paddles between the highest seep and the second pan; another lies ten yards downslope, spanning the second pan to the third; a third bridges the third into the murky lower sump. Linked in series through the warm, conductive brine, their bodies become a single, living extension of the ore beds beneath them.

When that happens, stand quietly on the dry hardpan above the pans and listen past the basin’s roar.

Along the damp stone lips around the linked figures, the tiny blue-white sparks do not go entirely dead as they do when a solitary creature feeds. Instead, the dry, uniform crackle breaks into a strange, uneven cadence. A rapid burst of five bright ticks snaps along the white salt bridge between the upper and middle pans; then a pause; then a slow, stuttering answer ripples along the crust beside the lower pool, tiny wisps of sharp ozone rising in the still afternoon air. The dark filaments along the creatures’ blunt crowns quiver in faint, matching surges.

What passes between them through the salted water is impossible to read. It may be nothing more than the balancing of charge—a slow biological equalization, like two vessels finding a shared waterline. Yet watching the hesitant, intricate pauses in those snapping sparks, it is hard to shake the feeling that something slower and far more patient than weather is being checked, tuned, or passed from one chest to the next so that it does not fade.

You stay an hour on the basin floor, and then another, watching the light refuse to lengthen or tilt. And if you stay long enough, you witness the one moment in which every scattered figure in the valley acts as one.

The dozen basalt rockers scattered across the miles of hardpan each keep their own locked tempo—four beats here, seven beats half a mile west, eleven further south, thirteen beyond the pale salt flats. Most of the time their slamming rhythms tumble over one another in a heavy, rolling polyrhythm that keeps the ground humming without break.

But at long, mathematically fixed intervals, all of those interlocking counts draw close to the same instant.

Minutes before any human ear could detect the narrowing gap between the beats, the figures across the basin know. Half a mile off, one of them stops mid-stride on the open flat. Near the chimneys, two drying figures step cleanly out of the lee onto the bare, humming gravel. Down in the terraced pans, the linked shapes slowly lift their right paddles an inch out of the murky water, breaking their own circuit so that the salt bridges flare suddenly bright along every wet lip.

Across miles of grey hardpan and bright mineral flats, every standing creature turns to face the nearest great basalt slab. They plant their wide, heavy feet squarely on the stone and lift their long arms out to either side—one arm gleaming bright copper, the other dull and pale—holding them wide like balance-poles against the motionless pale haze.

The rhythms tighten. The stray shrieks and distant thuds draw closer, crowding into a single ragged swell, and then, for two full seconds, every multi-ton slab across the valley sits seated at once. For that brief breath, beneath the endless roar of the chimneys, the hammering stops.

Then the pressure peaks together. Across miles of open ground, the basalt rockers lift in one long, layered pneumatic shriek—a sudden dry brightening in the valley’s noise—and drop back into their wet, polished sockets on the exact same beat.

The single synchronized *thud* hits the floor like a fist striking a taut drumskin. It jolts straight up through your boots and locks your jaw.

Out on the wide flats, the shockwave runs up through the splayed feet of every standing figure, ringing through their hollow, brine-filled chests. Simultaneously, across the whole pale expanse of the basin, loose copper curls and fine white salt-dust shake free from dozens of outstretched arms. For half a second, in the clear, unchanging afternoon light, faint glittering clouds of raw metal hang shimmering in the warm air around every distant silhouette before drifting down onto the sorted windrows.

And through the roar—barely heard with your ears, felt mostly as a dry, brassy resonance humming inside your own sternum—a single low chord rises from their pleated chests in sympathy with the bedrock. It holds for two steady breaths, clean and unwavering, and fades cleanly back into the turbine-buzz of the stone.

Then the rockers fall back out of phase, sliding smoothly into their separate counts of four and seven and eleven. Across the flats, the long arms drop. Down in the terraces, the broad paddles sink back into the warm brine, silencing the sparks once more. The walkers resume their unhurried diagonals.

Look closely at them as you walk back toward the long, worn slope of the rim, and the strangest truth about them settles in beside the stillness of the light.

There are no small ones trotting beside the large ones. There are no slow, crippled ones lagging behind near the acidic sumps, and no dry, abandoned husks wedged in the quartz windrows like the empty shells of the bronze crawlers. Every single one you pass is the exact same full, heavy, weathered size—grooved and smoothed by endless rounds of brine, vibration, and draft until it sits into its own shape the way the basalt slabs sit into their sockets.

Just as mineral deposition fills the rockers’ beds at the exact microscopic rate that stone grinds away stone, each round strips away a thin, invisible fraction of their anodic flesh and plates an equal measure back onto their cathodic side when they turn around. Their bodies neither build up nor wear down. They do not grow, they do not age, and they leave no dead behind to disturb the neat, straight windrows of the floor.

If you climb back up the long, gentle lip of the uplands, leave the heavy roar behind you, and return a century later, the plain afternoon light will still be lying unchanged over the flats. The same copper ribbons will be tearing free of the submerged ledges. And down across the grey hardpan, the same tall, half-bright figures will still be walking their quiet diagonals between the pans, the rockers, and the chimneys—running at full, patient load, carrying nothing away, and keeping the circuit closed forever.
