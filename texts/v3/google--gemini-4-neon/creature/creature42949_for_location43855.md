You could walk across the grates for three days and think the deck belongs only to the animals.

The eye is already busy enough sorting upholstery from iron, and the people here do not break the horizon any more than the condenser housings do. They are never standing tall on a gantry roof, never waving from a catwalk, never moving fast enough to catch the corner of your vision against the pale, bright haze.

You usually notice one only when you are twenty paces away, and even then your brain tries first to file the shape as another calf-sized hide-beast slumped neatly against a manifold bank. Then the angle shifts by two steps, and the curve resolves into a bare shoulder, a narrow back, a head bowed gently over cupped hands.

Sitting on a low concrete bund between two shallow ceramic pans, feet submerged past the ankles in the glass-still water, a person looks less like an occupant of the machine than a soft component cast to fit its remaining gaps.

They are recognizably human, though shaped so completely by thirty-two degrees and unbroken white light that they seem made of slightly different clay than anyone who has ever walked on soil. Their skin is the pale, matte cream of the porcelain insulators, faintly translucent over the knuckles and ribs, slick with the same clear film of warm condensation that coats the aluminum. Almost no hair grows on them—only the sparsest colorless down along the forearms, and scalps either smooth or covered in a thin wash of fine pale hair pasted flat by the damp. There are no calluses on their palms or heels, no sunburn, no squint-lines around the eyes. Their eyes are wide, calm, and slow to blink, the pupils neat and small against the glareless daylight.

Even the way they hold their bodies belongs to the heat. In air this warm and wet, sweat cannot dry; a brisk stride or a moment of panic builds heat faster than the body can shed it, turning the head faint and the chest tight. So they do not hurry. They have no hard, corded muscle, only lean, slack-limbed grace and a breathing rhythm almost as slow as the amphibians beside them. When one stands from a concrete bund, the weight transfers without a splash. Walking along the diamond-patterned walkways, they place each bare sole flat and lift it cleanly, arms hanging quiet at their sides, turning their heads with the unhurried steadiness of a needle settling on a dial.

If you stay still and watch, you realize how thoroughly the rest of the deck has accommodated them.

Every square inch here is already spoken for, yet they never scrape a ledge bare to claim it. When a person sits on a concrete bund, the slate-blue amphibians perched along its edge do not leap into the water; they merely shuffle their webbed toes sideways by an inch, forming a neat, glistening border to either side of the person’s thighs. Sit motionless for half an hour and two or three of the perchers will step gently onto a forearm or a bent knee, hooking their soft toes around the skin as if it were another warm rail, throats pulsing twice a minute.

Beneath the surface of the ceramic pans, the tiny limpet-like grazers creep up onto a submerged ankle and begin their patient, ticklish work, their metallic-tipped radulae rasping away dead skin and invisible bacterial film until the soles of the feet gleam pink and fresh. The people let them. Nothing accumulates on their skin any more than it does on the brass weirs; there are no sores, no cracked heels, no grime. When they sleep—curled half-in, half-out of a shallow pan with a cheek pillowed on a low bund, or leaning back-to-back against the broad, unblinking flank of one of the huge hide-beasts—the boundary between flesh, fauna, and housing blurs until you have to count breaths to tell where one ends.

They wear almost nothing. There are no plants for fiber, no dry air to cure leather, and cloth left in this constant humidity would stay heavy and sour forever. Nor do they carry salvaged metal; nothing on the deck ever rusts loose or breaks off, and no one here would dream of prying a bolt or severing a wire. What small belongings they keep are borrowed from the rare, clean deaths of the animals: a folded square of veined, oiled-paper membrane from a four-winged ceiling glider, laid beneath the knees or hips so the crisp aluminum grating does not press waffles into soft skin during a long rest; a curved, translucent ochre shield from a dead glass ray, carried lightly in one hand or tucked behind a ribbon-worm cord at the waist.

The ray-shield is their only vessel. With it, they skim the pale, odorless bacterial curds that collect in the still eddies behind stanchions and along the lips of the brass weirs—slick, faintly metallic jellies that taste of warm egg-white and clean pennies. With it, too, they catch pure water. The shallow pans are rich with dissolved minerals and trace metals, good for cooling the body but heavy on the stomach; the water beading on the undersides of the overhead porcelain insulators and galvanized I-beams is distilled sweet by condensation. It is a common sight, walking through the lower bays, to see someone sitting beneath a vertical guide-wire with their chin tipped back and mouth slightly open—or holding a pale ochre shield steadily beneath a porcelain skirt—waiting twelve unhurried seconds for each clear drop to break loose and fall.

Sometimes they eat flesh, though never by hunting. In a world packed to exact carrying capacity, killing leaves a gap that feels wrong to them, like a missing tooth or a slack flange. Instead, they watch with the patience of people who have nowhere else to be. They notice the exact moment an amber-eyed percher’s throat flattens and stays flat for five minutes, or when a four-winged hanger unclenches from a gantry soffit and falls straight down instead of gliding. They walk over before the slow surge of chitons and snails can finish its work, lift the body cleanly, and take only the soft, pale inner meat, eating it warm and raw before laying the tougher hide, plates, and metallic mouthparts back on the grating for the grazers to polish away.

And when one of their own dies—usually very old, for a body kept warm, unstrained, clean-skinned, and free of rot stretches easily past a hundred years, thinning until fine blue veins map the wrists like the veins in a glider’s wing—they do not dig, for there is nothing to dig into, and they do not burn, for nothing here can dry or catch flame. They simply lift the quiet body into the center of a shallow ceramic pan, fold the hands gently over the ribs, lay a ray-shield across the eyes against the bright white sky, and step back onto the bund. Within an hour the glass rays slide closer with their glassy gill-pulses; by the next turn of stillness the chitons have upholstered the pale limbs in overlapping pearl-gray plates. Even the bones vanish over a handful of slow periods, rasped cleanly into the shells of the floor-dwellers, where calcium is rarer than copper. Soon the white glazed tiles shine empty beneath the poured-glass water, and the rays shingle smoothly over the space.

There is no night to divide their days, no weather to mark a season, no sun to drag a shadow across a flange. Time for them is not a wheel; it is a faint current, measured only in tiny shifts and the slow drift of their own bodies across the flats.

Yet they are not torpid, the way the animals are. Beneath their low-voltage stillness, their minds are sharp, observant, and quietly restless—the one thing on the continental frame that has not quite settled into satiated equilibrium.

They know where they are by the stamped metal. In a landscape where ten miles of condenser hoods repeat without a hill, a tree, or a single patch of rust to distinguish one bay from another, the sharp serial numbers and directional flow-arrows punched into every flange and valve-wheel are their only geography. They do not know what language the letters once belonged to, nor what engineering system assigned the digits, yet they read them as effortlessly as a sailor reads headlands. Over generations, the crisp alphanumeric codes have softened in their mouths into quiet, damp syllables—names for neighborhoods, names for lineages, names for themselves. A woman sitting by a broad sump is known by the flange beside which she was born; a man passing through from forty bays west introduces himself by the weir he left behind.

Often, without urgency, they follow a flow-arrow. A young person, feeling the slow itch of sameness in the bay where they grew up, will pick a single copper conduit or manifold pipe, read the crisp arrow stamped on its collar, and begin walking the way it points. They cover only a few miles between rests, stepping carefully over bunds, cooling their shins in unfamiliar pans, pausing to drink from new insulators. Sometimes the pipe dives beneath a grating and surfaces three bays over; sometimes it loops cleanly back after weeks of wandering to the exact heat-exchanger where they began; sometimes it runs straight on until the mist swallows everything behind them. It does not matter which. The point is simply to trace the line, to learn the slightly different pitch of water sliding over a distant weir, to see whether the glass rays forty bays north carry a paler tint of ochre on their shields.

Along the way, they stop to check the gauges.

Every pressure dial, every thermometer, every clear sight-glass etched with its exact water-line draws them for a quiet moment. They never turn a valve-wheel—a child learns not to touch the wheels before they learn to speak, the way they learn not to pry up a chiton—but they lean close to the glass face, wipe away the thin bead of mist with the side of a wet thumb, and look in at the crisp black needle resting dead-center in the green band. They do not expect it to move. They do not fear it will fail. Looking at it is simply a way of saying *still here* to the vast, silent machinery beneath their feet, and hearing *still here* back.

They meet in small, loose knots—three, five, occasionally a dozen—spaced evenly along the concrete walls of a wide manifold bay so the warm air can slip between their shoulders. You can walk within ten yards of a gathering before you realize they are talking. Loud sound feels abrasive here, almost obscene; their voices are low, breathy murmurs that barely carry past the whisper-thin hiss of the weirs, spaced by long minutes of comfortable electrical quiet. When one wants to call another’s attention down a catwalk without raising a voice, they simply tap a fingernail once, lightly, against the galvanized top rail. The crisp vibration runs cleanly twenty yards through the steel, arriving against the other’s resting palm or heel like a drop of water.

Mostly they don’t even need the tap. You can see a traveler coming fifty yards off by watching the railings: a tiny, half-millimeter tuck of webbed toes passing steadily down the unbroken row of amber-eyed amphibians, a pale, silent ripple traveling at walking speed until a pale figure steps out of the warm mist.

What they trade when they sit together is microscopic history—the only proof in an unchanging world that one stretch of brightness is not the exact same moment as the last. They tell one another that a large hide-beast near a certain heat-exchanger finally shifted three hand-breadths east, exposing a crescent of factory-bright aluminum for the first time in living memory. They recount how two ceiling gliders dropped from opposite soffits at the exact same instant and passed through each other’s wake without touching. They pass along the quiet news of a death, or—rarer still—a birth.

Children are the only bright fracture in the quiet. Born straight into the shallow, body-warm water of a ceramic pan, a newborn meets almost no shock at all—only the sudden weight of air, answering with a brief, startled wail that is the loudest sound the deck ever hears. Overhead, a ten-yard sheet of folded gliders shudders in a velvet ripple; along the nearest rail, every amphibian leans back a millimeter, amber eyes fixed and unblinking. Then the baby settles against warm damp skin, and the quiet closes back in.

For a few years, though, children live outside the set-point. They splash in the pans, sending unheard-of waves sloshing messily over the milled brass weirs. They giggle, run six quick steps down a catwalk, poke gently at the pearly chitons, and flush bright pink in the heavy saturated air until the heat forces them to drop back into the glassy water, panting and laughing softly with their chins just above the surface. The adults do not scold them. They simply sit and watch with a quiet, distant fondness, knowing that the warmth and the brightness are patient teachers. By the time a child grows tall enough to reach the overhead porcelain insulators without standing on a bund, their steps have slowed to the hydraulic glide of their elders, their voice has dropped to the level of the weirs, and their breath has matched the machine.

And yet there is one secret thing they do—not a duty, not a survival habit, not anything the deck asked of them.

Every tank, busbar, and pan carries its faint, regulated direct current, a fraction of a volt impressed cleanly across the continental frame to keep the metal young and feed the invisible film at the bottom of the world. To bare, wet human skin, that current is barely there—a dry prickle when you lay a palm against a stanchion, a faint coppery tang if you touch a damp knuckle to your lip.

Long ago, someone resting a wet temple against a warm copper riser while their opposite shoulder leaned against a galvanized bracket discovered what happens when a tiny trickle of that steady potential bridges softly across the bone behind the ears.

It does not hurt. It does not shock. It simply nudges the quiet fluid of the inner ear—and, for as long as you hold still, the flat, dead-level continent tilts.

If you walk deep into the wide manifold bays, where copper busbars drop low beside the concrete bunds, you will sometimes come upon two or three people sitting in absolute stillness with translucent ray-shields laid over their closed eyes. Each of them has the damp hollow behind one ear pressed gently to a bright copper flange, and the fingers of their opposite hand laid flat on the galvanized grating or submerged in the mineral-doped water of a pan. Their shoulders do not move. Their chests barely rise. Only their heads lean by a fraction of an inch, as if listening to something very far away.

Inside the dark veined gray behind their eyelids, the endless horizontal plain is lifting. Without raising their pulse by a single beat, without breaking a sweat in the thick thirty-two-degree air, they feel themselves banking slowly into an impossible curve, rising on a long, silent swell, tilting down a slope that does not exist anywhere on the deck. They have no words for *hill*, or *wind*, or *wave*, or *flight*. They have never seen a bird beat its wings against blue air, or felt earth give way beneath a climbing foot. Yet in the faint voltage borrowed from the machine, they feel all of those things at once—a gentle, soundless vertigo, the one wild motion allowed inside the tolerances.

They hold it for a few slow breaths, or for an hour, while the amber-eyed perchers sit undisturbed beside their knees and the water glides without turbulence over the brass weirs.

Then, slowly, one of them lifts their fingertips from the grating and draws their ear away from the bright copper. In three heartbeats the phantom swell flattens out. The world locks cleanly back to dead-level. They slip the ochre shield from their eyes, sit blinking once in the soft glareless daylight, and look out across the low repeating bays until mist and metal meet the white cloud—calm, damp, unhurried, and held at the exact same steady warmth as everything else that lives here.
