At first, you assume the city is simply finishing itself out of habit.

The delayed mallet-blows chipping stone, the reeds tucking themselves beneath the eaves, the wooden sluice-gates lifting an inch on their frozen flax ropes—it is easy to take all of it for the long, serene momentum of people who departed ten thousand years ago. Nothing breathes here. There are no prints in the mud beside the runnels, no hearths smoking against the pale sky, no voices carrying across the moss-plains.

Then you spend a second morning beside the half-finished retaining wall on the upper terrace, and you notice something that momentum cannot explain.

Yesterday, the rough-dressed block of basalt sat flush against the mossy coping, shedding a neat crescent chip every five minutes from an unseen bronze chisel. Today, the block has been turned ninety degrees on its bed. Three dry wedges of hand-split oak have been tapped beneath its eastern edge to tilt it slightly toward the sun, so that the old, arriving blows now bite cleanly along a fresh face. Beside it, the crescent chips from yesterday have been swept into a small, neat pyramid—not scattered into the ferns.

Down in the river-pebble plaza where the clay-brick dye-works rises silently each afternoon and thins back into damp slate by dusk, someone has laid six folded bolts of unbleached flax linen directly on the bare wet stones in the clear morning light, weighted at their corners with smooth river cobbles. Nothing stands around them. Yet if you return at one o'clock, the phantom brick walls have risen knee-high around the bolts, and the linen lies submerged inside cold, dark vats brimming with mineral rainwater. By twilight, when the walls dissolve, the wet cloth will rest on the empty flagstones again, dyed a deep, bruised lichen-purple, waiting to be gathered.

Someone is farming the lag.

You do not see them until you stop looking for movement and begin looking for posture.

Stand at the edge of the terraced apple orchard, where the timber aqueduct carries its looped snowmelt uphill, and let your eyes travel along the low dry-stone wall. Near the arched bridge, something you took for a weathered boundary-cairn, or a tall bundle of cut reeds propped against the mortar, shifts its shoulder half an inch to keep a patch of pale lichen out of a sudden shaft of sun.

Even from twenty paces, you can tell it does not breathe. Its chest does not rise or fall. No plume of warm vapor fogs the cold air before it. It is tall and narrow—roughly the height of a person, though it stands at an angle that ought to spill it sideways into the ferns, leaning cleanly twelve degrees to the north as though the hill beneath its heels sloped differently from the hill beneath yours.

Come closer, and the quiet strangeness of its build resolves out of the mist.

It is not carved stone, nor is it flesh, nor is it metal assembled by gears. Its outer surface is plated in thin, overlapping scales of pale grey-green calcite and rain-washed shale—arranged like roof-shingles down its long back, its sloped shoulders, and its narrow forearms, beaded with clear condensation. Between the plates, where a joint bends, you can see tight bindings of damp, dark flax-cord and soft seams of wet bryophyte moss that keep the hinges slick and silent. Its hands are long, slender, and four-fingered, pale as lime-plaster and stained at the tips with verdigris from years of handling copper sluices and gutters.

Where a face would be, its narrow, slightly hooded head is smooth and blind, shaped like a river-worn stone or an upturned clay vessel, with neither mouth nor nostrils. Across the upper brow runs a single deep, recessed shelf—a narrow basin of dark, mirror-still water, rimmed with tiny needles of shade-ice. The water does not sit level with the horizon. It tilts sharply inside the basin, brimming to the northern edge, sunk low on the southern, utterly unspilling.

When it becomes aware of you standing on the bridge, it does not startle. It turns its tall, canted frame smoothly toward you, with no sound louder than dry sedge brushing against slate. As it turns, the dark water inside its brow-seam glides and settles, catching your reflection—your boots, your dark coat, the white sky behind your head—though for a quiet, unsettling moment the cloud-bank reflected in its face is the one that passed overhead twenty minutes ago.

You glance down at the soft black loam where it stands. The wet earth is unbroken. Even the delicate frost-needles rimming the sedge beneath its narrow heels have not snapped. Its weight does not press cleanly downward into the plateau; like the water in the octagonal cisterns, its down leans elsewhere, resting lightly against the bevel of the bridge-wall and the thin, cold air.

And if it lifts a forearm to adjust a mossy culvert-stone, and the high white-gold sun catches the pale, translucent horn-like plates along the inside of its wrist, you see what keeps it alive.

Inside its hollow, cold-walled body, clear mineral water glides steadily *upward*.

There is no pump, no beating chamber, no frantic pulse arguing against the cold. Just as the snowmelt runs endlessly through the orchard aqueduct without source or sea, gaining height around every quiet bend, each resident carries a closed loop of cold water circulating smoothly through its limbs and torso—drawing dissolved lime, dark peat-tannins, and fine slate-silt up from its heels, around its shoulders, across its brow-basin, and down again in an unhurried, unbroken siphon. They do not eat, because nothing inside them burns. They do not breathe, because nothing inside them suffocates. They run on the same worn-thin patience that holds a pillarless roof-truss suspended eighteen inches above its rotted walls.

Once you know how to recognize that quiet, canted poise, you begin to see them everywhere across the sixty miles of rolling bedrock—never crowded, always spaced at the city's own vast, unhurried scale.

One crouches motionless beside a sunken cistern in a distant quarter, testing the angle of the water with a single pale finger. Two more walk down a straight cobbled avenue half a league away, using the stretched geometry of the street the way a boatman uses a current: one steps off the left curb onto a flagstone six feet ahead, and by the time its trailing heel lifts, it is already forty yards down the lane, having crossed a quiet fold in the paving that left its stride smooth and unhurried. You pass three of them gathered beneath a covered timber arcade, each leaning at a slightly different angle—one straight upright, one tilted ten degrees east, one canted sharply toward the hills—matching the private tilts of the districts they walked in from.

They do not speak. The thin air carries voices poorly, and they have no lungs to waste on noise.

When two of them meet on a terrace, they pause a yard apart and incline their narrow heads. Inside their brow-seams, the dark water shifts, mirroring each other's slant—an exchange of bearings, passing word of which way gravity leans in the lower valley, or whether the afternoon walls have begun to rise in the eastern plazas. For longer matters, they use the stone. One presses a pale fingertip against a wet basalt coping, then taps it twice with a small bronze peg hung from its belt, sending a slow, sharp vibration into the grain of the rock. Because the stone remembers blows slowly, the knock may not travel the length of the bench for an hour. Neither minds. The first wanders off to lift a sluice-gate; the second remains, or returns at mid-afternoon, lays an open palm flat against the cold slate, and feels the answer finally surface through the stone.

Across wider distances—province to province, across miles of spongy fern-bog—they speak through the white snow-gentians.

Because a single gentian blooms simultaneously in twelve scattered courtyards, swaying in exact unison whenever any one stem is touched, the residents tend them with grave care. Watch a resident kneel beside a flowering stalk in a sheltered hollow. With a copper-stained finger, it nudges the pale stem twice to the west, pauses, presses it gently downward, and lets go. For three minutes the flower stands utterly still in the windless hollow. Then, without a breeze stirring the surrounding ferns, the white bloom bobs sharply north, pauses, and leans south—an answer sent from a rain-drenched basin thirty miles away, where another cold hand rests beside the same plant.

Their days are given over entirely to tending the loose stitching of the plateau.

They do not mourn the brokenness of distance, sequence, and weather; they cultivate it with the same manual, patient craft that dressed the city's lintels. Wherever the delayed gestures of the absent builders still arrive, the residents are waiting with fresh material. They carry rough blocks of slate up to the terraces where centuries-old chisel-blows still fall, wedging and turning the stone between each five-minute *tink* until a new drainage trough or stair-tread is finished by hands that vanished before the mountain peaks were planed flat. In a long, roofless hall where a weaver's phantom shuttle still passes back and forth through empty air every damp morning, they string fresh flax warp across a timber frame at dawn and return at noon to cut down a yard of tight, frost-proof cloth.

They shepherd the shade-mosses like cold, silent flocks. Carrying lightweight folding screens of split larch and hammered copper, two or three residents will spend a bright morning walking slowly across a broad plaza, casting narrow, deliberate wedges of artificial shadow over bare stone. The emerald bryophytes pour eagerly into the man-made shade, unrolling across dry mortar joints that need sealing or climbing cleanly up the outer walls of a leaking granary. When the moss grows too thick and wet along a shingled roof, a resident climbs onto the ridge with a polished sheet of beaten copper and tilts a pale square of reflected sunlight across the eaves; the moss dries, thins, and vanishes cleanly back into clean dry slate before the damp can rot the rafters beneath.

They fence the weather as casually as farmers fence sheep. Because squalls and icy fog snag cleanly on low boundary walls, you will find residents kneeling along the ridges, stacking dry fieldstone knee-high across the mouth of a shallow basin to dam a front of sleet and turn it aside from a ripening orchard—or opening a two-foot gap in an old garden wall so a narrow tongue of drizzle can slip through and fill a single cistern while the courtyard around it stays bone-dry in clear sunshine.

Along the bases of those boundary walls, where dampness oozes horizontally through the mortar and freezes into delicate icicles pointing straight out into dry air, they move with braided reed baskets and copper shears. They clip the horizontal icicles cleanly at the root. Because those needles froze across the seam between two climates, they do not melt into ordinary water; when a resident sets one inside a clay dish in a dry cellar, it thaws into a cool, dense puff of hillside fog and a spoonful of pure, restless snowmelt—which they use to dampen their lime-paste, cool their joints, or top up their own internal loops if a dry wind has thinned them.

Even the smaller anomalies of the plateau fall under their quiet care. Where a closed-loop aqueduct springs a hairline leak, a stray ring of snowmelt will sometimes break free—a clear, cold hoop of water no thicker than a willow twig, spinning upright an inch above the moss like a silent wheel, gathering stray lichen-crumbs and drifting aimlessly down the avenues. If left alone, it will wander into a dry wind and vanish. Whenever a resident encounters one of these stray loops hovering near a copper gutter, it bends slowly, coaxes the spinning ring into a shallow wooden ladle, and pours it gently back into the nearest cistern, where the water settles with a soft *plip* and joins the slant.

Once in a great while, they gather.

They choose the wide, circular basins where the old avenues converge and the wind blows steadily inward from every point of the compass at once—piling shed willow-leaves and pale lichen into waist-high cones that shiver endlessly around the central stone wellheads. Inside that heavy, inward-pressing draft, the worn fabric of the plateau holds firmer than anywhere else. Distances agree for a few yards. Shadows stay tethered to their stones.

There, standing ringed around the shivering cone of leaves, twenty or thirty of them may remain motionless for a whole grey afternoon, their shale plates clicking softly in the converging draft, their brow-water leveling slightly as they share the pressure of the knot. It is here, too, that they begin a new one among them.

When an outlying district has gone untended for a century, or when an orchard loop overflows with excess meltwater in a bright spring, two residents will choose a sheltered roof-truss that hangs suspended in mid-air above rotted pillars—a place that has already forgotten the rule of weight. Beneath it, over the course of a winter, they coil a clear thread of snowmelt inside hollow frost-rhubarb stems bound tightly with braided flax, tilting it patiently at twenty degrees until the water remembers how to climb its own bend without falling. Once the loop runs silently on its own, they dress it slowly in wet lime-plaster, thin splinters of hand-split shale, and pale root-threads dipped in peat-water. They carry the unfinished vessel to a terrace where delayed mallet-blows still fall, letting the old craft pare its shoulders into balance, and then bring it down to the circular wind-basin to harden in the steady inward air.

It takes seven winters—or one afternoon along a stretched right-hand curb—before the dark water inside its brow-seam tilts on its own, lifts its weight off the flagstones by the thickness of a hair, and turns toward the nearest leaking sluice.

They do not die in any way an animal would recognize. Instead, after centuries of walking the pale, cold leagues, a resident grows too patient. Its steps slow from miles in a day to yards in a week, matching the pace of the sliding market-squares. Pale lime-sinter thickens across its shale plates; dark-fronded ferns and bruised-purple peat-moss root deeply in the seams of its shoulders. Its lean grows steeper until it agrees completely with the ancient bedrock underneath.

When the time comes, it does not collapse. It walks slowly to a crossroads, or to the lip of a terraced plaza, or directly beneath the sagging corner of a floating timber roof, and settles its heels firmly into the loam for the very first time. Its internal loop slows, extends pale mineral threads downward through the flagstones, and links cleanly into a mossy culvert or an underground spring. Its brow-water freezes clear and stays tilted at twenty degrees.

Walk past the smooth, canted boundary-stones and rounded watch-cairns scattered along the moss-plains—the ones so ancient their corners have melted into blind grey pillows—and press your ear against the cold rock when the wind drops. Deep inside the stone, faint and steady and utterly unhurried, you can still hear the small, wet *shhh* of a closed loop of snowmelt turning in the dark, climbing and falling without going anywhere.

If you stay long enough on the plateau, you will eventually feel them watching you—not with hostility, nor with welcome, but with the careful, distant curiosity one might show toward a stray coal carried onto a moor of dry sedge.

To them, a breathing creature is a startling, almost violent thing. You steam visibly into the thin air. You carry a hot, wet hammer thumping seventy times a minute inside your ribs. Every step you take drives a deep, ragged print straight down into the mud, insisting with brute weight on a single vertical direction, dragging a loud, frantic, indivisible *now* across courtyards that prefer to drift between noon and ten centuries ago.

Watch closely, though, as the pale white-gold sun drops toward the endless rolling horizon and the thin air goes still.

You stand on the upper terrace, watching a band of silver cloud settle over the copper-roofed halls ten miles—or two hundred paces—below. Across the flagstones at your feet, the lush emerald shadow-moss lengthens rapidly, racing the dusk. And there, along the far parapet of the plaza, five tall, narrow silhouettes stand quietly against the darkening sky, each canted at its own small, impossible angle. One holds a copper screen tilted to guide the last patch of moss neatly into a cracked gutter. Another rests a pale palm against a basalt lintel, waiting patiently for an answer tapped three hours ago. None of them hurries. None of them breathes. In the clear, unforgiving twilight, they lean smoothly into the loose seams of the world, keeping the long, quiet unravelling in good repair.
