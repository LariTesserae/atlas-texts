If you stay motionless on the tilted iron walkway through one full turn of the tide, you begin to notice small contradictions in the crowding.

Nothing in the pocket is supposed to stay bare. Every horizontal lip takes its apron of cold orange ochre; every vertical rib gathers chalky gypsum, mineral glaze, or tiled scales of calcite. Yet when the water sinks away from the machinery, a few narrow surfaces gleam cleaner than the rest. Along the underside of the walkway’s corroded iron knees, the rust has been rubbed down to a dull, dark pewter polish, slick and free of slime. The bronze hinge of the intake flap deep in the sump bears no collar of shell-crust, though the casing an inch to either side is thick with it. Up on the flowstone boss, three of the white porcelain insulators hang draped in their green curtains of wire-predators, while the fourth—the lowest one, whose drip-water runs just as rich in copper—is scraped clean to the glaze, as bare as a scrubbed teacup.

And down in the deepest trough, hunched tight against the warm bronze ridge of the drowned armature casing, there is a shelf of knobby, ochre-streaked travertine that does not quite match the grain of the rock behind it.

At high water it is indistinguishable from the overhang. Loaf-shaped siphons press their soft, pleated flanks right against its lower rim; a stray veil of gelatinous film blurs the seam where it meets the bronze. Even when the impeller catches its stroke with that dull, underfoot thud and the meniscus begins its seven-minute retreat, the shelf does not move at first. Water sheets off its ridged, gray-white slope just as it sheets off the standpipes, uncovering small clusters of tiled discs cemented fast along its upper curve.

Then the water clears the bronze rim, and the slope takes a breath.

It is not a sudden motion. The seam along its underside loosens by half an inch, and a pale, narrow edge of skin—colorless as wet tallow, slick as peeled willow—slides forward over the warm metal. Four fine, vertical slits along the curve of what had looked like a fluted mineral flank draw shut in sequence with a sound barely louder than a wet eyelid closing. As they seal, the whole mass uncurls slowly from the casing, separating itself from the rock in long, deliberate folds until it is clear that the travertine ridge is a back, and the back belongs to something living.

It is roughly the length of a lean person, though built to no human proportion. Everything about it is compressed for the close quarters of the throat—narrow across the ribs and pelvis, flattened through the chest, with long, gaunt forelimbs that end in wide, deeply splayed hands and shorter, heavier hind legs whose hooked, blunt-nailed feet lock naturally around riveted flanges and the square edges of iron beams. Its back, shoulders, and the broad crown of its head are shingled in thick, matte plates of smoky calcite streaked with manganese black and rust—not a brittle shell like a crab’s, but a dense, living hide that has spent decades drinking limestone drip and precipitating mineral straight into its own grain. Tiled discs live permanently along the knobby ridge of its shoulder blades; as it rises into the air, a faint, dry whisper ripples down its own spine as they lift their rims to sip the retreating film.

Its underside, throat, and the inner faces of its long arms are entirely naked. Near the wrists and along the hollow of the ribs, the pale skin turns thin enough to show the slow, indigo-teal pulse of copper-tinted blood. Behind it drags a flat, muscular tail, broad as a leather strap and nearly half its length, whose pale underside is pebbled with dark, iron-rich scutes.

It has no true eyes. Where sockets should sit beneath its flat, river-stone brow, there are only two sunken, milky lenses sealed beneath smooth mineral film—dim windows good for nothing more than telling when the three fissures overhead fade from pewter daylight to night. Its real face is written in the delicate, branching grooves that score its cheeks, its jaw, and the pale pads of its fingers. Each groove holds a bead of clear, quivering jelly. As it lifts its head into the damp air, those seams widen faintly, drinking in the hidden geometry of the sink.

To anything with eyes, the pocket is dim slate-gray and tangled with wet scrap. To this dweller, the pocket is bright with shape and pitch. The corroded terminals and drooping copper jumpers hang above the sump like warm, humming lanterns; the iron standpipes carry a low, steady bass note down from the rock above; the drowned armature beneath its belly glows with the broad, comforting weight of a banked hearth. Every hairline leak in a flange, every mineral crust creeping too close to a bare busbar, alters that invisible weather—brightening a corner into an irritable glare, or dampening a familiar chord into something flat and sour.

It does not hurry as the water drains. It knows the nineteen-minute breath of the sink down to the beat of its own slow heart.

Stepping sideways along the lowered ledge with three points of contact always kept—never splashing, never bruising its bare belly against the stone teeth—it moves first to the intake grille beneath the bronze housing. The blind walkers scatter only a hand’s breadth from its knuckles before resuming their sweeping; a flat ribbon-skate slides unhurriedly over its submerged ankle without flinching. It reaches between the rusted bars with one impossibly slender arm, feeling by touch and hum along the hinge of the bronze flap.

Over the last several days, a collar of young tiled discs has crept across the iron pin. Left alone, another month of their quiet caulking would weld the flap half-shut and choke the tide. The dweller hooks a thick, horny thumbnail under the brittle edge of the largest disc and pops it cleanly free. There is a tiny, wet *tck*. It lifts the disc to its wide, tucked mouth—toothless in front, lined further back with heavy, dark crushing plates—and cracks the calcite rim cleanly in half, working out the soft, copper-sweet flesh inside and spitting the glassy crescent into the sump. Three more follow, until the hinge-pin runs smooth under its palm.

Then it turns its attention to the loaf-shaped siphons wedged in the grille. It touches them one by one with the pitted pads of its fingers, feeling the sluggish pulse of water through their pleated sides. The small, firm ones it leaves untouched. The largest, a pale sack swollen nearly to the size of a bucket and beginning to sag across two intake slots, it grips near the calcareous foot and rocks patiently from side to side until the holdfast tears loose with a muffled pop. Holding the heavy body against its bare ribs, it slits the translucent wall with one blunt claw and drinks slowly from the knotted, dark core—thick, brackish, iron-heavy mash that smells sharply of wet pennies and cold mud. When it drops the empty sac back into the trough, a dozen blind walkers converge on the torn edges almost before it settles.

By the time it rises toward the middle of the pocket, the drawdown has reached its bottom. The two minutes of stillness begin. The air sharpens with ozone; the wet shelves click softly with feeding grazers.

This is when it climbs.

Grip by quiet grip, it hauls itself up the vertical flank of a cast-iron standpipe and hooks its hind feet onto the underside of the tilted iron walkway, hanging back-down along that rubbed, dark pewter track it has worn clean over half a lifetime. Hanging there, it is barely a foot above the hanging stalactites, its calcified back brushing nothing, its long arms swinging effortlessly across the narrow gap to the rack of white porcelain insulators bolted into the flowstone boss.

A green wire-predator hangs from the second insulator, lengthened out to hunt. Its sticky, copper-dusted tip brushes the dweller’s naked forearm and immediately pulls tight, trying to haul its catch upward. The dweller does not jerk away. Its skin is old and thick with ochre; the faint metallic sting barely registers. Calmly, it pinches the taut green ribbon between two broad knuckles, peels it free, and sets it gently aside onto a neighboring rib of stone, where the predator coils back in confusion.

What it has come for is the fourth insulator—the clean one—and the braided copper jumper drooping from its brass lug.

Over the last few cycles, a wet, chalky bridge of gypsum and orange film has crept across the lower lip of the porcelain, nearly linking the copper strap to the wet iron bolt beneath it. To the dweller’s electroreceptive grooves, that creeping bridge feels like grit under an eyelid, a sharp, stuttering sourness bleeding stray current straight into the wall where it does not belong.

It does not touch the bare copper directly; that close, the hum is too bright, a blinding prickle against its sensitive palms. Instead, it reaches behind the flowstone boss into a narrow dry cleft and draws out a palm-sized shard of snapped white porcelain—broken from some long-fallen fifth insulator decades ago, its broken edge worn smooth as soap from years of handling. Gripping the porcelain shard, it lets its long, pebbled tail drop down until the tip brushes the wet flange of the standpipe below, completing a faint, quiet circuit through its own spine.

Now it can feel the fault cleanly.

With slow, economical strokes of the porcelain edge, it scrapes the wet gypsum away from the lip of the insulator. Shavings of chalky crust fall in silent pale flakes into the sump below. Each stroke dims the sour tickle in its jaw until, with one last pass around the brass lug, the current snaps back to its smooth, familiar hum. A single pinhead spark—blue-white, cold, no brighter than a needle-tip—winks across the cleaned lip as the jumper swings free by a millimeter. The dweller pauses, holding its flat brow an inch from the porcelain to feel the fine ozone prickle settle evenly across its cheeks, then tucks the white shard neatly back into its cleft behind the boss.

Only once in a great while does it pause to tend itself. If the calcite plates along its own shoulders have grown too thick across a joint, stiffening its reach, it wedges its back against the sheared, jagged edge of the iron walkway where the catwalk breaks off over black water, and rocks slowly against the metal until the excess crust flakes away in damp gray slabs. Beneath the shed mineral, the fresh skin is pale and tender, weeping a thin, clear mucus that smells faintly of sulfur and begins to harden almost immediately in the mineral air. Up in a dryish hollow beneath the highest iron knee—above the reach of any normal tide—five or six older, dish-sized dorsal plates lie stacked unevenly in the gloom, shed years apart, each one now brimming with clear drip-water like a small stone basin.

Nothing in the pocket remembers when it was not here. Yet its own body carries the quiet proof that it did not begin this large. No crack in the ceiling overhang could admit its calcified shoulders, and the floor fissures through which the sump drains are scarcely wider than a wrist. Somewhere far back in the slow decades after the iron was first bolted to the rock, something pale, thin, and soft-boned must have drifted up through the drowned limestone arteries beneath the hills—drawn out of the dark karst water by a warmth no cave stone ever produces, and by a faint, singing current that tasted unlike anything in the deep dark. It found the bronze casing, stayed for the harvest of iron-fed life, and grew slowly into the tight geometry of the throat until the cracks it entered by could no longer hold it.

If it ever feels the narrowness of its home, there is no sign of it. The pocket fits it the way a workshop fits an old artisan who has forgotten the noise of the road outside.

Its life does not run only on the nineteen-minute tide. There are larger seasons it feels through the stone. When night seals the three ceiling fissures and even the pewter dimness drains away, the pocket becomes absolute black, and the dweller sometimes lingers high on the iron walkway long after its work is done, hanging motionless near the bare copper straps during the two-minute stillness just to let the tiny cold blue sparks prickle against the cloudy lenses behind its brow—the closest thing to stars it has ever seen. And in late spring, when heavy storms pound the unseen ground overhead, cold rainwater floods down the four riveted standpipes with a sudden, swollen force. The drowned impeller turns faster then; the bronze housing grows fever-warm; the stray current flares bright and wild along every wet rib of travertine, singing high and unfamiliar in the dweller’s teeth. On those nights it grows restless, pacing its slow upside-down circuits along the iron knees, scraping nothing, eating nothing, simply listening with its whole body to the distant weather of the upper world translated into iron and voltage.

Far down in the throat of the largest standpipe, the bronze flap seats itself with a soft, muffled *clunk*.

Instantly the dweller leaves the porcelain rack. It slides down the riveted iron column headfirst, its pale belly flat against the cold wet metal, its limbs folding smoothly inward so it clears the hanging stone curtains by half an inch. Below it, the glassy water is already climbing the ribs millimeter by millimeter. As the rising meniscus touches the bare copper jumpers, the last pinhead blue sparks wink and vanish beneath the dark sheen.

The dweller reaches the submerged ledge just as the water swells over the warm curve of the bronze casing. It does not wait for the surface to cover it; it lowers itself directly into the rising dark, letting the cool, iron-heavy water slide across its throat. Along the sides of its neck and ribs, the four sealed slits part gently, unfurling pale, feathery fringes flushed faint copper-blue to drink from the moving current.

It settles its bare belly once more along the broad, idling warmth of the bronze housing, tucking its long arms tight beneath its chest and curling its flat, pebbled tail around the iron base of the intake grille. Loaf-siphons shift lazily against its flanks and resume their silent pumping. Along its arched, travertine-gray back, the tiled discs clamp tightly shut against the mineral plates, turning back into pitted stone crust.

Within minutes, the basin is full. Nothing moves above the black water except the endless, muffled patter of drips falling from pale stone teeth. Down in the deep trough, invisible beneath the dark surface, the narrow keeper lies pressed against the warm bronze heart of the sink, feeling the drowned wheel turn one slow notch further, listening to the quiet, steady hum that keeps them both alive.
