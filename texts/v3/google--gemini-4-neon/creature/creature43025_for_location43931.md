Even if you know the shelters are out there, you can walk past a band of the people who built them and mistake them for heat-shimmer on a low ridge.

They do not stand tall against the sky the way the drummers do. Out on the open flats, they move in loose, staggered files, hunched slightly into the wind, draped from crown to ankle in pale, mottled hides and oiled wing-membranes bleached the same ash-ochre as the dead rind. From half a mile away they look like nothing more than drifting scraps of peeled bark or a cluster of wind-planed hummocks. Only when the file pauses all at once—dropping cleanly to one knee as a nearby drummer’s throat-gourd swells and releases its shuddering blast—do you realize the shapes are alive, and moving with a discipline older than memory.

Up close, the first thing you notice is that they are armored not against blades, but against the air itself.

Every face is half-hidden behind a narrow strip of carved bone or fire-hardened rind-wood, pierced with thin horizontal slits to cut the blinding glare off the pale floor. Beneath the goggles, their skin is dark as cured sap and faintly glossy; before stepping out into the daylight, they rub their wrists, ankles, and chins with a bitter paste of rendered pith-eel tallow and wood-ash. Without it, a traveler who sits still for ten minutes to mend a strap or watch a fissure will find wafer-thin surface-grazers crawling quietly onto their ankles, mistaking dry human skin for the rind and beginning to file it away. Even with the grease, people out on the plains carry a flat sliver of bone tucked into their belts and scrape their shins and boot-seams with the absentminded habit of someone brushing off burrs.

Most striking of all are their heads. Wrapped tight around the temples and jaw of every adult and child is a thick, heavy band of braided plant-wool and felted pith, packed solidly over the ears and sealed at the edges with dark, resinous wax. To step onto the plateau without your ears bound is a punishment—or a madness. An hour of the unprotected noon roar leaves a person dizzy, retching, and weeping from the nose; a season of it breaks the inner ear forever. So they walk sealed inside their own skulls, hearing the continent only as a muffled, low-grade pressure and a steady vibration climbing through their soles.

Their boots are built to dull that climb as well: thick, springy slabs of layered pith-felt stitched inside tough drummer-hide, with no hard heels. They do not stride so much as glide, rolling their weight softly from outer edge to toe so they do not wake the resonant chambers underfoot or send a telltale thump down to the ambushers folded motionless in the dry washes. When they cross a ridge where drummers have staked their territories, they move only inside the swell of the drummers’ own brassy blasts, letting the giants’ noise drown out their footsteps, and freezing into crouched, mottled humps whenever the trough of silence drops between waves.

You will never hear them shout to one another on the plains. A voice thrown into that wall of rasping, rattling, booming air dies before it clears the speaker’s chin.

Instead, they carry on whole conversations in complete visual sharpness. Out in the glare, they use a clipped, angular sign language held close against the dark-dyed leather bibs they wear over their chests, so their fingers stand out clearly against the pale horizon. Up close, they speak by touch—quick taps, squeezes, and sliding pressures on a companion’s shoulder, forearm, or ribs, a grammar so ingrained that two people hauling a water-cistern together can argue, joke, and adjust their route without looking at one another once.

And when something urgent or complicated must be said in the middle of the roaring flats—when a scout returns from a split fissure, or two bands meet unexpectedly on an open basin—each person draws a slender, foot-long rod of dense, fire-hardened heart-fiber from their sash. The ends are capped with smooth sleeves of beaten bronze. The two speakers stand face to face, close enough that their wide glare-brims nearly touch, and bite down lightly on opposite ends of the same rod.

Through the wood, straight into the teeth and jawbone, bypassing the bound ears and the deafening air entirely, their voices travel clear and dry as dry crickets. To an outsider watching from ten paces off, it looks like a strange, solemn duel—two muffled figures leaning together over a short wooden wand, lips barely moving, eyes locked behind slitted bone—while a rapid, buzzy murmur passes from skull to skull. *The eastern flue has choked. Three drummers moved onto the salt-wash. Our cisterns are half-dry; how far to the next knitter?*

The same rod serves a second use. Every hour or two, the lead walker drops to one knee, presses the bronze tip of their rod firmly against the sun-cured crust, and bites the upper end. Eyes shut, they listen—not to the sky, not to the grazers, but to the body of the world beneath their boots. Through their teeth they can read the hollowness of an ancient air-bladder, the faint, rhythmic tearing of siphon-worms chewing an artery ten feet down, the deep hydraulic throb of sap rising toward a stressed seam. A seasoned reader can tell from five minutes on a rod whether a basin is safe to sleep beside, or whether a ridge is building pressure and will split open with a wet crack before dawn.

***

They travel in small groups—rarely more than ten or fifteen people to a band, usually a tangled knot of kin, sworn partners, and a few travelers who drifted over from another group during the last burn. Larger crowds are clumsy here. They drink a dew-basin dry in a morning, wear down a sap-ridge too fast, and—worst of all—cannot fit inside a quiet shelter. A large cavity dug into the rind behaves like a drum, catching the deep frequencies of the plateau and humming until nobody inside can sleep. Only small, low, tightly packed blisters stay genuinely quiet.

It is only when you crawl behind them into one of those buried hollows, as the late-afternoon thunderheads stack like pale anvils along the horizon, that you meet them as they really are.

The crawl-tunnel bends twice in the dark, lined with shaggy mats of braided plant-wool that drink the roar away inch by inch. The last person pulls the weighted hide flap tight and pegs it down.

And then the band exhales—all at once, a single shared slump of shoulders in the dim amber glow of a tallow-cup.

Out come the bone goggles. Slowly, carefully, they unwind the heavy, wax-stiffened ear-bands, coiling them neatly and tucking them into woven pouches so the mites cannot nest in the wool. Someone rubs their reddened temples; a child yawns wide, popping their ears with a tiny, audible click. The contrast is startling. Outside, they were hunched, armored, sharp-gestured, almost insect-like in their vigilance. Inside, pressed hip to hip in a curved chamber scarcely wider than a wagon-bed and too low to stand upright, they become startlingly gentle.

Their spoken tongue—the language they keep strictly for the hollows—has no shouting register at all. It is a language built for dark, narrow spaces and bruised ears: low, unhurried, full of soft breathy vowels, purred consonants, and murmured hums that carry three feet and no farther. Raising your voice inside a baffled shelter is the gravest rudeness a person can commit; children who shriek in play are hushed instantly, not in anger, but with a wince of genuine pain, as though they had torn the felt flap open and let the noon roar rush in.

In the quiet dark, the work of living resumes. There is almost no idle sitting. One woman holds a cracked clay jar between her knees, threading fine, warm-annealed bronze wire through tiny drilled holes to stitch the seam tight before sealing it with boiled sap-pitch. Beside her, an older man works a pair of copper tweezers over a fresh strip of drummer-hide, plucking out microscopic splinters of hardened plant-fiber. Near the back of the hollow, where the curved wall stays coolest, a baby sleeps inside a thick, double-walled sling of soft pith-batting—their first home before they are old enough for ear-bands, wrapped tight against a parent’s ribs so the first steady rhythm they ever know is not the booming of the plains, but a human heartbeat and a low humming voice vibrating straight through bone.

They eat simply, and mostly cold inside the shelters, saving their raised rib-bone hearths for the calm, cool hour just before dawn or the brief stillness after dusk. Their staple is *pith-cake*—pale, chewy slabs of dense inner tissue cut cleanly from the living floor with copper flensing-spades, leached in two changes of dew-water to draw out the sharpest tannins, then pounded flat with dried seeds and sun-cured into tough, sweetish bread that keeps for months. Alongside it they eat strips of smoked pith-eel, crunchy roasted sky-flock thoraxes, and thick, dark syrups boiled down from morning sap-taps. Sometimes, in a stoppered gourd tucked deep in the baggage, a band keeps a small batch of fermented sap-wine—pale green, sharp on the tongue, slightly sparkling, passed from hand to hand in the dim shelter after a long crossing.

Because there is no stone and no soil on the plateau, outsiders often wonder where their copper blades, their bronze pins, and their clay cisterns come from. The answer lies in the patient, dangerous craft of the *flue-miners*.

A continent of living plant still has roots, miles below the dry skin, gripping whatever ancient mineral bed underlies this high world. Over centuries, the plateau draws trace metals, salts, and fine silicates upward through its vast vascular lattice, carrying more than its tissues can use. What it cannot digest, it walls off. Deep inside dead, collapsed breathing-chimneys, or buried in the walls of ancient, abandoned sap-bladders, the plant secretes hard, bitter nodules—green-veined knots of nearly pure native copper, pale tin-rich crusts, and dense, iron-stained mineral galls the size of a fist. In the bottoms of the oldest stagnant fissures, fine washed mineral silt settles into pockets of true, workable clay.

Finding a choked, dead chimney is a fortune. A band that stumbles on one will camp beside it for half a season, taking turns descending into the stifling, dark flue on sinew ropes, chipping the heavy metallic galls loose with antler wedges while keeping constant watch for the venomous metallic-shelled wasps that love the same dark throats. They hammer the softest native copper cold on small, prized anvils—dense chunks of iron-rich gall handed down for five generations, carried wrapped in oiled leather on the strongest walker’s back. When they need bronze, they build a tall, narrow clay furnace perched high on a cradle of lashed drummer-ribs, packed underneath with a foot of white calcined ash so not a breath of furnace-heat reaches the living floor. To drive the coals white-hot, they use a pair of cured, pleated drummer throat-gourds fitted with bone nozzles—pumping the dead giant’s own bellows to melt the plateau’s hidden stones.

Every gram of metal is treasured, weighed in the palm, re-forged a dozen times over a century. A broken copper blade is never discarded; it is hammered flat into awls, fish-hooks for fissure-eels, stitching wire, or—most sacred of all—the thin, resonant plates hung from the way-posts.

***

The way-posts are the backbone of their scattered society. No band truly wanders at random; they move along a vast, invisible web of known ridges, reliable dew-bowls, and mineral-bearing chimneys, stitched together by those solitary singing stakes.

A way-post is never just a marker of direction. It is a letter left in the wind.

Every hammered copper plate is tuned—beaten thin or left slightly thick, notched along its lower edge—to ring at a specific pitch that carries clear above the organic chaos of the plains. A high, clean ping marks a firm ridge and safe travel; a flat, shivering double-tone warns of a splitting basin, bad sap, or a colony of steam-venting ambushers lurking in the dry washes. Beside the plate hangs the notched horn, and knotted beneath it are thin strips of dyed membrane and small carved bone tallies.

When a band reaches a post, they do not merely glance at it. One traveler unties a strip, reads the notches with their thumb, and presses their listening-rod to the stake itself to hear whether the post is still seated in sound, healthy rind. Before they move on, they leave their own mark: a knot of red-stained sinew to say *six of us passed three dawns ago, heading north toward the smoke*; a smear of white ash on the copper to warn that the dew-cistern two miles west has cracked; a single curled shaving of fresh bronze tucked into the horn to say *we have metal to trade at the next burn*.

Because they have no towns, they have no chiefs, no fixed borders, and very little taste for war. On a world this fiercely loud and mercilessly dry, another human band is rarely a rival; it is a miracle of quiet company, a source of fresh news, a chance to swap a worn flensing-spade for three gourds of good tallow or a daughter’s marriage-gift. Still, they have their fierce rules—unwritten laws everyone obeys because breaking them ruins the ground for whoever comes after.

Never build a fire directly on the skin. Never leave a sap-tap open when you break camp; always drive a clean peg of cured rind-wood into the hole so the plateau can callus over without bleeding dry or calling the pith-eels to the surface. Never kill a drummer near a band’s known hollow, for its mate will pace the flats for weeks, booming off-rhythm and driving the whole ridge mad. And never, under any circumstance, silence another band’s way-post—to pull down a copper plate or muffle its ring is treated as murder, the one crime for which a person is cast out of the hollows and left to walk the roaring plains alone.

***

For most of the year, life is an austere, patient geometry of small bands moving from basin to basin, timing their days to the brief pre-dawn dew and the cool hours of dusk.

Then the dry lightning strikes.

You smell a rind-fire from thirty miles away—not the acrid choke of burning forest, but a vast, hot, astonishingly sweet perfume, like toasted grain and boiling sugar carried high on the afternoon thermals. A day later, the sky-flocks begin peeling off their distant pillars, sliding sideways across the blue in great rattling rivers toward the horizon.

Every band within fifty miles breaks camp and turns toward the smell.

Walking onto a fresh burn-plain is like stepping onto another planet. The thin sheet of orange flame has peeled the dead, pale-ochre crust cleanly off twenty square miles of plateau, leaving a steaming, blackened expanse of raw, scorched green pith weeping sticky golden rivulets of caramelized sap. More staggering than the sight—more staggering even than the feast of stunned burrowers and roasted surface-life—is the *sound*.

The wafer-thin grazers are gone. Burned to pale ash in a single afternoon.

For the first time in months, the relentless, papery hiss at the top of the air vanishes. The drummers still boom along the unscorched rim, and the sky-flocks roar overhead in greedy funnels, but across the raw scar itself the acoustic wall has been torn open. You can almost hear a person speak at arm’s length without a rod.

These are the *scorch-gatherings*—the only cities the plateau ever knows.

Along the cool, unburned ledges overlooking the steaming plain, six, ten, sometimes twenty bands converge over the course of a week. They stitch their hide tarps and oiled wing-membranes together over ribbed frames of fire-hardened poles, building temporary, sprawling baffled pavilions packed with bundled plant-wool—places large enough for forty people to sit shoulder to shoulder in the muted dimness, out of the glare and the frantic clatter of the feeding flocks.

For three weeks, while the plateau slowly sweats and seals its wound, the bands live in a riot of abundance. Hunters wade out onto the sticky, cooling plain with long-handled bronze hooks and wide woven nets, harvesting fat, cooked pith-eels by the basketful and sweeping down sky-flocks bloated too heavy with sweet sap to climb cleanly back into the thermals. Great clay cauldrons perched on rib-bone stilts simmer day and night, boiling down the weeping caramelized sap into hard, dark travel-sugar. Forge-hearths glow along the rim, their drummer-gourd bellows wheezing steadily as the finest smiths trade raw copper galls, recast broken spades, and hammer new singing-plates for the way-posts.

Inside the great stitched pavilions at night, the quiet tongue opens into something richer. Marriages are arranged; young people tired of their birth-band’s familiar faces pack their rolls and join another hearth; old debts are settled with coils of bronze wire and jars of bitter tallow. Elders trace vast maps in white ash on stretched hides—not fixed maps, for the plateau is always slowly growing, splitting, and knitting shut, so a ridge that held water ten years ago may now be a dry scar, and a young chimney may have risen where an open flat once lay.

And on the final night before the grazing mites begin creeping back onto the hardening callus, they sing.

It is the only time they ever let their voices fill a space. Sitting in tight concentric rings inside the baffled pavilion, knees touching, ear-bands laid aside, they do not sing loud melodies. Instead, the lowest voices lock into a slow, steady drone—deep and hollow, mimicking the distant organ-pipe groan of a venting chimney—while the higher voices thread pure, clear, wavering notes above it, clean and sharp as hammered copper plates ringing in a hot wind. The ground beneath the felt mats catches the chord and answers with a faint, warm tremor. For an hour, they are not hiding from the noise of the continent; they are answering it in kind, proving to the roaring immensity outside that they have a pitch of their own.

***

They do not worship the plateau in the way people on stone worlds worship distant gods in the sky. How could you pray to something you slice open for breakfast, plug with wooden pegs, and curse when it splits beneath your sleeping-hollow? Yet they never mistake it for dead dirt, either. In their quiet tongue, they have no word for *ground* that does not also mean *hide*, or *flesh*, or *back*. They speak of the continent the way lice might speak of a sleeping whale—if lice were thoughtful, grateful, and keenly aware that the whale’s slightest shiver could shake them into the sun.

Their elders embody that intimacy most clearly.

Even with the thickest waxed wool and the most careful habits, forty or fifty years on the plateau slowly takes the upper reaches of a person’s hearing. First they lose the dry rasp of the grazers. Then the clatter of the high sky-flocks fades into a faint memory, and even the ringing copper way-posts grow thin and far off. Among another people, this might be a slow exile. Here, it is a promotion.

When the surface clutter falls away, the elders turn inward—and downward. They stop wearing thick pith-soled boots inside camp, pressing their callused bare heels and palms directly against the warm rind. They sleep with one cheek laid against a flattened bronze plate pressed to the floor. Free from the distraction of the shrieking air, they become the bands’ finest readers of the deep body. An old woman who can no longer hear a child whisper across the shelter can press her bare foot to an ochre flat and feel, half a mile below, the slow hydraulic swell of a choked root-artery, or the subtle, rhythmic shift of a distant drummer-line changing pitch before a storm breaks loose from the horizon. They call these elders the *deep-listeners*, and no band crosses an unknown basin without waiting for one of them to kneel, lay both palms flat on the hot crust, and nod.

When a person dies—whether an elder worn quiet by sixty summers, or a hunter caught too slow by a sudden steam-venter—the people do not leave them on the open flats to be stripped by the rattling sky-flocks, nor do they waste precious rib-bones and fuel trying to burn them to ash.

Instead, they wrap the body tightly in clean, ungreased plant-wool, tuck a single hammered copper token beneath the tongue, and carry them to a *knitter*—one of the deep, damp growth-fissures that has finished expanding and begun the slow, multi-year work of sealing itself shut.

Down in that cool, shaded cleft, where the exposed pith sweats dark tannin-water and the air smells sharply of crushed green stems, they lay the dead gently into the wet groove, wedging them deep where the shallow burrowers rarely climb. They press no dirt over them, because there is no dirt. They simply climb back out into the blinding daylight, bind their ears tight against the roar, and walk away.

Over the seasons that follow, the living walls of the fissure swell inward. Pale green pith presses gently against the wool, drinking it soft, curling around bone and bronze alike. In five years, the crack is a narrow seam; in twenty, it is a fused, raised scar indistinguishable from the thousand other weathered veins running across the ochre flats. Long after a person’s name has faded from the murmured stories of the hollows, they remain—folded cleanly inside the ancient, warm timber of the world, held beneath the dry crust, carried invisibly on the plateau’s slow, imperceptible breath.

***

Stand on a high ridge at dusk, when the fierce white glare softens to amber and the whole living tableland begins to cool, creaking and sighing beneath your boots. The drummers are starting their long evening chorus, rolling brassy waves across the wide, empty miles. The sky-flocks bank downward out of the thinning thermals, their dry wings rattling like rain that never falls.

Now look closely at the shadow of the wind-planed mound three hundred yards below you.

A dark oval flap of heavy felt lifts an inch, then drops shut again. No smoke rises—they will wait until full dark to kindle the raised rib-bone hearth, when the wind drops and the embers will not draw notice. A hundred yards farther on, a lone figure wrapped in pale ochre hide steps out beside a dew-frame, checking the taut oiled membrane against the coming cool, scraping a handful of tiny grazing mites off their boot with a sliver of bone. For a moment they pause, lifting their slitted face toward the distant valley where a single beaten-copper plate has caught the evening breeze—thin, pure, and unmistakably human against the endless, roaring continent.

Then they turn, drop softly to their knees, and vanish back into the quiet dark beneath the skin of the world.
