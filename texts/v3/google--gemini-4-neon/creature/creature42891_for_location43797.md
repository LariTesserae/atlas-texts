You can walk across the tableland for three days and swear that nothing with hands has ever set foot on it. Even when the mist lifts and gives you five miles of dim, rolling pewter, there is not a wall, a cairn, a ditch, or a trampled path anywhere in sight. If you pass within two hundred yards of the people who live here, you will likely mistake them for a clump of dark sedge hunched downwind of a dying steam-seep, or a knot of waterlogged peat heaved up by some old collapse.

You notice them first by a sound that does not belong to the engines.

Underneath the wet hammering of the pistons and the high whine of straining current, there is sometimes a thin, dry *tick-tick-tick* moving steadily across the flat—too regular for a failing vent, too quiet for an electrical squall. If you stop and strain your eyes into the sideways drizzle, you see four or five low, broad shapes gliding across the heaving turf in single file. Between the middle pair swings a perforated globe of dark metal the size of a bucket, slung from a short rod. Rainwater hisses on its lid, and through its pinholes leaks the only warm color on the plateau: a choked, dull orange glow of smoldering lignite.

They are not tall, and nothing about their silhouette fights the wind. Generations of living on a sponge with no shelter have bred them broad in the chest, heavy-boned, and low to the ground. They never lift their knees high when they walk; they move in a smooth, rolling glide on wide, slatted paddles lashed beneath their boots—cut from the shed horn-hooves of the twelve-foot grazers and laced with grease-cured sinew. Their ankles and knees stay permanently loose, absorbing the sickening swell of the living mat without breaking stride. Behind them, their wide impressions fill immediately with dark, tea-colored water and vanish before the peat finishes rebounding.

Up close, they smell of wet wool, smoked eel, hot mineral oil, and the sharp metallic tang of ozone.

They dress in heavy, overlapping coats stitched from the shaggy, water-repellent belly-hides of dead grazers and cured slabs of rubbery kelp-mat. Every seam is bound tight with braided tendrils of copper-accumulating moss—still alive, kept damp by the endless drizzle. Long, shaggy fringes of the living moss hang past their hems and drag lightly across the wet turf as they move. Whenever a distant thicket of reeds discharges into the low cloud, or a stray current surges through a vibrating ditch underfoot, their coats stir with a faint dry crackle and pale blue sparks bleed harmlessly off their dragging hems into the bog. An outsider's coat keeps out the rain until it soaks through; theirs keeps out the plateau's stray voltage as well.

Their skin is pale and oddly dense, almost waxy, darkened to a permanent rust-brown in the creases of their knuckles and along the rims of their fingernails from a lifetime of handling acidic water and wet iron-slurry. Their hair grows coarse and dark, matted with mineral grease and bound tight under oiled leather caps with thick ear-flaps. They do not shout to one another across the roar of the wind and the engines. Up close, they speak in a low, gravelly murmur pitched deep in the sternum, leaning shoulder-to-shoulder so the vibration carries straight through wet hide and bone.

Across distance, they let the bog speak for them.

When one band needs to hail another out in the fog, a walker drives a hollow, calcified sedge-rod deep into the trembling moss and raps its collar with an iron striker in a brisk, deliberately lopsided rhythm—three beats, a pause, two beats, a drag. Within a second, the palm-sized amphibians hidden under the nearby liverwort lock onto the cadence and throw it outward. Half a square mile of unseen reeds takes up the off-beat *chuff-whir*, rolling the signal across the flat faster than any human voice could travel against the gale. If there is another band within earshot, a different pattern comes pulsing back through the soles of your boots a minute later, answering from out of the mist.

They build nothing because the plateau will not suffer anything built to remain. A wall catches the wind and tears loose; a piling sinks through miles of wet plant-flesh; even a mound of piled peat softens and levels itself within a week.

Instead, they live in the warm lee of slow, steady mineral vents—quiet hissing fissures that lack the violence of the hammering columns and may bleed scalding vapor for three or four days before the pressure fails. There, they unroll broad, turtle-backed shells of oiled grazer-hide stretched over sprung ribs of calcified reed, weighting the edges down with sacks of damp iron grit scraped from old sinkholes. The wind slides cleanly over their rounded backs. Inside, five or six people huddle together on thick pallets of braided, wrung-out sphagnum, drying lignite pellets in mesh pouches against their own ribs, boiling blind bog-eels in shallow iron pans set over their ember-bells, and sleeping in short, light shifts while one person stays awake to listen to the pitch of the vent outside.

They know by the smell of the vapor and the shudder in the floor-mats when a vent has an hour left. Before it stutters and collapses, they roll the hide shells tight, empty the iron grit back onto the moss, sling the ember-bell between two poles, and glide off into the drizzle. Within twenty minutes, the depressed turf has sprung back, the warm sinkhole has swallowed itself, and the flat looks as untouched as the day the mountains finished dissolving beneath it.

If you ask—assuming you can learn the low, bone-carried cadence of their speech—why they never forge an axe, or smelt a kettle, or weave a rope that lasts, they will look at you the way a sailor looks at someone asking why they do not manufacture seawater.

The plateau already makes everything. It simply refuses to let anything stay made.

That is their real craft, and the reason they are here at all. They are not builders or farmers. They are catchers.

You see it if you follow a band when a herd of the tall, pale grazers turns in unison half a mile off, magnetite filaments trembling as they lope into the freezing rain. The people read the herds the way others read weather-vanes. They shadow the grazers at a careful distance, arriving as a broad dome of wet peat begins to swell, hiss, and twist upward into a raw forty-foot column of ribbed root-fiber and iron-crust.

While the cylinder runs at full fury—hammering three strokes a second, vomiting red slurry and scalding gray vapor—the band stays well back, hunkered low in the wet sedge with their copper fringes grounding them to the mat. They do not watch the towering engine itself. They watch the oldest member of the crew, who kneels thirty paces back beside a shallow, dark tea-colored puddle, staring straight down into the water.

Up here, reflections do not keep honest time. In the glassy surface of the puddle, beneath a false, brassy sky that does not exist overhead, the silhouette of the hammering cylinder begins to blur and double two full heartbeats before anything visibly changes in the air.

The kneeling watcher taps the wet moss twice with a horn-shod pole.

Two runners rise and glide forward on their broad paddles, leaning into the hot, acrid blasts of escaping steam. In their gloved hands they carry long, crooked bars of dull, unusually heavy iron. They move to the very rim of the churning turf—close enough that the pounding of the hydraulic pulse rattles the teeth in their skulls—and wait with their heels braced wide.

Then the slip arrives.

The rhythm breaks into a sick, overlapping clatter, an echo arriving ahead of its own blow. The fibrous piston hangs simultaneously at the top and bottom of its throat in a double-exposed shimmer. A hairline seam tears open in the boiling mist, and for two seconds the freezing drizzle lifts straight upward in thin, trembling threads of silver.

Right inside that double-exposure—during the brief hesitation when the cylinder is neither cast iron nor wet sedge, when the two versions of its own wall fail to line up cleanly—there is a loose, shivering edge. The runners drive their hooks straight into that seam and haul with their whole weight.

Half a second later, the pressure ceases to make sense. The forty-foot column slumps sideways without a crash, unraveling mid-fall into dripping ropes of ordinary cold moss, and the bog swallows it whole.

The runners stagger back onto firmer peat, lungs heaving, coats steaming where scalding water splashed their hides, dragging whatever they managed to pull out of the cycle before the plateau remembered it was supposed to dissolve.

This is where everything they own comes from. The dull iron of their hooks and ember-bells is bog-iron ripped loose at the exact instant of a stutter, forever locked out of unraveling back into root-fiber. The tight, springy ribs of their hide-shells are coils of calcified sedge caught mid-compression. Best of all—more precious than metal or fuel—are the thin, rubbery bladders that sometimes form in the condensation-collars of high-pressure vents: if hooked out during a slip, they hold a gallon of clear, sweet, uncontaminated water distilled in a chamber that only existed for forty seconds, tasting faintly of ozone instead of peat-acid and iron.

Not everything they pull out is useful. Reaching blind into a frayed seam brings strange things back to hand. Sometimes a runner drags clear a tightly wound knot of wet roots that sits on a palm and ticks evenly like an escapement for four days before going cold and slack. Sometimes they hook a flat shard of glassy, mineralized peat no bigger than a saucer; tilt it however you like in the dim iron twilight, and its surface reflects a bright, cloudless sky of tarnished brass, or the slow turning of a huge subterranean wheel of braided roots that has no business being under the mat.

They never throw these catches away. They tuck them into oiled hide pouches against their chests, trade them quietly when two bands meet in the fog, or give them to small children bundled in tar-scented slings on their mothers' backs, letting the little ones hold the warm, ticking knots to their ears while the wind shrieks outside the hide-shells. Eventually, after weeks or years, even a caught thing loses its grip on itself. A beloved brass-sky shard goes dull overnight and crumbles into wet sand; a springy iron hook wakes up one morning soft as rotted kelp. When that happens, they lay it back on the moss without ceremony and let the bog drink it down. Nothing up here keeps an edge forever.

Least of all the people themselves.

You cannot spend fifty years thrusting your hands into the teeth of failing laws without picking up some of the plateau's wear. It does not turn them into monsters; it wears on them the way salt air wears on a fisherman's joints, or coal dust settles in a miner's chest. They call it *carrying slack*.

Watch a middle-aged woman mending a broken foot-paddle inside a warm hide-shell, and if you keep your eyes on her fingers, you notice the bone needle sometimes clears the wet sinew a fraction of a second before her thumb pushes it through. Walk behind an older runner across a bright puddle during a squall, and his shadow lags two paces behind his heels, hurrying slightly to catch up whenever he stops to check the wind. One man's cough arrives in two slightly offset beats; another has a left wrist that stays cold as bog-water no matter how close he holds it to the ember-bell, because he pulled his hook back a hair too late when a peat-screw tore itself apart twenty years ago.

They treat these misfires with a dry, unbothered pragmatism. A body with slack in it is a little clumsy at fine work, a little slower to rise in the freezing damp, but it makes for the finest watchers on the tableland. Someone whose own presence already sits half a beat off-phase does not even need a tea-puddle to feel an extrusion beginning to slip; their bad knee clicks twice, or the air behind their ears goes suddenly thin and quiet, and they tap the moss in warning while younger eyes still see only a solid column hammering in the fog.

When most of them die—from the deep bone-chill that eventually wins every argument up here, or a misjudged arc of current that overwhelms their copper fringes, or simple wearing out—their band strips them cleanly of their caught iron, their hide coats, and their living moss-braids. Those things are too hard-won to waste, and pass immediately to the living. The naked body is laid flat on a quiet stretch of black sphagnum far from any active vents, without a marker, a song loud enough for the wind to carry, or a single handful of peat heaped over it. Within days, the wet, trembling quilt folds gently over the weight and closes smooth. The plateau digests them as thoroughly as it digested the mountains underneath.

Yet not all of the very old die that cleanly.

Ask a band about the ones who lived too long and carried too much slack—the watchers who reached sixty or seventy winters on the mat, whose voices had begun to whisper out of their own collars before they opened their mouths—and they will only nod out toward the long, empty intervals between the eruptions. They do not speak of them as ghosts, or as dead. They speak of them the way they speak of a caught tool that finally softened and went back to being part of the weather.

If you stay on the plateau long enough, walking those huge, roaring miles of wet moss where nothing rises above the knee, you may see one yourself.

It happens most often during an electrical squall, when a blinding white arc snaps from a thicket of vibrating reeds to the belly of the low cloud and turns every hanging drop of mist briefly fluorescent silver. In that stark, dead flash, half a mile out across an empty stretch of turf where no band is camped, you catch sight of a single, motionless figure. Low-shouldered, draped in pale, drenched hide whose copper-moss fringes glow faintly along the wet moss, standing ankle-deep in a glassy puddle that reflects a sky nobody else can see.

Before the dark rushes back and the thunder slaps you in the ribs, the figure lifts a horn-shod pole and taps the peat—three beats, a pause, two beats, a drag.

You do not hear the tap over the roar of the plateau. You feel it a heartbeat later in the soles of your boots, carried up out of the unseen reeds all around you, warning you to step left—just as the unbroken sphagnum where your right heel had been begins to swell, hiss, and twist violently upward into steam and iron.
