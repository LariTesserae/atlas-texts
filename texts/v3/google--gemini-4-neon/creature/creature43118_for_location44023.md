To see who truly lives in the basin, you have to step down off the dark slate rim, leave your ears behind, and wait until the hairs on your forearms learn to read the weather.

From above, the place reads like an empty model tended by a handful of pale insects. Down on the flagstones, among the rising sheets of chalk-grey stone, the emptiness begins to resolve into layers. Nothing here speaks, bleeds, drips, or decays. Every living thing is built from some compromise of salt, wax, dry fiber, and keratin, and every one of them inhabits a medium that you only half-perceive: the shifting electrical tension of dry air moving over razor-sharp mineral edges.

Start by looking up—not at the sky, but at the knife-thin top edges of the roofless walls.

In air this parched, static charge collects fiercely along every sharp corner and parapet. What looks from the plaza like an uneven crust of pale crystalline fuzz crowning the highest lintels is alive. Clustered tightly along the razor-rims are colonies of minuscule, branched plumes no wider than a coin, pale ivory at the base and smoked violet at the tips. They have no roots and draw nothing from the cured porcelain beneath them; they merely clamp onto the microscopic pores of the ridge with hooked bristles and drink the electrical gradient between the stone and the drifting air. Around them, charged dust-spores and loose salt-motes curve inward out of the convective drafts, snagged by the ridge’s pull and absorbed straight through their feathery filaments.

They look completely inert until a wall gives way somewhere across the city. When that distant facade collapses into its soundless white bloom and sends a flat tide of static rolling across the squares, every plume along the neighboring rooftops snaps rigid at the exact same instant. If the overhead haze has dimmed toward silver-blue, a cold, faint violet glow runs along the high geometry of the avenues—a silent ripple of dry fire that vanishes before you can turn your head to follow it.

Between these high fringes drift the smallest free-living things in the basin: hollow, salt-shelled spheres no larger than dust motes, bristling with dry cilia that ride the mutual repulsion of charged air. They never land while they are healthy. They spiral up through the hollow towers and cross the wide squares in slow, luminous curtains until an eddy drags one against a sharp parapet, where it adheres, splits along its dry seam, and begins a new tuft along the stone.

Drop your gaze from the parapets all the way to the floor, and kneel beside one of the straight bedrock fractures where a new wall is emerging.

Here, in the first handspan above the slate, the rising aluminosilicate is still cool and pliant as tallow. If you hold completely still—keeping your breath off the seam—you may notice a faint, continuous bevel forming along one side of the pale extrusion, as though an invisible thumb were drawing a groove just above the floor.

Half-buried in that soft lip live pale, blind, ribbon-flat grazers the length of a finger and scarcely thicker than a sheet of parchment. Their bodies are segmented into overlapping waxy scales, supple and matte, never fully exposed to the open air; ten minutes on the dry flagstones would cure their skins brittle and crack them apart. They belong exclusively to the narrow boundary where stone has not yet decided to harden. Moving at a pace slower than the shifting shadows, they plane away tiny shavings of raw, uncured mineral paste and trace salts before exposure turns it to porcelain.

Their feeding leaves a hair-thin undercut along one face of the rising slab. Days later, when that groove has been lifted two stories into the air by the relentless tectonic creep beneath the floor, that faint asymmetry becomes the hinge along which the entire wall leans, fractures, and shears cleanly into a plaza. Without these blind seam-followers, the basin would choke on blind, vertical slabs falling at random. Instead, the walls fail in slow, predictable directions.

And the tall shapers know how to read them—and how to move them.

---

Up close, the reed-like builders are far less mechanical than they appear from the ridge.

There are rarely more than thirty or forty of them in the entire bowl at once, and they are long-lived—slow, gaunt creatures that move through the deep charcoal shadows with a deliberate, unhurried economy. Their six jointed limbs are not solid bone or wet chitin, but hollow, fluted struts of pale, calcified fiber, matte as unglazed bisque. Inside those flutes run bundles of dry, conductive filaments and tiny pressurized chambers that stiffen or relax to move the joints with scarcely a flicker of friction. Their broad footpads are lined underneath with soft, dry lamellae, like the underside of a dried bracket fungus, compressing the powdery chalk beneath them without kicking up sparks or visible plumes.

Their flat, wedge-shaped heads have no ears, no nostrils, and no wet eyes. Along the beveled ridge of the brow sits a recessed band of dark, crystalline facets that reads polarized light, hard shadow-edges, and the faint optical shimmer where a cool thermal draft bends against a sunlit wall.

If you stay motionless in a doorway while two of them meet in a narrow corridor, you will begin to understand how much of a *who* they are.

They stop three paces apart, neither stepping into the light. Slowly, both raise their spatulate forelimbs until their flat wrists face each other across the empty air. Nothing moves between them that your eyes can see, yet the fine hairs along your jaw suddenly lift and lean toward the corridor, and a dry, metallic tang touches the back of your tongue. They are discharging slow, braided currents of static across the gap—measured pulses, pauses, shifting polarities—reading the charge-state stored in one another’s fluted chests.

Because there are so few of them in a city that continually rebuilds and erases itself underfoot, every shaper carries a living pressure-map of the basin. In those silent electric exchanges they pass along what has changed: which bedrock fracture has accelerated since the last dimming, which viaduct has developed a hairline shear, where a glider has been riding the drafts too low. Sometimes one reaches down with a cured shard of porcelain, gently lifts a pale ribbon-grazer out of a rising seam, and carries it two streets over to lay it along the base of an aging, obstructing wall—planting a slow, living chisel that will bring down a three-story facade a month from now and open a fresh avenue for the wind.

Watch them long enough, and you notice that not everything they carve is for the airflow or the waxy flora.

Near the bright, open center of the basin, away from the dark slate rim, you will occasionally come upon a rising wall that breaks the city’s sparse logic: a thick, unpierced pilaster left untouched where a doorway ought to be, its pale skin marked near eye level with three shallow, neat vertical ticks carved by a flat mandible.

Those are the nursery walls.

In a world with no water, nothing can lay a wet egg in the open dust. After two shapers entangle in the shadowed lee of a plaza—a slow, hushed embrace during the deepest dimming of the haze, passing dry packets of salt-rich seed between their abdominal plates—the bearer walks to a freshly extruding sheet of mineral, still cool and waxy at the floor. With her blunt ivory jaws she hollows a crisp rectangular niche into the yielding stone, presses a single faceted, leathery capsule inside, and trowels soft mineral paste smoothly back over the opening with her spatulate palms. Then she scores the three vertical ticks beside it while the surface is still soft enough to take the mark.

Over the seasons that follow, the wall rises, carrying the unborn shaper steadily upward out of reach of the floor, sealed inside curing porcelain like a relic in a pillar. The capsule draws trace mineral salts through its porous shell from the surrounding stone, buffered from the chill and hidden from the predators overhead. Every adult shaper who passes reads the three ticks—by sight and by the subtle interruption in the wall’s surface charge—and leaves that slab uncut.

When the young shaper is ready, often two or three stories above the flagstones, it does not hatch into a nest. It chews a clean, exact square straight through the cured porcelain from within and steps out onto a narrow lintel, unfolding its pale, fluted limbs into the pewter gloom. Its very first act in the world is to open a window; the right angle is already shaped into the spacing of its jaws.

And not far from those marked walls lie the strangest rooms in the city: small, three-sided enclosures tucked into the deadest eddies of the central squares, where no convective draft enters and no mica-stalk grows.

Inside them stand the elders.

Nothing rots in this basin, and a shaper does not simply collapse in an avenue when its conductive fibers begin to dry past recovery and crystalline salts slowly lock its joints. When an elder feels its charge thinning and its stride stiffening, it walks deliberately into one of these windless rooms, sets all six broad pads evenly upon the flagstones, tucks its spatulate forelimbs neatly across its narrow thorax, and stops.

Years pass. The hollow body dries to the exact chalk-grey of the surrounding walls, standing upright like a delicate piece of scaffolding forgotten in a courtyard. Yet even motionless, its fluted struts act as a dry capacitor, retaining for seasons the faint, complex geometry of electrical charge accumulated over a lifetime of carving the wind. Younger shapers enter these quiet rooms before cutting a major new viaduct or redirecting a central plaza. They stand motionless before an elder, raise their forelimbs, and feel out the fading patterns still held in the hollow frame—consulting a silent blueprint drawn in lingering static.

Along the baseboards of those same rooms, the tiny, fingernail-sized porcelain scavengers file past in orderly chains, grazing on shed mineral fluff and fallen spore-husks. They climb over broken shards and fallen dust, yet they never touch a standing elder while even the faintest thread of static lingers in its struts; the tiny charge repels their delicate antennae. Only years later, on the exact day the elder’s last spark finally grounds out into the stone, does a pale line of scavengers turn inward from the baseboard and climb its legs, dismantling it gently along its seams until nothing remains but a neat ring of powder waiting for the next wall to fall.

---

Above the roofless squares, where the cool trapped air of the bowl meets the slightly warmer drift crossing the quarry rim, live the gliders.

The basin is too small to support more than five or six of them. Because there is no wet tissue here to scab or knit, every scrape against a parapet or scorch from a cornered shaper’s defensive spark seals with a hard, amber bead of dry salt-resin. Each glider therefore carries its biography in the notched edges of its translucent, dust-grey membranes; stay in the bowl for a few shifts of the light and you begin to recognize individuals by the small, crisp tears along their trailing ribs.

They are creatures of astonishing thinness—a lattice of hollow, gas-filled struts supporting membranes so fine that the pewter light passes straight through them, turning them nearly indistinguishable from the curling porcelain ribbons peeled off the curing walls. Along the leading edge of the main wing-spar run two narrow, smoky bands of compound lenses, angled forward and slightly downward so a banking glider can line up six successive doorways at once and judge the exact geometry of an unobstructed plunge.

They are patient students of the city’s revisions. Whenever a shaper opens a new archway or a tall wall shears away to widen a square, a glider will circle slowly overhead for half a shift of the haze, tilting its brittle ribs to test the altered updraft spilling out of the breach, memorizing the new sightline before ever folding its wings to drop.

Full-grown shapers are rarely their target; an adult shaper backed against a wall can ground its hind feet, flare its fluted limbs, and snap a sharp, concentrated arc of static straight into a diving membrane, punching a dry hole that steals a glider’s lift for weeks. Instead, the gliders wait for the fingernail-scavengers that stray out of the baseboard shadows to cross a bright flagstone, or for an inexperienced young shaper freshly emerged from a nursery wall, not yet accustomed to keeping a lintel between itself and the open sky. Even a kill happens in total pantomime: the glider flares inches above the stone, pierces the thorax with a hollow, needle-fine proboscis to draw out the dense, dry nutritive oils and concentrated salts, and rises back on the rebound draft off a sheer wall, leaving an almost unbroken husk to tumble silently down the stepped plazas.

When the overhead haze dims to its deepest silver-blue and the thermal currents die away, the gliders cannot remain aloft, nor can they safely rest on the powdery floor where dust would clog their breathing slits and leave them unable to launch without a draft. Instead, they glide silently into the shadow beneath the highest, narrowest viaducts, hook the curved ivory spurs at their wingtips onto the rough underside of the stone, and fold themselves flat—hanging motionless like grey paper lanterns until the next brightening band of light wakes the drafts again.

---

That stillness—when the haze deepens, the mica-stalks fold their papery vanes shut against their quills, and the gliders hang folded under the bridges—belongs to the last inhabitants of the bowl.

You will never see them while the pewter light is bright. They do not belong to the pale city at all, but to the dark, freshly fractured slate and glassy slag of the rim that rings it.

Wedged flush into the angular ledges of the quarry wall, they are invisible: flat, wide, low-slung creatures shaped like overlapping shingles of dark charcoal slate, roughly the size of a broad platter. Their dorsal plates are fused with fine, iron-rich silica gleaned from the rim itself, giving them the exact dull, glassy sheen of fractured slag. Beneath their beveled edges hide eight short, heavy legs tipped with blunt claws of dark horn.

Only when the breezes stall and the light drops to silver-blue do they detach from the rim and slide down onto the pale chalk avenues.

Against the white powder they look startlingly foreign—moving shards of the dark rim loose inside the pale geometry. They do not stride like the shapers; they advance in crisp, hitching glides, keeping their low rims tight to the ground, leaving wide, brushed ribbons in the dust behind them. They come down into the city for the concentrated salts that the dark slate lacks: the freshest, most luminous piles of newly collapsed wall-powder, the unbroken purple-grey spore-burrs before pressure snaps them open, and—above all—the dense, cured porcelain shards that accumulate along the outer edges of the basin.

Their jaws are not flat chisels like the shapers’, but heavy, corrugated plates backed by an internal gizzard lined with swallowed grit of glassy slag. Slowly, silently, they ingest fallen porcelain flakes that would otherwise jam the narrow bedrock fractures near the basin’s edge, grinding them to heavy, dense pellets that they void along the base of the slate rim. In that quiet, inadvertent way, they keep the outer seams clear so the city’s floor can continue its steady, cold extrusion.

Yet the truce between the dark rim-dwellers and the pale shapers is uneasy.

If a shaper encounters one gliding down a corridor during the dim hours, neither attacks. The tall shaper backs cleanly into a doorway, tucking its long, reed-like limbs flat against the jamb and holding its charge tight; the low slate-backed creature pauses, lifts the front lip of its dark carapace a fraction of an inch—briefly revealing a pale, faintly luminous seam of salt-organs along its throat—and slides past without scraping the walls.

Still, the shapers never place their nursery walls or their still rooms near the outer avenues. A hungry rim-dweller that finds an unmarked pilaster near the slate edge—or one whose three carved ticks have weathered faint—will sometimes press its heavy dark rim against the porcelain and rasp patiently, hour after silent hour, until it breaches the hollow niche inside for the rich, dry salt-capsule sealed within. Once the overhead haze begins to brighten back toward pale pewter and the first cool convective ribbon stirs the dust in the plazas, the dark shingle-creatures turn back toward the quarry walls, climbing the fractured shelves until they vanish again into the stone.

---

Stand still in the center of the widest square as the light brightens once more, and the city no longer feels abandoned.

A cool draft wakes along the stepped plazas, and every mica-vane along the avenues pivots in silent unison to meet it. Far overhead, a notched, translucent wing detaches from the shadow beneath a viaduct and slips effortlessly onto the rising air. Two streets away, an old wall—undermined weeks ago by a blind ribbon-grazer carried there on a porcelain shard—quietly tilts, separates into clean geometric plates, and dissolves into a luminous white ring of powder; across every rooftop in the bowl, the high feathery fringes stiffen at once to drink the passing pulse of static.

In the charcoal shadow of a doorway nearby, a tall, pale shaper kneels beside a fresh seam of cool wax-stone, raises its blunt ivory jaws, and begins to carve a new right angle into the rising floor—while three stories above its head, a crisp, square window opens silently from the inside of a marked pillar, and a pair of pale, folded limbs steps out onto the ledge to feel the wind for the first time.
