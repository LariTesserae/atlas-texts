If you stood quiet enough on that middle bridge, and if the figure below did not sense the heat of your breath against the dead air, you might watch them vanish behind the curled olive leaves—and wonder, for a moment, whether you had seen a person at all, or merely a knot of lichened timber that had learned to shift its weight.

To follow them inside is to understand how thoroughly the gorge has shaped what lives within it. They are not exiles waiting out a long winter. They are a people who have made a covenant with cold, dampness, and slow time—a covenant written directly into the dense weave of their bodies.

Beneath the layered bark-cloth and grey moss-weave, they are compact and low-centered, seldom taller than a person’s chest, with deep, barrelled ribs and shoulders set slightly forward in an easy, permanent crouch. Tallness is a liability in a settlement whose ceilings sag under ice and whose doorways pinch narrower by an inch after every thaw. They move with a rolling, unhurried gait, knees kept soft so their feet feel the give of wet haircap moss before committing their weight, soles toughened to a dark, pebbled leather that grips frozen bark without a sound.

Their skin is cool even at the temples, waxy and dense, stained in the creases of wrists and knuckles with the iron-dark tannins of wet peat and the pale gum of bruised conifers. Where the cold touches them—ears, cheekbones, the broad, stub-fingered hands—the flesh has a dull, slate-grey cast, blood drawn patiently inward to guard the core. They do not shiver. Shivering burns through precious fuel and shakes loose the thin pocket of trapped warmth inside their layers. When the evening freeze drops down the slot of the canyon, a tender caught on an upper bridge simply grows still: shoulders tucking down, chin sinking into the felted collar, breath slowing to four or five shallow, unclouded draws a minute while their pulse drops to a heavy, measured drum. They endure the bite of the mountain the way a dormant hemlock does, pulling everything vital deep beneath the rind.

Their eyes are wide-pupiled and pale—grey as rime-ice, or the washed green of water standing over bog-moss—made for reading the faint silver glow bounced off frost and lichen in the lower corridors. In the gloom, they see with startling precision: the hairline split where trapped meltwater is starting to wedge apart a fused birch joint; the tiny black sheen of springtails waking on a puddle’s rim; the exact angle of a hemlock leader bent a hair’s breadth too far toward the weeping cliff. What they cannot bear is the open sky. If one of them ever looks directly up through a drainage shaft at midday, their eyes water immediately and shrink to tight, painful slits.

Because there is no fire here.

There has never been a hearth in the cleft, never a lamp, never a single coal carried down from the ridge. Flame is unthinkable in a city woven from living wood and dry-packed sphagnum; smoke would hang trapped between the sheer rock walls and suffocate every tier, and open heat would fool the dormant trees into pushing sap before the frost has broken, killing the trusses that hold the mountain apart. Nothing here is forged, boiled, or roasted. Everything that keeps them alive is accomplished by insulation, pressure, and the quiet heat of slow decay.

Their clothing is built on the same principle as their walls. Against the relentless drip of the gorge, they do not try to stay dry; they manage the wet. Outer cloaks are beaten from the papery inner bark of black birch, stitched with split root-fibers and rubbed patiently with conifer resin and the scarce fat of trapped voles until slush beads and runs off them like water off evergreen needles. Beneath that shell sit quilted pouches of woven willow-bast stuffed tight with shredded sedge-fluff and pale reindeer lichen. When a liner grows heavy with sweat and seeped meltwater, a tender peels it off and hangs it beneath the ice-proof overhangs of the upper hemlock eaves, where the thin, sub-zero drafts draw the moisture straight from the frozen fibers into the air—clothes dried by frost, crisp and light by the following afternoon.

And inside the domed chambers, once the rubbery alpine shrubs have curled their olive leaves shut across the arches for the night, the darkness is not quite absolute. Tucked into damp root-cradles beside the doorways and cisterns sit chunks of dead, punky birch heartwood seeded with pale green foxfire. The fungus burns no air and throws no heat; it sheds only a cold, ghostly sheen, just enough to catch the wet rim of a hollowed burl-bowl or the slow blink of a neighbor’s eye across the room.

In that cool, greenish dimness, the chambers smell powerfully of earth and sour yeast. Stacked into the thickest peat-masonry walls—where three feet of compressed moss and leaf-litter hold their stubborn, faint warmth just above freezing—sit vessels grown rather than carved: gnarled birch-burls coaxed over decades around round river-stones, then split and sealed with plugs of waxy leaves and spruce pitch. Inside them, cold-loving ferments work at a glacial pace. Crushed alpine roots, pounded juniper berries, bitter hemlock seeds, and flour ground from freeze-dried lichen steep in cold meltwater until time and wild yeast do the work of a cooking fire. When a chamber seals for the evening freeze, the bowls are passed from hand to hand in the dark. The mash is thick, grey-brown, sharp on the tongue, faintly effervescent, and leaves a slow, surprising ember of warmth deep in the belly as the bodies around it settle together into the moss-lined hollows of the floor.

They sleep in heaps—six, eight, sometimes twelve to a dome, wedged into nests of dry bog-cotton beneath shared pelts of felted bark-cloth, limbs interlaced, breathing into the folds of one another’s cloaks. No one sleeps alone in the gorge. To sleep alone is to lose the narrow margin between a body’s warmth and the frost waiting an arm’s length away behind the leaves.

***

They have very little use for spoken words out in the lanes. Sound dies quickly in wet moss, and opening the mouth to the freezing air wastes heat. Instead, they speak through the wood itself.

Because every arch, ramp, and doorway in the cleft is grafted into a single continuous skeleton of fused hemlock and birch, vibrations travel far along the living grain. A tender working on the fourth tier will press a bare palm flat against a load-bearing trunk and tap twice with a thick knuckle—a dull, woody *tok-tok* that runs down through twenty feet of scarred bark to a cousin checking a root-cistern on the second level. They have an entire vocabulary of these raps and rests: *ice heavy overhead*; *gutter blocked on the west seam*; *stone loose on the upper scree*; or simply, *I am here, keep your weight off the bridge*.

Even quieter—and far older—is the language they leave in the plants themselves.

A tender does not look at a corridor and see mere shelter; they read a chronicle spanning ten lifetimes, written without ink or chisel. Every scar in the cambium is a signature. A narrow sliver of raw black schist wedged into a birch fork tells whoever passes next winter which way the branch is meant to lean over the coming decade. A willow-strip knotted once around a young shoot means *let this grow wild to thicken the windbreak*; knotted twice means *pinch the tip back at every thaw until it bristles into a hedge*. Where a doorway has been worn dark and waxy by generations of passing hips, the wood beneath the polish still carries the deliberate twist given to it by a great-grandfather whose name no one speaks anymore, though everyone recognizes his hand by the way he always braided three roots instead of two.

They think at the speed of trees. To them, haste is a kind of blindness. If a frost-shattered boulder tears through an upper walkway, there is no scramble to patch it by dusk. The broken boughs are cleared, a temporary curtain of heavy lichen-cloth is hung to keep the drafts out, and two neighboring birch-leaders are gently bent down, lashed with peeled inner bark, and coaxed toward each other across the gap. It will take seven summers before their bark rubs raw enough to fuse, and fifteen before the bridge will hold a child’s weight. The tender who ties the first knot does so with complete serenity, knowing their own feet may never cross it.

Only inside the sealed domes at night, when the olive leaves have locked out the gale and the shared warmth of piled bodies takes the knife-edge off the air, do their voices wake. Even then, it is a tiny, intimate speech—soft clicks, low closed-mouth hums, breathy consonants spoken with lips close to a listener’s temple so the warmth of the breath is not lost to the room. They have no songs that rise above a murmur, and no legends of distant seas or kings. Their stories are maps of tension and weather: the winter the weeping wall froze dry for forty days; the year a storm tore open the upper canopy and buried the third tier in powder; the exact genealogy of the great double arch by the eastern cistern, tracing each branch back to the hands that first bowed it.

***

Life in the gorge is sorted vertically, following the slow gradient between the savage cold of the rim and the heavy, steady dampness of the floor.

The highest tiers—just beneath the wind-planed crust of juniper and glazed moss where the mountain gale hisses relentlessly overhead—belong to the young. Their blood runs hotter, their joints are quick, and their grip is sure on ice-slick timber. During the brief midday thaws, when the upper canopy loosens and slush rains silently through the lattices, it is the young who climb the braided root-ladders into the greenish glare. They break away overgrown sheaths of rime before the weight can snap a roof-truss; they pry sharp slabs of fallen schist from the scree and hoist them into young forks where the wood will grow around them into future terraces; they clear the drainage grooves with bare, calloused fingers that barely seem to feel the sting of meltwater.

It is the young, too, who watch for windfalls from the rim. Nothing large climbs down into the cleft alive, save the occasional alpine owl hunting voles, but the gales above are merciless. Once or twice a winter, a storm sweeps a snow-hare, a frozen ptarmigan, or—in a year remembered for a generation—a mountain goat off the icy ledges above, tumbling it down through the bristling canopy until it catches in the braided hemlock roofs like a fly in a net. When that happens, word travels down the trunks in a rapid flurry of knuckle-taps. The young work swiftly and without a sound before the carcass locks solid with frost, using sharp natural flakes of schist to divide what the ridge has dropped: sinew twisted into fine bindings, bones ground on wet rock into awls and needles, dense pelts scraped clean and worked soft with chewed willow tannins, fat packed into bark-pouches to seal seams and rub into chapped skin. Nothing is wasted. Even the marrow and blood are folded straight into the cold fermentation jars, turning the grey mash rich and dark for a season.

In their middle years, as their bodies thicken and their patience deepens, the tenders move inward to the middle lanes—the intricate heart of the city where the arches cross in triple layers, where the cisterns sit, where the great root-masonry walls must be constantly packed with fresh sphagnum and trimmed back so they do not choke the passages. These are the patient listeners, the ones who press an ear against a groaning trunk for half an hour without moving, reading the invisible balance of five stacked tiers of frozen water and living timber.

And as they grow old—as their hands stiffen into curved root-hooks and their skin turns the color of grey birch-bark—they drift downward to the lowest, darkest, wettest walkways, just above the deep springy bog that is slowly swallowing the oldest foundations.

Down there, daylight never truly arrives; even at noon, the corridors are lit only by the faint, cold smear of foxfire and the dull shimmer of dark puddles. Yet the air down near the floor hardly shifts at all. The vast, compressed bed of rotting needles, dead fern-fronds, and ancient peat beneath the walkways breathes up a faint, perpetual warmth—never pleasant, merely steady, spared the brutal plunges of the upper tiers. Here, the elders sit wrapped in moss-weave, tending the oldest mother-ferments whose sour starter-cultures have been kept alive for centuries, chewing tough strips of inner willow-bark until the fibers soften enough for the weavers above, and holding the newborns.

An infant born in the cleft does not see the slit of sky for its first two winters. Too small to keep its own heat, it lives tucked bare-skinned inside the inner layers of its mother’s or an elder’s cloak, passed quietly from one slow-breathing chest to the next down in the peat-warmed domes. It learns the feel of foxfire-lit moss, the sour-sweet taste of pre-chewed root-mash, and the deep, steady vibration of knuckle-taps through the walls long before its feet ever touch a frozen walkway.

When an elder’s breath finally grows too thin to stir the cold air, there is no wailing to disturb the heavy silence of the lanes. Their dry outer bark-cloth is gently unfastened—good felted birch is too hard-won to bury—and their body is washed with cold meltwater from the weeping rock, rubbed with crushed hemlock needles, and wrapped tight in a shroud of living, water-logged sphagnum. At dusk, as the olive leaves begin their dry rasp of curling shut, four tenders carry the light, curled body down the lowest root-ladder to the edge of the rising bog.

They part the wet turf with bare hands. Beneath the living green skin, the dark peat is freezing, acid, and still. They press the dead gently down into the black water until the moss closes over them without a ripple, leaving them where decay moves so slowly that their flesh will turn to dark leather and endure for centuries beneath the village. Into the soft mound above their shoulder, someone thrusts a single green willow-wand or a young hemlock seedling. In time, the pale roots will thread down through the quiet ribs, anchoring the next tier of walkways to the bones of those who built the last.

***

Because the village is always climbing.

No one among the tenders speaks of this as a tragedy, though every one of them can feel it in the soles of their feet. From below, the bog rises—an inch in a lifetime, a full walkway every few centuries—swallowing the oldest arches into cold, dark peat that feeds the warmth of the rooms above. From above, the mountain ridge imposes its hard, invisible ceiling: the wind-plane, where the ice-laden gale shears flat anything that dares lift a twig above the jagged lip of schist.

Between the rising floor and the shearing sky, the living space of the cleft is slowly, imperceptibly thinning. One day, far beyond the reach of any hand now tying willow-strips, the highest roofs will press against the rim, the last lower corridors will drown in black water, and the gorge will be full—a solid, frozen plug of bog and gnarled timber sealing the wound in the mountain shut forever.

They do not fight this. To them, a crack in the stone was never meant to stay open; their whole craft is the slow healing of a scar.

Yet once every generation or two, in the briefest, strangest pause of high summer—when the sun hangs directly over the jagged slot for an hour and the scouring gale on the ridge drops to a dry, thin sigh—something restless stirs among the leanest of the young. No ceremony marks their leaving. In the greenish twilight of the upper canopy, two or three figures strap tight pouches of fermented seed-cake and dried lichen beneath their bark-cloaks, and tuck bundles of live, damp willow-cuttings and moss-wrapped birch seedlings directly against their ribs so their own body-heat will keep the buds from drying out.

They climb the last braided root-ladder, push aside the glazed juniper crust, and haul themselves up over the sharp, raw lip of the rim.

Up there, the world is terrifying—vast, blindingly white with snow-glare, howling with dry air that steals the moisture from their lips in a single breath. Squinting until their pale eyes stream with tears, bent low against the open sky as if expecting a blow, they crawl out across the freshly split grey-black schist, feeling the stone with their scarred palms, moving slowly along the cruel spine of the mountain. Most are never heard of again; the ridge is unkind, and the wind takes what it wants.

But now and then, miles away along the jagged crest, a pair of them finds another dark seam where the mountain has cracked open faster than weather can round it down, and where cold water weeps along fresh fracture lines out of reach of the gale. They slip down out of the terrible light into the narrow shadow, press a bare knuckle to the wet stone, and wedge their first green birch-shoot into a crevice—beginning, in silence, the work of the next ten lifetimes.
