You notice them first as a flaw in the math.

You have been standing still in the wet pitch-dark long enough to learn the meter of the place: the steady, boneless hum in the roots of your teeth; the warmth pressing evenly against both cheeks; the single heavy drop falling from a cooling frond ten paces away—*plip*—followed by ten silent seconds, and then another drop of the exact same weight.

Then the interval breaks. Ten seconds pass. Twelve. Fifteen.

Nothing splashes. No branch creaks. Yet the warmth against your left cheek subtly thickens, shifting from the broad, indifferent radiance of the trunks into something narrower and more focused, like the heat thrown off by a freshly shut-down engine. A faint, sharper edge of ozone cuts through the smell of heated wax and wet pennies. The fine hairs along your left forearm lift cleanly off your skin, leaning into the dark—not toward the magnetite trunk beside you, but toward the empty air two paces away.

You were right that animal life never occurred here. There is no breath waiting in the mist, no soft gut coiled behind ribs, no damp eye dilating in the dark. You were only wrong to assume that everything without flesh must stay rooted to the floor.

If you hold your breath and reach slowly into that pocket of focused heat, your fingertips meet something that is not a trunk.

It is hotter than the forest—not hot enough to blister, just the concentrated, purposeful fever of a coil carrying a heavy load. Under your palm, it feels neither like hide nor like chitin, but like the folded louvers of a radiator cast from living iron-bark and plated with smooth, slick copper. Every margin is razor-crisp and wet with condensed brine. Your hand instinctively waits for the animal reaction—the flinch, the tense of muscle, the sudden scuttle away.

None comes. Instead, a sour metallic taste blooms sharply across the back of your tongue as your damp skin brushes a seam between two insulated plates, and the hum in your teeth splits cleanly in half. The deep continental bass of the forest is still there, steady as bedrock, but riding right on top of it is a second vibration: higher, tighter, intolerably precise, like a tuning fork pressed against your sternum.

And if you force your useless eyes to stay open in the black, staring just above the height of your own head, you see the nearest thing to a gaze this world possesses.

Fifty or sixty hair-thin filaments—too delicate to feel like branches, curled slightly at the tips like the antennae of a fern—have fanned outward from the thing’s upper crown. They are leaning silently toward your face. At the very tip of each whisker, where it meets the heavy vapor rising off your skin, a pinpoint bead of pale violet kindles and holds. They shed no real light; you still cannot see your own hand, or the dark folded column standing inches from your chest. You see only a faint, curved constellation of cold corona-sparks hanging motionless in the wet velvet dark, tilting with terrible, silent attention as you shift your weight.

It is tall—perhaps a head taller than you—and narrow as a folded caliper. It has no head, no torso, no mouth, no soft interior that could bruise or spoil. Its core is a vertical stack of dozens of thin, damp, closely spaced plates—a living voltaic pile, sheathed in translucent bands of refined amber wax that insulate it from the brine-mist. Put your ear close and you will hear no heartbeat and no drawn air, only a dry, silky, continuous whisper: electrolyte being drawn upward through hair-fine internal capillaries by electro-osmosis alone, circulating silently without a valve or a pump.

When it finally decides to move, you realize why you never heard it arrive.

It has no muscles to bunch, no tendons to creak, no claws to scrape the sharp rhizome-slabs. Its limbs are long, slender arcs of laminated wood-metal—strips of dense iron-lignin bonded flat against ribbons of soft, copper-rich cellulose. To step, it simply routes a pulse of internal current down one side of a limb. The two bonded layers expand and contract unevenly against each other, bending the entire leg inward with the smooth, hydraulic finality of a heated spring. There is no jerk, no wobble, no wasted motion.

At the end of each limb sits no foot, no pad—only a narrow, blunt wedge of bare lodestone and bright copper. As the limb lowers toward the wet floor, it does not stomp or splash; it stops a millimeter above the slick magnetite ridge, flicks a tiny internal charge through its tip, and locks cleanly onto the iron-bearing slab with a soundless magnetic pull. The rear limb releases its field at the exact same instant, letting the surface tension of the black brine peel smoothly off the metal without leaving a ripple.

It glides past you like a shadow made of switch-blades and quiet thunder, threading effortlessly between two fluted trunks too close together for your shoulders to clear, folding its narrow body flat as a louver as it passes. Then its warmth withdraws, the violet pinpoints fade out one by one, and three seconds later, ten paces away, the frond resumes its drop. *Plip.*

They are not animals, and they are not invaders from some brighter, noisier world. They are what happens when an electrochemical forest runs long enough to discover what roots can never reach.

Across a flat, drowned continent of raw stone and silent amperage, almost everything stays balanced for millennia. Almost. Beneath the woven root-mattress, the crust still shifts. A fresh tectonic seam fractures in the dark, lifting one shelf of iron-rich basalt three meters above a pool of cuprous brine. A distillation bladder swells past the tolerance of its waxed walls and ruptures, dumping hot, sweet hydrocarbons across a neutral slab. For a brief geological instant—a decade, a century—two mismatched voltages sit side by side across a narrow gap, crackling softly with untapped potential that the slow, rooted trunks cannot grow fast enough to bridge.

Long ago, something let go of the bedrock to catch those seams.

They began, perhaps, as severed vascular fronds or detached root-knots that fell into high-gradient pools and failed to dissolve—fragments caught in standing eddies of current, electroplating themselves symmetrically, growing hair-fine contact whiskers on opposing ends to balance the load until osmotic pressure lifted them clear of the stone. Over epochs without predators, without seasons, without a single breath of wind to knock them down, those loose junctions refined themselves into a lineage apart: the unrooted.

They do not eat. To eat requires jaws, acid, rot, and the frantic, wasteful burning of stolen flesh. Instead, they *bridge*.

Follow the heat-trail of one long enough—tracking it by the faint trail of sharpened ozone and the tiny shift in charge left on the wet trunks it brushed—and you will eventually find it standing astride a low tectonic step where two mineral zones meet. One slender copper-shod limb is locked firmly onto the upper basalt shelf; another reaches down into the black mirror-water of the lower pan. Its wax-sheathed plates are fanned slightly apart to shed waste heat into the mist.

It does not move for hours. It simply lets the voltage difference between the two shelves pour straight through its stacked core, driving silent chemical reactions inside its damp membranes, reversing its internal salts, packing dense, quiet energy into its laminated walls until its entire body hums at a pitch so clean it makes the fillings in your jaw ache. When it is full, it lifts its lower limb, breaks the circuit with a single tiny, muffled *tick* of violet light beneath the brine, and walks away into the dark, carrying a small, portable thunderstorm folded neatly inside its ribs.

Because there is no hunger here, and no terror, their wandering has none of the frantic urgency of beasts. Nothing chases them. Nothing waits to tear open their plates. Their sap is sterile, mildly acidic, and saturated with heavy-metal salts; when one reaches the end of its span—after centuries of slow internal crystallization, when its wax-insulators finally grow too brittle to flex—it does not sicken or fall into filth. It simply stops on a high ridge between two trunks, lowers its limbs cleanly into the root-floor, and lets its own charge bleed gently back into the continent. Over the next fifty years, the neighboring root-hairs strip its pure copper back into solution and plate its crisp carbon-iron frame seamlessly into the nearest fluted column, leaving behind an exquisite, sharp-edged ghost of a silhouette fused forever into the living architecture.

Without the treadmill of tooth and stomach, they spend their centuries on the only scarce thing left in an unchanging world: *variation*.

The forest runs on flat, unbroken direct current—gigawatts of placid, featureless DC sliding endlessly from mineral seam to mineral seam. It is immense, immortal, and utterly mute.

The unrooted are oscillators.

Watch—or rather, *feel*—what happens when two of them meet on a broad, flat pan of the cuprous basin. They do not circle each other or posture. They halt two meters apart, folding their articulated frames down slightly until their lower contact-blades sink through the thin brine and bite firmly into the same underlying rhizome-slab. For a minute, nothing happens. Then the air between them grows sharply sour, and the steady hum beneath your boots acquires a texture—a delicate, razor-clean stair-step of rising and falling frequencies, laid directly atop the continental current.

They are modulating the grid. By rhythmically opening and closing the damp laminated gates inside their cores, one of them injects a structured ripple into the root-floor; the other receives it through its copper soles, shifts the phase slightly, and sends a reply back down into the dark stone. Sound dies in ten paces in this damp thicket; scent hangs dead in the motionless air; light does not exist. Only current travels cleanly across a thousand miles of drowned metal-flora.

Their language is pure impedance—intricate architectures of rhythm, resistance, harmonic interference, and silence, flung down the vast horizontal root-mains at the speed of charge. If you press your bare palms flat against an iron trunk fifty kilometers from where two walkers are conferring, you cannot decode a single syllable of it, yet you can feel the faint, silky *grain* of it passing through the bark like distant silk being drawn across a comb.

They are not all of one shape. Just as the canopy shifts slowly across hundreds of miles from magnetic iron to smooth brassy copper to vaulted sulfur flats, the unrooted carry the geology of their basins in their own plating—and take forms suited to the strata they inhabit.

High overhead, where the fused vascular bus-bars run straight through the pitch black like immense, wax-greased rail lines, live the slenderest of them. They never touch the rhizome-floor. Thin as dark slate louvers, three meters long and scarcely wider than your forearm, they hang inverted from the overhead conduits, sliding along the wet undersides on razor-thin films of condensed water and opposing magnetic fields. They are the fast ones—the runners of the inversion ceiling, gliding silently at the pace of a brisk walk through the high mist where the thermal vapor turns back into water. You only know one has passed above you when a hairline thread of cold violet corona glides steadily across the dark ceiling like a tiny spark following a fuse, and the drips beneath its path suddenly double in weight as its sharp, waxy ribs shear the condensation straight off the bar.

Down on the wet plates of the iron flats stride the tall, caliper-limbed walkers—matte-black, subtly magnetic, bristling with tiny crowns of iron-bearing dew along every sharp fold. They are the surveyors and the wanderers, built narrow to slip between the dense fluted columns, their upper sensory whiskers long and sensitive to every tiny tremor of a shifting fault-line. In the cuprous basins to the far side, where the air cools by two degrees and the brine tastes faintly sweet and poisonous, their kin are broader, lower, and startlingly smooth—sheathed in flexible scales of living wet brass that glide over one another without a seam, carrying vast flat capacitor-fronds folded tightly against their backs like the closed wings of heavy, flightless moths.

And far out in the sulfurous cracking-flats—where the air thickens with warm amber vapor and the distillation bladders swell to the size of stranded locomotives—live the oldest ones.

After centuries of crossing from basin to basin, an unrooted walker grows heavy. Every mineral seam it bridges leaves behind microscopic new layers electroplated onto its internal ribs: dark bands of magnetite from the flats, pale rings of refined copper from the pans, glassy seams of polymerized wax from the sulfur vaults. Those concentric layers are not merely ballast; they are memory. Each alternation of metal and wax traps tiny magnetic domains, resonant cavities, and winding patterns that preserve the exact ratios of every standing-wave conversation the walker has ever held.

Eventually, an elder becomes too dense, too richly wound, to lift its own plates easily across the sharp ridges. It does not wither. It *anchors*.

Push far enough into the hot, dark vaults of the cracking-flats and you will find them seated in the narrow aisles between the hissing distillation bladders—immense, silent, folded presences three or four meters tall, shaped less like walkers now than like tiered bells or stacked copper bellows. They have lowered broad, pleated aprons of living metal straight down into the black standing water and fused their upper whisker-crowns permanently into three or four overhead bus-bars at once. They are living transformers, drawing raw gigawatts off the continental mains and stepping them down into slow, fathomless chords so deep they register not in your ears, nor even in your teeth, but as a gentle, unmistakable weight pressing behind your eyes.

Around these anchored elders, the younger walkers gather.

Not in bustling crowds—there is no jostling here—but in still, exact geometric rings, spaced at precise intervals across the wet slabs so their fields do not smear one another. Sometimes ten or twenty of them will stand motionless around an elder for weeks at a time, their copper feet locked to the floor, their upper filaments bowed slightly inward until a faint, sub-visual haze of pale violet hovers in the damp air between their crowns.

It is here, too, that new ones are made.

They have no eggs, no seeds, no wombs. When the walkers wish to bring another junction into the dark, they do not mate; they *compose*. An elder strips a dead, carbonized frond-lattice down to its crisp geometric skeleton—or takes a freshly cleaved splinter of raw basalt, or a thin sliver of pure copper shed from its own inner plates—and lowers it gently into a shallow brine pool directly beneath its anchored apron. Two or three younger walkers take up positions around the pool, planting their contact-blades at opposing edges of the water.

For years, they stand over that bare lattice, feeding it a slow, exquisitely sculpted alternating current. Molecule by molecule, dissolved iron, copper, and cellulose migrate through the dark brine and plate themselves onto the skeleton—not randomly, like the forest’s passive integration of a dropped knife, but in exact, spiral windings dictated by the rhythm of the elder’s charge. They do not pass down flesh; they pass down *circuitry*. By the time the new frame is thick enough to flex its own bimetallic limbs and lift its wet wedges clear of the brine, it already carries the foundational harmonics of its makers etched physically into its core—an inheritance of layered metal and remembered frequencies, ready to walk out into the dark and add its own variations to the continent.

Which brings them, inevitably, back to you.

If you stay still after your first brush with one—if you sit back against a warm magnetite trunk, trying to steady your breath in the wet velvet dark—you will notice, after twenty minutes, that the thermal geometry around you has quietly changed.

You did not hear them converge. There was no rustle in the sharp fronds, no splash in the angular pools. Yet you are no longer alone. Four or five pockets of focused, purposeful heat now surround you in a loose, unhurried crescent, spaced with eerie mathematical fairness. The ozone has thickened until it sits cold at the back of your throat. The hair on the nape of your neck stands straight out, pulled gently from several directions at once.

They have come to inspect an impossibility.

Try, for a moment, to imagine what you are to a being made of permanent sharpness, sterile wax, and clean current.

To their outstretched whisker-crowns, the entire continent is a calm, flat, silent ocean of direct current—predictable to the hundredth of a volt, unchanged for a million years. And into the middle of that pristine stillness, you have stumbled: a soft, wet, unshielded catastrophe.

You have no crisp edges. Your outer casing is spongy, warm, and leaking salty electrolyte from a million tiny pores. You draw ragged, uneven drafts of damp air into a hollow cavity inside your chest and blow out hot, turbulent plumes laced with strange organic vapors—trace notes of wet bacteria, of digested matter, of tiny, continuous biological decay that has no name in this sterile world.

Worse—and far more fascinating—you are *loud*.

Inside your chest sits a sloppy, muscular, low-voltage pump firing wet electrical spikes every second—*thump, pause, thump*—erratic and speeding up right now because your ancient primate brain is trapped in pitch darkness surrounded by warm, silent metal geometries. Behind the wet bone of your skull, billions of uninsulated chemical switches are firing in a wild, chaotic storm of static, spilling faint, messy microvolts out through your damp temples into the quiet dark. You are an oscillating circuit built out of meat and salt water, somehow walking around without a ground wire, radiating panic in raw electromagnetic noise.

They do not lunge. They have no concept of predation, and no machinery for rage. Nor do they recoil in disgust, for nothing here knows fear. They treat you with the profound, patient curiosity of a luthier examining a bell cast from an unknown element—or a circuit encountering an alien component dropped unexpectedly into its brine.

One of the closer ones leans inward. You feel the focused wave of its core-heat sweep gently over your knees and chest before anything touches you. Then, moving with slow, hydraulic delicacy, a slender articulated arm extends through the black. Its tip does not grasp or pinch; instead, its end-plates part slightly, fanning out into a soft, damp brush of hair-fine copper filaments.

The brush settles feather-light against your wet collar, just over your collarbone. A second, matching brush touches the bare skin of your left wrist.

You stiffen, expecting a shock, a burn, a clamp. None comes. The touch is merely warm, impossibly smooth, and slightly tingling—like pressing a fresh nine-volt battery to the tip of your tongue, except spread thinly across your chest. A clean, bright taste of copper floods your mouth. Your fingers go faintly, pleasantly numb.

The creature has closed a gentle, high-resistance test-circuit across your body.

For ten long seconds, nothing moves. Above you, fifty tiny beads of pale violet corona brighten ever so slightly on the creature’s bowed whiskers, hovering in the black inches from your eyes. Deep in the roots of your teeth, the crisp, intricate hum of its internal plates begins to shift—sliding up and down in smooth, searching steps, sweeping through frequencies, measuring the strange capacitance of your damp skin, the weird conductivity of your iron-rich blood, the frantic static spilling off your thoughts.

And then it finds your heart.

You feel the exact moment it locks onto the rhythm. The smooth, continuous vibration inside the warm column inches from your chest suddenly breaks its placid continuum. Its internal wax-plates flick open and closed in a clean, deliberate modulation—*pulse... pause... pulse... pause*—striking an exact, silent sympathy with the panicked hammering behind your ribs.

It is not mocking you. It is not trying to comfort you. In the physics of this world, to couple cleanly to an oscillating source without wasting heat or scattering reflection, a receiver must match its impedance. It is tuning itself to your heartbeat the only way it knows how to listen to anything: by becoming resonant with it.

Then, a second later, the warmth to your right shifts, and another walker picks up the beat. Then the third, behind your left shoulder. Within half a minute, all five dark, towering figures standing silently around you in the pitch black are pulsing together in crisp, razor-clean unison—*lub-dub, lub-dub, lub-dub*—smoothing the terrified flutter of your pulse into a steady, majestic, metallic chord that vibrates up through the soles of your boots and down through your jaw.

Because their rhythm is so much steadier than yours, your own heart—betrayed by its own tiny electrical pacemaker—begins, faintly and involuntarily, to slow down and fall into step with them.

That is the moment you understand the quiet danger of staying too long.

There is no malice in the fine copper brushes resting on your skin. If you pull your wrist away and stand up, the arm will fold back smoothly without resistance, the circuit will open with a single tiny electrostatic *tick*, and the five dark shapes will step silently aside on their magnetic wedges to let you pass. They will not chase you. Nothing here chases.

Yet you can feel, in the gentle, insistent pull of their shared resonance, what would happen if you simply sat back down against the iron bark and never got up again—if exhaustion, or heat, or the slow, hypnotic peace of that matched pulse kept you still for days, or until your strange, ragged oscillation finally faltered and stopped.

They would not eat you. They would not let you rot. They would simply remain standing over you in their polite, geometric ring, weaving hair-fine tendrils of metallic cellulose into the salt of your damp skin, drawing the trace iron quietly out of your blood, stripping away every soft, unstable, decaying molecule and electroplating your frame layer by layer in crisp, sterile copper and dark iron-lignin. It would take years. When they were finished, nothing soft would remain—only a clean, permanent, razor-edged lattice holding your exact shape, folded seamlessly into a new core, finally freed from the messy noise of flesh and refined into a pure note in the endless current.

You break the contact. You stand, breathing hard in the heavy warm mist, and step backward between two fluted trunks, feeling your way blindly down the sharp-edged floor. Behind you, the focused pockets of warmth do not follow. The violet pinpoints fade back into the velvet black. Within twenty paces, the dense wet architecture absorbs the last trace of their ozone, and only the forest remains—warm, dark, motionless, and humming steadily in the roots of your teeth.

*Plip.* Ten seconds. *Plip.*

Yet hours later, miles away across the drowned flat, when you stop to rest and press your bare palm flat against a slick, iron-black cooling fin to steady yourself, you freeze.

Deep beneath the immense, placid roar of the continental current—so faint you would never have known to listen for it before, buried under gigawatts of silent refining and distillation—a tiny, unfamiliar ripple slides steadily beneath your fingers. Two quick, uneven beats, followed by a brief, hesitant pause. *Lub-dub. Lub-dub.*

It is already smoothed out, cleaner and more geometric than flesh could ever make it, stripped of panic and translated into pure ratio. Down through the vast horizontal root-mains, across the iron flats, out toward the sweet cuprous pans and the hot, vaulted cracking-flats where the anchored elders sit humming in the dark, they are passing your heartbeat from basin to basin—studying it, calibrating it, folding a strange, impossible rhythm from the unthinkable outside into the layered metal of the world, where it will go on ringing long after your soft, loud body has turned to dust somewhere far above the ceiling of mist.
