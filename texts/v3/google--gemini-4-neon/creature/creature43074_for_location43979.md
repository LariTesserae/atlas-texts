If you crossed the plateau for a week, keeping to the drowned divides and the high whale-backed domes, you could easily come away believing that nothing larger than the shaggy grazers endures up here. The scale of the water swallows small movements; the wind flattens every silhouette into the streaming green; and whatever lives permanently on this roof of the world has learned the first law of the Precambrian stone—never offer the gale a vertical edge it can get hold of.

You usually notice them only when a boulder shifts its weight against the current.

What looked like a slick, pewter-colored rise of gneiss in the middle of a knee-deep spillway—its broad crest furred along the upstream seam with olive liverwort and trailing threads of pale beard-lichen—lifts its head an inch out of the sheet-flow. Water breaks in a clean silver vee across a blunt, wide-nostriled muzzle. Two dark eyes, shielded behind translucent inner lids against the horizontal rain, track a flash of highland char throttling up the submerged lip. Then a broad, dark-nailed hand reaches out, plants itself flat on the algae-filmed stone without a hint of slip, and the whole mass glides forward, belly skimming the bottom, leaving nothing on the surface but a brief swirl that the wind erases in two seconds.

They are heavy, low-slung people of the sheet-flow—roughly the bulk of a large bear, though longer in the limb and leaner through the haunch, built with the dense, unhurried geometry of things that expect to be pushed by water their whole lives. They rarely stand upright. In a place where a sudden squall can turn a quiet flat into a waist-deep surge in twenty minutes, height is a liability and buoyancy is a trap. Their skeletons are thick and nearly solid, heavy with mineral ballast like the bones of deep-diving sea creatures, so that when they step into a rushing chute they do not bob or fight for balance; they simply sink to the stone and walk along the bed, letting the tea-stained current stream over their shoulders while they move with calm, deliberate strides.

Their four limbs end in wide, five-fingered hands and broad, splayed feet whose thick, leathery soles are crossed with fine, horny ridges. Pressed against wet quartzite or slick gneiss, those pads grip like wet rubber on glass. Over the hard muscle and dense layer of cold-insulating oil lies a close, velvety pelt the exact shade of wet pewter—so tightly packed that runoff sheets off their flanks in bright curtains, and when a shaft of sun breaks through the cloud-deck, faint plumes of vapor curl off their backs as if they were warm rocks. Only along the broad shelf of the spine, the thick nape of the neck, and the crowns of their heads do the guard-hairs grow coarser and wiry. Up here, anything damp and patient enough eventually becomes soil: tiny rosettes of emerald liverwort, fine algae, and pale tufts of beard-lichen root permanently in that dorsal coat, giving the older ones a soft, streaming mane of living plateau. When they rest half-submerged in a flooded sedge-flat, water-dippers sometimes land directly on their backs to bob and whistle over the spray before diving off their shoulders into the rush.

Above the surface, they seem almost eerily quiet. To shout up here is useless—the steady chest-deep roar of the thirty-meter chutes, the hiss of rain, and the shriek of wind through miles of sedge shred any airborne voice before it travels ten paces. Their language does not live in the air at all. It lives in the stone.

Because the ancient range was planed down to one continuous, unfractured slab of gneiss and quartzite, with no loose talus or broken cliffs to scatter vibration, the bedrock acts like the soundboard of an immense, drowned instrument. When one of them wants to listen—or speak—it lowers its wide chest, its heavy forearms, and the broad, oil-filled bone of its lower jaw flat against a smoothed rock dome, letting the runoff sheet calmly over its back. Instantly, the chaotic white noise of the sky vanishes. Through the stone, the plateau becomes lucid, structured, and vast.

Pressed to the gneiss, they can read the whole watershed at once: the deep, steady boom of a spillway four valleys west; the sudden, tightening tremor that signals a storm-front dumping its weight on the upper catchment twenty minutes before the floodwave arrives; the muffled, rhythmic scuff of a herd of broad-hoofed grazers wading through a distant sedge basin; the dull thump of a torn peat-raft grounding on a quartzite shelf a mile away. And woven through that hydraulic grammar are their own voices—slow, resonant bass pulses generated deep in their barrelled, high-altitude chests and poured directly into the bedrock through sternum and jaw. A single sustained note, dropped below the rumble of the waterfalls, carries cleanly through the shield for miles. To hold a conversation across a flooded basin requires no raised head, no wasted breath against the wind—only two dark shapes pressed belly-down on opposite domes of wet stone, answering one another in slow chords while the horizontal rain drives unheeded overhead.

Closer up, in the murky, peat-stained water beneath the matted roots, they rely on a second sense. Their broad muzzles and the fleshy heels of their palms are pitted with sensitive electroreceptors—evolved over ages to read the faint bioelectric flicker of char and trout tucked beneath undercut peat ledges, or the slow pulse of mottled salamanders clinging under drowned stones. Even in the thickest cloud-bank, when visibility drops to three paces and the sky turns to solid moving grey, they navigate the braided channels by the feel of the current against their flanks, the low map humming in the stone beneath their soles, and the faint electric shimmer of living things in the dark water.

Without fire, without dry wood, and without a single loose rock to chip or stack, their craft is entirely a craft of living things and moving water.

With their thick, deft fingers, they strip the tough inner bark from gnarled, wind-planed dwarf heath and braid it underwater with long strands of dark sedge and water-crowfoot, twisting dense, slick cords that only tighten when soaked. They cannot build walls of stone, so they weave shelters out of the plateau itself. In the lee of a broad gneiss swell or tucked deep into an undercut peat bank, a family group will bend the springy, moss-sleeved limbs of stunted evergreen scrub into a low, overlapping vault, lashing the crooked wood together with sedge-rope and packing the frame a meter thick with torn slabs of living sphagnum—chartreuse, olive, and iron-rust. Because frost rarely locks the highland down, the moss never dies; within a single season it knits itself seamlessly into the surrounding carpet, rooting straight through the woven wood until the dwelling becomes one more pulsing, waterlogged cushion on the margin of the basin.

The entrance lies below the waterline, or hidden behind a curtain of combed-flat rush. Inside, the chamber is never truly dry—dryness is an alien, faintly uncomfortable state to them, something that makes their nostrils itch and their skin pull tight—yet it is dead still, sealed completely from the tearing gale. The air inside smells intensely of wet peat, crushed evergreen, fish-oil, and the warm, clean musk of damp pelts. Mottled black-and-ochre salamanders winter in the mossy crevices of the domed ceiling, unbothered. When a three-day maritime gale turns the open flats into thrashing white chop, four or five of them curl tight together in that dark-green belly of moss, their slow, deep breaths fogging the cramped air, raising the warmth of the chamber ten degrees above the freezing-adjacent runoff pouring over the roof.

Out in the open basins, they hunt like living topography. A lone individual can snatch a silver-sided trout straight out of a sheet-flow with a whip-fast sweep of one wide hand, or pry a drowned grazer's heavy bone from the silt to scrape it smooth against quartzite into a blunt weaving-awl. More often, they work in loose, silent pairs or small clusters of six or eight, coordinating through short, sharp taps of a knuckle against the bedrock. Three of them will wade into the throat of a flooded spillway and plant themselves side by side across the smooth rock lip, bellies braced against the push of the current, becoming a living weir. The water backs up, splits, and funnels violently through the narrow gap they leave on the leeward side—and the heavy char fighting their way upstream are swept straight into the waiting arms of two others anchored in the quiet eddy below. They eat cleanly and heavily, crushing the fish against wet domes of stone with the same unhurried efficiency as the sleek aquatic fishers, leaving the picked spines for the storm-petrels and gulls that wheel down through the spray the moment they move on.

They do not keep permanent villages, any more than the braided spillways keep permanent beds. Their society moves in the same loose, shifting geometry as the water: small family clusters—usually two or three adults, an elder whose dorsal lichen hangs in pale, foot-long banners, and one or two smooth-backed young—drifting across three or four neighboring catchments over the course of a season, meeting other groups along the great central divides, parting again when the rains shift. When two groups cross paths in a flooded valley, there is no shouting over the wind; they wade close until the current foams between their chests, press brow to brow and throat to shoulder, and exchange a quiet, rumbling vibration straight from bone to bone, tasting the news of the upper basins in the cadence of each other's hum. Then they often sit half-submerged together in a quiet tarn, grooming one another's backs with blunt nails—teasing out dead peat, tangles of rush, and clinging parasites while carefully leaving the healthy emerald sleeves of liverwort intact.

When the big squalls hit the upper plateau and the water rises half a meter in twenty minutes, tearing loose wide, buoyant rafts of living peat and sodden moss-mat, they do not hide from the flood. They ride it. An entire family will haul themselves onto a drifting island of sphagnum ten paces across, shifting their dense weight toward one edge or the other, trailing a broad hind-foot in the current as a rudder, steering the quivering green raft miles downwind across the flooded basins without burning a fraction of the energy it would take to wade. Sometimes, when the cloud-deck tears open mid-squall and hard shafts of sunlight race across the drowning valleys, turning the falling rain to blown glass, the younger ones abandon the raft altogether—throwing themselves belly-down into the broad whitewater chutes, arms tucked tight to their sides, letting the sheet-flow shoot them thirty meters down the polished gneiss lips into the churning foam-drifts below, surfacing through trembling chunks of white froth just to haul themselves up the margin and do it again.

They have lived like this for longer than the peat has been deep. And then, within the memory of the oldest elders, the plateau grew a new kind of weather—made of poured concrete, cast iron, and humming wire.

The years when the first machines were built must have been an agony in the stone. The scream of rock-drills biting into the ancient gneiss to sink the iron anchor-plates would have travelled through the bedrock for twenty miles in every direction, a jagged, blinding noise that drove every family off the central domes and deep into the quietest outer marshes. For months, the sky shook with the harsh, chopping roar of heavy aircraft hauling riveted steel and turbine housings out of the mist.

Yet once the drills fell silent, and the first thick black penstock filled with the weight of an upper basin, something extraordinary happened to the stone.

A new note entered the plateau.

Unlike the waterfalls, which swelled and faded with every passing squall, the turbines bolted into the Precambrian shield poured out a single, immaculate, unvarying bass hum—a mechanical heartbeat so steady and deep that it tuned the bedrock for miles around every powerhouse. Cautiously, over several wet seasons, the dwellers crept back to the smooth granite aprons outside the low, windowless steel buildings. They pressed their jaws and forearms to the concrete, expecting the jagged pain of the drills, and found instead a vast, warm, resonant song that did not stop.

Now, the scattered machines are woven seamlessly into their accounting of the highland.

On the worst days, when cold sleet mixes into the horizontal rain and the wind combs the sedge flat for forty-eight hours without a break, you might find half a dozen of them wedged tight shoulder-to-shoulder against the leeward wall of a powerhouse, drinking the faint, dry warmth bleeding through the riveted steel plates, letting the dense industrial thrum vibrate straight through their ribs into their sleep. The elders bring the young to the granite aprons to teach them breath and resonance: pressed flat to the slick apron, a young dweller learns to pitch its own chest-hum against the unseen turbine inside, dropping a fraction of a tone lower until the two frequencies beat slowly against one another, sending rolling interference ripples out across the puddles on the stone.

They have learned the machines' habits with the same precision they once gave solely to the clouds. Half a minute before an automated sluice-gate trips open, the turbine's note in the rock tightens—a subtle, rising strain that no human ear could catch over the wind, but which rings unmistakably in a jaw pressed to gneiss. At that faint shift in pitch, any dweller resting near the powerhouse slips quietly into the water and moves below the tailrace. When the sharp pneumatic hiss and heavy iron clang shake the valley, and a violent wall of whitewater flushes down the chute, they are already anchored in the lower eddies, waiting to scoop up the thick shoals of stunned, disoriented char swept helplessly over the lip.

Out across the open basins, the straight line of galvanized steel pylons marching from horizon to horizon has become their compass. Because of the sensitive electroreceptors along their muzzles and palms, they do not need to see a pylon through the grey rain-curtains to know where it stands. In the drenched highland air, the faint blue corona fizzing and snapping off the ceramic insulators sends a bright, prickling aura fifty paces out into the mist—a sharp, copper-tasting meridian that cuts straight across the deceptive, multi-valley distances. When a fog-bank swallows the plateau whole, travelers simply follow the electric tingle from whale-backed dome to whale-backed dome, never losing their bearing in the braided flats.

They stop at the concrete pylon footings as waystations. Pressed upright for a rare moment against a galvanized steel leg—which conducts vibration cleanly down from the heavy cables singing their resonant metallic chord in the crosswind—a traveler can listen to the wind-state of the next two valleys at once. Before moving on, they often knot a small braid of dark sedge, a tuft of iron-rust moss, or a notched char-bone onto the lowest steel cross-brace. To a distant eye, it looks like windblown debris snagged by a gale. To the next family passing through the mist, the tightness of the weave, the scent of oil still lingering in the fibers, and the height of the knot on the strut tell a quiet story: who passed, which way they went, whether a channel down-basin has abandoned its bed, or whether the loud flying machines have been out over the ridges.

Stranger still is their patience with the solitary gauge shacks perched above the flood-line. In a world of pewter, silver, luminous green, and blinding, manic bursts of white sunlight, the single amber warning lamp glowing dry and calm behind its cage is the only steady, warm point of light on the entire plateau. During the long, milky dusks, when the cloud-deck dims to bruised slate, one or two of them will haul themselves up onto the concrete weir beside a corrugated-iron shack and sit motionless in the pouring rain, watching that small amber eye. Sometimes one leans forward, presses its wide, ridged palm flat against the fogged glass port to wipe a clear circle, and stares intently at the brass-and-steel water-level recorder ticking steadily inside, its inked needle tracing the endless accounting of the flood. Now and then they answer the clockwork with soft, rhythmic clicks of their teeth against their palates, mimicking the dry mechanical pulse behind the glass.

They almost never let the maintenance crews see them. The jagged, hammering roar of a helicopter dropping through the cloud-deck or the grinding shriek of a ridge-track vehicle fills both air and bedrock with a noise they find deeply ugly. Long before a crew sets boots on a concrete apron, the dwellers have already slipped beneath the tea-stained surface, tucking themselves under undercut peat ledges or lying dead still in the drowned meadows, where their lichen-furred backs dissolve instantly into the streaming green.

Yet they leave quiet traces that the older, sharper-eyed technicians have learned not to put in the official logs. A circular, five-fingered smear wiped clean on the outside of a gauge-shack port, higher than any grazer's nose. A neat, impossibly tight knot of braided sedge tied around a pylon strut three miles from any landing pad. And most often, around the flat-bottomed aluminum work-skiffs chained to iron mooring-rings in the sheltered tarns: thick, tightly woven wads of living sphagnum and twisted heath-bark stuffed carefully between the iron chain and the aluminum gunwale, or lashed around the anchor-post.

The crews sometimes assume the last shift packed the chains to stop wear. They do not realize that an unpadded aluminum hull slapping erratically against wet gneiss in a crosswind sends a sharp, hollow, disharmonious rattle ringing through the unbroken bedrock for half a mile—an irritation to anyone trying to sleep or listen in the basin below. Rather than cut the chain or break the boat, the dwellers simply muffle it, folding the loud human thing gently back into the plateau's rhythm.

Still, once in a rare while, a technician stays overnight during a heavy turbine overhaul, when the main penstock valve is cranked shut and the great steel rotor inside the powerhouse finally coasts to a dead stop. Stepping out onto the granite apron in the grey rain, the engineer expects the sudden, heavy absence of the engine to leave only the roar of the spillways and the hiss of the wind. Instead, standing very still, they feel something faint and slow rising up through the thick rubber soles of their boots—a low, living bass pulse, deeper than any waterfall, swelling from somewhere three valleys out across the flooded stone, waiting patiently to hear if the iron heart will answer back.
