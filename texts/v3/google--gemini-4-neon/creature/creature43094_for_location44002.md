At first, if you spot one of them along a jutting rib of basalt, you do not recognize a human shape at all. You see only another dry, segmented thing clinging to the raw rock—low-slung, rust-and-ochre, crusted with pale gypsum powder and bristling with dull horn plates. Only when it straightens against the wind, braces a boot against a hammered copper spike, and hauls a resin-sealed gourd hand-over-hand out of a churning canyon of cane do the joints resolve into shoulders, elbows, and scarred, deliberate hands.

They are not natives of this valley, because nothing human could have had time to become native to a wound this fresh. They came down into the roaring split from the older, quieter, wetter caverns leagues above—miners, seam-runners, debt-dodgers, and second sons following the first tremor that cracked the crust open—and stayed because a fresh tectonic rent pours out wealth that the cold dark above has never seen. Light that does not smoke. Oils that burn like bottled lightning. Hollow timber that never mildews. Leather lighter than woven cloth.

They live in knots of four to seven—never larger, because few bare rock ledges can anchor more than a single bakehouse dome, and no water-train can keep a crowd alive down here. Within a single generation, the valley has remade their bodies, their habits, and the very shape of their language.

You see it first in what covers them. No square inch of skin is left to the kiln-dry air, because sweat here does not bead; it flashes straight to salt, stealing a person’s water before they even feel their throat tighten. They wrap themselves in layered coats of pleated glider-membrane—featherlight, windproof, rustling faintly like parchment—and lace overlapping dorsal plates from shed giant borers over their forearms, shins, and chests. Without the shell-armor, a snapping silica leaf or a wind-hurled seed-burr opens a leg to the bone. Their eyes hide behind narrow slit-visors whittled from dark slate, the skin beneath their lashes smeared heavy with charcoal and tallow to cut the flat, shadowless white glare of the ceiling. Their lips are perpetually greased with thick amber resin-wax so they do not crack open and bleed when they grimace.

Even their hair obeys the valley. Left loose, it stands out in a crackling, spore-clotted halo that catches every flying burr. Most shave their skulls bare or plaster their hair flat with bitter wax and braid thin filaments of hammered copper directly into the roots.

Copper is the quiet seam running through everything they wear. Because friction here charges a body like a thundercloud—and a single stray arc near a punctured gourd of volatile cane-oil turns a harvester into a torch—every worker wears a *bleed-tail*: a braided strip of copper foil and wire stitched down the coat seam and trailing from one heel or wrist so it scrapes against bare basalt whenever they step off the living wicker.

Before two harvesters hand off a sealed canister, or pass each other on a three-trunk walkway swinging above a gas vent, they touch their copper-ringed knuckles together. *Snap.* A sharp blue spark jumps the gap, equalizing their charge. It began as survival, but it has already hardened into custom. They tap knuckles when waking, when parting, when agreeing on a trade. Lovers press copper bracelets together before touching faces, sparing each other the sting of a charged lip.

When they want to speak, they do not bother with the air. The air belongs to the valley; a shout dies a hand’s breadth from the mouth and shreds a dry throat in minutes.

Across the basin, they speak with wind and stone. The charcoal-dyed flags snapping from high flexible poles carry a brisk, angular code—*vent blowing, borer beneath, ridge yielding, water-runner down*—read by squinting through the haze of luminous chaff. When a message needs precision, a crew’s Knocker kneels on an exposed vein of solid basalt with the antler mallet and hammers slow, clean phrases straight into the bedrock. The others do not listen with their ears—those are stopped tight behind bulky knuckle-bone guards stuffed with bast-fiber. They slip a heel from a sandal or press the outer edge of a bare forearm flat against the stone and feel the clean low thrums travel up the ulna. You can spot a veteran of the split anywhere by the thick, slate-gray calluses running down the sides of their forearms, and by a habit they never lose: before trusting any ledge, any doorway, or any person, they lay a palm flat on the floor.

Up close, in the howling drafts of a platform or the clatter of a cane-cut, they use *bridges*—short, flattened splinters of dense, solid root-wood carried on leather thongs around the neck. Two harvesters lean in until their slate visors nearly touch. One presses the flat end of the stick against their own throat or cheekbone; the other clamps the notched tip lightly between their front teeth. A low hum, a rapid cadence of tongue-clicks, a clipped throat-syllable—the vibration skips the screaming air entirely and rings clean inside the listener’s skull. Watching from ten paces off, it looks startlingly intimate: two dust-caked, shell-armored figures leaning brow to brow in a storm of flying husks, grinning around a splinter of wood as they argue over a rope knot or pass along a bitter joke.

They do not conquer the valley; they pick its pockets and ride its seizures.

Every crew has its own rhythm of scavenging, tuned to the frantic violence around them. They do not hunt the giant borers—they *follow* them. Pressing an ear-bridge to the basalt, a listener tracks the slow, heavy millstone-grind of a borer chewing a fresh seam of gypsum and slate beneath the roots. Where the great segmented beast surfaces to molt, the crew darts in to haul away its shed dorsal plates before smaller scavengers chew the edges, and sifts the warm mineral volcanoes it throws up for raw flakes of native copper and clear crystal.

They move across the heaving cane-sea with a strange, loose-kneed bounce, never putting their full weight on any woven mat until they feel whether a gas-bladder is swelling beneath it. With curved saws edged in knapped black obsidian, they score the hollow pressurized stalks just lightly enough to bleed off the hissing sulfur-sweet gas before hacking them free for resin-tapping or timber. When a six-legged grazer darts across a drying-rack to steal a salt-stained leather thong, they do not waste arrows against the chaotic updrafts; they flick a weighted length of braided copper wire across its clay-colored back. Grounded all at once, the grazer’s static defense vanishes in a hiss of blue sparks, leaving the confused creature skittering wildly on bare rock long enough to be clubbed for dry, nut-flavored meat.

Even the pale, ambush-hung basket-nets have a place in their ledger. A bold harvester—usually one of the youngest, still quick enough to outrun a recoil—leans from a raw shale rib with a twelve-foot pole daubed in sticky resin and deliberately plucks a single taut vibration-line. The basket slams shut on empty air with the crack of a broken beam, tangling its dry spines in its own fury. While it thrashes and reels itself upward, the rest of the crew sprints across the narrow cane-canyon below—or, if the net has already caught a diving glider, they hook its rim with ironwood gaffs and slash the trapped leather free before the choking digestive dust ruins the wings.

From the gliders themselves, they take more than coats. When a weighted sinew bola brings one tumbling out of a thermal column, the harvesters carefully cut away the rigid, pleated slits along its hollow chest, cure them dry in resin-smoke, and lash them to tall poles around their shelters. Facing into the valley, the dead glider-throats stay silent in ordinary gusts—but when a subterranean gas-bladder tears open or a tall dust-devil spins straight toward the ridge, the sudden pressure shift makes them shriek at a piercing, metallic pitch that cuts straight through bone ear-guards and sends every sleeping hand diving for the copper anchor-loops.

Because nothing here stays where you put it. A bakehouse dome is never a home; it is a temporary blister glued to rock that might split by morning. Every tool hangs on slip-knots. Every pack sits pre-lashed beside the low crawl-tunnel so a knot of six people can cut their shelter loose, abandon their windbreak to a surging wall of clicking pale shoots, and scramble forty yards up a steeper basalt rib in under a minute. They measure history not in years, but in lost ledges: *that happened two domes back, before the gypsum shelf tilted.*

And yet, crawl inside one of those dark, resin-plastered shells when a shift ends, and you see what truly holds them together.

There is no night in the valley. The luminous ceiling-curtains burn with the same flat, dust-white noon hour after hour, until a human mind frays at the edges from the sheer refusal of the world to dim. So the domes are built as engines of darkness—windowless, low-roofed, entered through a crooked tunnel baffled with double curtains of heavy glider-leather so not a sliver of white glare leaks through.

Inside, the roar of the valley drops to a dull, distant surf pulsing through the stone floor. It is stiflingly warm, smelling of scorched cane, warm amber resin, bitter axle-grease, and the dry, coppery tang of unwashed skin. When the harvesters unlace their borer-shell aprons, slip off their slate visors, and pry free the bulky bone ear-guards, nobody moves for five full minutes. They simply sit hunched on woven pith mats in the pitch dark, massaging their aching jaws, waiting for the phantom high-pitched whine inside their skulls to ebb.

Then comes the water.

There is no water anywhere in the basin, so every drop is hauled down through treacherous vertical chimneys from the cold, weeping fault-lines far above the rift by relay runners who are paid in glowing ceiling-dust and prime volatile oil. Water is kept in double-seamed bladders tucked in the coolest shadow against the rear basalt wall, guarded more fiercely than copper. When the bladder is passed around the dark circle, nobody gulps. Each person takes a single measured mouthful, holds it against parched gums until the dryness eases, and swallows it a trickle at a time before re-greasing their lips. Nobody spits—not here, not ever. To spit on the ground in front of a valley-hand is the closest thing they have to a declaration of blood feud.

In that thick, hot dark, stripped of armor and noise, they are startlingly gentle with one another. Without the glare and without speech, touch becomes the whole room. They check each other’s backs and shoulders by fingertip for silica splinters—razor-thin slivers of plant-glass that work deeper into muscle if left behind—drawing them out with fine bone tweezers by the faint, pale glow of a single uncorked thimble of ceiling-dust. When they sleep, they do not stretch out apart; they pile together on the pith mats, shoulder to spine, knee tucked behind knee, so that even half-deafened and exhausted, each person can feel another chest rising and falling against their ribs.

And nearly every one of them keeps one secret, useless weight sewn into an inner pocket: a smooth stone.

Nothing in the newly split valley has had time to learn how to be round. The shale cuts; the gypsum grates; the leaves are serrated mica; the very dust feels like ground glass. So when a harvester trades up in the wet outer tunnels, they pick a small, river-worn pebble from a streambed—ordinary grey limestone or dark chert polished slick by centuries of water—and carry it back down into the kiln. In the manufactured night of the dome, before sleep takes them, you can hear the faint, rhythmic whisper of callused thumbs turning those smooth pebbles over and over in the dark, reminding their skin that soft curves still exist somewhere beyond the roar.

Stay down in the split for five or six seasons, and the valley writes itself permanently into your bones. The older hands—the ones the crews call the *Stone-Eared*—eventually lose the high edge of the world altogether. The razor-whine of the brassy insect clouds fades out first, then the shrieking dives of the gliders, then finally the thin sound of human voices speaking through open air. They do not treat this as a tragedy. A Stone-Eared elder is the calmest person on any swaying platform; while younger harvesters still flinch when a seed-pod detonates behind their heels, the elders do not blink unless the bedrock shivers beneath their soles.

When a crew finally hauls its laden gourds of luminous powder, its bundles of hollow whistling-reeds, and its cured rolls of glider-leather back up through the narrow cracks to the damp, dim trading caverns above, everyone there knows them on sight.

They walk too lightly, knees bent as if expecting solid stone to heave beneath them. They talk too loud or barely at all, tapping fingers unconsciously against wooden tables to test the grain. They reach out and rap their knuckles against copper lanterns before stepping through doorways, waiting for a spark that never comes. Most of all, they cannot stand the wet. After ten minutes in a dripping upper tunnel, the slow, cold *plip-plop* of condensation makes their necks prickle; the smell of wet moss and rotting timber hits their noses like a cellar full of corpses, sluggish and unclean. They wrap dry leather over their eyes because the flickering tallow lamps cast shifting shadows they no longer trust, and the silence presses against their eardrums like a heavy, suffocating weight.

They trade fast. They refill the heavy water-bladders, buy fresh hammered copper, salt, tallow, and flint, and pick a new smooth pebble if they lost the old one in a shelter-collapse. Before a week is out, their skin feels clammy and heavy, and a restless itch settles behind their teeth.

Then they hoist their packs, climb back down into the narrowing throat of the fracture, and lean into the first blast of furnace-warm wind. As the white glare floods their visors and the vast, clacking, snapping percussion slams back into their ribs, they lace on their bone ear-guards, touch copper-ringed knuckles together until the blue sparks jump, and step back out onto the roaring, dust-bright sea—grinning, electric, and entirely awake in a world too fierce to ever grow still.
