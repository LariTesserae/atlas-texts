You can cross half the basin before you realize you are being watched, and you can cross the rest of it without ever hearing a human voice.

The first signs are easy to mistake for geology or luck. Along the narrow worn tracks that thread between the sinter chimneys, the sharpest salt-lips have been knocked flat with the heel of a hammer so a boot will not slice open on them. On one bank of ceramic insulators, the chalk-wasps have packed their pale mortar thick between every rib; on the next bank half a mile east, the ceramic is scraped clean down to the glaze, with nothing left beneath it on the basalt but a faint dusting of crushed lime. And if you stop to catch your breath on a high travertine rim and watch the shimmering distance long enough, you will eventually see a flash of white sunlight that does not belong to a lizard. A lizard's flash is nervous, needle-thin, gone in a blink as the body pivots. This light holds steady for two slow heartbeats, goes dark, holds again, and then sweeps smoothly across the ochre flats toward a ridge a mile away, where another slow star answers it out of the haze.

The people who live up here do not stand out against the stone, because standing out in four thousand meters of unfiltered light is a way to burn and dry out. When one finally separates from the shadow of a tilted basalt slab, what you see first is cloth the pale, dusty color of dried travertine—loose, heavy-woven linen and felted wool, rubbed with fine white gypsum until it throws back the sun. Wide-brimmed hats, stitched rigid with salted hide and split wire-shrub twigs, cast deep ovals of darkness over their faces. Beneath the brims, many wear narrow eye-slits carved from dark stone or hammered out of dull copper scrap, cutting the brutal glare of the salt pans down to a calm gray band.

Up close, their bodies carry the quiet, unmistakable carpentry of the altitude. They are deep-chested and spare-limbed, built with wide ribcages and slow, heavy pulses. Their skin is weathered to the color of old dark basalt, flushed a deep, coppery red across the cheekbones from blood thick with red cells, and often smeared along the nose and forearms with a chalky paste of brine-mud and ground sinter to blunt the sun. Nobody hurries. In air this thin, hurrying leaves you gasping with your mouth open, and an open mouth loses water you cannot spare. They move with the unhurried, pivoting deliberation of the basin's own creatures—step, pause, settle the weight, check the crust ahead—covering miles of broken ground without ever breaking into a sweat or lifting their shoulders to breathe.

They are few, scattered across the tableland in tiny households of two, three, or five, tucked miles apart wherever a reliable cloud-catchment meets a tilted apron of bedrock. There are no villages, no central square, no roofs rising above the skyline to catch the wind. Their dwellings are grown into the lee of the ancient basalt slabs, half-sunken into the crust and walled in front with loose cobbles wedged tight and mortared over with ground chalk-wasp nest, wetted with brine and baked iron-hard by the sun. From twenty paces off, a home looks like nothing more than another shoulder of volcanic rubble and cemented hive.

Only up close do you see the craft in it. Every roof is doubled: an outer apron of tilted stone slabs laid over an air-gap above an inner ceiling of packed mud and woven mat, shedding the overhead blaze the same way the louvered copper fins shed waste heat from the manifold houses. The doorway is low, angled away from the noon glare and toward the southern drop-off. Inside, the air is dim and surprisingly still, smelling of dry shrub-resin, clean machine oil, smoked salt-meat, and faint sulfur. Along the back wall of every room sits a capped vent—a hairline fissure in the underlying stone fitted with a heavy, hand-ground plug of porous sinter. Through the fierce hours of midday, the plug stays wedged tight, keeping the floor cool; after sunset, when the thin outside air drops sharp and cold in minutes, someone pulls the plug an inch or two, and a slow, dry breath of geothermal warmth rises from the bedrock to hold the dark room steady until dawn.

Water governs the shape of every threshold. Outside each doorway, rigged between two sinter chimneys or slung beneath a rock overhang, hangs a broad, tilted frame strung tight with fine salvaged copper wire and twisted sinew, looking like a loom left out to weather. Twice or three times a day, when the gray shelf of cloud heaves up over the southern rim and turns the basin damp and pewter-colored, these frames go immediately heavy with beads of fresh, saltless water. For twenty minutes they drip in fast, silver threads into scalloped travertine troughs, and from there down narrow stone channels into buried clay jars sealed with resin plugs. Anyone caught out on the flats when the cloud-surge rolls in simply stops where they are, loosens the chalk-dusted cloak from their shoulders, and spreads it across the rock to drink the mist before wrapping the damp cloth back around themselves—a brief, dark luxury of cool evaporation that lasts another half-hour after the sun burns the sky clear again.

Because the thin air swallows sound ten paces from a speaker's mouth, they have largely given up on shouting. Sitting together in the shade of an overhang or inside a cool room, they speak in a low, dry, economical murmur that uses almost no breath—clipped syllables, soft tongue-clicks, small turns of the chin and fingers. To an outsider, two of them working side by side over a dismantled relay box look almost mute, communicating mostly through the rhythm of their hands and an occasional quiet hum low in the throat.

Across the basin, they use the two things the plateau does carry: light and iron. Every adult wears a *flash-plate* looped to the thumb or tucked into the belt—a palm-sized disk of hammered, polished copper, or a thick, clear flake of split mica backed with dark hide. From the crest of a ridge, a single turn of the wrist throws a clean white needle across two miles of wavering heat. They have a whole grammar of held beats and slow sweeps: a single long flare to mark a safe crossing where a brine pan has crusted over; three short pulses to warn that a borehole cap is running hot; a double sweep low along the horizon to say the southern clouds are building thick and early. On bright mornings, if you stand high on the western rim, you can sometimes watch three households trading news across the wide floor—silent sparks leaping between the ochre terraces above the wheeling gliders, leaving the basin's padded hush completely unbroken.

When the cloud-surge blinds the flats, or after the sun drops behind the sawtooth ridge, they move their talk underground. The sandals and thick-soled boots they wear have a narrow strip of salvaged copper inset across the arch, and every belt carries a brass-headed striking pin. Step onto the single rusted rail line, or press a bare heel against a pipe flange or a buried grounding stake, and strike the steel. In the thin air, the blow is a dull, flat *tink* that vanishes before it reaches the next sinter cone; through the continuous iron and the subterranean conduits, it runs half a mile in a heartbeat, climbing cleanly up through the bones of a listener's ankle on the far slope as a crisp, unmistakable cadence of ticks and pauses. The buried grid of the old industry has become their shared nervous system.

They live off the plateau's crowded floor with the patience of people who know every inch of it cannot be hurried or forced. They do not farm, except in the narrowest sense—nudging the leathery chalk-dusted rosettes into the dampest shaded crevices beneath their water-frames, where the leaves grow fleshy enough to be pickled in brine and chewed on long walks. Most of their sustenance comes from the animals that own the rock, and their dealings with each of them are intimate and unsentimental.

With the bronze-shelled grazers, it is something close to herding. They do not build pens; they steer the sluggish ribbons with moisture and faint electric current. A household will drag a length of bare, scavenged copper wire from a station's grounding stake out across a damp travertine shelf, or chip a finger-wide groove to guide a weeping pipe flange a few yards onto fresh stone. Within days, the armored grazers shift over in dense, overlapping drifts to scrape the mineral films. Every child learns before they are ten to tell the two kinds apart at a glance. Along the clean, northern seepage lines live the dull, greenish "sweet-backs," scraped off the rock with a flat iron blade, cracked at the seam, and steamed over a dry vent-crack for a dense, salty meat like warm shellfish. Clustered thick around the cable runs and weeping flanges live the heavy, gleaming "penny-backs," whose shells have absorbed so much leached copper that they shine like wet coins. Nobody eats a penny-back; the flesh is metallic and bitter enough to double a grown man over for three days. Instead, the people wait for the oldest, heaviest ones to die—or cull them when their shingled heaps begin wedging under a valve housing—and stack the thick metal-rich carapaces in small, fierce clay kilns stoked with resinous wire-shrub twigs. From the ash they pull soft, impure buttons of smelted copper, or simply hammer the thickest shells flat on a basalt anvil into rivets, flash-plates, boot-strips, and patches for cracked jars. The basin mines its own metal through the slow mouths of its grazers, and the people harvest what the shells leave behind.

They read the rest of the wildlife the way lowlanders read weather vanes and clocks. When the flickering semaphore of the mica lizards suddenly blinks out across a wide patch of terrace, every head in the nearest shadow lifts—something has crossed the light, whether a diving glider, an early dust-whirl, or a stranger stepping carelessly off the worn tracks. They watch the ribbed crests of the gliders stacking on the updrafts: crests flushed deep arterial red unusually low over the flats mean a deep fissure has shifted and opened a fresh shaft of heat; crests gone pale bone-white high overhead mean the thermals are collapsing ahead of a cloud-surge. When an old glider eventually comes down on the salt flats, nothing goes to waste. Its hollow wing-bones become fine needles and blow-pipes for clearing gypsum dust out of delicate switches; its sparse, wiry plumage is bound into stiff brooms for sweeping the rail-bed; its leathery crest-membrane, oiled and cured, makes tough, supple gaskets for water-jars and oil-damped relays.

Even the chalk-wasps and the unseen burrowers beneath the crust have their place in the ledger. Scraping the honeycombed mortar-nests from the ceramic insulator skirts keeps the high-voltage lines from arcing during a wet cloud-surge, and brings home both a sack of prime wall-mortar and handfuls of fat, faintly acidic larvae that roast nutty and crisp on a hot stone slab. And deep beneath the tilted basalt, where blunt-snouted, nearly blind diggers have tunneled for centuries along the cooler fault-lines between the warm vents, the people watch for abandoned galleries—blocking the narrow entrances with fitted slabs to claim ready-made root-cellars where dried lizard-strips, resin blocks, and sealed water-jars stay out of the sun's reach.

Nobody up here quite agrees on when their people first stayed on the plateau, or why they never went back down. Some households still keep a tarnished brass badge or a laminated wiring diagram pressed flat under a sleeping-mat, worn so smooth by generations of thumbs that the printed letters have vanished into pale ghosts. Whatever company or city built the deep boreholes, the closed-loop turbines, and the solitary cast-iron manifold houses left them behind when the machinery grew steady enough to run mostly on its own. Mostly—not entirely. At four thousand meters, in drifting gypsum, crawling travertine, and wasps that cement stone with acid, nothing stays self-tending forever.

So the people tend the grid, not out of any loyalty to the unseen cities beneath the floor of clouds, but because the iron houses and the buried conduits have become part of the plateau's geology. If a louver-bank clogs with windblown salt and a manifold house overheats and trips offline, the weeping flange dries up, the penny-backed grazers scatter, the wire-shrubs along the conduit trench wither, and the local rail-line goes dead to tapped messages. They know every station in their reach by the exact pitch of the tremor it sends up through their soles, and by the angle of the brass needles crawling behind the fogged glass dials. They do not read the numbers stamped on the faces; they know where a needle belongs the way they know the curve of a horizon.

When a relay sticks inside a steel junction box, someone hears the absence of its heavy *clack*—or feels the wrong hum through a grounding stake—and walks out in the cooler hours of morning with a leather roll of hand-filed wrenches, a horn of dark grease, and a bone blow-pipe. They clear the salt-bloom from the condenser masts. They wedge basalt shims under a rail-tie when a shifting travertine saucer tilts the track out of true. When the squat, riveted maintenance bogie glides silently down the slope on its single rail, the people simply step off the bed and let it pass, watching its axles with an expert's eye; if a bearing runs dry and throws a faint hot smell into the hush, the next household down the grade is already waiting at the condenser mast with an oil-can, alerted five minutes earlier by three quick taps along the rail.

Only a few times a year does the world below the clouds make itself known. At the jagged western rim of the basin, bolted into the cliff beside the thickest bundle of braided power cables, hangs an iron cableway cradle. Without warning—no bell, no schedule anyone up here keeps—the heavy haul-cable begins to hum and shudder against its rollers, and an hour later a slatted steel box rises out of the bright white sea of cloud below.

The households nearest the rim gather quietly to meet it, keeping their scarves pulled over their noses at first, because the crate arrives smelling shockingly strange—damp, chilly, heavy with the wet green reek of lowlands, river-mud, and rot. Inside are sealed drums of turbine oil, crates of fresh ceramic insulators, coils of solder and copper braid, spare springs, and always, wedged between the hardware, plain canvas sacks of hard grain, dried beans, iron blades, blocks of dark wax, and bolts of coarse undyed cloth. The people unload it without hurry. In its place they pack the cracked insulators, the worn-out valves, the burnt relay coils—and sometimes, tucked carefully into a corner in a woven basket, a heavy stack of gleaming copper-rich grazer shells or bricks of bright, clean sulfur scraped from the deepest vents, a quiet accounting offered to hands they have never seen. Then someone throws the heavy iron toggle on the rim-post, the haul-cable shudders, and the cradle drops back down through the bright flat floor of cloud and disappears.

None of them has ever climbed into the box to ride it down. When the old ones speak of the cities below—in low, dry evening murmurs, gesturing loosely toward the western rim—they speak of them with a kind of quiet pity: places drowned under miles of thick, heavy air that must feel like breathing warm grease, where water falls unasked from the sky and turns everything soft with mold, and where a voice carries so far that no one can ever sit in proper silence.

Their own hours come alive at the turn of evening, just as the plateau shifts gear. When the sun drops onto the sawtooth western ridge and knife-edged shadows begin racing across the crowded stone, the sharp daytime glare breaks. Households that have spent the bright hours tucked into their separate overhangs emerge onto the worn tracks, walking slowly toward a shared travertine bowl or the broad, warm lee of a manifold house. As the last cloud-shelf rolls over the southern lip, turning the basin briefly wet and pewter-colored, they sit hunched together in their dampened cloaks beneath the overhanging eaves of the iron station, listening to the copper cooling fins tick softly overhead as the metal contracts.

Once the mist shreds away, the air cools sharply on the skin while the stone beneath them keeps radiating steady heat up through their heels and palms. This is when they trade: a jar of sweet cloud-water for a string of dried mica-lizards; a freshly ground pair of basalt eye-slits for a ball of shrub-resin; a copper-shod sandal sole repaired in exchange for an afternoon spent scraping chalk-wasps off the northern insulator bank. Old women and men whose ribs are wide as barrels and whose fingernails carry a permanent blue-green stain of verdigris sit closest to the borehole cap, resting a palm on the warm iron while they tap slow greetings down the buried conduits to neighbors two miles away. Children dart silently between the sinter chimneys, practicing the slow, deliberate sweeps of their first mica flash-plates against the last red slant of sunlight on the eastern ridge, already stepping lightly between the salt-lips and tunnel mouths without looking down.

And when one of them finally grows too light and dry to walk the tracks—their breath thinning out even in the deep stillness of the home—there is no wood to burn them and no soft earth to dig. At dusk, their household carries them up into the weathered volcanic ridges at the edge of the basin and lays them deep inside a dry, high-altitude fissure where the sun never quite reaches and the damp never climbs. They tuck the person's polished flash-plate beneath the folded hands, stack pale salt-slabs across the opening, and seal the seams smooth with wet chalk-wasp mortar. Within a season, the salt-bloom and rust-lichen creep over the seams, and the tomb vanishes back into the ridge—dry, light, silent, folded into the same crowded stone that holds the hives, the chimneys, and the humming iron roots of the grid, while the slate sky deepens toward black overhead.
