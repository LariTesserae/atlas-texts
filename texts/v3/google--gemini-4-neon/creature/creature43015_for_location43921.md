If you stay on that weathered promontory past the first ten minutes—long enough for your breathing to slow and the cold to creep through the soles of your boots—you begin to suspect that you are not alone in the dark.

Nothing moves sharply. There is no cry of alarm, no rustle of brush, no clatter of displaced scree. The thin air refuses to betray anyone. Instead, the clue arrives through the stone. Beneath the steady, tectonic hum of the mountain, a faint rhythmic thudding starts up somewhere down-terrace—three slow taps, a pause, two more—so low you feel it in the hinges of your jaw before your ears make sense of it. Fifty yards away, where a broad shelf of resistor-moss drapes over a shoulder of basalt, a mound you had taken for a frost-rimmed boulder shifts its weight.

A layer of living black wool peels back. A dark shoulder rises out of the steaming hollow, shedding brittle flakes of rime-frost that drift off into the wind without a sound.

They do not look like people from down below, though at a glance—especially in the starless gloom—their silhouette is close enough to startle you. They are broad-shouldered, low-slung, and heavy in the bone, built not to sprint or climb lightly, but to stay anchored where a silent gust can shear a loose rock clean off a ledge. Standing upright, they are perhaps a little taller than a lowland human, though they rarely stand full height in the open; they move with a rolling, knees-bent deliberation, keeping their center of gravity close to the basalt.

Up close, the resemblance to anything from the drowned world beneath the clouds thins rapidly.

Their skin is the color of wet graphite—thick, nearly poreless, and dull as vulcanized rubber, kept slick around the elbows, neck, and knees with pale amber lacquer from the distillate-shrubs so the dry upper air cannot crack it. They have no hair to speak of, and no outer ears—only smooth, dense plates of bone lying flat beneath the skin along their temples and jawlines, angled to catch the faintest shudder rising through their heels. Their eyes sit deep behind heavy, ridged brows, dark and unusually wide, lidded with translucent grey membranes that slide shut whenever freezing mist drives across the terraces. When those inner lids part in the dimness, the irises catch a faint, oil-slick sheen, built to read the bruised-red glow beneath a moss-bed or the hairline shimmer of a live sulfide vein in near-total dark.

Most striking of all is the way they breathe.

Their chests are massive, barrel-deep, banded horizontally by heavy ridges of cartilage and muscle that flex like the corrugated casings of the bellows-reeds. They do not pant, and their mouths—wide, tight-lipped, sealed against moisture loss—stay closed as they walk. Instead, every twenty or thirty seconds, the whole ribcage draws inward in a slow, disciplined squeeze, mechanically pressurizing a pocket of thin air deep inside a thick-walled core so their dark, iron-rich blood can drink from it. A heartbeat later, twin slits tucked beneath the jawline part for a fraction of a second, venting two tiny, silent curls of warm vapor that flash instantly into glittering ice-dust and blow away past their shoulders.

That slow, pressurized cycle governs everything about how they move. They do not waste motion. They wait, motionless as cast iron, while their chests build head; then they move in bursts of startling, heavy-limbed certainty, crossing an ice-glazed terrace or hauling a fallen limb with effortless leverage before settling back into stone-stillness again.

If you ever got close enough to see the palms of their hands or the broad, flat soles of their feet, you would understand why they leave no roads, no walls, and no cut stone behind them.

Their palms and heels are crisscrossed with thick, dark-brassy calluses—hardened ridges plated with bio-deposited copper and iron, drawn over decades from the mineralized oils and valve-back marrow they eat. When one of them kneels on a damp shelf and presses a bare palm into the resistor-moss, or plants a bare foot squarely across a wet sulfide channel, they do not merely touch the ground. They enter the circuit.

They feel the mountain the way a lowland sailor feels a swell through the floorboards of a hull. Through their soles, they can read the slow telluric tide swelling up from the quartz seams hours before the mist-bank crests the southern rim. They can feel the sudden local drop in resistance half a terrace away when a heavy valve-back steps into a brine pan, or the sharp, taut buzz of an arc-hunter lying coiled and fully charged in the wool forty paces ahead.

And they can feel you—an absurd, insulated, leather-shod dead spot thumping clumsily across the live stone, leaking warmth into the empty air and making no sense to the current at all.

---

There is not a single wall on these ridges because, to them, a wall is a dead thing—a foolish lump of rock that breaks the wind-mist, starves the ferns on its leeward side, and cuts off the ground-hum. They do not build shelter; they *bias* what is already alive.

Follow a band of eight or ten of them down into a sheltered basalt amphitheater, and at first you will see only a lush, waist-deep meadow of resistor-moss surrounded by heavy brakes of condenser-ferns. Look closer, though, and the arrangement is subtly, impossibly right. Beneath the black wool, tucked neatly against the copper-plated root-mats of the surrounding ferns, lie small clusters of dull-brass pellets—harvested from the neat trails left behind by slag-beetles. By tucking a handful of metal here and pinching back a root-runner there, the band has shunted a gentle, steady fraction of the valley’s telluric current directly into this hollow.

The moss here runs ten degrees warmer than the rest of the hillside.

To sleep, they simply part the coarse, frosted wool and sink down into the dry, humming belly of the mat, pulling the living fibers back over their shoulders until only the top of a lacquered hood or the faint puff of a breath-vent shows against the dark. Above them, they bend the broad, rubbery fronds of the nearest condenser-ferns together and lash the tips loosely with waxed tendon, forming a low, living roof that sheds freezing mist and breaks the silent wind. No stem is snapped. No stone is moved. When the band migrates across the saddle to another valley a few weeks later, they untie the fronds and pocket the brass pellets. Within an hour, the ferns straighten back into the draft, the moss cools down to its dull cherry baseline, and a traveler walking straight through their hearth would swear no sentient creature had ever paused there.

Their belongings are just as fleeting, borrowed straight from the dead ecology and destined to return to it.

They wear heavy, knee-length mantles felted from dead resistor-wool—coarse and matte-black on the inside to trap body heat, rubbed smooth on the outside with dark shrub-lacquer so freezing dew beads off like mercury. At their waists hang loops of astonishingly fine cord braided from the copper-plated roots of dead ferns, stiffened with wax and supple as leather. They carry needles and awls ground from the shed keratin capacitor-combs of dead arc-hunters, and short, weighted staves cut from the densest, calcified lower segments of collapsed bellows-reeds.

Their most prized possession, though, is always lashed across the small of the back or carried looped over one forearm: a smooth, heavy hoop of pale ivory-grey bone, roughly the width of a platter.

It is the calcified flywheel-ring cut from the thorax of a fallen gyro-glider.

When a band must cross one of the knife-edged basalt saddles between mesas—places where the wind combs silently across bare, ice-glazed rock with enough force to hurl a grown adult into a mile-deep gulf—they slip a braided copper cord around the axle of the bone ring and pull hard. The flywheel spins up to a fierce, invisible blur, humming quietly against the palm. Braced against the chest or held out on a short reed-spindle, the spinning bone resists the shearing gusts with stubborn, uncanny weight, anchoring the walker’s balance step by step across the slick void while the air tries noiselessly to peel them off the stone.

Even on calm terraces, the rings are rarely idle. An elder will kneel on a bare patch of quartz-veined basalt, set the rim of a spinning ring lightly against the rock, and let the smooth, pure vibration pour downward into the crust. Half a mile down the fault-line, another band feels that exact pitch rise through the soles of their feet—higher or lower depending on the ring’s weight and the notches worn into its rim—and knows not only that neighbors are approaching, but *which* family it is, and how many hands they bring.

---

They have no spoken name for themselves, because air this thin is a dead medium. Trying to push words through near-vacuum is something only a dying creature does, wasting precious pressurized breath on a whisper that dies three feet from the mouth.

Instead, they speak in three overlapping registers, shifting seamlessly from one to the next without ever opening their lips.

When two of them wish to speak privately—a parent calming a restless child in the warm wool, two lovers curled beneath a canopy of ferns, a pair of scouts arguing over the state of a crossing—they touch. They press their broad foreheads together, or hook forearms, or rest the flat of a palm against the other’s sternum. Through direct bone conduction, a minute click of the internal chest-valves, a controlled hum from the pressurized lung-chamber, or a faint muscular thrum along the jaw becomes an astonishingly rich, resonant voice. You could stand two paces away in the dark and hear nothing at all, watching two dark shapes lean motionless brow-to-brow for ten minutes while they exchange an entire afternoon’s gossip, grief, or dry amusement.

Across a shared terrace, they speak through the moss and the rock. Squatting twenty feet apart in the same resistor-bed, they open and close their copper-callused palms against the damp conductive wool, subtly damping and releasing the micro-currents flowing between them. To anyone else, it is invisible; to them, it is a crisp, nuanced language of drag and release, warmth and prickle, tension and sudden ease—carried straight through the ground from palm to sole. A sharp tap of a bone-stave on the basalt calls attention; a rolling double-beat of the heels warns of an arc-hunter shifting in the wool; a slow, steady grounding of both palms means *sit, let the pressure settle, there is no hurry*.

That patience runs through everything they eat and make.

They do not forage blindly like the valve-backs, nosing into whatever distillate-shrub hangs nearest. They are quiet, meticulous brewers of living chemistry. Moving slowly through a dense thicket of shrubs, a tender will inspect the translucent resin-sacs with a broad thumb, gauging the internal pressure and warmth. Into selected amber bulbs, she drives a hollow keratin needle and slips a carefully measured drop of bitter sap from a bellows-reed, or a pinch of roasted iron-moss ash, then seals the pinhole immediately with a smear of warm wax from her belt-pouch.

Left hanging on the living branch, the shrub’s own slow fractional refinement goes to work on the adulterated bulb. Weeks later, when the band circles back through the valley, those marked sacs no longer hold plain oil. Some have matured into dense, spicy tallows that burn slow and hot in the belly during a three-day march across barren rock; others hold a pale, sharp solvent-broth that clears stiffened lung-valves in the old; still others yield a cloudy, slightly numbing resin they pass around in the warm hollows after a heavy hunt, pressing their palms together in the dark while the moss hums beneath them.

When they want meat, they rarely waste compressed breath chasing a six-limbed valve-back across slick stone. They let the mountain do the killing.

Working in silence during the tense half-hour before a cloud-surge, three or four hunters will lay a long, thin line of braided copper-roots across a wet sulfide channel leading down to a favored brine pan, anchoring the far ends deep into two separate, heavily charged brakes of condenser-ferns. Then they settle into the frosted wool downwind and wait. When a heavy, iron-plated grazer ambles down the lacquered ledge to drink, one hunter simply lifts a small wax-insulated toggle on the braided cord, closing the loop. The terrace discharges straight through the wet channel in a single, blinding blue-white snap—soundless save for a dry *tick* and a sudden jolt through the rock.

They butcher the heavy carcass swiftly with sharp flakes of shed valve-back horn and hardened arc-hunter keratin, before the freezing air can turn the flesh to iron. The warm, metallic blood is drunk on the spot; the thick slabs of dark meat are wrapped tightly in slick condenser-fronds and buried deep in the glowing cherry-red core of the resistor-moss. Left there for six hours beneath three feet of insulating black wool, the meat slow-roasts in dry, humming heat, coming out tender, steaming, and smelling sharply of ozone, warm paraffin, and wet slate.

---

Every few hours, when the bruised violet cloud-deck swells miles below and spills its silent bank of charged vapor over the southern rims, the whole tenor of their lives shifts upward with the voltage.

Where the valve-backs hunch closer together and the arc-hunters pull deeper into their beds, the people of the ridges rise out of the wool.

The surge is their high tide, their market, their open square. As the cold mist rolls across the amphitheaters and the fronds of three miles of condenser-ferns stiffen and lift in unison, figures emerge along the highest basalt spires, their lacquered mantles glistening with sudden condensation, tiny beads of ice forming along the rims of their hoods.

This is when they speak across the mile-wide gulfs.

Standing braced against the silent gusts on opposing cliffs, a signaler raises a long, flexible stalk of dead bellows-reed threaded through with braided copper-root. As the tip climbs into the intensifying electric field, pale, ghostly blue corona-fire blooms suddenly along the upper length of the pole—a tall, soundless plume of cold flame burning brightly against the pitch-black sky. By gripping and releasing a wax-sheathed loop at the base, grounding the current through her own copper-callused feet into the rock and then letting it build again, she makes the blue fire flare, dim, pulse, and wink out in slow, deliberate sequences.

Across a mile of empty black air, on the rim of a mesa that would take two days of treacherous saddle-crossing to reach, another pale blue spire answers.

In that twenty-minute window before the upper winds tear the mist-bank apart, the whole dark country talks to itself. Bands trade news of where a stand of bellows-reeds has collapsed and drawn thick swarms of slag-beetles; where a herd of valve-backs has drifted; which high saddles are glazed too thick with matte ice to cross safely; who has died, and where a new child has entered the warm wool. Sometimes, if two bands are on neighboring ridges and the surge runs strong, young hunters will climb the highest promontories simply to play—raising two poles at once, making the blue corona dance and fork between their tips in intricate, voiceless flourishes that leave faint violet afterimages burning on the dark.

Down on the lower terraces during the surge, others gather the sudden abundance of pure, cold water pooling in the shallow basalt pans, filling cured resin-bladders before the skim-ice thickens. Some simply stand barefoot on the wet sulfide veins with their mantles thrown back, eyes half-lidded, letting the climbing telluric current flood straight up through their soles and shimmer across their skin in a faint, prickling aura—drinking the mountain’s surge the way creatures in the lowland sun might tip their faces to the warmth.

---

Children are born in the deepest, hottest pockets of the resistor-beds, timed when possible to the quiet lull right after a surge, when the moss still glows bright cherry-red beneath the wool and the air holds a lingering trace of dampness.

A newborn arrives soft-skinned, its chest-valves fragile and uncallused, its palms pale and completely deaf to the ground. Within seconds of its first tight, silent wheeze, the elders coat the infant from crown to heel in warm, clarified wax from a special amber sac, sealing its skin against the killing dryness, and press it directly against the bare, humming chest of its mother so its tiny, rapid breathing can learn the slow cadence of her pressurized ribs. For its first year, it scarcely touches the open air, carried tucked inside the warm, oiled folds of a parent’s mantle, feeling the world only as a shifting tide of bone-hums and muffled ground-tremors.

Only when its palms begin to darken with the first thin, brassy sheen of bio-deposited copper—usually around its fourth or fifth year—is it set down bare-footed on a damp sulfide vein during a gentle mist-front, held steady by two adults while its eyes go huge behind its inner lids and the mountain speaks to it for the first time.

Age comes to them as a slow stiffening of the engine.

Over decades, the cartilaginous bands of the chest lose their supple snap; the internal valves begin to leak tiny, constant threads of frost even at rest, leaving faint pale crusts below the jaw. Worse, the metallic calluses on their palms and heels grow thick and brittle, deadening the finer nuances of the current until the living hillside feels muffled and far away, like trying to read a friend’s palm through thick wool.

When an elder can no longer keep pace with the band across the ice-glazed saddles—or when the cold begins to win against the slow fire in their blood—there is no grave dug, because there is no dirt to dig, and piling loose stones over a body would only hide it from the cycle.

Instead, the band walks together down to a broad, quiet shelf where a deep sulfide channel cuts through the basalt toward a thriving brake of ferns. They lay the dead one gently along the etched vein, bare palms turned flat against the wet rock, face turned up toward the starless black. Their flywheel-ring is lifted from their back and pressed into the hands of a younger relative—keeping the old stone-pitch spinning among the living—while their copper braids and waxed tools are laid neatly beside their ribs. Each member of the band kneels in turn, pressing a forehead briefly to the cooling brow-bone, then placing both bare palms into the damp stone on either side of the body to feel the silence settle where a familiar resistance used to be.

Then they rise and walk back up into the dark terraces.

Within days, the armored swarms of slag-beetles emerge from the damp cracks along the channel. Slowly, methodically, without hurry or malice, they go to work. Their acidic gut-chambers strip the iron from the heavy bones and the copper from the hardened palms and soles, leaving behind neat trails of tiny, smooth, dull-brass pellets along the wet basalt. With the next cloud-surge, the condensing dew washes those gleaming grains straight down the etched channels and into the braided roots of the condenser-ferns below.

By the following season, the dead are no longer gone. They are part of the vein. When their grandchildren kneel on that terrace to warm their hands in the moss or read an approaching tide through their soles, the current runs just a fraction clearer, a fraction stronger, for the metal they left behind.

---

Sometimes, on the clearest, coldest stretches between vapor banks, a pair of them will walk out to the sheer southern edge of the outermost mesa and sit with their heavy legs dangling over the mile-deep drop.

Far below, the bruised violet roof of the lower clouds rolls endlessly from horizon to horizon, lit faintly from beneath by unseen, muffled flashes of lowland lightning. They never climb down toward it. They know, from generations of watching how the cold tongues of mist shear apart as they rise, that down beneath that violet lid the air grows impossibly thick and heavy—a wet, drowning soup so dense it roars continuously when it moves, crushing inward from every direction.

Once every few years, a freak thermal shear flings something up out of that buried sea and drops it onto the high basalt: a shredded leaf of shocking, impossible emerald green, or the frozen, burst body of some tiny lowland bird tumbled miles past the ceiling of its world.

When they find such a thing, the whole band gathers around it in the dimness, kneeling shoulder to shoulder with their knees pressed into the frost. They lift it gently on a broad, brass-callused palm, turning it over in absolute silence, marveling at the absurd fragility of its hollow bones, the dry softness of its feathers, the wide-open beak built for a place where creatures waste breath throwing noise blindly into the wind. They pass it hand to hand, pressing brow to brow to share quiet, bone-conducted wonder at what kind of roaring, ungrounded, sunlit madness must churn beneath the clouds.

Then they set the frozen scrap carefully beside a wet sulfide channel for the beetles, rise together in one slow, pressurized breath, and turn their backs on the abyss. Behind them, the silent black sky presses down; underfoot, the ancient quartz hums; ahead, the dark jungle of fronds and glowing moss waits—unbuilt, unbroken, and alive with their weight.
