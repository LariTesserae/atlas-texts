If you leaned over the stone coping at noon and stared straight down through the blinding turquoise glare, you would swear the chamber held nothing at all. The water is too clear to hide a shadow, and there are no shadows to hide in. Every inch of the pale plastered floor lies exposed under its racing nets of caustic fire.

You would have to watch for a long time—past the dazzle of the sun-pillar, past the silver static of the plunging column—before you noticed that one of the clear, oily ribbons of refracting heat is not moving with the current.

Where the hotter inflow dives down the north wall and sweeps toward the first low stone baffle, the clean straight line of the plaster step-risers behind it warps, thickens, and stays bent. Just below that wavering seam, tucked in the turbulent lee of the baffle, a curved double row of bright silver air bubbles refuses to rise. While thousands of others tear upward through the water around them, these hang suspended in a neat, trembling crescent, pulsing in place like beads of mercury sewn into invisible cloth.

That is where it rests when the room is quietest—if a room filled with continuous underwater thunder can ever be called quiet.

It has no eyes, no legs, no lungs, and no proper face. Even its shape is hard to settle on, because most of it is nearly the same refractive index as the hot mineral water that holds it. Only where its body folds back over itself does it betray a faint, milky opalescence, like thick blown glass cooled imperfectly, and only along its oldest margins has it taken on any real color: a crisp, chalky white rime of precipitated calcium, no thicker than a fingernail, matching the fresh mineral crust along the chamber’s waterline.

It came up through the terracotta pipe.

Not as it is now, when it can unfurl wider than a mason’s cloak, but as a single dense, rubbery knot no larger than a plum, torn loose from the dark, crushing fissures of the thermal vein when the builders first tapped the hill. Down in the deep fractures, its kind lives flattened between hot limestone seams in pitch darkness and immense pressure, tasting dissolved lime and sulfur, never knowing a space wider than a finger’s breadth. When the vein was opened and channeled into clay, the water carried this torn fragment uphill and down again through miles of humming conduit, finally spitting it out of the circular north spout into a small, screaming, brilliantly lit cube of plastered stone.

It could not pass through the narrow bronze bars of the south overflow. And after the first chaotic days of being slammed against the grate and tumbled over the baffles, it stopped trying to leave. It grew instead, drinking the rich minerals leaching from the young hydraulic mortar, learning the strange, impossible physics of an open room.

Its body is built around a central, heavy core—a tough, gristly sac not much bigger than a river cobble—that anchors it to the floor. Inside that sac it keeps a shifting ballast-cake of pale silt scraped from the clay pipes and fine white grit sloughed from the fresh plaster, swallowed grain by grain out of the slow sunlit gyres in the corners. Without that stolen weight of earth inside it, the plunging jet would tear it straight off the floor and press it to shreds against the hammered bronze drain.

From that silt-weighted base, three broad, deeply pleated vanes of transparent, cartilaginous membrane stream out into the flow. They look, when a stray angle of noon light catches their bevels, like the fluted inner ribbons of a giant sea-anemone, or pale silk dipped in clear tallow, stiffened here and there by delicate, hollow ribs of deposited calcite. Along the thickened outer rims of these vanes run neat chains of tiny, valve-lipped pockets. Inside each pocket sits a stolen bubble of air, snatched from the plunging waterfall column and locked away beneath a skin taut as a drumhead.

Silt below; silver air above. Ballast of earth and buoyancy of sky. By squeezing a few bubbles out of its upper pockets, or pulling a fresh mouthful of air-froth into them, it adjusts its weight to the grain.

Because it has no eyes, it knows the chamber by sound and by skin.

Underwater, the room is an acoustic engine—the booming crash of the jet, the hiss and static of rising air, the hollow groan humming through the terracotta pipe, the rattle of the bronze bars. Every one of those vibrations enters the creature’s trapped silver bubbles and makes them ring. The chambers along its vanes are of graduated sizes, from pinheads to fat pearls, and each answers a slightly different band of the underwater thunder. Together they form a vast, scattered ear.

Through them, it reads the geometry of the young masonry with startling precision. In the deep earth it came from, rock was rough, crumbly, and muffled; echoes died almost where they were born. Here, every plane is hard, flat, and freshly troweled, and every corner meets at a razor-sharp arris. To the creature’s vibrating pockets, the plastered walls feel blindingly sharp—clean, stinging planes of reflected noise that map out the six paces of the tank down to the width of a step.

At night, and in the dim bruised blue of early dawn before the sun reaches the rim of the shaft, the creature lies contracted and heavy behind the northern baffle. Its vanes fold tight around its ballast-core, hiding its silver beads inside pale pleats until it looks like nothing more than a slumped drift of wet lime-curds caught against the stone.

Then the first clock turns.

When direct morning light finally drops down the whitewashed shaft and strikes the submerged east stair, the creature wakes—not to sight, which it cannot conceive of, but to a sensation that never existed in the deep aquifers: the sudden, piercing expansion of warmth straight through its clear flesh. Sunlight passes through its glass-clear membranes and heats the trapped silver air inside its pockets. The bubbles swell. A gentle, insistent upward tug runs through every pleated ridge.

It loosens the broad, adhesive muscular flange beneath its core—peeling off the smooth floor with a faint, soundless hydraulic release—and begins to travel toward the light.

It moves in two ways. On the steps and walls, it creeps like an enormous, translucent limpet, rippling its heavy lower seam across the creamy plaster. As it climbs the east stair into the morning pillar, its sensitive, finely fringed underside glides directly over the faint curved arcs left by the masons’ wooden float and traces the crisp chisel-scoring around the pipe sockets. It lingers over these tiny imperfections, where microscopic eddies catch stray mortar-dust and torn specks of emerald algae whipped loose from the terracotta spout. Its mucous-lined pleats sweep the stone clean, filtering out the green shreds and lime-flour, leaving the fresh plaster behind it gleaming even brighter than before.

When the sun reaches high noon and the dense column of light stands straight down in the center of the basin, the creature takes to the current.

It releases the stair altogether, tilts its broad vanes into the roaring downward wash off the north wall, and kites. Held aloft by its sun-warmed silver beads and stabilized by the heavy grit-ballast slung beneath it, it rides the circular violence of the tank in a slow, controlled arc—sweeping over the razor-edged baffles, banking smoothly short of the hungry suction of the bronze grate, and soaring back up the south face where the current lifts. To anyone looking down from the coping, it would seem only that the nets of caustics had gone briefly mad: wherever the creature’s curved, watery lenses pass through the sun-pillar, they gather the glare and throw slow, doubled skeins of blinding white fire across the walls and the underside of the vault.

In that fierce midday blaze, something strange happens to its skin. Sunlight cures the outermost film of its translucent tissue the way dry air crusts fresh lime. Suspended in the dead center of the glare, the creature shudders violently from core to tip. Fine, papery flakes of pale mineral peel off its ruffled edges and scatter into the current, twinkling briefly like mica before joining the drifting silt in the quiet corners. Sometimes a whole tattered strip of shed membrane tears free, tumbles down the basin, and snags across the hammered bronze bars of the sluice, flapping there like wet parchment in the hiss of the drain until the outflow finally shreds it and carries it away into the dark. Underneath, the creature’s exposed flesh is raw, slick, and almost utterly invisible, singing with heightened sensitivity to every ripple of heat.

Occasionally, a tiny drop of cool condensation shaken from the two maidenhair ferns far up the light-shaft falls straight through the updraft, punches into the churning surface, and sinks a fraction of an inch before the hot water swallows it. The creature never knows the sky or the green fronds trembling above it, yet now and then a microscopic fern-spore or a grain of windblown summer dust rides that falling drop down into the gyres and sticks to its clear folds—a faint, bitter, utterly foreign taste from a dry world it has no organ to imagine.

Then, without warning, the second clock strikes.

Deep beneath the hill, the thermal vein swells. The terracotta conduit groans a half-tone lower, the column of hot water thickens, and the waterline climbs a hand’s breadth up the creamy walls, drowning the neat geometric lines of pale velvet moss. Inside the small, sealed room, hydraulic pressure tightens like a fist.

Instantly, the creature’s silver air-beads compress. Its buoyancy collapses.

It does not fight the drop. It folds its outer pleats hard against its sides and falls straight to the floor, clamping its sticky flange tightly into the sharp angle behind the northern baffle—directly beneath the pounding waterfall, where the plunging column curls back on itself in a boiling, roaring roller.

Up above, the bronze overflow sluice catches the surge with a sudden, resonant gulping rattle. The underwater thunder doubles until the plastered limestone itself seems to shiver. Down in the white turbulence, the plunging jet drives its curtain of fresh air bubbles almost all the way to the floor.

Now the creature opens.

Along the thick, chalk-rimmed borders of its vanes, dozens of tiny, pale siphons flare wide into the violent froth. It does not flee the surge; it harvests it. It gulps the tearing silver bubbles straight out of the boil, packing its flattened resonance-chambers tight again, replacing the gas that has slowly diffused away through the morning, until every vein along its length gleams like hammered pewter under the turquoise glare.

And at the height of those ten or twelve minutes—when the room is tightest, loudest, and closest to bursting—the hollow groan humming through the terracotta pipe deepens into a heavy, steady note that shakes the whole masonry box.

The creature’s freshly packed pockets catch that note. Its broad vanes lock rigid in the roaring wash, pulled as taut as vellum, and it begins to hum back.

It has no voice and no throat; the sound comes from the sympathetic vibration of a hundred silver-beaded chambers ringing in unison against its thin calcite ribs. If you were submerged beneath the waterline with your hands pressed over your ears, you would feel it not in your eardrums but in your teeth and sternum—a clean, piercing, glassy harmonic threading straight through the brutal bass of the waterfall.

It is an old reflex from the pitch-black fissures far underground, where dozens of its kind once clung to the wet rock seams and hummed together whenever the deep vein surged, anchoring themselves to the shared pitch so the flood would not wash them apart. Here, there is nothing else in the hot water to answer. No other membranes ring back from the corners.

Instead, the young, razor-sharp plaster walls catch its glassy hum and bounce it straight back across the six paces of bright water—crisp, unmuffled, and multiplied—until the creature sits wrapped in the echoing company of its own ringing body, singing into a bright stone drum that sings faithfully back.

After twelve minutes, the spring’s breath ebbs. The inflow thins; the bronze sluice settles from its gulping rattle back into a steady hiss. The waterline drops, leaving its dark, steaming band of wet plaster drying pale around the upper room.

Slowly, the creature lets its rigid vanes slacken. Its newly filled silver pockets gleam bright and fat along its clear flanks. As the afternoon wears on and the pillar of direct light drags its fire off the baffles and climbs the far wall toward dusk, the creature drifts quietly back into the shelter of the steps, heavy with fresh silt, buoyant with fresh air, swaying almost invisibly in the warm, roaring turquoise dark until the next surge—or the next morning—turns the room again.
