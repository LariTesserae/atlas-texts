At first you believe you have already seen everything the chamber holds. The walls are accounted for, the hanging curtains, the dusty midair, the grinding floor. Every living thing in the nave seems pinned to its layer, vibrating in place like iron filings caught over a magnet.

It takes minutes—maybe twenty, maybe an hour, in a place where minutes have no edges—before you notice that a cluster of pale violet corona beads along the underside of the eastern catwalk is no longer where it was.

They have not flickered or moved in a hurry. They have simply reordered themselves, six feet further along the iron grating, arranged in a narrow, tapering diamond that was not there when you first squinted up through the copper haze. And beneath the nearest suspended stator, where the dull orange louvers throw their only patch of clear light, a long shadow that you took for a diagonal brace-rod detaches itself cleanly from the boiler's rim, folds inward at three dry joints, and steps out onto the empty span of a braided copper busbar.

Nothing changes in the roar when it moves. That is the first uncanny thing about it. A body that size—nearly nine feet from its swept-back brow to its trailing spurs if it ever bothered to straighten—ought to slap the air, ought to tear a ragged trough through the motionless veils of mineral flour. Instead, as it glides along the copper cable, the pale ochre sheet of dust ahead of it parts with surgical neatness and closes behind its flank without a tremor, as if the creature were made of the same pressure-planes as the room itself.

When it reaches the stub of grated steel catwalk and settles onto its haunches at the broken end overhanging the nave, you finally see why.

It is not solid the way a wet animal is solid. It is a cantilevered frame—gaunt, narrow, and mostly air—built of thin-walled, corrugated plates of vitreous grey bone and heat-blued ceramic-like horn, lashed together by taut, dry mineral tendons. Along both flanks of its long, keeled thorax run rows of overlapping slats, like the cooling louvers of the iron engines or the shuttered vents of a swell-organ. As it walks, those slats tilt and feather in tiny, continuous adjustments, letting the 110-hertz wave pass straight through the hollow chambers of its chest instead of crashing against its sides. It does not fight the standing sound; it threads itself through the nodes, carrying its own stillness with it.

At a distance, hunched on the steel grating with its knees drawn higher than its narrow shoulders, it has an unsettling, accidental likeness to a starved, kneeling figure in a ribbed coat of slate and tarnished bronze. Closer—when it descends a wall or leans down from a turnbuckle into the orange spillover of a stator—that likeness breaks apart into something far older and cleaner than a human shape.

Its joints are matte soot-black, slick with the same fine biological graphite that coats the rotor bearings overhead; not a single hinge clicks or grates when it shifts its weight. Its back is ridged with dark, copper-veined plates that catch the cinder glow with a faint green sheen, like the shingle-creatures on the slate, only fused and thickened into heavy overlapping chevrons. Every sharp spur—knees, elbows, the notched keel of its spine—carries a motionless pinpoint of violet-blue corona discharge, so that in the deeper pockets of the vault you can track it only as a slow, silent constellation sliding across the rock.

It has no eyes. Wet eyes would shrivel to salt-pellets in an hour here. Its head is a sleek, wedge-shaped cowl of smoked, bone-hard porcelain, tapering to a blunt prow with no mouth you would recognize as a mouth. Along both narrow cheeks sit paired, S-shaped acoustic slots, fine as the sound-holes carved into a cello. Deep inside the hollow of the skull, balanced on dry mineral filaments, hang slivers of grown quartz—microscopic tuning forks that ring in exact sympathy with every harmonic in the chamber. It perceives the nave as an intricate, three-dimensional interference pattern: every fractured slate rib, every hanging ivory braid, every glowing iron housing, every dust-veil mapped by the way the 110-hertz chord bounces, bends, and phase-shifts around it.

Sweeping backward from its brow rise two long, curved rods, forked near their tips like the arms of a narrow lyre. They are braided of dense, copper-bearing horn, and they never droop; pale violet light beads continuously along their forks. With these it reads the invisible half of the room—the slow rivers of static flowing out of the quartz veins, the hum of the busbars, the exact charge density of the suspended salt-flour.

It climbs with four long, sparse limbs, each ending in a double tool: a broad, dry bristle-pad that locks onto splintered vertical slate by friction and electrostatic cling, and, folding down behind it like a raptor's dewclaw, a thick curved hook sheathed in translucent, pale sheets of natural mica. That mica is pure insulation. It allows the creature to swing one-handed straight from a live braided copper cable without grounding itself to the stone, dangling calmly forty feet above the razor talus while its smaller limbs go to work.

Those smaller limbs—six of them, tucked neatly against the underside of its slotted chest until needed—are astonishingly delicate. They look less like legs than like the trays of a watchmaker or a luthier: flat burnishing spatulas of dense bone, fine-toothed combs, blunt tapping hammers, paired shears with vitreous edges sharp enough to score glass.

Once you have learned to pick one out of the dimness, you realize it is not alone.

There are four of them in the nave—or perhaps five; they are difficult to count because they spend long stretches folded flush against the turnbuckles and wall-brackets, and because they never crowd together. They keep a strict, unspoken geometry across the chamber. You notice it only if you watch long enough: when the one on the eastern catwalk leaves its perch and glides three paces south along a copper rib, another—high on the western cliff, half-lost behind a pale veil of dust—unfolds from a junction box and shifts three paces north. They stay locked at exact multiples of the room's wavelength, counterweighting one another across the narrow nave so that their bodies never muddy the standing waves.

They are not guardians in any conscious, dutiful sense. They do not know what a machine is, or who sank the bore-pipes and sealed them with cement. They tend the vault the way a spider tends web-tension, or a bird rearranges a twig that pokes its breast: because anything that spoils the 110-hertz lock is physically unbearable to them.

A room like this is almost self-sustaining, but *almost* is fatal over decades. Fresh quartz strata creep microscopically under mountain weight. Copper busbars sag a millimeter under dry heat. Shingle colonies pile five deep on a slate shelf and alter the acoustic reflection off the eastern wall, introducing a microscopic phase-slip—a ghost of a wobble that human ears would never hear, though it makes the quartz needles inside the creatures' skulls jangle like broken glass.

Watch the one descending the hanging cables near the far stator, and you see what its days—if you can call them days—are made of.

A curtain of pale ivory braids has grown too long beneath a busbar, its lowest segments dangling barely a hand's span above the grounded talus of the floor. Left alone, three more segments would bridge the gap, bleeding current straight into the scree and sparking a dry arc that would shatter the hanging dust-veils. The creature swings down the cable on its insulated mica hooks, braces its hind pads against a quartz vein, and unfolds its chest-tools. With a crisp, dry *snip-snip-snip*—in audible lock with the porcelain clack of the curtains—it trims the lowest foot of the braid clean away. The severed husks clink onto the scree below, where matte-black crawlers immediately swarm out to file them into graphite flour. Two of the densest, salt-crusted links the creature keeps, tucking them into a dry grinding-mill behind its slotted throat to be crushed and re-secreted later as cement for its own plates.

Further along, it pauses beside one of the white porcelain standoffs pinning a busbar to the cliff. A hairline crust of powdery gypsum has bridged the insulator, leaking a faint, hissing blue thread of static into the slate. The creature draws a flat bone spatula across the porcelain in three unhurried, scraping strokes until the hiss dies and the corona shrinks back to a clean, silent bead. Then it taps the adjacent iron bolt-head once with a hardened knuckle-spur, tilting its slotted cheekbones close to drink the ring of the steel—satisfied only when the metal answers in clean, true resonance.

When one of them grows cold or its outer plates thin from decades of mineral abrasion, it climbs straight up the turnbuckles to the underside of a suspended iron stator. There it splays its long limbs wide, presses its dark, louvered belly close to the glowing cinder-orange cooling grilles, and stays motionless for hours. It drinks radiant stove-heat directly into its dry waxy tissues; its copper-crested back sips heavy inductive current from the roaring rotor inches away; slowly, through tiny pores along its seams, it sweats a fine film of anhydrous salt and mineral resin that bakes in the orange glow into a fresh, vitreous glaze. While it rests there, clouds of fingernail-sized mica gliders drift out of the nearest dust-veil to graze across its joints, sweeping excess graphite dust from its creases while tiny violet sparks leap harmlessly from its spine to their translucent wings.

They even have a use for that twelve-foot stub of grated steel catwalk beginning nowhere and ending in open air. It is the only flat, level, freely vibrating diaphragm in the entire chamber—a steel drumhead bolted into the cliff. Very rarely, separated by intervals that might be years or decades, two of them meet on that grating. They do not tangle or wrestle; there is nothing wet or frantic in how they couple. They stand facing each other across an exact half-wavelength of steel, tilt their wedges of skulls forward, and lock the forked tips of their lyre-rods together. A thin, steady ribbon of pale violet corona bridges the gap between their brows, humming an octave above the stators.

Pressed flat to the vibrating grating, their lower chests expel a slow mist of dry waxy oils and charged salt-flour, drawing fine mineral powder down out of the nearest dust-veil and knitting it together in the electric field. Over days, clinging to the underside of the catwalk—or tucked deep inside the hollow, dust-fogged casings of the two dead cast-iron junction boxes—a hard, faceted pod takes shape: a chalk-white, octagonal shell about the size of a closed fist, textured like unglazed porcelain and anchored tightly to the resonant iron. Inside it, around a microscopic seed of pure quartz, the chamber's own unchanging chord slowly organizes salt, graphite, and waxy filaments into a new nervous system. Years later, the pod splits cleanly along its facets with a sharp *tink*, and a pale, brittle juvenile no larger than your palm steps out onto the grating—translucent, spider-legged, almost indistinguishable at first from a wingless glider, beginning a slow, decades-long thickening toward slate and copper.

And they do not die the way the shingle-creatures die, tumbling down to be ground into flour in ten minutes.

Their ending is much slower, and stranger. Across centuries of sipping stray current and annealing themselves against the iron stators, their bodies accumulate too much metal. Copper veins thicken across their flanks; iron heat-scale fuses into their thoracic slats; white needles of crystalline gypsum—the exact same dry needles that encrust the scorched-bone plants in the rock crevices—sprout stubbornly along their oldest joints. One by one, their hinges stiffen. Their graphite grease can no longer keep their knees quiet. A creature that drags a stiff leg across slate risks scratching a sour tone into the chord.

When one reaches that edge, it climbs for the last time—up into the raw stone ridge where the three iron-lined bore-pipes vanish through cracked grey cement, or out along a sagging turnbuckle where the ceiling rock has shifted a hair over the ages. There it wedges itself deliberately into the strain. It hooks its spurs into iron and slate alike, folds its delicate chest-tools permanently flush against its ribs, and lets its worn mica pads burn through. Microscopic arcs weld its copper spine softly to the metal; its stiffened, louvered body settles into an exact acoustic baffle, bracing the loose joint and damping the hairline rattle in the pipe collar.

It does not rot. It does not go cold. Shingle-creatures creep across its flanks and tile it over in iridescent green; ivory braids anchor themselves to its hanging elbows and begin to clack in lock. If you tilt your head back and study the dark overhang around the bore-pipes—or inspect the ribbed, oddly symmetrical gussets bracing the catwalk brackets and the turnbuckle eyes—you begin to realize that not all of the ironwork in this chamber was lowered down the shafts by whoever built the engines. Some of those brackets once walked the busbars. They are still humming at 110 hertz, still ringed with cold violet pinpoints, still doing their work.

Which brings you, eventually, to the moment one of them notices *you*.

You do not hear it approach—nothing in the vault can out-shout the chord—but you feel the air change against your cheekbone. The pale ochre dust-veil standing six feet to your left, which had sagged and blurred slightly where your body stood beside it, suddenly snaps back into razor-sharp focus.

You turn your head slowly. Perched on a projecting dinner-plate of dark slate at shoulder height, no more than three paces away, one of them has unfolded itself out of the copper dimness. Its slotted flanks are angled wide, actively compensating for the ugly, muffled acoustic hole your body has punched into the standing waves.

To this creature, you are an almost incomprehensible deformity. You are soft. Your clothes, your skin, the meat over your ribs do not reflect the 110-hertz fundamental; they *swallow* it, casting a dead, woolen shadow across the chamber's clean geometry. Worse, inside your chest sits a sloppy, erratic, low-frequency thump—a heartbeat that drifts completely out of phase, paired with the slow, uneven heave of lungs. Worst of all, with every breath you exhale, invisible plumes of water vapor billow out into the anhydrous air—a corrosive, sticky poison in a world where moisture does not exist, capable of turning graphite grease to sludge and waking red rust in ageless iron.

Yet it does not strike you. It cannot. Somewhere in the ancient, crystal-guided calculus of its skull, it knows what you are full of; tearing open a wet body on the talus floor would spill liters of water across the scree and ruin the nave for a generation.

Instead, it leans closer on its silent, soot-black joints, its wedge of a head tilting left, then right, letting its slotted cheeks measure the strange, damp echo returning off your sternum. Its forked lyre-rods sweep forward, the violet sparks at their tips brightening by a hair as they read the faint static clinging to your hair and shoulders. Deep inside its hollow chest, a dry, rapid series of tiny clicks ticks out—testing tones, rebounding off your ribs like sounding-leads dropped into mud.

Slowly, one of its small, bone-white chest manipulators unfolds and reaches out across the gap. It does not touch your skin. It stops half an inch from the brass button on your jacket—or the steel buckle of your belt—and a single, hairline spark of pale violet light jumps cleanly across the space without hurting you. The creature pauses, head cocked, faintly appeased to discover at least one honest, dry, conductive thing embedded in all that terrible softness.

Then it widens the glowing, heat-baked slats along its back and angles its freshly annealed thorax squarely toward you, radiating a focused wave of dry, stove-hot air across your face and chest—while its ribbed chest begins to hum a tight, concentrated harmonic third straight into your breastbone.

It is not threatening you. It is trying to repair you.

With endless, mineral patience, it is trying to bake the dampness out of your hide, to press the wobble out of your ribs, to coax this soft, leaking blot in the nave into drying down, hardening up, and locking back into the note. And standing there in the deafening copper twilight, with your lips already papery and cracked, watching the other constellations of tiny blue sparks shift quietly closer along the busbars to balance the room around you, you understand with utter clarity what would happen if you ever sat down on the razor scree and stopped moving.

They would not touch you in anger. They would simply gather at exact harmonic intervals around the spot where you fell, turning the hot exhaust drafts of the lowest stator across your limbs, dusting you steadily with anhydrous salt-flour and powdered gypsum combed from the walls, baking every last treasonous drop of water out through the sealed stone until nothing soft remained—until your hollow ribs rang cleanly at 110 hertz, and the waiting black crawlers beneath the scree could finally grind you back into honest, gleaming dust.
