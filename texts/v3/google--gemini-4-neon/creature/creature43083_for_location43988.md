You can stand on that obsidian spire for half a morning, watching the steam-plumes rise and the herds creep, and still miss them entirely. Your eyes search the blinding plateaus for what eyes are trained to find: a clearing, a shelter, a path cut clean through the scree, a straight line raised against the cold. There are none. To see who lives here, you have to stop looking for a break in the press and look instead for the shapes that move against its grain.

There—halfway down a terrace of shattered basalt, wedged cleanly between the steaming, frost-rimed ribs of two kiln-backs—sits a dark crag you took for a jagged outcrop of magnetite. Watch it. Give it five minutes, then ten. Against the glare, its outline shifts by a hand’s breadth. Then, with a dry, calcified *clack* audible even through the roaring hiss of venting steam, the outcrop unfolds.

It is not tall by the measure of the boulder-sized beasts around it, yet it carries the compact density of an anvil. It stands on two heavy, columnar legs whose wide, three-toed hooves are shod in iron-dark horn, built to bite cleanly into slick rime and glass-sharp basalt alike. Above a thick, low-slung pelvis, its torso broadens into massive, sloping shoulders; from them hang two longer, double-jointed arms that can lock rigid to bear tons of shifting weight or curl tightly inward against the chest to shelter delicate work from the wind. Its head sits low between those shoulders, almost neckless, shielded from the welding-arc sky by a wide, jutting visor of dark vitreous bone. That bony shelf casts its entire lower face into permanent, razor-edged shadow. Beneath it, wide, unblinking eyes gleam behind thick, smoky quartz lenses—eyes built not merely to endure the unfiltered glare, but to read the world in infrared blooms of internal heat and the pale violet coronas of live voltage.

It wears nothing woven, nothing sewn along a straight seam. Instead, draped across its broad back and knotted directly through natural bony loops along its collarbones and ribs, hangs a heavy, living mantle: thick pelts of tarnished copper-lichen still mining freezing dew from the air, braided lengths of conductive foil-wing silk, and overlapping shingles of shed dynamo-shell tied together with strips of cured, iron-rich tendon. The mantle is never taken off. Over decades it is patched, pruned, and added to, soaked continuously in the waxy, dark antifreeze oil wept by the wearer’s segmented hide, until frost and patina blur any clear border between skin, cloak, and stone.

Watch it leave its niche. It does not climb down to the ground—there is no ground left empty to step upon. It steps *up*. One hooved foot plants cleanly on the ribbed carapace of the kiln-back beside it. The great furnace-beast barely registers the added load, its twenty-second piston-cycle continuing without a hiccup. Shifting its weight with the unhurried precision of a counterweight, joints locking and unlocking in slow, deliberate clicks, the figure walks out across the living plateau of backs. It steps from steaming shell to frost-slick shoulder to jutting ridge of raw quartz, pausing every few paces to lean its mossy flank into a rising plume of scalding exhaust, soaking the wet heat deep into its lichen cloak before moving on.

Nothing here hurries, and neither do they. Speed burns fuel; panic spends warmth you cannot buy back from subzero air. Every gesture they make has the measured economy of a lever.

Yet when a sudden violence tears through the slow choreography of the plateau, you begin to see what sets them apart from the beasts beneath their feet.

High over a narrow ravine, an ice-laden foil-wing cable finally gives way under its crust of frozen sleet. It snaps with a thunderous whip-crack, lashing down across the dense carpet of creatures in a wild shower of violet sparks. The broken end thrashes and spits against a bed of wet wire-heather, dumping raw, useless current into the basalt while the nearest kiln-backs merely shift their heavy hides a fraction of an inch.

Along the lip of the chasm, three hunched shapes that looked like boulders begin to rise. They do not run. Over the course of twenty minutes, they converge slowly on the spitting line, stepping carefully across the pressed flanks of the herd. The first plants its hooved feet deep into a wet seam of magnetite, grounding itself, and reaches down with broad, blunt outer fingers tipped in dense mineral nail. It takes hold of the live cable bare-handed.

Instantly, a pale blue halo blooms along the edge of its magnetite brow-visor; tiny arcs snap harmlessly off the damp copper-lichen draped over its shoulders, filling the freezing air with the sharp stink of scorched frost and pennies. The figure does not flinch. Its dense, brine-salted body absorbs the surge, bleeding the excess voltage slowly down through its locked legs into the bedrock, holding the thrashing wire dead still. Meanwhile, the second hauls the severed far end up from the gorge using a hooked rib-bone lashed to its wrist, and the third leans in close between them.

Its broad hand opens slowly—taking half a minute to uncurl against the cold—and from recessed slits in the palm, a fringe of fine, glistening filaments unfurls into the air. Copper-threaded nerves, slick with conductive brine. With painstaking patience, the third figure lays its bare palms across the two frayed ends of silk, feeling the phase and pressure of the current directly through its flesh, braiding the glowing strands back together one by one. When the weave is tight, it presses its jaw beneath its visor and smears the splice with a bead of hot, resinous wax secreted from a throat-gland. The wax hisses, freezes hard in the thin air, and seals the joint.

Together, the three release the line. It rises slightly, singing taut and clean across the gorge once more. For a long time afterward, none of them moves; they stand shoulder to shoulder on the cliff-lip, faintly steaming, letting the heat of the absorbed current bleed slowly out of their chests into the subzero light.

They do not mend the cables out of tidy habit. The cables are how they speak.

In a world deafened by grinding pistons, shrieking steam-valves, and cracking static, voices carried on thin air would die three paces from the throat. So they have no voices as you would know them. When two meet along a crowded terrace, they halt face to face, close enough that their broad, frosted shoulders nearly touch. Slowly, they lower their heavy visors until their magnetite brows meet with a dull, resonant *clack*—a greeting felt through the skull—while their outer fingers lock and their delicate palm-filaments interlace.

What passes between them is not sound, but modulated current: slow pulses of voltage, shifts in electrical resistance, subtle warmth drawn from the chest and returned at a different rhythm. To agree is to drop your resistance and let the other’s pulse pass cleanly through your arms into your core. To argue is to hold rigid impedance, forcing the other to push harder, generating a palpable, prickly heat between the joined palms. To lie—a rare, ugly thing here—is to ground a signal prematurely through the foot, letting half the meaning drain away into the stone before the listener can taste it.

And because the foil-wing cables stitch plateau to plateau across twenty-mile gorges, they do not need to huddle in villages to stay a people. A single individual can sit utterly motionless on a windswept terrace of dark quartz, one palm resting lightly against a humming silk span, and hold a slow, winding conversation with a dozen others scattered across fifty miles of jagged highland.

When an electrostatic front drifts across the continent at a walking pace, turning the air metallic and making every foil-fern stand rigid, the whole network comes alive. Seated miles apart along the ridges, plugged into the singing wires, they open their circuits together. They pass word of where a fresh tectonic tremor has cracked open a virgin seam of native copper on the northern shelf; they trade the slow, season-long coordinates of cooling kiln-back carcasses rich in salvageable iron tendons; they track the drift of condensation-mists and debate, over days of patient, overlapping pulses, where the next generation of herds ought to be turned.

Sometimes, when the front peaks and the ambient charge grows vast and clean, they stop trading news and simply sing—weaving the raw voltage of the sky into long, rolling harmonics timed to the twenty-second thrum of the pistons below, letting immense, silent chords ripple across the copper silk until half a mountain range hums in sympathetic resonance.

They build nothing. You will find no walls raised by their hands, no roofs, no smelters, no straight furrows cut into the highland. A wall in this place is a foolish thing; it only shuts you off from the living heat of the press. Instead, they live by subtle edits to the appetite of the world.

Watch one kneeling for an entire afternoon beside the vanguard of a creeping kiln-back herd. The beasts advance only yards in a day, scraping their iron-enamel jaws blindly across the wet wire-heather and mineral scree. The figure beside them does not shout or drive them with a goad; you cannot startle a living furnace that weighs as much as a house. Instead, with glacial patience, it pries a broad slab of barren, frost-slick obsidian from a nearby talus slope and wedges it firmly across the left margin of a mineral fissure. Along the right margin, it slowly crumbles a handful of purified sulfur nodules and bright copper-moss harvested from its own pouch, laying a faint, fragrant seam along the rock.

It looks like almost nothing—a child’s arrangement of pebbles beneath a sky of blinding glare. Yet the kiln-backs follow the scent of raw ore without fail. By tomorrow, the lead beast will angle its wide shovel-jaw two degrees to the right. Over the next two seasons, that single afternoon’s work will deflect ten thousand tons of steaming, slag-dropping fauna forty yards eastward, steering a slow river of living warmth directly across a shadowed tectonic trough where the iron-lichens were beginning to starve and freeze.

In the same unhurried way, they prune the dense thickets of black foil-ferns so the light reaches the dynamo-colonies tucked beneath; they carry living wedges of crimson iron-lichen in hollowed kiln-back valves strapped beneath their ribs—personal, portable hearths they warm with a single long, slow exhalation whenever the wind drops below the tolerance of their antifreeze-thick blood. They do not hunt, and they rarely kill. They harvest the slag-pellets left steaming behind the herds, cracking them between heavy, mineral-lined molars to extract the refined sulfur and salt; they tap the lukewarm broth around the hydraulic siphoners using narrow bone straws, resting a bare palm on the tall, spindly creature’s flank to feed it a gentle, pacifying trickle of current while they drink.

Even their beginnings are folded cleanly into the machinery of the plateau.

You will never see their young scampering across the scree. A newborn is too small to hold heat; exposed to the bare glare and subzero frost, its fluids would lock solid in minutes. So a parent—often two or three bound together by years of shared current—watches the herds for a slow cooling: a kiln-back whose internal fire has finally failed, whose chimneys have fallen silent while frost thickens along its ribbed back.

Before the spiraling bone-borers can strip the dead engine bare, the parents move in. Using heavy, salvaged borer-radulae gripped in their blunt outer fingers, they carve a narrow, sheltered cleft deep into the beast’s vaulted slag-chamber, where the residual mass of smelted iron and roasted moss will hold a steady, dark warmth for half a year. There, wrapped in thick pelts of living copper-moss and anointed in dark oil, the young one is laid to grow.

For months, the parents do not leave. They anchor themselves directly against the cooling hull, knees locked, backs crusted white with sleet, letting the rest of the herd press close around them as if they were stone. They thread thin, live strands of foil-wing silk down through the dead beast’s bony chimney-valves into the dark chamber below, lowering crushed mineral pulp and feeding the unseen child a continuous, low-voltage hum—not merely warmth to keep its blood liquid, but the earliest, gentlest rhythms of language. By the time the carcass finally chills to the core and the bone-borers dismantle its last iron plates, the young one crawls out into the blinding light already dense, already hooded in dark horn, already fluent in the slow talk of the wires.

And as the decades stack up—for they live on the timescale of young mountains—the same minerals that sustain them slowly claim them back.

Year by year, the salts and iron they drink settle deeper into their joints. The smooth *clack* of an unlocking knee grows stiffer, heavier, slower to answer. Silver creeping vines root permanently into the seams of their lichen mantles; layers of pale quartz patina thicken across their magnetite visors. Their internal heat burns lower, more stubbornly, until the effort of crossing a single terrace takes half a day.

When an elder feels the final stiffening coming on, it does not crawl into a dark cleft to vanish. It begins a last, season-long climb. Leaving the dense warmth of the herds behind, leaving the humming bellows-mats and the steaming basins, it ascends slowly up the raw, unweathered ridges to the highest spires of fractured obsidian—the naked places where the air is razor-thin, the light strikes hardest, and the great trunk-lines of foil-wing silk leap across five-mile chasms.

There, at a high junction where three or four main spans converge, the elder plants its three-toed hooves firmly into a cleft of dark stone. With excruciating care, it gathers the humming cables from every direction, wrapping them tightly around its broad shoulders and knotting them permanently through the bony loops along its collarbones. Then, facing out across the blinding continental uplift, it locks its knees for the last time.

It does not die quickly. Anchored to the high spire, nourished by the constant trickle of voltage pouring through the continent’s lines and the freezing dew absorbed by the copper-lichen welded to its ribs, an elder may remain awake for a century or more. It no longer walks, no longer eats solid slag, no longer unfolds its heavy arms. It becomes a living relay—pure junction and memory.

Younger ones make the long, freezing climb up the obsidian spires just to stand beside these frost-rimmed figures and press their wet palms against an elder’s calcified forearms. Through the slow, deep-voiced current still cycling in that nearly motionless body, they listen to what it holds: maps of ore-veins buried beneath three hundred years of shifting scree; the exact intervals of deep tectonic faults that open once a century; the lineages of distant herds; the old, vast chords sung during electrostatic storms before anyone now walking was even laid in a carcass to warm.

And when the last quiet spark inside an elder’s chest finally gutters out and freezes solid, nothing falls. Its bone-heavy skeleton, threaded with copper tendons and gripped tightly to the obsidian by the acid-secreting roots of its own lichen cloak, stays upright against the glaring sky—a silent, hunched pylon of horn and patina, holding the silk lines taut across the abyss, still conducting the voices of its descendants cleanly through its dead ribs.

Step back once more to the high ridge and look out across the twenty-mile basin below.

Nothing has changed, yet everything has. There is still not a single straight line anywhere across the blinding uplift, nor a single rivet, nor a wall raised by a hand. The kiln-backs still grind forward at the pace of glaciers; the dynamo-colonies still crackle in the black shadows; the condensation-mists still pool like pale milk in the young tectonic troughs.

Only now, your eyes know how to find them. Up on every third obsidian spire, you see the hunched, frost-white silhouettes anchored where the gleaming cables meet. Down among the roaring, steaming herds, you catch the slow, deliberate figures stepping across the carapaces, trailing faint halos of violet static and warm vapor—not masters standing apart from this raw, grinding continent, nor creatures trapped within it, but its patient, unhurried mind, running steadily on copper, frost, memory, and time.
