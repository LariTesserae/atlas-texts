If you stay in the hollow long enough for the shock of the noise to settle into the back of your skull, you begin to notice a flaw in the machine.

Kneel beside one of the dark pockets of rainwater tucked between the sedge tussocks. At first the surface looks exactly as it should: a tight skin of microscopic, razor-sharp concentric ripples driven by the sixty-cycle transformer hum beneath the moss, never growing and never smoothing out. Keep watching, though—give it a full minute—and you will see the rings breathe. Once every three seconds, perhaps four, the crispness goes faintly cloudy. The ripples blur, thicken, and snap sharp again. At the same moment, you feel a slow, almost subliminal loosening in the tremor beneath your heels, as if the immense engines beyond the cedar walls had dipped a hair’s breadth off their pitch and climbed back.

The engines have not dipped. Baseload turbines do not drift like that.

Something in the floor is leaning against the note.

Look out across the two hundred paces of springy green, past the lichen-plated dwarf birch and the low hump of fused roots, and you will see nothing out of place. Only tussocks of haircap moss, wiry mats of cranberry, dark creases of water, and the endless scything of swallows through the pale daylight. You could walk within six paces of one of the hollow’s true residents and step right around it, mistaking it for an old bog-hummock or a half-sunken alder log.

Even when you know what to look for, it takes patience to separate the body from the ground.

Folded down into the sphagnum, one of them looks like a long, slumped swell of waterlogged peat—roughly the bulk of a heavy man or a curled deer, though arranged with none of a mammal’s clean geometry. Its hide is dark as steeped tea, smooth and faintly rubbery where the acidic water has tanned it, and across its broad, shallow back grows an authentic pelt of living moss, tiny rosettes of pale lichen, and the hair-thin red runners of bog cranberry. Nothing about this growth is camouflage in any deliberate sense; in a cool, damp pocket that never freezes and never dries, anything that lies still for a week gets colonized. Marsh toads and chorus frogs regularly climb onto those folded shoulders to trill, sitting right over the creature’s hollow chest cavity because the resonance there throws their thin metallic calls a little farther through the turbine roar. The creature does not shake them off. It holds utterly still while a thumb-sized peeper vibrates on the ridge of its nape.

They wait like that through most of the bright, flat hours, half-buried in the wet creases, indistinguishable from the terrain.

What brings them up is not sunrise, nor dusk, nor hunger. It is the air overhead.

Whenever a slightly damper drift moves across the three corridors of high-voltage cables strung sixty feet above the bowl, the corona discharge sharpens. The frying, spitting sizzle deepens into a steady hiss, and the clean metallic tang of ozone drops a little heavier onto the moss. That is when the floor begins to move.

Forty yards out from the eastern cedar wall, a dark swell of sphagnum lifts slowly, without haste, shedding cold tea-colored water in quiet ribbons. There is no snap of bone or rustle of struggle—only the soft, wet suck of peat releasing weight, and a low hydraulic tension, like a thick green sapling straightening after snow melts off its crown.

It unfolds upward into the unobstructed daylight until it stands nearly nine feet tall.

Seen at full height in that empty room of air, it is startlingly narrow—a slender, waistless spire of bog-dark flesh balanced on two broad, flat, root-splayed feet that spread its weight so gently across the living mattress that the sphagnum barely dimples under it. Its limbs are disproportionately long and strangely jointed, bending with a supple, boneless grace. In a region where bedrock dissolved into flat clay eons ago and not a pebble remains beneath the moss, heavy chalky bone would be an extravagance; its frame is built instead of dense, flexible cartilage and water-stiffened fibrous cords, closer to wet alder wood or the springy skeleton of a sturgeon than to anything that walks on stone.

Its head is small, smooth, and wedge-shaped, pitched slightly back toward the pale sky. Where eyes might be, there are only two cloudy, lidless pale smears set low behind the snout—good enough to tell the open daylight from the sixty-foot tree-wall, useless for detail. Its mouth is a wide, lipless seam that stays cleanly shut.

What gives it presence—what makes it look suddenly less like a bog-beast and more like a slender, ancient instrument built for this exact room—unfolds only once it reaches its full height.

Along the sides of its narrow jaw, down the long column of its neck, and lining the outer edges of its raised, outstretched forearms lie dense curtains of fine, translucent filaments. While the creature crouches in the moss, those fringes stay plastered slick against its dark hide, looking like nothing more than pale submerged rootlets or wet sedge-threads. As it lifts its long arms toward the silver-grey cables arcing across the sky, the filaments do not hang down.

They rise on their own.

Beaded with cool condensation, hundreds of pale, hair-fine tendrils float straight upward into the empty air, fanning into a delicate, shivering halo drawn by the invisible alternating field of the trunk lines overhead. At dusk, if you stood close enough, you might catch microscopic pinpricks of pale blue-violet corona trembling at the wet tips of the highest threads. In the pale northern daylight, you see only the faint lift and quiver of them—like drowned hair rising in a slow current—while swifts and barn swallows whip effortlessly past the creature’s raised shoulders, snatching midges out of the charged haze around its head.

It stands there for twenty minutes, sometimes an hour, motionless as the gnarled birch. Through those lifted, salt-damp filaments it drinks the faint, steady gradient between the screaming wires and the grounded peat—not a shock, never a burn, just a clean, tireless electric tickle running down its wet nerves and pouring softly out through the soles of its wide feet into the acidic sponge below.

That charge does not feed it alone, though it seems to wake its sluggish, cold-blooded metabolism the way sun wakes a lizard on a wall. The filaments serve a second, quieter purpose. Clouds of midges, gnats, and craneflies rise endlessly off the damp moss, and as they drift through the faint static halo around the standing creature, hundreds of them are pulled lightly onto the beaded condensation clinging to its pale fringes. When the damper air moves on and the sizzle overhead eases back to its baseline crackle, the tall dark spire lowers its arms, folds its rubbery knees, and settles slowly back into the sphagnum. Once tucked low among the cranberry runners, it draws its long, splayed forelimbs patiently across the wide seam of its mouth, combing off the damp paste of midges, pollen, and dew.

They rarely hunt anything larger. Now and then, one of the starlings or crows packed along the overhead cables loses its grip, catches its wings across a spacer, or simply dies of old age and drops sixty feet into the soft floor, smelling faintly of singed feathers and ozone. When that happens, you might see two of the dark humps shift gradually over the course of an afternoon, creeping through the sedge with a wide-limbed, swimming gait until they reach the fallen bird. They do not tear at it. They press it gently down into a wet crease of sphagnum and leave it there for several days while the acidic bog-water softens the hollow bird-bones into rubbery, mineral-rich gristle, before returning to swallow what the peat has prepared. In a world made entirely of plant fiber and rainwater, a single fallen crow is a rare gift of calcium.

There are no more than five or six of them in the entire hollow—perhaps as few as four, though it is almost impossible to take a true count because they seldom stand at the same time and they move with the glacial economy of things that never hurry.

Yet they are rarely out of touch with one another.

In a basin flooded with turbine whine, cooling fans, frog-trills, and scolding jackdaws, ordinary voices would be drowned or forced into frantic high shrieks. They have chosen the opposite end of the register. Beneath the moss-grown skin of their narrow chests lie twin fluid-walled chambers tuned closely to the sixty-cycle bass of the surrounding substations. When one of them presses its throat or flat belly against the springy root-mat—or stands tall with its broad soles planted firmly in the wet peat—it vibrates those chambers just half a cycle off the transformers’ constant note.

The result is not a sound you hear with your ears. It is a physical interference wave—a slow, rhythmic swelling and ebbing of pressure that travels straight through the water-clenched fibers from rim to rim, blurring and sharpening the concentric ripples in every dark puddle along the way.

That is their speech. Across two hundred paces of roaring air, one of them leans slightly flat against the engines’ bass, sending a three-second pulse through the peat. A minute later, from somewhere near the northern birch, a second chest answers slightly sharp, setting up a slower, braided throb underfoot. If a fox trots along the sheltered cedar rim, the pulse pauses briefly; if a distant press behind the western trees misses a stroke during a shift change, two or three of them answer in quick succession, rippling the entire floor like a struck drumhead until the rhythm settles back into place.

To them, the whole hollow is a single resonant membrane. Through their flat soles and pressed jaws they read the frantic darting of voles and water rails in the dry-topped tunnels below them, the soft landing of a crow on a branch in the ivy wall, the exact weight of rain held in the yards of compressed peat beneath their knees.

And they know the location of the one hard thing in their universe.

Near the southern edge, half-swallowed by thick green sphagnum, sits the forgotten cast-iron boundary pin left behind when the engineers surveyed around the unbuildable pocket. Every few days, one of the creatures drags itself quietly down to that southern crease and curls beside the pin, wrapping a long, dark, web-fingered hand around the corroded shaft. Nothing else within sixty feet of the ground is rigid. Even the ancient cedar walls give slightly to the touch; even the dwarf birch is soft with lichen and damp. Only the iron pin is cold, dense, mineral certainty. Through that buried spike, the mechanical thunder of the surrounding plain arrives unmuffled by peat—stripped of its mossy softness, crisp enough to feel the individual chatter of bearings, the clean ring of steel housings, the faint metallic shiver induced directly in the iron by the overhead lines. Sometimes the creature presses its blind wedge of a face right against the shaft, lapping lightly at the orange-tinged water pooled around its collar, taking in the sharp taste of rust. Over the decades, while the rest of the pin has flaked into reddish scale, one palm-sized curve near its rounded crown has been rubbed smooth to a dull, wet pewter sheen.

They do not build shelters, clear paths, or mark territory. Even their dying leaves no ruin behind.

They live on a scale closer to the perimeter hemlocks than to the foxes or birds—tanned and preserved by the cool acidic water even while alive, spending almost no energy, letting the steady waste-warmth of the surrounding industrial plain keep them from ever having to endure a killing freeze. When one of them does finally grow too old and stiff to lift its fringes toward the wires, it simply chooses a quiet crease not far from the center, folds its long limbs tightly beneath its moss-carpeted back, and stops answering the slow pulses in the peat. Because its flesh is already poor in mineral bone and braided with tough, water-loving fiber, it does not rot into foulness. Within a few wet seasons, living bog-cranberry runners, sedge rhizomes, and creeping sphagnum knit seamlessly through its hide and cartilage, anchoring it permanently into the floor.

The low hump of fused roots wrapped in moss fifty yards from the dwarf birch is older than any of the factories beyond the trees, and softer inside than rootwood ought to be. Now and then, one of the living ones will come and rest its narrow flank against that hump for an afternoon, listening patiently, though nothing inside it has leaned against the hum in a very long time.

Only when one of the old ones goes silent does another replace it—so slowly that the hollow’s balance never tilts. Deep inside the dark labyrinth beneath that mossy hump, tucked away from the open daylight, a single leathery, pale sac is occasionally pressed into the warm, damp walls of an abandoned vole tunnel. What hatches from it is no larger than a marsh rail: blind, translucent as a submerged rootlet, trailing feathery external gills. For its first several years it never breaks the surface at all. It glides silently through the braided root-passages alongside the shrews and voles, grazing on cranefly larvae and sunken frogspawn, learning the sixty-cycle tremor from underneath while its fibrous limbs lengthen and darken, waiting until it has enough weight and fringe to push its back up into the roaring daylight and feel the sky pull at its skin for the first time.

Before the substations, the cooling plants, and the trunk cables existed, their kind must have lived a harder, thinner life. This sunken saucer was just one cold pocket on a flat, wet plain then—quieter, darker, locked in solid ice for months at a time. In those ages they would have lain half-dormant in the deep peat through long silent winters, rising only in the heavy, breathless air before summer thunderstorms, stretching their pale filaments up into the sudden electrostatic surge to catch wind-swept insects before the lightning broke and drove them flat again.

When industry ringed the hollow and strung three rivers of bare aluminum straight across its empty sky, the surrounding bogs were drained or buried under concrete. Here, the engineers stopped at the ancient trees and left the pocket to itself. For the creatures buried in the mat, the arrival of the machines was not an intrusion. It was the end of winter. The cooling plants blunted the January frost; the turbines poured an unbroken, comforting bass straight into the roots; the overhead wires hung a permanent, tamed thunderstorm sixty feet above their heads—always humming, always charged, never striking. Everything tentative in them settled. They locked into the finished state of the hollow as cleanly as the frogs and the peat.

If you stand still near the center of the green floor long enough—not moving, letting the cool damp climb past your ankles—one of them may notice you at last.

To its perception, you are a strange, slightly troubling shape. Your boots insulate you from the grounded moss, turning you into an awkward vertical knot in the electric field, ungrounded and humming faintly out of place. Inside your chest beats a rapid, erratic, wet little rhythm that matches neither the presses nor the transformers, and your footsteps carry the sharp, dense clack of real bone.

Thirty paces away, near the leaning birch, a mossy swell may lift smoothly out of the sedge and unfold into the pale daylight until it towers above the stunted tree. It does not advance. It does not threaten. It simply turns its smooth, blind wedge of a head in your direction and lets its pale, wet-beaded fringes float upward toward the crackling wires, tasting the static distortion you cast across the air. For a few seconds, the concentric ripples in the dark puddle beside your boot blur and thicken sharply as it sends a slow, questioning pulse through the peat beneath you, waiting to feel whether you will lean back against the bass.

You have no chest-chambers tuned to sixty cycles. You only stand there in your rubber soles, silent inside the roar, breathing cool air that smells of wet moss, sweet-gale, and faint ozone.

After a minute, having measured you and found nothing to answer—nothing solid enough to trouble the equilibrium, just a passing shadow like a white plume of cooling vapor drifting above the eastern canopy—it lowers its long arms. Its translucent fringes lay themselves back down along its dark hide. With a quiet, wet sigh of sphagnum giving way, it folds its tall frame smoothly into the cranberry mat until only a moss-plated mound remains, a chorus frog already hopping back onto its shoulder to trill, while the wires hiss overhead and the hollow goes on holding its exact temperature, its exact ripples, and its exact note without pause.
