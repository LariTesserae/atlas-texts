If you stand at the rolled rim of the bowl and look down into the roar, you will not see them at first.

That is partly because your eyes are dragged immediately by everything that flashes and sprints—the plum-sized beetles rattling across the sunlit gaps, the pale-feathered runners snapping lizards off the walls, the sudden spiral explosions of needle-beaked birds whenever a stray tongue of plateau wind curls over the lip. In a pocket that loud and bright, anything motionless ceases to register as alive. It drops back into the geology.

What gives them away, if you stay long enough for your ears to stop flinching from the tidal *clack-chime* of the bell-lizards, are three small wrongnesses in the wildness of the floor.

The first is water behaving against its own habits. From the wettest edge of the southern moss-mound, a single hair-thin trickle does not wander out and evaporate after three paces like the others. Someone has wedged three flat chips of rose-ochre stone into the emerald rim, sealing the gaps with damp mud-wasp clay, and steered that bright thread directly onto one of the mirror-bright floor inlays. Caught by surface tension along the millimeter-proud metallic seam, the water clings and runs twelve paces straight across the blazing dry granite before the heat finally drinks it.

The second hangs beneath the highest of the four hovering slats. There, the translucent chartreuse algae-moss that bridges the gap into the slat’s trembling, still-air envelope does not dangle in loose curtains. Its pale green filaments have been combed and twisted into taut, evenly spaced vertical cords, each weighted at the bottom with a pierced, hollowed copper beetle wing-case. Fat warm droplets detach from the hovering gray arc, catch on the braided green strings, and glide silently down without splashing into waste—while the copper weights turn slowly in the sunlit damp, flashing tiny amber sparks across the bare rock.

The third is a habit of the hunters. Watch the earless, pale-feathered runners as they stalk across the exposed floor, and you will see them veer cleanly around a low, smooth swell of rose-ochre stone tucked beside the westernmost dwarf fig—giving it a wide, stiff-legged berth even when a mottled skink darts right past its base.

Keep your eyes fixed on that smooth swell of stone, and wait.

Presently, it draws a slow breath, and a seam opens across what you took for pillowed granite.

They are neither lizard nor ape, though at a glance from the rim a newcomer might reach clumsily for either word before realizing how poorly both fit. Their bodies are low, supple, and long-backed—about the length of a crouched human from crown to hip, though they rarely stand upright, preferring to glide across the open stone on all fours or sit folded cleanly on their haunches with their long arms free. They have no fur; the noon oven of the bowl would cook anything wrapped in pelt that did not spend the bright hours underground. Instead, their bellies, throats, and limbs are clad in dry, fine-grained skin the exact pale rose-ochre of the basin floor, while their shoulders, spines, and the crowns of their heads are tiled in broad, overlapping plates of smooth matte keratin.

There is nothing jagged or defensive in the shape of those plates. Over decades of sliding beneath the overhanging lip of the bowl and pressing flat against the granite when raptors drop from the cobalt sky, the keratin has worn pillowed and satin-smooth, matching the bedrock’s own bone-soft curves. When one tucks its limbs beneath itself and lowers its wedge-shaped head onto its folded forearms, it vanishes into the pale floor so thoroughly that even a hawk skimming ten feet overhead sees nothing but another weathered bulge of ancient rock.

Three of them hold the bowl today, with a fourth still waiting to arrive.

The oldest is the one by the western fig—broader through the shoulders than the others, the ridges of its back-plates bleached nearly chalk-white by half a century of mountain sun. An old, pale furrow cuts cleanly across its left shoulder-plate where a cliff-raptor’s talon once skidded and failed to find purchase. Its face, when it finally lifts its head from its forearms, is narrow and remarkably still: small, tight-lipped mouth, heavy overhanging brow-plates shading deep-set eyes of pale amber-gray that blink with a quick, wet inner lid against the dry grit. It has no outer ears. Set cleanly behind the hinges of its jaw are two oval, flush-fitting horn-lids—like miniature clam-valves—sealed shut against the teeth-vibrating noon roar of the bell-lizards.

Watch the oldest one move across the open apron of bare stone, and you begin to understand the razor-sharp lines sunk into the floor.

It walks on broad, dry palms with its four long fingers curled slightly back so the chisel-flat horn nails do not dull against the granite. Its hind feet, however, step with an uncanny, fastidious precision straight along the broken mirror-bright arc, placing the cleft tough pads of its soles so the millimeter-proud filament runs lightly beneath its arches without ever cutting skin. Halfway across the floor, where two hairline inlays intersect, it stops. Slowly, it lowers its chest to the hot rock, folds its elbows out, and rests the bare underside of its lower jaw directly across the mirror-bright thread.

Look closely at its chin, and you would see a faint, glassy notch worn into the toughened horn along the jawline—a groove polished over decades of doing exactly this.

Then, by a sliver no wider than a grass-blade, the flush horn-valves behind its jaw crack open, revealing pale, wet, intricately fluted inner membranes.

Instantly, the deafening wall of bell-lizard song recedes into a thin, metallic hum, and the bowl sorts itself into crisp, separate lives straight through the bone of its skull. Along that silver-bright lens, the oldest one hears the drip-tempo of all four hovering slats at once—reading a subtle slowing in the warm *plip-plip* of the northern arc minutes before any cloud appears on the cobalt rim. It hears the dry rasp of a snail’s shell scraping across a crimson-veined leaf twenty paces away; the tiny *tock* of a pale-feathered runner’s hinged beak snapping shut behind the eastern thicket; the restless shifting of gray-furred cliff-dwellers deep in the horizontal rim-seams.

It is not merely listening. Beside the intersection sit three small, smooth pebbles that do not belong to the rose-ochre plateau—two dark basalt, one milky quartz—and a damp pad of emerald moss no bigger than a thumb. With two long dry fingers, the oldest one nudges the wet moss pad a half-inch onto the branching filament, then shifts the milky quartz pebble into a tiny hairline crack where the bedrock has settled and snapped the inlay apart. The acoustic focus tilts. A sound from the glassy eastern alcove sharpens; the chatter of the southern thicket softens and drops away. Whatever the makers built these inlaid geometries to do, the oldest one treats them as frets on an instrument, dampening one branch and bridging another until the whole thirty-pace basin lies legible beneath its jaw.

Across the floor, in the bright shade of the tall central dwarf fig, the second adult is already at work.

It is slightly smaller, longer-limbed, its smooth dorsal plates still a rich, warm rose-ochre unmarked by white sun-bleach. Where the oldest one keeps the lines and the sky, this younger mate is the bowl’s gardener and hunter. It moves through the afternoon heat with an unhurried, liquid economy that makes the frantic sprinting of skinks and runners look wasteful. Sitting upright on its folded haunches beside the lowest of the four hovering slats, it works a thick cushion of springy moss with its flat chisel-nails, parting the wet emerald mat to clear away choked fig-roots so the palm-sized puddle beneath the drip-line spreads wide and clean. Two pale-feathered runners linger three yards off, heads cocked at jerky angles; as soon as the plated gardener peels back a soggy flap of moss and glassy-bodied ants and pale grubs boil out onto the warm stone, the runners dart in, plucking the scattering insects with lightning strikes while staying politely beyond the reach of those long, dry arms.

Now and then a plum-sized copper beetle drones too low across the open gap between thickets. The gardener does not chase it. Its arm simply unfolds—faster than anything so slow-moving has a right to be—and its palm closes cleanly around the rattling insect in midair. With two chisel-nails it splits the thorax cleanly, eats the rich pale meat inside, and lays the unbroken, gleaming copper wing-cases carefully beside a braided highway of glassy-bodied ants. By sunset, the ants will have scrubbed every speck of tissue from the concave inner shells, leaving two clean, burnished bowls ready to be carried back beneath the western overhang.

Even the bell-lizards—the deafening chorus that fills the pocket—are tended with a quiet, calculating restraint. The gardener takes only an occasional slow or ragged singer from the sunlit walls, and never two from the same curve of stone. To silence a whole wall would leave a blind spot in the basin’s acoustic skin; so long as the chorus rolls unbroken around the bowl, any sudden thinning or dropped beat tells them before their eyes ever lift that a hawk has banked over the rim, or a snake has slipped out of a deep fissure.

And if you trace the gardener’s gaze upward, to the third hovering slat—the one standing slightly crooked over the eastern moss-mound, half-screened by a denser curtain of braided chartreuse algae-moss—you will find the fourth member of the household.

Passed cleanly around the narrow, curved tip of the matte gray arc, where the sleek ground-hunters never bother to perch, is a thin, supple loop of stripped dwarf-fig root-fiber. Hanging from it, suspended in an open-weave cradle of pale epiphyte lace barely an inch below the slat’s lower edge, rests a single pear-shaped egg the size of a goose’s.

It hangs dead inside the trembling two-inch optical envelope of still air.

Out beyond that thin shimmer, the afternoon heat bakes the pale rock, midges swarm, and stray gusts of mountain wind spill over the rim to whip the dwarf-fig crowns. Inside that two-inch boundary, nothing stirs. The air is breathless, humid, and warm as sunlit clay. Fat droplets materialize along the matte gray arc right beside the woven sling, swell until they flash white in the sun, and slip past the leathery shell to splash onto the emerald moss below. No ant can climb down the bare air to reach it; the hanging algae-curtains beside it are kept combed clean of any bridging twigs; and a small cluster of three dry copper wing-cases tied to the outer knot of the loop clacks sharply at the slightest tremble, warning off any bold skink or needle-beaked bird. When the high sun strikes straight through the wet green veil, the pale, leathery shell turns faintly translucent, showing a slow, dark curl shifting inside—keeping time with the steady *plip* of warm water.

Watching that hanging sling from the deep green shadow of the broad, crimson-veined shield leaves below is the third inhabitant: a half-grown juvenile, no bigger than a coiled hare.

Its back-plates have not yet thickened into bone-pale armor; they are still thin, flexible, and faintly translucent, flushed pinkish-amber by the blood beneath. Its ear-valves are soft-rimmed and clumsy, slow to seal tight. Whenever the early afternoon heat drives the basin into its loudest roar—the slats pattering fast, midges lifting in glittering clouds, thousands of translucent lizard-throats vibrating the stone walls—the small one clamps both broad, pink-palmed hands flat against the sides of its head and buries its narrow face into a cool pad of wet moss until the acoustic tide drops for half a breath. Too tender to risk the wide exposed floor under the raptors’ sky, it stays tucked among the corky fig-roots, practicing with solemn concentration on a stray, broken stub of mirror-bright inlay that runs beneath the leaves—laying its small chin carefully against the proud metallic line, then lifting its head in wide-eyed startlement every time a distant snail’s scrape bursts suddenly crisp inside its bones.

Then a single high cloud drifts across the cobalt sky.

The light dims by a shade; the air inside the sheltered bowl cools by a fraction of a degree. The entire bell-lizard chorus drops a key in a single ragged heartbeat. The patter of the hovering slats slows back to a measured *plip*. Overhead, the cliff-raptors bank away into the thin plateau wind, and from the glassy eastern alcove the gray-furred cliff-dwellers spill out in a bickering tide to strip fallen figs from the open floor.

Two sharp, quiet *tocks* travel instantly along the main mirror-bright arc.

The oldest one has struck a dried copper wing-case twice against the proud edge of the inlay. Across twenty paces of bare stone, the gardener hears it clear through the heel of its palm; beneath the eastern fig-roots, the small soft-plated one lifts its head from the moss, ear-valves fluttering open.

This two-minute dimming is their open hour. While the sky is empty of shadows and the stone cools just enough to soften the glare, the juvenile sprints—low, quick, and slightly clumsy—across the wide pale apron of bare rock from the eastern thicket to the center of the bowl, following the faint, rhythmic guide-taps of the oldest one’s fingernail along the silver-bright line. In the sudden spacious hush of the open floor, the three of them meet. The gardener drops three split, sun-warmed dwarf figs between them, crushed open so the small one can eat the sweet seeded pulp without straining its soft jaw. While the juvenile eats, the two adults lean shoulder to pale shoulder, their long chisel-nailed fingers working cleanly along the overlapping seams of each other’s back-plates, lifting out dried specks of plateau grit and brushing away the fine white salt-bloom left by a morning’s heat.

For ninety seconds, the bowl belongs wholly to them—a quiet, low-voiced household of soft throat-clicks and angled ear-valves sitting right in the candid center of the stone, while around them the gray-furred cliff-dwellers dart fearlessly past their knees to snatch fig-skins, and the pale-feathered runners watch from the hovering slats with tilted, unblinking heads.

Then the cloud slips past the sun. Bright unfiltered light slams back into the bowl; the bell-lizards inhale and crash back into their high, brassy key; the slats quicken. Before the first distant raptor-shadow flickers across the cobalt circle overhead, the small one has bolted back beneath the waxy shield-leaves, the gardener has slipped into the shade of the highest moss-mound, and the oldest one has folded itself back into a smooth, motionless swell of rose-ochre stone.

If you climb down into the bowl and crouch low beneath the rolled western overhang—where the smooth rock lip curves inward to form a shadowed, windless shelf invisible from the rim—you find the quiet proof of how long their kind has kept this pocket.

Tucked cleanly into the dry concave curve of the wall sit their stores, arranged with an almost unsettling neatness: nested stacks of copper beetle wing-cases sorted by curve and size; tight coils of stripped, water-softened dwarf-fig root-fiber; bundles of dried pale epiphyte lace saved for the next egg-sling; a few pale, hollowed bird-bones notched at the ends for combing wet algae-moss.

And beside them, laid in a single straight row along a shallow natural shelf of granite, sit forty-seven smooth pebbles.

Not one of them is rose-ochre. They are river-worn black basalt, milky quartz, pale green serpentine, speckled red jasper, and dark blue flint—stones that do not exist anywhere within a week’s walk of this bone-smooth roof of the world. Every pebble in that row was carried here in a dry palm or tucked inside a jaw-pouch by a solitary wanderer who crossed the immense, planed-flat tableland in the dark, hunting for the faint acoustic signature of another hidden pocket of water.

Because the bowl cannot feed a tribe, and they know it in their slow bones.

When dusk finally drains the cobalt sky to violet-black, the high-altitude cold drops fast over the plateau. The bell-lizards wedge deep into warm rock-cracks and fall silent at last. The copper beetles fold their wings. The four hovering slats slow to a rare, heavy *plip* every few minutes, still warm as sunlit clay, breathing tiny invisible domes of heat into the freezing dark.

Now the glassy eastern alcove—too hot and acoustically blinding to bear at noon—changes its purpose. Deep inside its smooth, soap-bubble curve, the gray-furred cliff-dwellers pack themselves into a thick, musky, churring quilt amid their chewed fig-hulls and damp leaves. Just inside the arched mouth of the hollow, blocking the thin cold drift of night air with their broad, smooth-plated backs, the two adults curl tight around the soft-plated juvenile. There is no quarrel between them and the burrowers; in the dark, the boldest cliff-dwellers creep forward to press against the warm, hairless bellies of the plated dwellers, sharing body-heat until first light turns the rim pale.

Sometimes, in the deepest, coldest hour before dawn—when frost feathers the outer tableland and not a single wing or lizard-throat moves anywhere in the basin—the oldest one uncurls softly, steps over the sleeping gray bodies without waking them, and walks out alone onto the cold, bare floor.

It lowers itself flat along the main mirror-bright arc, settles the worn glassy groove of its chin onto the millimeter-proud thread, and cranks both horn ear-valves wide open.

With the basin’s daytime roar gone, the curved stone bowl and its broken metallic inlays stop reflecting the pocket inward and begin acting like an ear turned out across the immense flat world above the rim. Pressed to that razor-thin line in the freezing silence, the oldest one hears the thin rush of mountain wind skimming twenty miles of bare stone; the distant, low pop of cliff-edges contracting in the frost; the faint, lonely whistle of night-hawks far out over the dark plain.

And every few years, it hears something else: the soft, unhurried, four-footed tread of a young wanderer—grown in some distant, hidden bowl days away across the barren rock—pausing every fifty paces out on the dark tableland, laying its own small chin to the bare granite, listening for the steady warm *plip* of water-engines and the faint rustle of fig-leaves below the wind-line.

When the small one tucked beneath the eastern thicket grows broad-backed and hard-plated enough to leave, it too will climb over the rolled lip on a still, cloudy dusk, carrying a single rose-ochre chip from this basin tucked behind its cheek, and vanish across the smooth bone-geometry of the plateau to search for a pocket of its own. And when the oldest one’s back-plates bleach thin and brittle, and its ear-valves stiffen so the inlays blur back into mere noise, it will not lie down to foul the emerald moss or the warm pools. Some quiet night it will climb out past the rim, walk fifty paces onto the flat expanse where the bowl disappears completely from sight, and fold itself smoothly into the rock beneath the thin wind—leaving the four floating slats, the braided green curtains, the row of foreign stones beneath the overhang, and the newly hatched one in its lace sling ready for the morning sun to strike the stone and set the whole small, loud, impossible engine roaring again.
