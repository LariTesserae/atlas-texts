You miss them on the first day, and probably the second, because you are looking for something that breathes. You watch the sun-struck avenues for a stride, a wingbeat, a twitch of antennae in a pool of weeping gum. You listen through the deafening hydraulic uproar for a call that breaks the rhythm of splitting wood. Finding none, you take the valley at its word and decide the city is empty.

You only begin to see what lives here once the heat drives you off the open cobbles and into one of the shaded underpasses, where pale, spongy pith-walls sweat lukewarm moisture into shallow basins grown directly into the avenue floor.

Sit still beside a basin for ten minutes. Let the boom of the swelling ironwood trunks pass through your ribs until your ears stop fighting it. Then look at the lip of the basin, where you assumed dried sap had crusted into a pale, translucent varnish.

A pressure-shift rolls down the block outside. The avenue darkens under a brief, warm squall of guttation, and the air inside the underpass dips and rebounds. Along the rim of the basin, the varnish lifts.

It does not crawl. It does not ripple like wet flesh. It separates cleanly from the spongy pith in crisp, overlapping facets—thin as onion-parchment, stiff as cured horn, veined with fine chalk-white ridges. A sharp, pleated seam unfolds across the water's edge, dipping a narrow bevel into the lukewarm sweat. Liquid climbs the translucent skin in sudden amber capillaries. Then the squall ends, the heat drinks the street dry outside, and the papery ridge snaps flat against the wall again with a faint, dry *tick* indistinguishable from a curling bark-shaving.

Once you have seen one move, you cannot stop seeing them. They are everywhere—pressed flush to the undersides of cantilevered balconies, slotted upright into the grooved jambs of doorways, layered like stiff stucco reliefs along the shadowed walls of newly extruded rooms.

They are neither animal nor plant. They have no lungs, no mouths, no legs, no blood, and no soft organs that the feverish afternoon could cook.

Catch a mature one inside a ground-floor chamber when the sun slants low through braided cane-louvers, backlighting it against the shade. Unfolded from the wall, it stands roughly the height of a person and the breadth of a pair of shoulders—tall, narrow, angular, built from dozens of interlocking, hollow geometric chambers. Its outer skin is a taut, semi-translucent laminate of amber-cured resin and chalk-pale mineral crust, knife-edged along every crease, matching the fresh, unweathered crispness of the architecture around it. There is no face, only a shifting arrangement of flat and angled planes, like a pleated screen or a dry, hollow husk that has been folded with extreme precision.

Look through the sunlit amber walls into its chest, and you will see what keeps it alive.

Inside each hollow cell, taut, glistening filaments stretch from corner to corner like the strings of an instrument, beaded here and there with tiny, hoarded droplets of lukewarm water. Suspended between them, drifting in mid-air inside the sealed chambers, are clouds of fine chalk-pollen and golden spores. They do not fall to the bottom. With every chest-rattling boom of a hauling trunk outside, every pistol-crack of splitting bark, every long pneumatic hiss of cavitating sap, the roaring vibration of the street pours straight into those hollow, tuned cells—and the suspended dust locks into razor-sharp, intricate geometric lattices. Nested hexagons, branching frets, concentric rings: they tremble, dissolve, and re-draw themselves ten times a second.

They do not breathe air; they run on the uproar. The city's relentless hydraulic din is their circulation, their weather, and their thought.

They never walk across the dusty root-cobbles. A thing made of tense, dry chambers and brittle mineral seams has no business striding through open glare and falling bark-masonry. On short journeys, they travel flush against surfaces—never lifting fully clear of the wood, never breaking contact with the shaded vertical planes of walls, lintels, and arched vaults. A single vertical margin peels a fraction of an inch from the ironwood, swells slightly with trapped warm vapor, and seals down two inches farther along; a dry, pleated crease travels silently across the rest of the faceted body until the whole frame has shifted along the wall, like a shadow detaching and re-attaching itself by slow, crisp folds.

For longer distances, they let the broken geometry carry them.

They know the city's folds the way a sailor knows currents. Watch one slide edge-first into the narrow vine-framed doorway of a ten-foot wedge building, or press itself flat against a lintel where causality is slipping—that uncanny three-foot span where fresh green bud, mature furrowed timber, and sun-bleached hollow deadwood flicker over one another in the same space. When the beam blinks into hollow deadwood, the faceted body is already slotted inside the cavity; when the wood reasserts itself as fresh green timber, the inhabitant is gone, carried sideways through a seam to emerge in an upper gallery three streets west, or down inside a cool root-arcade two floors below a plaza that has not finished heaving out of the ground.

Some of them linger deliberately astride those flickering seams, never wholly leaving them. You can find an old one braced along an arched viaduct where two decades occupy the same yard of wood: its left chambers pale, supple, and damp with sour condensation; its center taut, sun-warmed honey-amber, humming with intricate pollen-diagrams; its right flank already scorched chalk-white and flaking into fine dust that stalls in the thermal draft and clicks cleanly back into place along its outer ridge. By straddling the slip, it borrows the freshness of an earlier hour to keep its dry edges from cracking apart, holding itself together across the city's frantic demolitions.

They do not eat leaves, and they ignore the sweet, sticky pools of weeping sap—leaving them untouched, which is why the streets feel so eerily sterile to a traveler looking for flies or beetles. What they need is simpler and stranger: moisture, mineral salt, sour vapor, and resonance.

When a guttation squall breaks over a block, look up at the third-story buttress-leaves and the shaded eaves. Dozens of them unhinge outward from the walls all at once like louvered shutters catching rain, fanning their pleated planes into the warm spray. Droplets bead along their translucent skins and vanish rapidly into fine pores along every seam; then, seconds before the dry heat swallows the street again, they fold flush against the timber in a synchronized, papery clatter that sounds, from the cobbles below, like nothing more than dry fronds rattling in an updraft.

Their real nurseries and hospitals lie beneath the avenues.

Slip a pry-bar—or simply your gaze—past one of the woven twig drainage grates, down into the hot, dark underworld of root-tunnels where vast, bulbous pitcher-cisterns hang from the vaulted ceilings. Down here, away from the scouring sun, the air is thick with damp, acidic steam vented from the warm sour broth inside the pitchers.

Clinging flat to the sweating, leathery bellies of those hanging cisterns are hundreds of the inhabitants, pressed close to drink the sour mist through their seams. In the dry upper streets, the fever-heat slowly bakes them brittle, crazing their amber skins with hairline splits and slackening their internal filaments until their pollen-lattices blur into meaningless haze. Down in the root-cellars, the acidic vapor softens their resinous coats just enough for fractures to weld shut and new translucent laminae to lay themselves down along the joints.

And at the foaming inner rims of the pitchers—where digested bark-lint, dissolved resin, and leached salts skin over in the dark heat—new ones begin. They form first as thin, pale, curled slips no wider than a palm, peeling gently off the wet lip of the cistern like delicate mineral film. Most slacken, fold inward, and drop back into the sour broth to be digested again. But the root-tunnels hum constantly with the muffled, low-frequency throb of the churning metropolis overhead. Now and then, a freshly peeled slip dries at the exact angle needed to catch a harmonic pulse humming down a hollow taproot. Its edges stiffen. A first crisp crease snaps down its center, trapping a pocket of warm vapor and a pinch of golden spores. Within hours, it begins creeping edge-over-edge up the damp cellar-shafts toward the twig grates, pale and three-chambered, timid of the glare, learning to hold its shape against the roar.

At the opposite extreme of the city—high on the four- and five-story gables, clinging to the sun-baked cornices where no dampness ever reaches—live the oldest ones.

They are far too large and rigid now to fold down through a drainage grate. Weeks or years of dry glare have cured their layered skins to deep, opaque burnt-ochre, heavy as horn, ridged with thick white salt-crusts along every fold. Some span the whole width of a gable-end, branching into fifty or sixty thick-walled chambers. They survive up there on hoarded interior water and sheer concussion. When the melon-sized woody seed-pods dry out along the cornices and detonate, hailing hard buckshot across the frond-awnings, these old horn-stiff residents angle their broad, armored facets straight into the blast. They take the shockwave like bronze bells; you can feel the low, sustained ring of their chambers vibrating through the rafters for minutes afterward.

Eventually, even the strongest dry too far. There is no decay, no rot, no corpse for scavengers to strip. One afternoon, under a high sun, an elder's internal tension gives out along a mineral seam. It parts cleanly with a single sharp snap—just one more pistol-crack in the valley's constant uproar—and shears into a shower of curled, papery scales that drift down into the avenue to mingle indistinguishably with the bark-shavings dusting the cobbles.

Yet it is in the middle stories—the ground-floor rooms, the narrow stepped lanes, the street corners, and the vaulted organ-pipe arcades—that you realize these are not merely clever, acoustic parasites clinging to a wild botany. They are citizens, and the city's eerie, corrupted mimicry of civic life is the direct result of their hands—or rather, their pressed, vibrating faces.

They have no voices to cast across the open air; any shout would be shredded instantly by the volume of the streets. Instead, they speak through wood.

Watch a street corner where the plant-mass has recently extruded one of those broad, flat slabs of pale, fresh bark. Two or three of the shoulder-wide inhabitants slide down from the surrounding eaves and press their translucent chests flush against the still-soft, furiously dividing surface. Inside their backlit chambers, their pollen-lattices shift into dense, rapid, interlocking figures—exchanging the shifting map of the morning's folds, the pressure-state of the third-story cisterns, the warning of which gabled block's heartwood is softening toward collapse.

Their tuned vibrations pass straight through their resinous skins into the wet cambium beneath.

The plant tissue has no mind of its own—only frantic, hyper-accelerated growth and a hair-trigger sensitivity to pressure and resonance. Under the steady, structured hum of the pressed panels, the bark's surface veins begin to divide along the standing waves, etching crisp, parallel rows that almost resolve into neat columns of lettering. For a few minutes, while the inhabitants remain pressed to the board, the ledger is legible—not to eyes, but to any faceted frame that slides over the raised grain afterward and reads the pattern back into its own chambers. Then the speakers detach, peeling away along the adjoining wall. Deprived of their tuning, the mindless cambium keeps dividing, loses the thread, and splinters the neat parallel veins into frantic, meaningless bifurcations.

The same quiet negotiation shapes every room they occupy.

When a cluster of them settles inside a freshly unfolded ground-floor chamber, folding themselves neatly along the rising root-wood to share a calm acoustic node out of the afternoon glare, the rapidly growing wood conforms to their sustained, rhythmic pressure. It extrudes cleanly around their contours—knee-high ledges where their lower edges rest, narrow alcove tables where three or four can press their margins together to share a harmonic current, doorways scaled precisely to the height and breadth of their unfolded frames. The city does not build for absent humans; it grows furniture around the habits of its dry, upright tenants, who themselves seem caught in the echo of civic forms—thresholds, counters, galleries—drifted in through whatever broken seams lend the folded courtyards their different skies.

And when a dispute breaks out—when two inhabitants press sharp, dissonant vibrations into the same newly grown tabletop, or when a sudden collapse down the block startles a gathering into snapping their pleats shut and fleeing through a spatial fold—the harmonic signal shears in half. The confused cambium jolts, tangling the half-finished bench into an impenetrable cage of thorns, or panicking into runaway division that plugs the entire chamber solid with raw timber.

Even the phantom bazaar—the deafening, unmistakable acoustic texture of ten thousand muffled voices, distant hammering, and slamming shutters that greets you at every corner—is their work. Perched by the thousands inside the hollow ironwood pillars, the vaulted root-arcades, and the tight resonant alleys, they angle their stiff, faceted planes like acoustic louvers and tuned reeds. They catch the blind, brutal uproar of splitting cellulose and cavitating sap and braid it, bend it, and gate it down the street grid, turning raw hydraulic noise into modulated corridors of signal. No voice answers the roar because they are the ones sculpting the roar into speech.

To see them all at once—not hidden along jambs or flattened into stucco reliefs, but gathered in the open—you have to step through one of the spatial folds.

Find a narrow, wedge-shaped building barely ten feet wide from the street, push past the braided creepers hanging across its vine-framed doorway, and step into one of the vast, open-air courtyards tucked impossibly inside its footprint.

The daylight here is ordinary and warm, though the pale blue sky overhead carries clouds that do not match the sky you left behind outside the door. And the noise is different. The street-roar does not hammer directly into these folded interiors; it enters muffled, slowed, and deepened through the thick tiered galleries of pale shelf-wood, ringing through the wide square like the long, steady hum of a struck bowl.

Here, away from the tearing updrafts and the scouring chaos of the avenues, they do not need to cling flush to the walls.

The tiered galleries are crowded with them. Hundreds upon hundreds of tall, shoulder-wide, faceted figures stand free along the balustrades and across the pale shelf-wood floors, unfolded to their full geometric stature, angled inward toward the sunlit square. In the clear, candid afternoon light, their translucent amber-and-chalk bodies glow softly like stained parchment or smoked mica. Not a limb twitches; not a chest rises or falls with breath. Yet inside every single frame, visible through the sunlit resin walls, the suspended chalk-pollen is alive in slow, vast, synchronized motion—nested lattices and shifting rings passing from figure to figure down the entire length of the gallery like a wind moving across tall grass.

They have no eyes. They will not turn to stare when you step out onto the fibrous cobbles of the courtyard.

What they register instead is the sudden, inexplicable arrival of a soft, wet, sound-swallowing anomaly in the middle of their tuned square.

You do not ring. Your footsteps fall on the root-pavement in heavy, blunt, arrhythmic thuds. Your pulse is muffled inside meat; your breathing is a slow, damp, strangely tiny tide moving in and out of a single warm hollow. Where the clear, bowl-like resonance of the courtyard strikes you, it simply dies, soaked up by cloth and skin.

Slowly, silently, along the nearest tier of the pale shelf-wood gallery, dozens of tall, faceted forms tilt their angled chests by a few degrees, turning their taut amber planes toward you like listening dishes. Inside their translucent chambers, the serene, shared geometry wavers. The suspended dust blurs, scatters, and begins frantically trying to map the contours of the dull, wet silence standing in the sunlight below them.

If you stay still, holding your breath as long as you can, they will remain open—leaning faintly in their crisp, dry curiosity, adjusting their facets to trace the strange acoustic shadow you cast across their square.

If you speak—if you clear your throat, or call out a single word into the bright, warm air—the sloppy, wet percussion of a human voice hits the gallery like a thrown stone.

None of them answers. Instead, all along three tiers of shelf-wood, hundreds of pleated amber planes snap flat simultaneously with a sharp, dry crack like a whole street of wooden shutters slamming shut at once. Their translucent glow vanishes into dull, opaque bark-brown as they flatten flush against the gallery walls, indistinguishable from the wood behind them. And beneath your feet, and along the pale balustrades where they stood a second before, the suddenly untuned cambium groans, shivers, and begins extruding jagged, defensive ridges of raw splinters and thorns.

Step back through the vine-framed doorway into the narrow, roaring lane outside. The ordinary sky is plain and warm overhead; the avenue looks empty again, nothing breathing, nothing striding across the pollen-dusted cobbles. Yet now you know what the dry *tick* under the eaves is when a squall passes, why the veins on the corner bark-slab almost spell a word before splitting wild, and why the hollow pillars hum with the cadence of a crowded market. The valley is not building a metropolis for nobody. It is packed to the cornices with quiet, unbreathing, tuned citizens—holding their crisp, fragile geometry together inside the noise, one folded afternoon at a time.
