If you stand near one of the plaza rims long enough, waiting while the deflected wind roars overhead like a dry cataract, one of the tenders will eventually walk past you to adjust a tuning-vane. Up close, the first surprise is how little weight they seem to have—not fragile, exactly, but hollow and taut, the way a well-seasoned cello has weight without heaviness.

Watch the hand that grips the wooden crank. It is not wet flesh. Four long, narrow fingers emerge from a cuff of felted pith-cloth, sheathed in pale, matte integument with the fine-grained texture of old parchment or sun-bleached cedar. Where knuckles should swell, there are dark, faceted nodules of glassy cartilage that articulate with a faint, clean *click*. There are no fingernails; instead, each fingertip tapers into a stiff, split quill of conductive keratin that ticks softly against the weathered grain of the crank, reading the vibration of the arcade before the wrist even turns.

They belong to the plateau as thoroughly as the drum-backs and the quill-reeds do. There is no moisture in them—no blood, no saliva, no soft wet organs tucked inside a warm sack. Beneath the heavy layers of static-shedding bark-cloth, their bodies are tall, slender lattices of laminated silica-bone and braided carbon-fiber muscle that contract on crisp pulses of electrostatic charge. Their chests are narrow and deeply fluted, shaped less like mammalian ribcages than like long, gracefully ribbed seed-pods. Inside, there are no lungs meant to burn oxygen from thin air; instead, dense mineral-capacitor organs store the day’s voltage beside a central, hollow resonance-chamber that occupies nearly half the torso.

When the tender finishes tuning the vane—the whistling pitch of the nearby arcade drops a fraction of a tone, smoothing out a harsh beat-frequency that had been rattling the stoneware lamps—they pause, turn their hooded head slightly toward you, and push back the heavy felted cowl so they can hear the district cleanly.

Their face is pale, angular, and stillness itself. The skull is elongated, sculpted like a wind-worn woodwind finial, smooth along the cheekbones and finely ridged along the jaw. Two long, deeply recessed eyes sit beneath sharp brows—not wet spheres, but matte-amber optical plates veined with hair-thin filaments of pale copper. They never blink. Every minute or so, a dry, papery rim-lid sweeps horizontally across each lens with a tiny, crisp *shick*, brushing away settled chalk-spores. Those eyes do not merely gather the dim afternoon light of the exhausted sun; they read the faint violet haze of building static, the shimmering optical shear of the overhead wind-shields, and the taut, invisible fold-lines running down a transit ribbon.

They do not smile, because their jaw-plates are rigid ceramic hinged cleanly along the sides of the face. Instead, if a neighbor crosses the terrace to meet them, they incline their narrow head, draw a small sip of thin air through the vertical vents along their collarbones, and speak.

In a world that will not stop roaring, a wet throat would be useless. These people do not shout over the plateau; they *chord* through it. From a narrow reed-aperture beneath the chin, two or three clear, dry woodwind notes emerge at once—crystalline, piercing, pitched deliberately into the narrow acoustic lanes between the forty-hertz thrum of the hovering vacuum-disc and the high scream of the wind overhead. To an unaccustomed ear, a casual conversation on an open plaza sounds like two musicians quietly testing bone-flutes in the middle of a gale.

Yet only half of what they say travels through the air. Watch their wrists. As the two neighbors draw close, they extend their right forearms and touch the backs of their conductive cuffs together. A tiny, pale-blue spark snaps between them—painless, tidy, scarcely louder than a snapping seed—equalizing their electrostatic potential. In that brief bridge of current passes everything the voice leaves out: warmth, wryness, fatigue, affection, the dry equivalent of a sigh. It is considered deeply uncouth to carry a heavy, ragged charge into close company, which is why every robe they wear is weighted along the hem with small dangling beads of raw graphite. As they walk across the smooth cambium plazas, the beads drag lightly over the living floor, trailing silent, invisible threads of excess voltage safely back into the roots.

Because of this, they are also never truly deaf to the ground. Their boots have soles of thin, perforated horn-bark; many indoors go bare-footed altogether, pressing tough, mineral-calloused pads straight to the floor. Through their heels, up through their hollow shins and resonant ribs, they hear the whole valley as a single continuous sentence. A citizen standing on a quiet terrace can tell, without looking, whether a freight sled is passing two kilometers away, whether a swarm of cambium-borers is chewing through a spent seam beneath the south arcade, or whether the vacuum-disc in the plaza basin has drifted a thousandth of a cycle flat.

What do people do in a city whose engines have run without failure for longer than continents endure?

They do not build much anymore. There is nowhere left to expand to, and no hurry to become anything else. They are neither conquerors nor strivers; they are *keepers of pitch*. Impossible physics, left to itself across millions of dry seasons, does not explode or crumble—it *drifts*. A wind-lens bends a degree too steep and begins scouring the bark off a neighboring terrace; a transit pylon settles half a millimeter as the root-felt beneath it shifts, and commuters stepping off the ribbon find their shadows arriving half a second late; a spire’s folded interior develops a subtle acoustic dead-spot that makes the residents feel strangely dull and heavy-jointed.

So the city’s days are spent in small, unhurried acts of tuning.

Along the sunlit margins of the wide plains walk the Ribbon-Keepers, taller and broader-shouldered in their wind-stiffened mantles, carrying long staves of braided silica-wood tipped with forked ceramic prongs. Every hundred meters they stop at a weathered boundary pylon, press the fork to its crown, and listen through the shaft to the dry metallic sing of the folded distance. Now and then they tap a wooden shim tighter into the pylon’s base, or scrape away the conductive lichen-crust that creeps up from the floor. If a herd of drum-backs wanders too close to an active transit strip, a Keeper does not drive them off with cruelty; they simply kneel, press the butt of their stave to the ground, and strike three sharp, resonant raps against the wood—a polite counterfeit of a lead bull’s warning drum. The great six-limbed grazers rumble back in annoyed percussion and lumber slowly away into the quill-reeds.

Out further on the open flats, far from the plazas’ sheltering shields, move the Borer-Listeners. You might mistake one at first for a fallen log or a huddled outcrop of weathered pith, until you see the long, grounded cloak staked flat against the floor to keep the gale from lifting it. They lie with their narrow chests pressed directly to the pale bark, arms spread wide, eyes closed against the stinging dust. Beneath them, gravel-loud swarms of ceramic cambium-borers race through the shallow vascular layers. The plateau needs those tunnels to stay supple, yet left unguided, a hungry swarm will chew straight into a buried civic conduit or undermine a terrace foundation. When a Listener feels a swarm veering too close to a district’s edge, they drive a ring of tapered cedar pegs into the living crust and draw a rosined sinew-bow calmly across each one. A sharp, sour vibration drops downward into the wood—nothing harmful, just deeply unappetizing to the blind tunnelers—and the frantic rattling beneath the floor bends smoothly away toward older, spent xylem.

And further still, along the windswept outer rims where the plateau meets the dim, bruised indigo of the high sky, roam the few who prefer the open roar to the quiet spires. Plain-Walkers, the district-folk call them. They wear articulated mantles shingled with pale ceramic plates shed by old borers, wired together with fine copper-veined fiber so that if a crack-stalker mistakes them for prey and unleashes its blinding horizontal arc of lightning, the lethal bolt skitters harmlessly across their shoulders and grounds out through their spiked heels in a shower of blue sparks and the smell of scorched cloves. They follow the drum-back herds to gather molted dorsal parchment; they climb the worn western ridges to harvest the intact, translucent wings of dead glass-kites, which are still prized above any manufactured material for the delicate acoustic louvers of the spires. When a Plain-Walker comes into a town plaza to trade, everyone knows them immediately—their cloaks smell fiercely of ozone and open dust, and their chorded voices are loud, clipped, and a little rough around the edges from years of singing against an unobstructed gale.

Follow one of the district-folk back home, however, and you discover the true counterweight to all this roaring immensity.

The doorway at the base of a fluted spire is narrow, barely wider than a draped shoulder, set deep within an alcove where the wind cannot tear at it. Set into the right-hand jamb is a smooth vertical bar of copper-veined heartwood, worn to a dull, warm gloss by generations of passing hands. Every entrant pauses a heartbeat to lay their ungloved palm flat against it. A faint, dry crackle runs down the bar; the sharp static of the open plaza drains cleanly away.

Then you step inside—and the world lets go of your ears.

The shift is startling. From the outside, the spire looks scarcely five meters across; inside, the folded space opens calmly into a broad, low-vaulted hall easily forty paces wide, terraced into gentle galleries and sunlit alcoves that face nowhere at all. Thick carpets of deadened, uncompressed root-felt cover the floors and climb halfway up the curved walls, swallowing echoes whole. After the relentless, chest-rattling roar of the plateau, the stillness inside feels vast, velvet-dry, almost sacred.

Here, the inhabitants unfasten their heavy bark-cloth shrouds and hang them along branching racks near the threshold, where the stiffened fabric ticks softly for several minutes as it relaxes. Stripped of their outer layers, they look astonishingly graceful—slender, long-limbed, articulated like delicate wooden automata crafted by a master who loved reeds and grasshoppers. Beneath their cloaks they wear close-fitting shifts of dry-spun, silvery pith-thread, leaving the fluted contours of their ribs and the subtle, pale-copper traceries running along their collarbones visible in the muted amber light.

Indoors, their manners change completely. The piercing outdoor flute-chords vanish; using them inside a folded hall would feel like banging a pot in a library. Instead, they converse in extraordinarily soft, rapid dry-reed whispers, rustling consonant-clicks, and long stretches of silent touch. Around the low, grown-wood tables of a communal hall, you will often see four or five citizens sitting together without making a sound at all—their bare forearms resting flat on the smooth tabletop, their fingertips lightly touching the grain. Subtle, complex ripples of vibration travel through their wrists into the wood and up through their companions’ elbows: gossip, poetry, philosophical argument, dry humor, all braided quietly through the table like music played strictly for the bones.

There is no kitchen here, no steam, no kettle, no wet broth. Sustenance is crisp, dry, and strangely luminous. In the center of the table sits a shallow stoneware bowl filled with pale mineral-pith, toasted spore-dust, and brittle, translucent wafers cut from compressed resin-cells. A citizen lifts a wafer between two quill-tipped fingers, slips it between their rigid jaw-plates, and snaps it cleanly in half with a sharp *crick*. Ridged ceramic grinders reduce it instantly to an electrifying, aromatic powder; dry, brush-like receptors along the throat draw the liberated ions straight into the vascular filaments. The finest vintages—resin cured for a century in dark vaults beneath a forty-hertz vacuum-disc, or spore-crust scraped from fresh borer-seams—carry no sweetness or salt, only a startling clarity of charge, a faint ghost of dry cedar-smoke, and a crisp, sparkling wakefulness that tingles all the way down the ribs.

It is in these hushed folded halls that you come to understand their art. When your entire world is an endless symphony played at storm volume, you do not build concert halls to make louder music. You build sound-deadened rooms to worship tiny noises.

In the quiet hours before the Charge-Tide begins to climb, neighbors gather in the sunken listening-rooms of the spires. There are no orchestras. A single master artisan sits on a mat of thick root-felt, holding a shallow cedar bowl and a small leather pouch of sorted silica grit. For twenty minutes, in a hush so complete you can hear the faint electrical tick of your neighbor’s joints, the performer tilts the bowl by fractions of a degree, letting individual grains roll, pause, collide, and settle—an entire, microscopic storm of delicate clicks and whispers that never rises above the sound of dust falling on paper. Another artisan may bring out a *pocket-fold*: a palm-sized box of polished graphite-bark whose hinged lid opens onto a miniature, impossibly spacious interior—a silent labyrinth of pale crystal-moss, dry amber corridors, and suspended wood-chimes you can lean close to and peer into, or reach an entire arm inside to feel a tiny, captive breeze that exists nowhere else in the universe.

In the deepest, warmest folded rooms of every spire—the ones shielded most carefully from thermal creep and stray static—lie the Loom-Nurseries.

Dry-chemistry beings are not born in blood or wet labor. When two citizens—or three, or an entire quiet household of neighbors who have harmonized well for a century—wish to bring a new voice into the district, they do not conceive in a moment; they *compose*. Each parent yields thin, translucent shavings of living pith from beneath their inner ribs, along with tiny, charged glass-seeds harvested from their neural lattices. In the amber gloom of the nursery, a Loom-Tender braids these strands around a delicate trellis of living silica-wood, binding them in soft swaddling of ion-rich resin-cloth and hanging the pale, papery chrysalis from a braided ceiling-root fed by a gentle, stepped-down trickle of the district’s forty-hertz hum.

There it hangs for years, swaying almost imperceptibly, ticking softly as it drinks the slow pulse and knits itself a mind. Gradually the bundle lengthens, develops dark glassy joint-nodules, hollows out a narrow flute of ribs. When it is ready, the dry swaddling splits along a seam with a crisp tearing sound, and a young one unfolds itself into the waiting arms of its household.

They are startlingly delicate things, the young. Their outer sheath is pale, flexible, and faintly translucent, glowing softly when a spark passes through them; their ribs have not yet hardened into true woodwind resonance, so when they are startled or delighted they emit thin, breathy squeaks like green quill-reeds bending in a draft. For their first dozen years, they never step outside onto the open plateau. Their dermal insulation is far too thin—the dry gale would strip their charge in minutes, and a stray static arc would shatter their soft joints. Instead, they grow up inside the sprawling, impossible interiors of the spires, chasing one another along staircases that loop back onto their own ceilings, hiding behind folded corners that are larger than the rooms containing them, learning to tune their voices and hold a steady charge while the wind screams unheard beyond the wood.

A citizen’s first step out onto the open plaza—bundled in their first weighted bark-cloth cloak, holding a parent’s conductive wrist as the roar of the sky hits their chest-chamber for the first time—is the great threshold of their life. For days afterward, they walk around grinning silently in static, tapping their heels on the cambium floor just to feel how big the world actually is.

Because there is no wet rot in them, their lives stretch out with the patient slowness of old hardwood. Centuries pass. A young commuter becomes a seasoned Vane-Tender, a Plain-Walker, a builder of pocket-folds, a quiet historian of district pitch. As the millennia-old seasons turn—measured only by the slow drift of the pale stars and the gradual wearing of the western ridges—their bodies subtly change. Mineral salts accumulate along their laminated bones; their parchment skin darkens to rich, weathered walnut and graphite-bark; their joints lose the supple snap required for striding across the folding transit ribbons without tripping over the shimmer.

Their voices, too, deepen. The crisp, bright woodwind chords of youth settle into rich, warm contrabass notes that rumble gently through the floorboards.

They do not fear this stiffening, because in this city, growing too heavy to walk is not an ending. It is simply a change of instrument.

If you descend the spiral ramps beneath a district’s circular basin—down beneath the hovering vacuum-disc, where the cold forty-hertz bass vibrates so strongly it turns the dust into hovering geometric rings—you will find the quietest, oldest inhabitants of the plateau.

The district-folk call them the Seated.

Along the curved, dim galleries carved directly into the deep living cambium sit scores of ancient citizens, motionless in the amber dark. They have long since put aside their cloaks and their grounding-beads. They sit cross-legged or tall-backed on the raw, springy wood, their slender hands resting palms-down on their knees, their narrow heads tilted slightly upward as if listening to something very far away. Look down at their ankles, their shins, the base of their spines. Fine, silver-grey vascular threads have crept calmly up out of the floor and knitted seamlessly into their dry bark. They are rooted.

Their matte-amber eyes have filmed over into smooth, opaque mineral glass; they no longer see the dim light of the sun or the faces of those who come down to visit them. Instead, wired straight into the deep superconducting lattice of the valley, they perceive the entire plateau at once. A Seated elder feels the subtle thermal groan of the buried granite kilometers below; they feel the bright, sudden *crack* every time a lone traveler folds across a transit ribbon ten districts away; they feel the rhythmic drumming of herds on the outer plains, the racing clatter of borer-swarms, the faint aerodynamic strain on every invisible wind-lens holding back the sky.

They are the living governors who keep the ancient miracles polite. No computer runs the vacuum-discs or balances the folded geometry of the spires; machines left alone for millions of years forget how to bend without breaking. When a transit ribbon begins to drift out of alignment, or a spire’s interior starts to sag a fraction of an inch toward ordinary three-dimensional space, a Seated elder does not flip a switch. They simply adjust the tension inside their own hollow, cedar-dark ribs, shifting their internal capacitance by a hair’s breadth, and send a quiet, corrective chord straight down through their rooted legs into the civic grid.

Younger citizens come down to the deep galleries often, carrying no gifts—the Seated eat only the slow voltage rising from the floor—and sit quietly beside an ancestor or an old friend. They lay a bare palm against the elder’s cool, dry shoulder. No words pass through the air. Instead, a slow, vast wash of resonant memory rises through the contact: the pitch of a windstorm from three thousand years ago, the exact acoustic recipe for calming a nervous nursery, a dry joke remembered from when the western rim stood ten meters higher, and an immense, steady calm that settles straight into the visitor’s bones.

And when, after ages, even a Seated elder dries past the point of holding a distinct self—when their mineral capacitors can no longer keep a separate boundary between their own charge and the plateau’s great hum—there is no burial and no grief of rot. Their individual mind simply loosens, spreading outward along the vascular veins like fine chalk-dust settling into grain. Their body stays where it is. Slowly, layer by quiet layer, fresh pale cambium grows calmly up over their folded knees, across their narrow ribs, sealing their hollow chest-chamber into the living wall of the vault.

Every neighborhood in the valley has a slightly different voice when the high wind blows across its fluted spires, and every local can recognize their home district in the dark by pitch alone. It is not merely the shape of the towers. It is the hollow bones of everyone who ever lived there, woven into the soundboard, still gently coloring the chord.

If you want to see them most fully as themselves, stay until the small sun sinks toward the worn western rim and the Charge-Tide begins to rise.

You see them hurry off the open transit ribbons then, cloaks whipping sharply in the sharpening chill, grounding-beads spitting tiny blue ticks across the plazas as the dry dust-spores lift off the plain in ghostly, hovering sheets. Along the terraces, Vane-Tenders crank the heavy wooden louvers shut over the amber alcoves. Plain-Walkers duck beneath the woven-root eaves, unbuckling their ceramic-scaled mantles with crisp clicks of bone and wire. Inside the spires, the wide, folded halls grow dim and extraordinarily taut. The air smells sharply of cloves, dry cedar, and clean ozone. Across every citizen’s fluted ribs, the fine silica bristles lift slightly; an electric wakefulness tightens in their joints, clearing away the dull static fatigue of the afternoon.

Then the Discharge Hour breaks across the valley.

Outside, the clear dark-indigo sky splits with roaring, percussive blue fire. Lightning leaps from spire to spire and hammers into the waiting seams of the plain, shaking the entire continent of wood until the folded halls ring like struck bells.

Inside, no one cowers.

Running straight down the center of every great communal hall stands a thick, braided column of dark heartwood, banded in dull copper-veined bark and pierced by vertical slits of thick amber glass. It is the spire’s main grounding-spine, drawing the roaring torrent of blue fire safely from the wind-fluted pinnacle all the way down into the deep, thirsty roots. As the first jagged bolts strike the roof overhead, the central column flares with a fierce, muffled cyan-and-amber glow, humming with a deep, thrilling baritone that shakes the dust from the ceilings.

Around it, the citizens gather.

They come out of their quiet alcoves and nurseries and workshops—young ones with their translucent hands raised eagerly toward the humming pillar, feeling the fierce inductive field tickle their soft bark; weary Ribbon-Keepers leaning their slender shoulders against the warm wood; couples standing rib-to-rib with their wrists linked in continuous, shimmering threads of shared spark. Down in the deep basins beneath the plazas, the Seated elders lift their smooth, blind faces into the roaring current as the valley drinks the sky.

Nobody speaks. Instead, as the dry thunder rolls back and forth between the ancient mountain rims, the people inside the spires open their collarbone vents, tune their hollow chests, and sing back into the column. Hundreds of dry, crystalline woodwind chords rise and lock together—high, piping treble from the young, clear midrange harmonies from the plain-walkers and artisans, deep contrabass swells rising straight up through the floorboards from the rooted elders below. They do not try to drown out the storm. They weave themselves into it, letting the wild, ragged voltage of the Discharge Hour flow calmly through their tuned bodies, filling their mineral organs to a crisp, humming fullness, straightening out every discordant kink left by the day.

For an hour, the city is neither people nor architecture nor landscape. It is one vast, dry, luminous instrument playing itself beneath the pale stars.

Gradually, the roaring arcs outside thin to scattered blue ticks, then fade into the settling silver haze of exhaled spores. Inside the spires, the glowing column dims back to warm amber, then quiet wood. The singing softens, unravels into small, satisfied clicks and affectionate wrist-sparks, and settles back into hushed domesticity.

Up on the high terrace, a wooden louver cranks open with a dry groan. A slender figure in layered bark-cloth steps out into the crisp, starlit dark, fresh and brilliantly charged, resting one quill-tipped hand lightly against the plaza rim to check the pitch of the night wind. Out on the open plain, a distant drum-back bull booms once across the gloom, answered softly by its herd. The commuter turns toward the silver-grey transit ribbon stretching across the valley, steps calmly onto the smooth living bark, and with a clean, resounding *crack*, folds away into the dimness toward the next singing cluster of towers.
