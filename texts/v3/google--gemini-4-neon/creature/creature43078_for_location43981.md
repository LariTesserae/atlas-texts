You do not see them on your first circuit of the berm, nor on your second. Even when you wade down into the warm, ankle-deep amber water and cross the open flats in broad daylight, you can pass within twenty paces of one and take it for an eroded mooring-bollard, a slump of wet moss clinging to an anchor-plinth, or a fluted shadow tucked beneath the overhang of a newly risen gallery.

Wait for the hourly chord.

When that deep, woody note swells up through the floor from all four quarters at once—when the standing curtains of water lock solid as blown glass, the localized rain halts mid-fall, and even the castanet-clicking runners go still around their foaming puddles—do not watch the buildings. Watch the spaces beside the columns, and the pillowy domes of the petrified cambium plinths.

During those ten held seconds, things detach themselves from the architecture.

Three small, narrow-shouldered figures straighten up along the eastern arcade where nothing seemed to be standing a moment before. Two more rise from a crouch on the central plinth, shaking warm droplets from their long forearms. Across the entire silent saucer, perhaps thirty or forty of them come abruptly into focus, made legible only because the city has finally stopped moving behind them.

And then you hear them.

For fifty-nine minutes of every hour, the basin belongs to hydraulic thumps, ripping cellulose, shrill waders, and hissing vents—a racket too dense and broad for ordinary voices. So during the ten-second hush, while the whole wetland holds its breath, they speak. Low, resonant, humming calls travel cleanly across the dead-flat mirror of water—not songs, though they have the warm timbre of wooden flutes blown softly, but brisk, compressed exchanges tossed from north to south, east to west. A name announced near the rim. A brief warning about a choked drainage vein under the western lawn. A single rising two-note question from a figure perched on a half-weathered balustrade, answered immediately from two hundred paces away by three voices in quiet unison.

On the ninth second, every voice cuts off together. A heartbeat later, a gutter coughs loudly near the northern berm, the water walls crash and tremble back to life, a flock of bladder-winged waders bursts upward against the clouds—and the figures fold back into their slanted, unobtrusive crouch, vanishing once more into the bright, noisy clutter of the basin.

Once your eye has learned their silhouette, though, you cannot unsee them. They are everywhere, working the city’s restlessness the way coastal scavengers work a tide.

Up close, they are slight—rarely taller than a grown human’s chest even when fully unbent, though they spend very little time standing straight. Their limbs are long and spare, their ribcages narrow and unusually supple, built for slipping through fluted clearances and curling into the tight dry alcoves beneath shifting cantilevers. Their matte, water-shedding skin is the exact steeped-tea olive and bruised umber of the living floor, paling to wet ivory along the inner wrists, throat, and the remarkably broad, four-toed soles of their feet. Those soles are ribbed and half-translucent, tough enough to grip algae-slick cellulose at a run yet sensitive as fingertips; standing barefoot in the warm shallows, they can feel the slow hydraulic swell of a column-bud preparing to break the moss thirty paces off.

They have no hair. Instead, a sparse crest of pale, stiff, reed-like filaments runs from the crown of the skull down the length of the spine. Watch those filaments closely and you can read the basin’s weather two seconds before it happens: they lift and fan gently outward when humid air is about to condense into a sudden patch of localized rain, and they flatten tight against the skin an instant before a twelve-foot wall of water loses its containment and drops with a thunderous *whump*.

Their heads taper slightly backward, smooth as old cedar worn by river currents, with wide mouths lined in flat, pale crushing plates rather than sharp teeth. What gives them away immediately as creatures native to this bent little world are their eyes: large, wide-set, pale amber beneath protruding translucent brow-ridges that shed overhead drips like tiny eaves. Each iris holds a faint secondary ring around its outer edge, catching polarization and subtle refractive breaks. When one of them approaches a newly extruded archway, it does not test the threshold with a stick; it simply cocks its head left and right until that pale outer ring catches the clean seam stitched through the afternoon daylight, measuring at a glance where the exit stair-head will unfold forty paces away.

Outside the ten-second hush, they rarely use their voices at all. Up close, they communicate in a crisp, dry patter of palatal clicks, wet palms slapped softly against the water-film, and brief whistles pitched into the thin acoustic gap between the sub-floor rumble and the waders’ cries. Across the wide mirror-flats, they rely on their long, narrow arms—throwing slow, unmistakable geometric holds against the bright sky. Two crews standing on opposite sides of the saucer can conduct an entire negotiation without making a sound, their forearms angling like weathered semaphore blades above the shallows.

They are not the city’s citizens. Citizens expect walls to stay put, streets to lead where they led yesterday, and roofs to belong to them. These dwellers have no such illusions. They are closer to squatters, counterweights, and patient burglars living inside a vast, half-erased piece of civic bookkeeping.

Because they have worked out the basin’s secret—or enough of it to survive.

Left alone, a freshly risen pavilion holds its form for perhaps ten or fifteen minutes before the buried xylem-grid decides it is idle and unspools it back into warm frothing slurry. But the dwellers have spent generations learning what the ancient foundations still recognize as *use*. They do not build houses—there is no stone here, no dirt, no dry timber, and nothing laid on the vascular lawn stays undisturbed for long. Instead, they *petition* the floor, and when it answers, they try to hold the answer open.

Watch a crew of six decide they want a dry roof for the afternoon—perhaps to cure a batch of tools, tend an injured foot, or sleep out of the sun. First, they gather on an open stretch of mossy lawn where the sunken calloused xylem-brick shows faintly beneath the amber water. Carrying smooth, heavy staves of cured cellulose, they walk a slow, intersecting circuit in the shallows, tapping the floor-skin in an uneven, practiced cadence that mimics the dense footfall of long-vanished crowds. Within a minute, the surface water begins to shiver in crisp concentric rings. The crew steps neatly back—never far, knowing the exact perimeter of the coming plinth—as the vascular mat groans, snaps, and extrudes a vaulted, already-weathered canopy thirty feet into the bright air.

The moment its drainage gutters slit open and begin to pour, the real choreography begins.

One of the youngest in the crew becomes the pendulum. Without pausing, she trots through the canopy’s southern archway, vanishes cleanly across the folded seam, and pops out forty paces west from a low stair-head that did not exist a second before; she turns immediately, splashes back across the open flat, and steps through the arch again, repeating the loop every two minutes so the spatial link stays registered as active traffic. A second dweller climbs effortlessly up the fluted ribs—his ankles rotating wide to grip the damp beveled ledges—and works along the eaves with a blade of fractured runner-shell, clearing stray clumps of velvet algae so the warm gutter-runoff never falters or backs up. Inside the shade of the gallery, two others quietly shift three melon-sized spheres of compressed, glossy dark cellulose from step to step, imitating the slow weight of occupied benches.

Through sheer, delicate fakery, a skilled crew can convince a pavilion to stay standing for two or three hours—sometimes even through the cool of the night, trading shifts so the pendulum never stops walking and the gutters never choke.

Yet it is always a fragile bargain. Sooner or later, a runner slips into a drainpipe, or the pendulum loses rhythm chasing a stray lung-eel, or—most commonly of all—another crew two hundred paces away across the saucer prompts a massive sunken amphitheater or tall pier of their own, tipping the basin’s strict conservation of civic volume. There is a sudden hydraulic sigh overhead. The columns soften like boiled reeds. Within twenty seconds, the crew is scrambling out through drooping arches with their bundles held high above their heads as their carefully tended roof melts into warm frothing slurry around their shins, leaving them ankle-deep in bright sunlight while porcelain-shelled runners come racing across the water to devour the foam of their bedroom.

Because of this, relations between the basin’s four or five loose work-crews are a blend of brisk cooperation and quiet, exasperated rivalry. A tall pier raised in the northwest forces a sunken amphitheater to open in the southeast within thirty seconds—which temporarily drains the surrounding flats, stranding plump runners and exposing sweet, tender root-shoots along the depression’s rim. Often you will see a crew in the northwest pause before prompting a pier, raising their long forearms in crisp signals across the bright water so a friendly crew in the southeast can sprint into position with wide tendon-nets ready to catch the sudden harvest. Other times, a selfish prompt ruins a neighbor’s carefully held drying-gallery, provoking an indignant flurry of sharp hand-signs across the sunlit shallows—and, at the next hourly chord, a brief, sternly hummed reprimand that everybody in the saucer hears.

With no stone, no metal, no clay, and no fire anywhere inside the warm, saturated rim, their entire craft is built out of water, pressure, and the basin’s bent local physics.

They make their hardest tools out of dissolving buildings. Whenever an old arcade collapses into its ring of warm fibrous slurry, dwellers wade in alongside the clicking runners to scoop the densest, cleanest pulp into porous bags stitched from scavenged wader wing-membranes. These wet pouches they wedge tightly into the hissing pinhole stomata of a rising pier, or lay directly in the path of a twelve-foot standing curtain of water just before its unseen switch trips. Under the sudden violent hammer of collapsing water or pressurized sap-vents, the slurry compacts into glossy, dark-umber blocks as heavy and hard as bog-oak. From this cured slurry-wood they whittle staves, counterweights, and curved gutter-scrapers, sharpening the edges with thin, chalk-white shards of porcelain runner-shell bound tightly in braided lung-eel sinew.

Even stranger is how they use the anomalies that an outsider treats as mere spectacle.

When a wafer-thin gallery cantilevers twenty yards over empty air and creates a pocket of flattened gravity beneath its eaves, the dwellers climb up into the weightless hush to do their finest weaving. Threads teased from wet vascular ribbons normally clump and drag in damp air; under the cantilever, they float cleanly out in straight, docile lines, letting nimble spatulate fingers braid intricate nets and carrying-harnesses while speckled wader-eggs drift gently beside their shoulders like slow bubbles. It is also where they keep their youngest infants while working—tethered on soft vascular leads inside small woven slings that bob six inches beneath the damp ribs, safe from the shifting floor below, wide amber eyes tracking the bright clouds and the darting silhouettes of waders.

In the dry alcoves where rain pauses at eye level for three or four heartbeats before snapping downward in a sharp crack, you will sometimes find a lone craftsman hunched in intense concentration. They hold fine sinew knots or tiny shell-splinters directly behind a motionless hanging raindrop, using the suspended bead of clear amber water as a magnifying lens for three seconds at a stretch—hands freezing the instant momentum returns and the bead explodes against the moss, then resuming seamlessly as the next sheet of droplets hangs still in the warm air.

Their kitchen, too, runs on the city’s plumbing. They do not chase the hand-sized porcelain runners across the open flats; they simply wait by a dissolving pier with submerged nets, letting the clicking swarms race into the foam. Skewered on pale cellulose splinters, the sweet, pale runner-meat is tucked directly into the hot, cedar-scented steam venting from pressurized stomata along a newly risen column, emerging tender and fragrant in three minutes. They tap pale sap from the springy floor-mat into sealed bladders of oiled lung-eel skin and tie them along the underside of newly grown vine-bridges, where thick ribbons of water race steadily uphill; tumbled and shaken endlessly against gravity by the ascending current, the sap ferments overnight into a bright, tart, faintly sparkling drink that tastes of steeped tea and struck wet flint.

Among the basin’s other animals, they move with unsentimental intimacy. They quarrel constantly with the stilt-legged waders over prime cantilevered real estate, ducking the furious snapping of chalk-white bills while politely leaving the floating eggs untouched—waiting instead to collect the oiled-paper wing-membranes whenever an old bird drops into the shallows. They watch the ribbon-bodied lung-eels closely whenever a collapsed water-curtain sends them undulating across fifty paces of moss, noting which sunken transit-channel the eels choose to dive into, since eels always follow the strongest rising pulse in the sub-floor xylem veins.

Only toward the broad-backed, calf-sized mossy grazers do they show something close to tenderness. They never hunt them, and they always leave half the nutty spore-buds along the anchor-plinths for the beasts to crop. In return, the unhurried grazers serve as living gauges of the floor’s intentions. Whenever a grazer stops chewing and ambles ten paces to the left, nearby dwellers shift with it without even looking down, letting a ribbed stair-tower burst safely upward from the empty patch of algae behind them. On hot, quiet afternoons, you will sometimes see two dwellers kneeling beside a resting grazer on an anchor-plinth, gently prying encrusted algae from between its overlapping damp scales with runner-shell blades while a pair of small olive-skinned children nap dryly against its mossy flank.

Children here learn the basin’s crooked geometry before they ever learn to speak in the ten-second hush. Their favorite game is a breathless, silent version of tag played across linked archways—darting through a southern threshold to tumble out forty paces west from a temporary stair-head, laughing in sharp clicking bursts as the steps fold back into moss beneath their heels. To a child raised inside the berm, walking in a straight line across an open flat looks oddly clumsy, like something only a heavy beast would bother to do; the natural way to cross four hundred paces is to borrow three folds from the city and arrive before the water stops rippling where you left.

At the other end of life, the oldest dwellers give up the stitched seams altogether. As decades of crouching in warm amber water stiffen their ankles and thicken the ribbed soles of their feet into horn-pale pads, they retire to the few permanent anchor-plinths—the pillowy, petrified cambium domes that never extrude and never collapse. Draped in thick, living shoulder-capes of velvet bryophyte peeled from old cornices and kept alive by the warm mist, the elders sit cross-legged in the open sun like weathered civic statuary, long palms pressed flat against the slick cambium.

They are the basin’s memory and its weather-glass. Because their hardened soles and palms read the deepest sub-floor veins better than any young walker’s, a quiet elder sitting on the central plinth can feel an approaching hydraulic imbalance a full half-minute before the surface water shivers. Without opening their wide amber eyes, an elder will simply lift two long fingers and slant their wrist toward the northeast—and across the bright, noisy saucer, three different work-crews adjust their footing, snatch up their curing-pouches, or pull their tethered infants clear of a roof about to give up its stiffness.

When one of the elders finally stops breathing—often, as the dwellers quietly note, during the ten-second hush, as though waiting for the silence to step cleanly out of the racket—there is no ground to dig and no flame to burn. The others unwind the elder’s braided tally-cords, lift off the living moss-cape so it can be laid back onto wet timber, and carry the slight, quiet body to the steps of a pavilion whose arches are already beginning to soften at the eaves. Nobody runs the pendulum through its doorway. Nobody clears its gutters. They stand back in a loose ring in the warm ankle-deep shallows, hands hanging open at their sides, gently waving away the first eager porcelain runners with clicking sticks as the fluted columns sigh, slump downward, and dissolve into a frothing white pancake that sinks smoothly back into the living plate of the floor.

Nothing marks the spot except a widening ripple across the amber water. Yet for weeks afterward, whenever a freshly extruded balustrade rises with an unfamiliar crook along its handrail, or a stair-head unfolds a low, rounded bench shaped neatly to the curve of a bent spine, the dwellers run their long spatulate fingers lightly over the damp, already-weathered cellulose. At the next hourly chord, somebody will hum a brief, quiet observation across the still flats—that the old blueprint is still taking notes, even if it no longer remembers what the city was for.

What they themselves are to this place, nobody—least of all themselves—can tell you. They carry no stories older than the oldest living elder’s tally-cords. Perhaps they are what remained of the original citizens after millennia of weathering down to fit a shrunken, ruined dish. Perhaps they wandered over the low lip in some forgotten dry era and stayed until the bent gravity, warm sap, and rhythmic floor remade their bodies to match. Or perhaps the living floor itself, endlessly trying to balance an equation whose variables vanished ages ago, grew them slowly out of old cellular memory—small, slightly lopsided, persistent approximations of the footsteps it still waits to feel.

You might wonder why they never simply leave. The perimeter berm is barely waist-high, soft as an old pond lip, and there is no wall above it.

Once every few years, a bold adolescent will wade out to the rim, hook long olive arms over the mossy ridge, and look across into the ordinary world beyond. They stare out at dry grass and stubborn dirt, at trees taking half a century to raise a single rigid bough, at heavy sky where rain only ever falls straight down without once pausing to shine at eye level. Watching them from behind, you can see the pale reed-like filaments along their spine flatten tight in quiet, bewildered vertigo. To feet born on a pulsing hydraulic plate, the ground beyond the berm feels horrifyingly deaf—unanswering, immovable, locked in a single endless weight.

After a few minutes, the young one lets go of the rim, drops lightly back into the warm amber shallows, and trots away toward a newly rising arcade—pausing only as a low, woody marimba chord begins to swell up through the floor from all four quarters, lifting their head alongside thirty other small, wet figures across the suddenly silent basin to speak before the gutters cough again.
