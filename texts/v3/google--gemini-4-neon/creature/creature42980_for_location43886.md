You do not see them at first because you are still looking for someone who stands.

In a room where nothing rises to the height of a human shoulder, your eyes slide over every curved hump and low dark alcove as though it were only another water-jar, another hunched mound of wet mortar, another sling of moss sagging under its own steam. You shift sideways to keep your ribs off a smoking brazier, brace your palm against a rounded, slip-slick shoulder of clay to steady yourself as the floor shudders over the void—and the clay draws a slow, heavy breath beneath your hand.

Not the groan of lashed timber. A tight, ribbed expansion, warm and dense, followed by the soft creak of green hemp taking strain. The rounded back shifts two inches sideways, and what you took for an unfired amphora tilts its blunt head over one collarbone to look at you.

Crouched there between the hearth-pots, knees drawn tight against its chest and heels hooked cleanly over a crossbeam, it is scarcely larger than a half-grown child—though nothing in its compact, coiled frame looks young or fragile. It has the posture of something that has never once stretched out flat, and would not know how if you set it down in an open field. Its whole body curls inward like a wet bud or a clenched fist, shoulders rounded to mirror the domed rafters, long forearms resting across its shins so its broad hands hang loose and ready just above the damp floor-tiles.

Its skin is the color of fresh river-clay darkened by embers—warm ochre, wet copper, flushed dull brick-red where the fire-pots glow beneath it—and it gleams with the same thin slick of slip and condensate that coats the walls. Look closely along its collarbones and the curve of its ribs and you can see faint, sharp parallel striations, like the drag-marks of a bone scraper pulled cleanly over leather-hard earth, and the crisp concentric whorls of thumbprints pressed into the hollows behind its shoulders. None of it has blurred with sweat or age. If a razor-edged strip of split bamboo opens a fine line across its knuckles while it works, the cut does not well with dark blood for more than a heartbeat; it beads a single clear drop of white sap, snaps its edges shut, and vanishes back into smooth, wet hide without scab or scar.

Because the thin air up here wants to pull everything apart, it keeps itself lashed together the same way it lashes the hut.

Tight braids of damp green hemp and narrow strips of sinew wrap its wrists, its ribcage, and its ankles, cinched down in neat, unhurried knots. The bindings bite deep enough that warm wet flesh swells slightly between the turns—until you look closer and lose the seam between craft and body. A hemp cord loops twice around its forearm and sinks smoothly under the skin like a tendon; a pale aerial root slips out from the crook of its knee, drips hot water onto the tiles, and coils back around its shin to brace the joint. Bright, freshly beaten copper pins—greasy with raw polish, untouched by green tarnish—stud the outer knobs of its elbows and the ridges of its hips, holding crooked bones together like mortised beams. Whenever it breathes, you hear the faint, springy creak of green wood flexing under rope.

Its breathing is not like yours. Where you gasp and wheeze against the starved whistling air, its laced chest works in a slow, deliberate double-pump: first pulling the scalding steam deep inside with a soft, hollow *whuff* like a leather bellows, then holding the breath trapped behind its cinched ribs for a long, pressurized beat—close enough that you can hear a faint internal simmer, like water hovering on the edge of a low-altitude boil—before venting a thin, hot hiss through wide nostrils. Its heart does not keep a steady pace; you can watch the pulse at its throat flutter in rapid bursts synced to the rhythmic pressure-knocks of the stratospheric wind, pause completely when a massive gust tilts the whole suspended nest sideways, and kick back into motion with a heavy, wet thump.

Its face is the strangest part—strangely earnest, intently focused, and somehow left slightly unfinished, as though whoever pinched it out of warm mud stopped the instant it had enough features to work by. No hair grows from its rounded, gourd-smooth skull; instead, along the nape of its neck and the ridge of its brow, tight spirals of pale green shoots and damp hot-house moss sprout straight from the pores, unfurling a fraction of an inch as you watch before softening and folding quietly back beneath the skin. Its mouth stays pressed in a quiet, absorbed line, often holding a wet reed, a copper pin, or a bone awl crosswise between its lips while its fingers are occupied. And its eyes—set wide apart beneath a broad, thumb-ridged brow—look like two wet river-pebbles pressed deep into soft clay: dark, glossy, without whites or discernible pupils, catching only the dull orange coal-glow from below. Whenever a heavy sheet of scalding water slaps down from the ceiling mats, a thick, milky inner lid flicks sideways across them without interrupting their gaze.

Its hands, by contrast, are finished to perfection. Long, strong, double-jointed fingers taper to blunt, supple tips, and the thumbs are remarkably wide and flat-padded, scored with sharp, high ridge-whorls—the exact thumbprints glistening fresh in every damp mortar seam around you. Its bare feet are nearly as nimble, soles deeply grooved, toes curling effortlessly around lashed poles or the sharp rims of clay troughs so it can perch sideways on a vertical wall or hang belly-up beneath the ceiling mats while both hands work free.

For a few feverish minutes, crouched in the smoke, you think there is only the one.

Then you crane your neck past a curtain of glossy taro-fronds, and two arm-lengths away—wedged upside-down behind the wicker partition that passes cleanly through a simmering terracotta vat—crouches another. Identical down to the bright copper pin in the left elbow, the twin knots of hemp above the wrist, the tiny curling fern-tip emerging behind one ear. It is trimming the wet lip of a gourd with a bone scraper, the thin damp ribbon of rind curling off the blade and immediately tucking itself back into a fresh wet patch on the shelf.

Lean sideways to peer into the narrow gap between two stacked water-jars—that impossible inward tunnel of identical freshly mortared niches stretching deeper than the tiny exterior shell could ever hold—and you see a third, a fourth, a fifth, dwindling back into the murk-red steam. Knees to chins, backs curved neatly into the alcoves, elbows tucked tight so no two touch, every single one of them shaping, lashing, scraping, pressing, in absolute, breathless synchrony.

Yet the longer you watch, the less sure you become that you are looking at a colony at all.

The one crouched beside your knee finishes tightening a clove hitch around a weeping strut, presses its broad thumb against the wet slip to brace the joint—and does not pull its hand away. Instead it leans gently forward, pressing its palm, its laced forearm, its rounded clay-slick shoulder straight into the damp mud of the wall. There is no splash, no resistance. The mortar softens and takes it in the way fresh wet clay accepts a slab, closing smoothly over its ribs and copper-pinned spine until nothing remains but a glistening smear of river-mud—while a heartbeat *before* its heels vanish, on the far side of the cellar, a bulge in the clay beneath a hanging trough has already split with a tiny wet snap and unfolded into the exact same crouched shoulders, already threading a bone needle before its shins have pulled free of the shelf.

They do not obey the walls any more than the wicker does, and they do not keep cleanly to a single moment any more than the echoes do.

Watch the one perched beside the central brazier reach smoothly into the unbroken belly of a boiling terracotta cauldron—its ochre forearm sinking straight through dry, fired clay without spilling a drop—and at the exact same instant, five warm thumb-impressions dimple the wet slip right beside your elbow, pulling a fresh ridge upward to plug a leaking seam with no wrist attached. Somewhere behind a dripping wall of broad leaves, a stone pestle grinds rhythmically in a clay bowl, *shhhk-clack, shhhk-clack*; when you part the leaves, there is only a freshly mortared bowl-imprint, still hot—and three feet to your left, a crouched figure is working its empty palm in tight, patient circles over bare air, half a breath ahead of the stone it will hold. Occasionally two of them pass through the same narrow choke between shelves without turning sideways, their wet shoulders sliding into a single shared ribcage for a dizzying second—four-armed, braiding sedge and tending embers out of one cinched torso—before parting cleanly back into two hunched figures with a faint *shhhk* like wet clay lifting off stone.

You cannot count them because they are not quite plural and not quite singular. Like a single length of sinew stitched back and forth through thick hide, you see a dozen separate, busy loops on the inside of the pocket, never sure whether there is only one relentless maker here, folded inward through its own seams so many times that it now crowds its own elbows.

Whatever its number, it never stops, never sleeps, and never allows itself to dry.

Now and then, one of the crouched figures pauses beside a steaming trough, dips a broad palm into water hot enough to blister human skin, and drinks in slow, steady pulls, its pale throat pulsing fast beneath the hemp bindings. Another snaps a newly popped shoot out of a mortar crack, chews it with a soft wet crunch, and spits the green fibrous mash straight into its palm to roll it into fresh caulk for a weeping joint. Sometimes one simply sits hunched over a buried charcoal fire-pot with its open palms and bare soles held an inch above the dull orange embers, drinking the heat directly through its clay-slick skin until thin ribbons of steam rise off its knuckles—and then immediately scoops a handful of boiling runoff over its own shoulders and brow, slathering itself wet again with sudden, urgent care.

Because more than anything else in the world, it is terrified of firing hard.

Everything in this boiling womb is kept eternally wet, eternally fresh, eternally mid-gesture for that one reason. To dry is to finish; to finish is to harden; to harden is to become brittle, locked into a single shape that cannot bend when the stratosphere leans against the walls, cannot take a new thumbprint, cannot heal a crack without shattering into dead shards. The endless steam is not an accident of the low boiling point. It is the whole work of its life—keeping the mud, the green shoots, the sinew, and its own unhardened flesh suspended forever in the supple violence of the first second.

You see that terror flash raw only when the outside gets in.

A colossal gust slams the exterior hides, tilting the domed floor over the emptiness below. Somewhere near the rafters, a woven reed mat shifts a hair's breadth against its mortar, and a needle-thin pinhole opens onto the screaming ink-violet void. A sliver of blue-white frost flashes into existence and shrieks as it boils into vapor an inch inside the room.

Every crouched figure in the cellar flinches in the exact same fraction of a second—a single, shared shudder running through all of them as though they share one nerve. Every pair of pebble-dark eyes snaps upward toward the bright, brutal slit. A low, dry vibration rises from their laced chests, not a cry so much as the tight groan of stressed green timber.

They swarm toward the leak—climbing the vertical shelves with gripping toes, unfolding out of the ceiling mortar right beside the tear—yet none of them lets its wet fingers touch the raw cold edge of the hole. Once, if a figure reaches a hair too close while slapping fresh mud around the rim, you see why: where the frigid stratospheric knife kisses the back of its slick knuckle, a coin-sized patch of warm ochre skin flashes instantly into pale, bone-dry, dead white ceramic, spiderwebs with fine cracks, and flakes away like eggshell before the creature yanks its hand back with a sharp hiss and plunges the stinging wrist deep into a simmering vat to soften the dead edge back into living slip.

So they hem the wound with thick coils of wet clay and packed sedge, hemming it tight from the outside in, while two of them pull bone awls from their lips and strike them together in a rapid, dry, frantic rhythm—*tk-tk-tk-tk*—a thin percussive clicking that cuts straight through the roar of boiling water. High in the ember-lit tangle of the rafters, the clicking is answered. Out of the smoke scuttles the brittle, ash-colored stitcher with its papery charcoal-dusted body, darting along the screaming seam to pull translucent gray thread from nothing and sew the raw tear in the air closed like wet leather. The instant the last tiny stitch hangs glistening in the dim mist, the crouched dwellers surge back in, plastering warm river-slip directly over the mended mat, burying every memory of the cold black sky beneath three fresh coils of clay and tucking a wet taro-bud into the center of the patch.

With the other rare spark of life in the room, they are almost absentmindedly gentle. If one of the blind, pale, pottery-grooved amphibians slips out of a runoff trough and slides across a crouched dweller's shin, the dweller tilts its calf slightly to make a warm bridge toward a deeper basin—and when the little creature vanishes backward into the overlap of a jar and a reed mat, a broad flat thumb carefully lifts the dry, herringbone-woven shed skin it left behind, folding it neatly into a tiny square to reinforce the lip of a leaking gourd.

Look past the big structural work—the bracing, the lashing, the endless coiling of walls—and you begin to see what they are actually building in the cramped moments between repairs.

Because nothing wears out and nothing is ever cleared away, their craft grows obsessively smaller as the room packs tighter. Inside the wide water-jars sit nested rows of smaller, thumb-pinched pots no bigger than hazelnuts, each holding three drops of simmering water and a single bright grain of moss. Beneath the heavy river-stones stacked beside the fire-pots, if you lift one with a burning grip, you find the flat underside intricately grooved with tiny, winding, branching channels carved by a bone needle—crooked miniature creek-beds scarcely wider than a hair—and if you watch closely, you may catch one of the crouched figures tipping a single scalding drop from a fern-tip onto the top of a tilted slate, its dark pebble-eyes gleaming as it follows the water meandering downhill along a river two inches long. They weave microscopic baskets out of single split fibers of hemp and pack each one with a speck of wet silt and a green shoot the size of an eyelash.

Somewhere impossibly far below the pale, frozen curd of thunderheads lies a wide world of riverbanks, warm rains, reedbeds, and potter's yards. Whether the dweller was torn up out of that low world long ago, or born up here out of nothing but a stubborn, scalding refusal of the empty dark, it no longer knows how large a valley is supposed to be. It remembers only the recipes—mud, charcoal, sedge, river-stone, boiling water, green shoot, sinew, copper—and so it keeps trying to press an entire lush, roaring lowland into this single cramped fist of mud lashed to the roof of the sky, folding valley inside valley, shelf inside jar, second inside first second, forever unfinished because to finish would mean there was nothing left to keep the sky at bay.

And as for you—crouched sweating and breathless in the middle of its feverish nest—it holds no anger toward you at all.

At first it moves around you the way water slips around a cobble in a fast channel. A broad, slip-slick palm nudges your elbow two inches left without malice so a fresh ledge of cobbles and ash-mortar can extrude from the wall behind your spine. A crouched back wedges quietly against your knee for leverage while its hands pull a ceiling thong taut.

Then, if you stay crouched there long enough—gasping in the starved thin air, your dry skin cracking in the heat, your ears still popping unevenly against the shifting pressure—it begins to notice that you are coming undone.

You feel a firm, rhythmic tug at your calf and look down to find one of them hunkered beside your boot, its brow furrowed in quiet, intense concentration, neatly lashing your ankle to a weeping timber strut with a double clove hitch of damp green hemp so the floor's shuddering won't shake you loose. Another unfolds smoothly from the mud beside your shoulder, presses a warm, smooth smear of fresh river-slip cleanly over a frayed seam in your sleeve, and smooths the edge with its wide, ridged thumb until the cloth disappears seamlessly into wet ochre clay. When you cough harshly against the hot smoke, a third leans in out of the taro-fronds, pebble-dark eyes wide and earnest, and raises a shallow, warm terracotta bowl to your mouth—brimming with scalding, muddy, root-steeped water—steadying your chin with the exact same tender, unyielding pressure a potter uses to true a wobbling rim.

There is no cruelty in those hands, only an overwhelming, tireless craft that cannot bear to leave anything loose, dry, or exposed to the thin dark outside. The floor creaks beneath your heels. A fresh coil of clay slaps smoothly onto the tile right against your knees, already rising to cup your shins. A pale green shoot snaps open from the damp crease of your collar. If you do not drag yourself free while your joints are still dry enough to stiffen, it will not harm you—it will simply keep moistening you, lashing you, bracing your ribs with bright untarnished pins, and folding you gently inward, thumbprint by warm thumbprint, until the altitude stops hurting your chest and you forget which side of the clay wall your hands belong to.
