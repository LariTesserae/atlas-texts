At first, across all that wet stone and distance, you scarcely notice the people at all. They do not cluster in crowds, and their clothes do not shout against the basalt. You might stand on a high terrace for ten minutes watching a squall drag across the western flats before realizing that the two dark shapes moving steadily along the lower causeway are not stray grazers, or that the faint smudge of rust-orange beside a canal embankment is someone’s wool scarf lifted by the wind.

Once your eye adjusts to the scale, you begin to see them everywhere in small, unhurried numbers—never hurried, never loud, moving through the wide geometry of the city with the easy attentiveness of people who know every step of a very large house.

You can tell someone who was raised on the plateau by a single unconscious habit. Walking off a sheltered civic terrace toward an exposed avenue, they tilt one shoulder slightly forward half a pace *before* their boot crosses the pale wind-curb. They do not look up or check the clouds; their body simply expects the clean, invisible shear where calm air ends and the highland gale takes hold of their coat. Their stride is long and even, shaped by arrow-straight causeways and high-altitude air that punishes haste. Most are deep-chested and lean, with faces weathered to a quiet ruddiness along the cheekbones and fine squint-lines at the corners of their eyes from years of midday silver glare off wet rock.

They dress for sharp weather and long silences. No stiff, rustling fabrics—in air this quiet, a noisy jacket feels rude. Instead, they wear thick, matte wool felted so densely that sleet beads and rolls right off it, cut into heavy calf-length coats with high collars and deep pockets, dyed in slate, peat-brown, unbleached cream, and the stubborn ochre of alpine lichen. Their boots are laced high and soled in a soft, dark composite that grips wet fracture-edges without clattering on public stairs. Even their tools and packs are dull-finished and quiet: oiled leather satchels, ceramic flasks, woven baskets strapped close to the back so nothing swings or knocks in the wind.

Their voices match the thin air. Because nothing in the city roars or hums, no one has ever had to shout to be heard, and sound travels cleanly across empty stone before vanishing into the open sky. People speak low and plainly, leaving wide pauses inside a conversation without rushing to fill them. Two neighbors might share a warmed stone bench along a canal landing for half an hour, passing a tin of hot salted broth back and forth while a raft of geese lifts off the next retention pan, exchanging only four or five sentences the whole time and parting well satisfied. When they need to catch someone’s attention across a broad plaza or a wet flat, they rarely yell; they raise a gloved hand, or give a single clear, two-note whistle that cuts cleanly through wind without startling the herds.

They live among the animals with a calm, unsentimental courtesy—neither masters nor strangers, just another species sharing the same warmed stone and shallow water.

In late spring, when the shaggy slate-and-cream grazers shed their winter undercoats by rubbing against the raw basalt corners of retaining walls and civic bollards, residents walk the outer boulevards with light wicker baskets, plucking thick hanks of lanolin-rich wool from the sharp rock edges and stunted juniper. They call it *ledge-fleece*. A woman heading for a transit pier will pause without breaking her thoughts to pull a fistful of pale wool off a dressed stone corner, tuck it into her coat pocket, and wait patiently while a broad-horned cow finishes licking mineral salt from the weeping seam beside the stair. No one chases the herds off the avenues unless they have bedded down directly across a clinic door or a loading ramp, and even then, people only nudge them gently aside with a flat wooden staff and a low clicking of the tongue.

The same quiet treaty holds with the rest of the plateau’s traffic. No one tries to make a housepet of the silver-backed ridge foxes that winter in the warmed undercrofts beneath the residential blocks, and no one drives them out either. Fish heads and gristle are left neatly on the lowest drainage ledge, never on a doorstep; compost bins are fitted with heavy stone lids that no fox paw can tip. It is ordinary to see an old man repairing a fishing net on a sunken courtyard step with a lean fox curled three paces down the stairs, eyes slitted against the sleet, sharing the same patch of warmed masonry. On the straight, icy canals, small crews of residents work in flat-bottomed skiffs—poled silently between the bridge footings—drawing up cold-water fish in woven traps while sleek aquatic hunters surface twenty meters downstream to watch them with dark, indifferent eyes. Waterfowl eggs are gathered from the outer retention pans only during a brief window in early summer, never more than one from a clutch, counted carefully and carried home packed in damp moss.

Because the rock is so young and the plants so slow, the people who live here have turned the making of soil into a quiet passion.

Up on the plateau, real dirt is rarer than dressed stone, and far more cherished. Behind almost every residential block, tucked into the wind-baffled lee of a sunken courtyard, sit neat rows of dark stone troughs where kitchen scraps, fish bones, goose droppings, cut sedge, and fine mineral silt are turned patiently month after month until they darken into true, sweet-smelling loam. Almost everyone keeps a hand in it. On clear afternoons, you see small groups of residents—often grandparents and children together—walking out onto the raw basalt aprons between districts with canvas satchels slung across their chests, kneeling beside sheltered gravel pockets to spoon handfuls of dark, inoculated earth into the cracks and press in cuttings of alpine willow, heather, or cold-hardy thyme. They do not expect to sit under the shade of those willows in their own lifetimes. They plant them the way other cultures lay cornerstones, smiling when a stunted green shoot survives its third winter without dying back.

The same unhurried craft runs through everything they make. The local potters dig pale, gritty clay from the newly cut stream-beds and fire it into heavy, speckled stoneware glazed with iron-rich mineral runoff scraped from weeping rock faces; the cups are thick-walled so tea stays hot in cold air, and rough enough on the outside to warm cold fingers without slipping. Weavers spread huge frames across the ground floors of unused civic halls, turning ledge-fleece and imported flax into dense tapestries and blankets heavy enough to muffle the draughts of a winter squall. Even the cooks work with the spare honesty of the high ground: clear fish broths brightened with sour sorrel and crushed juniper berry, dense loaves of dark barley bread, sharp pale cheeses aged in the cool utility strata beneath the plazas, and roots grown in the deep, softly lit hydroponic vaults below the transit piers.

As for the vast, silent machinery that keeps the plateau thawed, sheltered, and steadily rising—no one treats it as sacred, and no one forgets it is there. It is simply municipal work, tended by a small, weather-beaten corps of residents known simply as the *bench-walkers* or *crust-minders*.

They look no different from anyone else, except that their coats are usually dusted pale along the hems with fresh mineral bloom and they carry slender ceramic-tipped staffs and flat acoustic plates in waxed canvas cases. Early in the morning, they ride the silent transit spurs all the way out to the northern rim, where the last residential terraces give way to the raw, ascending slabs shrouded in pale mist. Out there, with sleet ticking off their hoods, they walk the brand-new benches listening to the deep waveguides through the soles of their boots and the plates pressed to the wet rock. They check where a newly locked bedding plane is weeping too heavily, mark out where a fresh meltwater stream is trying to carve its first channel, and make tiny adjustments—a millimeter shifted over a season, a stress-line eased across two kilometers of crust—so a future avenue will drain cleanly fifty years from now.

They are not hurried engineers staring at glowing walls of data; they have the unruffled patience of old ditch-diggers and dry-stone wallers. At district tables in the evenings, they argue mildly with the soil-keepers and canal-wardens over whether to let a newly formed mirror-pan stay as a nesting shelf for waders or tilt the substrate a hair eastward to feed a young grove of rushes. When a single crisp *tick* sounds deep underfoot in the middle of a meal—the bedrock settling another fraction of a hair—nobody drops their spoon. Someone might glance down at the floorboards and remark, almost affectionately, *"North shelf's taking its weight today,"* before reaching for the salt.

Step through one of the heavy, recessed doors from the huge grey terraces into a home, and the whole world shifts from stone and horizon to warmth and enclosure.

Because the plateau outside is vast, pale, and relentlessly open, people build their interiors like nested wooden bowls. Timber is scarce up here—brought up from lower latitudes or slowly harvested from sheltered subterranean groves—so every plank is oiled, fitted with care, and kept bare to the grain. Floors are layered with thick woven mats of cream-and-slate wool; wide stone windowsills are crowded with clay pots of rosemary, mountain mint, and gnarled, miniature junipers kept like small green companions. Lamps burn low and amber, never harsh, to rest eyes tired from a day of wet glare. A stove or warmed stone hearth bench anchors every main room, with damp boots lined neatly beneath it and a heavy iron kettle always close to simmering. Through the thick, recessed glass, the sleet and the sweeping silver clouds look as silent and distant as a painting.

Outside those warm windows, whole wings of the city sit empty—clean, frost-free, and dark. To a visitor from a crowded lowland, the sheer scale of the unused residential blocks and vacant plazas might seem lonely, even ghostly. To the people who live here, those spaces are not abandoned; they are simply *unopened*.

They treat the empty quarters with a cheerful, practical respect. A boat-builder claims a vacant ground-floor hall along the eastern canal for a season to shape ribs of steamed ash; a group of musicians rehearses in an empty terraced atrium because the damp dressed stone gives strings a clear, dry resonance; at midwinter, three neighboring districts open a great unused civic hall, sweeping the mineral dust from the floor and setting up long trestle tables lit with tallow lamps for a three-day feast of smoked fish, roasted roots, and juniper cordial. When the gathering ends, they wash the stone clean, close the shutters, and leave the rooms neat for whoever will unpack their belongings there a century or two from now.

And though each district may hold only a few hundred souls—small enough that everyone knows everyone else’s gait from half a kilometer away—no one lives in isolation. The silent transit carriages knit the vast tableland together effortlessly. A weaver finishes her afternoon work in District Three, steps onto a smooth stone pier in the grey drizzle, and rides thirty kilometers across sleet-swept basalt flats in twelve minutes without kicking up a drop of spray, arriving dry-shod at her brother’s terrace in District Nine in time to help gut the evening’s catch. Inside the matte-hulled carriages, passengers sit well apart out of quiet habit, exchanging brief nods, watching the drifting herds and shifting weather fronts slide past the windows like clouds over dark water.

Children grow up unusually observant here. With so few cars, no traffic noise, and miles of shallow water and raw stone at their doorstep, they learn to read the plateau the way other children learn street names. They can tell forty kilometers out whether a dark curtain on the horizon is dry snow, wet sleet, or mist rising off a newly warmed rim. On clear afternoons, they gather along the ankle-deep mirror-pans on the outer plazas, building intricate miniature dams out of sharp gravel and sailing scraps of juniper bark across the shivering water until a sudden gust spills the whole pan over a stone lip and starts a brand-new trickle down the steps. They know the exact year the rock under their schoolhouse stopped rising, and they measure family stories against the youth of the land: *before the south canal ran clear*, *the winter the lichen first took on Pier Four*, *when Grandmother’s courtyard was still bare mist*.

The oldest residents carry a particular, quiet wonder that never quite turns sentimental. Some of them can still remember standing at the edge of the central shelf as children and watching the benches where their grandchildren now live slowly emerge out of pale cloud, millimeter by millimeter. They have watched moss turn grey cliffs olive, seen the first pairs of geese become thousands, and watched thin gravel pockets darken into soil deep enough to hold a willow’s roots.

They know they will never see the plateau finished. They will never walk through a crowded avenue here, or sit under tall trees, or look down a valley smoothed round by ten thousand years of snowmelt. Ask any of them whether that feels like a loss, and they will usually look out across three kilometers of wet silver stone, watch a hawk ride the invisible warmth above a terraced roof, and smile a small, private smile.

Someone has to live in the morning of a place—to keep the hearths lit, mend the nets, tuck dark earth into the cold seams, and listen while the stone finishes becoming solid beneath their feet. As evening settles and the western squall sweeps across the central terraces, the recessed windows begin to glow amber in low, scattered clusters across the grey vastness. Behind the glass, kettles steam, boots dry on warm stone, and the quiet people of the high ground sit easily inside a house built for centuries, listening to the sleet tick softly against brand-new rock.
