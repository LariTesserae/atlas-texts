You can stand on a terraced bench for an hour, watching the cloud-banks spill over the slate rim and listening to the damp hush settle around your boots, and still believe the plateau is empty of anything that thinks.

The herds give themselves away once your eyes adjust to the shifting mist. The arthropods show themselves the moment you peel back an apron of wet moss. But the people who live up here have spent thousands of generations learning how not to interrupt a circuit, and to an outsider’s eye they have nearly disappeared into the resistance of the ground.

Usually, you notice a single wrong detail first.

A clump of coil-wood along a weeping ledge lets out its tiny dry *click*—and then, half a breath later, clicks again, slightly out of sequence with the sun-warmed shrubs beside it. If you look closely at the gnarled root-bed beneath that bush, you see a flat sliver of carved antler wedged carefully against the calcified latch, holding the tension back so the branch grinds slower and finer. Just past it, a low hummock of sodden bryophyte felt seems to shift its weight half an inch against the slope.

Then the hummock stands up, and water slips off its shoulders without a sound.

They are shorter than you, broader across the ribs and shoulders, built with the dense, low-slung economy of creatures shaped for thin air and spongy footing. At first glance they look like walking pieces of the bog itself. They wear heavy, seamless mantles felted from the thick mats of waterlogged plateau-moss, cured with the waxy oil of bladder-fungi and threaded throughout with glinting, hair-fine filaments harvested from the colonial weavers. The mantles hang nearly to their ankles, shedding the cold mist in quiet beads while doing a second, more vital job: the woven metal silk bleeds the faint bio-electric flicker of their nerves and muscles cleanly down into the wet peat beneath them. To the broad-winged hunters drifting through the cloud-bands overhead, a standing figure in a fresh cloak reads no differently than a lump of oxidized shale.

When one of them pushes back the heavy cowled hood, the face beneath is startlingly smooth, weathered the matte, mottled slate-grey of wet bedrock. Their brow-ridges sit low and heavy, shading pale amber eyes that blink slowly behind a clear inner lid whenever the high-altitude glare breaks through the mist. They have no protruding outer ears to catch the biting drafts; instead, shallow, crescent-shaped hollows lie tucked smoothly behind the hinges of their jaws, tuned less to airborne noise than to bone-conducted tremor and subtle shifts in barometric pressure. Along the ridges of their brows, the backs of their forearms, and the hollows of their collarbones grow dense, fine tufts of damp, copper-tinted filaments—not quite hair, more like delicate sensory moss—that stir and lift faintly whenever a charged cloud-front drags across the upper basins.

Most striking of all are their feet. Even in air cold enough to turn their slow exhalations into faint white wisps, they go barefoot. Their soles are broad, splayed, and deeply callused, dark as old peat and riddled with fine, salt-moist ridges. They cannot afford to insulate themselves from the earth. To wear thick leather boots up here would be to walk blindfolded and deaf through a crowded room.

Watch one of them cross a braided channel and you will see how deeply they belong to the buried engine. They do not stride. They roll their weight silently from heel to wide-spread toes in exact phase with the fourteen-second shudder rising through the limestone, setting each foot down just as the subterranean ram-pumps finish their stroke and begin their long, retreating sigh. Their weight vanishes into the plateau’s own movement. No puddle ripples beyond the rings the ground is already making; no silt-ambusher coiled in the copper-bright shallows feels a foreign vibration.

If you follow them—keeping well back, trying and failing to make your own boots as quiet as their bare heels—they will lead you straight into the middle of a village you would otherwise have walked right over without knowing it existed.

There are no walls, no roofs of cut timber, no blackened rings of hearth-stones. To cut stone or pile deadwood here would be foolish—the wet cold would soak through masonry in an afternoon, and fire is unthinkable in a world where nothing stays dry and smoke draws every eye across twenty miles of open terraces. Instead, they live *inside* the thatch.

Where two conductive root-mats cross beneath an old stand of dwarf-conifers, the saturated peat naturally swells into broad, springy mounds kept gently warm by the steady bleed of ground-current. Over decades, a kinship group—rarely more than twenty or thirty individuals—coaxes the living stems of surrounding cloud-trees and coil-wood downward, bending and braiding the pliant green branches into low, vaulted ribs and packing the gaps with live bryophyte mats. Within a few seasons the moss knits seamlessly together, thickening to five or six feet of drenched green insulation.

From three paces away, a dwelling is just another rounded hummock draped in pale lichen, indistinguishable from a hundred natural swells across the saucer-valley. Only when a hand parts a heavy, overlapping curtain of living sedge do you see the low, arched opening tucked into the leeward slope.

Inside, the hush deepens into a dry, cradling stillness. The floor is a raised lattice of stripped, resin-sealed stems hovering a couple of inches above the weeping shale so cold ground-water slides away untouched beneath it. Overhead, the ceiling is a dark, tightly woven basket of bare, metallic-oxide roots that hum so faintly you feel it more in your teeth than your ears. As the valley’s galvanic current drains steadily through those braided fibers, the whole dome radiates a gentle, dry warmth—never hot enough to scorch a knuckle, just the steady, blood-warm heat of a sleeping animal’s flank. The air inside smells of warm iron, dry wool, sour fermented paste, and crushed evergreen needles.

When a subterranean karst chamber shifts its fracture over the course of a century, cooling a terrace or silting shut a nearby brine-vent, the people simply gather their tools and walk to a warmer basin. Within two summers, the bent branches thicken, the sedge closes over the doorway, and the plateau swallows the hollow back without leaving a scar.

Because they have no fire, they have no forges, no chimneys, and no hammers—yet their hands are full of fine metal.

Walk down to the rim of a terraced seep-basin where warm, mineral-laden brine meets the cold acidic bog-water, and you will find their foundries lying completely open to the sky. Submerged just beneath the rust-orange scum, suspended from taut lines of colonial weaver-silk anchored to exposed copper veins, hang carefully carved forms of dense peat-wax and bone, rubbed dark with powdered pyrite so their surfaces carry a charge. At the upper end of the basin, the weaver-lines are spliced into clusters of mature, melon-sized bladder-fungi sitting swollen in the forks of stunted trees.

As the mist rolls in and the fungi drink static from the sky, bleeding measured pulses down the wires, the plateau goes quietly to work. Atom by slow atom, dissolved copper, malleable iron, and trace silver plate themselves onto the submerged wax molds. There is no clang of iron on anvil to shatter the silence and wake the predators in the channels. There is only patience. A small sewing-awl or fish-hook grows thick enough to use in a single wet month; a broad, curved harvesting-blade or a deep boiling-vessel takes two or three years of steady current.

When a maker finally lifts a finished knife from the bright silt and melts the wax core out inside a hot brine-vent, the metal is seamless and dull-gleaming, its surface patterned with faint, rhythmic bands as fine as wood-grain. Every heavy afternoon storm-front that drove the buried pumps faster and flooded the fungi with blue static leaves a denser, brighter line in the alloy. An elder running a bare, sensitive thumb along the flat of an old blade can read the weather of the decade it took to grow.

Their cooking runs on the same borrowed patience. They do not roast; they poach and ferment. Around the rust-rimmed brine-vents, you will spot smooth basalt sinkers tied to thin cords of braided sedge, vanishing down into the warm, pulsing throats of the earth. Tied beneath the water, wrapped tight in layered waxy leaves, sit bundles of wild tubers, fatty strips of stout-bodied burrower meat, and dense loaves pressed from the grey nutrient-slurry milled by the coil-wood shrubs. Every fifteen seconds the karst pump drives a fresh surge of hot, salted mineral water past the bundles, cooking them slowly to a tender, briny richness over the course of an afternoon.

Beside the sleeping-hummocks, sunken hollows lined with cured grazer-hide hold warm, gently bubbling mashes seeded with the same bacterial mats the plated arthropods tend beneath the conifers. These vats break down the bitter, metal-heavy sedge-roots into thick, sour, energy-rich pastes that keep through the lean, clouded winter months. For drinking water—since the bog-water is acidic and the vents are too salt-heavy—they send their children out along the narrow gullies with slender cups hollowed from shed grazer-antler. Moving with breathless steadiness, the young ones glide the smooth horn rims along the undersides of the colonial weavers’ humming threads, harvesting the uniform rows of pure, distilled dew-beads that cling there without snapping the silk or shorting the line to ground. A careless touch earns a sharp, clean jolt that snaps the wrist back and leaves a bright taste of copper in the mouth—a lesson the plateau teaches without a single raised voice.

Voice is a thing they do not possess.

Their throats are built to hold warmth and trap moisture from the thin air, not to throw vibration into the mist. Even an infant startled from sleep makes no wail—only a tight, silent shudder of the ribs, a quick sour tang of alarm-musk released from glands behind the jaw, and a sudden, prickly spike of surface charge that makes its parent’s forearm filaments bristle from three paces away.

Instead, they speak in the plateau’s own currencies, layered by distance.

Across a wide basin, they talk in light and silhouette. Sewn to the cuffs and shoulder-flaps of their sodden moss-cloaks are strips of cured, iridescent amphibian skin and small, mirror-bright plates of grown copper. Standing motionless on a sunlit ridge, a scout tilts a wrist or angles a mineral-tipped walking staff, sending crisp, silent flashes across half a mile of drifting mist—brief silver-green winks that another watcher reads as easily as flag-semaphore. Under heavy cloud-decks, they carry pairs of small, dried capacitor-pods pinched between thumb and forefinger, tapping them together to throw tiny, soundless pinpricks of pale blue coronal light against the grey glare.

Closer—within a few yards, standing on the same saturated terrace—they switch to ground-speech. Bare heels and wide toes press into the conductive peat-felt in intricate, barely perceptible sequences of weight and micro-current. Nothing splashes; no concentric ring mars the nearest moss-puddle. Yet through the wet sponge and the braided roots beneath, the message arrives cleanly in the soles of the listener’s feet—a cool, unmistakable grammar of pressure, rhythm, and faintly tingling shifts in surface voltage.

And when they wish to truly converse—to trade histories, to comfort the grieving, to argue over the routing of a weaver-line, to court—they come close enough to close a circuit.

Two of them will sit knee to knee beneath the dry warmth of a root-dome and clasp bare forearms, damp slate-grey skin pressing against damp skin until the fine copper-tinted filaments along their wrists mesh together. If a whole kinship group gathers to decide a migration or recount a memory, they sit in a tight ring and take hold of a single shared speaking-cord—a supple, salted length of braided root-fiber and weaver-silk kept moist in a horn tube. Through that linked bridge flows their deepest language: rapid, shimmering runs of tiny bio-electric pulses modulated by the saline pores of their palms, counterpointed by subtle twitches of tendon, rising and falling warmth, and measured squeezes of the fingers.

It is not mind-reading. It is a rigorous, learned syntax built out of cadence, polarity, and tension, capable of irony, poetry, and bitter precision. You cannot shout in it—pushing too much charge simply feels like a crude, clumsy sting, the mark of a child or a fool—and you cannot speak in it without letting the other person feel the exact steadiness or tremor of your own pulse. Even their name for themselves is not a sound, nor a word you could write on paper; it is a quiet, three-beat drag pressed twice into the heel of a neighbor’s palm, mimicking the way a fresh root takes hold of a current.

Their relationship to the rest of the plateau’s crowded life is one of quiet, intimate splicing rather than mastery.

They do not fence the heavy-fleeced grazing herds, nor drive them with dogs or shouts. Instead, certain tenders walk shoulder to shoulder right through the silent, moss-matted beasts, moving with the same unhurried, cushioned tread. The grazers barely turn their broad heads; smelling of peat, ozone, and damp wool, the tenders are simply smaller, upright neighbors who smell right and step in phase. While a heavy old bull tears steadily at wet sedge, a tender will kneel beside its forelegs and carefully saw away the heaviest, overgrown branches of mineral-crusted antler using a taut wire of braided abrasive silk. The beast tolerates the work—relieved of the unbalanced weight—and the tender walks away with dark, current-sensitive horn-tines that will be carved into compass-needles, latch-shims, and dew-cups. In return, when a deep swallow-hole begins to sag and collapse beneath a deceptive skin of moss, the tenders quietly lay down a line of crushed bitter-salts and shorted weaver-wire across the approach, steering the blind-walking herd safely up onto sound slate.

They keep a similar truce with the living machines. They never strip a hillside of coil-wood or drain a bladder-fungus dry; they simply tune them. A wedge here slows a slope of wooden ratchets so they mill harvested seed-pods instead of quartz grit; a pair of forked wooden pegs there lifts a colonial weaver’s shimmering thread three inches higher so it bridges a gap into a cold root-hollow without disrupting the tiny weavers sheltering under the ledge. They leave neat piles of chewed mineral-silt beside the gear-arthropods’ fermentation hollows, and in lean weeks skim a little of the warm, refined nutrient-slag from the lower drainage lips without disturbing the articulated chains of limbs passing pellets overhead.

Yet for all their stillness, their lives are not without edge or shadow.

Sometimes the deep geology beneath a basin shifts—a fault-chamber cracks wide, a basalt plug jams crooked in its throat, and the fourteen-second heartbeat beneath a whole valley falters, stretches to thirty seconds, and dies. Within a month the brine-vents go cold. The root-grids lose their gentle warmth; the moss-blankets turn rigid with rime-frost every clear dawn; the grazing herds drift silently over the watershed ridge to warmer seams.

When a basin dies, its people must pack their grown metal, their salted speaking-cords, and their etched records and move into the terraces of another group. Usually, hospitality is offered without hesitation—circuits are widened, new hummocks braided along the margins of the warm shelves. But in hard decades, when two or three neighboring pumps fail at once, silent, terrible disputes break out along the high divides.

War up here makes no noise at all. There are no war-cries, no clashing shields. Instead, rivals creep across the mist-drowned ridges in the small hours before dawn, cloaked tight in their woven shrouds, to sever a rival group’s weaver-lines so their growing-pools stall and corrode, or to drive bare copper spikes down into the conductive roots outside an enemy’s sleeping-domes, shorting the heat straight down into the cold shale so the sleepers wake shivering in dead dark. In the worst feuds, a raider will stand on the bank of a rival’s fishing-channel and deliberately drop three heavy stones into the copper-silt—a brutal, unthinkable obscenity of sound that drives away the gliders, trips every coil-wood mill on the slope at once, and calls the heavy, mud-colored ambush predators surging up out of the deep bends.

Even in peaceful seasons, the plateau demands constant, unforgiving attention, never more so than when the afternoon weather shifts.

When that dense, bright-topped bank of cloud builds over the western basins and rolls eastward like a low white wall, you see the whole hidden settlement respond in unison before the first silent raindrop touches the moss.

Out on the terraced shelves, makers hurry to unhook the taut weaver-lines from their submerged wax molds; left connected during a storm-surge, the sudden flood of blue static from the ridge-line fungi would plate the metal rough, brittle, and spongy, ruining two years of patient growth. Tenders out with the grazers rise smoothly from the sedge as the herd turns uphill, drifting alongside the broad, prickling antlers toward the better-drained slate benches.

Then the pearl-grey glare swallows the slope. The rain begins, heavy and soundless. Beneath every bare sole, the deep hydraulic throb quickens—fifteen seconds, twelve, nine—driving hard, shortened strokes up through the wet limestone. Along the upper ridges, the bladder-fungi begin to flare internally with cold pale-blue light, bleeding sharp, erratic surges straight into the saturated ground.

Instantly, every person caught in the open drops their stride and shifts into the storm-shuffle.

Because the wet peat is suddenly carrying steep, shifting gradients of voltage between the surging root-busbars and the weeping brine-vents, taking a long step across a seep-line can bridge enough current up one leg and down the other to stop a heart on the spot. So they pull their ankles tight together, hunching low inside their damp Faraday cloaks, and mince forward in tiny, sliding, inch-long shuffles—feet never separating by more than a hand’s width—reading the prickly danger of the ground through their callused soles as they work their way toward the insulated dry-mats of cured grazer-wool tucked inside the braided hummocks.

Inside the warm root-domes, children press close against the broad flanks of their elders, wrapped in dry fleece, feeling the ceiling above them hum noticeably louder and warmer as the storm-current pours through the valley grid. Outside, the mist smells sharply of wet wool, ozone, and hot shale. Nobody speaks—not even by touch—for a minute or two at the height of the surge, listening with their jaws and shins to the tightened, pounding pulse of the continent beneath them, letting the storm pass through the plateau’s veins like a fever breaking.

Twenty minutes later, ordinary blue sky tears open overhead. Bright, crisp white sunlight spills back over miles of glistening green. One by one, the curtains of lichen part. Bare feet test the cooling moss, feel the buried engine settle back from nine seconds toward fourteen, and step out again into the quiet work of the afternoon.

Because every subterranean ram-pump across the hundreds of square miles beats slightly out of phase with its neighbors, the plateau has no single universal pulse. Instead, every ridge and watershed carries its own intricate, overlapping rhythm—two or three distant hydraulic strokes meeting in the wet stone beneath your calves like slow polyrhythmic drums muffled under fathoms of felt.

For the people who live here, that overlapping beat is both clock and compass. A traveler crossing an unfamiliar highland in blinding white mist does not need stars or cairns. They simply stop, stand barefoot on the springy moss for half a minute, close their pale amber eyes, and listen upward through their shins. The slow, staggered interference of the eastern basin’s stroke against the southern chamber’s sigh tells them within a hundred yards exactly where on the beveled roof of the continent they are standing.

And they keep their history in much the same way—written not in ink, which would blur and rot in the perpetual damp, but in metal and in bone.

Inside the driest, oldest root-hollows, wrapped in waxed bryophyte felt, rest thin leaves of electro-grown copper and pale iron no wider than a palm. By carefully interrupting and redirecting the current across a submerged plate during its growth, their keepers coax branching, frost-like ridges, tiny smooth nodules, and rhythmic banded waves into the metal itself. To an outsider, a plate looks like an accidental piece of natural mineral crust. To a keeper whose bare, sensitized fingertips glide softly across the cool ridges, it is a ledger of births, a map of dead and living brine-vents, a lineage of kinship groups stretching back before the western escarpment last slumped.

When one of them grows too old for the storm-shuffle, when their internal warmth begins to fade and their forearm filaments turn brittle and grey, they are neither burned nor sealed in stone.

In the quiet clear hour after a front has passed, their kinship group carries them out beneath the oldest, broadest stand of cloud-trees in the valley—the ancestral grove where the conductive root-mats lie thickest and warmest against the weeping shale. They lay the body gently into a shallow cradle parted in the bright copper-silt of a warm seep-line, resting the bare palms face up against the damp earth, and carefully weave live, dark-barked feeder roots across the wrists, the ribs, and the ankles. Over the body they lay a thick, living apron of fresh moss, smoothing the edges until the slope looks untouched.

There is no weeping sound. Only a wide, silent ring of bare feet pressing a long, slow, fading cadence down into the bog-water, while hands link one last time around the speaking-cord to pass the dead person’s signature pulse from elder to child so the pattern is not lost.

Over the turning of the years, the plateau does what it does to everything laid inside its wet refineries. Rather than letting the bones dissolve into nothing, the warm, mineral-heavy brine slowly replaces calcium and skin with fine, gleaming films of precipitated copper, pale iron-scale, and bright bands of crystalline salt. The braided roots grow thick right through the ribs, drinking the accumulated metals back into the valley’s great conductive grid.

The people say that an old grove never feels quite the same as a young one. When an elder sits back against the moss-padded trunk of an ancestral cloud-tree, feeling the steady, blood-warm hum bleed softly into their spine, they claim they can tell the subtle, familiar resistance—the slight, particular drag and warmth on the valley’s current—of everyone who ever lay down beneath those branches. The dead do not depart to a sky above the blue, nor sink into the dark beneath the slate. They simply become part of the wiring that keeps the frost off the living.

There is only one place on the whole vast tableland where these quiet people ever seem unsettled, and few of them visit it more than once or twice in a lifetime.

Sometimes a curious youth, or a solitary elder whose basin has long since gone cold, will walk all the way out to the western rim—past the last braided channels, past the final stunted coil-wood thickets, out to the very lip of the beveled slate escarpment where the wet moss hangs over nothing at all.

There, sitting with their bare heels dangling above the pale abysses where the clouds gather and swell before spilling inland, they meet the edge of the silence.

There is no conductive peat beneath their feet out on the bare lip. The fourteen-second throb of the buried engines fades to a faint, distant shiver behind their heels. And rising up through the luminous, rising mist-banks from miles below comes a sound they have no grammar for—the distant, continuous, terrible roar of open wind tearing across dry canyons, of unbound rivers crashing recklessly over bare stone, of thunder and storm and ten thousand noisy, unshielded creatures shouting into the air.

They sit motionless on the wet slate for half an afternoon, their heavy hoods pushed back, the fine copper filaments along their brows trembling wildly in the updrafts from the loud world below. They watch the white cloud-sheets brim the cliff and slide silently inward past their knees, drawn toward the warm, humming green expanse at their backs.

Then, without a sound, they draw their bare feet back onto the cushioned moss, wait a beat until they feel the slow, steady stroke of the hidden earth return through their soles, and walk quietly back down into the crowded, gleaming hush—tending the endless, untouched machine that neither belongs to them nor runs quite the same without them, high above the sky.
