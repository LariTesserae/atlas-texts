To see the people of the terraces clearly, one must first stop looking for them against the stone.

From fifty paces away, a woman carrying a skin of ice up a stepped lane looks like a shifting piece of the slope itself—bundled in unbleached grey felt, greased hide, and shaggy fleece turned inward against the skin, her shoulders rounded beneath the curve of a horn-lashed pack-frame. Even her face is half-hidden behind a strip of loosely woven wool wrapped across nose and mouth, stiffened white around the nostrils with frozen breath. Only when she steps out of a shadowed alley into the sudden glare of noon does she resolve into flesh: dark, narrow eyes crinkled permanently against the high-altitude brilliance, cheekbones burnished to the color of old copper by sun and frost-burn, skin kept glossy with mutton tallow so the metallic air will not split it open.

They are built the way the city is built: compact, deep-walled, and stingy with heat. Centuries in the thin air have given them broad, barrel-like chests, heavy shoulders, and thick wrists, with slow, steady pulses that scarcely quicken on a stair-cut that would leave a lowlander gasping on his knees. Their hands are broad and calloused, the knuckles thickened from cold and stone-work, the fingertips stained permanently with tallow, dung-ash, lichen-dye, and wet slate dust. They move with a low, gliding economized stride in thick-soled felt boots, never swinging their arms unnecessarily, never hopping a step where a flat-footed placement will do. To watch a crowded alley at midday is to watch a current of quiet grey wool flowing around the matted flanks of pack-oxen—no jostling, no shouting, every body yielding an inch before contact, every breath measured and kept close to the chest.

Because mouths stay covered outdoors and raised voices unravel to nothing in the thin air, they are a people who read faces from the bridge of the nose upward. A slight lift of the brow, a softening at the outer corner of the eye, the angle of a woolen hood—these carry the weight of greetings, warnings, flirtations, and old resentments. When they do speak outside, their voices are pitched low in the throat, a spare, murmured cadence with few breathy sounds; they have a proverb that an angry man empties his coat into the wind. Haste, loud laughter, weeping in the open lanes, or wild gestures are regarded not merely as bad manners but as a kind of foolishness, like leaving a door-curtain tied back in winter. Their humor is correspondingly dry and quiet—a muttered remark passed sideways through a woolen veil while scraping dung from a wall, answered only by a brief, silent crinkling around a neighbor’s eyes and a slow nod.

Inside the stacked terraces, life is organized not by streets or grand clans, but by warmth.

A household is a vertical thing—what they call a *stack* or a *smoke*. At its root sits the low, stone-vaulted byre, packed shoulder to shoulder at night with long-haired cattle and spiral-horned sheep. Directly above the byre, separated only by split-slate slabs chinked with turf and clay, lies the warmest, dampest room in the tier. Here sleep the ones whose blood runs thinnest: the very old, the newborn, the sick, and the women who spin and weave through the winter months, their joints eased by the heavy animal heat rising steadily through the floor. They know the beasts beneath them without looking—by the wet, rhythmic sighing of a cow settling onto frozen straw, the dull scrape of a horn against basalt, the sharp sudden shift in weight that means a ewe has gone into labor in the dark.

Above the warm-room sit the hearth-tiers, where the soapstone lamps burn and the slow dung-fires smolder beneath flat cooking stones, and above those lie the upper rooms—drier, brighter, colder at night, belonging to the younger adults, the herd-watchers, and the winter stores of barley, dried blood-cake, and frozen tubers. Because each flat gravel roof serves as the work-yard and thoroughfare for the house tucked behind it up the slope, no household is ever truly sealed off from its neighbors. You live inside the acoustics of stone. You know when the grandmother in the tier above has woken by the soft shuffle of her felt soles across your ceiling; you know when the family beside you is winnowing grain, grinding barley in a basalt hollow, or dressing a lamb by the steady, muffled thump that travels through three feet of rubble-packed wall.

This closeness has bred an intricate, unspoken etiquette. No one bangs on a lintel or shouts into a doorway; to call on a neighbor, you tap twice on the cold stone jamb with a knuckle or a horn toggle and wait until a shadow shifts behind the heavy felt curtain, then slip through sideways so the least possible heat escapes. Between adjoining rooms, neighbors communicate without stepping into the freezing lane at all—a rhythm of dull knocks struck with a stone against the shared party-wall: two slow taps to ask for a live coal, three quick taps to warn that a meltwater runnel on the roof has iced over and is backing up toward the eaves.

Water makes citizens of them more surely than any king could. There are no palaces in the city, no garrisons, no stone thrones, and no single ruler who could possibly govern a ribbon of dry-laid terraces stretching for leagues across separate mountain spurs. Instead, each spur-ward governs itself through its *runnel-keepers* and *cistern-elders*—usually older women and men whose knees no longer carry them to the high pastures, and who spend the bright middle hours of the day walking the contour-lines with horn picks and chert scrapers. They clear grit, bird-droppings, and stray frost-heaved chips from the hand-pecked water channels; they measure the midday weep of the south-facing walls with notched bone sticks; they settle the quiet, bitter disputes that arise whenever a household reshapes its roof-gravel in a way that steals three cups of condensation a day from the terrace below.

Water-theft is one of the gravest crimes known on the plateau, ranked just below the deliberate undermining of a retaining wall. Yet even justice here moves on the slow, heat-conserving clock of the high country. There are no whips or gallows. A man who cheats the cisterns or refuses his share of the winter stone-mending is met with a gradual, terrifying cooling. His neighbors cease to bank their hearths along the wall he shares with them, letting frost creep quietly through the rubble fill into his sleeping room. No one lends him a bull for his heifers; no one trades him salt or juniper-splints; the women at the ice-cellar turn their shoulders slightly when he approaches, leaving him to wait until the last shaded hour when the damp stones begin to freeze. Most offenders break within a single season and make quiet restitution—a winter spent hauling quarried slate for a neighbor’s wall, a half-score bags of dried dung stacked neatly against the cistern-keeper’s door. For the rare soul who commits murder or breaks the great ice-cellars, the sentence is simpler and final: they take his felt boots, his tallow-pot, and his fire-stone, and walk him past the last dung-plastered wall onto the open scree. No one needs to strike a blow. The shadow of the ridge finishes the work before sunrise.

Their material world is astonishingly spare, and because it is spare, almost everything they touch has a history longer than a human life.

A beam of gnarled dwarf-juniper spanning a byre ceiling is rarely replaced; it is oiled with bone-marrow, watched for splits, and known by the name of the great-uncle or grandmother who helped haul it up from the lower gorges forty years before. A soapstone lamp blackened to a glassy gloss may have lit the births of six generations. Every piece of bone from a butchered ox or sheep is spoken for before the carcass cools: ribs split into hide-scrapers and loom-battens, knucklebones smoothed into gaming pieces for children who sit huddled on the warm floor above the cows, shoulder blades notched into shovels for ash and dung, shinbones ground into fine needles and awls. Antler and spiral horn are steamed over bubbling broth-pots until they soften enough to be bent into pack-saddles, ladle-bowls, door-pegs, and small, curved knives for harvesting tough barley.

Copper is treated almost like a living creature—rare, warm-colored, stubbornly enduring. A household that owns a copper awl keeps it wrapped in greased lambskin inside a hollowed horn case; when it wears down after eighty years of piercing thick hide, the stub is never discarded, but hammered cold with a smooth river-pebble into a small pin or a tiny, dull-voiced bell hung around the neck of the lead cow in a herd. That faint, dry *tink* on a misty ridge can be heard by a shepherd long after the beast itself has vanished into the freezing fog.

That rare metal, along with timber, rock-salt, and occasional bundles of tough lowland hemp, enters the city through the *gorge-walkers*—a lean, slightly restless breed of men and women who alone among the plateau-folk descend the broken river-chasms toward the unseen valleys far below. They travel in small, roped trains with the surest-footed pack-goats, carrying bales of dense mountain felt, dried medicinal wormwood, carved hornware, marmot pelts, and cakes of hard, smoky cheese. The people of the terraces regard the gorge-walkers with a mixture of gratitude and quiet unease. They return weeks later looking hollowed-out and strange, coughing out the thick, wet air of the lowlands, smelling faintly of green rot and leaf-mold. They bring back not only juniper poles and thumb-sized ingots of hammered copper, but unsettling stories—of rain that falls warm and heavy from a roof of black cloud, of rivers that never skin over with ice, of wooden towers called trees that groan and thrash in the wind like mad beasts. Most listeners nod politely behind their mufflers, rub a little tallow into their hands, and are quietly relieved that they will never have to see a place so damp and unruly.

For all their caution, the city is not joyless; its pleasures are simply tucked inward, like its hearths.

Beauty here is small, dense, and startlingly intimate. Because the mountains and masonry are an endless grammar of grey, ochre, and frost-white, the eye hungers for tiny concentrations of color and texture. Women scrape the crustose lichens—rust-red, sulfur-yellow, weathered amber—from dead boulders out on the scree, steeping them in stale urine and meltwater over slow embers to dye thin skeins of spun wool. They do not dye whole coats; that would be wasteful. Instead, they stitch narrow bands of rust and pale gold along the hems of grey felt door-curtains, or braid bright threads into the forehead-tassels of a favorite pack-ox, or embroider tiny geometric water-channels along the cuffs of a child’s mittens. Men spend the long, dark winter evenings beside the tallow lamps incising microscopic patterns into horn toggles and bone combs—running spirals like sheep-horns, stepped zigzags like terraced roofs, delicate silhouettes of ravens and pale-flanked valley deer—rubbing soot and grease into the scratches until the lines stand out crisp as frost on dark basalt.

Their music, too, is made for low ceilings and thick walls. You will never hear a drum pounded loud enough to shake an alley, nor a horn blown across a canyon. Instead, after the shadows snap the wet stones dry in late afternoon and the felt curtains fall shut, small gatherings form in the warmest rooms above the byres. Someone brings out a shallow wooden resonator no larger than a soup-bowl, strung with twisted sheep-gut and bowed softly with coarse cattle-tail hair. Another clicks two polished rib-bones lightly between thumb and forefinger. Then three or four voices begin to hum—a deep, layered throat-singing that scarcely stirs the flame of the soapstone lamp, yet fills the low chamber with a rich, honeyed vibration. Neighbors sitting on the far side of the stone wall lean their bare shoulder blades against the cold slate and listen with their spines, feeling the song travel through the masonry like shared heat.

They eat as they live: slowly, heavily, and without waste. A winter supper might be a thick, dark porridge of roasted barley meal stirred with melted tallow, chunks of frost-sweetened tuber pulled from the tiny sunken gardens behind the windbreak walls, and strips of dried, dark meat softened in sour milk or blood-broth. Tea does not grow here, so they steep dried silver wormwood and crushed juniper needles in snow-melt, drinking it scalding hot from small horn cups—bitter, resinous, and sharp enough to clear the wool-dampness from the throat. Children chew on hardened balls of dried curds that take an hour to soften in the cheek, or suck marrow from cracked hare-bones while curled on the warm floor beside the broad-headed hounds.

Those hounds are scarcely separate from the people. Heavy-muzzled, thick-furred, with short ears tucked tight against frostbite, they almost never bark; barking wastes heat and carries poorly anyway. Instead, a hound guarding a doorway or a flock warns intruders with a low, rumbling vibration deep in its barrel chest—more felt through the soles of felt boots than heard. They sleep buried in warm ash heaps outside the doors by day and pile against the felt curtains by night, sealing the bottom draft with their own heavy flanks. In return, they receive every gristle-end, every scraped hide-trimming, and the quiet respect owed to fellow keepers of the warmth.

The birds, too, are folded into the daily rhythm of the wards. The glossy black choughs and plump snow-finches know the exact hour each terrace shakes out its grain-sieves or dresses a goat on the upper work-roofs; they drop from the masonry chinks in silent, wheeling clouds, bold enough to pluck a stray barley-husk from beside a weaver’s boot. People do not stone them. In a world where solitude is death, the presence of so many bright-eyed, warm-blooded scavengers clinging to the stone ledges feels like a quiet confirmation that the mountain has room for life.

Only one creature is kept at a solemn distance, and that is the great bone-eating vulture that rides the cold air along the highest cliff-rims.

In a land of fractured basalt and permafrost, there is no earth deep enough to bury the dead, and no fuel plentiful enough to burn them. So when a person’s inner fire finally goes out—usually quietly, on a wool pallet in the warm room just above the cattle, with kin sitting close enough to hold their hands until the fingers turn waxy—the body is washed with a rag dipped in precious meltwater, wrapped in an old felt cloak that has already served its years, and carried before dawn by four strong bearers up the steepest, narrowest stair-cuts to the high spurs far above the topmost water-runnels.

There, on bare, wind-scoured ledges tilted away from the city’s catchment basins so no drop of thaw will ever run down into the cisterns, wait the *sky-dressers*. They are ordinary citizens who take the duty in silent rotation, wearing greased hide aprons and carrying heavy hammer-dressed basalt stones. They do not chant or weep aloud. With patient, practiced efficiency, they strip the cloak so it may be washed and reused, lay the body open to the thin sun, and break the long bones so that nothing will linger half-consumed to draw rot or stray wolves. Then they step back behind a windbreak rock and sit motionless in the biting cold while the huge shadows detach themselves from the cliff-rims—first the ravens, dropping in quick, glossy arcs, and then the bearded vultures, landing with a dry rustle of vast wings to swallow the fractured bones whole and carry the marrow back up into the pale-edged blue.

By midday, when the sun warms the stone enough to draw a faint sheen of dampness across the ledge, nothing remains except a dark grease-stain on the granite and a handful of pale quartz pebbles. The sky-dressers wash their basalt hammers with clean frost-rime, fold the old cloak under one arm, and walk unhurriedly back down the steps into the maze of warm walls.

They do not think of this as grim, nor do they think of themselves as trapped upon a harsh roof of the world. To them, the lowland gorges—wet, loud, rotting with soft wood and restless water—sound like a feverish place where nothing lasts and no one can hear themselves think. Up here, the sun is honest: it warms what it touches and makes no false promises about the shadow. The stone gives up its blocks at its own pace, one winter’s frost-crack at a time. The lichen spreads across the retaining walls a fingernail’s breadth in a lifetime, turning a grandfather’s new masonry into an ancestor’s patched rust and gold.

And at dusk, when the great blue shadow of the western cordillera sweeps across the basin floor and locks the damp alleyways in instant frost, the tens of thousands of inhabitants slip inside behind their grey felt curtains. The cattle shift and breathe beneath the slate floors. The tallow lamps wake in a slow constellation behind thick stone walls. The city draws its immense, quiet warmth inward, settling down against the ribs of the mountain like a vast herd hunkered out of the wind, patient as rock, waiting without hurry for the noon thaw.
