At first, you think the city runs itself. You can walk a quarter-mile along an upper gantry, past humming copper mains and chalked pressure gauges, and see only the faint drift of heat off the roofs and the occasional white breath of a relief valve.

Then you stop walking—because the thin air forces you to stop, leaning your elbows on a hot iron rail until the bright spots clear from your eyes—and once your own pulse slows down, you begin to notice the people.

They were there the whole time. You simply missed them because you were looking for human speed.

Forty feet below you, wedged into the shade of a boiler wall, a figure in a pale linen coat is sitting on a folded canvas stool with a tin-brimmed hat tilted low over their eyes. They have not moved since you turned the corner. Their hand rests lightly against a warm ceramic conduit, fingers spread, listening to the slow crawl of liquid inside. Across the ravine, two more figures are crossing a bolted stair-street at a pace so measured it scarcely reads as motion against the dark brickwork—a flat-footed, unhurried glide, knees never quite snapping straight, chests rising and falling in deep, economical sweeps. When a heavy iron pawl drops somewhere beneath the viaduct with a dry *tock*, neither of them turns their head; they simply pause half a beat in unison, the way lowlanders pause when a church bell strikes the hour, and then continue up the steps.

There are thousands of them tucked into the twelve-level honeycomb of the basin. You just have to learn to see at the tempo of expanding iron.

Look closely at the ones who walk the open terraces and the dim alleys alike, and you see how thoroughly the mountain has shaped their bodies and their gear. They are lean and remarkably deep-chested, with broad, barrelled ribs built to pull enough oxygen out of a sky that has none to spare. No one hurries here; hurrying turns a person’s lips slate-blue and brings on a hammering behind the eyes that lasts until dusk. Even in an emergency—a ruptured still, a fouled counterweight—they move with a terrifying, deliberate calm, stepping cleanly over the razor-edged basalt talus in boots soled with thick, layered felt and studded with soft copper rivets that bite into oily rock without striking sparks.

Their wide tin-brimmed hats are battered and pocked with tiny dimples. Out on the ridges, the tin throws back the unblinking daylight that would otherwise bake a skull dry in an hour; down in the sweating under-galleries, it serves another purpose. Whenever a thermal shudder runs through the ironwork and a cluster of glassy gypsum icicles snaps from a pipe forty feet overhead, the shards rain down with a sharp, crystalline *ping-ping-tink* off the walker’s brim. They do not flinch or look up. They only dip their chin an inch to spill the pale splinters into the gutter and keep going.

Their coats are stranger still. Cut loose from heavy, undyed linen, they are never washed to softness. Over months of moving through the parched upper air and the silica-heavy damp below, the cloth drinks up the basin’s dissolved salts and gypsum dust until it dries into a pale, supple armor that stands slightly away from the skin. A new apprentice’s coat rustles like ordinary fabric; an older worker’s coat has turned stone-grey at the cuffs and hem, stiff enough to hold a crease like thin bark, knocking faintly against their shins as they walk. It keeps the scalding steam off their forearms in the galleries, shields them from blister-hot iron railings, and holds a pocket of still, insulating air against their ribs when they step out onto a sun-struck switchyard.

Because the thin air kills voices at thirty paces—and because shouting wastes breath no one wants to spend—the people of the basin are extraordinarily quiet. When two of them meet in a narrow stair-street, one steps into a passing-niche carved into the slag-brick and waits without comment. Conversation happens close, face to face, in low, dry, unhurried phrases separated by generous intervals of silence. Those silences are never awkward; they are simply the space required to draw a clean breath and let a thought settle.

When they need to speak across a distance, they do not use the air at all. They use the city.

Every household, kiln-room, and watch-post has a small mallet of dense horn or wrapped brass hanging beside the door. In a basin where thin air swallows a shout, a braided copper main or a cast-iron return pipe carries a sharp rap for three hundred yards without losing a syllable. Stand quietly in a shaded stairwell and you begin to distinguish the human taps from the mechanical *tocks* of the ratchets: two crisp knocks followed by a pause—*passing through the east flue*; three soft taps on a warm water-main—*the condenser sump is climbing*; a single slow double-tap at dusk from a kitchen pipe, answered five seconds later from two terraces down—*the broth is drawn, come up when your shift clears*. They keep the rest of their correspondence in chalk. Every blank hand’s-breadth of dark brick beside a valve, a screw-jack collar, or a rail switch carries dense columns of neat white strokes, dates, amperage figures, and small, wry notes left by a worker six hours earlier for the one coming on behind them.

---

Where the people actually live is easy to miss from the high walkways, because they do not build houses separate from the industry. They inhabit the temperate band hidden inside it.

The top two terraces are too brutal for domestic life—baked by day, stripped of heat the moment the sun drops behind the western crag. The lowest galleries, down among the sweating zinc curtains and lead gutters, are too wet and mineral-choked; sleep down there for a year and your lungs begin to rattle with fine white dust. Between the two lies a sheltered middle stratum, wedged behind the thick slag-brick walls of the kiln-houses and beneath the shaded cisterns, where the heat rising out of the young basalt and the cool, dark mass of the overhead viaducts cancel each other into a steady, dry warmth.

Step through a heavy, felt-gasketed door off an iron catwalk and the harsh industrial geometry softens at once. The rooms are narrow, low-vaulted, and lined with pale glazed stoneware tiles that shrug off the damp. There are no broad glass windows to let in the searing glare—only deep, louvered air-slots and high transom panes cut from the city’s own electrically grown, bubbleless glass, tinted a faint smoky amber that turns the hard blue daylight the color of steeped tea.

Everything inside a home acknowledges that the mountain beneath it is still moving.

Shelves are braced with slotted iron brackets; bedframes and workbenches rest on threaded brass feet that can be turned a half-twist with a thumb when the floor begins to cant. In the center of every kitchen table sits a small, shallow saucer of dark pressed oil. No one eats from it. It is the household level. Over the course of a season, as the raw basalt swells unevenly beneath the foundations, the dark meniscus creeps slowly toward one rim of the dish. When it touches a painted red hair-line near the lip, someone picks up a stick of chalk, goes out to the nearest retaining pier, and adds a tally mark for the collar-turners. A few days later, you hear the deep, patient groan of cast steel turning a quarter-rotation in its socket beneath the house, and the oil slides quietly back to the center of the saucer.

You notice the children first by their eyes, and then by their stillness.

Children born at this altitude have a compact, watchful gravity that unsettles the rare outsider who climbs up from below the clouds. They never sprint down a walkway or shriek in the alleys; they learn before they can tie their own felt boots that running leaves you dizzy and gasping, and that dark iron in the sun will take the skin off a palm before you feel the burn. Instead, they develop a spider-like economy of movement, climbing bolted stair-streets with three points of contact and testing the air an inch off any rail with the back of a knuckle before they grip it.

Their games match the city’s pulse. On the shaded landings between the slag-brick walls, two or three of them will sit cross-legged for an hour, building delicate, precarious spires out of the razor-edged obsidian splinters swept from the freight yards, seeing whose tower can survive the faint floor-tremor of the next stretch-beam pawl. Down in the damp stairwells, they draw two parallel chalk lines six inches apart on a sweating iron pipe and bet dried apricot pits on which of the palm-sized, stoneware-plated raspers will inch across the finish line before sundown. They grow up knowing the names of twenty different mineral crusts before they ever see a live leaf.

Because there are no plants anywhere in the three-mile basin, food arrives out of the clouds like a slow tide.

Along the sheer outer retaining walls, where the mountain drops away into the dirty wool of the canyon mist, heavy wire-rope hoists descend out of sight. They are driven by the same silent lead counterweights and solar stretch-beams that turn the factories, creeping upward at thirty feet an hour. Every few days, a latticed iron basket clears the rim, bringing up what the high rock cannot grow: sacks of hard grey millet, dried lentils, barrels of salted mutton, blocks of dark pressed oil-cake, bolts of raw linen, and wrinkled dried fruit hard as horn. On the downward trip, the baskets carry what only this mountain makes: crated slabs of hexagonal basalt, ingots of naturally drawn bright tin, lenses of flawless glass, and jars of pure blue copper sulfate.

The people of the basin almost never go down those ropes, and the lowlanders below almost never come up. The trade is conducted by stamped tin manifests wired to the basket handles. On the rare occasion a lowland factor rides a hoist up to settle a dispute over tin weights, the basin folk treat them with gentle, amused pity—seating the breathless visitor on a shaded bench with a stoneware cup of watered vinegar and waiting two full days for their lips to lose their purple tinge before opening a ledger.

Inside the middle-level kitchens, every scrap of that imported food is coaxed back to life with fierce patience. You cannot cook with the tepid, mineral-heavy water from the lead gutters; it leaves a gritty white ring in a pot in ten minutes and turns a person’s kidneys to gravel inside five years. Every pantry has a glass alembic still nestled against a warm steam-return pipe, dripping clear, twice-distilled water into stoppered carboys all day long.

And because they have no gardens, they keep crocks.

Along the warmest back wall of every dwelling—the wall shared with a kiln flue or an electrolytic vat—stands a tight-packed rank of dark stoneware jars, their wooden lids weighted with smooth lumps of cast lead. Inside them live the only crops the basin grows: ancient, jealously tended mats of grey yeast, pale brine-fungi, and thick, gelatinous vinegar-mothers fed on imported sugar-cake, millet water, and measured pinches of pure mountain salt. These crocks are heirlooms. When a young couple sets up a room of their own three terraces over, their mothers ladle a pale, sour ribbon of live ferment out of the family jars to start their wall. The kitchens smell not of woodsmoke or fried greens, but of warm sour dough, clean sharp vinegar, toasted grain, and hot wet stone. They bake dense, crisp flat-loaves directly on oiled slabs of basalt set over capped geothermal vents, and eat them soaked in salty, clear broths brightened with pickled root-shavings and a spoonful of living brine.

---

Yet if you stay long enough to follow the people out of their kitchens and down onto the central basin floor, where the newest stone shoulders upward in raw, stepped domes, you realize that their real gardens are not in the crocks at all.

They are underground.

To an outsider, the basin looks like a factory clamped onto a volcano. To the people who live here, it is an orchard—only one made of voltage, pressure, and cooling crust. Having no soil, no seasons of rain, and no green things to sow, the deep human instinct to tend, graft, prune, and wait on a harvest has migrated entirely into the rock beneath their boots.

The people who work the great braided copper grounding cables—the ones that plunge straight down into magma-warm fissures—do not call themselves miners. They do not break stone with powder or beat it with picks. They are *growers*. You see them kneeling on thick folded wool mats beside the hot cracks, tin brims pushed back, reading the hairline drift of brass galvanometers and adjusting heavy sliding copper bars along ceramic resistance coils by a quarter of an inch.

What they adjust today, they will not harvest.

The plastic stone receiving their low-voltage current lies miles beneath the city, creeping upward at the pace of a fingernail growing. The hexagonal cavities being coaxed into alignment now, the hair-fine veins of pure bright tin being drawn through the molten lattice like warp threads on a loom, will not cool, harden, and push up through the basin floor for fifteen or twenty years. A woman who shifts a grounding lead on a warm Tuesday afternoon is tending a crop her daughter will saw open when she is grey-haired.

This stretches their sense of time into something vast and oddly gentle. Because paper dries brittle as dead leaves on the upper decks and rots in the sweating galleries below, their agricultural ledgers are hammered onto thin sheets of pure tin, strung on copper wire rings and hung in dry niches beside each rising dome. Before a crew sets a slow, cable-drawn saw to a freshly emerged rib of basalt, an elder unhooks the tin ring and reads the stamped notations left two decades earlier by someone long since dead: *Third dome, east fissure. Held steady at four measures through the dry season; backed off half a point when the sulfur spiked. Aimed for wide-cell glass.*

Then they take small, long-handled brass hammers and tap the newly risen stone, leaning close with an ear near the warm obsidian face, listening to the pitch of the hidden geometry inside. If the basalt rings with a clean, hollow chime—proving the cavities rose true and uncrushed—a rare, quiet smile breaks across their dry faces. They speak of the mountain the way lowland farmers speak of a stubborn, beloved field: *The north seam came up tight-grained this spring.* *Old Kellin bled the south cable too hot in his last year, look how the tin tangled.* *Give the central dome another season before you saw it; it hasn’t finished settling its ribs.*

This work splits the city into subtle, complementary tribes—not by wealth, for everyone eats the same flat millet loaves and wears the same stiffened linen, but by altitude and climate.

The ridge-folk, who tend the mile-long blackened stretch-beams, grease the stone troughs, and manage the cloud-hoists along the outer rim, have skin baked to a dry, fine-wrinkled bronze and eyes permanently narrowed to bright slits against the hard blue sky. Their hands are calloused from hot iron and chalk; they smell of oiled steel and dry sun. Down in the shaded under-galleries, the condenser-tenders and electrolytic pot-watchers are pale as tallow, their hair perpetually damp under their tin hats, their fingers stained faint verdigris at the cuticles from scraping copper sulfate crusts off the zinc fins. They smell of hot wet slate, alum, and pennies.

The two halves of the city meet in the middle-level dining halls and stair-streets, trading dry heat for damp shadow, marrying across the levels so a household often has one person who knows the moods of the sun-beams and another who knows every leaky flange beneath the third viaduct.

---

They are not entirely alone in their quietness. Though animal life is sparse enough to seem almost absent, the basin’s people have woven the few non-human creatures that share the rock into the rhythm of their days with an unsentimental, watchful tenderness.

Down in the sweating dark beneath the bridges, the palm-sized, stoneware-plated animals that creep along the wet iron elbows are treated like slow, quiet livestock. The gallery-tenders call them *blind-nuts* or *crust-backs*, and never harm them. Left alone, the alum needles and sulfur whiskers blooming on the sweating zinc would choke the drainage gutters in a month; the raspers graze those mineral carpets clean, leaving neat, pale-wet trails across the dark metal. When a pipe-fitter has to unbolt a leaking steam-elbow, they first slide a thin, oiled wedge of horn under the flat rubbery underside of any rasper clamped nearby, coaxing it gently loose without cracking its glazed plates, and set it on the warm pipe beside it. Some of the older tenders paint a tiny dot of red ochre on the back plate of a particularly large, diligent one, recognizing it years later three galleries away and greeting it with a soft, breath-saving click of the tongue.

High above, the four-winged gliders of oiled vellum remain strangers—too remote, too light, riding invisible pillars of hot air where no human lung could draw a breath. Yet the ridge-folk know their habits intimately. When a pair of gliders folds flat at dusk and slips down the mouth of an idle brick chimney to roost out of a thick steam-plume, the watchman chalks a small four-winged mark beside the damper handle below so no one lights that flue until the gliders leave at dawn. Now and then, an old glider loses a thermal or takes a sudden gust off the western crag and drifts down onto a flat iron roof, dead and dry as a paper lantern, its slender grey ribs weighing no more than a handful of dry twigs. The people do not throw them away. They carefully detach the translucent, mica-bright wing membranes and stretch them in small wooden frames inside their kitchen louvers. The oiled vellum reacts to moisture long before human skin can feel it—tightening drum-taut when the parched sky clears, slackening with a faint papery sigh hours before a heavy bank of mist heaves upward out of the cloud-floor below.

And deepest of all, in the warmest drainage sumps beneath the condenser blocks, there is the colorless film coating the submerged lead.

A visitor passing through sees only an oily precipitate, smelling faintly of pennies, motionless and dead. The oldest, dimmest-eyed current-readers know better. Once a week, one of them climbs slowly down the sweating iron ladders with a hooded oil lamp and a pair of clean glass slips. They crouch beside the dark tepid water, holding the flame low so it skims the surface at an oblique angle.

In that slanted light, the colorless slime is not smooth at all.

Over weeks—far too slowly for any eye to watch it happen—the film gathers itself into faint, milky ridges, forming tiny, delicate hexagons across the submerged lead plates. The old tenders do not pretend to know whether the slime is alive in the way a yeast-mother is alive, or whether it is simply a strange mineral sympathy responding to the stray currents leaking up out of the hot cracks below. They only know that it listens. When a deep grounding cable shifts out of true miles beneath the city, or when a pocket of raw sulfur interferes with the electrical loom in the plastic stone, the milky hexagons in the sump-film go ragged and cloudy three months before any rising basalt dome shows a flaw on the floor above. The elders dip their glass slips, study the faint oily patterns by lamplight, climb slowly back up into the middle levels, and adjust a copper resistance bar by the width of a fingernail.

---

For all their glass stills and careful habits, the mountain eventually gets into everyone who lives here.

You see it in the elders who spend their afternoons sitting on the sun-warmed stone benches in the wind-sheltered alcoves of the middle terraces. The thin air and the slow accumulation of silica and salts leave them pared down, dry-skinned, and light-boned, with slightly enlarged knuckles and fine pale dust caught permanently in the deep creases of their palms. Their voices thin out into a dry whisper, like fine gypsum shifting inside a stoneware jar. They do not stop working; they simply move up to tasks that ask for even less breath and even more patience—scraping salt from glass stills, stamping tin ledgers, tapping rising stone to judge its ring, or sitting beside the saucers of oil while the younger ones turn the great foundation collars below.

When an elder’s breath finally grows too narrow to draw, there is no earth in the basin to dig a grave, and no wood to build a pyre. Nor would anyone think of sending a lifelong grower of stone down the wire hoists into the damp, alien wool of the lowlands.

Instead, the family washes them one last time with clear distilled water and dresses them in their own oldest linen coat—the one that has spent forty years drinking the basin’s salts until it stands stiff and pale as sculpted clay. They carry them slowly down the bolted stair-streets to the oldest, quietest part of the city: the deep vaulted niches tucked beneath the great outer retaining piers, where the earliest zinc condenser curtains hang in permanent shadow and the warm, mineral-laden steam drips in a steady, measured thread from the dark overhead rock.

They set the wrapped figure upright in a narrow alcove chipped into the young basalt, chalk a single line of name and years onto a tin plate hung beside the niche, and leave them to the water.

Over the first season, the mineral drip glosses the stiffened linen with a faint, glassy sheen. Over five years, hollow needles of white gypsum and translucent silica build across the folds of the coat, softening the shoulders, sealing the brim of the tin hat to the collar, fusing the boots cleanly to the rising basalt floor. Within a decade, the alcove holds a pale, crystalline pillar that catches the dim lamplight like frosted glass—no longer quite a body, not quite ordinary rock, quietly sharing the weight of the overhead viaducts as the mountain inches upward beneath them all.

---

Late in the afternoon, when the western crag throws its sharp, surgical edge of shadow across the basin, the blackened iron stretch-beams along the upper ridges begin their silent evening retreat. Across three miles of packed brick and copper, the counterweights settle. Every ten minutes, a distant iron pawl drops home with a clean, dry *tock*.

Along the sunlit eastern terraces, solitary figures in tin-brimmed hats pause beside their valves and resistance coils, checking the last chalk tallies of the day before the light fails. High above them, a four-winged shadow banks once against the hard blue zenith, flashes like mica, and glides down toward an unlit chimney mouth. Below, in the sweating dark, a stoneware-plated rasper finishes an hour’s crawl across a damp flange, and the colorless film in the sumps holds its faint, patient geometry in the warm water.

Inside the middle-level walls, doors shut softly on felt gaskets. A horn mallet raps twice on a copper main—the evening mark—and down through the quiet honeycomb of alleys and bolted stairs, the city answers with the gentle clink of ceramic cups, the rustle of salt-stiffened linen being hung on pegs, and the slow, deliberate breathing of a people who have learned how to ride a rising mountain into the sky, millimeter by patient millimeter.
