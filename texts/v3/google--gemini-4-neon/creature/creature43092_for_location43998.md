Even after your eyes have adjusted to the glare and broken the stillness apart into skaters, gliders, and schools of silver nerve-cords, it takes longer to notice the people.

That is partly because they have stopped trying to be tall, just like the stone. And partly because, at a glance, they belong so thoroughly to the basin’s worn-down palette that the eye slides right past them as another pale root-knot, another smooth knuckle of bone-colored artifact, another hunched congregation of waders.

You catch them first by a mistake in the rhythm of the light.

Across the flats, the needle-legged water skaters wink in frantic, twitchy static—quick sparks of hunger and alarm. Then, from the shadow of a bruised-jade hummock, a flash answers that is too steady, too deliberate: a clean, white flare held for half a heartbeat, dipped, split into two sharp pulses, and cut off. A hundred paces away, near the weeping grey ribs, a second flare answers in the same unhurried cadence.

Follow the spark back to its source, and the pale curve resting at the edge of the shallows shifts, uncurls, and rises to knee-height.

There are never many of them—perhaps two dozen in the whole saucer, no more than the cool ribs can comfort at noon and the warm shallows can feed. They are roughly human in scale, though built lower and broader through the hips and ribcage, with a heavy, settled grace that barely dimples the thickened water when they wade. Ages in this smooth-rimmed cup seem to have weathered their bodies the way warm rain weathers limestone. There are no sharp edges to them: shoulders slope softly, joints are round and padded, and their entirely hairless skin has the wet, pale-chalk sheen of old porcelain—slick with a natural waxy film that sheds the relentless humidity and throws back the worst of the white sky. Along the rims of their low, folded ears, the backs of their wrists, and the thin webbing stretched between their long fingers, the flesh thins slightly, catching the sun with a faint rose translucence like the margins of the dissolving artifacts.

In the fierce noon glare, their eyes look blind—smooth ovals of milky, opalescent white. Those are only their outer lids, tough translucent shields that cut the tin-bright courtyard down into clean silvers and cool greys so they can walk the open water without squinting. Only when they duck into the tight green shadows under the hummocks, or when the hourly rain breaks the glare, do those pale veils slide back to reveal wide, dark, velvety irises built for soft light.

Beneath their collarbones and along the pale, slightly sunken hollows of their throats and ribs lie smooth patches of skin fine as inner-shell membrane. In a basin that swallows sound and exhales it as heat, ears are clumsy tools; they have grown into listeners of warmth. Stand near one when a branch drops on the far side of the basin, and you will see them tilt their chest toward the muted *thup*—not hearing the splash, but feeling the tiny, drifting bloom of heat that rolls across the water in its wake. They can tell the weight of a four-winged hunter’s strike, the clumsy flop of a tabletop swimmer, or the uneven step of a child from the temperature of the air brushing their necks.

Watch them move across the bright flats for ten minutes, and you realize the silence is crowded with talk.

Bound to the backs of their wrists, threaded through narrow cords around their knuckles, and hung at the hollows of their shoulders are small, polished mirrors—chips of pearlescent mollusk shell, cured amber plates from dead water skaters, and pale slivers scavenged from the thinning edges of the ancient machinery. With tiny turns of a wrist or angles of a shoulder, they catch the brutal sun and throw crisp sparks across the basin to one another. Two figures wading on opposite errands will trade whole conversations across fifty yards of blinding water without slowing their stride—teasing, warning, asking who has checked the traps under the east hummock, pointing out a sleek predator slipping toward the bone dome long before the skaters’ panic-ripple even settles.

When they stand closer—especially along the faint, straight ghosts of the sunken conduits—their language grows deeper and stranger.

Near the buried channels, shadows lag a sliver of a second behind the things that cast them. The basin’s people treat this looseness in the world as grammar. Holding a palm out above the sunlit water, a speaker moves their fingers through a fluid string of shapes; the lagging shadow trails just behind on the bright surface, overlapping the living hand so that two signs occupy the same inch of water at once—what was just said lingering beside what is being said now. A mother can soften a scolding by letting a gentle gesture linger in shadow beneath a sharp correction; an elder telling a story weaves past and present into a single shimmering chord of hands. To express certainty, a speaker holds their hand dead still until the shadow finally catches up and agrees.

Yet they are not mute. They have broad lungs, strong diaphragms, and deep, resonant throats. They simply live in a place where voice has heat and weight.

Any sound made openly here is eaten by the floor before it travels a span, returning instantly as warmth and damp vapor. Children learn this the hard way. A toddler who trips in the ankle-deep water and opens their mouth to wail makes no cry past their own chin—only a faint, muffled *hup* and a sudden, stinging puff of hot mist right against their own cheeks and throat. Startled by the scald, they gasp and cry harder, raising a miniature fog-bank around their own face until an older sibling or parent splashes quietly over, scoops them up, presses a cool hand wet from the weeping ribs gently over their mouth, and holds them until their ribs stop heaving.

Adult quarrels are quieter still, and harder to watch. Two figures will stand chest to chest in the shadow of a hummock, throats corded, mouths working in fierce, unheard shouts. Ten paces away, the basin stays wrapped in its sunlit hush—yet the air between their faces shimmers and fogs, beads of sweat bursting along their brows as they scald each other with the thermal backsplash of their own swallowed anger. When they finally break apart, both are red-cheeked and dripping, steaming faintly in the bright air, wading away to cool their faces against the matte-grey ribs.

Because sound becomes heat, voice is their fire.

There is no dry wood in this flooded saucer, nothing willing to take a flame. So when it is time to cook, or to work clay and shell, they sing.

Toward late morning, four or five of them will gather in the open shallows over a flat stretch of submerged stone where the buried machinery lies close beneath the porcelain grazers. In the center of their ring sits a wide, pearlescent mollusk shell filled with sliced root-hearts, waxy bruised-jade stalks, and clean meat from a morning’s catch. They kneel knee-deep in the clear water, lean forward until their chins nearly brush the elastic surface, and pour a deep, steady chord straight downward into the floor.

To an outsider watching from the rim, the scene is uncanny in its quiet. No note escapes the circle. The water skaters thirty feet away do not even pause. Only the singers’ broad chests rise and fall in slow unison, their throats vibrating with a low, wordless drone that never reaches the air. Beneath them, the ancient silt drinks the sound greedily and exhales it straight back up. Inside the ring, the shallow water begins to tremble, then cloud with tiny silver beads, then lift into a neat, soundless boil around the shell. Steam rises in a thick, savory pillar straight between their bowed heads.

They sing their meals hot. They sing wet coils of silt and ground nacre until the minerals fuse into pale, smooth bowls and scraping blades. A single woman, kneeling alone with her lips held close above a cupped shell, can hum a broth to the exact warmth of milk just by reading the heat blooming back against her cheeks.

And when one of them dies—usually slowly, after many quiet decades spent thinning and growing paler beside the cool ribs, eating less and resting longer in the bright stillness—the entire basin gathers. They lay the body on a bare stretch of submerged conduit, kneel shoulder to shoulder in a tight ring, and give up all the sound they have kept locked in their chests for years. Deep belly-chords, ragged wails, long overlapping drones of grief. Not a whisper reaches the rim. Instead, the water around the dead turns white with furious, silent boiling; a tall, ghostly column of steam climbs straight into the bleached sky, thick enough to bend the glare across the whole saucer. Often it tips the humid air past its limit and brings the rain down early. They do not bury their dead in a floor that crawls with grazers; they sing them up into the weather and wash the clean, pale bones afterward in the weeping runoff of the ribs.

In the bright hours between meals and rain, they live not as priests of the ancient artifacts, nor as scavengers, but as quiet housekeepers of worn-out reflexes.

They build nothing tall—only low, woven hammocks and cupped nests slung beneath the drooping, waxy canopies of the hummocks, knotted from pale aerial roots and the toughened, shed wing-membranes of papery gliders. They sleep there just above the water, sharing the cool undersides of the leaves with clinging clouds of gliders and color-shifting climbers.

Every day, a few of them wade out along the ghost-lines of the buried conduits with smooth shell scrapers, gently peeling away mats of translucent polyps and creeping roots so the channels stay clear and the world’s mild looseness stays predictable. They do not know what the conduits were built to carry, only that when a channel clogs, the water around it grows sluggish and the glare turns harsh and unkind.

At the bone-colored dome, they sit comfortably among the spindly, long-necked waders, leaning their smooth backs against the warm curve so the upward-creeping film of water slides *up* their spines against gravity, lifting away grit and dead skin toward the trembling convex lens at the crown. Sometimes one of the older ones climbs to the dome’s shoulder and peers down through that hovering lens of water—using its strange curvature to inspect the faintest seams of the buried conduits beneath the silt, or tilting their head to watch the warped reflection of the sky, reading the exact minute the vapor will thicken into the next shower.

Near the conduits, they harvest the perfect water spheres that bead and skitter across the surface without merging. Using their slightly webbed, oil-slicked fingers, they can lift one of those elastic globes straight off the skin of the water without breaking it. With gentle prodding, they slip things inside—a drop of cool condensation from the ribs, a tiny glowing nerve-cord from a dead swimmer, a rare pale seed—and carry the trembling bead back to a hummock like a drop of blown glass. Children trade them in the shade, rolling them across their palms and watching the trapped light refract.

Even their hunting belongs to the basin’s strange physics. They have no grudge against the sleek, low-slung predators that prowl the flats; the saucer is too small for wars. When the skaters’ mirrors flash a basin-wide alarm, the people simply step up onto a root-shelf and let the hunter pass. Yet when they go after the wide, toothless tabletop swimmers that rise from the silt, or spear the ribbon-thin fish darting between islands, they use the conduits the same way the predators do. A hunter wades smoothly along a buried line, pearl-tipped spear poised low, watching the shadow-lag until the air loosens by a fraction of an inch. For a blink, their striking arm smears sideways like grease on glass—occupying two dry inches at once—and the spear closes the gap before the swimmer’s shadow has even begun to bolt.

Then the white glare peaks, the far rim wobbles behind rising curtains of vapor, and the basin reaches the turn of its tight, restless clock.

Overhead, the humid air gives way. Fat, warm drops fall straight down out of the bright sky, dimpling the water into a million silver craters and raising that muted, velvety sigh—the only true sound the basin ever permits.

The change in the people is immediate, and deeply tender.

The instant the glare breaks, the mirror-sparks die across the flats. Long-distance talk ceases. All around the saucer, standing knee-deep in the churned shallows or tucked beneath the dripping canopies of the hummocks, the pale, milky outer lids slide quietly back. For ten minutes, their wide, dark rain-eyes open to the world. Under the softened sky, you see them really *look* at one another—taking in the true colors the glare bleaches away: the bruised jade of the leaves, the iridescent burst of spawning amphibians hopping around their shins, the warm flush of their own smooth skin.

The hourly shower is their twilight, their hearth-hour, repeated eight or ten times a day.

They drop their scrapers and spears and gather in small, close clusters—sitting on the soaked root-tangles, or kneeling shoulder to shoulder right out in the warm downpour, letting the rain hammer silently against their backs. Because the rain is loud enough to occupy the basin’s appetite—and because they want no heat between them—this is when they use their voices without burning. They press forehead to forehead, or rest their lips lightly against the thin thermal skin of a partner’s collarbone, a child’s temple, a friend’s cheek, and hum softly directly into bone.

It is an intimate, private kind of speech that never touches the air or the floor. A quiet thrumming travels straight from skull to skull, rib to rib—too soft to raise a puff of steam, audible only to the person pressed against you. In those ten minutes of velvety rain, names are given, old stories are passed down, hands are held, and arguments that scalded the air an hour earlier are cooled and forgiven. You see two figures sitting knee-deep near the weeping ribs, arms looped loosely around each other’s wet shoulders, foreheads touching, dark eyes half-closed while iridescent frogs leap across their knees and the wide tabletop swimmers cruise blindly past their ankles.

Then, cleanly as a curtain lifting, the rain stops.

The white glare slams back down onto the water. Steam rises in slow, pale sheets from the hummocks and from the people’s own glistening shoulders. The velvety sigh sinks back into the deep, dry hush, and the water flattens into polished tin.

One by one, the milky lids glide smoothly back over the dark eyes. Foreheads part. They rise from the wet roots, adjust the little nacre mirrors knotted to their wrists, and wade back out into the shining courtyard, their footprints erased behind them by the shifting tapestry of porcelain grazers.

If they ever knew who laid the conduits or raised the weeping ribs—if they are the last weathered descendants of those builders, or merely wanderers who slipped over the low lip ages ago and let the basin grind their sharp edges away—none of them keep track of it now. They have no wish to climb the soap-worn rim. Out beyond the bowl, sound runs away wildly into the sky where you cannot hold it, shadows never wait behind a hand to finish a thought, and water only knows how to fall down.

Across the bright expanse, beside the bone-colored dome, a pale figure pauses ankle-deep in the glare and turns a wrist toward the sun. A clean, deliberate double-spark leaps across the silent water. Near the cool grey ribs, another wrist tilts in answer—one crisp flash, warm and unhurried—and then the blinding quiet closes smoothly over them again.
