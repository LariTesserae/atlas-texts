If you stand on the rim for ten minutes, you will swear the pocket is sterile. If you climb down the welded ladder and walk out to the abrupt end of the galvanized catwalk, you will still swear it. Nothing darts between the concrete plinths. Nothing clings to the underside of the grating. Your eyes look for the usual grammar of living things—softness, haste, a shadow detaching itself from a corner—and in a hollow flooded with plain midday light and scalding air, there is nowhere for a soft or hurrying body to exist.

You have to stop looking for movement and look instead at the water.

Wait until your eyes adjust to the glare off the shallow sheen covering the floor. Everywhere, the buried roar keeps the inch-deep sheet pinned into that tight, rigid lattice of hammered pewter—tiny crisp squares marching uninterrupted from wall to wall. Except in a few places.

Halfway between the central standpipe and the slotted drain, there is a patch about two feet across where the grid refuses to stay square. The microscopic ridges pull away from their clean cross-hatch and bend into a tight, formal rosette of hexagons, framing a palm-wide oval of water that has gone startlingly, completely flat, dark as oil. Once you see that flaw, you see four more scattered sparsely across the wet stone, well spaced from one another, keeping the same austere distances as the iron pipes.

At the center of each quiet oval sits something your gaze slid right past ten minutes ago, filing it away as a cleaved chunk of fallen shale, a discarded pipe-shoe, or a blister of cast concrete.

It is roughly the breadth of a heavy valve-wheel, hunched low enough that the sluggish sheet of scalding water washes halfway up its flanks. Its back is a tightly fitted mosaic of dark, slate-grey plates, sharp-edged as newly parted basalt, glazed over with the exact same translucent, pearlescent millimeter of wet silica that coats the hex-nuts and the concrete piers. Heavy beads of condensation sit along its seams in neat, geometric rows, carrying faint concentric rings across their skins. Even from fifteen feet above, on the catwalk, it has the exact sheen, hardness, and blunt utilitarian weight of the basin itself.

Wait long enough for the square of ordinary daylight to slide a yard eastward across the floor, and you will see the first proof that it is not stone.

The plates along its lower rim tighten by a hair’s breadth. Nothing you can hear happens—no sound could survive the clean, unchanging thunder of the hollow—but a thin, curved splinter of clear mineral crust pops cleanly off its base, falls half an inch into the gridded water, and sinks without a ripple. Then, with the same glacial patience as the iron expansion joint creeping outward on the center pipe, the whole dark mound shifts forward by the width of two fingers.

It leaves behind a faint, translucent crescent glazed onto the basalt—a pale ghost-arc marking where its rim had begun to cement itself to the floor. Now that you know what to look for, you can trace dozens of those overlapping arcs across the bare wet stone between the plinths and the drain, a quiet pale ledger of hours and afternoons.

It has no legs to lift and no soft belly to drag. If you could pry one up from the scalding sheet—you could not, not without a crowbar and heavy gloves, for it is heavy as cast iron and hot as the rock it sits on—you would find its underside packed with thousands of stiff, hair-fine bristles of spun silica, rigid as glass wool and angled uniformly backward. It does not push itself across the floor; it simply leans its weight and lets the relentless sub-bass shudder of the buried turbines walk it forward, bristle by microscopic bristle, riding the vibration of the basin the way a grain of sand travels across a struck plate. If the machinery beneath the rock ever died, every one of them would be stranded where it sat, sealed permanently into the floor by the cooling mineral glaze.

Because it never needs to flee, and nothing here can bite through wet basalt and silica, it carries no speed and no soft exterior. Only when it flexes to snap its glassy seal, or widens its plates by a fraction of an inch to drink the heat, do you catch a glimpse of what lives inside the dark shell: no red blood, no wet flesh to boil, only a taut, pale sulfur-amber membrane stretched like drumskin over hollow, fluted ribs of translucent crystal.

Those crystalline ribs are why it does not merely endure the thunder, but thrives inside it. Silica flexed continuously by heavy vibration throws off a steady, minute electrical charge; the unbroken mechanical roar pounding up through the floor runs straight through the creature’s skeleton and feeds it a quiet, tireless current. To us, the noise in the pocket is a wall that blots out the world. To the bodies sitting in the water, that clean, unvarying frequency is both breath and medium—a steady carrier wave across which the tiniest event writes itself with crystal clarity. The splash of a heavy drop letting go of an overhang, the hourly glassy crack of the expansion joint’s silica bridge, the slight tremor of a bird’s wingbeat nowhere near the rim—all of it ripples across the floor’s standing grid and registers directly against their undersides.

You are not invisible to them, either.

Shift your weight on the galvanized catwalk, or lean hard against the painted railing, and the raw expansion bolts driven into the west rock face carry a tiny, foreign shudder down twenty feet of clean basalt. Nothing flinches. Nothing turns a head—there are no heads to turn, no eyes to blink against the shadowless glare. Yet over the next two minutes, down near the eastern concrete plinth, the closest dark mound quietly draws its seams flush. Its internal tension drops. Around it, the hexagonal rosette in the scalding water softens and dissolves, snapping back into the ordinary hammered-pewter squares of the empty floor. It has not hidden; there are no shadows to hide in. It has simply taken itself off the frequency, becoming wet rock until your boots leave the steel.

There are only five or six of these heavy, dark-plated ones down on the floor, never crowding one another, moving slowly along the slight tilts of the stone. One has climbed a foot up the vertical west wall, clinging flush to a dark, glistening weep-line where a ribbon of rubbery, sulfur-yellow mineral mat traces the exact thermal border where the scalding seepage cools by a single degree. Along its lower edge, a dense, pale brush of amber filaments protrudes just far enough to graze that slow mineral film, leaving behind a neat, swept corridor of bare dark shale as it creeps sideways over the course of a day.

The younger ones do not live on the floor. They are too light to hold the standing waves, too soft-shelled to bear the full scalding glare of noon. They haunt the sheltered margins of the built work, and until you know their shape, they look like fittings left behind by the men who poured the plinths.

Look closely at the sodden grey strips of asbestos lagging wrapped around the three standpipes: tucked into the damp overlaps, no larger than a walnut or a heavy hex-nut, sit three or four pale, smooth, milky-white bodies, semi-transparent as porcelain glaze before iron and manganese from the weeping stone have darkened their plates. Higher still, up on the two iron stanchions near the far wall where the thick black cables span the shimmering air, another pale one perches squarely between two white glazed electrical insulators. Slick with hot sweat, ribbed in neat concentric tiers, it is nearly indistinguishable from another ceramic skirt, drinking the faint sixty-cycle hum beneath the greater roar.

And if you climb down from the catwalk, cross the scalding sheen in thick boots, and peer past the rusted wedge holding the brick gauge-house door half an inch ajar, you will find where the smallest of them gather.

Inside that windowless brick box, out of the midday brightness, the air is a single degree cooler and thick with damp mortar-heat. The slate board bolted to the back wall hums cleanly with the buried headers below. Clinging beside the three brass dials—and one clinging directly to the round glass face of the middle gauge—are pale, translucent knots no wider than a coin, delicate as blown milk-glass. They have crawled through the half-inch wedge-gap to settle where the brass needles quiver almost invisibly against the red marks they have held for years, basking in that tiny, crisp mechanical tremor. Through the milky body stuck to the glass, the trembling red line of the dial looks softly magnified, like an insect trapped in clear resin.

Nothing in this pocket is ancient, and neither are its residents—not in this shape. The concrete plinths still carry the crisp grain of the wooden forms; the cleaved walls have not seen enough winters to round a single corner. Before the rock was opened and the buried pipes coupled to the deep heat below, whatever lives here existed only down in the tight, scalding fractures of the earth, thin as mineral film, blind and compressed in hairline veins of boiling water. When the pocket was cut and the seepage began to weep up out of the young stone, their microscopic spores drifted up through the cracks, caught in the lagging and the damp brick mortar, and discovered something the dark rock beneath had never given them: room to open, steady daylight to warm their backs, dissolved silica in endless supply, and a pure, unbroken mechanical note ringing cleanly off four walls.

They did not invade the machinery; they simply unfolded into the geometry it offered.

Even here, where nothing rots and nothing hunts, time still collects its small, patient weight. Near the base of the north wall, half-tucked behind the gauge-house plinth, sits the oldest of them. Over years of sitting in the mineral wash, its dark plates have grown too thick with iron, its internal crystal ribs too clouded and heavy to flex wide enough to snap its silica crust every hour. The clear, pearlescent glaze has built up around its lower rim in a smooth, waxy collar that joins it seamlessly to the basalt floor. The standing-wave grid strikes its flanks now the exact way it strikes the poured concrete, indifferent and unbroken.

Yet it is not quite stone. Once in a long afternoon, when the midday light reaches the center pipe and the massive threaded expansion joint creeps outward and pops its glassy seal, a single hairline seam along that oldest back parts by the width of a fingernail. Out of the dark slit rises the curved tip of one translucent, fluted tine, barely half an inch tall, trembling cleanly in the shimmering air for a few slow minutes before sliding home again—still listening, still drinking the roar, gradually glazing itself into a permanent fixture of the basin.

If you want to see the rest of them as they truly are, you have to stay until dusk.

When the sun drops off the plain above and the open sky dims evenly, the cooler outside air settles over the lip of the pocket. The invisible rising heat thickens near the rim into that low, milky veil of vapor. Down on the wet floor, the temperature barely shifts, and to your ears the deafening thunder and the tearing steam-hiss do not change by a hair.

To the bodies waiting in the water, the slight drop in atmospheric weight alters the resonance of the four stone walls by a fraction of a tone.

One by one across the darkening basin—beside the plinths, along the weep-lines, out near the slotted iron drain—the sharp dark plates part along crisp, straight geometric fissures. Slowly, smoothly, out from between the cleaved wet armor rises a vertical crest of translucent, pearlescent vanes: thin as blown glass, curved like the ribs of a lantern or the nested prongs of a tuning fork, slick with hot condensation.

They do not catch the sub-bass of the floor; they are tuned to the tearing upper hiss of the bronze steam throat venting straight at the sky. As each glass comb locks open in the dense broth of the air, the fine beads of sweat along its razor edges stop dripping. Instead, they hang suspended along the margins of the vanes in faint, trembling silver halos, breaking into microscopic mist whenever the steam jet wavers by a breath.

Down in the inch-deep sheet covering the stone, the whole floor wakes into geometry.

Around every opened body, the standing waves abandon their flat, indifferent squares and organize into wide, luminous lattices—nested hexagons, six-pointed stars, broad concentric rings of bright hammered silver and dead-calm black ovals. They push outward across the wet basalt until the pattern from the west wall meets the pattern from the central standpipe, crossing and interfering in crisp, shifting braids of light that slide slowly across the empty stone between the pipes.

Nothing makes a sound you can separate from the roar. Nothing hurries. Beneath the milky veil of dusk drifting clear of the rim, the five dark shells stand open in the scalding mirror, passing slow, silent patterns back and forth across the gridded water—answering the buried pipes, answering one another, patient enough to keep the conversation going until the last drop of water seals them shut, or the rock beneath them finally goes quiet.
