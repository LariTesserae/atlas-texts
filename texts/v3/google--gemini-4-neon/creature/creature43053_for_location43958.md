You can walk the basin for half a day and believe that nothing lives here except the grazers, the snails, and the high, silent gliders. The valley invites that mistake. Its surfaces are so bright and clean, its distances so bare, that your eyes assume anything large enough to matter would show itself at once.

What gives them away is not a shape, but a flaw in the noise.

Stay on one of the central terraces until the hydraulic roar has flattened into a constant pressure behind your eyes. Because there is no wind and no weather, the valley’s sound never drifts; every chimney holds its exact pitch, every falling sheet of water hammers its basin at an unvarying cadence. Then, without warning, a reedy whistle from a perforated tower a quarter-mile east drops a half-step, holds cleanly for four seconds, and snaps back. A moment later, a deeper booming from the far side of the colonnade softens—as if a curtain had been drawn across its mouth—then brightens again, twice in quick succession.

Only when you follow the shifted note with your eyes do you see the pale arm withdrawing from an oval aperture high on the tower’s flank, and realize that the narrow, glistening silhouettes you took for half-formed buttresses along the shadowed underside of a platform are watching you.

They are easy to miss while they are still. When one finally steps out from behind a silver curtain of runoff onto the bare hexagonal pavement, you understand why: they are cut from the same pale palette as the valley itself.

They stand taller than a person, perhaps seven feet when drawn fully upright, though they rarely hold themselves straight on the open plazas. They move in a long, slightly crouched glide, lean and narrow-hipped, with a deep, flattened ribcage that gives them something of the keel-like proportion of the grazers. Their skin is completely hairless and faintly slick—the color of wet river-pearl or skimmed milk in the shade, warming to pale ochre across the shoulders and back where the clear sun falls on them hour after hour. They wear no woven cloth; there is no fiber here to spin and no cold to keep out. A few carry a narrow loop of translucent sinew around the waist or shoulder, hung with a shard of snail-shell or a small curved plug of dried cartilage, and nothing else.

Watch their feet as they cross the thin gliding film of water. Their soles are broad, pale, and unusually soft, finely ribbed with shallow wet grooves that channel the runoff away cleanly, much like the microscopic grip-hairs on the plant stairs. They neither splash nor slip. Even at a dead run down a sloping spillway, they leave no scuff on the living epidermis, skimming across the bleached-jade plates with an almost hydroplaning economy.

Up close, their faces are long and quiet, built for a world of wet glare and deafening sound. Their eyes are large, dark amber, and nearly always shielded behind a pale, translucent inner lid that cuts the reflection off the flooded terraces; only when they duck beneath the shadow of an unfinished lintel do the lids draw back fully. They have no outer ears to catch the roaring air. Instead, twin narrow bands of taut, pale membrane run vertically down the sides of their throats and along the upper curve of their ribs, flush with the skin. As you watch, those strips subtly tighten and slacken—rippling like the gills of a resting fish—tuning out the broad flood of the valley’s roar to isolate a single overtone half a mile away.

What strikes you most, once you have seen more than one of them together, is how differently they carry time.

In a basin where nothing rots and nothing weathers, their skin does not wrinkle or sag. The youngest among them—lithe, impulsive creatures who chase one another across the wet spillways and lean dangerously out over the outer rim—are almost wholly soft-jointed and matte, faintly translucent at the fingertips and inner wrists, where branching vessels show through like veins in a newly unfurled leaf. You would be forgiven for thinking they had never quite finished forming, caught in the same taut, supple emergence as the arches around them.

The oldest are unmistakable, though not because they look worn down. Decades of drinking the warm, mineral-heavy exudate leave a slow deposit in their bodies that cannot be shed. Along their collarbones, brow-ridges, shins, and the outer edges of their wrists, delicate plates of clouded, pale-quartz mineral grow directly out of the bone and break cleanly through the skin, polished glass-smooth by the constant mist and falling water. In the clear sun, an elder looks partly calcified—rimmed in wet alabaster, moving more deliberately than the young, carrying the weight of the valley’s dissolved salts inside their own frame.

---

They have no villages, no huts, no hearths. Nothing in the basin will accept a post or a nail, and there is no wood, stone, or clay to stack. Even if there were, the warmth here never breaks and nothing needs to be cooked.

Instead, they live inside the sparse geometry as naturally as water lives in the drainage channels. When they rest, they wedge themselves into the dry, windless hollows at the bases of the widest acoustic chimneys—where the torrents sheet down the outer flutes rather than the interior—or lie along the shaded ledges beneath the cantilevered platforms. Often you will find three or four of them floating face-up in the shallow, lukewarm rectilinear basins, submerged to the cheekbones with only their noses and upper throats clear of the water, letting the steady low-frequency thrum of the floor rock them into a shallow sleep that never fully stops listening.

Their appetite is as edited as the rest of the valley’s life. Most of what sustains them pours ceaselessly from the architecture itself, though they do not drink indiscriminately. To a stranger, the runoff tastes faintly sweet and clean everywhere; to them, every scattered pavilion has its own vintage. The water sheeting off the tall vascular chimneys is thin and sharp with dissolved salts. The heavy, metronomic drops swelling at the glossy green growth-tips of the half-arches are denser, richer in sugars, almost syrupy on the tongue. They move across the miles of pavement along slow, unhurried circuits, pausing beneath particular lintels to tilt their faces up into a known stream, or cupping their palms beneath a swollen bud to gather a mouthful before it falls.

For denser nourishment, they pry the small quartz snails from the misty undersides of arches—working carefully with the flat of a thumb so they never nick the waxy plant-skin—and crack the shells cleanly against a mineralized knuckle. The thicker shards of shell are kept; they are among the only hard edges the valley offers.

And once in a long while, when a pale-ochre grazer finally folds its long legs beside a spillway and does not rise, or when one of the silent parchment gliders weakens on the updrafts and spirals down onto a sunlit terrace, they converge from across the basin with startling speed. In a place without scavengers or decay, a body does not break down into soil; left alone, it would merely slide with the runoff toward the spillways, fouling the clean bright lines until it tumbled off the rim. They strip it before an hour has passed. The flesh is eaten raw and rinsed in the warm currents; the long tendons are pulled clean and braided; the broad wing-membranes are carried to the dry, sun-warmed lip at the very edge of the basin and weighted with cartilage rings until they cure into stiff, pale sheets. Nothing is wasted, and nothing is left behind to stain the pavement.

---

If you stay among them for even a single afternoon, you begin to realize that the deafening valley is not an obstacle to their speech—it is their voice.

Shouting into the roar is useless beyond arm’s length. They do not try. Instead, they speak across miles of gleaming emptiness by bending the valley’s permanent chord.

Climb partway up one of the hollow vascular chimneys and watch a pair of them at work. One braces their grooved soles against the damp interior wall and lays a broad palm across an oval vent, partially choking the compressed air escaping from the falling torrent inside. Instantly the tower’s reedy whistle drops a clean interval. A flick of the fingers splits it into a quick staccato trill. Far away across the bright expanse, another figure steps into a silver curtain pouring down a fluted wall and holds a cured sheet of glider-skin edgewise into the stream, dividing the sheet in two and changing the hollow drumming in the basin below from a steady roll to a double beat.

They converse by subtraction and interference. Every hollow column, every spillway, every drum-tight leaf-basin is a key on a miles-wide instrument whose baseline note never stops sounding. To your ears, it takes hours before you can even detect the tiny shifts in overtone and rhythm; to them, with their listening-membranes tuned and their bare soles reading the standing waves through the floor, the entire valley is loud with news. A young one perched on a distant colonnade will intentionally throw a high chimney out of tune just to draw an irritated, low-pitched booming answer from an elder half a mile west. Warnings, invitations to a newly fallen glider, idle banter, and long, braided songs of shifting harmonics travel effortlessly from rim to rim without a single throat ever shouting into the air.

When they wish to speak privately, they do the opposite: they close the distance entirely.

Two of them will step together in the open glare of a plaza—completely exposed, surrounded by the bright acoustic flood—and press the flats of their forearms, or their foreheads, or the smooth ridges of their collarbones directly against one another. Beneath their sternums sits a small, air-filled resonance chamber, a quiet internal echo of the hollow chimneys. They hum directly through bone and water, a rapid, intricate pattern of pitched tremors that never enters the roaring air at all. If one ever trusts you enough to lay their bare wrist against your collarbone, you feel it at once: not words, not quite music, but a warm, lucid pulsing that travels straight into your jaw and inner ear, astonishingly intimate inside the public din of the valley.

And beneath both of those languages lies the floor itself. Lying flat on the warm hexagons, cheek pressed to the gliding film of water, they can read the whole basin like a taut hide. They feel the faint pulse-change when a group crosses a terrace three terraces down; they know when one of the high chimes shifts by the vibration in their teeth. Only the pale-ochre grazers escape the floor’s notice, their perforated cartilage feet passing through the water without splash or thud—which is why the dwellers always give the grazers a wide, slightly wary berth, treating them as silent ghosts that drift through an otherwise completely legible world.

---

Yet the thing that stays with you longest is not how they speak, but how they write.

Remember what happens when you drag a sharp edge across the living architecture: a pale groove opens, clear fluid beads instantly along the score, and within an hour the valley’s stubborn stasis erases the mark completely, restoring the waxy surface to its exact prior contour without scar or callus.

To them, this is not a frustration. It is a slate, a clock, and an art.

Walk along the low parapets of the central terraces and look closely at the glistening pale jade. Now and then you will catch sight of fresh, razor-fine incisions filled with bright beads of sap, catching the clear sunlight like lines of spun glass. They make them with shards of quartz snail-shell, or—among the elders—with the honed mineral edge of their own wrists.

Because every line closes in roughly sixty minutes, nothing they write is ever meant to last, and everything they write is alive with urgency. You will see two of them kneeling on opposite sides of a broad hexagonal plate, deep in a silent, absorbed argument: one scoring a rapid diagram of intersecting arcs and acoustic intervals, the other cutting quick amendments beside it before the opening premise swells shut and disappears. You will see young ones drawing sprawling, tangled portraits of grazers or gliders across the flat causeways, laughing in sharp bone-hums as the creature’s legs heal away behind them before they have finished the head.

They measure all durations by the closing of a groove. A shallow scratch holds for twenty minutes; a deep score pressed to the firmer cambium takes an hour. If an elder wishes to hold a complex calculation—or the exact sequence of vents needed to reproduce a rare harmonic—they cannot file it away in a library. They must return to the parapet every forty minutes and re-trace the softening line with a shell-tip, keeping the thought open against the plant’s relentless healing. Sometimes a whole group will maintain a vast, intricate diagram across three adjacent hexagons for an entire day, taking turns walking the luminous glass lines in quiet relay, until whatever question they were testing is settled—and then they step back together and watch the floor slowly seal itself into smooth, blank green.

When one of them dies, the living do not leave a stone. They score the dead one’s shape—or the geometric pattern of their favorite chord—deep into the sunlit center of a plaza, and sit in a loose ring around it with their palms flat on the humming pavement, watching the bright beaded lines draw closed over the course of an hour until nothing remains to show anyone had ever been traced there.

---

What they truly think of the valley—and of its unfinishedness—takes longer to see. They do not worship the frozen city, nor do they treat it as a ruin. To them, a ruin is something broken by time, and time has never broken a single millimeter here.

Instead, they live with a quiet, constant preoccupation with the missing space.

You notice it first in the way they move around the half-formed structures. Where an arch curves upward from opposing piers and stops two feet short of meeting, capped in its swollen translucent buds, they never walk casually through the gap as if it were empty air. They pause beneath it. They tilt their heads back to trace the unfinished curve with their eyes.

Once, on a wide sunlit terrace near the center of the basin, you may witness something that makes the hair rise on your arms.

Two of them climb the fluted piers of an incomplete arch, bracing their grooved soles against the slick, glossy margins of the embryonic tips. Carefully, with slow, balanced grace, they lean outward across the two-foot void until their extended arms meet in midair, fingertips locking cleanly where the keystone ought to have grown. They hold the pose for ten, fifteen minutes—bodies taut, trembling faintly in sympathetic vibration with the booming floor—while a third kneels on the bright wet hexagons below. On the pale pavement, for the first time in all the valley’s long stasis, the shadow of a finished arch lies whole and unbroken. The kneeling one traces the outer curve of that borrowed shadow with a snail-shell shard, cutting a bright glass line into the jade floor, and keeps tracing it as the two above slowly tire, let go, and climb back down. For the next hour, the completed arch lingers in beaded sap on the ground before the plant swallows it back.

The elders go further.

Because their calcified joints grow stiff and heavy, the oldest among them spend less time racing across spillways or climbing the high chimneys. Instead, you find them standing motionless beside the cantilevered ribs and unfinished wall-leaves, laying their broad, pale palms gently against the swollen, glossy growth-tips. They stand like that for hours, eyes half-hooded against the wet glare, listening-membranes drawn tight along their throats.

It is impossible to tell, watching them, whether they are urging the meristem to wake or soothing it to stay asleep.

They know—perhaps better than any outsider could—what the pressure beneath their soles truly is. Lying with their ribs pressed to the pavement, they feel the immense, straining force of the vascular rivers pumping upward into buds held on the hairline edge of division. They spend their lives mapping the phantom rooms the city never closed, stretching cords of braided sinew from rib to rib to see where a vault would have risen, testing with their own shadows what the plazas were meant to hold.

And yet they also know what completion would mean.

If the valley ever exhaled and finished its sprouting, the bright, open glare of the terraces would vanish under towering vaulted canopies. The raw embryonic tips would seal into mature bark and tough epidermis, choking off the sweet, tireless guttation they drink. The hollow acoustic chimneys would lengthen or close, dropping the basin into a strange, heavy quiet where their voices could no longer carry from terrace to terrace. Worst of all, the active stasis would break: mature tissue would ripen, shed, brown, and rot, filling the clean spillways with dark silt and bringing down into the valley all the crawling, softening, decaying things that have never been allowed inside.

To be finished is to begin dying. They live inside the held breath that keeps them alive, and still they cannot stop leaning across the empty air to feel the shape of the word the valley never spoke.

---

Even at the very end of a life, they obey the rules of the basin.

When one of the elders grows so heavily rimmed in pale quartz that their joints can scarcely bend and their chest-hum fades to a dry rattle, the others carry them across the wide gleaming terraces toward the outer rim, where the living pavement terminates in a clean right-angled lip above the drifting tops of clouds.

They lay the body gently along the dry, sun-warmed edge. No one shouts over the roar. One by one, the living kneel and press their foreheads or wrists briefly against the cooling collarbone, listening for the last faint resonance as the warm updrafts from the cloud-sea below brush across the hollow chest. Sometimes a younger one carefully snaps free a small, smooth flake of mineralized bone from the elder’s wrist to keep as a stylus, carrying a piece of the dead one’s slow mineral weight back into the plazas to score fresh hour-marks into the jade.

Then one of them cuts a single straight line into the waxy parapet beside the rim. They sit together in silence—surrounded by the distant, bright roar of the basin behind them and the immense quiet of the open cloud-tops ahead—watching the clear sap bead along the groove, soften, and slowly draw shut.

The moment the surface is completely smooth again, leaving no proof that anything ever rested there, they slide the pale body gently off the clean lip into the thin bright air. Far below the terrace edge, a pair of parchment gliders bank effortlessly on the warm updraft, silent and unlanding, circling wide over the gulf as the white cloud-tops receive what the valley cannot keep.

Back near the center of the basin, if you pause beside a low parapet as the steady daylight pours down across the wet green hexagons, you will no longer believe the place is empty. A quarter-mile off, a tall vascular chimney drops a clean third, holds the note, and releases it; a distant spillway answers with a double cadence, bright and deliberate inside the unvarying flood of sound. Somewhere just out of sight behind a silver curtain of water, a pale hand is resting against a smooth green wall. And right beside your elbow, if you look down at the waxy lip of the parapet, you may catch the faintest glint of a hair-thin line of beaded sap—already half-healed, already closing—disappearing smoothly into the pale, immaculate skin before you can make out what it was trying to draw.
