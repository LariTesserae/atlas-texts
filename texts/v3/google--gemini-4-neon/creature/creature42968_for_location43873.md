If you stay in the valley past the blinding hours of noon—if you can bear the numbing air long enough to watch the high glare soften and the shadows lengthen into ink—you begin to realize that you have been walking over the roofs of its inhabitants all afternoon.

They do not live on the dry stone, because there is no dry stone meant for living on. They live inside the water, and inside the dark.

The first sign of them is never a silhouette against the rim. It is a small disturbance in one of the dead-still central cisterns, down where the stairs plunge straight beneath the surface of the faintly metallic, slate-blue water. As the sun drops behind the upper slate-combs and the sharp, freezing dusk settles into the alleys, a pale line rises quietly out of the depths, step by submerged step. Water does not splash off it. It sheets away in the same thin, glass-quiet ribbons that glide down the corbelled walls at midday.

At first glance, even a few paces away, it is easy to mistake one for a stack of wet, hand-dressed limestone that has somehow detached itself from a vault.

They are built for the narrow places of the bowl—long-limbed, flat-chested, and strikingly narrow across the hips and shoulders, able to slip sideways down a forearm-wide alley without scraping an elbow. Standing crouched on a stone kerb, one is roughly the height of a seated person, though its limbs are disproportionately long and angular, folding tightly at the knees and wrists when it rests. It has no hair, no clothing, no scales or shell. Its skin is smooth, taut, and damp, shifting from slick charcoal-grey wherever water clings to it to a dusty, chalky white wherever the thin mountain air dries the microscopic glaze of rock-flour on its back. Along the gaunt knuckles, the collarbones, and the insides of the long forearms, the skin thins to a cold translucency, showing the faint, sluggish shadow of slate-blue blood moving just beneath.

Their faces are spare, almost featureless until you look closely. The eyes are small, set deep beneath heavy ridges of bone, and covered by pale, milky lids that remain drawn almost shut even in twilight. The fierce silver-blue sky of midday is agony to them; a direct shaft of high-altitude sun will blister their skin and blind them within minutes. What guides them instead is touch, vibration, and a sharp, chemical sense of taste. Their mouths are wide and lipless, set low, and their nostrils are mere vertical slits that seal tight when submerged. Along the sides of their narrow ribcages lie three pairs of fine, flat flaps, nearly invisible when pressed shut against the dry air; underwater, they part slightly to expose pale, feathery fringes that draw oxygen directly from the frigid cisterns.

Because their bodies are as cold as the water they sleep in, they have no need for fire, shelter from the chill, or hurry. They move with a strange, economical deliberation—long pauses punctuated by sudden, precise shifts of weight. They rarely stand fully upright. Moving along the honeycomb of terraces, they travel low, keeping three limbs anchored to the sharp masonry edges, their long, flat-tipped fingers and splayed toes gripping wet stone with soft, finely grooved pads that leave no scuff and wear no hollow into the kerbs, even after centuries of passage.

There are not many of them—perhaps a few dozen scattered across the whole two-mile basin, rarely more than two or three within sight of one another. Yet they are the reason nothing in the valley looks old.

To understand what they are doing, you have to watch one work in the wet, grey hush before dawn, when cloud-banks spill over the upper rim and smother the bowl in fog.

A mason climbs out of the lower dark carrying a fist-sized hammerstone of dense black basalt and a flat wedge of grey clay pressed directly against its own damp chest to keep it supple. It ascends the middle terraces slowly, stopping at one pan after another to dip two long fingers past the meniscus. It is not merely checking the water level. Its ridged fingertips can read the microscopic grit suspended in the pool, the slickness of the stone floor, the faint mineral poverty of water that has passed too cleanly over weathered rock.

Where a pan runs too clear, the mason crouches beside the lip, an inch above the waterline, and begins to tap the dry limestone with its hammerstone.

*Tock.*

A long pause—five seconds, ten.

*Tock.*

It is the exact sound you hear echoing out of the blind vaults at noon, easily mistaken for a heavy drop detaching from a lintel. Each measured strike raises a fresh, pale dimple on the block and drops a crisp spall of chert or a pinch of powdered limestone into the brimming water. The mason is not carving decoration, nor merely repairing frost-cracked edges. It is feeding the valley.

The colorless, slippery film clinging to the darkest, oldest seams at the base of the central vaults is not a stray smear of rot left behind in an empty ruin. It is the sole crop of this entire two-mile machine—a cold-water growth that lives without sunlight, drawing its meager strength directly from the raw, freshly fractured mineral faces of limestone, flint, and iron-veined slate. Water that sits still in weathered stone soon starves it. Only water that has crept slowly off freshly cleaved fog-combs, warmed by a degree in the noon pans, picked up the fresh rock-flour of newly bruised chisel-marks, and steeped through unmortared silt-packed joints carries the right faint metallic brew to keep the film alive.

That is why the valley looks like a quarry floor abandoned ten minutes ago. Every sharp corner, every dusty-white hammer bruise, every unrounded flake lying in the shallow pans is the residue of constant, patient milling. The masons are slowly eating the mountain, a milligram of stone-dust at a time, using two square miles of gravity, condensation, and sunlight as a stomach.

Down in the central cluster of windowless beehive vaults, where the high sun never reaches, the harvest takes place. The vaults have no doors because light is the enemy of both the film and the masons; entering a chamber would mean letting the blinding sky spill across the submerged seams. Instead, a mason kneels on the wet kerb beside one of the low square apertures at the waterline, reaches a long, slate-grey forearm straight into the pitch-black interior, and works purely by feel. With a thin blade of ground slate—curved slightly like a shallow spoon—it gently lifts ribbons of the colorless, saliva-thin film from the oldest blocks, never scraping a seam bare, always leaving a sheen behind to regrow over the coming seasons.

It draws its arm back out with a glistening, nearly invisible smear clinging to the stone scoop and presses it smoothly into its mouth. A single mouthful of that cold, tasteless mineral jelly, paired with a metabolism that slows to near-stillness in the dark water, is enough to sustain a mason for weeks.

Those hand-sized apertures serve a second purpose, one you would only witness if you lay flat on a freezing kerb in the middle of a moonless night and watched the waterline closely.

Out of the dark square holes slip the young.

They are tiny—no longer than a human hand for their first decade of life—and almost completely transparent, like scraps of pale jellyfish fitted with delicate white cartilage and oversized, dark-rimmed fingertips. The masons lay only two or three pale, rubbery eggs in a century, tucking them deep inside the warmest, thickest seams of the central vaults where the film grows heaviest. When they hatch, the young are too frail for the upper terraces and too small to lift a hammerstone. For years they haunt the flooded interiors of the vaults, grazing directly on the slippery walls, venturing out through the waterline apertures only in thick night fog to crawl along the weeping outer flanks of the towers and learn the feel of dry-laid stone. They are silent, boneless-looking little things, folding themselves back into the black square holes at the first hint of grey in the eastern sky. Only when their shoulders finally grow too wide to pass through the aperture do they leave the nursery vaults for good, descending instead down the open cistern stairs to join the slow, submerged adults.

Up near the fog-combs along the high rim, where the slate slabs stand wedged too densely for any human body to pass between the rows, the masons work together on the rare nights when heavy cloud wraps the entire valley in wet darkness. A creature with so little caloric margin cannot heave heavy rock by brute strength. Instead, three or four of them gather silently around a frost-split slab, communicating without a single vocal sound. One presses its palm flat against the foundation block and taps twice with a knuckle; the dry, crisp *click* travels instantly through the water-weighted stone into the fingertips and heels of the others.

They work by wedges, levers of dense flint, and sheer patience. Over the course of an entire freezing night, four of them may lift and seat just one freshly split plate of grey slate into its grooved channel, tilting it by fractions of an inch into the prevailing drift, packing its base with wet clay and spalled flakes until it stands locked and canted just right to drink the morning mist.

Their sense of time is almost unrecognizable. Because they live for centuries—slowed by the cold, insulated from disease and predators in their sterile high bowl—they do not fight the valley's gradual mineral drift; they converse through it. When a mason on the upper benches wedges a fitted pebble into a V-notch, or scrapes a hair-thin crust of lime from an overflow lip, it is not merely tuning the water. Three days later, when the noon thermal swell arrives an hour early or a millimeter thin at a central cistern half a mile downhill, the mason waiting in the slate-blue dark reads that change with its fingertips as clearly as a letter. Across decades, as mineral crusts slowly thicken in old notches and redirect the water's invisible paths by inches, the masons do not tear the masonry down to restore an ancient blueprint. They follow the water. They peck fresh pans beside the new lines, abandon older channels to dry pale and matte, and gradually build upward and inward, letting the valley rewrite itself like a vast, slow-growing reef of chipped stone.

Nothing leaves this closed circuit—not even the masons themselves.

Age comes to them not as frailty, but as a slow petrification. Over centuries of steeping in lime-heavy, rock-flour water, microscopic mineral crusts begin to take hold in the fine grooves of their skin, along their ribs, inside the hinges of their knees and wrists. Their pale backs turn permanently chalk-white and rough to the touch. Their movements grow stiffer, their pauses longer, until they can no longer climb the upper terraces or swing a hammerstone without cracking their own calcified joints.

When one reaches that point, it does not die out on the kerbs where its body would foul the glassy pans. It climbs one of the narrow, steep flights of steps on the flanks of the central vaults—flights that, at the time, still lead to an open, low-linteled doorway—and lowers itself into the dark, brimming water inside. There it folds its stiff limbs against the wet stone and lets its slow heart stop.

The next night, two or three others arrive with flat corbel blocks and lumps of grey clay. Working in total silence, they wall the doorway shut from the outside, fitting the unmortared seams tightly until the facade is smooth, flush, and indistinguishable from the neighboring masonry. The narrow flight of steps remains outside, climbing six or eight risers only to end abruptly against a blank wet wall.

Inside that sealed dark, nothing rots in the ordinary way. Over the next twenty or thirty years, the colorless, slippery film creeps slowly over the calcified body in the freezing water, reclaiming every last trace of salt, phosphorus, and soft tissue, carrying it invisibly through the unmortared seams in the weeping glaze that coats the tower flanks, returning the mason drop by drop to the sterile bowl until nothing remains inside the chamber except a clean, mineral-crusted skeleton resting beneath the water.

When the night thins and the pale, freezing hour after dawn arrives, the valley clears itself of movement long before the first white knife of sunlight clears the eastern rim.

Up along the fog-combs, a mason wipes the last wet clay from its knuckles and slips quietly down the terraced alleys, pausing once to tap a single dry *click* into a wall—a signal passed downhill from block to block across two miles of stone. The translucent young vanish back through the hand-sized apertures. Along the middle benches, the adults leave their basalt hammerstones tucked neatly inside submerged corners of the pans, indistinguishable from cobbles.

One by one, they descend the sunken flights of stairs into the deep, shadowed cisterns at the center of the bowl. The slate-blue water closes smoothly over their chalk-pale heads without leaving a ripple. At the bottom, tucked beneath the overhanging corbelled ledges where no noon glare can ever reach, they press their narrow backs against the dressed blocks, part the pale breathing-slits along their ribs, and let their pulses fall to a single faint beat every few minutes.

By the time the clouds burn back into hammered silver-blue and the sun pours its blinding glare across the wet masonry, the valley is once again utterly still. Thousands of stone lips wait for the midday warmth to tip their mirrors. Fresh flakes of chert glint in the sterile pans. And just beneath the glassy surface, a few feet below where any visitor might kneel to peer into the shadows, the builders lie motionless in the cold dark, listening through the stone to the slow, bright *tock* of falling water, waiting for the sky to hurt less.
