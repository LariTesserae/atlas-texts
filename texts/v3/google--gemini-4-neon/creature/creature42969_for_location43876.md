You could drift across a hundred leagues of the drowned plateaus and swear that nothing thinks here—that whatever once pressed bowls from pale clay and wedged boulders into rings must have dissolved back into the silt long before the mountains lost their teeth. Even if you passed within an arm’s length of one of them, you would almost certainly mistake it for a swell of pale bedrock under a blanket of moss, or a thick knot of holdfasts where a ribbon-kelp had twisted back upon itself.

The first sign is never a shape. It is a small disobedience in the plants.

A thicket of feathery rust-plumes, which everywhere else leans steadily toward the nearest fissure of rising heat, will turn slightly inward toward a smooth hollow beneath a kelp overhang. The water there is a shade warmer than the surrounding blood-warm gloom, and cleaner, as though something has been drinking the suspended spores in slow, unhurried draughts. If you linger—not for a minute, for minutes here mean very little, but for the span it takes a smear of jade light to fade out of the water—you may notice that the pale ridge curled inside the clay bowl is rising and falling. Once. Then stillness for so long you doubt your eyes. Then again, so gently that the golden dust resting along its collarbone lifts half an inch into the water and settles back without disturbing a single frond above.

They have no name for themselves. A name is a sharp fence drawn around a thing to keep the rest of the world out, and nothing that survives this high, warm, weathered sea keeps its fences for long.

When one finally uncurls from its seat and drifts out into the dimness between the kelp-ribbons, it does not look built for swimming the way a fish is built—there is nothing sleek, darting, or predatory about it. They are long-limbed and heavy-hipped, taller than a human would have been, yet softened at every contour until their shoulders, ribs, and knees have the worn, gentle geometry of the drowned stone around them. Their skin is the color of bleached clay stained faintly with bruised amber, slick with a thin, translucent mucilage that drinks vibration and lets the crowded bladder-vines slip across their ribs without catching. Along the thinned margins of their flanks and forearms, the flesh grows nearly translucent, showing the slow, dusky shadow of heavy bones within.

They have no true eyes—not the sharp, focused lenses of creatures that hunt or flee. Across the broad, smooth brow sits a pale, slightly recessed band of skin that drinks the thick, lingering light directly, reading a smear of tarnished amber or slate-violet the way a sleeper reads sunlight through closed lids. Beneath the jaw and down the sides of the long neck lie soft, pleated gill-frills, colored like old rust-moss, which open and close with a languid ripple, drawing in the spore-laden water and tasting every faint shift of warmth, mineral, and silt.

Their most unmistakable features are their hands.

They are extraordinarily broad and heavy, at the ends of long, unhurried arms—wide-palmed, with five thick, blunted fingers joined halfway up by supple webs of pale skin. The undersides of the palms and fingertips are richly veined and densely whorled, more sensitive than any other part of their bodies. They do not carry spears, knives, bags, or cords. There is nothing to cut here, nothing to hunt, and nothing that requires haste. Everything they have ever shaped, learned, or left behind across the vast plateaus has been made with the press of those wide palms, the weight of their bellies and thighs, and an almost geological patience.

Their legs are long and strong, tapering into broad, smooth-soled feet with prehensile toes built less for kicking through open water than for gripping the slick, rounded bedrock or hooking gently around a holdfast when an internal wave rolls across a ridge. When they travel, they move with a slow, dreaming gait—half-walking, half-drifting—pushing off a smooth dome of stone and letting a warm convection plume carry them fifty paces through the mid-water before they lazily reach out a foot to catch the next swell of rock.

Because gravity here is weather, they never fight it. When a heavy front settles into a basin, pressing the moss-mats flat, they fold their limbs and sink smoothly to the bedrock, walking along the pale contours with steady, deliberate weight, or curling into a hollow to wait it out. When weight thins to a suggestion and the silt lifts in slow vertical curtains, they unhook their toes and float sideways among the spiraling kelp, turning lazily end over end without any preference for which way is up. Once in a great while, caught in the middle of a forest as a gravity front tilts the entire column of water, one will simply hang suspended beside the sheer, trembling wall of liquid, resting a palm lightly against the edge where water meets a pocket of luminous mist, watching the world lean sideways until it slides back into place.

They know the shimmering seams in space the way an old animal knows the trails beneath a forest floor. Where a stranger might drift blindly through a refraction pane and find the valley behind them inverted overhead, they pause before the invisible shimmer—they can feel it first in their throat-pleats, a faint prickle like water that is neither warmer nor cooler than itself—and lay a flat palm against the pane. By the slight, almost elastic give against their skin, they can tell whether the seam stretches distance, folds it, or turns a ridge upside down. They use them freely, without awe or hurry. A solitary wanderer wishing to cross from a warm thermal basin to a cooler, slate-violet plateau will slip sideways into a seam between two feathered stalks, hang for a heartbeat in the strange, overlapping thinness of the turn, and emerge thirty leagues away, brushing a ribbon of loosened silt from its shoulder.

And like the oldest kelp, they bear the wear of loosened time directly on their bodies.

The young—the rare ones, still only a few decades or cycles out of their cradles—have crisp, clear edges. Their skin is a brighter, cleaner amber, free of epiphytic growth, and they move with a startling briskness that sends tiny curls of silt spinning in their wake. They are the only ones whose voices still briefly resemble sound: soft, buoyant clicks and hums deep in the throat that can actually travel six or eight paces through the mucilage-heavy water before unraveling into warmth.

As the cycles pass—and no one counts them, for there is no sun, only the twice-cycle sheds of golden seed-snow and the vast, slow turning of the overhead currents—that sharpness wears gently down. Their outlines begin to soften. Watch an older one raise a hand to part a curtain of bladder-vines, and you will see the gesture slightly smudged at its edges, the palm occupying two or three adjacent instants of its own slow arc at once, faint and overlapping like dye spreading in warm oil. They do not experience the present as a thin edge dividing what was from what will be. They inhabit it as a broad, warm shallow. Because causality here is polite and unhurried, they rarely react to anything; instead, they move alongside it. An elder will cup its hands in the dim water a heartbeat before a heavy seed-pod drifts down into them, or lean gently against a boulder moments before a mile-wide internal wave arrives to bend the forest flat. It is not foresight. It is simply that they have stopped insisting on being completely separate from the water a second ahead of them.

Nothing here is killed for food. They live on the abundance that saturates every inch of the medium—grazing on the tender, sweet-fleshed inner skins of the translucent bladder-vines, drawing in mouthfuls of the blinding golden seed-snow during the shedding seasons, and scooping up the pale, nutrient-rich mineral slurry where ancient stone softens along the margins of spatial seams. Often, they simply hang motionless in a fever-warm thermal plume with their throat-pleats flared wide, absorbing heat and microscopic spores until their limbs feel heavy with contentment.

They share the vast hush with the few other animals of the sea in an effortless, wordless courtesy. When one of the immense, eyeless sheet-grazers glides slowly over the kelp-tops, a wanderer will sometimes rise quietly beneath it, turning onto its back to let the creature’s vast, parchment-thin underside ripple gently over its chest, shoulders, and knees. The grazer drinks the excess film of velvety moss and pale calcium-crust that accumulates on older skin, leaving the wanderer faintly gleaming and sharpened in the dimness, while the wanderer yields its overgrowth without a single sudden motion that might disturb the great sheet’s drift. Down near the bedrock, they never molest the thumb-sized, spiraled grazers that creep through lines of distortion; instead, they patiently gather the empty, chalky shells left behind along the seams, tucking them into the crook of an elbow until they have a handful.

Because sound dies almost as soon as it leaves the body, they do not speak across distances. For most of their immense lives, they are alone—wandering across terraced plateaus so wide that two of them may not cross paths for thirty seed-sheds.

When two do meet, emerging unexpectedly from opposite sides of a dense curtain of ribbon-kelp, there is no startlement and no rush of greeting. They slow, hovering a few feet apart while their throat-pleats taste the long history of waters the other has passed through—the iron-sweetness of a distant thermal field, the cool, chalky tang of the high saddles, the faint resin of a drifting moss-raft. Then, if both wish to speak, they draw close until their forearms, temples, or broad shoulders nearly brush.

Their speech is warmth and pressure.

Deep inside their chests, they set up a slow, resonant vibration—not loud enough to carry as sound even two paces away, just enough to break immediately against the other’s skin as patterned tides of heat. A conversation feels like standing beside a living chimney in the dark: long swells of blood-warmth rising into sudden, brief blooms of fever along the collarbone, punctuated by subtle, deliberate pauses of coolness and tiny, shifting releases of bitter or sweet silt-scent from the throat-frills. Sometimes they lay their broad palms flat against each other’s ribs or cheeks, feeling the other’s overlapping instants blur gently into their own. In this way, over the course of an afternoon—or three days, while a slow current turns overhead—they share everything that matters: where a gravity front has shifted a meadow loose, which refraction pane has widened into a new valley, where an elder has finally settled to sleep, and how the light looked when it lingered slate-violet over the far northern shelves.

Yet most of their fellowship does not happen face to face. It happens across centuries, written patiently into the smooth stone of the world.

Those faint, palm-sized depressions worn evenly along the ledges are their letters, and their memory. When one of them feels a long stillness coming on—or has witnessed something rare, like a new thermal chimney breaking open or the migration of a continent-sized moss-raft—it settles beside a clean shelf of pale bedrock and lays its broad palm flat against the stone. It does not scrape or pound. It simply rests there, sometimes for a full tide-turn, sometimes for days, letting its body heat, the faint chemistry of its mucilage, and the slow, softening water work together at the boundary between skin and rock. Over generations, returning wanderers lay their own hands into the same shallow hollows, deepening them by fractions of a hair, and because lingering here applies to more than light, the hollows hold a faint, quiet temper of all the patience that has pressed into them. A traveler arriving fifty years later, sliding a hand into the worn print along a ledge, waits until the rock warms to match its palm—and feels, blurred and softened like dye in oil, the residue of whoever rested there before: a ghost of their weight, the angle of the current they leaned against, a calm impression of where they were going next. A row of depressions along a ridge is a chronicle that never hurries to be read.

The rings of torso-heavy stones wedged into shallow basins serve a different purpose. They are anchor-beds, built only during the rare, merciful hours when a weight-thinning front passes across a ridge. In that buoyant weather, a solitary wanderer—or two who have met by chance—can heave a smooth boulder heavy as a torso with chest and thighs, nudging it slowly into place beside another until a low circle takes shape in a hollow. When heavy gravity returns, the ring holds firm against the rolling internal waves. They return to these rings to shed old layers of mucilage, to ride out violent gravity tilts, or simply to sit together without drifting apart. That a single ring might be begun by one traveler and finished two hundred years later by another bothers them not at all; and when, over millennia, the bedrock fuses around the stones like skin over ribs and the stretching of space pulls the circle gently askew into a long, wandering oval, they do not call it ruined. To them, an alignment that has drifted with the folding of the world has simply grown ripe, proving it has belonged to the plateau long enough to move with it.

Beneath the densest overhangs of velvety kelp, where fever-warm trickles rise gently from unseen hairlines in the rock, they press the low mounds of pale clay—kneading silt and mud with their heels and palms, mixing in the empty chalky shells gathered from the spatial seams. Because those shells carry a faint, residual slipperiness from the distortions where their makers lived, a clay cradle inlaid with them feels subtly larger inside than it looks from without, easing the long limbs of whoever curls into it.

It is in these bowls, and inside the fused stone rings, that new life begins—so rarely that a whole plateau may see only one birth in a lifetime. When two wanderers linger together through an entire shedding of the golden seed-snow, tangled limb over limb while the blinding living snow coats their shoulders and the canopy hums silk-on-silk above them, their smudged edges sometimes soften enough to overlap deeply. Long after they have drifted apart again, one of them seeks out a warm clay seat beneath a thick overhang, presses fresh silt around its rim, and delivers a single pale, translucent caul, soft as a kelp-bladder and tethered briefly to its own belly by a ribbon of skin.

The parent stays curled in the bowl for a whole turning of the currents, fanning warm water across the caul with slow sweeps of a broad palm until the young one breaks gently free—astonishingly small, bright amber, and painfully crisp at its edges. For a long while it rides tucked behind the parent’s collarbone, clinging to the velvety moss growing there, peering out with its sensitive brow at the slow world, darting out to chase rising bubbles or clicking softly in its throat just to feel the small, bright warmth of its own voice dissolve against its cheeks. The parent never rushes it and never corrects its quickness. They know the warm water, the silence, and the centuries will round off all its sharp corners soon enough.

At least once in the immense span of a life, almost every wanderer finds its way out to the far edge—past the last terraced ridges, where the broad plateaus finally shelve away and the ribbon-kelps thin into solitary, pale streamers trailing horizontally into nothingness.

There they stop, hooking their toes lightly into the final smooth lip of bedrock, and let their long bodies float out over the abyss.

Below them hangs the trembling, translucent skin of the inverted sea, and beneath that, miles of pale, unseen void from which a steady, milky light rises endlessly upward. They have no memory of air, no legends of a lower world, no fear of falling—for the ocean here does not fall. Yet they linger at the edge for days at a time, letting their arms drift down until their wide, whorled palms rest ever so lightly against the underside of the water’s skin. They never push through. They simply feel the vast, buoyant emptiness pressing softly back against their hands, letting the immense, sideways altitude settle into their bones, listening with their skin to a silence even deeper than the silence of the forests behind them.

And at the very end—when so many cycles have turned that a wanderer’s outline smears across four or five instants at once, when its chest-warmth takes half an hour to answer a greeting and velvety moss grows thick as a fleece across its back and arms—there is no sickness, and no sudden stopping. There are only two ways of letting go of the boundary.

Some choose the stone. They walk slowly back to an ancient fused ring or a favored clay cradle beneath the kelp, curl their long legs beneath them, and lay both broad palms flat against the bedrock. Gradually, over seasons no one counts, their breathing slows until it matches the half-hour rhythm of the great internal waves. Calcium and pale silt knit their calves and hips seamlessly into the shelf; feathery rust-plumes root along their shoulders and turn inward toward the faint, steady blood-warmth still radiating from their ribs. They are not dead—not cleanly, for nothing here ends cleanly. If a younger wanderer drifts past a century later and lays a palm gently against that moss-covered brow, the stone-fused elder still remembers. Long after the visitor has settled down to wait, a slow, broad tide of warmth will rise from deep inside the mossy mound, carrying a blurred, peaceful impression of currents that turned before the nearest ridge folded overhead.

The others choose the ceiling.

When their bones have grown light with age and tiny, natural bladders of buoyancy form beneath their thinned skin, they do not anchor themselves against the next weight-thinning front. They simply unclasp their toes from the pale rock, fold their broad hands softly over their chests, and let go. Slowly—so slowly that a drifting seed-filament outpaces them—they rise past the swaying kelp crowns, past the continent-sized moss-rafts hanging suspended at mid-depth, up out of the jade and tarnished amber and slate-violet altogether. They ascend into the darker, warmer, older water overhead, where light dies and every remaining edge softens past recognition.

No one who rises into the dark ceiling ever comes down again as a single body. Yet when a mile-wide wave of heavier, warmer water rolls silently across the drowned plateaus below, bending the forests flat in a long, hushed embrace before moving on, the ones sitting in the clay bowls lift their pale brows toward the gloom and open their wide palms—greeting the slow return of those who have finally grown large enough to become the sea.
