At first, even when the cloud tears loose and the sun slams back into the bowl, it is easy to miss her.

Tucked into the easternmost of the two slate windbreaks along the north wall, she looks like another fallen shard of quartzite draped in hoarfrost. Her outer coat is pieced from the pelts of the white burrowers, stitched hair-out so the hollow guard hairs catch the unfiltered glare and burn with the same pale halo as the grazers sitting upright on the blocks across the floor. Beneath that white shell, pressed close to her ribs, she wears cured dark moss-felts and black-tanned inner hides that soak up every thread of light passing through the fur. Like the burrowers, she is built to hold a pocket of heat inside a mirror.

She does not move while the white-out holds. The moment the first silent tongue of cloud curls over the razor lip, she stops whatever her hands are doing, draws her wrists up inside her sleeves, and bows her chin into her collar. Her breathing slows until it is barely visible—long, cavernous draws taken strictly through the nose, filtered through a curved muzzle-flap of dampened moss-felt pinned beneath her eyes so the freezing air does not scorch her throat or steal the water from her lungs. Hoarfrost feathers her white-furred shoulders and the rim of her hood. For five minutes she is simply stone.

Then the wind shifts, the fog rips away into the drop, and steam curls in faint wisps off her back.

Only then do her hands emerge. They are broad, dark-knuckled, and scarred across the backs with pale, hairline cuts from unweathered slate; her nailbeds carry the bruised plum tint of blood grown thick and heavy in thin air. She lifts her head, and the mask across the upper half of her face catches the light: a narrow visor carved from the translucent horn of a mountain climber, pierced with two horizontal slits no wider than a grass blade and pinned to the edges of her hood with dull, cold-hammered copper pins. Below the horn, her cheekbones and the bridge of her nose are smeared thick with rendered burrower fat mixed with dark crushed char—an ink-black band that drinks the reflected glare before it can blister her skin.

She has lived in this crescent pocket for enough turnings of the sun that her body no longer keeps time by hours, only by the half-hour tide of cloud and brilliance.

Nothing she does is hurried. Hurrying means panting, and panting at this height drains the water straight out of the blood. When the sunlight strikes the north wall, she rises in one compact, heavy-chested motion—broad-ribbed, light-boned, her height shrunk slightly by years of crouching beneath low rock—and moves across the bare floor in measured, silent intervals. Her boots are soled with layered climber-hide and pegged with bone; they make no more sound on the fractured quartzite than the dry *tick* of a falling pebble.

Her first business after a cloud-surge is always water.

In the western windbreak, angled toward the brightest reflection off the eastern cliff, she keeps three wide funnels stitched from the dried translucent bracts of the lantern-plants, their seams sealed with dark plant-resin and weighted down with slate splinters. During the five-minute white-out, feathery frost blooms thick across the chilled inner walls of the parchment. The instant the sun returns, the amber hoods trap the heat just as the living plants do; before the ice can sublimate back into the dry sky, it beads, slips down the slick parchment throats, and drips in thin silver threads through bone spouts into a flat bladder of climber-stomach. She unstoppers the neck, takes two slow swallows, and tucks the bladder back inside her dark inner coat so it will not freeze solid when the next cloud shadow sweeps the floor.

Water is never wasted, and neither is shadow.

Behind a tilted slab near the north overhang, where the sun never reaches and the air stays hard as iron all day, she keeps her larder. Meat needs no salt up here; a skinned burrower pushed two arm-lengths into that razor-edged shadow turns rigid within an hour and stays sweet for weeks. When she is hungry, she chips off thin shavings with her chert scraper and lays them across a sun-blackened flat of slate in the western windbreak. Within three minutes the savage radiation thaws and steams them dry at the edges; she eats them crisp and half-raw, chewing slowly with her mouth shut, eyes fixed on the far ledges.

Everything she uses comes out of the pocket’s tight, fierce loop, save for the chert and the raw copper—and even the copper is hammered cold on a quartzite anvil, beat thin with a water-rounded pebble carried up from lower ridges years ago, because there is no timber up here for a forge, and a fire in this thin air burns pale, hungry, and brief. She uses the slender copper pins for almost everything cold fingers cannot knot: fastening her coat at the throat, tacking loose corners of her shelter roof before a gust can tear them away, pinning overlapping sheets of dried plant-bract together like shingles before she binds them with braided sinew. Frost and finger-salt have turned the heads of the oldest pins a faint indigo-green, the color of the moss and the metallic flies.

Beside the pins in her dry crack-cache lie the bundles of dried, darkened plant stalks. Each season, after the lantern-plants have sheltered their brief storms of moths and beetles and their translucent hoods have gone crisp, she harvests the spent stalks, peels away the papery bracts for her roof and water-funnels, and splits the hollow stems down the middle. Inside each stem lies a thin pith rich in the bitter, syrupy sap the plant uses to keep its tissues from bursting in the nightly freeze. Pressed beneath heated dark slates in the western windbreak, the split stalks shrink and cure into dense, blackened rods. She carries a short stub in her cheek during the coldest hours, letting the sharp, burnt-sweet resin dissolve under her tongue. It steadies the slow hammer behind her eyes, widens the thin vessels in her toes and fingertips, and keeps her waking clean when the air drops below freezing.

Because she makes no sound, and never breaks the pulse of the bowl, the pocket has long since folded her into its own quiet choreography.

She reads the burrowers the way lowlanders read weather vanes. While she sits in the eastern windbreak scraping fat from a fresh pelt, a single sharp flick of a black-tipped tail on the far ledge—followed by forty white bodies diving beneath the indigo moss in one silent ripple—tells her everything she needs to know. She does not waste motion looking straight into the blinding zenith; she simply pulls her hands beneath her white cloak and goes still against the slate as a narrow-winged harrier slices past overhead with a whisper like torn cloth.

She sets her hair-and-bone snares sparingly among the deepest moss-mats, never taking more than the mats can bear, and whenever she cleans a catch she leaves the warm entrails on a single flat boulder near the eastern cliff. One harrier—an older female with a notched primary feather on its left wing—has learned that boulder. It drops out of the glare twenty paces from where she crouches, tears the meat in silence with its pale breast glowing like snow against the dark moss, and leaves her snare-lines undisturbed.

The pale-horned climbers know her even more intimately. In a bowl fed only by vapor and hoarfrost, salt is rarer than warmth. When she empties her bladder onto a shallow trough of bare quartzite behind the windbreaks, the thin air evaporates the liquid within minutes, leaving a faint, bitter crust of pale minerals on the stone. Toward late afternoon, when the glare softens along the high ledges, three or four climbers drift down off the vertical slate on their translucent, ice-colored hooves. They stand barely two arm-lengths from her knees, grey tongues working patiently over the pale crust while she stitches parchment or re-twists sinew. She never hunts them; she knows each one by the nicks along its pale horns. Only once in six years has she taken from the herd—an old nanny that misjudged a skin of black frost and shattered its foreleg on the sharp blocks below the eastern cliff. Its bleached horn now forms the ridgepole of her shelter; its sinew lashes the roof; its split hooves sit across her eyes, cutting the killer sun down to two calm threads of light.

Her closest companions do not arrive until the light fails.

When the sun drops behind the ridge, the pocket changes character in minutes. The bruised indigo sky deepens to a hard, star-crowded black; the mirror-walls stop throwing heat; the last beads of water beneath the moss-mats lock into glass. Inside the living lantern-plants, the trapped warmth leaks swiftly through the papery bracts until the hollow hoods grow as brittle and cold as the stone around them.

Before the dark settles fully, she crawls on hands and knees under the shallow overhang into her tiny crouch-shelter, pulls the weighted burrower-pelt flap shut across the opening, and pins its edges tight from within. Inside, it is barely wide enough to curl her knees toward her chest, its low amber ceiling smelling of dried leaves, tallow, and cured sinew. There is no fire—only the slow furnace of her own blood, held in by stacked stone, chinked moss, and layered parchment.

Soon after her breathing settles, the dry bracts above her head give a faint rustle. One by one, squeezing through tiny gaps where the moss chinking meets the rock, the thumb-sized warm-blooded mice abandon the freezing lantern-plants and slip into the only warm glasshouse left in the bowl. They are bolder in the dark than in the glare. Five or six of them nose beneath the edge of her white-furred cloak, burrowing into the dry hollows behind her knees and against the pulse at her throat, shivering at first, then settling into tight, furious little balls of heat whose frantic hearts drum against her slow, cavernous ribs all night. Above her forehead, pale long-legged spiders cling motionless to the underside of the bleached horn ridgepole, legs drawn tight, waiting out the freeze.

Through the long dark, her exhaled breath rises and coats the cool inner ceiling of the stitched parchment roof in a thin, glittering rind of frost. When the first fierce lance of morning sun strikes the amber roof, that rind melts before the outside air has warmed a single degree, trickling down the taut seams into a pecked groove along a flat slate by her knee. She wakes, reaches out in the warm honey-colored gloom, and drinks her own night’s breath while the mice dart back out through the chinking to snatch torpid beetles off the sun-struck stone.

Yet she is not here merely to endure the pocket.

Each clear afternoon, when the razor-edged shadow of the western wall slides across the bare floor and touches a worn notch in the stone, she leaves her windbreak and walks slowly toward the open lip. There, poised over miles of thin air and the bright, undulating ceiling of clouds, stands the slender stack of three pale quartz blocks—each sharp tip seated into a hair-thin dimple pecked into the stone beneath it, balanced so delicately that a heavy gale or a careless hand would bring them clattering down.

She kneels behind them on the sun-warmed slab facing the drop, lowers her chin until her horn-slit eyes sit level with the stack, and peers through the tiny, diamond-shaped pinhole of indigo sky where the upper two points kiss.

Twenty miles away, rising like a jagged dark island out of the blinding sea of cloud-tops, stands a distant peak. Centered precisely in the pinhole between the quartz points is a pale, notched shoulder halfway up its flank—another high crescent, invisible to anyone who does not know where to look.

From a pouch tucked beneath her inner hide, she draws a flat, cleaved plate of pale quartzite no wider than her palm, its back worn glassy-smooth by generations of thumbs, its face left raw and mirror-bright along the fracture plane. Bracing her elbow on her knee so her hand will not tremble, she lifts the stone and catches the ferocious afternoon sun. A thin, silent needle of white light leaps off the lip of the pocket and shoots straight across the empty air toward the far mountain.

She holds it steady for the count of ten slow breaths through her nose, tilts the stone down into her shadow, and waits.

Down below the undulating cloud deck—down in the wet, heavy valleys she has not smelled in years—the sky is a perpetual ceiling of mist, and the turning of the seasons arrives late and blurred. Up here, where the light still has sharp edges, a sparse chain of watchers keeps the high spines above the fog, passing word from peak to peak faster than any runner could cross the jagged gorges between them. Sometimes the clouds rise and drown the far ridge for days. Sometimes the afternoon passes in unbroken emptiness, and she lowers her mirror and returns to her snares without marking the stone.

Then, on a clear afternoon, from the bruised flank of the distant mountain, a tiny cold star winks back through the pinhole between the quartz points.

Once. A pause. Twice. Then a long, steady burn that holds for five breaths before vanishing into the glare.

Her face behind the smeared black tallow and horn slits does not smile, but her broad shoulders drop a fraction of an inch. That brief, silent grammar is enough: *Still breathing. High weather holding clear. The frost-line is dropping on the western ridges.* In another turning of the sun, when the winter lock approaches, a different rhythm of flashes will travel down the spine from ridge to ridge, and someone younger will climb the razor trail from below—carrying fresh nodules of raw copper, a new chert scraper, and the heavy breath of the lowlands—to sit beside her in the western windbreak, learn the angle of the quartz mirror, the habits of the scarred harrier, and the curing of the blackened stalks, and stay on in the amber shelter when her knees finally grow too stiff for the ledges.

Until then, she reaches beside her knee for a sharp splinter of gray slate. Leaning over the sun-warmed slab facing the drop, she sets the stone point beside the long row of neat vertical grooves—the leftmost ones shallow and worn pale at the edges by frost and blown dust, pecked by the old man who taught her here before she took the eastern windbreak alone; the middle ones deeper and slanted slightly left from her own bent thumb. With three clean, quiet strokes, she adds one fresh white mark to the row, leaving plenty of blank, pale grain waiting to the right.

High on the north wall, a frost-wedged flake of slate snaps loose with a faint *tink*. Far out past the drop, the bright floor of clouds begins to swell upward against the sheer cliff face.

She slides the quartz mirror back inside her coat, rises, and walks unhurriedly to the north wall just as the first silent white tongue spills over the razor lip. Behind the stacked slate, she tucks her hands beneath her white-furred ribs, bows her horned brow, and closes her eyes. The dazzling white-out swallows the bowl, hoarfrost blooms like feathers across her shoulders and the dark moss cushions alike, and for five minutes the cathedral of stone looks utterly empty once more—holding its breath until the light returns.
