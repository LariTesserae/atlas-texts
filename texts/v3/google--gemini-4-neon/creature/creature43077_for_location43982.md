If you stay still long enough after a down-surge collapses—hanging cold and weightless while the grit-cloud folds back into the seams and the last skittering cobbles settle—you may hear a sound that does not belong to the current.

The water is slack. Nothing is rolling across the flagstones. Yet through the soles of your feet, or the flat of a palm pressed to a ruined parapet, comes a dry, deliberate cadence: *tock... tock-tock*. Clean and sharp, traveling effortlessly across fifty paces of interlocking stone.

Seconds later, thirty yards off in the bruised pewter haze beside a drowned colonnade, something that had been indistinguishable from a rounded spine of masonry shifts its weight, lifts its head a hand’s breadth off the paving, and answers.

They do not look like fish, and they do not look like anything that ever walked in dry air.

At first glance, seen crouched low against the lee of a squat pillar, one might pass for a boulder worn smooth by centuries of meltwater—a broad, gently domed mass of matte pewter-grey hide, roughly the bulk of a heavy man hunched tight around his knees. Only when the current slacks and it unfolds do the proportions reveal themselves. There is almost no neck; the wide, flattened skull flows straight into massive, sloping, shield-like shoulders built to split a downward plunge of water without snapping the spine. Beneath a heavy overhanging brow-ridge sit two small, deeply recessed eyes with pale, silvery irises and horizontal pupils, shielded from flying grit. There is no protruding nose, only twin recessed clefts above a wide, lipless, down-curved mouth lined with pale, blunt crushing plates.

Where the broad dorsal hide is thick, rubbery, and pale slate-grey—pitted across the oldest ridges of the shoulders and elbows with tiny, shallow scour-marks that heal over decades into glossy white whorls—the underside is startlingly delicate: a translucent, milk-blue flesh along the throat, the inner forearms, and the long ribs. Down either flank runs a neat row of pleated, muscular gill-valves. In calm water they open and close with a slow, stately rhythm, exposing faint pale filaments within; a heartbeat before a grit-laden surge erupts from the seams, every valve clamps shut as tight as a mollusk, holding a single cold breath of oxygen-rich meltwater until the storm passes.

Their limbs are built for the floor, not the open water. Two short, pillar-thick hind legs end in broad, splayed, three-toed pads whose leathery undersides grip wet flagstones with stubborn traction, backed by a thick, flattened tail-spade that presses flush against the stone behind them like a third anchor. In contrast, their forelimbs are remarkably long and supple, hanging almost to the paving when they crouch upright, ending in four blunt, heavily knuckled fingers and a stout opposable spur.

And along their lower flanks, just behind the ribcage, hang the strange, rounded bulges that set them apart from every other creature on the plateau: deep, ribbed muscular pouches folded directly into their thick hide, packed tight with water-smoothed stones.

---

Without those stones, they cannot hold the floor.

Near-freezing meltwater is dense, and their own lean, slow-burning bodies sit just shy of neutral buoyancy—heavy enough to sink slowly in stillness, light enough that an unanchored swimmer caught in an exploding down-surge would be tumbled end-over-end across a hundred yards of paving and flung cleanly over the curved outer parapets into the abyssal troughs below. Rather than fighting the surges with muscle, they ballast themselves with the ruins.

Each mature walker carries between four and a dozen stones tucked deep into its flank-pouches, held fast by steady muscular contraction and released only when the folds deliberately relax. You can read where one has been by the heft of its hips. Those that spend their days along the sheltered central cisterns carry only a few light, pale limestone cobbles—once the carved lips of vanished cornices—shifting lightly against their ribs. Those that travel the exposed outer causeways, where the coldest currents accelerate and spill downward into the dark below, pack their flanks heavy with compact, egg-smooth black basalt until they walk almost like divers in lead, creeping hand-over-hand along the soap-smooth parapet walls, never lifting more than one grip at a time.

Yet they are not slow when they choose to move.

Watch one cross the hundred yards of bare, scalloped paving between a ruined viaduct and a stepped circular court. It does not walk the whole expanse, nor does it kick frantically against the cross-eddies. It crouches at the edge of the masonry, pressing its broad, flat chin to the flagstones while the dark sensory pits running along its collarbone and ribs read the water overhead. It feels the dense plume of colder meltwater forming twenty seconds before it drops.

Just as the vertical plunge strikes the open plaza and explodes outward, the walker pushes off with its thick hind legs, tucks its long forelimbs tight under its chest, and angles its broad, domed back into the blast. For three silent seconds the voiceless wall of water lifts it two feet above the skittering cobbles and hurls it cleanly across forty yards of empty paving in a single, weightless, grey glide. Right before the outward rush collapses—before the violent backward suction can yank it off course—it drops its basalt-weighted flanks and fans its tail-spade downward. Gravity catches it instantly. Its splayed hind-pads strike the worn ramp of a double-sided stairway; its long fingers hook into a widened mortarless gutter; it slides two paces with a faint, dry scrape and locks fast.

A ribbon of silver-grey swimmers flicks past its brow. A milky bell-creature tumbles harmlessly over its shoulder-shield. The walker remains motionless as a cornerstone until the grit-cloud settles, then rises smoothly onto its knuckles and glides over the flat crown of the stairs into the quiet eddy on the far side.

---

In a world with no wood, no fiber, no metal, and no flame, stone is everything they own, everything they trade, and the medium through which they speak.

Vocal cords would be useless here, and high-pitched cries would scatter into the suspended stone-flour. Close up, when two walkers meet in the lee of a breached sluice, they converse in silence: a brief flash of pale milk-blue flesh as their gill-valves part, a subtle inclination of the blunt fingers, or a tight, sharp clap of broad palms that sends a clean pulse of water against the other’s sensory pores—felt not in the ears, but as a crisp, unmistakable tap against the ribs and cheekbones from ten paces away.

Across the vast, misty terraces, however, they speak through the plateau itself.

They have no external ears. Instead, the flat undersides of their jaws, the heels of their palms, and the broad plates of their shins contain dense, ivory-like acoustic bone wired straight into the inner skull. Because the plateau is built of immense, tightly interlocking flagstones resting on older masonry below, a solid strike carries astonishingly far through the rock while the water above stays hushed.

For this, every mature walker keeps a single small, dense basalt pebble—a voice-stone, worn glassy by decades inside a small fold near the collarbone. Lowering its chin flat to the paving, it taps out slow, economical cadences: a double-knock to warn of an unusual density-drop forming beneath the ice overhead; a sharp triplet followed by a deliberate grinding scrape of stone on stone to mark a migrating gravel-bar that has newly buried a stairwell. Their conversations are unhurried, separated by long pauses while surges pass and clear. Two walkers separated by an entire sunken arena may take half an hour to negotiate the movement of a single fallen lintel, their rhythms faint and dry beneath the velvet silence of the water.

When they pass close, they sometimes touch palms—not for warmth, for their flesh is the exact mineral cold of the meltwater, but to read the slow pulse of each other’s flank-valves—and exchange a single cobble from their ballast-pouches, weighing it thoughtfully in their long fingers before tucking it away. A smooth piece of pale, veined granite passed from flank to flank carries its own quiet geography: *the eastern sluice has scoured deeper; the old threshold there is open again.*

---

They are neither hunters of the open water nor mere scavengers; they are tenders of weight and flow.

Nothing green grows on the plateau, and their slow, cold metabolisms ask for very little, yet they know every corner of its sparse abundance. They harvest the slate-shelled crawlers sparingly, prying a few from the lee of a retaining wall with a flat limestone wedge and cracking the carapaces neatly between their blunt jaw-plates, always leaving most of a colony untouched—and sometimes rolling a drift of fist-sized rubble into a new arc so a fresh calm pocket forms where the crawlers can gather. When they reach into the deep drainage trenches and collapsed vaults beneath the plazas for the long-legged, pitted crabs, they always tap the fallen lintel twice with a knuckle first, chin pressed to the masonry, listening for the dull, fleshy thud of a massive, whiskered bottom-dweller shifting irritably in the dark silt within. They never harm the translucent bell-creatures; they watch them instead, reading the invisible twists and dead-eyes of the cross-currents by the way the milky bells tumble and spin through the gloom.

More than feeding, though, their days are given to the slow, patient maintenance of the stone.

Because loose rubble-bars migrate across the plazas every hour, the ancient channels are constantly threatening to choke. If a bar of rounded cornice-fragments seals off the central perforated plinth of a circular court, the water trapped in the older buried arches beneath goes stale and stagnant, stripped of the dissolved oxygen and fine glacial snow that sustain the lower vaults. Conversely, if a breached retaining wall finally gives way, a vertical down-surge can blast straight into a sheltered lower terrace and scour it bare.

So the walkers intervene—never with mortar, never with new-cut rock, only by placing weight against weight.

You may come upon four of them braced shoulder-to-shoulder on the gently sloping floor of a circular court, hind-pads wedged into the concentric gutters, tail-spades locked, straining in complete silence against a wedged basalt slab blocking the central plinth. They work with the water rather than against it, waiting for the precise moment a down-surge hits and momentarily lifts the jammed rock a hair’s breadth on a cushion of compressed silt, heaving together until the slab rolls clear. At once the paving beneath their palms gives a deep, hollow shudder as the drowned chambers below drink in a rushing column of fresh, bitter meltwater. The walkers do not linger to celebrate; they scatter back to the rim before the tightening vortex can catch them, tucking a few loose cobbles into their flanks as they go.

Stranger still is what they do at the chest-high stone troughs.

Along the open plazas where those long rows of slotted basins stand, the turbulent water threads through the narrow vertical openings in silent, pressurized jets. In the quiet intervals between down-surges, five or six walkers will sometimes gather along the lee-side of a trough-row, rising upright on their stout hind legs so their broad chests and lateral-line pores align squarely with the rushing jets. Reaching over the soap-smooth rims with their long arms, they carefully wedge chosen cobbles and rare, water-drilled fragments into individual slots—narrowing one jet to a thin blade, blocking another entirely, widening a third.

As the stones lock into place, the silent row begins to change. A low, rhythmic, hydraulic thrum builds in the cold water—scarcely audible to the ears, yet pulsing cleanly through the teeth, the sternum, and the water-filled pores along their ribs. Each row of troughs across the vast plateau is tuned to a slightly different cadence. When the suspended stone-flour thickens to a blinding fifty-pace veil, a walker lost out on the bare flagstones can crouch, open its flank-pores, and find its way home across three terraces purely by the distinctive beat of each trough-row thrumming through the gloom.

Yet they do not only tune the troughs when the silt is thick. Sometimes, when the water is as clear as bruised pewter allows and the ice-shadows drift lazily overhead, a small cluster of them will stay braced against a singing trough-row for an hour at a time, palms resting lightly on the worn rims, gill-valves pulsing in slow unison, simply letting the ordered pressure of the unseen higher watersheds thrum through their bones. When a fierce surge eventually strikes and blows half the wedged cobbles clattering onto the paving, they show no haste and no frustration. Once the grit settles, they gather the scattered stones and begin again, adjusting the pattern to match the subtle new angle of the flow.

---

Their true shelters—and their young—do not belong to the upper plazas at all.

Drop down through a wide perforated plinth, or slip beneath the collapsed stairwell of an outer cistern, and the violent chaos of the plateau softens abruptly. Down here, beneath the broken paving, lie the older, smoother slabs and the buried crowns of lower arches, stacked layer upon buried layer. The pewter light dies out altogether into a clean, mineral dark. The scouring grit settles out; the surging blasts of water slow into steady, silent, laminar streams threading through endless corridors of drowned masonry.

Here, clinging to the undersides of ancient vault-crowns in quiet, pale clusters, live the young.

They bear almost no resemblance to the heavy grey walkers above. Newly hatched from leathery, stone-weighted eggs wedged deep into mortarless crevices, the young are small, soft-boned, and nearly translucent—as pale and buoyant as the milky bell-creatures, with long, delicate, feathery external gill-plumes fanning out behind their heads like frost-crystals. Up on the open plateau, a single blast of suspended stone-flour would shred those plumes to ribbons in seconds. Down in the dark arches they hang weightless and unharmed, fed on soft mash of crushed crawler-meat and stunned ribbon-swimmers carried down from the upper terraces by attentive adults.

They grow with the glacial patience of everything else in this water. Over long years their soft bones drink in the dissolved minerals of the meltwater, thickening and growing dense. Their delicate external plumes slowly shrink and withdraw behind newly formed muscular slit-valves; their translucent skins toughen to matte pewter-grey; the folds along their flanks deepen into tough, empty pouches.

The first time a young one follows an elder up through a buried stairwell into the dim pewter light of the plateau, it cannot keep its footing. Even a mild cross-eddy lifts it helplessly off the scalloped stone, its long limbs flailing in silent alarm. The elder stays pressed close beside it in the lee of a low retaining wall, holding it anchored with one broad forearm while its other hand gathers smooth, fist-sized cobbles from a nearby rubble-bar, pressing them gently one by one into the youngster’s taut flank-pouches—adjusting, testing, adding a heavier dark basalt stone—until the young walker feels its own weight settle cleanly into the flagstones for the first time.

---

Time in this cold water does not wither them; it turns them gradually to stone.

A walker may live for a century, perhaps two, and over all those decades the mineral-rich meltwater works its quiet alchemy on their flesh and bone. Their skeletons grow steadily denser, heavier, less yielding. Along the overhanging ridge of their brows, the broad dome of their shoulders, and the blunt knuckles of their hands, a faint, pale chalky patina begins to bond permanently into the scarred hide—the exact same mineral crust that coats sheltered masonry, no longer wiped clean by the surges.

Eventually an elder no longer needs basalt in its flank-pouches; its own calcified body has become ballast enough. Crossing a hundred-yard terrace grows stiff and laborious. One by one, the oldest walkers give up their roaming. They drop their voice-stones into the caches of the young, retreat into the quiet bays behind ancient baffles, the sheltered corners of drowned colonnades, or the buried thresholds one layer beneath the paving, and settle into near-motionless stillness.

There they remain for years, breathing once every half-minute through sluggish flank-valves, their broad jaws resting permanently against the flagstones. Younger walkers bring them cracked crawlers and the soft bellies of trench-crabs, laying the food quietly beside their blunt mouths without expecting a glance in return.

Because they never lift their heads from the rock, these calcified elders become the long memory and the deep ears of the plateau. Through the continuous interlocking masonry, they feel things no roaming walker could ever notice: the slow, week-long creep of a cobble-bar three terraces away; the faint, hairline settling of a buried arch four layers down; the subtle sharpening in the meltwater’s bite when the unseen ice-sheets far above the murk begin to fracture and thicken. Younger walkers come to them in silence, crouch shoulder-to-shoulder in the lee of a pillar, press their own palms and chins to the same slab, and listen as the old one taps out slow, economical maps with a single mineral-crusted knuckle—warning of a vault about to collapse, or guiding them to a buried sluice newly scoured open by the shifting grit.

And when an old one finally ceases to breathe, the living do not cast its body over the curved outer parapets into the dark troughs below.

Instead, three or four walkers lift the immensely heavy, stone-dense corpse between them and carry it across the terraces—moving only in the slack intervals between surges—to wherever a retaining wall has begun to lean, or a sluice-cheek has lost its lower course to the scouring current. There they ease the body into the gap, tucking its broad domed shoulders tight between the ancient granite blocks and wedging smooth cobbles around its ribs and limbs until it locks fast by weight and gravity alone. The next fierce down-surge washes fine pale grit into the seams and seals it clean.

If you swim close to a collapsed wall that a violent vortex has recently stripped down to its older courses, you may look right past it without realizing what you see: a broad, pitted curve of pale, calcified bone, wedged seamlessly between two water-worn lintels, indistinguishable at a glance from the quarried stone around it, still holding the cold current true.

---

Whether they are the vastly altered, long-patient descendants of whoever cut these concentric flagstones—having shed every memory of what they once meant to house or measure until only the bare habits of weight and water remained—or whether they crept up out of the dark outer troughs ages after the plateau drowned, shaped over millennia to fit an abandoned geometry the way a crab grows curved to an empty shell, there is no way to tell. They carve no new blocks. They leave no mark upon the rock. They seem to hold no distinction between the architecture and the mountain, nor between the stone and themselves.

Hang suspended in midwater above the wide central terraces, and the place keeps its strange, silent equilibrium. Shafts of bruised grey light break and reform as wind-torn ice passes unseen overhead. Clouds of pale stone-flour bloom and shred. Translucent bells tumble lazily through the breaches; silver ribbons flick sideways in unison; loose cobbles knock softly down worn ramps.

And down on the scalloped paving, half a dozen matte pewter figures lie flattened flush against the ancient slabs—flank-valves sealed, basalt tucked close to their ribs, palms pressed to the rock—letting the voiceless wall of water roar harmlessly over their backs before they unfold, rise into the dim slackness, and set about moving one smooth cobble half a pace to the left.
