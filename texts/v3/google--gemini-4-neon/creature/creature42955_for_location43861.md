If you walk the avenues at a brisk pace, looking for movement, you will leave convinced the basin is unmanned. The eye is trained to hunt for things that hurry—for flashes of bright cloth, swinging arms, voices lifted against wind. None of that exists here. To see who keeps the oil baths full and the hornbeam walls sheared to a plumb line, you have to stop in one of the wide, empty yards and let your own tempo drop until it matches the basin's middle register: slower than the wagtail on the cistern rim, faster than the swelling of the wooden switch-blocks.

Only then does a pale vertical shadow beside a transformer-shed separate itself from the espaliered alder and shift its weight. Only then do you notice that the shape crouched at a rail junction forty yards away is not a hooded junction-box at all, but someone kneeling on a folded square of waxed felt, listening to the underside of the steel rail with a short wooden sounding-rod pressed against one ear.

There are never many of them—perhaps nine or eleven across the whole half-mile bowl, spaced so thinly that two can pass an entire afternoon without crossing the same avenue. At a distance they read simply as spare, upright figures in the practical dress of old municipal linesmen: long, matte coats and aprons woven from the precinct's own pale fibrous cordage, steeped in boiled tallow and clarified sap until the cloth hangs heavy and sheds the morning drift like slate. Their colors are the colors of the place—dull pewter, wet hornbeam bark, faded iron-oxide, peat-brown. On their feet they wear thick-soled boots of layered cork and vulcanized gum, completely silent on the springy turf and dryly insulated against the sunken third rail.

Up close, the impression of ordinary workmen quietly gives way to something older and more thoroughly acclimatized.

They are tallish and narrow-boned, built with an economy that makes every gesture look measured in advance. Their skin has the cool, fine-grained, matte texture of stripped winter willow—a pale olive-grey that never flushes in the autumn chill—and their breath scarcely clouds the cold air even at dawn, as though their bodies ran only a few degrees warmer than the mossy heat-exchanger walls. Their faces are calm, symmetrical, lightly weathered with fine horizontal lines at the brow and eye-corners from decades of gauging tolerances against pale glass. What hair they keep is sheared as close and even as the clipped crest of the embankments, a fine, dull silver-dun like cured sedge. Their eyes are wide-set and dark amber, the color of the oil in the substation sight-glasses, and they blink so seldom that an outsider caught in their gaze feels briefly as though they are being read by an instrument.

Their hands tell the most. Long-fingered, dry, and cool to the touch, they are stained faintly green-black in the deeper creases from bryophyte sap and tallow grease, yet scrupulously clean across the pads and the flat, pale, horn-thick nails. Almost every one of them wears a narrow copper ferrule or a smoothed brass thimble permanently on a forefinger or thumb, polished bright from decades of testing low-voltage contacts by touch. They do not need a voltmeter to tell whether a signaling line is carrying its proper trickle; they rest the brass-shod knuckle lightly against a bare busbar and feel the sixty-cycle tremor travel up the ulna, gauging its steadiness by a faint, familiar metallic sourness at the back of the jaw.

Whether they are the distant, much-weathered descendants of whoever first sank these foundations, or something that already belonged to the damp ground and learned the use of spanners, the distinction has blurred as thoroughly as the boundary between brick and alder in the millhouse walls. They do not command the precinct; the precinct already runs itself through ninety-nine parts of the day. They exist for the hundredth part—the slight drift, the millimeter of excess growth, the dry bearing, the tiny eccentricity that, left untended for a season, would pull the whole basin out of true.

Watch one through a morning and you see how little effort they expend. At first light, while mist still sheets down the eastern condenser-towers, a keeper walks the wooden catwalk with a bone needle and a spool of waxed thread, mending a single hand-sized tear in the linen mesh before the wind can widen it. Later, along the avenue of pollarded cable-trees, another moves from trunk to trunk, lifting each glass drip-cup against the pale sky to judge the clarity of the amber sap, touching one fingertip to the meniscus and then to the tip of the tongue—measuring sugar-weight, moisture, and trace current in a single, unhurried taste. Where an alder limb has swelled too greedily around a bolted flange, a curved steel pruning-knife shaves away a pale ribbon of cambium just thick enough to relieve the stress on a porcelain insulator, and a thumbful of dark resin seals the cut before the noon breeze can dry it.

Because sound carries too cleanly across the sunken floor, they treat loud speech the way a machinist treats chatter in a lathe: as friction, and therefore waste. Up close, when two meet beside a pressing-shed to trade a carboy or balance a counterweight, they speak in a flat, dry murmur pitched just beneath the hum of the transformers—sparse syllables that travel three feet and dissolve into the damp air. Mostly, they communicate through placement and small, agreed-upon geometries. That clean rag draped neatly over a brass valve-wheel is not an accident of tidiness: folded lengthwise, it tells the next walker that the oil bath has been bled and topped; folded into a triangle, that the gland packing is weeping and needs tightening before dusk. A small flat slate laid beside a rail switch notes a chattering brass shoe on trolley four. Across longer distances, they use the basin's own low-voltage signaling lines, tapping brief, sparse ledger-clicks onto small glass-domed sounders bolted inside the substations—never conversation, only state and measure.

From the outside, there is no way to tell where they sleep. None of the industrial houses have curtained windows, door-knockers, or smoking chimneys; open flame is an alien thing in a town built of peat-moss, tallow, and living timber. Instead, their quarters occupy the warm, dry interstices of the machinery: narrow mezzanines tucked directly above the oil-cooled transformer banks, and deep cedar-lined recesses built inside the double-skinned millhouse walls where the copper heat-exchangers radiate a steady, gentle warmth that smells faintly of dry bryophyte and ozone.

Inside, a keeper's room looks like a watchmaker's cabin grafted onto a root-cellar. There are no ornaments, only the quiet beauty of things kept ready: shelves of thick glass carboys holding clarified sap graded from clear as cistern-water to deep chestnut; hanging hanks of pale cordage; low, narrow cots stuffed with dry, springy peat-moss and dressed in heavy unbleached linen. A single cast-iron warming plate wired directly off the low-voltage busbar serves for all cooking. On the long workbenches—their pine tops darkened and hollowed by generations of sleeves—tiny brass screws and mica washers sit sorted in shallow porcelain dishes beside thick, calf-bound logbooks. Open any ledger and you find pages filled with tight, vertical columns of penciled figures: dew-yields, sap-pressures, relay-throws, the dates of root-grafts set eighty or a hundred and forty years ago. The handwriting barely changes from one volume to the next, as though the same patient, cramped hand had simply gone on holding the pencil without interruption.

They eat once a day, usually just after the evening louvers groan shut, and they eat almost entirely from the precinct's closed circulation. On the iron plates they warm thick infusions of clarified root-sap diluted with chilled water drawn from the black cisterns, steeped with bitter sprigs of creeping thyme and crushed hornbeam buds until the broth turns pale gold and slightly viscous. With it they take dense, dark wafers baked slowly from ground pith-cake meal and salted with trace minerals precipitated out of the condenser troughs, and occasionally thin raw slices of a firm, pale fungus harvested from the shaded undersides of the slate drying-lofts. Because their pulse is slow and their bodies waste neither heat nor motion, a single cup and two dark wafers carry them cleanly through twenty-four hours.

They are not entirely alone in the warm rooms. Down near the stone bases of the transformer banks and inside the dry relay housings live the slow, pale-shelled beetles that sometimes venture across a copper grounding-strap at midday. The keepers know them well and leave tiny smears of sweet sap on the undersides of the junction-boxes to keep them close. Nearly blind and the color of bleached bone, the beetles creep through the narrow clearances between brass contacts and oil-damped arms, grazing on the fine white fungal threads and damp mites that would otherwise bridge an insulator or foul a switch. A keeper opening a relay cabinet will gently lift a beetle from a contact bar with the flat of a brass screwdriver, set it carefully on the warm brick sill, finish oiling the pivot, and return it to the dark before closing the latch.

Toward the few creatures that cross over from beyond the forty-foot rim, their attitude is quieter still. When the solitary grey wagtail drops over the hornbeam crest and alights on an oxidized catch-basin, any keeper standing nearby simply goes still, hands resting lightly at their sides, watching the bird's brisk, hot-blooded bobbing with mild, unblinking attention—the way one might watch a spark blown in from a faster, noisier season outside the bowl. They never shoo it and never try to tame it; when it flicks away over the northern embankment, they return to their gauge-stick without a word.

Time moves through their ranks at the same patient gradient as it moves through the architecture. Children are extraordinarily rare—seldom more than one in the entire precinct at a time, appearing every twenty or thirty years as if only when a place on the grid is quietly preparing to open. A young keeper is a small, grave shadow in a cut-down waxed coat, trailing two paces behind an elder along the mossy avenues, carrying nothing heavier than a copper oil-can or a wooden tray of spare porcelain washers. Before they learn to enter figures in the logbooks, they learn how to walk the sunken rails without brushing the third-rail shoe, how to read the midday silvering of the groundcover leaves, and how to hold a wooden sounding-rod between a casing and the bone behind the ear until the sixty-cycle murmur separates into its distinct, layered parts.

At the other end of that span sit the eldest keepers, who have grown so cool, dry, and dense-grained with age that they rarely descend the wet iron stairs anymore. They spend their days in the warm upper lofts of the central millhouse, seated in low wicker-and-iron chairs beside the slow-turning wooden drums, wrapped in heavy felted shawls with their bare feet resting on the warm copper heat-conduits. They do not look frail so much as seasoned—shrunk down to straight grain and quiet attention, like the hornbeam blocks clamped in the outdoor switches. Eyes half-closed, they read the basin through the faint tremor in the floorboards and the slow drift of a needle behind curved glass. When a distant sheave begins to run dry three buildings away, or a heavy press settles half a second out of phase, the elder feels the slight roughness in the hum, reaches down, and taps twice on the copper conduit with a brass-ringed knuckle. Down in the yard, a younger tender pauses beside a rail, tilts their head, and turns calmly toward the cableway.

When an elder finally cools all the way down and stops, there is no bell, no procession, and no disturbance of the load-bearing turf. At dusk, two keepers carry the light, dry body up the inspection-stairs and into a deep, shadowed hollow parted gently inside the forty-foot eastern embankment. Wrapped tightly in pale waxed cordage and packed round with damp cushion-moss, they are settled into the woven heart of the hornbeam and willow-root, where the living retaining wall slowly closes over them and draws them into its own forty-foot discipline. Nothing is burned; nothing is left to slump into ruin.

Late in the afternoon, when shadow spills across the sunken floor and the air sharpens by a few degrees, that is when you are most likely to meet them. One by one, as the wooden switch-blocks contract and the caged filament bulbs and pale blue-green mercury lamps flicker awake across the yards, doors ease open along the mossy flanks of the substations. The keepers step out into the rising mist for the evening rounds—not in a group, just singly, emerging from opposite corners of the basin with small brass oil-cans, folded squares of bleached linen, and narrow pruning-knives tucked into their apron-loops.

If you stand completely still in the western yard as dusk gathers, one may pass within ten paces of you along the parallel steel rails, moving at the exact unhurried pace of the electric trolleys. They carry no lantern; their cork soles know every recessed cistern lip and rail-frog by the slight change in the spring of the turf. Passing beneath a blue-green mercury lamp, the keeper will turn their head and hold you for three or four calm seconds in that steady, dark-amber gaze—without alarm, without welcome, measuring you with the same quiet equanimity they give to a drop in barometric pressure or an unscheduled gust over the rim. If you keep your boots off the porcelain conduits and your hands off the drip-cups, they will incline their head by half an inch, step silently over the wet rail, and recede into the mist curling around the mossy pylons—leaving behind only the clean, faint scent of tallow, crushed fern, and ozone, and, a minute later from somewhere behind the transformer wall, the soft, oiled *clunk* of a brass valve turned a quarter-inch tighter for the night.
