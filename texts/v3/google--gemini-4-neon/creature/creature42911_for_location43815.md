You can stand on the shelf for an hour and never guess that it is inhabited.

In the flat pewter light, the floor looks too open to keep secrets. You see the seven or eight scattered pins holding the warm pocket in the sky; you see the frantic choreography of crabs, drifters, hounds, and falling wings; you see the bare, curved ribs of dark glass enclosing the bowl. Nothing resembles a chimney, a path, or a wall laid by hands.

Then the warmth spikes. The plinth of metallic leaves clicks shut against the back wall, the chalk-white hoop dims for a single heartbeat, your ears pop, and hot gray vapor pours over the rim until the world shrinks to ten paces.

That is when the rhythm changes.

Through the soles of your boots—not through the roaring air, which the fog has thickened into a damp wool of crab-clatter and hissing runoff—comes a sharp, deliberate pulse: *tap-tap-tap, pause, tap.* Clean as a fingernail struck on a wineglass, traveling straight through fifty yards of unbroken stone. No curing fracture breaks in threes. No bell-crab keeps that spacing.

A moment later, shapes detach from the base of the curved perimeter ribs.

At first, sliding low through the ten-pace soup, they might pass for large glass-hounds moving slowly. Then one pauses to brace a knuckle against a wet basalt edge, and the silhouette resolves into something altogether stranger and unmistakably deliberate. They are low-centered and broad across the shoulders, no taller than a human chest even when they straighten—which they almost never do outside their shelters. They move across the slick glass in an economical three-point crouch, bare heels or strapped pads of rough hound-hide kissing the water film, draped from neck to calf in pale, shimmering capes stitched from the frilled membranes of ribbon-drifters. In the dim mist, those capes catch the rising drafts and ripple slightly around their backs, breaking their outlines into moving sheets of vapor.

Up close, their bodies read like a long negotiation with uncured stone.

Their skin is pale slate-gray, hairless, and finely pebbled, tough enough to turn a casual brush with a fresh basalt edge. Wherever friction and impact fall heaviest—across their knees, the points of their elbows, their shins, and the backs of their broad, four-fingered hands—they do not try to stay soft. They deliberately smear the weeping, iron-sweet mineral brine from fresh fractures into their own calluses and let it cure. Over weeks of living in the warm damp, cloudy, overlapping scabs of pale silicate build up across their joints like natural armor plates. When they reach for a tool or tap a companion’s shoulder, their knuckles give off a faint, dry mineral *tink*. When a plate grows too thick and splits along a stress line, they pry the brittle flake loose with a bone pick and leave it tucked into a right-angle crease to mark a claim or a boundary.

Their skulls are narrow and smooth, stripped of anything the wind can snag or the cold shear can bite. Instead of outer ears, they have twin recessed slits tucked behind the hinge of the jaw, sealed beneath tight translucent membranes that snap shut under a brow of cartilage the instant a sky-predator’s braking-boom rattles the basin or a seam cracks too close. Their wide-set dark eyes blink sideways beneath pale inner lids that wipe condensing mist away every few seconds. In this bruised, shadowless light, color means little to them; their gaze is tuned instead to sheen and refraction. They can read the ninety-degree crystal grid between the blackened tripod stakes as clearly as painted lines on a floor, and they can spot the hydroplaning wake of a glass-hound in a shallow trough long before the beast itself separates from the wet stone.

Barely a score of them hold this shelf, counting two young small enough to ride cinched against a parent’s ribs in slings of oiled hound-hide. They have no spoken name for themselves; in a bowl this loud, ordinary speech is wasted breath. Instead, they live inside two quiet languages built around the acoustics of glass.

Across distance, they speak through the floor. Every adult carries a weighted awl of sky-predator bone lashed to the wrist, and when they need to warn, summon, or count, they drop a heel flat against the wet basalt and strike crisp, complex cadences directly into the stone. Because there is no soil or gravel anywhere on the shelf to muffle the tremor, a pattern tapped near the back wall arrives cleanly at a bare foot fifty paces out toward the drop-off. Up close—inside their shelters, or huddled shoulder-to-shoulder in the fog—they press forehead to forehead or knuckle to collarbone and hum low, buzzy chords through closed teeth, letting bone carry what the roaring air would tear apart. Only when they must reach someone perched out under the jagged cliff lip, cut off by a deep fracture, do they unhook paired dorsal plates harvested from the oldest bell-crabs, notched along the edges so their pitch sits cleanly in the narrow gap between the wild crab-chorus and the organ-drone of the updraft. To an outsider, it sounds like one stubborn, slow-clacking crab. To them, it means *fog thinning*, or *wings low over the hoop*.

Every fifteen minutes, the fog buys them a brief, frantic lease on the open floor, and they spend it without wasting a breath.

As soon as the winged hunters vanish upward into the warm ceiling, the shelf’s tenants pour out in silent, rehearsed teams. Nothing they do is casual; every task is timed to the precise span before the cold shear slices back over the rim.

Two or three slide swiftly toward the center of the terrace, where the matte chalk-white hoop floats dry above its trembling dome of water. They treat the hoop with practical reverence and an edge of dread. No one touches its pale surface—there is a story tapped into the stone of an elder whose forefinger once brushed it during a slip, and came away white and powdery forever after, incapable of sweating, bleeding, or bending. Instead, they work inside the invisible two-inch halo where condensing mist refuses to settle. On curved hooks of wing-bone, they hang small woven pouches of salted ribbon-drifter flesh, damp strips of hound-sinew, and fresh drifter-membranes just inside that dry envelope, retrieving whatever finished drying since the last fog-pulse. In a world where there is no wood, no fuel, and no fire, that thin dry margin around the hovering ring is their hearth.

While one tends the hanging pouches, another kneels by the gravity-defying mound of water beneath the hoop, batting away frantic knots of pale ribbon-drifters with a flat paddle of crab-shell as they scoop the thick, shimmering water into hollowed silicate bowls. Dissolved minerals concentrate abnormally heavy in that trembling dome; they use the slurry to cure hides, to salt their meat, and to thicken the mineral plates on their knees and hands.

At the same time, two others crouch near the three blackened tripod stakes closer to the cliff lip. They stay clear of the braided, hair-fine legs—getting too close makes the fillings of the jaw ache and leaves a metallic taste on the tongue—and work instead along the margins of the refracted crystal grid etched into the floor. Elsewhere across the basin, uncured basalt-glass shatters in wild, curved splinters when struck. Inside the turned lattice, fresh seams cleave cleanly along straight geometric planes, flaking off like dark pages. With swift, practiced taps of bone hammers, the knappers pry up fresh spear-points, scrapers, and narrow chiseling blades before the stone cures too hard to work.

Others still creep along the newly opened fissures near the runoff troughs, where pale, blind worm-grazers have unfurled their fan-shaped gills into the rushing water. The dwellers never kill them; the grazers scrub away a chalky mineral scum that would otherwise sour the shallow pools. Instead, they brush the delicate fans lightly with tufts of damp copper-green felt-moss, lifting off smears of faint blue-white mucus that glows dimly for several hours. On their way back toward the walls, they dab tiny pinpricks of that cold light beside the thresholds of their shelters and along the slickest drop-offs.

When the air pressure begins to tighten again—their clamped ear-membranes feel the shift seconds before the mist even thins—the warning knock runs sharp and rapid through the floor. *Clear coming. Back to the ribs.*

If someone is caught too far out when the cold shear rips the fog away into the void, they do not run. Running on open glass beneath the bruised sky draws a sky-predator down like a falling anvil. Instead, the stranded figure drops instantly flat into the nearest angular water trough, pulls the pale drifter-membrane cloak taut over their mineral-plated back so the film of runoff sheets smoothly across them, snaps their ear-slits shut, and freezes. Even when a broad leathery shadow blots out the dim ceiling and the thunderous clap of trapped air drives the breath from their lungs twenty paces away, they do not twitch until the wings bank back out over the abyss and the bell-crabs dare to begin clicking again.

With the glass-hounds along the sheer back wall, the truce is colder and far more intimate. Both share the same margins; both hunt the same mist-thickened corridors. The dwellers line the mouths of their shelters with a paste of crushed copper-moss and sharp iron-brine that stings the hounds’ sensitive acoustic funnels, and they string hair-fine cords twisted from vibrating reeds across the narrow passages between stone ribs, hung with loose chips of bell-crab shell that rattle at a touch and ruin a pack’s silent glide. Now and then, when winter-cold shears blow harder or a pack grows bold enough to stalk a child near the plinth, four or five adults turn the hunters into meat. They herd a hound into a stepped pocket along the wall, wait until it locks its funnels forward, and slam two thick, cured silicate plates together inches from its skull. The percussive shock blinds its hearing for three vital heartbeats—long enough for aligned-lattice blades from the tripod quarry to finish the work. Nothing is wasted: the wet slate-colored hide becomes hydroplaning foot-pads and warm bedding, the meat is cured in hoop-brine, and the long acoustic cartilage is scraped hollow into listening-horns that let a sentry press an ear to the wall and hear deep rock stresses groaning hours before a seam splits.

Their shelters are the cleverest thing about them, and the reason a stranger could walk the entire perimeter and swear the rock was solid.

On a mountain that is still being cast, ordinary building is useless. Leave a wooden hut—even if wood existed here—and within a season the floor would push up razor seams through its middle; leave a stone wall, and the thickening cliffs would swallow it into seamless basalt. You cannot occupy rock that is still growing. You have to train it.

Near the sheer back wall, flanking the low plinth of slowly turning metallic leaves where the stone visibly thickens over an afternoon, the dwellers grow their homes like cavities cast around a core. They lash together low, arched frames from the curved wing-bones of fallen sky-predators and braid them tight with tough reed-cord, smearing the entire armature thickly with greasy fat rendered from ribbon-drifters. Wedged snugly into the right-angle creases where the mineral brine sweats heaviest, the greased arch resists the bonding stone. Over hundreds of fog-pulses, dark basalt-glass creeps up and over the frame in glossy, layered folds until a seamless hood of newborn mountain encloses it. Then they cut the bindings, drag the oiled bones out through the narrow mouth, and inherit a curved, narrow-throated alcove that looks from ten paces away like any other fold in the cathedral ribs.

The work never really ends. A grown room tries slowly to heal shut, like a puncture in living skin. Every dozen fog-pulses, someone crouches at the entrance with a heavy chisel from the tripod grid, chipping back the fresh millimeter of gleaming lip and sweeping the razor splinters out into the runoff.

Inside those alcoves, the deafening basin drops away to a deep, muted hum. The walls stay faintly warm to the palm, still curing behind their glossy skins. Floors are padded thick against fresh edges with laboriously stitched mats of copper-green felt-moss backed by layered hound-hide. Bundles of dried reeds, notched crab-clackers, spare bone-awls, and glowing moss-daubs hang from natural mineral nodules left deliberately unchipped along the ceiling. When a child is born in one of these warm hollows, the mother presses a tiny sliver of moss-stained bone into a fresh, weeping seam near the back of the room. Nobody counts years here, where the sky is always late afternoon; you know a child’s age by how much of the bone remains sticking out of the thickening basalt. By the time the rock closes smoothly over the tip, the child is old enough to walk out into the fog alone.

They keep their dead the same way, only deeper. When one of them falls to a hound, breaks on a fresh seam, or simply grows so thick with mineral crust along the spine and knees that they can no longer crouch fast enough for the floor, the household does not cast them off the rim into the clouds. They wash the body in warm runoff, wrap it in its own drifter-membrane cloak, and wedge it cleanly into a deep stepped hollow directly behind the plinth of metallic leaves. Over the course of a season, the refractive shimmer of the plinth thickens the dark stone around the niche, folding smooth basalt-glass over the bones until nothing remains outside but a subtle swell in the cliff face. Their ancestors are not buried beneath the mountain; they are cast into its ribs. Whenever a long, resonant groan vibrates up through the shelf as some deep internal stress settles itself, the children press their bare heels flat to the floor and listen, believing it is the old ones shifting their weight inside the stone.

Yet there is a quiet restlessness moving through the alcoves lately, tapped softly from heel to heel when the fog is thick and the young are asleep.

Time here has always been measured in the steady, loud generosity of an unfinished world—the fifteen-minute turnings of the fog, the reliable thumb-widths of rising glass, the warm iron-brine sweating without pause. The oldest among them, whose knuckles are thick as knuckles of quartz, remember when the shelf was narrower by twenty paces, when the hovering hoop hung perilously close to the abyss, and when the rock cracked twice as often because it was younger and fiercer.

Lately, the plinth’s metallic leaves seem to turn a fraction more reluctantly. Behind it, the new terraces thicken a hair thinner over an afternoon than memory insists they should. And just a few fog-pulses ago, while scraping back the throat of the deepest alcove along the north rib, one of the knappers found something lodged in a dry crease beneath the felt-moss—not a clean splinter of basalt-glass, not a flake of crab-shell, but a dull pinch of fine gray grit, rubbed round by nothing anyone could name. They passed it from palm to four-fingered palm in silent jaw-humming awe. None of them had ever seen sand before.

They know, in the unsentimental way of creatures who live by reading stone and pressure, what the first grain of sand means. The pins will not hold the sky open forever. Someday—perhaps not in this generation, perhaps in the next—the white hoop will drop into its pool and get wet. The leaf-plinth will lock motionless. The warm updraft will fail, the high cold air will pour down over the rim, and the basin will go quiet, brittle, and old. Their bodies, built for iron-brine and warm mist, will not survive an old mountain.

Out under the jagged outer lip, wedged into natural ledges where the roaring updraft blasts warm spray continuously against their chests, two lookouts always keep watch. Most of the time, there is nothing below or beyond but endless bruised pewter cloud. Once in a great while, though, a freak cold shear slices far deeper than usual, tearing a brief, ragged corridor across the high gulf. Through that rent, miles out across the empty drop at the same impossible altitude, the lookouts sometimes glimpse another low, pulsing smudge of warm fog that has no business existing in the freezing sky—a faint, distant pocket where another set of pins is coaxing fresh geology out of nothing.

They do not speak of leaving yet. The shelf is still warm; the crabs still clack in rolling waves; the seams still wink pale blue as they split the floor. Still, in the dry quiet at the backs of three of the grown alcoves, tucked beneath the stitched moss-mats where the hounds cannot smell them and the children do not play, the sharpest craftspeople have begun setting aside the longest, lightest wing-bones from fallen sky-predators and curing wide, seamless sheets of drifter-membrane inside the hoop’s dry halo. They lash the struts together during the long waits of the Clear, testing their flex against the warm air with patient, mineral-crusted hands—waiting, not in panic, only in readiness, for the afternoon the rock beneath their heels finally stops cracking and learns how to be old.
