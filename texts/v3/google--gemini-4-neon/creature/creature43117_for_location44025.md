In the dim pewter alleys, before you can make out a face or tell whether a figure climbing the wet basalt stairs is young or old, you see their hands.

They flash pale against the light-drinking black stone. Every wrist, palm, and knuckle in the basin is dusted with chalk-white sinter scraped from the drainage sluices and ground fine between blocks of pumice—or bound with narrow bands of bleached bird-skin and flat, pierced beads of white shell. Against the oiled dark hides and the slick, wet-gleaming basalt, those pale hands move like swiftlets under an arch, cutting sharp, legible shapes straight through the warm mist. A woman squatting beside a steaming runnel flicks her fingers toward a stone parapet three stories up; a mason wedging shell-shims into a wall pauses, wipes his wet palm down his hip, and answers in six crisp, chopping strokes without lifting his chin. Even an infant slung in a pouch of greased seal-hide has sinter-chalk smeared across its tiny wrists, already imitating the quick tilt and snap of its mother’s thumb long before it can balance on slick stone.

When they step closer, emerging out of the heavy warmth and the low amber glow of the doorway lamps, you see how thoroughly the basin has shaped the bodies that inhabit it.

They are compact, dense-limbed people, broad through the shoulders and forearms from a lifetime of hauling raw basalt, woven quill-baskets, and wet sinew nets up stairways with no wheels or beasts of burden to share the weight. Because no sun has ever broken the overcast above them, their skin is pale—olive-gray or bone-ivory beneath the dim twilight—yet it is never dry or chalky. Every morning, after washing in the warm mineral seepage, they rub rendered fish-oil and seal-grease over their arms, necks, and faces to guard against the constant salt-damp, the caustic sting of bird guano, and the microscopic glass-dust that rises wherever obsidian is worked. They gleam faintly in the alley-light, slick as wet cobbles, smelling of clean oil, brine, and warm stone.

Their eyes are wide and dark-irised, almost untouched by squint-lines; without glare or shadow to narrow them, they open full to drink the low light, trained to read a flicker of white-dusted fingers fifty paces down a terraced lane or spot the dull sheen of an oily fish-fin in a dark tidal weir. Their hair is cropped close with fresh obsidian flakes or braided tight and greased flat against the skull so it will never tangle in a net, snag on raw scoria, or offer a grip to a snapping cormorant.

Look down at their feet, and you see their truce with the infant rock. Bare skin would be ribbons after twenty yards of glassy pahoehoe and needle-sharp basalt. Every person in the city walks on heavy, clumsy-looking wraps built of layered seal and whale hide, lashed high around the calves with braided sinew and reinforced along the soles with stitched pads of tough belly-hide and flat, drilled slats of bone. The volcanic rock chews through those treads steadily; you see people sitting on every flat step with bone awls and rawhide thongs, lacing fresh patches onto their soles while they chat with their free hands.

Their voices are not absent—only folded inward, reserved for closeness and for the sheltered hush behind the hide curtains. Out in the open tiers, the wall of sound makes ordinary speech useless, so the hands carry almost the whole weight of public life. It is not a clipped code of trades and warnings, but a full, restless, richly idiomatic language that uses the fingers, wrists, elbows, and the tilt of the collarbone. There are dialects by altitude and quarter: the wide, sweeping arm-signs of the outer seawall, designed to punch through thicker harbor mist; the tight, wrist-only private signs flicked low against the thigh in crowded lanes when two neighbors are trading gossip or bargaining over a jar of rendered oil; the rapid, joking shorthand of children darting between the legs of haulers.

And when a gesture is not enough—when something must be insisted upon, confessed, or shouted over a sudden crisis—two people step straight into each other’s space until their chests nearly touch. One lays a broad, steady palm across the other’s collarbone, thumb resting near the hollow of the throat, and pitches a tight, barking stream of syllables straight at the ear. They do not only listen with their ears, many of which grow dull to high pitches by middle age from the endless shrieking of the towers and the pounding of the caves. They listen through bone. A palm on the clavicle lets you feel the buzz and catch of a neighbor’s breath as clearly as the words; soles pressed to wet stone tell you if the surge in the lava-tubes beyond the shelf has shifted its beat by a fraction of a second. Even two people quarreling bitterly over a cracked jar or a stolen egg stand heel-to-heel, holding each other by the shoulder-bone like kin or wrestlers.

To watch the city work is to realize how differently a human world arranges itself when there is not a single stick of timber, not a root, not a grain of soil, and no open fire anywhere in miles.

There is no wood-smoke in the air, no charcoal smudge on any wall, no hearths roaring behind stone chimneys. Heat here comes up out of the earth, and light comes from fat. Along the lower lanes and in the broad communal yards, the steam-tenders work over deep, narrow wells cut straight into the hot bedrock, capped with heavy, pitted slabs of basalt that take two workers to lever aside. When a slab shifts, a thick column of mineral vapor rolls out, smelling faintly of iron and wet flint. Into these scalding pits, cooks lower stitched seal-stomachs and oiled bladders packed with fish-roe, chopped squid, crushed shore-crab, gull eggs, and scraped pale lintel-fungi—poaching them into dense, savory broths and gelatinous cakes without ever striking a spark. Nearby, in the low, heat-drenched grease-houses, thick carved basalt vats sit wedged directly over the hottest fumarole vents, slowly rendering blubber and oily fish-heads into clear amber liquid. The renderers—broad-armed, gleaming from elbow to brow with grease—skim the pots with wide mussel-shell ladles, pouring oil into clay jars that feed every down-wick lamp and coat every skin in the city.

Out in the open stone plazas, the percussion of the knappers cuts neatly through the basin’s roar. They sit cross-legged on thick folded hides with heavy aprons of stiff rawhide draped across their thighs, striking glassy black obsidian cores with dense basalt hammerstones and shaping delicate edges with pressure-flakers made of whalebone tipped with cold-hammered native copper. Their forearms and shins are pale maps of hair-thin white scars. Volcanic glass fractures to an edge far keener than iron, and it chips just as easily on bone or grit; these people live in a world of daily, disposable sharpness. A fish-gutter may flake three fresh blades before the tide turns, tossing the dulled glass back into baskets to be ground down or wedged as grit into wall-joints. Beside the knappers sit the grinders, rocking pale sea-mammal ribs and hollow seabird leg-bones across blocks of rough gray pumice until they yield sewing needles, barbless fishhooks, hair-pins, and shrill, finger-long bone whistles—the one instrument in the basin sharp enough to slice clean across the bird-roar when a wall slips or a child tumbles into a hot seepage-pit.

High above the plazas, clinging to the whitewashed flanks of the rookery-towers, move the tower-keepers. They are leaner and longer-limbed than the haulers below, with knotted fingers and forearms crosshatched with hook-shaped white scars from cormorant beaks and shearwater claws. They climb the honeycombed masonry using recessed basalt toe-holds, wearing thick hide gauntlets and narrow, curved visors of slit whalebone lashed across their eyes—not against sunlight, which never comes, but against snapping bills and the blinding spray of guano. They do not raid the birds; they steward them with an unsentimental, intimate precision. Reaching blind into the dark, reeking stone pockets, a keeper feels by touch alone whether a nest holds three eggs or one, lifting a single speckled egg while leaving the clutch unbroken, clearing sodden rot from choked niches, plucking loose molted down for lamp-wicks and stiff primary quills for basket-weavers. Each household knows the shifting moods of the birds nested six feet above its ceiling—by the weight of the flapping on the parapet, by the pitch of the quarrels vibrating through the corbeled stone, by the smell drifting down the ventilation chinks. Sometimes a notoriously fierce, crooked-beaked cliff-gull will hold the same corner ledge for ten years, and the family below will flash its private sign-name whenever it drops down to snatch trimmings from their fish-board.

Down at the lowest, loudest edge of the city—along the raw basalt lip where the ocean hammers straight into the sea-caves—work the weir-tenders and coracle-folk. Here the pressure hits so hard your ribs hum and your molars ache. Wrapped in double layers of glistening dark seal-pelt, smelling of deep upwelling brine and crushed krill, they wade waist-deep along the flooded stone weirs, hauling up knotted sinew nets heavy with silver shoal-fish, pulsing dark squid, and clinging beds of shellfish. Beyond the weirs, they pole flat-bottomed coracles ribbed with curved jawbone through the dim mist. Many of the oldest lip-walkers have lost the last of their ear-hearing altogether; they navigate the dark swells by the heave of the basalt beneath their knees and the tug of current up the woodenless bone shafts of their poles, conversing in huge, slow shoulder-signs and gripping each other’s forearms whenever they pass on the slick quays.

And everywhere between the high towers and the wet quays crawl the sluice-chippers and stone-wedgers, the quiet guardians who keep the city from hardening into a solid tomb. Because the warm seepage never stops running, the brittle chalk-white and ochre sinter never stops building. Left untended, a drainage runnel chokes shut within a few years, sending scalding mineral runoff spilling over low stone thresholds. So teams of lime-dusted workers move perpetually through the alleys with dense basalt picks and bone chisels, flaking the sharp crystalline rims off the channels—saving the cleanest white crust to grind into hand-chalk and hide-scouring powder—while masons drag identical, newly split slabs from the inland quarry-benches to wedge into any wall that settles.

Living alongside them—woven so tight into their routines that it makes little sense to call them either wild or tame—are the basin’s other citizens.

The lean, short-haired scavenging dogs rarely bark; out in the alleys, a bark dies two feet from the snout. Over generations, they have become eerily attentive readers of the human visual world. Slick-coated, smelling of fish-oil and damp hide, their ears small and often notched from crab-pinches or gull-pecks, they trot at a mason’s heel with eyes fixed on his chalked wrist. A flick of two fingers sends a dog darting down a slick stair to flush a bold gull off a drying-rack; a flat palm pressed downward curls it quietly around the warm basalt rim of a steam-well, where it snaps brine-flies lazily from the still air and sleeps with its chin tucked over a child’s wrapped foot.

Even the pale-bellied frogs packed into the wet crevices between the paving slabs are treated as living instruments of the city. Nobody crushes them; when a mason pries out a loose basalt block, he scoops the small slick bodies carefully into his palm and sets them back once the new stone is wedged. The high, relentless trill of the frogs sits neatly in the band of sound between the bass cave-boom and the upper bird-shriek, and people listen to it without knowing they are listening. If a stretch of alley-wall suddenly goes quiet—if the frogs pull deep into the cracks or abandon a runnel—every householder along that lane stops mid-gesture. Silence in the crevices means the geothermal seepage has cooled, or shifted course, or turned sour with deep volcanic gas. The frogs know the bedrock before the masons do.

Step past the heavy double curtain of oiled seal-hide hanging across a low stone doorway, and you understand what these people crave when the workday eases.

Inside, the roar drops by half—never to stillness, only to a thick, muffled subterranean rumble that wraps the room like warm water. The air is closer still, fragrant with rendered fish-oil, dry bone, and warm wet rock. Tiny carved basalt saucers hold glowing wicks of braided bird-down floating in clear yellow oil, casting a steady amber wash across stacked clay jars, racks of brine-cured fish, and corbeled walls that weep an unbroken sheen of condensation.

Here, behind the hides, you discover their quiet obsession with smoothness.

Out in the basin, nothing natural is smooth. The bedrock is infant glass and needle-edged scoria; the sinter crusts grow in brittle, crystalline knives; the obsidian tools bite to the bone; even the sound has a raw, bruising grain. There are no wave-tumbled pebbles here, no wind-sanded driftwood, no soft moss or turf. Smoothness does not exist until human hands make it—slowly, patiently, across decades. So inside the dwellings, everything cherished is worn round. A drinking-scoop carved from a whale’s shoulder-blade and polished glossy by eighty years of lips and oiled thumbs. A stone hammerhead whose grip has been satin-smoothed by four generations of sweat and grease. A pale, drilled knuckle-bead hanging on a sinew cord against a throat. Even the copper wrist-rings, dull green when neglected, are rubbed bright and rounded against greased palms until they catch the orange wick-light.

In the amber dimness, the rapid, sharp-edged gestures of the alleys slow down. Family members sit on coiled mats of thick seal-hide, oiling each other’s salt-roughened shoulders with long, flat-palmed strokes—taking a quiet, physical comfort in skin that has no sharp edge. When they want music, they do not try to fight the sea-caves with thin, bright tunes. Instead, they lean their backs against the warm, weeping masonry or sit shoulder-to-shoulder in a tight ring and make *rib-song*: deep, closed-mouth hums and throat-drones pitched a hair above or below the constant subterranean boom. When three or four voices catch the right interval against the cave-surge, the whole corbeled vault begins to pulse in slow, heavy waves you feel in your teeth and behind your breastbone.

Elders tell histories here not by straining their voices, which tire quickly in the barked pitch, but by holding their chalk-dusted hands close to an oil-saucer and letting their fingers move in a slow, flowing shadow-dance—wrists becoming shearwaters banking through mist, thumbs lifting like the gentle tide in the weirs, knuckles rolling like a shifting retaining wall. Young children fall asleep with one cheek pressed to a warm hide mat and a small palm resting lightly on a grandmother’s moving forearm, reading the end of the story through skin and tendon after their eyes have closed.

Perhaps the deepest strangeness of the people who live here—though to them it feels like the only sane way a world could hold together—is how they inhabit time.

No winter ever arrives to test their stores; no spring ever breaks the sky open; no drought dries the bacterial slime in the runnels; no wind tears a hide from its whale-rib frame. The seabirds hatch in unbroken, overlapping relays so that there is never a week without eggs, never a week without screaming fledglings. Without seasons to chop the years into neat, returning pieces, people measure duration by maintenance and slow accumulation. A child is not six winters old; she is two roof-hides old—the span it takes for a well-oiled seal-hide roof-sheet to rot through in the endless damp and need stitching anew over the same pale whale-ribs. An old feud began "a thumb-width of sinter ago," measured by the thickening chalk crust along a communal sluice. A mason’s career is counted by the pumice grinding-blocks worn down from thick loaves to concave crescents no thicker than a shell.

Because nothing erodes into soil and no weeds ever creep over an empty stone threshold, the past does not soften or disappear here. A house emptied of one family stands sharp-edged and wet until another family drags up fresh hides and lights the oil-saucers. People walk every day on the exact, unrounded slabs laid by ancestors whose sign-names are no longer remembered, stepping over the same weeping seepage, listening to the same unchanging note in the sea-caves. If you tried to describe to them a place where the sky burned empty and blue, where wind howled and trees grew tall and then shed their leaves in bitter cold, they would tilt their heads and flick the sign for *broken* or *unsteady*. To them, silence feels like falling upward; open sky feels like standing without a roof in a quarry-slide.

Even their dead belong to this closed, steady loop. There is no earth in the basin deep enough to dig a grave, and no timber to build a pyre. When an elder’s breath finally goes still inside a warm vault, their kin wash the body in mineral water from the street sluice, slip the polished copper rings and smooth bone beads from the wrists and neck, and stitch the body tightly into its oldest, softest sleeping-hide. Two slabs of dense, unpitted basalt are lashed cleanly to the ankles.

At the dimmest slate-dusk turn of the sky, when the pewter ceiling weighs lowest and the tide eases out through the stone weirs, four kinfolk carry the wrapped weight down the wet stairways and lay it in a jawbone coracle. They pole out past the steaming quays, past the outer basalt seawall where the cavern-pipes roar loudest, out into the warm mist above the dark upwelling where the deep cold and warm currents churn together. There are no spoken prayers over the thunder. The family lifts their chalk-pale hands together, flashing the dead person’s sign-name once—slow, high, and wide—before sliding the weighted hide over the gunwale. It sinks straight down into the unseen richness that feeds the shoals, the crabs, the birds, and the weirs. Nothing piles up on the raw rock; nothing is wasted.

Back in the alleys, the dead do not vanish cleanly, either. Years later, a child born into the same corbeled vault may inherit that exact three-motion sign-name, so that the same bright flash of white-dusted fingers continues darting across the dim, wet stairs between the rookery-towers—just as the next oiled hide takes its place on the old whale-ribs, just as the next frog wedges itself into the warm wet chink of the stone, holding its note inside the basin’s unending, warm, deafening breath.
