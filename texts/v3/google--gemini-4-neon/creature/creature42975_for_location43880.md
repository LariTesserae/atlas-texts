You will stand in the chamber for twenty minutes, perhaps an hour, convinced that nothing lives here except the moss, the ferns, and the pale hanging vines.

Your eyes are trained to look for the kinds of life that leave smudges behind them: chewed edges on a frond, a slick of oil on a handrail, droppings in a gutter, the hurried scurry of small weight across wet leaves. Here there is none of it. The air holds only the clean scent of bruised rock, warm vapor, and wet green. The walkways remain bare and sharp-cornered. Even when the chamber takes its slow, roaring breath every four or five minutes, you see only water, mist, and swaying plants.

The first sign is not something you see. It is a discrepancy in the sound.

Listen closely during the thirty seconds after the warm vapor strikes the cold ceiling vault—when the roar doubles and the walkways brim over in bright steps. Beneath the heavy drumming on the thin decks and the deep splashing in the basin, you hear those sharp crystalline *pings* that seem to be newly cut stone and matte alloy adjusting to the shift in warmth. Most of them are. Wait through three or four cycles, though, and you will notice that a few of those pings do not happen during the cooling or the warming. They happen half a second *before* the pressure drops. And once in a while, a ping from the high eastern cornice is answered—a quarter-beat later, at a slightly lower interval—from the dark underside of the third tier across the shaft.

Once you hear the answer, you stop looking at the tops of the walkways and start looking beneath them.

Kneel on the damp moss of a lower tier and peer up at the dry, warm underside of the deck projecting overhead. At first it appears seamless, a single matte-slate plane rimmed with its thread-thin greenish-white light. Look closer, near the hairline three-millimeter gap where the deck locks invisibly into the bedrock. There is a section of the underside where the matte gray is a shade darker, slightly velvety, edged with an almost imperceptible bevel. You might take it for an inspection plate or a double-thickness baffle—until the low thrum of the next thermal exchange begins to climb through the soles of your feet.

Before the submerged amber brightens, before your ears even pop with the falling pressure, the bevel lifts.

A clean seam opens between the hovering deck and the darker shape pressed flush against it. It does not drop clumsily like a bat loosing its claws. It peels away in a single, unhurried slide, gliding outward toward the glowing lip of the walkway—thin as a slate shingle, broad as a spread cloak—and slips off the edge into the hollow center of the vault just as the warm vapor plume begins to lift off the black water below.

Around the perimeter of the shaft, beneath the fourth tier, the sixth, the narrow recessed landings near the ceiling, others are doing the same. Six of them. Then twelve. Then twenty, emerging out of the dry architectural shadow as if the walkways themselves were shedding skins.

In the rising column of bathhouse-warm steam, they cease to look like plates and begin to look like what they are.

They are built in planes rather than bulk—tall, articulated, impossibly spare creatures whose bodies seem to have been drafted by the same strict geometry that cut the chamber. Each consists of a slender, flexible central axis, no thicker than a human wrist and perhaps two meters long, flanked by four or six broad, hinged vanes that fold over one another like overlapping leaves of slate. When closed flat against a deck, their outer surfaces are matte dark anthracite, water-shedding and dry, nearly indistinguishable from the urban alloy. When they open into the rising thermal, they reveal what stays hidden at rest: inner surfaces pale as wet quartz cleavage, finely fluted from spine to margin like the underside of a gill-mushroom, catching the dim greenish-white edge-lights and the dull floor-amber in shifting, pearlescent bands.

They do not beat the air. They have no frantic wing-stroke, no labored muscular heaviness. They are light in a way that feels almost architectural—hollow-ribbed, strung with taut, drum-thin membranes and buoyant channels that drink the rising heat. Turning their vanes by fractions of a degree, they bank and spiral upward through the seventy-pace hollow, riding the warm plume past the moss-heavy galleries in slow, intersecting circles.

Watch their margins as they pass through the drifting spore-clouds shaken loose from the undersides of the mosses. The fine pale flutes along their inner vanes dampen and glisten, combing the warm aerosol—spores, dissolved silica, sharp microscopic mineral dust washed from the newly sheared rock—straight out of the vapor. Nothing here needs to bite a fern or strip a vine, because the chamber itself exhales everything they eat into the air every five minutes. Their metabolism is as clean and mineral-bound as the room: water, spores, fresh rock-salt, and heat. They shed no dander, ooze no oil, carry no gut-rot. When one glides past your face two meters out over the drop, the air it stirs smells only of cold wet quartz and clean steam.

As the warm plume strikes the ceiling vault and condenses into the thirty-second deluge, they do not scatter for shelter. That violent percussion is the brightest moment of their world.

They have no eyes. Where a head might sit—high along the narrow central spine, tucked beneath a small forward cowl of folded slate—there is instead an asymmetrical fan of hair-thin, crystalline tines, pale and translucent as spun quartz. Those tines tremble constantly, reading the air. To them, this subterranean cathedral is not in permanent dusk at all; it is blindingly legible, lit from wall to wall by sound. Every drop striking the black basin, every sheet of water tearing off the upper ribs, every crisp rebound off freshly sheared basalt paints the seventy-pace room in high relief.

As the roar doubles, they answer it. Flexing the articulated mineral plates along their spines, they loose those clean, glass-clear *pings*—high, precise tones pitched so neatly into the room's own acoustics that an untrained listener mistakes them for settling stone. They call across the falling curtains of rain, not merely to locate one another, but to measure the vault, to test the tension of the air, to braid their voices into the chamber's thirty-second storm. Sometimes three or four of them align vertically in the rising turbulence, matching pitches until a brief, shimmering harmonic hangs audible even over the hammering decks—a chord that feels less like song than like the stone itself suddenly remembering a bell.

Then the deep thrum eases beneath the floor. The amber glow dims back to dull coal. The torrent thins to its steady, heavy baseline.

Most of them spiral smoothly back toward the shelter of the hovering tiers, folding their pale fluted interiors shut at the last instant and sliding flat against the warm, dry undersides like letters slipping into envelopes.

Not all of them, though.

Some descend all the way to the lowest landing, where the mirror-black water lies shallow over the thermal slabs. Watch the center of the basin after the mist clears, and you will see them standing in the dark water. They do not swim, and they do not wade on legs. They balance upright on the slender, knife-edged lower keels of their spines, slicing the surface so cleanly that they can glide across the flooded floor without raising a splash, leaving wakes that close back into glass before they can break the rebounding ripples of the dripping ferns.

Here, down at the lowest accessible level, you can see them closely enough to realize they are not merely strange fauna drifting on a draft. There is deliberation in their stillness, and patience in their company.

Two of them stand three paces apart in the shallow water, folded half-shut so they resemble tall, narrow plinths of wet slate leaning slightly toward one another. Between them, the black mirror of the floor-water is not quite smooth; a tight, trembling grid of tiny standing waves shimmers on the surface, holding its shape for ten seconds, twenty, before shifting into a new geometric lattice. They are speaking through the water column—trading slow, sub-audible tremors down through their submerged keels, using the shallow basin and the warm submerged slabs beneath it as a sounding board.

Near the base of the wide stairless ramp, another moves up out of the water. It does not walk; it lowers its outer vanes until their smooth, water-shedding lower edges skim the thin wet film coating the stone, gliding smoothly up the incline with the unhurried poise of a figure on ice. When it reaches the sealed dark mouth of a service tunnel, it pauses before the flat mineral sluice. There are no handles, no sensors you can see. The creature simply leans its pale-seamed spine within millimeters of the closed rock and emits a low, dry, multi-toned hum—the exact twin of the faint vibration that lives inside the three-millimeter gaps behind the hovering decks.

Without a click or a scrape, the seamless mineral edges slide apart. The dweller folds narrow, glides into the dark tunnel beyond, and the rock flows shut and re-fuses behind it without a seam.

Only then do you begin to recognize the subtle signs of their stewardship—the things you looked straight past because you were looking for clutter.

Walk into one of the recessed service alleys along the third or fourth tier, behind the sheltering black-green shade ferns, and look down into the dry corners where the silt does not wash. There, propped neatly against the freshly cut basalt, are small, deliberate arrangements of flat quartz shards and angular stone chips—three here, five there, leaned at exact angles against one another like tiny stone screens. They are not nests. They are acoustic baffles. Whenever a newly cut wall-channel creates a muddy echo, or a sluice-aperture develops a faint, distracting chatter during the thermal breath, the dwellers gather freshly cleaved fragments from the sharp-grained gutters and wedge them into the corners until the room's return rings clean again.

Look, too, at the smooth pale silt in the drainage channels along the bare ramps: here and there, untouched by moss, runs a single, hairline crescent groove—not a footprint, just the faint cursive mark of a lower keel banking gently around a junction landing.

And if you remain still long enough—seated quietly on the wet basalt of the lowest landing while the water drips in its steady metronome from the hanging ferns—eventually one of them will notice you.

It happens between breaths, when the chamber is at its steadiest baseline roar. One of the upright figures gliding across the black floor-water slows. Its folded dark planes pivot smoothly, turning its narrow spine toward the landing. Beneath its small slate cowl, the delicate fan of pale quartz tines opens a fraction wider, angling directly at your chest.

To its acoustic sight, you must be an astonishing anomaly. Everything else in this chamber is either crisp, unweathered rock, taut matte alloy, smooth water, or clean wet leaf. You are none of those. You are soft, uneven, muffled in fabric, heavy with warm meat and slow-moving blood—an acoustic dead-zone that swallows the sharp echoes of the vault and gives back only a dull, blurry thud.

It glides closer, stopping two paces out in the shallow water, just where the amber floor-glow catches the milky silica seams along its hinged flanks. For a long moment it holds completely still. Then it sends out a single, delicate crystalline *ping*—so clear you feel it behind your teeth—bouncing the note directly off your sternum.

It listens to the muddy return. It listens to the faint, wet double-beat inside your ribs.

Slowly, one of its tall outer vanes opens just a hand's breadth, revealing for a heartbeat the pale, finely grooved interior shimmering faintly with green-white edge-light, as if offering a look at its own clear geometry in exchange for your strange opacity. Then it folds shut, turns with effortless grace along its knife-thin keel, and glides back toward the center of the basin, leaving you with the quiet impression that you have not been judged as a threat or a curiosity, only noted—like a soft, damp stone placed unexpectedly in the middle of a finely tuned instrument.

To see what they love most about this place, though, you have to watch the falling water—and look very closely beneath the highest tiers.

Halfway down the seventy-pace hollow, where the glass-smooth curtains of black water drop thirty meters from the upper cornice and then strike that invisible mid-air shear to shatter all at once into heavy rain, the smaller, younger dwellers play. They are half the height of the elders, thinner, their outer slate still pale and semi-translucent like smoked mica. Between thermal breaths, they peel off the upper undersides and drop almost vertically alongside the black glass ribbons of falling water, riding the razor-thin boundary of downward-dragged air inches from the wet sheen. They fall with frightening speed—straight down toward the roaring white fracture line—and at the exact unmarked altitude of the shear, they snap their pale inner vanes wide. The violent lateral snap of the shattering rain catches them like a sudden gust and whips them sideways across the hollow, spinning them out into the calm, sheltered dark beneath the middle walkways, where they catch the warm undersides with a soft, ecstatic cascade of tiny glass-bright pings.

Higher still, pressed flush against the dry undersides of the sixth and seventh tiers, right alongside the humming three-millimeter rock-seams, lie the youngest of all.

You would need to press your cheek to the moss of the walkway below and stare until your eyes adjusted to see them: flat, translucent films no larger than a spread hand, adhered directly to the warm matte-slate alloy. They look like thin patches of drying mineral glaze, faintly veined with milky quartz threads. They do not move yet. For weeks, they simply cling beside the three-millimeter seam, drinking the steady hum of the deck's invisible lock and the warm, spore-rich drafts that curl under the glowing lip, slowly thickening, darkening on their outer face, growing their first microscopic tuning-tines until they can hear the full height of the room.

When one is finally ready to detach, it never does so alone.

As the barometric pressure drops and the thick plume of warm steam lifts off the basin floor, five or six full-grown dwellers rise early into the hollow core and hold station just beyond the glowing edge of the upper tier. They do not touch the fledgling. Instead, as the small, pale-gray vane shivers, lifts its edge from the warm alloy, and slips clumsily off the lip into the vast empty air for the first time, the elders bank around it in a wide, turning ring—releasing a steady, woven lattice of gentle pings. They are building it a map in real time: marking the walls, marking the cold wet sweep of the falling glass ribbons, marking the height of the shear below, holding the whole booming cathedral steady in sound while the newborn learns how to tilt its planes and ride the warm breath upward.

That, more than anything else, is why they are here—and why they will not stay forever.

They are creatures of the first wave, just like the fierce emerald moss and the unbitten ferns. They travel through the sealed dark tunnels and deep conduits of the unseen city beyond the walls, always listening for the resonant, bright signature of a newly cut shaft.

In older chambers—places that have breathed for decades or centuries—the sharp rock fractures eventually round off under runoff. Deep soil gathers along the hovering decks. Dead fronds accumulate and soften into dark humus; gnats and cave-crickets find their way down the optical channels; cobwebs blur the ninety-degree corners of the service alleys. Slowly, the air grows heavy with organic smell and suspended rot, and the crisp, bright acoustic return of the walls turns furry, dim, and muffled. In an old shaft, their pale fluted vanes clog with silt and decay, and the room goes dark to their quartz tines.

So whenever the city carves a fresh thermal cathedral out of clean basalt, they are the first citizens to arrive through the hingeless sluices—slipping into the roaring dusk while the fractures are still angular, the silt is still sharp-grained, and every splash comes back off the stone like a struck glass.

Right now, the chamber is at its brightest for them. Beneath the basin floor, the thermal slabs begin their quiet shift again. The submerged amber brightens by a single degree. The pressure drops, popping your ears, and all around the seven hovering tiers, dark matte-slate shapes silently unlatch from the dry undersides and open their pale, grooved wings into the rising steam—singing in crisp crystalline pings across the hollow air, making the most of the sharp edges and the clean rain before the moss creeps too far, before the stone softens, and before the cathedral grows old.
