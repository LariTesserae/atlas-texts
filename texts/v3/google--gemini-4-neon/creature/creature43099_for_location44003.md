*People* do not live on the roof of the mountains for a whole lifetime. The seasonal hunters and resin-tappers are right about that: stay too many months in the open glare and the roar wears something smooth inside your head, until silence feels like falling and sleep breaks into shallow, brittle pieces.

Yet if you walk the springy swells long enough—past the first week, when your ears stop ringing and begin instead to ache deep behind the hinges of your jaw—you start to notice small, patient discrepancies in the landscape. Things the plateau could not have made by itself.

A lone vent-stalk standing on the lip of a saucer-basin, for instance, whose wind-voice does not howl erratically as the afternoon updraft shifts, but holds a single, clean, hollow fifth that cuts straight underneath the insect rasp and lingers in your breastbone half a mile downwind. Look closer at its bleached fluted shaft and you will see that three of its oval pores have been neatly plugged with dark, resin-hardened peat, while a fourth has been beveled smooth as a flute’s embouchure. Wedged just inside the leeward lip, half-hidden by the twitching tails of roosting swifts, gleams a curved tongue of green-tarnished copper—cut from a bowl some hunter thought they had lost to the turf three summers ago.

Or walk across a wide swale after a ten-minute mist-band tears apart, while steam still lifts off the cushion-shrubs. Sometimes, thirty paces off the bone-marked path, a seam between two gnarled mounds closes a heartbeat too smoothly, the pale moss knitting back together with no rush of flushing ground-birds and no sudden silence from the stridulators.

To find whoever lives here, you cannot look up into the empty indigo sky, and you cannot look out across the brassy rolling miles. You have to go down—past the shallow three-foot night-pits the seasonal folk cut for their hide-roofed camps, past the dry, tangled upper lattice where the lizards flicker and the rodents hammer their warnings, all the way to the bottom of the fifteen-foot botanical roof.

Down there, the world changes its mind.

At twelve feet beneath the green-ochre crust, the ferocious sunlight narrows to faint, dusty needles that slant between braided woody pillars and die before they reach the floor. The air loses its scorched surface edge and settles into a steady, cellar-like warmth, heavy with the smell of old cedar-bark, wet thyme, and dark tannin. Most startling of all is what happens to the noise. Filtered through fifteen feet of compacted dead moss and fibrous lattice, the saw-toothed scream of the insects softens and recedes until it sounds like surf breaking over a distant reef. What comes through clearly is the bass: the slow hydraulic groan of wind drawing through the vent-stalks, the wet tick of condensed mist dripping from root to root, and the dull, rolling thunder of a grazer herd trotting a quarter-mile overhead.

And down here, for the first time in three days’ walk, there is stone.

It is the ancient, worn-smooth granite of the vanished peaks, ground flat as old river-flags before the living floor ever sealed it from the sky. In the shallow saucers of that bedrock lie still, tea-colored pools—dark mirrors that barely shiver when a flock of falcons screams past overhead.

If you crouched beside one of those dim pools and held your breath until your lungs burned in the thin air, you might look at a pale, gnarled root-buttress leaning against a stone shelf for five full minutes before realizing it was breathing.

Slowly. A single long expansion of a deep, narrow-waisted ribcage, held for twenty heartbeats, then let out in a thin, almost soundless sigh.

They are not human, though from a distance in a bank of mist—unfolding themselves beside a bleached vent-stalk—a startled hunter might mistake one for a starving, impossibly tall hermit draped in dried moss. Up close, in the cool shadow beneath the mat, the resemblance falls away.

They are built for two things that seem to contradict each other: squeezing through low, horizontal galleries of tangled wood, and drawing enough oxygen out of the ceiling of the sky to stay alive. Their chests are barrel-deep and long, tapering abruptly to supple, flattened waists and narrow hips, with limbs so spare and elongated they look almost unfinished until you see them move. Their arms hang past their knees, ending in wide, paddle-flat hands—not hooved, not clawed, but cushioned on the undersides with thick, split pads of pale, velvet-smooth skin. Their fingers are long, double-jointed at the middle knuckles, tipped with blunt, chisel-shaped nails worn bright as horn from years of stripping bark and teasing apart iron-tough stems.

Their skin is the color of dry peat and sun-scoured bone, dusted thinly across the back, forearms, and calves with fine silvery-ochre down. It is an uncanny match for the moisture-catching hairs on the surface foliage—and serves the same quiet purpose. When one lies motionless near the damp throat of a vent-stalk during a passing mist, dew beads across its shoulders in tiny silver drops, which it gathers cleanly with the flat of a palm or a long, narrow tongue.

Most striking of all is the head: sleek, small for the length of the spine, and entirely smooth along the sides. They have no outer ears. Behind the angle of the jaw sit two sunken, crescent-shaped slits bordered by firm ridges of cartilage; when they rise toward the deafening surface, those ridges clamp tight shut like the nostrils of a diving seal, turning the blinding air-roar into a muffled pressure against the skull. Their eyes are wide, set slightly forward, ringed in pale amber and shielded by a thick translucent inner lid that slides cleanly across the cornea to keep out the dry dust of the under-mat and the white glare of noon.

It is the lower jaw that looks strangest to a human eye—broad, slightly heavy, hollow-boned beneath taut pale skin. Watch one pause at a junction of four root-vaults, and you will understand why. It does not tilt its head to listen to the air. It drops onto its broad palms, lowers its chin, and presses the underside of its jawbone directly against a thick, iron-tough creeping root.

For ten seconds it stays completely still, eyes half-lidded, reading the plateau through its teeth.

To them, the living floor is not merely shelter. It is a single continuous instrument—a drumhead leagues wide, stretched over the bones of dead mountains—and they are both its tenants and its keepers.

Left entirely to itself, the fifteen-foot mat is a clumsy, self-suffocating thing. Old dead growth piles up in the saucer-basins until its weight cracks the lower arches and collapses into the bedrock pools, souring the hidden water or tearing open a ragged sinkhole where the warm wind rushes in and drinks the basin dry. The dwellers prevent this with the patient, unhurried persistence of beavers or cathedral-masons, though they build out of living wood and scavenged bone.

Walk a mile through their deeper vaults—bent double, brushing your shoulders against dry roots—and you begin to see the craft everywhere. Where a roof-arch sags under the weight of a surface swale, they have wedged bleached grazer-ribs and cut segments of fallen vent-stalk as vertical props, lashing the joints tight with braided plant-sinew until new living rootlets grow over the bindings and fuse the pillar into the ceiling. Around the shallow granite depressions, they pack low, crescent-shaped dams of peat-mud and yellow plant-resin, guiding the slow trickle from the upper crust into linked, quiet reservoirs. On the dampest pillars beside those pools, they cultivate pale, rubbery shelf-fungi—scraping away the bitter green molds with bone spatulas and leaving clean white caps the size of a palm that taste faintly of raw chestnut.

They do not hurry. In thin air, haste burns too much breath. Their movements in the dark are fluid, unhurried, and astonishingly light—gliding along the curved root-galleries on all fours, weight distributed across four wide splayed pads so silently that even the nervous burrowing rodents six feet above them do not break stride.

When they do hunt those rodents, they do not dig or chase; tearing through the mat ruins their own ceilings. Instead, a hunter settles into a dark junction directly beneath an active warren, presses one wide palm flat to the woody ceiling to locate the hurried patter of feet, and begins to tap a single chisel-nail against a taut root. Not randomly—it mimics the exact staggered, urgent cadence of a startled burrower hammering its hind feet to warn of a falcon’s shadow. Above, the fat, panic-blind rodents bolt along their emergency runs away from the phantom alarm, straight down a narrow downward-sloping chute the hunter braided shut with supple stems an hour before. A long, pale arm reaches up into the dark pocket. One quick, neat twist. No blood spilled onto the water-vaults, no torn turf to alert the vultures circling a mile overhead.

For moisture between mist-rains, they need never rise to the crust at all. They carry slender, hollow wing-bones of vultures and kites, ground to a beveled point at one end; creeping up into the dry upper ribs of the mat, they press the bone tube straight into the fleshy underside of a surface water-rosette and draw out slow mouthfuls of cool, slightly bitter sap, plugging the pinhole afterward with a smear of resin so the plant does not bleed dry in the sun.

Yet for all their quiet husbandry, it is what they do with *sound*—and with tension—that makes them truly themselves.

Run your fingers along the thickest horizontal roots linking one saucer-basin to the next, and every so often you will touch wood that feels different: stripped clean of loose fibrous bark, rubbed smooth and glassy with yellow plant-resin, and lifted slightly off the damp floor on small notched knuckles of grazer-bone.

They are sounding-lines.

The plateau is too loud for voices to carry on the surface, and too labyrinthine for sight to carry more than twenty paces underground. So over generations too numerous to count, they turned the woody veins of the living floor into a web of strings. Press a palm or a hollow jaw to a resin-slicked root, and the chaos of the plateau sorts itself into an astonishingly precise map of weights and tempos.

A rolling, uneven pattering like dry beans poured across leather: a ribbon of splayed-hoofed grazers drifting across the next swale east. Tight, low, elastic double-beats that rise sharply and vanish: a pack of tufted-eared predators running low to split a straggler from the herd. A sudden, vast slackening of pitch across every line at once, dropping half a tone like a loosening harp-string: a pale band of mist has just spilled over the rim of the plateau two miles upwind, and the thirsty upper fibers of the mat are already swelling with moisture.

And through those same taut roots, they speak to one another across leagues of dark.

A conversation between two kin-groups in neighboring basins is nothing like human speech. It is slow, physical, felt in the ribs and the teeth—a steady heel-of-the-hand thrum to open the line, followed by crisp clusters of fingernail clicks, damped silences, and long, resonant slides of a resin-dusted thumb along the wood. They signal the state of a water-pool, the weakening of a roof-arch, the arrival of a seasonal human camp, the birth of a young one, the slow failing of an elder. Sometimes, when two basins are well-watered and no storm is rising, the lines carry no news at all—only slow, interlocking rhythms passed back and forth through the dark for an hour at a time, half-game and half-greeting, vibrating gently against the soles of the grazers sleeping overhead.

Even their sleep is threaded to the floor. They do not lie flat on the damp stone, nor do they sleep in unbroken human stretches; the plateau never goes silent, and its acoustic tides turn too often. Instead, in the dry vaulted ribs halfway between the bedrock and the crust, they sling narrow, open-meshed hammocks of braided root-fiber suspended away from the noisy walls—save for a single taut watch-cord looped loosely around one long wrist or finger. Hanging there in the warm dimness, swaying imperceptibly as the wind moves across the swells above, they drift in and out of a shallow, restorative stupor, their huge chests rising and falling twice a minute, waking instantly if the steady pulse against their wrist breaks cadence.

They are not blind creatures of the dark, however. They know the sky—and they rise into it on their own terms.

Wait beside a cluster of lone vent-stalks when a sudden milky mist-front rolls in from the unseen edge of the world, blotting out the indigo zenith and plunging the stridulating insects abruptly half-quiet. Or watch the flat horizon in the brief, breathtaking span of sunset, when the warm updrafts slacken and the rolling turf turns the color of beaten brass, just as the great soaring vultures spiral away toward the distant cliffs.

That is when the seams in the cushion-shrubs part.

One by one, they unfold out of the living floor—gaunt, pale, slightly stooped at the shoulders, moving with a queer, liquid economy across the springy mat. On open ground they rarely walk upright for more than a few steps; their center of gravity is too low and their arms too long. Instead they flow forward on all fours with their wide flat palms splayed wide, barely depressing the mossy crust, pausing every dozen paces to rise smoothly onto their hind legs beside a bleached vent-stalk. Standing motionless there, chin tilted slightly back, pale down catching the golden light, arms folded tight against their deep ribs, they look almost like a second, slenderer chimney grown from the same root.

Their cartilage ear-slits are clamped tight shut against the din, yet they come up here for the sound as much as for the work.

They climb the hollow vent-stalks with effortless grace, hooking their splayed toes and chisel-nailed fingers into the oval pores along the sun-dried walls, clinging thirty feet above the rolling plain while the warm evening wind rushes past their ribs. Around their narrow waists hang belts of braided sinew weighted with small tools: curved scrapers of grazer-rib, plugs of resin-packed peat, dried hollow seed-pods, and—prized above almost anything else—fragments of scavenged metal.

A wild vent-stalk is a creature of whim. Left untended, its pores clog with wind-blown chaff or split wide under dry heat; the afternoon draft howls through it in a ragged, tearing shriek, pulling too fast and parching the dark root-chambers directly underneath.

The dwellers tend them the way a sailor tends rigging and a musician tends organ-pipes. They clear choked condensers so the mist-plumes weep steadily down the fluted inner walls into the tea-pools below. They widen certain shaded upper pores to invite colonies of bristle-winged swifts and aggressive bats—whose guano feeds the pale mushrooms in the dark, and whose sudden panicked eruption into the open air warns of approaching storms long before the mist arrives. Near the damp bases, they leave snug, resonant alcoves for the brightest-throated frogs, culling the overcrowding gently so the chorus stays deep and clear.

And always, patiently, they tune the chimneys.

A human who has never lived inside a wall of ceaseless noise imagines that the opposite of noise is silence. The dwellers know better. Silence does not exist on the roof of the world; it is an impossibility, like standing water under a noon sun. The only refuge from chaos is *chord*.

With bone knives and resin plugs and hanging baffles of dry gourd, they shape the mouth and pores of every major vent-stalk in their territory until the shifting updrafts no longer tear and shriek across the rims, but settle into wide, steady, deliberate intervals—a low, humming drone in the north basin answered by a warm, hollow fifth half a league to the west. When they are fortunate enough to hold a strip of thin beaten copper, they punch a neat hole through it with a sharpened stone, hang it on dry hide inside a leeward slit, and let the evening breeze nudge a bone clapper lightly against its rim. The note it throws out into the dusk is neither insect, nor frog, nor bird—a pure, liquid ring that threads cleanly through the skull-rattling roar and settles the nerves of everything listening underneath.

At night, under the sharp cold stars, when the night-feeders push up through the mossy gaps and the plateau’s pitch drops into its deep wet chorus, the tuned chimneys hum across the miles like distant harbor bells—marking the boundaries of basins, the depth of water-pools, the shape of a featureless world.

Their lives unfold slowly in the rhythm of that humming floor.

Their young are born small, pale, and startlingly fragile, without the firm cartilage locks over their ear-slits. To a newborn, the surface roar would be fatal, or at least maddening. Mothers bear them in the deepest, quietest saucer-basins—fifteen feet down against the cold granite, nestled in dry hollows lined with shed moss, plucked rodent belly-fur, and their own fine combed down. For the first two years a young one never sees daylight, knowing the sky only as thin, dusty pins of brightness overhead and the far-off murmur of hooves above the ceiling.

Only when their ear-slits begin to firm do their mothers carry them—clinging tightly to the silvery down along their backs—up to the underside of the living crust during the brief ten-minute mist-veils, when the insect rasp falters. There, in the damp green twilight beneath the cushion-shrubs, the mother presses her child’s small jaw gently against a taut resin-rubbed root and holds it still, letting the little one learn the difference between the soft pattering of rain-drops, the slow sway of a vent-stalk, the drum-roll of a fleeing herd, and the heavy, blunt tread of the visitors who come up in summer.

As they grow old—and in that slow, thin-aired quiet, some live forty or fifty years—the bones of their skulls and jaws grow denser, carrying ground-vibrations even more vividly than in youth. Their hide darkens toward old walnut; the deep creases along their shoulder blades and knuckles grow rough enough that faint, silvery blooms of real dry crustose lichen sometimes take hold there, harmless and pale, giving the oldest among them the look of living bark. They stop climbing the high chimneys and spend their days beside the deepest water-saucers, repairing the peat-dams, tending the young while the agile adults forage, and keeping long, unhurried conversations alive along the root-lines.

When an elder’s breath grows too shallow for the thin air and their joints stiffen beyond the reach of the vaults, they do not die in the dark chambers. Decay lingers too long in those cool vaults, and foulness spreads fast through linked tea-pools.

Instead, on a clear evening as the sun drops toward the flat horizon and the turf turns the color of beaten brass, the dying one makes a last slow climb—sometimes helped along the steeper pillars by two younger kin—up through a seam in the cushion-shrubs and out onto the wide, open floor. Or, if they have strength enough left in their long fingers, halfway up into the dry, sun-baked throat of an old, dying vent-stalk. There they lie down with their cartilage slits sealed shut, their broad palms resting flat against the warm living crust one last time, looking up into the clean indigo dark as the stars come out.

By mid-morning the next day, the slow-turning gyres of huge, hollow-boned vultures have tightened and dropped out of the thermals. They do their work fiercely and cleanly in an hour, stripping away everything that could rot and carrying the thin flesh back up into the empty sky.

A night later, under cover of dusk or drifting mist, two pale figures slip out from the nearest seam in the moss. They gather the dry, sun-whitened bones—the long hollow arms, the deep curved ribs, the smooth dense jaw—bind them carefully in braided sinew, and carry them back down into the dark. Nothing is wasted, and no one is truly gone. An elder’s ribs shore up a sagging vault above a water-pool; a long bone bridges a sounding-line; a clean, resonant shoulder-blade hangs inside a vent-stalk throat, catching the warm updraft from the lowlands miles below and ringing softly through the roar.

And what of the seasonal humans who come up onto the roof each year—the quiet bands of herders, hunters, and resin-tappers with wax packed in their ears and broad woven soles strapped to their boots?

To the dwellers beneath the mat, humans are strange, clumsy, half-deaf weather.

They can feel a human party coming half a mile before a single boot crosses their basin. Nothing else on the plateau walks with that heavy, flat, two-beat cadence—*thump, pause, thump, pause*—too blunt to be a grazer, too slow to be a predator. Worse is the smoke: when humans cut their shallow night-pits and burn dense plugs of dried woody root, thin stinging ribbons of sweet smoke seep down through the upper lattice, making the dwellers blink their translucent lids, clamp their nostrils, and drift quietly along the bedrock galleries to an adjoining swale.

The seasonal folk have no spoken name for them—up here, nobody wastes breath naming things you never see clearly—but among themselves they use a quick, respectful hand-sign: two flat palms held chest-high, pressed gently downward and spread apart, meaning *the weight under the skin*, or *the ones who hold the roof*.

Most of the time, the two peoples pass like layers of cloud that never touch. Yet over centuries, a faint, unspoken commerce has grown up in the seams between them.

Humans climb the ancient mountain roof for grazer-meat, fat rodents, and the clear, potent golden resin that forms only in the deepest root-knots—places a human cannot reach without hacking open fifteen feet of tough, iron-braided floor. The dwellers, who have neither fire nor forge, covet two things humans carry up from the unseen valleys: strips of bright dyed wool (which do not fade quickly in the dim vaults, making clean visual marks around a dark stalk-opening) and metal—above all, thin hand-hammered copper.

So a seasoned hunter who knows the roof’s manners never curses when a tarnished copper bowl or a chipped copper knife vanishes overnight from beneath the root-arch where they wedged it. They wait until the next morning, or the morning after a passing mist. More often than not, lying neatly in the dry hollow where the metal rested, they will find three fist-sized lumps of translucent amber deep-resin, still cool from the bedrock dark, wrapped in a length of root-cord braided more finely and strongly than human fingers have ever managed.

Now and then, something quieter still passes between them. A hunter caught far off the bone-marked paths when a thick milky mist-bank drops down out of nowhere—blinding the horizon, drowning every landmark in white—sometimes freezes in sudden dread as the insect roar drops away, hearing a single, unmistakable note pulsing cleanly through the booming frogs. Not the wild howl of wind in dry wood, but a steady, patient chime of beaten copper, struck once every ten heartbeats from a vent-stalk half a mile ahead, pausing only when the hunter’s heavy woven soles step back onto the compressed trough of the trail.

They are not tame, however, and they are not kind in any human way. They are fiercely protective of the floor that keeps them alive.

Every few summers, a careless band comes up from the lowlands—young hunters who do not know the signs, who dig their camp-pit too deep and punch straight through into a dark water-saucer, fouling a clear tea-pool with grease and wood-ash, or who hack down a standing vent-stalk with copper axes to feed a bright, wasteful fire against the evening chill.

The dwellers do not meet them with spears in the open sunlight. Their limbs are too light, their bones too hollow for a brawl in the brassy glare.

Instead, when night falls and the careless campers stretch hides over their sunken pit to deaden the noise, the floor beneath their ribs refuses to settle. Through the taut woody roots directly under their blankets begins a low, relentless, off-beat drumming—not loud in the air, where wax earplugs could block it, but vibrating straight through their hipbones and jawbones in a jagged, arrhythmic stutter that makes sleep impossible. By the second night, every hide-cord holding their dew-catchers aloft has been cleanly severed from beneath the cushion-shrubs, letting their precious water spill into the thirsty turf. By the third morning, when the first heavy-eyed hunter steps out of the doorway onto what looked like firm mossy ground, a whole patch of freshly undermined crust gives way cleanly beneath their broad soles, plunging them waist-deep into a sour pocket of wet peat and swarming, biting beetles.

No face ever shows itself. No threat is ever made. Yet the plateau itself seems suddenly attentive, sour, and unwilling to bear their weight—until they pack their spears, shoulder their skins, and march three leagues on to a swale they have not broken.

Then the mist drifts over, dampening the bruised turf for ten minutes before ripping apart in the sun. The cushion-shrubs knit back over the empty fire-pit. Down in the cool dark against the ancient stone, long pale hands pack fresh peat-resin along the cracked edge of the water-basin, wedge a clean grazer-rib beneath the torn arch, and press a smooth hollow jaw gently against a taut woody root.

Overhead, evening drops down across the flat, ancient horizon. The high flocks spiral away toward the unseen cliffs; the sun turns the rolling carpet of living plant the color of beaten brass and slips out of sight. Under the first sharp stars, miles above the hidden valleys, the plateau shifts into its deep, wet night-chorus—booming frogs, rattling wings, and the long, steady chords of the tended chimneys humming out across the empty dark.

And beneath the springy skin of the world, in leagues of warm, root-vaulted shadow, hundreds of wide splayed palms rest lightly against the vibrating wood, listening to the weight of everything living above them, and quietly keeping the roof from falling in.
