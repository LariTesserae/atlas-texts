At first, even after your eyes adjust to the blue-green sparks and you have made your peace with the swarming grazers and the shadow-birds, you will still assume the factories are running themselves.

It is an easy mistake to make. The trams glide past on their greased silk-cables without drivers. The osmotic boilers hiss and build pressure in empty bays. The looms clatter along their dark viaducts with no one visibly tending the bobbins.

Then a spark pops overhead, and in that half-second of cold glare, you notice something small and deliberate happen on the catwalk one tier down. A six-kneed stalker drops out of the dark onto the ribbed hull of a passing tram-pod, and before it can pry a single grazer loose, a long, flat-bladed wooden pole reaches out from behind a condensation-tank and flicks the hunter cleanly off into the void. Not violently—almost politely, the way a baker brushes flour off a sill. The dark closes back in. Forty seconds later, a soft, dry *clack* of wood against carapace arrives from somewhere behind your right shoulder.

Once you know to look for them, you begin to see them everywhere—folded into the junctions, crouched beneath the eaves of the looms, clinging silently to the wet flanks of the freight-trams as they drift across the gulfs.

In the brief green flash of a shorting node, their silhouettes are startlingly, almost painfully familiar. You see the stoop of a night-shift foreman checking a gauge; the broad-brimmed hat of a lineman tipped against the drip; the heavy, calf-length coat of a municipal inspector carrying a flat satchel under one arm. They have the exact posture of people who belong to a working town—tired, methodical, mildly proprietary about their own stretch of rail.

It is only when one steps close enough to share the greenish blister-light of a junction platform that you see how little they have in common with flesh and bone.

They are extraordinarily narrow, high-shouldered, and almost weightless—built for a world made of braided cane and hollow stalks. What looked from ten yards off like a heavy waterproof greatcoat is, up close, a layered sheath of stiffened cambium-cloth and dark, waxed leaf-blade that does not end cleanly at the collar or the wrists. Where a cuff frays, it does not reveal skin underneath, but a neat bundle of pale, damp vascular cords coiled around a hollow lignified marrow-tube. Their coats are fastened with horn-like thorns and braided root-loops, yet at the shoulders and along the spine the fabric grafts directly into their darker, bark-smooth backs. Clothing and carapace are the same unfinished idea, cured just far enough to keep the cellar-damp out of their joints.

Their heads are long, narrow, and smooth as peeled willow, pale under the broad black leaf-brims of their hats. They have no noses to speak of—only two clean, vertical vents set beneath a low brow-ridge, pulsing gently as they draw in the cool, fern-scented air. Their eyes are small, milky, and nearly lidless; in the near-total dark they look blind as the meristem buds, until a galvanic spark blooms nearby and the pupils snap instantly wide and ink-dark, drinking in half a second of crisp, high-contrast geometry before fading pale again. Their hands are long and cool, tipped with wide, spatulate pads veined like wet leaves, sensitive enough to feel a hair-thin current leaking through an insulated vine without ever touching the bare node.

If you stand beside one on a catwalk, you realize they barely flex the cane beneath their boots. Inside the ribbed cage of their chests, they are mostly empty space—stacked chambers of thin pith and quiet, oscillating membranes, hollow as the cavity they live in.

Because of this, they do not fear the drops. A worker tightening a calcified hoop on an overhanging boiler will lean out over four tiers of nothing with one toe hooked under a truss, entirely unbothered. If a vine parts and sends one tumbling into the dark, they do not scream—partly because the air would swallow the sound anyway, and partly because they are light enough to catch the faint drafts rising off the dynamos, spreading their stiffened coat-flaps to glide sideways toward a lower tram-cable. If they fall into a pocket of stalled gravity, they simply hang there alongside the motionless raindrops, unhurried, casting out a knotted line of braided silk until it snags a railing and lets them haul themselves back into downwardness.

They rarely speak aloud on the open viaducts. Why would they, in an air that drinks words like wet felt and spits them back out forty seconds late from the wrong corner?

Instead, they talk through the town.

Watch two of them meet at a junction where the rail-blisters are dim. Neither opens a mouth. Instead, both lay their flat palms—or, if their hands are full of tools, the pale ridges of their chins—directly against the slick black railing between them. Instantly, a dry, intricate clicking runs through the hardened xylem. You can barely hear it with your ears, yet if your own hand is resting on the catwalk ten paces away, you feel it in the hinges of your jaw and the soles of your boots: crisp, rhythmic little taps, pauses, subtle shifts in pitch, whole schedules and dry complaints passing through the lignified skeleton at the speed of sap and wood.

Across wider gulfs, where no single beam connects them, they speak in flashes. Every worker carries a damp, fist-sized bladder tucked inside a coat-fold, packed with live electric lichen and fed by a single tapped strand of phloem. Squeeze it gently, and it pops with a muted blue-green pulse through the translucent skin of their wrists. Two workers on opposite tiers will hold long, hushed conversations in brief emerald winks—a tram delayed on the outer line, a boiler running cool, a tremor gathering near the moss-bottom—looking for all the world like stray electrical faults to anyone who doesn't know the cadence.

And yet they *do* have voices—soft, papery, dry-reed whispers produced by passing air through the hollow chambers of their chests. Out in the open dark, they use them only with uncanny calculation. A lineman perched high in the rigging of a half-formed warehouse will lean out into the void, murmur three precise syllables toward an empty stretch of catwalk far below, and immediately turn back to his knots. Forty seconds later, a junior tender walks directly onto that spot, pauses as the whispered instruction arrives cleanly behind her left ear—*slacken the third vine*—reaches up without looking around, and eases the tension-line on the exact beat. To them, the broken acoustics of the cavity are not a flaw; they are a shelf you can leave a message on for someone who hasn't arrived yet.

---

What they are doing here, through shift after hushed shift, is not merely operating machinery. The machinery could almost run without them.

They are persuading the town to remain a town.

Because the cavity is newly pushed out of the dark and none of its rules have fully cured, everything down here wants to drift. Left to itself, a factory forgets it is a factory and relaxes into a grove of dripping ferns; a bridge forgets it is forty paces long and collapses into four; a municipal plate forgets what *19* means and blooms into a neat diagram of a seed-coat. The great looms weave luminous cambium-cloth fast enough to keep the streets growing, yes, but cloth alone only makes *matter*. It does not make *commitment*.

That is what the inhabitants provide—not through magic or violence, but through tireless, stubborn, gently bureaucratic habit.

The **pacers** are the most visible of them. You see them on every tier, walking alone with ironwood staffs and long coils of knotted tendon looped across their shoulders. Their entire shift consists of walking the bridges back and forth, back and forth, planting their boot-heels firmly on every third rib and tapping a steady count into the deck with their ferrules. A viaduct that goes unwalked for a few cycles grows loose in its measurements; the pacers walk it solely to remind the braided cane how long it is supposed to be. They are deeply territorial about their spans. If a lantern-bellied stalker crouches too long at the mouth of a bridge—trying, by sheer motionless hunger, to coax the far end closer so it can snatch passing grazers—a pacer will march straight up to it, rap the deck smartly beside its backwards-bending knees, and glare with milky eyes until the predator gives up and drops sullenly into the dark.

Behind them come the **plate-tenders**, hunched and ink-stained, carrying stoppered gourds of dark heartwood stain and fine brushes of split reed. At every junction they stop before the pale shelf-fungus plates growing out of the bark. When a numeral begins to blur—when the loop of a *6* starts sending out fine, curling radicles, or a *4* tries to split into cotyledons—the tender carefully paints the crisp dark line back over the stubborn fungus, tracing the digit three or four times with firm, patient strokes until the plate settles back into civic obedience.

They do not always win. Some crossings simply refuse to hold an Arabic numeral, no matter how many coats of ink are laid down. When a plate insists on remaining a seed-coat diagram, or a cross-section of a woody stem, the tenders sigh—a soft rustle in their hollow ribs—and accept the new address. None of them trust written numbers for their own records anyway. Their ledgers are long, narrow strips of pale cambium-cloth worn wound around their waists like sashes; instead of ink, they record tallies, tram-weights, and work-orders in tiny, precise origami pleats, pinched creases, and knotted root-threads. A written *12* can sprout leaves when you aren't looking, they know, but three tight folds and a double knot will stay three folds and a double knot even if the room turns inside out around you.

Inside the hushed factory bays, the **minders** keep watch over the looms and osmotic boilers with the quiet, watchful ease of nurses in a fever ward. When the fourth loom in a row quietly loses its grip and dissolves into swaying black-stemmed tree ferns, the minder does not sound an alarm or shut down the line. She simply steps smoothly into the bay, picks up a small oiled ratchet of dense heartwood, and stands beside the fronds, turning the ratchet in her palm so its soft *tick-tick-tick* matches the cadence of the working looms on either side. With her other hand she guides the translucent ribbon of cambium-cloth steadily through the fern-stems, humming the dry, sub-audible pitch of the drive-shaft through her chest. Ten seconds later, reminded of its own rhythm, the fern snaps cleanly back into wooden cams, bobbins, and greased levers, and she moves on down the aisle without a change of expression.

When a full tremor rolls through the cavity—that sudden dip in the cool air, the brief dimming of the galvanic leaks—the inhabitants do not flee. They brace. You will see whole crews step smoothly back from a pumping station as it turns inside out over four heartbeats, immediately draping damp felt sheets over the exposed, pulsing coils of root-pipe so the sudden draft won't dry them and the opportunists in the rafters won't dart down to chew the soft pith. As soon as the building folds itself cleanly back into its shell, they strip the sheets away, check the pressure-blisters with their damp fingertips, and resume the shift. When a shallow condensate pool lifts off its leaf-basin and drifts horizontally across a catwalk in a single shivering slab of cold water, workers simply step aside and tip their broad hats to let it pass, watching the frantic little translucent shrimp kick past at waist height on their way to the next tier down.

Out at the ragged margins of the town, where three-lane boulevards terminate in mid-air over the dark void, work the **tip-keepers**. They are the boldest and quietest of the lot, smeared elbow-deep in faint luminous sap, smelling strongly of ozone and raw green stem. Slung in woven harnesses directly beneath the barrel-sized white meristem buds, they receive the fresh bolts of woven cloth brought out by the trams and feed them gently into the pulsing, blind mouths of the growing streets.

Without them, the town would still grow, but it would grow wild—balustrades turning into briars, warehouses blooming entirely into massive damp fiddleheads that never decide whether to be a roof or a smokestack. The tip-keepers press carved ironwood templates against the cooling, embryonic tissue, guiding an extruded flange into a true I-beam, coaxing a wet swelling into a crisp curb and a neat drainage groove. Because ordinary plumb-bobs cannot always be trusted out over the unformed void, each tip-keeper carries a slender reed-tube containing a single drop of heavy, dark heartwood sap suspended in clear condensate. Before they let a new staircase harden, they watch the drop. If it drifts sideways or hangs motionless in the middle of the tube, they tap a sharp warning down the vine-line and wait—sometimes for hours, hanging patiently in the cool dark—until downwardness finishes arriving at that stretch of empty air.

---

They do not wage war on the astonishing abundance of animal life packed into their narrow home. There is too much of it, and too little solid ground to waste on hatred. Instead, they live inside the swarm with an unsentimental, practical intimacy.

The plum-sized fistula-beetles that chew out the soft pith of unfinished buildings are treated almost like municipal masons. When a crew needs a dry storage locker, a ventilation flue, or a hollow dwelling-chamber inside the thick knuckle of a viaduct pier, they score the outer bark with a sharp thorn and smear the cut with sweet, unsteeped pith-syrup. Within a shift, a glossy wet colony of beetles arrives, tunneling cleanly through the soft interior while religiously sparing the load-bearing vascular bundles. Once the chamber reaches the desired size, the workers plug the entry with bitter electric lichen, and the beetles politely relocate to the next marked girder.

Along the power-vines, linemen keep small, docile herds of the paper-thin pale grazers tethered on loops of fine spider-silk, sliding them gently along the high-voltage phloem cables so their comb-like mouths keep the electric lichen trimmed evenly before it can bridge a node and blow a substation. When a membrane-moth flock drifts through a transformer bay—half the moths flapping straight through solid lignum girders as though the beams were still mere rumors—the workers wave long tassels of damp moss to herd them away from the wet ink of fresh municipal plates, whose numerals the moths love to drink.

Only the shadow-birds earn genuine resentment. You cannot swat a flat dark silhouette that has no body casting it, and if one glides across a loom-drum and snaps up a cambium-maggot right out of the fresh white cloth, the resulting hole in the fabric does not mend cleanly; later, when the cloth is fed to a street-tip, that missing scrap becomes a stubborn blind spot in the masonry—a stair that is there when you look at it and absent when you put your weight on it. So the loom-minders hang small, cracked galvanic blisters along the edges of the factory bays, baiting the shadow-birds with bright arcs and sacrificial trays of pale grubs, keeping them well-fed and busy far away from the spools.

---

When the shift ends, there is no whistle. Instead, a subtle drop runs through the osmotic boilers along the central spine—a lowering of the sub-audible hum by perhaps half a tone, felt instantaneously in every jawbone across the cavity.

Work pauses. Palms tap railings in brief farewells. Small groups disengage from the looms and catwalks, clinging to the wet sides of passing tram-pods like dark moths to carry them homeward through the dark.

Because solid volume is so scarce, their homes are tucked into the crevices of the infrastructure itself. Some dwell inside the beetle-hollowed chambers of the massive viaduct piers, behind circular doors of tightly woven cane that swing shut on living vine-hinges. Others live in deep, lantern-shaped baskets woven from surplus cambium-cloth and slung directly beneath the wide, lily-ribbed decks of the boulevards, swaying imperceptibly over bottomless pockets of cool dark.

Inside these suspended rooms, the world narrows and softens. The walls are lined with dry moss-felt and thick layers of shed coat-fabric. A narrow tap run off the nearest boiler-line circulates lukewarm condensate through a coiled root-pipe along the floor, raising the temperature just enough that moisture stops beading on the furniture. Best of all, inside a closed chamber no wider than two outstretched arms, the air forgets its strange emptiness. Sound arrives immediately, from the exact mouth that made it.

Here, the broad leaf-hats come off and hang on wooden pegs. Coats are unlaced at the chest to let the hollow ribs breathe. A small glass-clear bladder of bright lichen sits on a low woven table, casting a steady, gentle sea-green glow across four or five pale willow-smooth faces. They speak aloud now, in soft, rustling, overlapping murmurs—comparing notes on which junctions have grown stubbornly short today, laughing with a dry sound like wind through winter reeds at a boiler that turned inside out right as an inspector leaned against it.

They brew **sumps** in gourd-kettles set atop the warm return-pipes: cloudy, faintly metallic broths of steeped pale pith, toasted beetle-chitin, crushed fern-tips, and a pinch of salted condensate. They drink it through slender hollow stalks, letting the warmth settle into their porous inner chambers.

And they keep small, inexplicable habits—vestigial customs extruded from the same half-remembered civic blueprint that gave this skyless cavern three-lane boulevards and smokestacks. Every worker carries a flat, rectangular box pressed from stiff, dark bark when they leave for a shift—shaped unmistakably like a tin lunchbox, though inside it holds only two damp cakes of pressed pith, a spare wooden rivet, and a knotted cloth tally. They hang neat numbered plaques beside their basket-doors, even though nobody uses them for directions and half of them have gently turned into pressed-flower reliefs of unknown plants. Before they settle into their slung sleeping-hammocks, some of them carefully wind small, useless gears carved out of ironwood—ticking devices with no faces and no hands—just for the comfort of hearing a steady, un-delayed *tick-tock* beside their pillows while the town creaks quietly wider outside.

In the quiet hours between shifts, they play games over the void. Three or four neighbors will perch on the lip of a hanging porch right beside a known pocket of stalled gravity, each holding a pouch of polished, differently colored heartwood pips. One by one, they flick the pips gently out into the dark empty air beyond the railing. Half an arm's length out, the pips slow, stop, and hang motionless in mid-air. Over an hour, in hushed concentration, the players build intricate, floating three-dimensional lattices of suspended wood-beads out over the chasm, nudging each other's pieces with long slender reeds, scoring quiet victories through geometry that could never stand on a table. The match ends only when a tremor rolls through the cavity and downwardness abruptly reasserts itself, dropping the entire constellation silently into the moss-pulp far below—at which point everyone nods in satisfaction, packs up their reeds, and goes to sleep.

Every household also keeps a small niche of **kept things**: objects that have proven unusually stubborn about remaining what they are. A dried seed-pod that has never sprouted in the damp. A sliver of calcified vine that, when flicked with a fingernail, produces a crisp, instantaneous bell-tone even out on the open catwalks. A single shed municipal plate whose dark numeral has remained a clean, un-blooming *7* for longer than anyone can count. They do not worship these objects, exactly; they simply admire them, the way travelers in a marsh admire a dry stone.

---

Not all of them stay neatly calibrated.

Down the narrow side-alleys of extruded root-slate, where the curved fibrous outer wall of the world stands visibly eighty yards away, the freight-trams continue to glide past the signal-posts and recede into the dark for five full minutes, moving along an agreement of distance that ignores the wall completely. Someone has to ride those trams to clear the jams and inspect the feeder-claws harvesting pith out in whatever deep, stretched margin lies beyond the boundary.

The workers who take those routes—and the salvagers who spend too much of their lives wading through un-calibration squalls—slowly lose their crisp civic edges. The others call them the **loose-footed**, or simply the **far-linemen**.

You can spot one immediately in a galvanic flash. Their stride doesn't quite match the floorboards beneath them; they take three unhurried steps and cross twenty paces of viaduct, arriving before the faint creak of their departure has faded. Fine black maidenhair fronds sprout politely from the seams of their coat-collars, swaying in drafts no one else can feel. When they lean against a railing to tap a message, their fingers sometimes sink half an inch into the hardened xylem before remembering that wood is solid.

They are never exiled or feared. They can no longer tend a loom without encouraging it to become a thicket, so instead they serve as couriers and deep-harvesters, riding the impossible trams out beyond the eighty-yard wall and returning hours later with bundles of unusually dense, heavy, silver-pale pith that no local crane can reach. When a loose-footed worker stops by a hanging porch for a cup of warm sump, the hosts are careful to keep their voices steady and their hands planted firmly on the table, offering them a solid rhythm to lean against while they drink.

---

Ask one of them where they came from—tap the question patiently into a damp rail—and they will tilt their long willow-smooth head in polite bewilderment. Nothing down here is old enough to have a history. The cavity feels as though it were pushed out of the dark ten minutes ago, and to them, the past is just a slightly softer, dimmer version of the present—a time when there were fewer tiers, looser gravity, and shorter streets.

Yet they know perfectly well how new ones begin.

When a tier expands far enough that the shifts grow thin and the junctions start drifting faster than the pacers can walk them, three or four older workers who share a clean, steady rhythm will gather in an enclosed room near a humming substation. Together, over several cycles, they weave a **cradle-coat** out of the finest, whitest bolts of newly loomed cambium-cloth, stitching the seams with damp phloem-thread and lining the hollow inside with warm, freshly steeped pith from the central works.

They hang the coat from a low ceiling-vine right beside the vibrating wall of the transformer. Every evening after shift-change, they sit around it with their palms pressed gently to its pale sleeves, tapping their own quiet cadences into the damp fabric—the measure of a forty-pace bridge, the shape of a true *12*, the hum of a boiler holding steady pressure, the patient lift of a pole flicking a stalker off a tram.

Little by little, the bundle takes on a posture. Hollow chambers form inside the pith, drawing air through tiny new vents. The collar closes upward into a smooth, pale bud of a head; dark bark-sheathing thickens along the shoulders; milky eyes open to catch the green flicker of the substation. When the new one finally unhooks itself from the ceiling-vine and steps down onto the woven floor, it already knows how to balance on slick cane, already feels the shift-change hum in its jaw, and already possesses an inexplicable, stubborn conviction that *14* ought to follow *13*—even if its elders must walk beside it for a few cycles, showing it how to coax *14* back out of a dandelion.

And they know how they end, too.

Because the town is still young, true old age is rare, measured less in years than in the sheer weight of tremors a body has absorbed and held steady against. After enough cycles of walking bridges, gripping shuddering boilers, and insisting that numbers stay numbers, an elder begins to grow heavy.

Their hollow chambers slowly fill with dense, dark heartwood. The cambium-cloth of their coats stops yielding at the elbows and knees, hardening into crisp, deeply ridged lignum. Their steps become slow and audible—heavy, wet *thuds* that bend the braided cane beneath them. Soon they are too dense to glide if they fall, too stiff to climb the high rigging of the meristem tips.

They do not mourn this. There is no graveyard down here, because there is no earth to dig one in.

Instead, an elder who feels the stiffness reaching their ribs takes a long, slow walk through the cavity, looking for a place that is having trouble holding itself together—a junction that keeps collapsing from forty paces to nine, a tram-corner where gravity keeps stalling out in mid-air, a warehouse bay whose looms keep relaxing into ferns.

When they find the right spot, they do not make a ceremony of it. They simply step to the edge of the platform, plant their heavy boots squarely across two structural ribs, rest their hardened palms on the railing, and stand still.

Over the next few shifts, fine pale feeder-roots slip out through the soles of their boots and knit cleanly into the deck. Their broad leaf-hat fuses downward into a pitched, rain-shedding cap; the folds of their coat lock into crisp vertical flutes; their chest settles into a deep, permanent, low-frequency hum. Within a few cycles, they have become a **stanchion**—a dark, collar-folded municipal post standing quietly at the corner of the crossing, faintly warm to the touch.

The living workers treat these posts with quiet, practical affection. They tie freight-cables to them, lean their satchels against them during breaks, and press their foreheads to the dark bark to tap a word of news as they pass. And around every rooted elder, for ten or fifteen paces in every direction, the cavity finally cures. Downwardness holds true. Condensation drips cleanly from eaves to sumps without stopping in mid-air. The municipal plate bolted to the nearest truss holds its dark numeral without wavering for cycles on end, and if you drop a pebble beside their boots, you hear it hit the floor at the exact moment it strikes.

---

If you stay motionless on one of the damp black catwalks long enough for the condensation to bead on your sleeves and a pale grazer to finish testing the leather of your boot, you may eventually notice a pale figure standing quietly twenty feet down the viaduct, half-merged with the shadow of a dark, collar-folded post.

A green spark pops on an overhead phloem-cable. In the half-second of cold light, you see the broad leaf-brim tipped slightly toward you, the smooth willow-pale face, the small eyes snapping briefly dark as they take your measure—assessing your strange density, your motionless posture, the odd, un-hollow weight of you pressing down into the slick black ribs of the deck.

The dark folds back in. You feel nothing move, hear no footsteps in the felt-dead air.

Then, gently, unmistakably, a dry, rhythmic series of three crisp little taps runs up through the soles of your boots from the railing beside you.

They are checking to see if you are real—and if you are going to hold your shape until the end of the shift.
