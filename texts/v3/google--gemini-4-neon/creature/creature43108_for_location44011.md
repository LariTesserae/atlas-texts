You can walk the upper esplanades for half a day and remain convinced that the plateau belongs solely to its own feverish geometry. The glare off the wet tiles is bright, the distances between pavilions are immense, and every sound—the booming collapses, the hiss of thermal louvers, the crisp *pop-crack* of widening stairs—belongs so cleanly to water and cellulose that the mind stops searching for anything else.

You notice the first inhabitant only by the shape it leaves behind.

Step close to a solitary three-story pavilion rising out of a broad reflecting deck, and inspect the beveled doorway where two opposing growth fronts met and halted. Everything here obeys clean symmetry. Yet halfway up the eastern jamb, the razor-sharp edge curves inward into a tall, narrow crescent—a smooth vertical recess, scarcely wider than your forearm and nine feet high, gleaming with fresh moisture. It is not rot, and it is not a scar. The pale ivory pith simply stopped growing there with the same crisp mathematical obedience it shows toward its own walls, as if something vertical and living had stood pressed into the jamb for an hour, and the pavilion had politely built around it.

Half a mile away, a viaduct cracks like a rifle shot. The floor shudders under your soles.

On the shaded flank of the pavilion, ten paces from where you stand, one of the fluted pilasters detaches from the wall.

There is no rustle, no grunt, no scramble of claws. Only a single faint, waxy *tick*—like a tight leaf-seam parting—and a slender vertical strip of pale celadon steps smoothly away from the column it had seemed to belong to.

At first your eye still tries to read it as an architectural fragment sheared loose by the distant tremor. Standing upright, it is nearly ten feet tall, yet astonishingly slight—a triradial column of stacked, crescent-shaped cartilage plates wrapped around a central hydraulic spine. It has no fur, no feathers, no wet amphibian skin, no jointed insect shell. Its cuticle is taut, glossy, and heat-reflective, colored the exact milky jade and wet ivory of fresh pith, veined along its inner folds with hairline threads of deep bottle-green and amber. Under the bright noon sun, its thin beveled edges glow the same translucent lime-gold as an arch pushed near its breaking point.

It does not turn toward you. It has nothing to turn with.

There is no head at the top of its spine, only a smooth, split apex where three narrow barometric slits lie flush with clear, trembling fluid. There are no eyes anywhere on its body—only thin, dark-green photoreceptive seams tucked along the margins of its ribs that distinguish fierce noon glare from sudden cloud-curdle, and pale shade from the telltale yellow translucency of an over-pressurized stalk. It does not watch you because watching has no purpose on a mountain roof where nothing hunts from the sky and nothing creeps from the undergrowth. To it, you are not a figure crossing a courtyard; you are a brief, dry, erratic tremor in the tiles, too small to be a rising plinth and too irregular to be a pump.

Its entire body is built to listen to pressure.

Down the shadow of its central axis run neat vertical rows of jewel-clear blisters—fluid sacs holding suspended grains of pale mineral that shiver at the slightest vibration. At its base, the vertical column splits into three long, hollow, triangular-sectioned struts spaced at clean hundred-and-twenty-degree intervals. Each strut ends in a wide, wafer-thin, hexagonal skate of smooth, acid-proof horn. When it lifts a limb and sets it down upon the shallow sheet of warm water covering the plaza, the beveled rim slices the film so cleanly that the surface scarcely dimples. No frog-ripple, no splash—just a silent, glassy glide.

Through those hollow, fluid-filled struts, it hears the whole plateau the way a blind musician might hear a pipe organ from inside the loft. It feels the sluggish, heavy swell of an underground cistern filling two terraces away; the rising, taut soprano hum of a spore-spire stretching toward critical turgor; the brief barometric drop half a mile to the west where a chimney-tower is about to flex open its fleshy louvers.

And it survives here because it knows the plateau’s only language.

Every pore along its waxy flanks exudes a faint, dry-scented ester—the exact molecular halt-signal that the highland’s opposing growth fronts use to recognize one another and stop. Wherever one of these tall, folded creatures leans against a wall to wait out the noon glare, the living wood mistakes its body for an adjacent pillar. The floor tiles beneath its resting skates do not bulge upward to topple it; a pinching doorway halts a millimeter from its ribs; the enzymatic puddles that dissolve fallen arches slide harmlessly off its horn-soles. When it finally unsticks and moves on, it leaves behind those eerie, razor-edged ghost-niches—three-dimensional footprints carved cleanly in pale pith that take an hour or two to close back into smooth green seams.

It is neither a scavenger nor a wood-borer. It has no interest in the sweet, fennel-scented wreckage of collapsed viaducts, and it never chews or tears a living fiber. Instead, it is a quiet surgeon of turgor.

Follow one—if you can keep it in sight—when it senses a nearby structure straining toward failure.

Walking across a twenty-minute expanse of bare, gleaming tiles under thin high-altitude sun would waste water and invite exposure, so it rarely walks far. Instead, it waits at the rim of a terrace until one of the cold shear-winds drops off the cloud-rimmed escarpment and sweeps across the warm water film.

Then, with a crisp, fibrous *snap* indistinguishable from a shifting staircase, two of the tightly folded, accordion-pleated membranes tucked along its flanks pressurize and fan outward.

In half a second, the austere pilaster becomes a blade of translucent ivory—twin ribbed vanes six feet wide, stretched taut on slender hydraulic spars, angled steeply into the gust while the third pleated vane trails behind as a vertical rudder. Leaning almost horizontal above its beveled horn-skates, it catches the wind and planes across the gleaming water-sheet at startling speed, leaving only a thin, vanishing silver line behind it. From half a mile away, a traveler sees nothing but a sudden flash of pale lime-gold skimming the mirror-flat esplanade like a shard of sunlight blown across glass. Then the wind drops, the vanes pleat shut with a single wet click, and the creature slides flush against the base of a forty-foot aqueduct whose central span has begun to swell translucent under ferocious internal pressure.

Watch closely, and you can see the chemistry happen.

Folding its outer pleats tight around its spine so it once again looks like a decorative rib of the bridge, it presses a porous ventral seam directly against the swollen stalk. It releases a tiny, precise dose of a second biochemical phrase—the exact hormone the highland uses to cleave open its own thermal louvers.

The aqueduct obeys. Along a clean vertical cleavage plane, the taut cuticle parts silently without bruising or fraying, opening a hairline silver slit into the pressurized pith. A thin, whistling jet of clear, scalding-hot sap—rich with dissolved sugars—shoots straight into absorptive canals tucked inside the creature’s folded ribs. You can actually see it brighten as it drinks: its pale milky chambers flush luminous jade-gold as the hot pressurized fluid refills its hydraulic core.

It never takes enough to weaken the span. It bleeds just enough pressure to drop the bridge’s quivering internal pitch a half-step out of immediate rupture, then secretes the closing cue and steps cleanly away. The slit seals back into a hairline dark-green seam, completely unscarred. By feeding, it buys the strained viaduct—or a cantilevered spore-spire—another several hours of clean, upright grace against the sky, long enough for the flora to finish casting its spores into the upper crosswinds.

Sap gives it heat, water, and hydraulic fuel, yet sap alone cannot build laminated cartilage or horn-skates. For nitrogen, minerals, and fats, it waits for the sky to curdle.

When a cluster of hollow chimney-towers vents hot, spore-laden vapor into the cold abyssal air and drops a sudden, violent curtain of warm rain over a single terrace, the tall ones do not flee into the dry colonnades. They glide straight into the downpour. Hidden completely inside the blinding white roar of the private storm, where no eye could pick them out from the falling water, they unfurl all three pleated vanes upward at once, locking their edges into tall, fluted alabaster chalices.

The warm rain hammers into their glowing cups, heavy with microscopic, oil-rich spores wrung out of the plateau’s breath. Fine comb-like grooves along the inside of the vanes channel the water down toward absorptive fringes tucked along the central spine, straining out the rich microscopic dust while clear rainwater sheets silently off the scalloped rims and back onto the hexagonal floor. Five minutes later, when the localized storm snaps off and bright noon returns to the terrace, the chalices have already pleated shut. Only mirror-flat water and solitary pavilions remain.

They never utter a sound into the air, yet the plateau is full of their quiet conversation.

When one wishes to locate another across two miles of bare, sunlit geometry, it stands motionless out on the open pavement, sets all three hollow skates firmly astride the pulsing dark-green cambium seams between the floor plates, and contracts its internal pressure-blisters in slow, deliberate intervals. The signal does not enter the air; it enters the hollow beams beneath the tiles, traveling as a faint, syncopated chord tucked inside the subterranean rush of pumped sap. A traveler standing still on the plaza might notice nothing—or might feel, through the soles of their boots, a strangely measured double-beat threading the heavy peristaltic heave of the floor, answered a moment later by a fainter echo arriving from a colonnade half a mile east.

When two meet to mate—often inside a newly grown circular amphitheater or beneath a freestanding arch glowing lime-gold along its crown—they approach without haste, unfold their ribbed vanes, and lock edge-to-edge along their fluted flanks. For half an hour, they stand motionless in the center of the empty court, fused into a single, symmetrical, six-vaned pavilion of translucent ivory. Their combined halt-signals freeze the floor beneath them into absolute glassy stillness while genetic fluid passes silently between their ventral pores. Then a cold gust shears over the escarpment, the vanes snap shut, and they skate apart in opposite directions.

It is their young that reveal how completely they have made peace with a world where nothing is allowed to last.

An egg laid on a terrace would be washed into an enzymatic puddle or swallowed by a rising plinth within hours; an egg hidden in a pavilion would be crushed when the vault inevitably shears. So the gravid adult listens instead for that distinct, rapid percussion of three crisp cracks ringing out across the bright plain—the sound of a brand-new spore-spire pushing up through the tiles, wet and razor-edged and fever-hot.

Gliding to the freshly extruded crown before it rises out of reach, the parent presses its flank to the tender cuticle, triggers a clean chemical cleft, and slips a small clutch of waxy, amber-translucent cysts straight into the hollow, pressurized chamber at the spire’s apex. The cleft seals instantly behind them.

Inside that rising column, shielded from cold shear-winds and bathed in warm, sterile sap-vapor, the embryos race through their development at the exact frantic tempo of the tower itself—growing as the pith stretches, hardening their tiny beveled skates as the walls swell taut against the noon light.

And when the spire finally reaches the absolute edge of structural failure—when its pale span turns luminous under unbearable turgor and rips open with a rifle-shot crack—the collapse is the birth.

Flung sideways forty feet into the air inside the roaring arc of steaming water, the cysts experience a violent, instantaneous drop from internal tower pressure to thin mountain air. That sudden decompression splits their waxy coats cleanly open. Before the sheared bays of pith have even crashed onto the plaza and begun weeping their sweet, scalding enzymatic liquor, the newborns—no taller than your forearm, translucent as sliced celery, already fitted with miniature horn-skates and a single pale, taut vane—hit the crest of the splash-wave. Riding the outward rush of warm water like tiny slivers of blown paper, they skim cleanly away from the dissolving wreckage, catch the first slant of wind off the wave’s back, and glide straight up to the nearest standing pilaster to fold themselves flat and begin listening to the pipes.

They are not quite alone on the roof of the mountain, though the only other animal life here is so radically opposite in temperament that the two lineages scarcely intersect.

Where the tall, triradial ones belong to height, tension, wind, and chemical dialogue with the growing walls, the other inhabitants belong wholly to water, stillness, and surrender to gravity.

You find them only when you lean over the sharp wet lip of a sunken reflecting basin—one of the broad, stepped jade amphitheaters filled to the brim with steaming mirror-water—and stare straight down into the clear warmth.

At first you think the submerged hexagonal tiles are merely catching strange tricks of the noon light: neat, trembling circles of concentrated brilliance, bright as focused sunlight through a burning-glass, hovering motionless across the pale celadon floor.

Look closer, just above each ring of bright light, and the water itself seems to have thickened into low, circular domes—broad as platters, scarcely an inch high at their smooth crowns, clear as blown crystal except for a delicate, radiating filigree of golden-green threads suspended along their flat undersides.

They have no limbs, no mouths, no eyes, no voice. They never swim, and they never ripple the surface of the cistern. Each dome is a living, heat-resistant hydrogel lens—a slow, boneless, radically simplified creature, perhaps the distant descendant of whatever clung to the high mountain tarns before the architectural flora paved over the peaks. Its curved, glassy upper body focuses the fierce high-altitude daylight—and gathers scattered light even through thick drifting steam—down into the bright caustic rings that nourish the dense mats of photosynthetic symbionts woven inside its own flat base, while its porous underside quietly wicks trace sugars directly from the warm, living tiles of the basin floor.

They do not fight the plateau’s endless hydraulic revision. They ride it.

When an underground cistern empties or shifts, tilting a wide walkway two degrees and sending a shin-deep sheet of hot runoff gliding silently across the tiles, the clear domes loosen their grip on the submerged celadon. Secreting a thin film of slick mucilage beneath their rims, dozens of them lift a fraction of a millimeter off the floor and hydroplane noiselessly with the retreating water—weightless glass platters sliding down newly grown jade stairways, curving smoothly through clean drainage runnels, and settling without a sound into whatever fresh sunken court catches the flood a hundred yards downhill.

Occasionally, a stepped plinth extrudes abruptly upward out of a basin, lifting a clear dome high and dry into the direct sun while the excess water pours over all four razor-sharp sides. Stranded on the hot, gleaming deck, the creature does not shrivel. Within minutes, its crystal-clear skin clouds over into a tough, matte-white, chalky crust—indistinguishable from a circular ring of dried sap-foam or mineral deposit. It lies completely sealed and motionless on the sun-blasted plinth for hours or days, waiting until a sudden localized warm rainstorm—or the rolling splash-wave of a distant collapsing arch—washes across the tiles, softens its chalky seal back to glass, and floats it silently down into a new basin.

Only once in a long while do the plateau’s two silent lineages cross paths. A newborn sail-creature, newly blasted from a rupturing spire and still soft-ribbed from the decompression of its birth, will sometimes ride a splash-wave straight into the quiet hollow of a flooded amphitheater. There it rests for its first calm hour, braced upright on its tiny horn-skates in the steaming, mirror-flat shallows while its waxy cuticle cures in the noon light—surrounded on the pale celadon floor below by a constellation of bright, trembling light-rings cast by the motionless glass domes, neither creature acknowledging the other.

Even in death, nothing here lingers to spoil the clean edges of the drafting board.

When one of the tall fluted ones grows too slow to outrun a shearing colonnade, or finally spends its last hydraulic strength after a decade of riding the mountain’s fever, its pale ribs deflate and fold down onto the gleaming tiles. Because its waxy cuticle still carries the lingering scent of the halt-signal, the plateau hesitates for a few hours. For half a bright afternoon, a traveler might stumble across its drained husk lying across an empty promenade: a flat, folded fan of translucent ivory cartilage and amber veins, clean and dry as a discarded architectural blueprint, already curling gently at the margins in the high-altitude sun.

By evening, the chemical signal fades. The dark-green cambium seams beneath it pulse softly open. Warm enzymatic moisture rises through the pores of the hexagonal tiles, and before the next noon sky clears over the escarpments, its minerals have already been drawn down into the hollow beams and pumped upward into the sharp, wet crown of some new colonnade half a mile away.

Stand still on one of the upper terraces after you have finally learned how to see them, and the vast, bright emptiness never feels quite the same again. The plateau is still loud without a voice, still blindingly clean, still devoid of watchers. Yet now you catch the faintest double-beat threaded through the liquid vibration beneath your soles. You notice the bright caustic rings trembling on the floor of a steaming basin. And when you walk up to a solitary pavilion whose proportions seem impossibly austere and quiet, you find yourself looking twice at the shadowed eastern pilaster—waiting for a cold gust to shear in off the abyss, for the crisp, wet *tick* of a seam unsticking, and for a tall, lime-gold sliver of the architecture itself to unfold into the wind and skim silently away across the gleaming flats.
