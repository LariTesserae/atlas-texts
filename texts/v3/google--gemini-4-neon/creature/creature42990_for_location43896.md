When at last you meet one of them, you do not hear them coming.

You know them the same way you know everything else in the dark: by a shift in the stillness. A curtain of salt-bristled pendants hanging from an overhead fin of basalt slowly pivots away from you, tracking a second static wake that does not belong to your body. The smell of the gallery thickens by a fraction—a warmer note of heated paraffin, a trace of crushed ironstone and sulfur. Then, from a shelf at shoulder height, comes a single dry *tink*, and half a second of cold violet light flares as strained quartz is dragged across bone.

The dark snaps shut again almost before your pupils can contract, leaving the figure printed in pale negative across the inside of your eyelids.

They are shaped by the squeeze of the world. Narrow-shouldered, flat-ribbed, long in the forearm and the shin, they are built like wedges made to slip sideways through seams that would trap a wider chest. They crouch comfortably where you would have to crawl, one palm braced against the hanging ceiling, one heel wedged into a lip of shale no wider than two fingers. Even in the brief violet glare, there is nothing soft or glistening about them. Their skin is matte and dry, the color of smoked tallow dusted with warm ochre flour—not parched or cracked, for water was never part of their making, but supple in the way of burnished horn or old wax kept warm in the hand. No sweat ever beads on their brows; instead, fine seams along their neck and ribs exude a nearly imperceptible film of clear, waxy oil that keeps the dry hide pliant and gives them the same faint, sweet mineral scent as the galleries they walk.

Everywhere their body meets the razor geometry of the rock, it has answered in kind. Along the outer ridges of their forearms, their hips, their knees, and the heels of their palms, the skin has built itself into dark, laminar plates of dense callus, tough enough to scrape across a fresh cleavage plane of slate without parting. Where a sharper fin of volcanic glass has opened them in the past, the scars lie flat and glassy, pale yellow-white. They have no wet blood to spill; when a blade of rock cuts deep, a slow, thick bead of amber oil wells from the split, binds immediately with the floating mineral soot, and sets into a dry resin crust before a drop can fall. Over their shoulders and shins they lash thin, overlapping shingles harvested from the gypsum-plated striders and bound flat with mineral-tanned sinew—armor fitted so tightly to the bone that nothing dangles, nothing catches in a narrow chimney.

Only the inner pads of their fingers are kept bare and uncallused, rubbed smooth with fine chalk and tallow. Those fingertips are their real eyes.

They do have eyes—large, set wide beneath smooth brow-ridges, faintly milky in the spark’s afterglow. They never blink away tears, because there are none; instead, a dry, pleated inner lid sweeps slowly across the surface every few minutes like a soft brush, clearing away settled stone flour. Those eyes see little of shape or edge. They are built to catch the ghostly stripe of a fluorite vein along a ceiling rib, the faint phosphor-dot rubbed into a basalt handhold, and the sharp violet wink of a spark-licker working a quartz seam forty paces off.

What keeps them from slicing themselves open in the pitch-black labyrinth is the air itself. Growing sparse and fine along their brow-ridges, the backs of their wrists, and the long hollow of their spine are pale, stiff filaments, each frosted with microscopic grains of conductive salt. Like the blind pendants hanging from the rock, those filaments lean into the static grain of the dark. A knife-edge of basalt or a protruding splinter of volcanic glass gathers a tight, sharp electric charge along its rim; long before a walker’s shoulder comes within a hand’s breadth of the blade, the filaments along their skin prickle and pull away, mapping every overhang, fin, and drop-off in crisp, silent pressure. They thread a mile of razor-lined crevices without once brushing a wall they did not mean to touch.

Nor do they pant as they climb. Watch their narrow flanks in the faint fluorite glow and you will see their ribs expand and settle scarcely twice a minute. Inside them are no wet lungs to lose moisture to the kiln-dry air, only pleated, papery bellows that draw warm gas slowly across dry chemical membranes, stripping what they need and exhaling almost nothing. When they speak aloud—which is seldom, in a place where sound dies three paces from the mouth—there is no wet resonance in it, no sigh or throat-clearing. It is a clipped, gentle currency of dry tongue-clicks, faint dental hisses, and quiet woody hums vibrated through the bones of the jaw, audible only to someone pressed within arm’s reach in the same shelf of rock.

When they want to speak farther, they use the stone.

A walker pauses beside a continuous rib of dark basalt, presses the flat of their knuckles—or the blunt ironstone pestle carried at their hip—against the warm rock, and taps. Three short strikes, a measured rest, a slow dry scrape. In the motionless air, the sound barely crosses the gallery. Through the solid strata, it runs cleanly for half a mile. Somewhere beyond thirty thin walls of shale and six vertical slots, another walker resting in a sleeping blind feels the faint vibration travel through the cheekbone pressed against the floor, shifts a hand to the stone, and taps back.

For the most part, though, they move without leaving a sound at all. Their feet are wrapped in thick, stitched pads of fibrous dust-felt stripped from the soles of dead striders, or left bare and horn-hard, and they walk with an extreme, almost ceremonial discipline. Beneath every overhanging lip lies that neat triangular apron of ancient stone flour, untouched since the continent cracked. A walker does not wade through it. They step cleanly from bare rock rib to bare rock rib, bracing palms against ceilings to swing over wide drifts, never kicking up the slow-settling clouds that hang in the air for an hour. To disturb a dust apron is noisy in the way of sight and static—and it is also a kind of speech. That rare, sharp-edged footprint you find interrupting a pristine slope is almost never a slip of the boot. A single heel pressed deep with the toe aimed toward a crawlspace means the seam beyond holds open; two prints set side by side facing back mean the rock ahead is strained and groaning, ready to settle.

Everything they carry is flat, dry, and patient. Strapped tight to their waist and ribs are bundles of split-bone spark-strikers, chipped blades of volcanic glass wrapped in sinew, coils of cold-beaten copper wire, chalk-bone needles, and stoppered marrow-bones packed with dense ochre tallow. No fire has ever burned in their hands, nor could one; there is nothing green to kindle, and smoke would never clear from the baffled halls. Their metals—native copper, tin, and lead pried cleanly from the seams—are worked cold, pounded flat on ironstone anvils with rounded quartz cobbles over days of quiet, unhurried tapping. Copper is prized above everything else, not for brightness in a world without light, but for how cleanly it bends without snapping, how crisply it holds a groove, and how faithfully it conducts the charge of the rock. Nearly every walker wears a thin hammered band of copper around one thumb or wrist. Pressed against a quartz vein, the metal hums faintly against their skin when the crystal lattice is wound tight under continental stress, warning them which ceiling slabs are quiet and which are waiting to drop.

They eat rarely, and nothing they keep ever spoils. From a bone tube at their hip, a walker thumbs out a dense wafer of rendered tallow kneaded with pulverized sulfur and crystalline marrow, letting it soften slowly in the dry warmth of the mouth—coated not by saliva, but by a thin, warm digestive oil—to burn away over days of quiet travel. Now and then they slide a thin copper spatula between two plates of shale, lift out one of the parchment-thin creatures with a soft *snick*, fold it along its geometric creases, and chew it dry as salted husk. A single stoppered bone of tallow and a pouch of sulfur-paste will carry one of them across three hundred miles of warm dark.

Because nothing rots, nothing washes away, and nothing cools, they do not experience time the way surface peoples do.

For anyone from a world of rain and seasons, time is a current that erodes what lies behind it; here, time is simply another layer pressed tightly against the present. When a walker reaches a junction of three galleries and finds a hammered copper tag dangling on dry sinew from a basalt tooth, there is nothing on its surface to tell them whether the hand that hung it passed five days ago or five centuries ago. The metal has not rusted green in the waterless air. The little cone of white gypsum powder beneath the fresh handhold notch beside it sits in the exact crisp triangle it formed the instant the chisel struck.

The walker takes the copper plate between thumb and forefinger and draws it slowly across the smooth, uncallused pads of their middle fingers. What looks in a violet flash like mere scratches is a dense, precise topography: fine parallel lines counting elbow-spans to the next pillared hall; slanted notches marking the angle of the hanging dust-beards when the walker passed; cross-hatched sigils naming what thrives in the galleries ahead—thick ledger-stacks of parchment-folk, a wide field of spark-lickers, a pinch too tight for a loaded hip—and at the bottom, the maker’s own tight geometric spiral, hammered into the copper like a signature.

Beside the old tag, the walker hangs nothing if nothing has changed. If a seam has settled a finger’s width tighter, or a new fluorite rib has cracked open, they scrape a fresh line onto a spare plate and loop it onto the same stone tooth. If they open a cache tucked into a blind alcove, they take a quartz tooth or a coil of sinew they need and leave a bone of rendered tallow or a freshly notched striker in its place—trading easily, without haste, with someone whose footsteps may be separated from theirs by ten generations. To them, the absent and the long-gone do not feel far away. Their tools are still warm from the kiln-heat of the rock; their chalk dust is still sharp on the ledges; their copper tags still sway gently when you pass.

For years at a stretch, a walker keeps to a vast, solitary circuit—hundreds of miles of interleaved shelves, vertical chimneys, and narrow galleries known completely by the pitch of tapped rock, the sequence of mineral smells, and the subtle static texture of every sharp lip. Two walkers may inhabit overlapping strata for half a decade and never cross paths, knowing one another only as a familiar signature-spiral on a hanging plate, or a distant rhythm tapped up through the basalt floor while resting in a slate blind.

Then, slowly, the continental tide turns.

Over decades, the deep electrical charge of the broken crust shifts from one side of the planet’s skin to the other. Nothing cools; no breeze stirs. Only the delicate beards of mineral soot along the razor edges lean, degree by degree, from north to south. The blind curtains of pendants pivot silently in unison. Far down in the dense shale stacks, the parchment-thin creatures unfold one by one and begin to drift along the warm stone.

The walkers feel it first as a quiet, persistent tug along the salt-frosted filaments of their spines—a faint pulling in the dark that does not ease when they turn. Without urgency, over months and years, they leave their solitary rounds. Tapping long, braided sequences into the deep basalt seams as they go, following the slow drift of the parchment-creatures and the new grain of the rock, they travel inward toward the great low pillared halls where the stacked stone sheets part like the leaves of an open press.

There, in the crowded, non-echoing silence, dozens of them gather.

There are no fires, no raised voices, no lamps burning in the dark—only a dense, warm stillness heavy with the scent of paraffin, sulfur, and dry chalk, broken every few minutes by the brief cold-violet wink of a quartz spark as someone inspects a hammered blade or checks the wear on an old sinew strap. They greet one another not across open air, for there is little open air to cross, but by wedging close along the stacked shelves, laying bare palms together, and sliding smooth fingertips lightly up the inner wrist to read the pattern of copper rings, the dry amber scars, and the familiar warmth of another living skin.

Bundles of copper tags pass from hand to hand in the pitch black, circulating news of a thousand leagues of stone: which deep cleft finally pinched shut with a mile-wide *tock*, where a rich seam of native tin fractured clean, whose signature-spiral has not been hung fresh in two turnings of the tide. They trade heavy strider-bone for fine quartz teeth, hammer new snares side by side in slow rhythmic taps that hum softly through the stone floor, and pair in the warm dark.

Children begin at these turnings. A newborn comes into the labyrinth already dry, pale as freshly split gypsum, wrapped in a thin waxy sheath that settles into supple skin within hours. They do not cry—in a baffled world where noise dies at three paces and draws scavengers, wailing has no use; instead they tap rapidly with tiny flat knuckles against a parent’s collarbone. Bound tightly to the parent’s chest or ribs in a sinew sling, fed small pellets of chewed tallow-paste from the tip of a thumb, a child spends half a continental tide—fifteen, twenty years—walking silently at their parent’s heel. They learn the labyrinth not as a map seen from above, for there is no above, but as a vast, three-dimensional melody of static, smell, and vibration: how to read the strain in a quartz vein through a copper ring, how to step cleanly over a powder apron, how to dismantle a dead strider without dulling an obsidian edge, how to listen through a cheek pressed to the basalt. Only when the dust-beards begin to lean the opposite way does the young walker hammer their own spiral onto a first flat square of copper, hang it beside their parent’s at a junction, and slip sideways into an unclaimed branch of the seams.

And because they live at the pace of the electric tide, they grow old the way the rock does.

After four or five turnings—when the dust-beards have leaned north and south across a century or more—the warm waxy oil inside a walker’s joints and marrow begins slowly to thicken and crystallize along the grain of the stone. Nothing rots in them, and nothing softens; they simply grow heavier, more deliberate, their quiet climbing marked by faint, dry clicks in the wrists and knees. They feel themselves becoming *set*, like a deep fracture finally sliding its last millimeter to rest.

Long before their hands stiffen past use, an elder climbs toward one of the high, narrow horizontal shelves—slots too slick or tight for the six-legged striders to reach, where the scavengers’ copper-hard rasps will never reduce them to pale flour. Along the outer lip of the ledge they lay their possessions in a clean, orderly row: their split-bone spark-strikers, their ironstone mortar still ringed with bright yellow sulfur, their braided wax-cords, their last stoppered tallow-bones. Above their chest they hang every copper tag they have carried across a lifetime of walking, looped together in a silent cluster from a stone tooth. Then they wedge themselves cleanly between the warm floor and the warm ceiling, fold their flat hands over their ribs, and stop moving.

They do not decay. They do not grow cold.

If you crawl into a high gallery in the dark and your hand brushes a dry, smooth shoulder resting on a shelf, it holds the exact unchanging kiln-warmth of the rock around it—the exact warmth of the living. Only when your fingers trace further, feeling the fine, motionless beard of pale mineral soot growing outward from the brow-ridge and the knuckles—leaning gently in unison with the dust-beards along the basalt edges—do you understand how long they have lain there.

The living do not turn away from these shelves. They climb up to sit beside the Still Ones in the dark, letting the static of their own salt-frosted filaments settle into quiet accord with the elder’s, drawing the hanging copper plates one by one across their fingertips to read the oldest paths in the rock. If they need a chalk-bone needle or a coil of copper wire, they take it cleanly from the row along the lip and leave a freshly notched striker in its place, keeping the long exchange unbroken. In a world that has never known rain, the dead do not vanish into earth; they simply remain, warm and intact, folded into the crowded architecture of the seams.

In the gallery where the violet spark just died, the afterimage fades at last from your eyes.

For a long, breathless stretch, nothing moves. The pendant-curtain hangs silent above the cleft. Then comes a faint displacement of warm, paraffin-scented air—no footstep, no rustle of cloth, only the subtle bend of static drawing close. A single bare, uncallused fingertip touches the back of your hand.

It is not cold, and it is not hurried. Dry as warm chalk, it lingers for half a second against your skin, reading the strange, soft, faintly damp texture of someone arrived from a place where water falls from the sky. You feel a tiny shiver run through the air around your wrist as the salt-frosted filaments along their forearm register your alien, ungrounded heat.

Then the fingertip lifts. With a single hushed *snick*—no louder than a parchment-creature unfolding from a stack of shale—the walker slides sideways into a vertical slot barely the width of your ribs and vanishes into the endless, near-touching dark. Beside your shoulder, a flat hammered copper tag sways once, almost imperceptibly, on its dry tendon. And miles away, through solid crust that has never hurried and never cooled, a deep seam settles a fraction of a millimeter closer to rest with a single muffled *tock*.
