You could cross the basin from rim to rim and leave convinced that whatever stacked the stones was gone ages ago. Even if you passed within thirty paces of one of them, you would likely read it as a slumped shoulder of wet phyllite, or a weathered outcrop tucked tight against the base of a wall. They do not startle when you approach, because they do not hear your footsteps through the air, and even when they sense the faint, unfamiliar thud of your boots coming up through the bedrock, they rarely bother to turn their heads until you are close enough to make out the sheen of condensation on their bare backs.

They are built roughly to the human measure—a waist, two arms, hands made for lifting and setting weight—yet they look as though the valley has pared away everything in them that was soft, loose, or hurried, leaving only what can endure cold and pressure without giving ground.

They wear nothing. Anything woven would rot in the reluctant dampness, and there is nothing here to spin or stitch. Their skin serves in place of cloth: thick, matte, and dense as cold river-clay, colored the mottled grey-ochre of damp shale and pale lichen, with paler calloused plates across the shins, knees, and the outer edges of the forearms where they rest against rock. It is hairless and cool to the touch, slick with the same fine, permanent film of moisture that coats the scree. Across the ribs and shoulders, their frames are strangely deep and broad—barrel-chested, with thick collarbones and wide, flat shoulder blades—built to hold slow, capacious lungs and to take the ceaseless acoustic bruising of the air without tiring.

If one rises from the base of a slate wall to look at you, the motion takes so long you almost think your eyes are drifting. There is no sharp catch of breath, no tension in the neck. It unfolds joint by joint, long-limbed and spare below the heavy chest, planting broad, flat-soled feet whose fissured pads grip the wet stone without a sound. Its face is narrow, heavy-browed, and unmoving. The eyes are wide-set and pale as pewter, almost never blinking—in air that never stirs, nothing blows against them, and in the frozen evening light there is no glare to squint away from. Its mouth is wide and lipless, kept closed to hold in warmth and moisture.

Where outer ears should be, there are only low, thick ridges of folded skin pressed tight against the skull, narrowing the apertures down to angled slits. They cannot hear the world the way an outsider does, through open air; if they could, the valley’s flat roar would have hollowed out their minds long before they reached full height. Inside those heavy skulls, bone and fluid have thickened to baffle the ambient wall of noise down to a dull, background pressure, no more noticeable to them than the pulse of blood in the neck. Instead, they listen with their skeletons. Their cheekbones, jawlines, and broad collarbones are dense and faintly resonant; when one of them wants to know what is moving a hundred yards away, it does not cup a hand to its head—it crouches, lays its palm or the flat of its forearm against the bedrock, and reads the faint distortions travelling through the granite beneath the roar.

There are very few of them—perhaps twenty, perhaps fewer, spread across a day’s walk of scoured stone—and because they spend long, unmeasured intervals sitting or lying nearly motionless, it is almost impossible to be sure you have not counted the same one twice.

They are not merely sheltering behind the waist-high dry-stone walls you find scattered across the gravel flats. They are tending them.

In a valley without wind, a wall that encloses nothing looks like an absurdity, until you kneel down behind one and feel what happens to your chest. The flat roar of the basin is not uniform; it hangs in rigid, invisible strata of standing waves, fierce along some bands of rock and strangely thin along others. A thirty-pace line of rough-stacked slate breaks the lower, teeth-loosening thrum at waist height, throwing a narrow strip of acoustic shadow across the gravel on its lee side. In that sheltered trough, the air still rings, but the low grinding backs off just enough that a body lying close to the bottom course does not feel its ribs shudder.

More than that, the walls are farms. Where the standing waves are bent and cancelled by stacked rock, the vibration nulls out—and where the vibration nulls out, the pale grey-green ring-lichen takes hold. One of these people will spend stretches that would be weeks in the world below carrying flat shards of slate from a scree slope, setting them down with immense care, nudging the top stones an inch left or right while pressing a bare palm against the ground behind the wall to feel the tremor die out of the gravel. Then it walks away. Years later—or whatever passes for years where the dusk never deepens—it returns to find a clean ribbon of pale lichen crusting the leeward stones, ready to be scraped free with blunt, horn-hard fingernails.

They eat slowly and seldom, running on a metabolism pared down nearly to the pace of stone. A handful of scraped lichen, chewed for half an hour until its faint mineral starch gives way; a wad of the ink-black moss squeezed between palm and rock until the iron-sour water pools in the hollow of a hand to be lapped up; the hollow stalks of the humming sedge split open with a thumbnail for the thin, damp pith inside. Their hearts beat perhaps a dozen times a minute. Between breaths, their broad chests stay still so long you think they have forgotten the next pull of thin air.

Now and then, one kneels beside a trembling black puddle to hunt. They do not trust their eyes when they reach for the translucent salamanders clinging to the siltless bottom—the eye catches the doubled, lagged outline of where the creature’s toes were a beat ago, and a hand aimed at what it sees closes on empty supercooled water. Instead, the hunter lowers its bare wrist quietly through the geometric ripple-grid and waits until the water numbs its skin past feeling, attending only to the minute interruption in the standing lattice where the salamander’s blunt head pushes against the vibration. Then the broad fingers close once, smoothly, where the creature actually is, and lift it dripping into the dimness.

With the hollow-horned grazers, they share a cold, mutual truce. Neither has the breath to spare for a chase. They pass within a dozen paces of one another across the open stone aprons without altering stride, two kinds of slate-colored slowness reading the same pressure-shifts in the air. Yet when an old grazer finally grows too rigid to lower its narrow muzzle to the lichen and sinks down beside a gully, one or two of the people will quietly arrive—sometimes from miles away—and wait motionless beside it until its ear-slits stop twitching. They waste almost nothing of the lean, dark flesh, eating it cold and raw over long, unhurried sittings, for meat barely spoils in this damp chill. When only the bone remains, they do not carve it or shape it into tools; they leave the ribs to bleach in the pewter twilight, though sometimes one of them will pick up the broad, perforated crest of the skull, hold the porous bone flush against its own jaw and temple, and turn slowly in a full circle. Through the grazer’s natural baffles, the basin’s roar breaks apart into fine, distinct bands, letting the listener hear the distant hiss of the far rim or the faint creak of a settling talus slope miles off. When it has heard enough, it sets the skull gently back onto the gravel and walks on.

Even rarer is the gathering when one of the high pale kite-creatures loses its hold on an acoustic shelf. For ages they hang overhead, indistinguishable from shreds of frozen cloud; then, by some small failure of cartilage or balance, one tilts too far, slips through the pressure ledges, and slides silently down out of the pewter ceiling to flatten across the shale like a dropped sheet of wet vellum. Word of such a fall travels through the bedrock faster than a body can walk. Half a dozen of the people may converge around it, standing still for a long stretch before anyone bends to touch the thin, ribbed membrane. They eat its pale, copper-tasting flesh and draw free the flexible cartilaginous struts—not to bind into spears or frames, but simply to flex them slowly between their thick fingers, watching something spring back in a place where almost nothing else ever bends.

You never find paths linking their walls and hollows because they do not travel the basin the way an outsider must. They know every settled failure of space across the valley floor by muscle and pitch, the way a blind swimmer knows the seams of a cistern.

Watch one cross the northern flats and it is startling how casually it uses the breakage. It walks in a slow, rolling stride toward the straight, faintly blurred ribbon running through the bedrock—the seam where forty yards of distance were quietly omitted long ago—lifts one broad foot, and steps through. There is no hesitation, no glance down. In the space of a single pace, the figure is abruptly smaller against the distant rim, the roar shifting down half an octave across its bare shoulders without making it flinch. To them, the seams are not wounds in the world; they are hallways. There are shorter ones tucked into the dry gullies, narrow hinges barely a shoulder-width across that fold a steep ridge down to three steps, and places along the western talus where a body walking at a particular slant carries half its own weight for twenty paces before gravity takes hold again.

They have lived inside this weathered equilibrium so long that small habits of the valley’s breakage cling to their own bodies. Follow one across a flat stone apron and you may notice its shadow pooling directly beneath its soles, dark and circular as though lit from an overhead sun that does not exist, or else trailing half a pace behind when it steps out of a crouch, reluctant to give up the shape of where the body just sat. If you stand close enough to one in the dimness, you sometimes feel the air around its ribs humming faintly out of phase with the valley’s chord, like a second string vibrating a fraction below the first.

Though they spend most of their existence alone, each tending a loose circuit of walls, moss-beds, and puddles, they are not indifferent to one another. By some quiet, internal agreement—perhaps triggered by a subtle drift in a seam, or simply the slow accumulation of solitude—two of them will sometimes arrive at the same ring of flat boulders beside a damp hollow.

The ring is barely wide enough for two to crouch inside. They do not look at each other’s faces when they meet, nor make any sound into the deafening air. They step over the low stones, fold their long legs beneath them until their knees nearly brush, and lay their bare forearms flat along the worn-smooth upper rims of the boulders, wrist beside wrist, elbow resting in the shallow grooves polished by generations of skin.

There, they talk.

In the air, the bronze roar swallows every small sound whole, but through dense granite, clean impact travels sharp and clear. One presses the heel of its palm firmly into the stone and taps once with the flat of a knuckle; the other feels the tight, dry pulse travel up through its own ulna into its collarbone. A fingernail drawn slowly across wet quartz; a shift of forearm weight; a deep, closed-mouth hum vibrated down from the sternum into the jaw and out through a cheekbone laid briefly against the rock. Their language has no haste in it. A single exchange—whether the floating shale slab on the western slope has shifted a hair’s breadth, whether the seam near the northern scree has grown narrower, whether a new patch of sedge has come up along the eastern rim—may take what would be an entire afternoon in a world that still has afternoons.

Between pulses, they simply lean together on the boulders and stare down into the black hollow beside the ring.

The supercooled water keeps its crisp, stationary lattice of ripples, refusing to freeze and refusing to show their pale grey faces back to them. Instead, kneeling there with their forearms touching on the smooth stone, they gaze down into a sharp noon-blue dome pierced by a small, blistering sun—a sky none of them have ever stood beneath, bright enough to make their pewter eyes narrow slightly. Sometimes they watch it for hours, motionless as the rocks they lean on, waiting for the rare, inexplicable interval when a smudge of white cloud drifts across that impossible blue floor, proving that somewhere on the far side of the reflection, the air still moves.

Children are extraordinarily rare among them—so rare that a whole stretch of decades might pass without a new one appearing on the flats. When one is born, it is carried for years tucked tight against its parent’s thick chest, held in the crook of one broad arm, its small, soft skull pressed directly against the parent’s collarbone so the roar reaches it only muffled and mixed with the slow, glacial drum of the heartbeat inside. Even after it can walk, it moves with one small hand kept flat against the adult’s hip or thigh, learning the map of the seams and the pitch of the hollows by touch before it ever trusts its own feet to the scree. You might catch sight of one sitting behind a waist-high wall, solemnly pressing a single fingertip against a hollow blade of sedge, wide-eyed at the sharp, exact buzz travelling up into its bone.

If you want to understand what still endures inside these silent, stone-paced minds, you have to follow one up to the western rim—to the spur where the roar drops abruptly to an eerie, muffled hum, and the leaning cairn of pale river-rounded cobbles tilts over the drop in quiet defiance of gravity.

There are no rivers here, and no stones worn round by running water anywhere in the basin’s bedrock. Yet there are perhaps forty or fifty such cobbles scattered across the high valley, pale and smooth as birds’ eggs, every single one of them known to the inhabitants by weight and contour. Nobody remembers whether their forebears carried them up before the world below fell away into bruised pewter, or whether they were gathered ages ago from the lip of a seam that once opened onto a gravel bar before it healed shut. They are the only things in the entire basin that still hold the shape of water that actually flowed.

Every cobble stays in slow circulation. One of the people will carry a single pale stone tucked into its palm for a long stretch, warming it slightly against its cool skin, before climbing to a quiet pocket on the rim and setting it onto a leaning cairn—or lifting the topmost stone from a stack of seven and replacing it with the one it brought, leaving the lean intact. It is both a record and a test. Because the valley’s twilight never shifts and its note never decays, weight and balance are the only clocks left. Returning to a spur and finding seven river cobbles still angled out over the abyss, slick with cold dew and unfallen, tells them that the basin’s settled breakage has not slipped any further, and that another hand has been here since they last climbed the slope.

But they do not come to the quiet pockets only to check the cairns.

Stepping out of the valley’s roar into that small, muffled hollow is strangely raw to them—almost dizzying, like emerging from deep, supporting water onto bare gravel. Their ribs feel suddenly light; the rasp of their own shallow breathing sounds loud and fragile in the still air. Yet they come here deliberately, crouching beside the leaning cobbles with their knees drawn up to their chins.

Sometimes, one of them will hold two of the pale river-stones an inch apart and bring them together once.

*Clack.*

In the open basin, the sound would be drowned before it left the fingers. Here, in the rare pocket of hush, the sharp, clean click rings out into the dim air—and then it shrinks, thins, and stops. It actually ends, leaving only the muffled hum behind it. The figure sits very still after the sound has gone, eyes fixed on the pewter void beyond the crags, as if listening to the shape of the absence it just made.

Very rarely, when two of them meet on that western spur rather than in the boulder rings below, they will open their wide, lipless mouths and speak into the air. Their throats are stiff and unused to shaping breath; what comes out is not a sentence, only a single dry, gravelly syllable, hoarse as shale sliding over shale. One offers a sound, and both sit motionless while it fades completely into nothing. Only after it has wholly vanished does the other answer with a sound of its own. In a place trapped inside a single note struck at the beginning of things and never allowed to die, making a small noise that is permitted to finish—to be born, to diminish, and to be gone—is the closest thing they keep to song, or grief, or prayer.

They live so long in the cold, unhurried stillness that it is hard to say when one of them finally grows old. Their skin grows thicker and paler across the shoulders, faint mineral creases deepening around the eyes; their steps grow even slower, pausing longer between shifts of weight until they seem nearly rooted to the phyllite. You will never find their bones in the boulder rings, nor shallow graves scratched into the gravel flats.

When the cold finally begins to settle past the bone and the slow drum in the broad chest begins to miss its intervals, an old one climbs alone to a quiet pocket along the western rim. It sets whatever pale river-cobble it has been carrying gently onto the nearest leaning cairn, steadying it with a broad palm until the valley’s quiet refusal of gravity takes hold of its weight. It rests its bare forearm one last time against the smooth stone, feeling the distant, muffled thrum of the basin it has tended and listened to for longer than any season below could count.

Then it turns outward, steps past the inward-leaning teeth of the rim, and walks steadily down the outer crags into the opaque drop of bruised pewter, where there is no timberline, no cloud-deck, and no floor—leaving behind a waist-high wall of rough slate, a fresh ring of pale lichen blooming in its quiet lee, and seven slick cobbles leaning out over nothing, unchanged inside the long, unvarying roar.
