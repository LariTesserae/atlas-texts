You could walk ten miles across this tableland—fighting every step through the springy, soaking wall of stalks—and swear with your hand on the wet stone that nothing lives here except the roaring green machine itself. You would be looking for the wrong signs. You would be listening for a cry lifted above the noise, or watching for a sudden dart of motion between the fronds, a flash of an eye, a warm body recoiling from the cold spray. None of that exists here. Whatever shares this highland with the biome-engine has no voice to spend against a wall of sound, no muscle wasted on flight, and no pulse of its own to betray it.

You only find the first one by accident, through your palm.

When you brace yourself against one of the thick, fluted stalks to keep your footing on the slick root-mat, you grow used to its relentless hydraulic purr—that rapid, pressurized shudder of water ripped out of the basalt below and driven straight up into the mist. Then, miles from anywhere, you lay your numb hand on a trunk no different from a million others, and the purr is wrong. It is thinner, damped, dropped by half a pitch, as if someone had laid a heavy wet cloth over a ringing bell.

If you bend down and force your head and shoulders into the dim, ozone-smelling tangle beneath the overlapping shingles, down where the diffuse November light barely reaches and cold droplets run steadily off the backs of your ears, you see what you would have walked straight past.

Clamped around the lower third of the fluted stalk is a broad, folded shape the size of a curled hound. At first glance it looks like nothing more than a gall, or a cluster of malformed fronds pressed tight against the trunk. Its outer skin has the same matte, light-drinking blue-black as the leaf undersides, scored with repeating geometric ribs that catch the sliding rainwater in a tight sheen like oiled graphite. Beneath its curved ridges sit pale, clouded bladders swollen with fluid. It has no head, no eyes, no jaws, no legs tucked underneath it.

Look closer at the edge where it meets the stalk.

Everywhere else on the plateau, the living carpet hates a border. Torn fronds suture themselves along invisible grids; tendrils grade smoothly into dark mineral glaze on the rock; moss and root and stem weave into one another without a seam. Nothing here is permitted to stay separate for long.

Around this clamped shape, there is a clean, dry hairline of refusal.

As you watch, frost-fine filaments—microscopic threads sent out by the stalk's epidermis—creep across that narrow margin, aligning in crisp rows to knit the shape back into the continent's singular skin. They almost touch. Then the dark, pleated rim of the clamped body gives a tiny, crisp shudder—barely visible, no more than a blur along its edge—at an off-harmonic pitch that doesn't match the trunk's vibration. Every approaching filament snaps cleanly off at the root and dissolves into the wet film. A few seconds later, the stalk tries again. Again, the rim shivers, and the line holds.

That is the first truth of what lives here: it is not alive because it breathes or runs or hungers. It is alive because it keeps a boundary against a world trying relentlessly to weave it back into the loom.

Hold still, crouched in the freezing spray, and you begin to see how it is built. It is less an animal than a folding acoustic instrument—a laminated architecture of thin, curved carbon-silica plates hinged along taut, wet membranes, enclosing a nested series of translucent hydraulic chambers. Inside it, there is no heart. Why evolve a fragile, frantic pump when an entire continent is hammering pressurized water through every vertical stem?

Instead, along its underside, hair-fine vitreous needles slide neatly between the stalk's cellular walls, slender enough to pass without triggering the plant's rupture alarms. They tap straight into the upward vascular surge. The plateau's own violent pumping drives mineral-laden sap directly through the creature's internal baffles, spinning tiny spiraling valves that strip out dissolved iron, silica, and chemical tension while the sheer pressure differential fills and vents its chambers in a slow, majestic, borrowed rhythm. When the trunk swells, it swells. When the trunk's surge ebbs, its outer plates settle tighter. Its blood—if a cold, glittering brine of mineral fluid can be called blood—moves only because the highland moves it.

And then it leaves the stalk, and you see how a thing navigates a forest with no gaps wider than a fingernail.

It does not push the foliage aside. It unlocks its rear pleated flanges from the fluted skin and vents its trailing chambers in a silent sigh of clear water. Instantly, the back half of its body collapses flat as a wet slate shingle—thin, ribbon-edged, flexible along its hinged mineral ribs. That flattened edge slides effortlessly into the hair-thin seam between two overlapping fronds. Once the leading plates have threaded through and pressed against the next trunk a foot away, the vitreous needles slide in, borrow a tiny sip of hydraulic pressure, and inflate the forward chambers with a series of faint, crisp *ticks*—utterly indistinguishable from the ratcheting of growing plant cells. Slowly, without stirring a single leaf or spilling a cup of standing rainwater, it draws its collapsed rear half through the gap after it, folding and unfolding like a dark bellows threading through the warp of a loom.

You blink away a cold drop from your eyelashes, and the stalk in front of you is humming at its full pitch again. There is no trail. Nothing even sways.

***

Up in the packed canopy, they seem solitary and small—quiet, flat-bodied parasites clinging to the margins of the machine. You only learn what they actually are if you force your way down beneath the living carpet.

It is hard, bruising work to get there, prying apart the springy, forearm-thick stalks, tearing through the damp mid-layer of runners, wedging past a yard of thick, pulsing rhizomes until your boots slip onto bare, razor-sharp rock. Down here, the dim grey light vanishes entirely. You are in a low, freezing, pitch-black crawlspace between the underside of the living mat and the freshly split basalt of the newly raised crust. Water sheet-flows across the unweathered stone in roaring subterranean sluices, foaming icy white over fractures that have never been rounded by a single century of erosion. Overhead, silver-white root filaments hang like dense wet curtains, hissing and snapping as they wedge into hairline cracks to pry the bedrock apart atom by atom.

Down here, where the rushing water carves two or three feet of raw clearance beneath the roots, the folded knots from the canopy open out.

If you sweep a pale light across the dark sluice—or simply let your hands feel the sudden, impossible architecture rising out of the freezing spray—you find them spanning six or eight feet from tip to tip. Unfolded, they are startlingly wide and delicate: curved, arched vaults of matte blue-black plates and translucent, veined ribs splayed outward like the open skeleton of a ray or the overlapping vanes of a dark fan. They brace themselves above the roaring sheet-water on dozens of slender, stiff, glass-tipped struts wedged firmly into angles of the raw basalt, holding their wide backs just beneath the hanging root-curtains.

Watch what happens to the rock directly beneath one of those arched bodies.

Everywhere else on the plateau, the bare stone is under siege. The silver-white filaments coat every facet, leaching out silica and metal, softening the fresh fractures, beginning the long, deafening work of turning naked crust into uniform green tissue and, eventually, soil.

Beneath an unfolded vault, the basalt is untouched.

Its curved, oiled-slate back catches the descending root-filaments and shivers them cleanly off before they can reach the stone below. Worse—or stranger—its wide ribs intercept the relentless sub-audible bass thrum hammering down from the vibrating canopy above. Underneath its arched shadow, the raw rock sits bare, gleaming, razor-edged, spared from the loom.

And the creature is working on it.

From the translucent ribs of its underside, scores of slender, quivering threads dangle down into the freezing white torrent rushing past its struts. They aren't hunting fish or larvae; nothing swims in this sterile, freshly split meltwater. They are feeling for *stone*—specifically, for the tiny, unweathered crystals jarred loose by the fracturing crust upstream. A microscopic prism of pale feldspar. A sharp flake of dark pyroxene. A gleaming grain of native copper or magnetite torn raw from a basalt vein.

One by one, the fine threads pluck these grains out of the rushing sluice, lift them up out of the water, and lay them with meticulous, geometric precision along the sheltered basalt beneath the vault—or cement them in tidy, gleaming rows along the creature's own translucent inner ribs, fixing each crystal in place with a slow bead of clear vitreous sap.

At first it looks like mere accumulation, the mineral equivalent of a caddisfly building a case. Then you crawl closer, ducking your soaked shoulders under the edge of the arched rim—and the world abruptly changes around your ears.

The deafening roar of the subterranean sluice does not vanish, exactly, but it *splits*.

Those hair-thin ridges of sorted quartz, iron, and volcanic glass cemented to the sheltered rock are not random. They are spaced to the exact wavelength of the continent's roaring bass thrum and the rushing pitch of the meltwater. As the vast standing waves of the plateau roll down through the bedrock and strike those tiny crystal rows, the pressure waves collide, invert, and cancel one another out. Directly under the arched vault, tucked inside a space no larger than a crouched body, the crushing atmospheric noise thins into a hushed, shimmering chord—a pocket of eerie, crystalline stillness hollowed out of the solid roar.

There are no buildings on this plateau. No brick has been laid upon it, no clearing cut through its endless green weave. Yet here, under the roaring roots, they are carving rooms out of cancelled sound.

Look closely at the underside of a large one's arched ribs, and you see that it is not alone. Clinging to the sheltered, crystal-inlaid hollows of its vaulted belly are smaller versions of itself—palm-sized blisters of wet slate and clouded glass, thin as shells, riding out of the torrent's reach. They begin whenever a rock-slip in a sluice shears a crystal-lined shard from an adult's rim, or whenever two of them deliberately pinch a tiny wedge of tissue off along a seam and press it into a damp crack of raw stone. Most such fragments are quickly overwhelmed by the biome-engine's repair filaments and woven straight back into green stalks. Only the ones that hold the right embedded crystals—and land at the right harmonic intersection of the highland's song—catch the ambient tremor in their tiny mineral lattices and begin to shiver on their own. *Snap. Snap.* Cutting the claiming threads. Tapping a microscopic needle into the nearest root-hair. Borrowing their first drop of pressure, learning the quiet art of remaining separate.

***

To see them together—to understand how many of them the endless green highland actually hides—you have to climb up onto one of the jagged twenty-foot spines of newly uplifted rock that jut out of the canopy, and wait for a shift to roll across the plateau.

You see the shift miles off before you hear its deeper pitch: that dark ripple running over the dim slate-green hills, the great coordinated tilt of millions of fronds, the white wall of transpired mist venting from the bladders across the ridges. As the sub-audible hum slides down a register until your vision blurs from the vibration in your skull, the packed mat around the base of your rock spine begins to stir against the rhythm of the foliage.

They rise out of the canopy slowly, unhurriedly, like dark tide-things surfacing from a dense sea of wet shingles. Dozens at first, then hundreds, emerging from between the fluted stalks around the crag's foot. They climb the slick, vertical, moss-plastered flanks of the basalt on clicking rows of stiff, glass-tipped struts, pulling their broad, matte blue-black mantles up out of the vibrating leaves until they coat the jagged peak in overlapping shields of oiled slate and clouded mica.

They do not hide from the squall-shift. They climb into the fullest weight of it.

As the cold white mist swallows the ridge and the continent's roar swells until it feels solid enough to lean against, they draw closer together across the raw stone tooth—and lock their pleated rims. One creature's fluted edge slides cleanly into its neighbor's groove. Even now, they do not fuse; along every joined seam, you can see that razor-thin hairline of dry refusal, both rims shivering faintly in matched phase so neither can knit into the other. Yet mechanically they become a single immense, curved bowl—a vast, living parabolic shell of layered carbon-silica draped across the uplifted basalt, braced straight into the incoming wall of sound.

Their translucent internal bladders swell with cold mist and borrowed tension until the whole clustered crag catches the flat silver sky in a tight, cool, pearlescent gleam.

Then the bowl leans into the surge, and the roar *parts*.

If you are huddled on the sheltered lee of the basalt spine, behind their joined, arched backs, the sudden change is dizzying enough to drop you to your knees. The bone-shaking bass pressure slides cleanly off your ribs, deflected up and around the curved wall of interlocked plates. The howling flute-song of a million hollow stalks recedes to a distant, papery hush. For the span of a few breathless minutes—for as long as the shift rolls over the ridge—a dome of startling near-silence opens around the crag. You hear your own ragged breathing. You hear a single cold drop fall from your drenched sleeve and *plip* sharply onto naked basalt.

And inside that stolen quiet, they begin to speak.

It is nothing like a voice. Nothing like birdsong or insect whine. It is made of the smallest, crispest sounds imaginable—sounds that would be obliterated instantly outside the dome.

Across the clustered hundreds of them, crystal-lined rims graze delicately against crystal-lined rims. Taut, mist-wet tendons stretched beneath their translucent plates are plucked by tiny mineral spurs. A shimmering cascade of micro-chimes, glassy ticks, and frost-fine clicks sweeps back and forth across the curved shell—sounding less like living throats than like thin ice forming rapidly across a black mountain lake, or fine glass beads spilling down wet slate. Mathematical lattices of tone and phase ripple from plate to plate, answering, inverting, braiding together.

And while the quiet holds, they trade stone.

Watch closely in the drifting white vapor where two curved undersides lift slightly to meet along a narrow gap. Slender vitreous filaments pass a tiny, gleaming grain of dark magnetite or pale olivine from one creature's inner rib-lattice to the other's. Each one carries, cemented in precise gleaming lines beneath its vaulted plates, a mosaic of raw minerals harvested from valleys fifty or a hundred miles apart—passed rim to rim across the highland during these brief hushed meetings. They are not hoarding wealth. They are comparing the shape of the newly split crust underneath the green mat: where a seam of stubborn quartz still resists the roots, where a fresh fault has opened overnight, where the subterranean sluices run richest with unweathered glass.

Then the squall-shift moves on toward the dim horizon. The continent's bass hum slides back up a register. The white mist thins into steady, fine rain. Without haste, the interlocked rims uncouple with soft clicks, the dome of quiet collapses back into the familiar deafening roar, and the dark shields stream slowly back down the wet basalt tooth, flattening and sliding neatly back into the packed green wall until the crag looks bare and empty again.

***

They do not rot when they end. Nothing rots here yet; there is no loam to swallow a corpse, no scavenger to strip it.

Instead, an old one—its translucent ribs grown thick and heavy with layers of deposited silica and rows of traded crystals, its hydraulic chambers gradually stiffening until its borrowed pulse comes slower and stiffer—simply stops ratcheting through the stalks. It makes its way up onto a high basalt spine, or settles down over one of its sheltered patches of raw stone in a subterranean sluice. It vents its bladders flat for the last time.

And it stops shivering.

The biome-engine wastes not a second. Frost-fine filaments pour across the motionless rim, knitting cleanly into the dark plates. Within hours, the curved shell is glazed seamlessly into the bedrock or the nearest fluted trunk. Within days, broad slate-green fronds and thick vascular stems sprout straight up through its hollow chambers, claiming its carbon back into the endless loom.

Yet the crystals it cemented along its inner ribs do not dissolve quickly, and its arched, tuned mineral lattice remains buried inside the living mat like a sunken bell. Wherever one of them stops moving, the standing wave of the plateau bends slightly as it passes through that spot—dropped by a fraction of a pitch, carrying a faint, permanent harmonic quirk. Even as the green engine works through the long ages ahead to grind this raw continent down into soft, quiet earth where heart-beating things might someday walk, these first, heartless inhabitants are quietly stitching tiny, enduring chords into its bones—the memory of what the naked stone sounded like before it ever knew soil.

***

They do not see you when you come onto the plateau. Having no eyes, they know nothing of your silhouette against the grey haze, or the dull color of your soaked coat.

What they feel is the *hole* you punch in the world.

Across a continent where every stem, bladder, and rock seam rings in crisp harmonic geometry, you are an astonishing, ragged blur. Your wet wool and soft, water-logged flesh do not transmit the standing waves; you swallow them, dragging a messy, silent acoustic shadow behind you wherever you force your way through the stalks. You radiate an absurd, feverish heat into air just above freezing.

And worst of all—loudest of all, down in the subtle phase-currents they read with their pleated rims—is the impossible percussion trapped inside your chest.

*Thump-thump. Thump-thump. Thump-thump.*

Fast. Uneven. Completely out of phase with the majestic, sub-audible pulse of the highland. To a creature that has never owned a beat of its own, you are a creature carrying a tiny, furious, isolated storm locked inside a bag of skin—refusing to match the continent's rhythm, refusing to knit into the green mat, wandering across the fresh stone even more fiercely separate than they are.

You may not realize they have noticed you until you stop walking for a few minutes—wedged chest-deep in the wet, springy stalks, catching your breath while the cold mist beads on your eyelashes.

First, you feel the vibration in the stems immediately around you grow strangely dense, damped, drawn inward. Then, if you hold very still and look down past your soaked sleeves into the dim blue-black tangle, you see that three of the broad, matte shapes wedged vertically between the nearby stalks are not fronds. Without rustling a leaf or shaking a drop of water loose, they have ratcheted closer by inches.

One of them has surfaced slowly from the wet velvet moss around your drenched boots. It does not flee your warmth, and it does not strike. Gently, with infinite mineral patience, it extends the thin, pleated rim of its oiled-slate mantle until the dry, shivering margin rests lightly against the wet leather over your ankle—just barely touching, shivering every few seconds so the surrounding stalks won't knit it to you, holding perfectly still as it drinks in the wild, tiny, private rhythm hammering down through your bones.
