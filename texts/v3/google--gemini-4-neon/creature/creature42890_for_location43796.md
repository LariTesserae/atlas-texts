If you stay on the middle catwalk only long enough to spot the amphibian on the gauge, the matte-white snail on the mortar line, and the blind fish turning beneath your boots, you will leave thinking you have taken the vault’s whole census. You will think the place is a terrarium that happened to pick up three stray tenants on its way up the mountain.

Wait through one full cycle of the tipping cistern instead.

Wait for the soft wooden *clunk* high under the gable, the sheet of warm condensate spilling down the stepped porcelain weir, and the milky veil of steam that rises off the floor coils to erase the far glass wall. Stand still while the cold panes pull the water back out of the air and the crisp, high-altitude light returns in green and silver slabs.

When the far end clears, the pale bundle you took for a spare roll of mineral wool—or a painter’s drop-cloth draped neatly over the unfinished span of the second catwalk, right where the iron decking ends mid-air—is six feet closer than it was.

It does not startle when you look straight at it. It has not been hiding from you; it was simply waiting out the mist in the posture that sheds water best.

At rest, it is almost impossible to separate from the fixtures. Folded down along the pale-grey flange of an I-beam, knees drawn tight under its chin and long arms tucked lengthwise along its shins, it reads as a slung counterweight, a wrapped conduit, a sack of dry insulation waiting for the fitters. Its hide is the same muted, bone-pale tone as the uncured concrete and the dry mineral mats, with a fine, matte grain over the shoulders, spine, and outer limbs that drinks ambient moisture. In the minutes right after the cistern tips, that pale surface darkens a shade into soft dove-grey, just as the new wool trays do; when a cloud peels off the roof and hard sun slams straight through the clerestory, it pales again as the steam lifts off it in a faint, clean wisp.

Then it shifts, and you see that nothing mechanical bends quite like that.

It unspools itself from the beam with the slow, felt-baffled continuity of the gantry overhead—no jerk, no scramble, no wasted leverage. Fully extended, it is nearly the length of a tall person, though far narrower through the ribs and hips, built for the packed three-dimensional clearances between suspended trays, vertical irrigation fins, and low brass unions. It almost never puts its weight on the stamped steel grating below. The diamond ridges that bite through soft soles would score its skin, so it treats the floor as the plants do: as a source of heat and rising mist rather than ground. Instead, it travels through the middle and upper thirds of the chamber, stepping along riveted pipe-hangers, hooking its long, narrow feet around insulated conduit, swinging from truss to truss with a quiet, unhurried economy that barely stirs the hanging curtains of aerial roots.

Only the faintest sounds betray its passage: the damp, rubbery release of a palm lifting off wet galvanized steel; a soft brush of broad leaves parting and closing again; now and then, a tiny, wet-dry click deep in its throat, almost indistinguishable from the expanding brass thermostat on the east wall.

Up close—if it crosses a beam within arm’s reach of your catwalk—its strangeness becomes very exact.

It has no hair, no feathers, no scales, nothing that could mat or mildew in permanent stove-side steam. Where the skin stretches thin across the inner bend of its elbows, the hollow of its throat, and the shallow webs between its fingers, it turns nearly translucent, like the amphibian on the pressure gauge or the waxy pitchers bolted to the vapor leaks. Beneath that watery veil, you can make out the faint, slow pulse of pale-rose vessels—slower than a human heart, beating in an odd, steady cadence that seems to answer the low transformer hum vibrating up through the steel arches.

Along its brow, its high, smooth crown, and the sharp line of its collarbones, the flesh is subtly fluted into neat, shallow channels. At first they look like ornament, or the folded ribs of a young frond, until a heavy drop falls from a high copper union straight onto its head. The water does not scatter across its face or blur its vision; it catches in the pale grooves above its temples, splits cleanly past the corners of its eyes, glides behind its jaw, and runs down the ridge of its spine to drip silently off the tips of its elbows into the troughs below. It is built to live in rain that never stops.

Its eyes are large, pale, and set wide beneath those fluted brows, clear as distilled water when the vault is dimmed by passing cloud. Watch closely when the cloud-top drifts past the exterior sill and the mountain glare hits the clerestory again. In exact synchrony with the copper expansion-rods lengthening along the ridge and the frosted glass louvers tilting back into place, a second, pearlescent membrane slides smoothly across each eye, veiling the dark pupil in a soft, milk-glass sheen. When shadow returns, the film draws back. Its gaze clears and clouds with the weather outside the double-glazed seals, as obedient to the altitude as the building itself.

Its hands are the thing that lingers longest in the mind. Each ends in four long, caliper-slender fingers and a deep-set thumb, tipped with wide, pale pads slightly tacky with moisture. There is nothing feral in how it uses them. When it grips a wet brass union or steadies itself against a porcelain baffle, its fingers settle with the quiet, unhurried literalism of a draftsman setting down a compass. Along the inner creases of its knuckles and the heels of its palms sit faint, pale grooves—already shaped to the diameter of half-inch copper tubing and the flat edge of steel flanges.

Yet look closely at those grooves, and at the rest of its long, pale body, and something quietly unsettling surfaces: it has no scars.

Not a single nicked knuckle from a sharp sheet-metal edge. Not a healed scrape, not a weathered line around its mouth, not a grain of soil or grease lodged beneath its pale, rounded nails. Its skin is as unmarked as the unrusted rivets, as freshly set as the corner panes in their oily linseed putty. Whatever this creature is—whether it came up in the same crates as the porcelain baffles and woke when the immersion coils first glowed, or slipped out of the curing cistern before the water finished clouding, or simply condensed wherever high sky, warm metal, and living vapor meet—it is as brand-new as the vault.

And yet it already knows every clock in the room.

Three heartbeats before the eleventh minute finishes, before the high copper cistern under the gable reaches its tipping point, the resident pauses wherever it happens to be. It slides down a vertical feeder pipe to a tier just beneath the stepped porcelain weir, folds its pale knees against a clay-filled trough, and tilts its shoulders outward. When the soft wooden *clunk* sounds overhead and the warm nutrient condensate spills downward through four stories of mesh and roots, the resident does not flinch or duck away. It lets the warm mist envelop it completely, its fluted collarbones channeling the runoff across its narrow chest while its skin darkens and drinks.

After the mist begins to thin, it feeds—unhurried, almost fastidious.

It climbs along the middle tier to a pair of bolted flanges where warm vapor seeps continuously around the hex nuts, feeding clusters of swollen, translucent pitchers. Reaching out with two long fingers, it does not tear the pitchers free or bruise their waxy rims. It tilts one gently on its stem, parts the narrow, quiet seam of its mouth—no teeth visible, only pale, smooth gums and a narrow tongue—and drinks the warm, faintly sweet, mineral-rich condensate collected inside, then lets the pitcher settle upright against the brass nut again to refill.

Later, when the mist has cleared enough to show the pale concrete cistern through the floor grating, you may see it descend to the lowest catwalk, kneel carefully with its shins balanced along the smooth angle-iron rim so the sharp grating never touches it, and lift one of the brass inspection hatches. It lowers one long forearm straight down into the warm, cloudy lime-water, fingers spread loose and still between two glowing copper coils. After a minute, the blind, ribbon-finned fish noses out from under a concrete baffle and glides lazily across the submerged pale wrist, brushing its translucent fins along the skin before drifting back into the milky shadow. The resident does not try to catch it. Instead, its fingertips trail lightly along the wet concrete formwork seam, lifting a tiny, chalky flake of curing lime-bloom to its tongue before it withdraws its arm, shakes the cloudy water off cleanly into the cistern, and lowers the brass hatch back into its seat without a clatter.

It treats the other small citizens with the same unsentimental, exact regard.

When it passes the circular brass pressure gauge on the third stanchion, it shifts its weight onto the opposite conduit so the pipe will not vibrate and disturb the thumb-sized amphibian flattened against the warm bezel. Once an hour, it checks the freshly poured concrete curb where the matte-white snail rasps its neat wet track through the alkaline crust. Where a galvanized floor-bracket interrupts the mortar line, leaving a gap of bare, dry steel the snail cannot cross without losing the lime seam, the resident reaches down, lifts the snail by the thin porcelain lip of its shell—so lightly the pale horns barely retract—and sets it down neatly on the far side of the bracket, watching motionless for half a minute until the wet silver trail resumes.

It has no nest, no cache of salvaged objects, no sleeping-hollow lined with shed leaves. There is nothing anywhere in the vault that says *mine*. Instead, its attention belongs wholly to the unfinished seams of the place, as if it were quietly overseeing its own settling-in.

Whenever the silent overhead gantry begins its minute-hand crawl along the ridge I-beam, the resident moves up into the bright, hot air near the clerestory to meet it. It perches on a steel cross-brace above the empty cradle, watching the four braided cables lower a fresh tray of dry, bone-pale mineral wool. As soon as the latches release with their four tiny metallic clicks and the cables begin to wind back into the carriage, the resident leans forward and lays both flat palms directly onto the dry mat. It holds them there while the sharp, dusty dryness yields to the room’s steam, feeling the edges of the wool darken and soften under its fingers. Often, before it climbs away, it will reach into the adjacent tray, gently unwind the tip of a silver-veined climber that has been leaning across the gap, and drape the pale tendril onto the newly dampened mat—not forcing it, just shortening the distance by an inch.

At the unfinished end of the second catwalk, where the next section of iron decking hangs suspended from its silent trolley, it visits the dangling plumb-bobs. It touches a brass weight with one wet fingertip, holding it just lightly enough to feel the faint electrical thrum of the buried transformers traveling up the taut waxed twine, then cups its hand around the bob until it hangs dead still, perfectly vertical over nothing. Sometimes it lingers beside the exposed low-voltage copper busbars, where the climbers wrap in tight, thickened spirals, and rests the pale hollow beneath its jaw against the warm metal for minutes at a time, eyes half-veiled, soaking in the mild inductive hum the way the frog drinks heat from the pressure bezel.

Its only real restlessness comes near the far gable, where the last curved panes of plate glass sit in fresh, sharp-smelling linseed putty.

When a mountain cloud drifts up from below the sill line and presses directly against that spotless corner glass like cold wool, dimming the vault to pearled twilight, the resident climbs down from the upper trusses. It settles on the ceramic manifold box where the three copper pipes meet, careful not to crush the hair-fine filament of green moss creeping across the brass pressure valve, and presses the flat of its forearm—or sometimes its pale brow—straight against the freezing glass.

The contrast makes a thin, barely audible sigh escape its throat. Beyond that half-inch of double-glazed seal lies everything the chamber was built to exclude: empty violet sky, thin air, cold wind flattened to a dull pressure against the mullions, no moisture, no coils, no humming copper. The warmth of the resident’s skin cuts a wide, clear patch straight through the running curtain of condensation, opening a brief, spotless window into the cloud-wool outside. It stays pressed there until the cloud peels away and the harsh mountain glare slams back through the glass, sending its frosted eye-films sliding shut. When it lifts its arm away, the clean outline of its pale skin lingers for only a few seconds before the steam heals the glass over again.

When evening finally drops below the mountain ridge and the empty sky turns black and bitter, the vault does not go quiet, only deeper. The copper expansion-rods pull the roof louvers tight; the submerged coils beneath the grating brighten to a low, steady ember-glow that throws a faint diamond lattice of orange light upward through the mist and across the broad folded fronds. The steam vents sigh a little louder through their porous ceramic sleeves.

That is when the resident climbs back toward the ridge. It does not hide in the foliage; it seeks the warmest, plainest geometry it can find—sometimes curled tight inside an empty steel cradle waiting for tomorrow’s mineral-wool tray, sometimes draped lengthwise along the thick ceramic steam-sleeves near the gable, long limbs tucked neatly along its ribs so it becomes a bundle of pale fixture once more. Breathing in slow, porous rhythm with the venting steam, it stays there until the hard, un-hazed daylight strikes the upper clerestory again.

Wait long enough on the middle catwalk, and you will realize it has been aware of you since the moment the heavy sealed door shut behind you and your boots first rang against the sharp new steel.

It does not retreat to the far gable, and it does not try to drive you back down the aisle. Instead, once your shirt has stuck to your back and your breathing has slowed to the close warmth of the chamber, it glides quietly along an overhead pipe until it perches directly above the heavy brass unions just in front of you. Condensation beads along its pale elbows and drops past your shoulder onto a broad silver-veined leaf with a muted *plink*.

A cloud passes outside. The roof louvers tilt open, and the milky inner veil slides back from its clear, water-pale eyes. It tilts its fluted brow a fraction of an inch—taking in the dry wool of your sleeve, the brass button at your cuff, the rapid, lowland pulse visible in your neck, the way your boots grip the stamped grating without anchoring into it.

It makes no sound, and it asks nothing of you. It simply watches with that calm, literal patience, as if waiting to learn which tray you were unspooled from, what clock you run on, and where you intend to put down your roots.
