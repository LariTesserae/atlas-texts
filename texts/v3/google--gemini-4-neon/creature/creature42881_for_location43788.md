If you stand still after the lightning dies, letting your eyes give up their useless straining against the dark, you begin to realize that the figures wedged into the basalt notches are not hiding from the valley. They are folded into it the way a knot is folded into a rope.

They are smaller than you might expect from the weight of their hammer-blows—compact, low-bellied, with long, rope-muscled forearms and broad, sloping shoulders built less for brute pushing than for pulling their own weight up thirty feet of slick aerial root in three smooth reaches. In the brief orange spit of a flint-spark, what catches the eye first is the texture of them. They wear no pelts, no woven cloth; anything loose or shaggy in this basin would snag on thorn-vines, rot in the warm squalls, and harbor a crawling coat of mites within an hour. Instead, their skin is nearly bare—tough, loose-slung over dense tendon, mottled the color of wet slate and bruised plum. Along their forearms, the ridges of their brows, and the line of their spines grows a sparse bristle of stiff, wire-fine hairs, never thick enough to hold water, standing just proud enough from the hide to register a drifting spider-silk vine or the faint cool downdraft of an unseen wingbeat before anything touches flesh.

Almost all of them are smeared from collarbone to shin with a gritty, pale-ochre paste scraped from the hot mineral vents along the valley’s belly and mashed with the bitter, oily sap of crushed rosette leaves. It dries into a chalky, sour-smelling armor in the creases of their elbows and knees, sealing the constant small nicks of volcanic glass and tasting foul enough to make the raining beetles and flat centipedes skitter right off them.

Their feet and hands tell the whole story of the ground beneath them. Their soles are thick as cured horn, deeply fissured without bleeding, and their long toes—the innermost opposable and heavily muscled—do not slap flat onto loose clinker. They grip raw basalt ridges the way a perched bird grips wire, curling over razor angles with a delicate, testing pressure before committing weight. Their hands are even stranger in their contradiction: broad-palmed, with thick, scarred callus-plates shielding the outer heel of the hand and the backs of the knuckles where they wedge against stone, yet ending in wide, surprisingly supple finger-pads crowded with nerves. A full-grown knapper can brace a palm directly on a fresh fracture of obsidian without flinching, and a second later read the microscopic serration of a flake-edge just by drawing it across the bare skin beneath their thumb.

Their faces stay angled slightly down, heavy-jawed and narrow-templed. They still have eyes—small, dark, oil-slick slits tucked deep under projecting, bony brows that shield them from whipping canes and flying stone-chips—but those eyes are almost vestigial, useful only for catching the sudden blue-green smear of a startled creature’s mucus or flinching away from the rare, blinding violet of sheet-lightning. To them, light is not revelation; it is an irritating, flat glare that briefly bleaches out a world they know with far greater precision through pressure, scent, and echo.

What actually governs their heads are their ears and their jaws. Their outer ears are not broad dishes that thorns would shred; they are thick, leather-rimmed, intricately folded flaps lying nearly flush against the skull, ringed with fine muscles that let them clamp nearly shut during a deafening squall or flare slightly, independent of one another, to slice a single dry frequency out of the basin’s roaring soup. And when they want to know what is happening three ridges over, they do not stand up to look. They crouch and press the flat of a jawbone or the heel of a palm straight against a bare rib of volcanic rock, letting the dense bone drink the tremor of a shifting scree-slope or the low, rolling thunder of barrel-ribbed grazers changing course half a mile away.

---

To understand how they think, you have to stop imagining the valley as dark.

For them, the darkness is full of architecture—not of walls and corners, which would crumble in a month, but of acoustics, heat-gradients, and tension. They know the basin the way a blind swimmer knows a fast river: by the way the current piles up against hidden stones. A dense cane-brake sounds tight, papery, and dry when the wind hits it empty, and muffled, wet, and ticking when a pack of low-slung scavengers is threading through its roots. An overhanging basalt slab rings hollow when a fissure beneath it is widening after a heavy rain. Every brief squall remaps the soundscape for twenty minutes—muting the high trills of the insects, amplifying the drip of filling plant-cups, making the slick canes groan louder under the weight of water—and they adjust their routes automatically to the altered echoes.

Because every living throat in the valley is screaming, clicking, booming, or hissing, they almost never use their own voices at a distance. A shouted call would simply dissolve into the biological wash—or worse, draw the blind mid-tier climbers swarming down through the lianas to investigate a warm-blooded sound.

Instead, their public voices hang from their hips on braided inner-bark cords.

These are among the very few objects they refuse to abandon. While a jagged cane-knife or a water-soaked lichen-twig can be discarded after a single afternoon and remade in minutes from the clutter underfoot, a good pair of clackers takes months to earn. They hunt for rare knots of dense, drowned heartwood dragged down in landslides, whittle them into palm-sized blocks with obsidian scrapers, and wedge them for weeks inside the dry, sulfurous mouths of geothermal steam-vents until every drop of moisture is baked out and the wood cures almost to the hardness of stone. Struck together, they produce a sound nothing born of flesh and breath can make: a razor-short, utterly dry percussive spike that slices straight through the wet chorus of wings and shrieks and bounces cleanly off the basalt walls.

Alongside the clackers hangs a notched marrow-bone—usually the thick femur of a bulk-feeder, scraped hollow and filed along one ridge with twenty uneven grooves—and a sliver of dense flint to drag across it. Every adult’s kit has its own recognizable timbre. One individual’s wood-blocks ring high and slightly hollow; another’s carry a tight double-overtone; an elder’s bone-rasp has a worn, whistling catch on the fourth notch. Across a mile of churning, pitch-black foliage, a sudden *clack-clack—clack* followed by a rising zip of bone doesn’t merely mean *danger from the south ridge*. It tells every listener in the trough *who* is up on the spine, how close the stampede is, whether the herd is running fast or grazing slow, and which vine-corridor is still open.

When they come together—huddled five or six deep beneath a tilted basalt shelf while a lukewarm squall hammers the canopy overhead—the wood-blocks go silent. Up close, they speak in an entirely different register: bone-to-bone and skin-to-skin. They sit shoulder pressed to shoulder, knees tucked tight, humming low, gravelly vibrations deep in their chests that carry straight through ribs and collarbones without leaking ten feet into the noisy air. Meanwhile, their fingers move constantly—tapping quick, intricate grammars against a neighbor’s forearm, tracing the curve of a shoulder-blade, squeezing a wrist in rhythmic pulses. Through those dry taps and chest-hums pass everything that matters: where a deep rock-seam is brimming clean water; which ridge-apron feels loose underfoot; a mockery of someone who slipped in algae and dropped their sling; the quiet, heavy cadence that means an old companion stayed behind on the far slope and did not answer the morning call.

---

Watch one of them work a core of volcanic glass, and you see how deeply patience lives inside all this frantic motion.

They do not strike blindly in the dark. Perched wedged in a stone notch high enough that ground-scavengers cannot rush them without clattering loudly over loose clinker, the knapper holds a freshly pried lump of obsidian near their cheekbone and taps it gently with a fingernail or a pebble. They are listening to the stone’s interior. A piece riddled with hidden cooling fractures answers with a dull, flat *tock*; the knapper tosses it over the edge without a second thought. Clean, tight volcanic glass answers with a bright, ringing *ping*.

Then comes the other true heirloom they carry everywhere: the hammerstone. In a valley without rivers, naturally rounded cobbles do not exist. A good hammerstone is made over decades—a dense fist of fine-grained basalt battered against thousands of cores until its sharp edges wear down into a smooth, pitted, pear-shaped weight that nests perfectly in a calloused palm. These stones outlive their owners. When a knapper grows too old or falls, someone pries the smooth, grease-darkened rock from their fingers and slips it into a vine-sling, carrying the shape of dead hands forward into the next generation.

*Tink-tink-clack.*

The sound is rhythmic, deliberate, almost meditative. If the band is trying to stay unnoticed, the knapper curls a broad, leathery leaf over the striking platform like a hood to swallow the brief shower of orange sparks. More often, high on a bare volcanic rib where ambush is difficult, they let the sparks fly—tiny, spitting flares that flash across creased knuckles and focused, down-tilted faces. They never inspect a detached flake by holding it up to the faint ceiling-glow; they know by the crisp, glassy *shhhk* of the separation whether the blade ran long and razor-true or hinged thick and useless. Within three minutes, the best flakes are wedged into the split end of a fresh hollow cane, bound tight with damp, chewed strips of inner-bark fiber that shrink like iron as they dry, and the sharp debitage is simply swept off the rock to join the glittering drifts below.

---

They do not hunt the valley’s giants head-on, nor do they attempt to clear and hold any patch of ground. They are riders of the churn—masters of the brief, violent openings that the basin’s instability tears apart every afternoon.

When a hillside of loose clinker finally lets go under the weight of a grazing herd, most creatures flee the roar or panic into the air. A loose band of eight or ten of these people—what might be called a knot, though its membership shifts every few squalls as groups braid together and pull apart—hears the slide differently. Through the soles of their feet and the shifting slap of echoes off the opposing wall, they read the slide’s exact size, where the rock-apron will pile up, and which hollows below will be buried.

Before the dust has even settled into the wet leaves, they are moving—not along the razor ground, where ankle-deep clinker shifts unpredictably, but fifteen feet up, swinging silently along the thigh-thick lianas and springing from one swaying palisade of hollow cane to the next. They move with an uncanny, flowing economy, never gripping a vine without testing its tautness for a fraction of a second, letting the bend of one cane fling them cleanly into the fork of another.

At the ragged hem of the fresh landslide, they drop down for four minutes of furious, synchronized harvest. A slide is a feast laid open: crushed burrower-tunnels spilling stunned, armor-plated grubs that they pry from shattered rock with notched marrow-bones; torn lianas weeping clear, slightly sweet sap; whole slabs of damp stone flipped upside down to expose sheets of pale, faintly glowing fungus; sometimes a young bulk-feeder caught with a broken leg beneath a tilted slab of basalt. They work with blistering speed—flensing strips of warm meat with disposable obsidian slivers, jamming sweet tubers and broken pods into their woven vine-slings, dunking lichen-sponges deep into newly cracked water-seams and wringing them straight into their mouths.

Above them, perched motionless on a bare spine of rock, a single lookout listens past the local chaos. The lookout hears what the harvesters are too busy to catch: the rising, chittering hiss of a scavenger swarm converging from two troughs away, drawn by the blood and the glowing fungus, and behind it, the snapping of canes as a territorial pack of mid-tier climbers drops out of the canopy to contest the wreck.

*Clack-clack-clack.* Sharp, triple, urgent.

A split second later, a muffled scraping whistle answers from the trough below. The harvesters do not grab one last handful. They drop the bloody flint flakes where they kneel, hoist the heavy vine-slings diagonally across their chests, and pour upward into the hanging curtains of aerial roots. By the time the first narrow-chested scavengers boil out of the root-tunnels snapping blindly at the air, the band is already forty yards away, swinging weightlessly over the thrashing chaos, leaving behind only a few fresh-chipped blades and the faint sour smell of mineral paste.

---

Because nothing here obeys a sun or moon, their lives have no days or nights—only the irregular pulse of squalls, thermal shifts, and herd-movements. They never sleep all at once, and they never sleep flat on the stone. When exhaustion catches up with a knot, they seek out either a narrow vertical chimney high in a fractured basalt face—where they wedge themselves in, backs braced against one wall and horn-soled feet against the other—or a dense thicket of springy canes ten yards above the ground. There, in five minutes, they lash together a taut web of vine-slings stretched between four living stems.

It is an alarm system as much as a bed. If a heavy climber sets even one hooked digit on a cane thirty feet away, the shiver travels straight up the hollow stem into the taut cords, snapping three pairs of folded ears awake before a single leaf rustles. Always, one or two sit awake at the edge of the web, knees drawn up, knuckles resting lightly on the vibrating lianas, quietly braiding fresh inner-bark cordage by feel while the others sleep in tangled heaps of warm, scarred limbs.

Children are born into this motion and learn its grammar before they ever touch basalt with their bare soles. For their first two years, infants ride strapped high between an adult’s shoulder blades in tightly woven, springy vine-cradles lined with crushed dry moss and broad, waxy leaves—tucked safely out of reach of low thorns and ground-bites. They learn to grip the stiff bristles along their carrier’s shoulders automatically whenever the body beneath them coils to leap. Before they can speak or knap, their mothers and fathers tap simple rhythms against their ribs and place smooth, harmless bone-rings or worn pebbles in their small palms to click together.

When a child is old enough to climb down onto the glass, the older ones wrap their soft soles for a season in layered, tough leaf-sheaths bound with bast, peeling the wraps away gradually on flatter slabs while rubbing warm resin and mineral grit into the tender skin until the natural horn-callus thickens. Their first real lessons are not in striking stone, but in *stillness*—learning to freeze completely when a double-clack drops from above, folding their small ears flat, letting a roaring tide of horned grazers thunder past three paces beneath their perch without making a single wet, frightened sound.

And when age finally stiffens their hips, slowing their grip on the wet lianas or dulling the high frequencies in their ears, they are neither cast out nor carried like dead weight—for no one can carry another adult across a sliding basalt ridge. Instead, the oldest gradually migrate upward. They leave the frantic lower troughs to the nimble knots and climb, slow and methodical, onto the high, bare volcanic spines that pierce the canopy, where the bulk-feeders cannot climb and the foliage thins to stunted scrub.

There, wedged into sheltered rock-notches beneath the bruised pewter ceiling, the elders become the long memory and the wide ears of the basin. They sit through squall after squall, taking in the whole multi-mile roar at once—tracking the slow drift of the great herds, noting which ridge-wall has begun to groan with fresh fissures, listening for the clacker-signatures of scattered bands moving below. Younger climbers swing up to their perches every few squalls, bringing slung bundles of meat, split pods, spongy water-bundles, and raw nodules of fresh obsidian. In return, the elders hand down piles of finely knapped blades, freshly cured wood-clackers, and—through long, unhurried sessions of knuckle-tapping and chest-humming—a complete acoustic map of how the valley has rearranged itself since the last storm.

When an elder’s hammerstone finally goes silent on a high ridge, whoever finds them takes only the smooth, pitted stone and the notched femur-rasp from their belt. There is no soil to bury a body in, and no stone piled high would survive a season. They simply ease the body off the bare spine into the heaving black-green sea below, letting the valley reclaim its salt and marrow within the hour, and carry the dead one’s voice-bone down into the troughs until a younger hand wears its notches smooth.

---

Yet if you stay long enough in the hot, roaring dark—if you learn to filter out the shrieking climbers, the wet slap of membrane wings, and the brittle snap of trampled cane—you may eventually hear something that makes no sense at all in terms of sheer survival.

It happens along the high, jagged rifts where the valley’s belly exhales its steady, mineral-stinking updrafts. Growing thick along the lips of those vents are tall stands of mature hollow cane, their stems bleached pale by the warm sulfurous fumes. If you climb near one of those rifts when the wind shifts, you will hear a long, braided chord rising cleanly above the chaotic din—a deep, thrumming drone answered by two higher, hauntingly clear notes that swell and dip as the hot draft pulses from the crack below.

They have climbed out along the unstable lip of the rift with fresh obsidian flakes and carefully slit the living canes at measured heights, shaving the edges of the notches thin and beveling the joints inside until the standing thicket itself becomes a vast, temporary instrument.

It has a practical use, of course—the pitch changes sharply whenever a squall-front approaches or the rift-pressure shifts, warning every band within a mile that the air is about to turn. Within a month or two, the canes will split too wide, or a landslide will shear the whole brake away, and the song will vanish as completely as their footprints.

Still, if you listen closely in the brief hush after a ten-minute downpour, while steam hisses softly off the sunless stone, you will sometimes find a knot of them sitting right beside those singing canes. Not knapping, not weaving, not watching for scavengers. Just wedged close together in the warm rock, broad scarred palms resting lightly against the vibrating hollow stems, eyes closed, folded ears turned gently toward the rift—drinking in the one clean, deliberate harmony they have carved out of a roaring world that will never let them keep anything else.

Then a distant slab shifts somewhere across the basin. A dry double-clack cuts sharp through the mist from a neighboring spine. Their palms lift from the singing cane in unison; vine-slings tighten across pale, clay-smeared shoulders; and without a single wasted sound, they drop off the lip of the rock into the surging foliage, swallowed whole by the dark that belongs to them.
