At first, you do not see them, because nothing in the valley looks separate from the valley for long.

In the freezing, stuttering grey between two stamping flanks of a bellows-herd, a dark hump shifts out of time with the rest. It is too narrow for a beast, too quick for a benthic presser, and it does not shudder when the twin jets of scalding steam blast outward behind the herd’s shoulders. Instead, it leans straight into the edge of the plume, holding up a thin, dark slab of ground shale. Hot condensate beads instantly on the cold stone and runs down a worn thumb-groove into a stiff bladder stitched from pale gut. Right before the four-second cycle resets and the hooves come down again, the figure ducks low, tucks the bladder beneath a grease-slick apron, and sidesteps cleanly through a hand’s-breadth gap of churning slush.

Then the voltage squall rolls off the low hills. The metallic gnats overhead lock into humming ribbons. A ragged blue arc drops out of the fog, skips across three frost-glazed backs, and hits the crouching figure dead between the shoulder blades.

It does not convulse. Blue fire skitters smoothly down the outside of its hunched coat—a loose, knotted mesh of copper-sheathed tendon salvaged from the hexagonal barnacle-feeders—and runs straight down a weighted braid dragging in the iron-brine behind its left heel. For one flickering beat, the figure is outlined in ozone and pale sparks, smelling of singed grease and hot pennies. Then it stands half-upright, wipes iron-slime from its eyes with a thick, flat palm, and turns to look at you.

They are people, or what people become after enough generations inside a press with no walls.

They do not gather in towns or camps, for nothing here is built and too many grounded bodies in one spot pull lightning straight out of the cloud deck. They move in small crews of five to twelve—rarely more—threading endlessly through the shifting lanes between herds, hunched slightly at the neck and knees so they never rise above the line of the backs. Tall things take the sleet full in the face; tall things catch the eye of the louver-winged gliders riding the steam; tall things draw the charge.

From a dozen paces away, they look stitched together out of the valley’s own leavings. They wear layered coats of cured glider-hide, still keeping the natural wing-slots intact across their ribs and shoulders; by pulling a loop of braided tendon at the collar, they can draw the leathery louvers tight against sideways sleet or crack them open to bleed sweat when they crouch beside a herd’s heat cycle. Over the hide goes the grounding-shroud of copper tendon, knotted loosely at the joints and left to trail a yard behind the heel like a wet tail. Their exposed skin—only narrow strips around the eyes, the heavily callused flats of the hands, the scarred rims of the nostrils—is the color of cold tallow stained rust-brown in every crease by iron-eating slime. Static has taken most of their hair, leaving scalps stubbled with brittle, pale down that stands on end whenever the air thickens before a squall.

Stand close enough to one of them and you hear it: their breathing does not keep a human pace. Chests rise slowly, lock for a beat, and let go in a thin grey plume on the exact four-second drop of the bellows-herds half a mile off. Their ears are mostly useless for soft sound, the drums thickened and scarred by the endless hammering, yet they read the valley through the soles of their hide-wrapped boots and the roots of their back teeth. They can feel a two-cycle drift in the bedrock hum through the mineral grit packed into their molars before the sky even begins to dim.

This is why they never leave. There are no fences at the rim of the basin, only low, wet swells of black gneiss and shale climbing gently out of the fog. Every few years, some restless boy or half-grown girl decides to walk out and see what lies beyond the seam where the flickering sky meets the mud. They make it over the ridge, out onto dry, quiet ground where the light does not strobe and the stone lies dead underfoot. A mile past the hum, they fall to their knees. Without the sixty-cycle drone to lean against, their pulses lose their anchor and thrash wildly against their ribs; their lungs forget when to draw; their vision smears at the edges as though continuous, unbroken daylight were too heavy for eyes stamped in a twilight press. Most crawl back down the weeping shale before sundown, weeping with relief the moment the freezing slush hits their shins and the deep roar locks back into their jawbones.

Surviving in the press is a matter of timing, not strength.

Because nothing green grows, there is no wood, no dry fiber, and no fire anywhere in the valley. Heat exists only inside the living herds and in the vents that blast it into the fog. To cook, the crews carry long, pale skewers of straight bone, threading them with rubbery strips cut from conveyor-worms—which they catch by jamming a wedge of calcified horn into the seam where a worm’s blunt mouth clamps its own tail, breaking its ratchet until the ten-yard loop goes limp and drowns in its own channel. Then they crouch directly beside a bellows-beast as its flanks draw in, wait for the dull orange glow to bloom behind the translucent membrane between its ribs, and press the skewered meat flat against the hot, hissing hide for two full strokes. They snatch it back a split second before the beast’s hooves drop and the standing wave kicks brine over their knees. The meat comes away seared grey on the outside, cold and metallic at the core, and they pack it in the refined white salt pellets spat out by the barnacle-feeders.

When a bellows-beast grows old or its internal rhythm misfires—glowing a half-second early, dropping its hooves out of step with its three thousand neighbors—the crews spot the stutter across a quarter-mile of fog. The basin has no mercy for an out-of-phase body. Within an hour the herd’s synchronized shifts will crack the laggard’s ribs, or a pond-wide benthic presser will sense the broken beat and glide beneath its belly. The crews dart in with sharpened bone wedges and copper-edged scrapers during that narrow window, slicing away warm, lean rib-meat and thick pads of throat-fat before the presser settles its stone-hard weight down and crushes the rest into grey slurry.

Even sleep requires borrowing the valley’s own geometry. You cannot lie down in the freezing pans, and there are no roofs to break the sleet. Sometimes, when a benthic presser has just finished a heavy feed and lies sluggish for a day, a whole crew will climb onto its broad, weathered back. Raised a foot above the slush, armored in ancient callus thick enough to insulate them from the ground-current, the beast becomes a slow, drifting raft. Eight people will wedge themselves tightly into the shallow hollows of its back, button their leather louvers shut, tuck their copper tails beneath their thighs, and sleep huddled together while the living slab beneath them creeps three yards an hour through the fog, radiating a faint, sour warmth from the crushed bone and iron-slag digesting in its belly.

More often, they sleep inside the things that are not there.

Every crew keeps a puddle-reader—usually an older woman, or someone whose eyes have turned slightly milky from decades of sixty-cycle flicker, yet who reads the dark supercooled water with terrible sharpness. While the others wait in the sleet, she walks slowly across the broad silt-pans, head bowed, staring straight down between her boots at the phantom girders and high rusted catwalks mirrored in the slush. She watches where a heavy, reflected stanchion appears to meet the mud, and plants her heel there.

Step into the footprint of a column that does not exist, and nothing solid stops your shoulder. Your skin only feels a sudden, dry chill and a faint taste of old iron at the back of the throat. Yet the sideways sleet seems to thin and hesitate around that square yard of air; the magnetized gnats veer away from it; the deep bedrock roar drops just enough that six people can sit back-to-back in the ankle-deep brine, knees drawn up on smooth river-stones, leaning against empty space that almost—not quite—holds their weight.

Look up from one of those puddle-read shelters, two hundred feet into the dim sky where the rising steam plumes flatten out horizontally against an invisible ceiling, and you may catch sight of the valley’s other dwellers.

They never touch the ground alive. To the naked eye looking upward, they are scarcely visible at all: long, pale interruptions hanging in the flat sheet of trapped steam, swaying slightly from empty air like wet rags pinned to a line no one can see. Look down into a calm puddle beneath them, though, and the water shows them plainly—hunched, spider-limbed things clinging upside-down to the rusted iron girders of the phantom roof, knees tucked tight to their narrow chests, hooked calcified toes locked around beams that exist only in reflection. Their faces are smooth, blind, and flat, set with wide, toothless mouths that hang open to drink the rising grease-scented vapor and the clouds of iron-winged gnats drifting up into the warm ceiling-pocket.

Mostly they are silent, rocking gently beside the swaying yellow lamps in the water. Only when a heavy voltage squall rolls down off the hills do they wake, throwing their narrow throats back to let out a thin, metallic whistle that rides just above the high whine of the sky.

Once in a great while, during a hard brownout when the invisible roof seems to thin or stutter, one of them loses its grip on nothing and plummets two hundred feet out of the open fog. It hits the shallows with a wet, hollow crack. Crews will sprint through the knee-deep channels to beat the gliders and benthic pressers to the fall. The meat of a hanger is rank and oily, fit only for the starving, but its bones are priceless. Having grown pressed against the phantom iron of the upper rafters rather than the worn stone of the flats, its skeleton is unlike anything else in the basin: dead straight, hollow, stoneware-hard, and meeting at crisp right angles at the joints. Every knife-handle, every steam-skimmer, every measuring-rod the crews carry is cut from a fallen hanger.

The basin does not merely feed and shelter the crews; it stamps them, and like the raw-boned waders out in the deeper channels, the registration is never quite clean.

A woman giving birth in the valley hides in the lee of a warm, dying herd-flank, timing her labor to the four-second hammer so her cries are swallowed by the steam-vents. Sometimes she delivers a single child—slate-blue, silent until its lungs catch the sixty-cycle flutter, whole in its own skin.

Just as often, she delivers a slip.

The instant the child slides out slick into the greased leather, before the cord is even tied off with thin copper tendon, two runners from the crew are already splashing hard out into the fog, fanning downwind with their heads cocked, listening through the percussion for a second, identical wail. They almost always find it within three hundred yards: a second infant lying naked on a flat shelf of wet shale or bobbing in the shallow brine beside a conveyor-worm, its cord severed cleanly as if by a cold blade, screaming on the exact same breath as the first.

They have to reach the offset before a glider drops out of the steam or the freezing water takes it, because the two are not ordinary twins. They are one person stamped twice across the mud. If the offset drowns in the silt, the baby in the mother’s arms will quietly cough up iron-brine an hour later and go limp. When both survive, they grow up braided in ways that make outsiders’ stomachs turn. They learn to walk on the same afternoon across half a mile of fog; if one catches a scalding vent across the forearm, a red welt rises on the other’s skin half a breath before the next voltage dip catches up.

Older crews often carry a doubled or even tripled elder—one stout and clear-eyed, one thinner and perpetually a fraction of a second late to blink, one nearly mute whose left ribcage flickers with a faint, offset ghost whenever the light sags. They answer to one name, share one bone knife, and sleep wedged together like nested shells. When they disagree, it sounds like a single hoarse voice arguing with itself from three corners of the puddle.

You only hear them speak at all during the sag.

Under full voltage, the crews work almost wordlessly. The continuous roar shreds ordinary voices, and at sixty cycles a second their minds run narrow, bright, and mechanical, communicating in clipped jerks of the chin and sharp taps of a bone rod against a neighbor’s leather shoulder.

Then the broad, dark band rolls silently across the basin from west to east.

The flickering grey sky drops half a shade. The deep bedrock hum falls a half-step in pitch. The hammering herds slow from a crisp strike to a heavy, labored thud. And all across the flats, for ten or fifteen or twenty dim seconds, the valley loosens its grip on human throats.

A crew stops dead in the slush. Heads come up; leather hoods drop back; people crowd face-to-face and pour out a week’s worth of speech in a low, hoarse, overlapping rush. This is when they name the slip-born, trade copper tendon for shale condense-plates with a passing crew, settle grudges, laugh with cracked lips, and press bare hands against each other’s salt-chapped cheeks.

Because small causal knots come untied in the sag, their speech has bent to fit the looseness. They are used to an answer arriving in someone’s mouth before the question has been asked, and they do not flinch when a young man laughs in the dim a beat before his elder finishes the joke. A widow, holding her breath as the hum drops, will sometimes hear the dry, familiar cough of a husband lost three winters back hang intact in the mist beside her ear—clear, unhurried, unmoored from when it happened—until the voltage catches again with a high whine, the suspended splashes crash back into the channels, and the roar seals their mouths once more.

Yet survival and family are not the whole of what keeps them moving through the freezing press. Watch a crew for a full shift and you will notice the strangest thing they do—the task that feeds nobody, warms nobody, and never ends.

Hours each day, they walk behind the stamping bellows-herds, stooping into the churned brine to snatch up the warm, dark cylinders of smelted iron-silt as soon as they drop from the animals’ undersides. Each crewman draws a straight, notched strip of hanger-bone from inside his vest—a gauge, copied down through generations—and lays it carefully along the cylinder’s flank.

They measure the depth of the crisp right-angled grooves. They run a thumb across the shallow, serial-like notches. They squint hard at the clean corners before the supercooled water can confuse the geometry into mud.

Most cylinders they toss back into the slush with a flat, disgusted flick of the wrist. A few—perhaps one in four hundred, where the grooves sit true to the bone notches and the edges hold razor-clean—they wipe carefully on their sleeves, wrap in warm, oiled leather, and tuck straight against their bare chests to keep them out of the brine.

If you catch a crewman during a sag and ask what the good ones are for, he will stare at you in genuine bewilderment, then look down at his hands with an uneasy, hollow shame, as though you had asked why his heart beats in four strokes. *That one held true,* he will say, his voice cracking as the light begins to brighten. *The mark was clean.*

It never lasts. Even tucked bone-dry against living warmth, the basin’s damp eventually finds it. Within an hour or two, the sharp right angles soften like cold tallow, the serial notches blur and forget themselves, and the prized cylinder slumps into a wet clump of gritty black sand that trickles down the crewman’s ribs and back into the marsh. When it does, he simply shakes his apron out, reaches for his bone gauge, and stoops for the next warm piece falling from the next stamping beast.

And yet they do not stop—perhaps because the valley has stamped the need for inspection straight into their marrow, or perhaps because of what happens once in a blue moon, when a puddle-reader holds a freshly passed cylinder face-down over a still pool just as a brownout rolls through. For half a dim second, down in the dark water, the hooded yellow lamps hanging from the phantom girders seem to burn a shade steadier, and somewhere high up along the empty iron catwalk, something unseen gives a faint, crisp *tick*, as if a counter on the other side of the seam just turned over by one.

When one of the crew grows too old, too calcified, or too badly out of phase to keep the four-second step, there is no earth to dig and no wood to burn. The others carry the dead out to the quietest, deepest rock-basins along the worn shale swells, where the water lies supercooled and glass-clear beneath thin crusts of hexagonal ice. They weight the body with cooling iron-silt cylinders—dozens of them, passed and rejected alike—and lower it gently face-up onto the polished gneiss.

The salt and cold preserve them for years before the iron-slime slowly plates them flush with the stone. When a crew pauses beside those deep pools to rest, and looks down through the clear, freezing water, the reflection of the soaring phantom roof lays itself cleanly across the bottom. Down among the swaying yellow lamps and rusted girders, the dead elders appear to lie peacefully stretched along the high iron catwalks, looking up through the water as if they had finally climbed off the mud and finished their shift.

Only far out toward the western edge of the basin—miles away where the low flats tilt gently toward some unseen, frozen sea, and distant herds stack above themselves in humming vertical mirages—do the crews ever meet something they cannot measure or name.

They see them rarely, and never twice in the same channel: tall, narrow figures walking straight upright through the freezing murk. They wear no slotted glider-hide and drag no grounding-tails of copper tendon. Lightning arcs cleanly across their bare shoulders without turning their heads, and when they walk through a bellows-herd, the great ash-colored beasts do not trample them; the herds actually part half a step around them, clamping their steam-vents shut in sudden, uncanny silence. Their chests do not rise on the four-second beat.

Whenever a brownout rolls across the flats, while the crews are crowding close to whisper and laugh in the dim, these tall figures stop dead in their tracks and go faint—nearly translucent, shivering at the edges like a reflection disturbed by wind—until the voltage climbs back and sharpens them into solid flesh again.

The crews keep their distance and drop their eyes when one passes. Yet now and then, a tall figure will pause beside a crouching crewman, look down in silence at the warm, notched iron-silt cylinder cooling in his callused palm, and give a single, crisp shake of the head. Then it walks on toward the stacked, humming horizon, leaving behind a trail of narrow footprints in the mud that fill immediately with clear water—and if you step close before the vibration shakes them smooth, you will see no girders in those prints, no catwalks, and no swaying yellow lamps, only the quiet, unbroken reflection of a bright blue sky that has never hung above this valley.
