If you stand still in the basin long enough for the roar to stop sounding like noise and begin sounding like weather, you realize that the place is not empty at all. It is only empty of creatures that hurry.

The small, flattened amphibian on the porcelain insulator is the first sign of this, and the easiest to overlook. You have to learn where to look for it: never on the wet peat, never on the iron, never on the fluted stems. Those surfaces all carry the basin’s stray current, a faint alternating prickle that would seize the nerves of anything so thin-skinned. Only the glazed white ceramic of the old insulators is dead to the voltage, while still drinking in the steady waste heat of the copper stators below. So the amphibians live out almost their entire lives on islands of warm porcelain, pressed so flat against the curve that their pale, clouded bellies look like thickened drops of condensation.

They have no insects to snap at, and they do not hunt. Once every few minutes, one will creep half an inch along the glaze on translucent, pad-tipped toes and sweep its wide, soft mouth across the wet edge where ceramic meets corroded brass. Along that seam grows a pale, slick film—neither quite algae nor mold, a faint metallic jelly that feeds directly on the trickle of current and dissolved copper. That is their pasture. When it is time to breed, they do not trust the vibrating floor; they slip beneath the sheltered underside of the insulator’s ceramic petticoat and glue tiny, clear clusters of eggs to the dry-warm hollow of the bell, where beads of condensation creep down the glaze and keep them wet without ever washing them loose. The larvae are scarcely thicker than wet hairs. They cling by tiny ventral discs to the warm glass faces of the pressure dials, motionless behind the misted numerals, waiting weeks to grow limbs.

Look up from the insulators into the bright, hanging water-bladders that curtain the narrow corridors, and you find the second hidden layer of life. Against the steady pond-glass daylight filtering down through the canopy, the fluid inside those swollen sacs is not wholly clear. Suspended in the pale green brilliance are slender, ribbon-like swimmers no longer than a finger, thinner than soaked silk, entirely without eyes or fins or shells. They drift in slow, effortless figure-eights, turning with the internal convection of the warming sac. They never touch open air; if a bladder ruptures, they collapse on the peat like threads of wet gelatin and are gone in minutes. Inside the living walls, though, they are indispensable. As they glide across the inner face of the membrane, their soft margins scrub away the fine mineral scale that would otherwise chalk over the pores and blind the plant’s osmotic pull. Without them, the bladders would stiffen and lose their pressure; without the bladders’ sunlit, pressurized belly, they could not exist.

Down at the bottom of the corroded iron chutes, where the valves dump their warm gouts of concentrated brine every forty-five seconds, a third kind of life clings to the metal edges: fingernail-sized, blind limpets whose flat shells have taken up copper salts from the runoff until they gleam a dull, verdigris green. They graze the salt-crust as it cools, moving an inch in an afternoon, indistinguishable from rivets until one shifts a hair’s breadth to let the next surge of brine wash over its rim.

All of these belong neatly to the basin’s quiet arithmetic. Yet none of them can explain the millimeter of clearance on the twelve-foot flywheel.

Look closely at the glossy jade stalks pressing in around the turning iron spokes. A botanical mass vigorous enough to swallow brick footings and weld itself seamlessly around mounting bolts does not stop a millimeter short of a moving rim out of politeness. Lean closer—careful of your sleeves—and you will see it: along the inner face of the nearest ribbed stem runs a clean, pale bevel, a fresh paring wound still weeping clear, watery sap. Look down at the brass pin of the trip-valve, and you will see where its dark patina has been rubbed bright in a thumb-sized crescent that no tuft of moss could ever reach. Look up into the narrow seams between the buttresses, and you notice that the curtains of hanging aerial roots have not simply parted by chance along the compression-trails; here and there, a thick cord has been looped aside and held back with a single neat hitch of stripped bast. Look behind a heavy iron stanchion near the eastern pump, and you find a small wedge of seasoned bog-oak driven tight between flange and bark to quiet an uneven rattle.

Something with hands lives here.

You do not hear them coming, because nothing they do stands apart from the soundscape of water, wood, brass, and voltage. Their footsteps are only the same slow, sucking sigh of compressed root-cord filling in behind a lifted heel. You find them the way you find the amphibians—by stopping still and letting your eyes unfasten from the green glare.

Round a fluted buttress on the narrow trail near the second pump, and one of them may be standing three paces ahead of you, turned half-sideways in a vertical seam between two stems, waiting patiently for a piston to finish its long stroke.

At first glance, they are easily mistaken for an outcrop of wet bark and old equipment. They wear almost nothing against the greenhouse warmth—cloth rots to slime in this saturation within a month—only a short, belted wrap of dense, felted root-fiber that sheds water like peat-moss, and a harness of braided copper wire slung across the chest. On their feet are heavy, blocky clogs strapped tight across the instep, carved from dark resinous heartwood and soled with thick slabs of black vulcanized rubber pried decades ago from dead electrical switchboards. Without those clumsy soles, the alternating current leaking through the wet floor would cramp their calves within an hour; even with them, pale threads of moss constantly try to take root along the damp wooden rims.

Their skin is the strangest thing about them. Constant heat and mineral saturation should have left it raw and peeling, yet it looks oddly dense and smooth, pale-olive in the green light, faintly puckered along the palms and slick with a thin, translucent sheen. Every few days they scrape a waxy, bitter-smelling exudate from the undersides of the overhead canopy plates and rub it into their shoulders, forearms, and shins. Water beads on them the same way it beads on the fluted stems, running off in bright silver tracks. Their hair is cropped close to the skull so nothing catches on the low iron fittings, and their shoulders have a slight, permanent forward set from years of turning sideways through narrow green rooms.

Watch them work, and you understand why you did not see them moving. They do not cross the basin at a human pace; they move on the flywheel’s clock.

As the twelve-foot rim glides past—one spoke every fifty seconds—the tender leans smoothly into the gap, drawing a curved brass knife from the copper baldric. Iron would rust to lace in a season here, and steel sparks against wet stone, so their blades are heavy, soft brass, honed often against old porcelain shards. With one unhurried, economical draw, they pare a thin ribbon of swelling green tissue off a stalk that has crept half a millimeter too close since yesterday. They catch the severed sliver in their free hand, step cleanly back into the seam before the next spoke arrives, and fold the crisp white inner pith into their mouth. It tastes of wet sorrel, rain, and faint iron.

A moment later, they crouch beside the riveted pump housing. They do not put their ear to the iron—the percussion of falling drops on the drum-leaves would drown out anything subtle inside the casing. Instead, they unhook a foot-long brass rod tipped with a smooth, bite-worn cylinder of dense hardwood. They close their molars gently around the wood, press the brass tip flat against the cast-iron cylinder, and shut their eyes.

Through the jawbone and the dense bones of the skull, the airborne racket vanishes. What travels up the rod is pure structure: the slow hydraulic groan of the osmotic bladders swelling inside the chamber, the clean slide of the leather-and-root packing along the piston wall, the tiny, crisp tick of a brass spring preparing to trip. If a grain of grit has worked under a valve seat, or if a bladder-wall is thinning toward a split, they feel it behind their eyes like a snag in a piece of cloth.

Before moving on, they pause by the porcelain stanchion. A crust of greenish mineral scale has begun to bridge the cracked glaze toward the iron bolt—give it another month, and the current will track across the damp salt and short the insulator, heating the ceramic until it cracks clean through. With a gentle, practiced forefinger, the tender slides beneath the cool belly of the glass amphibian resting there and lifts it onto the back of their wet wrist. The creature does not flinch or kick; its throat continues its ten-second pulse against the skin. Using a flat scraper ground from an old rib-bone, the tender works the scale cleanly off the white glaze, rinses the seam with a cupped palm of leaf-water, and tilts their wrist until the amphibian slips calmly back onto its warm island.

There are three of them in the basin. Never many more, and seldom fewer; a hollow no wider than a parish churchyard has only so many valves to ease and so many shoots to pare, and too many bodies would press the narrow seams past comfort.

They do not speak to one another. A human voice dies at arm’s length in this loud green belly, broken apart by the tight baffles of tissue and swallowed under the bass dynamo drone. Over time, even the habit of shaping words with the throat falls away. Instead, they speak through the basin’s own taut anatomy.

If the eastern receiver is running a shade too hot, or a root-collar has begun pinching a tie-rod out of true, one of them stops beside the nearest tensioned iron bar and taps it with the flat butt of their brass knife. Two quick ticks, a long pause, a single dull strike. The vibration races fifty yards through the iron and along the braided root-cables that lash the stations together, ringing out cleanly inside the hollow gourd-bladders on the far side of the bowl. Thirty seconds later, from somewhere deep behind a curtain of hanging sacs, an answer travels back along the same wire: two sharp ticks, confirming. Up close, when two of them meet in a narrow compression-trail and must turn belly-to-belly to pass, their language is simpler still—a bare hand laid flat against the other’s wet shoulder-blade, or two forefingers pressed side by side against the same fluted battery-fin so both can feel the exact strength of the current humming inside the stalk.

They live entirely off the surplus of the pact. Besides the crisp, mineral-sweet inner pith pared back daily from the machines, they harvest dense, pale shelf-fungi that sprout in clusters where the warm brine chutes empty into the peat—firm and salty, tasting faintly of oysters and wet copper. And tucked into the sheltered, warm ledges beside the copper-wound stators sit rows of salvaged stoneware oil-jars and inverted porcelain insulator-bells, plugged tight with wedges of waxed pith. Inside these vessels, mashed root-starch, bruised green shoots, and salted sap ferment slowly in the steady electrical warmth, shaken smooth day and night by the endless low-frequency hum until they turn into a thick, pale, mildly sour paste that smells of warm bread and wet peat. For water, they drink straight from the cupped axils of the giant leaves, where the canopy’s distilled condensation pools clear and tepid.

When the pond-glass daylight overhead finally deepens toward the dark bottle-green of evening—or simply when a body asks for stillness, since hours mean very little beneath the unbroken dome—they do not lie down on the wet, prickling floor. They climb ten feet up the thickest buttresses, using notched foot-holds long ago calloused smooth by the living bark, and settle into the high, dry-insulated cradles they have woven from peeled aerial root-sheaths and strips of perished rubber belting slung between glass standoffs.

Often, though, they sleep in the water itself. High in the older fluted stems, where three massive leaves join the trunk, broad, thick-walled basins hold shallow pools of clear, warm rainwater insulated from the floor’s stray voltage by twenty feet of living cellulose. A tender will slip into one of those smooth green bowls with a block of light cork-bark wedged beneath the nape of their neck, floating half-submerged in the tepid bath until their ears sink just beneath the surface. Underwater, the bright, relentless rattle of ten thousand drops hitting drum-leaves disappears altogether. Only the deep, velvet hum of the buried dynamos passes through the water and into their bones, steady as a heartbeat, rocking them down into a dreamless, green-lit sleep.

How the household began, none of them could tell you even if they still cared to use words. Perhaps the first one was an old lowland pump-engineer a century ago who stayed behind when the coal carts stopped coming and the marsh flora began creeping through the brick louvers—fascinated by the first pale osmotic bladder that forced a rusted piston back into motion, staying to oil a pin and trim a stray leaf until the weeks became years. Since then, the three have replenished themselves the way the basin replenishes its water: by slow seepage.

Once in a decade or two, someone out on the open lowlands hears that deep bass hum vibrating up through the dry turf a quarter-mile off, follows it to the low clay rim, and steps three paces down out of curiosity, or grief, or simply because the noise inside their own head has grown too sharp to bear. Most climb back out within an hour, chilled by the wet saturation, bruised by the tight corridors, unnerved by the unbearably slow turning of the great iron wheels. Once in a long while, someone sits down on an insulated wooden stool beside the central stator, feels the heavy, patient cadence settle behind their sternum, and discovers that this loud, cramped green interior is the first place their mind has ever gone completely still. When the others pass them on the compression-trails, they do not speak or beckon. After a day or two, one of them simply leaves a pair of rubber-soled clogs and a curved brass knife resting dry on a porcelain ledge.

Once a person stays long enough to take on the basin’s tempo, the open air beyond the clay rim becomes almost uninhabitable. On the rare occasions one of the younger two climbs the three paces to the rim to peer out across the lowlands, the world outside feels painfully thin and broken. The dry wind stings their wax-softened skin and scrapes their throats; the open sky glares without mercy; the silence between gusts feels hollow and shrill, shattered without warning by the jagged, erratic cries of birds and the senseless thrashing of wind-blown grass. Nothing out there keeps time. They linger only a minute or two before ducking back beneath the glassy overhang, exhaling as the warm, copper-scented humidity closes around their faces and the steady percussion of condensation wraps them safe again.

Near the exact center of the hollow, where the squat copper-wound stator sits cradled in its seamless collar of living wood, lives the oldest of the three. Their hips have stiffened over decades of damp until squeezing through the outer vertical seams has become slow work, so they rarely walk the perimeter anymore. Most hours of the day you will find them seated on a low stool of dark bog-oak wedged permanently into the root-buttress, rubber-soled heels tucked neatly clear of the weeping peat.

Their skin has taken on the cool, weathered luster of wet slate, and the veins along their forearms lie pale and quiet beneath the waxed surface. They sit motionless for twenty minutes at a time, eyes half-closed against the steady shadowless glare, both bare palms resting flat against the strict, parallel ranks of ribbed battery-fins that rise in geometric order around the stator. They are not resting; they are reading the basin. Through the cool slickness of the living tissue and the steady, familiar prickle of alternating current against their palms, they feel the metabolic warmth of the stalks, the subtle tension in the root-cables, the balance of pressure across all five stations at once.

If a flywheel twenty yards off begins to drag by a fraction of a hair against an encroaching rib, the sympathy in the living floor shifts by less than a quarter-tone. The elder’s eyes do not even open. Slowly, without hurry, their knuckles drop to the iron stanchion bolted beside their knee and tap three patient, measured notes into the metal. Somewhere out in the bright green maze, one of the younger two pauses mid-stride, feels the message ring through the taut aerial roots brushing their shoulder, and turns silently down the compression-trail with their brass knife drawn.

And the basin settles back into its chord—neither wild nor tame, neither wholly plant nor wholly iron—held in its exact, humming equilibrium by the tiny ribbon-swimmers in the sunlit sacs, the unblinking amphibians on their warm ceramic islands, and the three quiet, wax-slicked bodies that serve as the last soft gasket between cellulose and cast iron, moving forever at the speed of a wheel that takes five minutes to turn.
