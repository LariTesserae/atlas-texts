If you stay beside one of the fizzing pans long enough after the warm rain passes, and kneel close enough to feel the fine mist of rising gas against your knuckles, you may eventually notice a pebble that does not belong.

It sits half-submerged where the dark runoff narrows between two weathered basalt ledges, lying dead-straight across the voltage drop of the pool. At first touch it feels like any other damp, copper-crusted stone. Lift it out, though, and it is far too light. Beneath the dull, rose-colored skin of freshly plated metal—still beaded with tiny pinpricks of bubbles—lies a narrow, leaf-shaped core of hard grazer tallow, kneaded thick with magnetite grit so the stray current will bite into it, and pinned cleanly in place with a twist of braided bronze scrub-root.

Someone has left a knife here to grow.

You can walk a hundred miles across these terraces and find no house, no hearth, no track that survives the next shift of the magnetic dust. Yet the plateau is not uninhabited. Its people simply live the way the rest of the highland lives: spread wide, kept low to the stone, and folded so neatly into the rhythms of the rock and the current that an eye trained for walls and firelight passes straight over them.

If you remain still beside that pan—letting your breathing slow to the thin air, letting two or three of the plateau’s long, regular shifts roll under your heels—you will eventually hear them coming.

Not through the air; the thin dark eats footsteps twenty paces off. You feel them first through the basalt: a faint, crisp *tink—tink* that rides cleanly above the deep bass hum of the buried works, sharp as a pebble dropped on an anvil. Then, much closer, a soft, dry rustle of grit and the warm smell of tallow, dried meat, and damp ironstone.

Only when a stray bead of blue corona fire gutters along the pale reeds at the pool’s edge—or when a silent fork of lightning flickers miles away inside the cloud deck—do you actually see them.

There may be three of them, or half a dozen, standing motionless ten paces back from the water. At a glance, in that split second of cold blue glare, they look carved from the same weathered ironstone as the escarpments: lean, long-backed, remarkably deep in the ribcage. Their chests rise and fall in slow, cavernous pulls that make the thin air seem effortless. Their skin is pale slate-gray, dry and tough as sun-cured hide despite never having known a sun, dusted along the shins, forearms, and knuckles with fine black grit that settles into every crease and joint.

They wear almost nothing against the warm dark—only broad belts of tanned grazer belly, wide pouches hung with bone toggles, and heavy, matte-gray cloaks cut from cured lichen mats that drape stiffly over their shoulders and make a crouching figure indistinguishable from a settled boulder. Their feet are bare, broad, and heavily calloused, bound only by open leather thongs threaded with narrow, dull-bright strips of copper that run beneath the arch and press directly against the basalt. To wear thick insulating boots here is to walk deaf and half-blind; they want the rock straight against their soles, the warmth of every buried seam climbing clean into their ankles, the steady tickle of stray voltage telling them where the wet ore-veins run.

Look at their heads in that fleeting blue spark, and you will see what truly marks them as creatures of this plain. Their dark, coarse hair is drawn back into dozens of tight, flat braids, and woven through every braid are filaments of hair-thin electroformed copper and tiny, hollow zinc beads. It is not ornament—or not only ornament. When a storm cell gathers overhead, or when the subterranean load begins to climb before a shift, those fine metal threads stir imperceptibly against their temples and necks, humming with a quiet static prickle that warns them minutes before the bedrock shudders.

Their eyes are small, pale-ringed, and recessed beneath heavy brows, kept half-closed in the steady dark. They do not look at you as they draw near; they angle their chins slightly sideways, reading the faint thermal plume rising off your face, the damp smell of your wool, and the sharp magnetic warp bent into the local field by whatever steel you carry. Only when the blue corona blossoms along a distant ridge, or when cloud-lightning briefly reveals the vast terraced horizon, do their lids open wide. In that single second of cold light, they drink the whole landscape at once—every grazing herd, every venting plume, the contour of every shallow basin—and carry the blue afterimage burned behind their eyes for the next half-hour of darkness, stitching it effortlessly onto the continuous map they hold in their feet.

They do not speak across distance. Shouting burns precious breath and dies uselessly in the thin air before it crosses a single flat. When they are spread out across a terrace—following a herd of blind grazers or checking their submerged pans—they speak through the stone itself. Each carries a slender staff of hollow glider-bone weighted at the butt with a dense, grown lump of copper, or wears a heavy plated knuckle-ring on the index finger. Pressed firmly to bare basalt and struck in crisp, staggered doublets and triplets, the high-pitched clicks travel half a mile through solid bedrock, cutting straight over the low subterranean throb.

Up close, their spoken language is almost startlingly intimate. Because sound falls off so quickly, two people talking stand close enough to share the warmth of each other’s breath, speaking in a low, unhurried murmur that rarely rises above a whisper. Often one will lay two fingers lightly against the other’s collarbone, wrist, or jaw as they speak, letting the cadence of the throat pass bone-to-bone as much as through the air. To be listened to here is to be touched; to pull away out of arm’s reach is to end the conversation.

---

Follow a band of them for a dozen of the plateau’s shifts, and you begin to understand why the surface remains utterly unbuilt, unscarred, and empty of ruins.

They have no fire. There is no wood to burn, the air is too thin to carry a clean flame, and the ground beneath them is already warm. Everything another people might ask of fire—heat, cooked food, shaped metal, protection in the dark—they draw directly from the plateau’s quiet alchemy.

When a grazing herd drops an old bull or a sick calf, or when the band itself brings down a beast with heavy bone spears tipped in copper, they do not build a hearth. They slice the dark, iron-rich flesh into paper-thin strips and lay them out directly along one of the straight, arrow-true thermal seams that cut across the basalt. Over a handful of shifts, the steady waste heat rising from the engines below bakes the meat dry, dark, and tough as leather, leaving behind nothing when they gather it up except a faint, greasy sheen that the matte-black crawlers scour clean within an hour. The hollow bones are split for marrow and shaped into staves and needles; the tallow is rendered soft in warm stone hollows and kneaded with iron grit to make cores for the pans.

Water is gathered with the same quiet patience. They never drink directly from the fizzing electrolytic basins; the runoff is too heavy with dissolved salts and copper. Instead, they carry folded pads of the thick, rubbery gray lichen that grows beside the seeps. Pressed over the mouth of a hide bladder and slowly filled from a pan, the dense fungal mat drinks up the trace metals and weeps clean, warm, slightly flat water through its underside. Better still are the wide, gossamer sheets they stitch from the wing-membranes of the silent thermal gliders—fetched down when a glider dies of age or is snared near a low vent. Folded, a whole sheet fits inside a belt pouch; unrolled and weighted with stones downwind of a sighing rock blowhole, it catches the invisible column of humid exhaust, beading slick with warm condensation that runs down a stitched crease into a drinking skin.

And then there is their metallurgy—the slowest, cleanest craft imaginable.

Because they have no furnaces, they never smelt, cast, or hammer hot iron. They grow their metal cold, atom by atom, in the shallow pans. A skilled maker can shape almost anything out of tallow, dried lichen, or tight-wound scrub-fiber: a curved skinning knife, a barbed fish-like hook for dragging crawlers from deep thermal cracks, a delicate hollow rattle, a fine needle, a flat sensing-blade no thicker than a reed wall. Dusted with wet magnetite sand and staked precisely across the stray current of an active seepage basin, the form is left to sit in the dark while the water fizzes gently around it.

Forty turns—roughly a day and a half by an outside clock—will grow a workable copper skin over a needle. Two hundred turns will build a dense, heavy knife-blade, layered subtly with paler zinc if the runoff shifts after a warm drizzle. Because the direct current reverses polarity for three seconds at the end of every cycle, every tool grown on the plateau carries microscopic alternating bands inside its grain, like growth rings in a tree. When the maker finally returns—sometimes days later, sometimes weeks later on the wide loop of their wandering—they peel away the soft tallow core, burnish the dull rose edge against fine-grained ironstone until it takes a razor bite, and hold it to their ear to flick the flat with a thumbnail. A blade grown in a steady, quiet pan rings pure and high; one grown through volatile load-shifts hums with a faint, dry buzz and is used only for rough scraping.

And when a blade snaps, or wears down to a stub, or belongs to someone who has died, it is never discarded on the flats. They carry the worn metal back to the upstream lip of an active pan and lay it on the anode side of the voltage drop. Over the next several hundred turns, the plateau gently un-makes it, lifting the copper back into the fizzing water and plating it out downstream onto ordinary wet pebbles.

That is why you find no middens, no rusted scrap, no graves marked with heirlooms. Whatever the plateau lends them, they dissolve back into the current before they move on.

---

Without a sun, a moon, or seasons of cold and heat, their lives are measured by the pulse inside the rock.

Every three quarters of an hour, the deep hum under their heels drops half a step—heavier, slower—and for three seconds the bedrock shudders while the buried current flips end-for-end. To them, this is not an interruption; it is the basic unit of existence, as natural and unremarkable as breath. They do not speak of hours or days. A distant seepage basin is "fourteen turns downwind." A storm cell gathers over "five turns." A child is born "two turns after the western vents went quiet."

It shapes even the way they sleep. No one on the open flats sleeps for eight hours straight; to lie unconscious through ten consecutive polarity reversals is to invite a pale-gray stalker right onto your throat. Instead, they rest in loose, light stretches between turns, curled on their sides in natural wind-hollows under their stiff gray lichen cloaks, backs pressed lightly to a neighbor’s ribs. Minutes before the hum drops, their copper-braided hair begins to prickle faintly against their cheeks, and they wake without a start—not panicking, simply drawing their knees under them and setting their bare palms flat against the stone.

When the three-second shudder arrives and the magnetic grit gives its wide, dry *hiss* across the terraces, the whole band sits utterly still, heels grounded, blades resting across their thighs. They know the stalkers wait for those exact three seconds, when the scrambled field blinds the grazers and the tremor hides the scrape of claws. Sometimes, when a band needs tough hide or lean meat, they turn the predator’s own habit against it. Two of them will stand slightly apart at the rim of a fizzing pan, mimicking the loose posture of a straggling grazer, letting the stalker slide within ten paces during the quiet minutes before the shift. The instant the hum drops and the grit flips—when the stalker breaks cover in that three-second blind rush—the people do not scatter. They pivot cleanly inside the shudder, meeting the pale, crest-snouted lunge with a braced copper-tipped shaft and a loop of weighted sinew. Before the fizzing in the pan has even settled back to its normal pitch, the fight is over, silent as everything else on the plain.

Over longer stretches—hundreds or thousands of turns—their movements follow the slow breath of the continent itself. A cluster of rock blowholes that has been sighing warm humidity for half a year will gradually grow drier as pressure shifts down into some distant subterranean trunk fifty miles away. The pans begin to crust over with dry white salt; the rubbery lichen stiffens and shrinks; the blind herds drift slowly downwind toward fresher mist. Without hurry or regret, the bands roll up their glider-sheets, collect the half-grown copper forms from the drying pools, and walk away across the terraced dark.

---

Yet there are places they always return to.

Remember those rare, sunken circles you might scuff your heel against once in fifty miles—the broad discs of vitrified ceramic worn completely flush with the weathered basalt and half-buried in dark dust?

To the people of the plateau, those discs are the center of the world.

In an endless highland of weathered terraces where every ridge looks like the last and every flat carries the same low hum, a ceramic disc is unmistakable in total darkness. The instant your bare foot crosses its rim, the world changes. The iron grit does not cling to its pale, glassy skin. More startlingly, the stray current stops.

Across thousands of miles of open, electrified stone, those sunken discs are the only islands of true electrical silence. Step onto one, and the faint, constant prickle in your ankles vanishes. The metallic taste at the back of your teeth fades. The copper wires braided in your hair go slack and completely quiet. Even when the three-quarter-hour shift rolls across the surrounding basalt—making the dark sand hiss around the perimeter and sending pale blue static rippling along the far ridges—the ceramic remains smooth, cool-edged, and wholly still, carrying only the muted mechanical throb of the engines far below, stripped clean of all static.

Every few hundred turns, two or three wandering bands will drift toward the same disc from opposite horizons, drawn by quiet agreements tapped out through the bedrock or simply by the shared memory of a venting cycle ending. Whoever arrives first unbinds bundles of stiff bronze scrub-bristles and sweeps the buried disc clean, pushing the magnetized dust back beyond the basalt rim until the whole smooth circle—thirty paces across—lies bare in the dark.

For twenty or thirty turns, the disc holds a gathering.

Here, away from the live seams, people whose nerves have grown raw from walking through a storm-charged sector can finally lie flat on their backs and sleep through three or four turns unbroken, knowing the stalkers rarely cross onto the bare, unshielded glass where the bedrock vibrations carry every claw-click cleanly across the floor. Children—who normally walk tethered to a parent’s belt by a cord of braided scrub-root so they will not wander into a deep blowhole or stray during a shift—are turned loose to run barefoot across the smooth circle, laughing softly in the thin air, tossing handfuls of iron grit beyond the edge just to hear it hiss and align itself on the basalt.

Around the center, people sit knee-to-knee in tight rings, trading small, dense riches by touch alone: bright copper needles grown in the rich northern pans exchanged for broad zinc-coated scrapers from the southern flats; heavy sheets of cured glider-membrane traded for bundles of dried grazer sinew; a finely balanced tapping-rod passed hand to hand so every listener can judge the pitch of its ring against the ceramic. Old grievances between bands are settled here, because no one likes to argue while their skin is buzzing with static. Here, too, women come to give birth when their time draws close, so a newborn’s first breath and first cries happen on quiet ground, unjolted by the sudden reversal of the rock.

And when someone dies out on the open flats, their band carries them to the nearest disc if they can reach it within a dozen turns. They lay the body out for one full shift in the quiet center, sitting close with their palms resting on the cooling shoulders and ribs while the people who knew them murmur soft, bone-conducted stories into each other’s collarbones. Before they leave, they carefully unbraid every copper filament and zinc bead from the dead person’s hair—to be laid upstream in the next active pan and dissolved back into the plateau—and then carry the bare body half a mile downwind of the disc, setting it gently on an open shelf of basalt. By the time the band has walked ten turns away, the silent, wide-winged gliders will have dropped out of the dark thermals, and the flat-shelled crawlers will have emerged from beneath the stones. Nothing is wasted; nothing is left behind to mark the spot.

---

If you ask—could you ever share enough breath and quiet touch to ask—what they make of the engines running hundreds of feet beneath their bare heels, they would have no word for *engine*, and no sense that anything was ever *built*.

To them, the buried works are simply the anatomy of the earth. They know the arrow-true thermal seams do not follow the grain of the rock, and they know the ceramic discs are smoother than wind or water ever made basalt, just as they know the great turning cadence below is far too regular to be weather. Yet it has been running longer than any lineage can remember—tens of thousands of years, an inconceivable number of turns—and anything that old, that vast, and that indifferent ceases to be a tool and becomes a world. They do not worship it, nor do they fear it. You do not pray to the breath in your own ribs, or curse the slope of a hill for making you climb.

Still, among every few bands, there are always one or two of the very old who pay a different kind of attention.

You might see one of them at the edge of a gathering, stepping off the quiet ceramic back onto the bare basalt while the younger ones trade and rest. She is gaunt, her skin creased deep with iron dust, her pale eyes almost entirely clouded over, her hair heavy with pale copper wire worn smooth over decades of shifts. Slowly, carefully, she lowers herself full-length onto a flat, weathered terrace—not resting, not sleeping, simply pressing her cheek, her bare palms, and the whole length of her deep ribcage directly against the warm stone.

She stays there through two turns, three turns, four.

Every child on the plateau knows the three-quarter-hour drop, the shudder, the three-second flip. Every adult knows the slow, drifting seasons of the venting sectors, lasting thousands of turns. What the old listeners hear, buried deep inside the heavy continuous hum, are changes measured in lifetimes.

Through the bones of her jaw and her sternum, she feels the vast, distant stroke completing itself miles beneath the ironstone—pausing that tiny fraction of a second, and reversing. She remembers how that same stroke felt forty thousand turns ago, when she was a girl trailing behind her mother’s ankles. To an outsider, the rhythm is utterly mechanical, utterly unchanged. To her, lying bare-cheeked against the basalt, the pause at the bottom of the eastern trunk has grown the barest hair longer than it used to be. There is a faint, dry tremor—hardly a vibration, more a whisper of roughness inside the bass—that was not there before the last great drying of the southern pans. Downwind, a seam that once baked meat crisp in four turns now takes five; far out past the unseen escarpment, a new cluster of rock blowholes has begun to sigh with a cleaner, sharper trace of mineral steam.

She does not try to understand why, and she does not imagine anyone could ever reach down through hundreds of feet of solid rock to mend what turns there. She only listens until she is certain of the drift.

When the next shift rolls through the plain—the hum dropping half a step, the black grit hissing as it flips end-for-end, a distant fork of blue cloud-lightning briefly outlining the endless dark terraces and the scattered herds—she pushes herself slowly back up onto her calloused heels. She walks back onto the brushed ceramic, rests two iron-dusted fingers lightly against the collarbone of the youngest leader in the band, and murmurs softly into the thin, warm air.

A few turns later, the gathering breaks apart without ceremony. Glider-sheets are folded small; hide bladders are cinched tight; lichen cloaks are drawn over lean shoulders. One by one, the bands step off the smooth, silent ceramic back onto the humming basalt, letting the stray current climb once more into their ankles and the fine magnetic dust settle back over the abandoned disc behind them.

Within thirty paces, the thin air has swallowed their quiet voices whole. A few hundred paces further, even the faint *tink—tink* of their weighted staves fades beneath the deep, tireless turning of the rock, and the warm, unbuilt plateau closes seamlessly over them in the dark.
