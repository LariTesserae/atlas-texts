If you watch the frantic traffic of the pools long enough, you begin to notice a handful of stones that do not belong to the accident of the mountain.

Most of the tools strewn across the wet basalt are raw debris—chipped splinters popped loose by heat, snatched up by whatever passes first, dropped as soon as a sharper flake springs from the wall. The crawlers and pink-hided scavengers use what they find, and nothing more. Yet here and there, resting in a shallow scallop of water or jammed carefully into a vertical seam, lies a blade of obsidian whose edge carries a tidy row of tiny, overlapping crescent scars. Not the ragged fracture of thermal stress, nor the bruising of a crawler’s pincers, but the deliberate, rhythmic work of pressure-flaking—an edge refreshed again and again, kept clean as a surgeon’s lancet.

Beside one of those blades rests a round basalt hammer-stone, worn to the gloss of a river plum. It is far too heavy for any thumb-sized scavenger to lift, and far too smooth for a mountain split open yesterday.

While you are still looking at the pebble, a wet crease in the sunlit left wall—a hairline crack no wider than a coin—widens by nothing at all, and a hand slides out.

It is pale, hairless, and steamed to the translucent pink of boiled tallow. Along the back of the wrist and over each ridge of knuckle grow thin, overlapping scales of milky silica, deposited there like hard opal calluses out of the mineral water. Six long, lean fingers fan across the slick rock—no true thumb, only two opposing outer digits, double-jointed so the hand can flatten itself like a ribbon inside tight fissures. The broad pads of the fingertips are stained slate-black, grained deep with the dust of young volcanic glass.

The hand lifts the glossy basalt pebble. Half a breath later, a small, wet *suck* of displaced water arrives from the obsidian overhang above your head.

You look for the arm, the shoulder, the body behind the crack, and find only sheer black stone packed with crawlers. Then your eyes drift to the shaded right wall, two paces across the alcove. Another hand—identical, bearing the same pale scar of an old sulfur-scald across the third knuckle—is braced flat against a steaming shelf, holding a wedge of raw black glass steady against the rock. A third, slightly smaller, or simply farther away inside the folded inches of the floor, hangs down from the underside of the leaning obsidian fang, palm open above a shallow basin.

There is someone in the pantry with the crawlers. You did not see it at first because you were looking for a body that occupies one continuous place.

It is larger than the cleft should comfortably hold—perhaps the length of a gaunt, crouching adult if you could ever draw it out and lay it flat on honest ground—yet it crushes nothing and clears no space. It drapes itself through the pocket’s slack. Its narrow ribs lie coiled inside the palm-wide ledge that takes a fleeing scavenger fifteen unbroken seconds to cross; its lean shins anchor somewhere in the back fault where the thermal vent weeps silently behind your molars; its shoulders lean out of a wet fold on the right while its left forearm reaches through the left wall to fetch a stone. Even in the plain, honest glare of afternoon daylight, you can never see more than a few pieces of it at once: a pale, curved flank rising half an inch above a steaming basin like a smooth boulder; a row of long, spatulate toes hooked around a ridge of sulfur crust; a narrow ridge of spine, plated in cloudy mineral scales, sliding slowly under a crowd of dome-backed grazers without disturbing their work.

When you finally find its head, it is resting chin-deep in the hottest pool at the back of the niche, looking straight toward the open mouth of the cleft.

The skull is narrow and wedge-shaped, built to slip into seams, smooth as wet bone and bare of hair. There are no outer ears—only two taut, translucent membranes set behind the hinge of the jaw, pulsing faintly whenever a delayed sound arrives through the basalt. Two low shelves of milky silica overhang the eyes like goggles, shielding them from scalding splashes; beneath them, the eyes themselves are wide, clear, and pale sulfur-yellow, with narrow horizontal pupils that shrink to fine threads in the midday glare. Its mouth stays shut—a tight, pleated seam built to keep boiling mineral broth in and razor-grit out, never opening to hiss, pant, or call.

It is kiln-blooded, and utterly patient. Everything else in the alcove moves in a breathless, silent scramble, snatching and prying and fleeing across the mosaic. The occupant moves like warm oil.

Watch it knap a blade.

It wedges a fist-sized core of young obsidian against the silica plates of its own drawn-up knee, half-submerged in a basin. Its right hand holds the glossy basalt hammer poised two inches above the glass, motionless. Nothing moves—until, across the dark face of the obsidian, a fine white crescent of fracture blooms cleanly by itself, and the sharp shadow of a detached splinter shifts a hair’s breadth to the left on the sunlit floor. Only then does the occupant bring the basalt stone down, gently and without haste, tapping the exact spot where the crack already sits. The flake pops free and drops straight into a waiting lower palm that opened a moment before the blow fell. Half a breath later, the crisp, dry *tink* of struck glass drifts up from a wet scallop near the outer lip.

It does not fight the frayed laws of the cleft; it uses them as its workbench.

Because the pocket refuses to keep cause and effect neatly stacked, the occupant hunts and gathers almost without effort. It does not chase the needle-legged skaters that dart across the scalding meniscus. Instead, it watches a basin’s flat surface for the tiny, inward-shrinking rings of an aftermath whose drop has not yet fallen, slides two long fingers beneath the spot, and waits for a panicked predator to tumble loose from the ceiling into its grip. When a pink-hided scavenger bolts behind a fold of basalt, the occupant does not lunge after the flesh; it watches the scavenger’s lagging shadow linger on the sunlit shelf for two full heartbeats, reads the exact angle of its retreat from the dark silhouette still clinging to the stone, and extends an arm through a crack on the opposite wall to intercept it before it finishes arriving.

Yet its craft is not only for hunting. Mostly, it is for upkeep.

The mineral water here wants to turn everything that lingers into stone. Greasy films of silica and pale crusts of sulfur continually creep across the occupant’s skin, cementing the creases of its double-jointed wrists, clouding its brow-ridges, thickening along its ribs until a joint threatens to lock. Every hour or so—though time sags unevenly in the heat—it selects one of its finest, freshly knapped obsidian crescents and grooms itself with terrifying stillness. Holding the razor-thin glass between two black-dusted fingertips, it slides the edge cleanly beneath the cloudy scales growing over its own knuckles and eyelids, lifting them away in thin, translucent curls. The shaved flakes drift down into the steaming basins, where wide, pleated ribbon-things immediately ripple over them to graze on the mineral salt.

And whenever it works a block of young basalt, the debitage—the shower of tiny, needle-sharp micro-flakes too small for its six-fingered hands—scatters across the crowded shelves. The little fauna swarm the clippings before they finish settling. Sometimes the occupant seems to drop a particularly keen obsidian splinter into a dense pool on purpose, watching in pale, sulfur-eyed stillness as half a dozen crawlers and boiled-pink scavengers pile over one another in a soundless brawl. When two competing hunters lunge for the blade and pass briefly through each other’s flanks like reflections on dark water, a long pale hand reaches calmly into the blur and plucks out the larger of the two—holding it until the flesh snaps dense and struggling against its palm.

The small scavengers treat the occupant less like a predator than like weather, or terrain. They climb straight over its submerged forearm to reach a sulfur crust; they borrow its smooth basalt hammer-stones when it lays them down to rest, dragging them off with frantic, tiny grips until a long finger reaches quietly out of a crack and lifts the stone back out of their claws.

Only after you have watched for a long while do you realize that not all the hands you are seeing belong to the same living body.

Like the dome-backed grazers on the floor, the occupant molts. Because the kiln-heat and mineral salts harden its outer hide over uncounted cycles of the pocket’s slack time, it periodically splits its own crusted skin along the spine and steps silently out—pale, raw, and tender as fresh tallow—into the steaming water.

The shed skin does not collapse.

Slowly, its dorsal seam closes back together. Stiffened by its outer plating of silica, hollow-chested and lighter than the flesh that left it, the cast-off husk rises on six-fingered hands and slips off into the narrower faults.

They are everywhere once you learn to tell them from the living limbs: thinner, paper-pale, their eyes clouded over like drops of dried glue, their joints clicking silently as they haul themselves along the undersides of overhangs. They are material echoes of the occupant—anatomical afterimages lingering for weeks or seasons the way a shadow lingers for two heartbeats. They do not eat, though now and then one will crack a dome-backed grazer with a pebble and push the steaming meat into its own sealed mouth until the scraps spill back out through a gap between its dry ribs. What they keep are the habits of the hands that wore them. They gather freshly popped obsidian from crevices too tight for the parent body; they worry dull basalt chips against wet stone; they carry the occupant’s favorite smooth hammers from pool to pool, keeping them from sliding over the outer lip.

Now and then, in the bright sunlight near the center of the floor, the living occupant reaches for a wedge of glass at the exact moment one of its older husks crawls over the same shelf. Their forearms cross and pass cleanly through one another like smoke over water. For a heartbeat the fresh pink flesh wears the brittle, salt-crusted skin like a loose sleeve—and then the living fingers close around the obsidian edge the husk was carrying. Both snap abruptly solid. The empty skin does not resist; it opens its hollow, black-dusted digits, yields the blade, and scuttles gently back into the hairline dark of the wall. When a husk grows too ancient and mineral-choked to move cleanly—its joints frozen into white salt—the occupant reaches up to where the brittle shell clings to the obsidian roof, peels it down in quiet strips, and chews the mineral-rich lining soft between its grinding plates, leaving nothing behind to clog the seams.

Twice an hour, when the young mountain beneath the pocket exhales, the occupant stops pretending to fit inside the margins.

It knows the surge is coming before the basalt groans—perhaps because it feels the future tremor in the slack water, or hears the soundless groan already arriving behind its jaw. A beat before the deep furnace-heat rises, its limbs gather their smooth basalt hammer-stones and tuck them into deep horizontal notches in the rock. The husks press themselves flat against the walls, hooking their stiff fingers into the fresh fractures.

Then the walls weep in earnest. Scalding sheets of condensate flood the basins, lifting the living mosaic into dense, churning currents, popping fresh splinters of black glass from the ceiling like dark rain. As the water brims toward the outer lip—where it crosses into ordinary mountain air and explodes instantly into ragged plumes of ice-crystal mist—the pocket flickers violently out of alignment with itself.

In that brief, blinding bright confusion, the occupant dilates to fill all the folded inches at once.

For ten breathless seconds, the pantry-sized alcove seems packed from floor to obsidian fang with a single, tangled, many-armed presence. Pale forearms overlap in three pools simultaneously; six hands, then ten, then a shimmering thicket of wet, silica-scaled fingers snatch newly popped obsidian blades out of the rushing water before the overflow can sweep them over the brink to shatter in the cold. Living limbs and hollow husks blur together in the midday glare, passing through one another as they herd the richest drifts of fresh glass back into the deep scallops. You catch a glimpse of a long, pale torso arched across a space far too small to contain it—two narrow heads turning in unison toward the same falling stone, or the same single head seen twice from slightly different moments.

Then the deep furnace eases back down into the spire.

The flood subsides. The overflow snaps shut, leaving only a thin fringe of hoarfrost melting rapidly back from the outer lip. The flickering settles, and the occupant folds neatly away again into the hidden yards of the rock, until all you can see is a single wet elbow gleaming behind a cluster of crawlers and a slender, six-fingered hand rinsing a newly caught obsidian crescent in a finger-deep pool.

Sometimes, in the long, muffled quiet between surges, it comes right up to the threshold.

It will not cross the invisible line where the kiln-heat meets the freezing altitude. Out there, the air would snap-freeze its boiled hide in three breaths; worse, the honest physics of the open sky would force all its folded length into one narrow span of rock, collapse its wandering husks into dead mineral rinds, and bind every gesture strictly to its own aftermath. It knows where the world ends, down to the millimeter.

Yet it leans its narrow, smooth face just shy of that boundary, resting its chin on the warm basalt lip while plain afternoon sunlight pours straight across its sulfur-yellow eyes. Far below, the dull white shelves of cloud drift slowly over an empty blue drop.

Now and then, its long fingers lift a spent, blunted chip of obsidian—or a dry curl of silica shaved from its own brow—and flick it gently across the edge.

The warm, wet sliver crosses the invisible wall, flashes instantaneously with white frost, and drops away into miles of thin air, vanishing without a sound. The occupant watches the empty place where it fell for several slow heartbeats, the membranes behind its jaw perfectly still, as if waiting for a delayed *tick* that this time will never climb back up the rock.

If you stand at the mouth of the cleft long enough, leaning slightly into the wet kiln-breath to trace the tools in the pools, you may notice a smooth, plum-glossy pebble of basalt resting on the wet stone inches from your boot—an edge of the shelf you could swear was empty a moment ago.

You reach down to look closer, and half a breath later a soft, dry *tap* arrives from the obsidian roof behind your shoulder. Down at your feet, a pale, six-fingered hand—crusty with milky silica, fingertips stained black with volcanic glass—emerges from a crack no wider than a coin, touches the already-resting pebble with terrible, quiet care, and glides backward out of sight, leaving concentric rings of warm water that tighten smoothly inward until the basin is flat as a mirror.
