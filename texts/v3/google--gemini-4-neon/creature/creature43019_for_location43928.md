From a mid-slope catwalk, watching the dark oilskinned figures dart through the mist between carts and scaffolds, a visitor assumes they are a labor force brought in from somewhere beyond the ridge—men and women who climb down at the end of a shift to dry villages in a wider, sunnier valley.

There is no wider valley for them. Nobody commutes six miles up narrow wet viaducts through cloud thick enough to blind a lantern. The people who work the terraces live inside the terraces, wedged into the masonry with the same stubborn economy as the liverworts and the wild ferns.

Because not a yard of bare mountain remains, their homes occupy the spaces left over by engineering: the deep brick vaults beneath the viaduct piers, the narrow vaulted galleries tucked directly behind retaining walls, and—most prized of all—the dry pockets built flush against the warm backs of the transformer rooms and steam-return manifolds. You can walk past a dozen family quarters without knowing they are there. Their doors are low, iron-bound, flush with the mossy brickwork of an alley, distinguished from a pump-house or a tool-crib only by a brass boot-scraper worn thin in the center, a hung bundle of drying willow withes, or a neat chalk mark beside the latch showing which shift is currently asleep inside.

Step through one of those doors, and the true religion of the valley reveals itself: dryness, and radiant heat.

Outside, everything weeps, sweats, and runs. Inside, the brick walls that back onto a transformer housing or a heating header give off a steady, dry, faintly resinous warmth that never drops, winter or summer. Overhead, slatted alder racks hang from iron hooks, draped with heavy knitted woolens, canvas tool-rolls, and patched waxed oilskins that drip into shallow lead trays below. Heavy rubber boots stand inverted over rows of warm copper pegs fed by return-water bleed pipes so the insides dry before the next watch. There are no loose rugs, no clutter on the concrete floors to wick dampness upward, and the small windows—deep-set, double-glazed with old ribbed wire-glass—are half-curtained from outside by dripping fern fronds, admitting only a watery, pewter wash.

And in every room, beneath the clink of stoneware mugs and the whisper of wool, lies the deep, steady electrical hum of the brick wall behind the bed-alcoves. Valley-born people sleep badly without it. On the rare occasions when one of them travels down the sorting-yard line to the lowlands, they lie awake for nights in dry wooden inns, unsettled by how dead the floor feels beneath them—no vibration in the plaster, no muffled *thump* of a sluice-gate seating itself three tiers down, nothing moving through the dark.

***

A hundred years of cold water, dim light, and steep wet stone have shaped the people as visibly as the mountain drafts have sheared the coppiced willows.

They are seldom tall, and they move with a low, economical grace that looks almost cautious until you see how fast they cover ground. Knees slightly bent, shoulders tucked, weight held over the balls of their feet, they test every slick slab and algae-filmed grating by feel before committing a stride. Nobody swings their arms or walks upright with their chin lifted; there are too many low pipe-racks, dripping eaves, and overhead trolley wires for that. Even indoors, they duck slightly through doorways that have plenty of clearance.

Their skin, where the oilskins and woolens cover it, is remarkably pale—the flat, cool ivory of cellar shoots—while their cheeks, ears, and wrists are chapped and weathered a dull brick-red from mountain squalls. Their hands are broad and permanently marked by the valley: short-clipped nails rimmed with dark peat and black bearing-grease, calluses built up along the outer palms from hauling wet rope and gripping cold iron bars, the skin of their fingertips slightly thickened and pale from hours spent in cold water-troughs. Look closely at a glazier’s forearms and you will see dozens of tiny silver thread-scars from wire-glass; look at a moss-cutter’s knuckles and the creases are stained faintly green-brown with sphagnum tannin that no amount of lye soap ever quite takes out.

Their eyes are adapted to the valley’s permanent overcast. They read subtle differences in grey the way lowlanders read bright color: the faint milky cast in runoff that means mortar is washing out of a culvert behind a wall; the dull yellow-olive tinge at the base of a fern crown that warns of cold-rot two days before the frond wilts; the barely perceptible sag in a timber roof-purlin under wet moss. On the rare noon when the cloud-deck tears completely open and direct mountain sunlight hits the glass bays, half the valley squints and pulls the brims of their oilskin caps down low, complaining of headaches until the stratus mercifully rolls back over the northern rim.

Their voices, too, belong to the acoustic blanket of the basin. Out on the decks, shouting is useless—the wet greenery and low cloud swallow it within twenty paces, and a raised voice carries an edge of panic that sets everyone’s nerves on edge. So their public language is mostly silent, built for visibility through drizzle and heavy mittens. It is a wide, clean vocabulary of forearm sweeps, wrist turns, and finger counts: a flat palm patted downward twice means *sluice opening below*; two fingers drawn across the throat and pointed skyward means *squall cresting the headwall*; a slow circular turn of the fist means *cart coming off the loop*.

When fog drops too thick for hand-signs, they use the skeleton of the valley itself. Air refuses to carry sound here, but cast iron and steel carry it for half a mile. Almost every worker carries a short, heavy box-wrench or an iron sluice-key at the hip, and knows how to speak through the pipe-racks and catwalk rails. Two sharp taps and a pause on a steam-return pipe calls a glazing crew down a bay; a rapid, urgent chatter of four strikes against a rail-truss warns that a load has slipped or a retaining tie-rod has snapped. Indoors, when they do speak aloud, they talk close-mouthed and low, leaning in, wasting few words. When an outsider from beyond the rim laughs loudly in a basement canteen, heads turn with the same quiet startlement they would give a cracking pane of glass.

***

Though the whole basin shares the same damp grammar, six miles of stepped rock and several hundred feet of altitude sort the inhabitants into distinct kinds of people.

Up along the highest terraces, where the stainless-steel fog-catchers stand in the raw breath of the northern crags, live the **Rim-folk**—the windbreak pruners, mesh-menders, pulley-tower riggers, and cableway dispatchers. Their quarters are squat, thick-walled concrete bunkers half-sunk into the rock behind the upper headwall, bolted down against gales that can strip a galvanized roof clean in ten seconds. They are the leanest and hardest-bitten people in the valley, with squinted eyes, hoarse throats, and heavy wool scarves wrapped up to their cheekbones. They are the valley’s skin—the first to feel a temperature drop, the first to see ice forming on the wire mesh, the first to strike the iron pipes to send frost-warnings rippling down through three hundred tiers of glasshouses. They pride themselves on living where the cloud is still clean and wild, before it has picked up the smell of grease and peat.

Across the vast middle sweep of the valley live the **Glass-folk**, who make up most of the population: the fern-tenders, cress-harvesters, moss-framers, glaziers, track-switchers, and mobile masonry gangs. They live in a dense, crowded honeycomb of brick corridors tucked directly under the cultivation decks, where the ceilings always smell faintly of wet soil and warm compost. Their lives are governed by the rapid nervous twitch of the terraces—louvers cranking shut and open, trays rotating up toward brighter bays, rot scraped out before it spreads, tarpaulins wrestled down over storm-breaks. They are quick-handed, gossipy in their low-voiced way, endlessly inventive with wire, putty, salvaged tin, and timber shims. Their kitchens smell of sharp highland herbs, boiled watercress, bitter medicinal tisanes, and dark oatbread steamed in lidded tins over electric coils.

And down at the very floor of the basin, where the tiered canals converge and the flat daylight scarcely penetrates through the stacked viaducts overhead, live the **Bottom-folk**—the turbine-minders, sump-cleaners, filter-rakers, and transformer-keepers. Down here the air is warmer, heavier, and thick with the clean metallic bite of ozone, hot oil, and wet limestone silt. The lamps stay on around the clock, casting everything in a steady amber and pale-green wash. Bottom-folk rarely climb to the rim; some have not seen the sky above the northern ridge in ten years. Their skins are the palest of all, their hearing almost entirely tactile. You will see them crouching beside a submerged turbine housing with a turned alderwood sounding-rod pressed at one end to the iron casing and at the other to the bone just behind their ear, eyes half-closed, listening to the pitch of the falling water for the tiny gritty stutter that means a willow branch or a cracked brick has slipped past the upper grates.

They trade across the tiers constantly—not with money so much as with favors, salvaged fittings, and small luxuries: a rim-rigger swaps a bundle of dense willow-burl carving wood for a bottom-minder’s fine brass oil-can; a glass-bay mother sends a stoneware crock of spiced cress-pickle down on an empty rail-bogie and gets back a pair of freshly vulcanized rubber soles for her boy’s boots.

***

Because the valley never stops, nobody sleeps at the same time. Day and night are barely distinct beneath the stratus anyway; the only true divisions are the three overlapping shifts—the Grey Watch, the Mist Watch, and the Lamp Watch—and the steady hydraulic pulse of the basin. People tell time without looking at dials. They feel the eleven-minute flush of the upper root-beds through the soles of their boots, hear the low shift in transformer pitch when the lower packing-decks come on load, and know the hour by which cable-buckets are passing the alley mouth.

Children are born into this restless quiet and shaped by it before they can walk.

There are no open meadows for them to run in, no bare dirt to dig, and outside any doorway lie rushing water-flumes, automatic rail-switches, and steep drops onto wet iron. So very small children spend their first years inside the dry interior warrens and the netted upper sorting-lofts, crawling across clean concrete warmed by steam-pipes, playing with offcuts of rubber gasket, smooth brass nuts, and dried sphagnum tufts. Once they are steady on their feet, their parents fit them with braided hemp chest-harnesses clipped by a brass ring to a taut overhead guide-wire strung along a sheltered terrace. You see them gliding back and forth along twenty yards of wet catwalk like tiny cable-buckets, completely calm, watching their parents strip broken glazing bars or bundle watercress.

Their games are quiet and mechanical. They carve little boats out of sheared alder bark and race them down the shallow concrete overflow gutters, betting smooth glass marbles melted from broken bay-panes on which sump-grate will catch them first. They press their knuckles to the radiator pipes and tap out long, giggling conversations in wrench-code with children three apartments down. By eight years old, a terrace child knows which moss hides rotten mortar, how to hear a dry pulley-bearing from fifty feet away, and how to read a cloud-bank coming over the headwall for whether it brings drizzle, sleet, or a glass-lifting downdraft.

Nobody here retires into stillness, because stillness is the one thing the valley cannot afford. Instead, as knees stiffen and lungs grow tired of cold mist, the old ones migrate inward and downward—off the swaying scaffolds and slick catwalks, into the warm, dry, precision-work heart of the complex.

They become the **mesh-menders**, sitting under bright amber lamps with curved needles and spools of hair-fine stainless wire, repairing torn panels from the fog-catchers. They become the **spore-keepers** and **cuttings-minders** in the warm, quiet propagation cellars, tracking humidity and rot in damp-warped ledgers with stubs of waxed crayon. The sharpest-eared among them sit in tiny brick switch-boxes perched over viaduct junctions, wrapped in layered wool over hot copper water-bottles, with brass listening-tubes pressed straight into the retaining walls. They are the living memory of the hundred-year patch job. An eighty-year-old woman in Switch-Box Nine can tell a masonry crew, without leaving her stool, that the cracked culvert under Tier 62 was shored with pine instead of oak in the frost of thirty winters ago, and that the faint, steady *tick* she feels through her boot-heel means the iron collar has finally begun to slip.

And when they die, there is no earth to bury them in. High along the northern headwall, in the oldest, thickest dry retaining buttresses above the flood-line, rows of small square niches are recessed into the stone. After the body is reduced in the high-heat drying kilns down by the sorting yards, the ashes are sealed into a niche behind a hand-cut square of dark slate. No long epitaphs—only a name, two dates, and a deeply incised trade-mark filled with white lime-paste: a glazing knife, a sluice-key, a pruning hook, a sheaf-wheel. Within two wet seasons, velvet moss creeps over the edges of the slate and softens the lettering, until a grandchild or an apprentice climbs up on a quiet watch with a stiff brass brush, scrapes the stone clean, and leaves a fresh sprig of highland thyme wedged into the mortar joint.

***

The valley’s human inhabitants are not quite alone, though a visitor walking the catwalks might think so. The prompt scarcity of wild beasts is real enough: no burrows, no undisturbed woods, no slow ponds, no birdsong. Yet life does not surrender every seam, and the terrace-folk know the small, pale, silent neighbors that share the wet machinery with them.

They know the **lime-slugs**—fat, slow, chalk-white creatures that cling to the undersides of concrete sluice-lips and graze the slick algae along weeping brick foundations. Workers scrape them ruthlessly off the tender fern shoots into salt-tins, yet nobody minds them along the culverts; old masons say a stretch of wall with no silver trails on it has gone too dry or cracked through to an air-pocket behind the stone.

They know the **lamp-moths**—tiny, slate-grey, cold-tolerant things with furry thoraxes that appear in the dimness before evening and press themselves completely flat against the wet glass cages of the amber work-lamps. The crews watch them closely. When the moths sit with their wings slightly lifted, the night will stay merely wet; when they flatten tight as pasted leaves with their antennae tucked completely beneath them, a high-altitude frost is sliding down the northern peaks, and within ten minutes the iron pipes begin to ring with the tap-code to drain the exposed lines and close the windward louvers.

And once in a long while, a **mountain pipit**—blown down from the bare crags above the cloud-deck by a violent downdraft—lands shivering on a rusted gutter or flutters inside an open bay, terrified by the endless green and glass. When one appears, a rare, hushed ripple passes across the terraces. Hand-signs flash bay to bay: *bird on Forty-Two*. Nobody shouts or reaches for it. A harvester quietly lays a pinch of crushed oatcake or a few drowned moths on a dry parapet; if the small grey bird has beaten itself into a corner of a glasshouse roof, a glazier climbs silently up with a hooked pole, eases a high louver open, and stands perfectly still in the drizzle until the pipit finds the gap and vanishes back into the rolling mist. It is an old, unspoken superstition among the decks that a bird dying under glass brings a wind-peel before the next moon—though in truth, the people simply feel an odd, tight tenderness for anything small and warm-blooded that gets caught inside the machine by accident.

Deeper still, in the hidden microclimates no visitor ever sees, there are smaller things yet: pale, long-legged cellar spiders spinning damp webs across the warm copper conduits behind the transformer vaults; tiny, blind white springtails grazing the microscopic fungal bloom on the undersides of rotting cedar moss-frames; and down in the very deepest sump-galleries, in a single forgotten dead-eddy behind a cracked masonry baffle where the turbine draw cannot quite reach, a colony of translucent, ghostly little freshwater shrimp—descendants of mountain-spring creatures washed in a century ago when the valley was first concreted over, still turning slowly in the pitch-dark, lime-rich water.

Nothing noisy. Nothing bold. Everything here that survives has learned to be small, damp, pale, and patient.

***

To understand the people who live here, you have to watch what counts as cruelty among them, and what counts as love.

In a world ruled by cold water, gravity, and rot, anger rarely explodes. A fierce argument between two terrace-hands looks, to an outsider, almost eerie in its restraint: tight faces, sharp clipped hand-signs, a spanner set down a fraction too hard on an iron rail, two backs turned in absolute silence. The worst insult—the nearest thing to a vendetta—is petty neglect: failing to clear a clogged grating on your neighbor’s run, leaving a louver-shaft ungreased so the next shift has to fight it, letting a torn tarpaulin flap without throwing a hitch over the line. To invite rot or standing water onto another person’s tier is a betrayal of the whole basin.

Affection is equally quiet, and almost always tactile and practical. It is warming someone else’s rubber boots over your own copper peg before their shift begins. It is sliding a dry pair of knitted wool wrist-warmers into the pocket of a tired partner’s oilskin without mentioning it. It is giving up the warm brick side of the bed-alcove on a night when frost is spitting through the upper meshes, or stepping smoothly into the windward position on a slick scaffold so your mate can work in the lee of your shoulders. In courtship, young workers leave small, dry, carefully chosen things on a doorstep or a bench: a freshly honed putty-knife with a smoothed alder handle, a clean piece of soapstone, a tightly stoppered glass phial of sweet oil distilled from highland herbs.

Some of the young do leave, every generation. They ride the empty freight cars down through the mist-choked mouth of the valley to the lower sorting yards, and from there out into the wide, flat country where the rivers run over mud and the roads are dry dust. A few stay gone and send back letters full of strange complaints about heat and bright sky.

More of them come back after a year or two.

They step off the returning rail-bogie in the dim pewter light, breathing in the cold iron-and-fern smell with their eyes half-shut, and when their families ask them what it was like out beyond the ridge, they struggle to put it into words. They say the sky down there feels torn open—like a glasshouse roof ripped clean off in a gale, leaving you exposed with nothing overhead. They say the noise of engines and shouting voices bruises the ears, and the sun hurts like a bare filament, and worst of all, the ground feels strangely lonely: dry, flat, and unmoving, asking nothing of your hands, giving nothing back through your boots.

***

Toward evening, when the heavier bank of mist pours like slow surf over the northern headwall and swallows the fog-catchers, the willows, and the upper tiers one by one, the valley does not go to sleep—it simply turns inward.

Across thousands of wet glass bays and narrow brick alleys, the amber interior lamps blink on automatically, bleeding warm gold through the dripping panes and the rising cloud. Inside the glasshouses, the Lamp Watch crews pull their oilskin hoods tight, chalk their tallies onto wet slate, and roll the fresh racks of ferns and moss toward the lifts. Overhead, the cable-buckets keep emerging from the white wall and vanishing back into it, *tick-tick-tick* along their greased sheaves.

And behind the mossy masonry, tucked into the warm brick pockets beside the humming transformers, the off-watch crews peel off their dripping coats, hang their woolens to steam, and sit close around stoneware bowls of hot, peppery cress-broth. Someone taps a slow, gentle three-note goodnight on the cast-iron heating pipe; a moment later, faintly, an answer taps back from two tiers down. Outside, the water keeps falling, the turbines keep trembling under the weight of the mountain, and the ferns keep pushing quietly against the wire-glass—and inside, with their palms resting against the warm, vibrating brick, the people of the valley rest lightly, half-listening even in their sleep, the quiet, tireless nervous system of a wet world that would unravel in a single season without their hands.
