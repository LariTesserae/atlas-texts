To look at them at noon is to misunderstand them entirely.

Spaced along the bright margins of the floor, hunched inside their private circumferences of chalk, they seem as solitary and mineral as the salt-rimmed saucers beside them. The glare flattens the far side of the chamber into a milky shimmer, turning each distant maker into a pale blur tucked into a wall-scallop, scarcely distinguishable from a fold of gypsum. You might watch for an hour and never see two of them look in the same direction.

Move closer, though—close enough to see the fine chalk flour they rub deliberately across their narrow backs and shoulders to blunt the vertical burn of the ceiling—and you begin to see how completely their bodies belong to this light. Their spines arch in a permanent, graceful stoop, presenting only the smallest ridge of pale skin to the blazing zenith. Everything vulnerable lives underneath. Their ribs, laced with faint blue veins, curve downward to shelter their long hands, their bellies, and the small, sharp pool of violet shade pooled directly beneath them. That tiny patch of violet is the only coolness they own. They work inside it, sleep inside it, and carry it with them whenever they shift a pace or two across the floor.

Beneath the jutting shelf of the brow—thick, matte bone, faintly pitted like dry coral—their faces sit in perpetual twilight. Their amber eyes are rarely turned upward. Only when a cloud-smudge drifts across the unseen plain above, dragging its bruised grey shadow slowly over the chalk, do they tilt their chins by a fraction of an inch. Then the vertical slits dilate, drinking the brief, soft dimming of the dome, watching the weather glide across the floor like a cool palm before the glare closes over them again.

Yet they are never alone. The eye is fooled by the white air; the ear is not.

Because the dome is smooth and unbroken, the chamber holds them in a single, hushed membrane of sound. They do not need to cluster together to keep company, because distance here does not soften a note. Forty paces across the blazing floor, an old maker taps a flake of grey chert with a sliver of bone—*tink*—and a younger one kneeling beside the eastern creeper hears it as cleanly as if the elder sat at its shoulder.

They know one another entirely by cadence.

Each maker has a signature in stone and breath: the weight of the strike, the angle of the pitch, the exact number of heartbeats allowed to pass before the soft puff of air that clears white dust from the edge. An elder’s tapping is dry, unhurried, and resonant, spaced by long, serene silences that stretch to half a minute or more; every blow detaches a blade thin as a fingernail. A young maker’s strikes arrive slightly too bright and too close together, betraying an impatient wrist or a flake held too steeply against the palm.

Sometimes, when a younger one chips a clumsy, flat-sounding stroke into the stillness, no head turns. No one calls out. A voice would feel coarse in this warm, glassy air, too wet and heavy for the bowl. Instead, across the blinding middle of the room, an older maker simply waits ten breaths, then delivers a single, pure *tink* of the right pitch and weight. A pause. Another *tink*. Nothing more. Across the glare, the younger one stills its hands, shifts the chert in its lap, and tries again, tuning its rhythm to the answer hanging in the dome.

This is how they speak—not in words, but in intervals, in the dry rasp of stripped creeper-fiber rolled across a pale thigh, in the soft click of a thumbnail testing a gypsum point, in the shared attentiveness of the silences between. When one falls ill, or slows with age, the others know it long before they see it: a familiar percussion along the northern wall grows thin, misses its measure, or fails to resume after a passing cloud.

Their days are built around an economy so patient it barely looks like effort. Nothing here is abundant, yet nothing is scarce to a creature that wastes neither water nor heat. They know the temperament of every damp seam in the chamber—which wall-sweat will evaporate before noon, which high crystal ledge sheds a drop every four minutes, which saucer turns too bitter with dissolved salt after a dry week overhead. Their braided creeper-wicks are quiet, exact instruments, weighted with pebbles and hung just so along the smooth scallops to intercept condensation before the stone drinks it back.

They eat little and slowly: the pale, onion-skinned snails pried from their mineral crusts with gypsum needles; a pinch of sour, watery pith scraped free from the glazed stems of a chartreuse fern; an occasional alabaster lizard taken without chase, snared in a hair-thin fiber loop laid across a warm wall-ledge where the reptile comes to wait for midges. When the afternoon heat begins to slacken and the rising thermals falter, bringing the nearly invisible clouds of midges sinking toward the floor, a few makers will smear a broad, translucent splinter of gypsum with sticky snail-mucus or crushed creeper-sap and hold it upright in the glare like a tiny pane of glass. By dusk, the clear stone is dusted with silver wings, which they lift cleanly off with the flat of the tongue.

They hoard nothing. A blade that dulls is simply set down beside the knee; a wick choked with crystalline salt is left hanging to stiffen into stone. The floor keeps everything for them, and there is no rush to carry weight when fresh chert flakes can always be worried loose from the lower seams.

If you look closely at certain makers hunched tighter than the rest, their folded knees pressed close against their chests, you may catch a faint, rhythmic stirring inside the violet shadow beneath their bellies.

Their young are born one at a time, no larger than a thumb, blind, and nearly clear as wet glass—so translucent you can see the tiny, pale-pink pulse fluttering inside the chest. For its first long season, the newborn cannot endure the vertical glare. Its skull is still smooth and thin; the heavy brow-visor has not yet begun to grow. It lives entirely inside the pocket of shadow cast by its mother’s hunched ribs, clinging to the cool, vein-laced skin with needle-fine fingers, drinking thin, watery milk while the white noon burns millimeters beyond its toes.

Only as the bony ridge above its eyes begins to thicken—hardening slowly from pale, pliant cartilage into the chalky eaves of adulthood—does it start peering out past its mother’s knees into the bright emptiness. Its first tools are the salt-frosted scraps lying forgotten within arm’s reach. One afternoon, inevitably, it picks up two blunt chips of flint and strikes them together: *clack. Clack.*

The sound is awkward, shallow, and far too loud for the hush of the bowl. Yet all around the wide, glaring perimeter, forty paces of bright air away, hands pause over half-twisted fibers and half-finished blades. For a few breaths, the entire chamber goes completely still, listening to the bright, uneven tapping of a new pair of hands—and then, one by one, the older rhythms softly resume, folding the clumsy little noise into the chorus of the rim.

What the daytime observer never sees is what happens when the sun finally drops below the edge of the unseen arid plain.

It happens gradually, with no horizon to mark it. The ferocious milk-white overhead softens first to warm ivory, then to the pale grey of wet ash. The sharp violet shadows tucked beneath every belly begin to blur, bleeding outward across the chalk until the whole floor is one gentle shade. If the surface sky is clear and moonless, the dome cools to a deep, velvet dimness, holding only the faintest ghost of starlight piped through sixty paces of crystal reeds. When the moon is full above the desert, the entire ceiling glows a soft, luminous pearl—bright as a frosted lamp, cool as standing water.

And with the fading of the glare, the cathedral opens.

For the first time all day, the makers unfold their long hind legs and rise. Standing, they are unexpectedly graceful—slender, light-boned, moving with a quiet, springy crouch across the open center of the floor that noon had kept forbidden. Their bare heels make scarcely a whisper in the chalk flour. Their amber pupils, released from the punishing light, widen until their eyes look dark, liquid, and enormous beneath their heavy brows.

Night is when they belong to one another.

They do not gather in crowds—their temperament is too quiet for crowds—but in small, shifting pairs and trios scattered across the cool plain of the floor. In the pearled half-light, they sit knee to knee, taking turns picking flakes of mineral salt from the pitted ridges of each other’s brows and the hollows between the shoulder blades where a single pair of hands cannot reach. They press their cool foreheads lightly together in greeting, lingering for a breath to catch the faint scent of wet chalk, fern-sap, and warm clay.

Now they trade what the margins have yielded: a clean, dark nodule of chert prized out of a southern fissure exchanged for a tight coil of fine creeper-cord; a smooth, uncracked saucer shared quietly by three makers sipping from the rim with cupped palms. In the deeper scallops away from the central dimness, pairs press their pale, vein-laced chests together and mate in near-silence, only the softest catch of breath carrying across the dark bowl.

Sometimes—once in a season, or once in a decade—something foreign drops into the chamber through the narrow fissures that feed the seams. A wind-blown thorn from the desert above; a dry, hollow beetle carapace, black-green and iridescent; a bleached pebble worn by surface rain. On those nights, a small ring of five or six makers will gather in the middle of the floor, kneeling close in the moonlight, passing the alien fragment slowly from hand to hand. They turn it beneath their widened amber eyes, testing its weight against their palms, tapping it once with a fingernail to learn its sound—a sound that belongs to no stone in their dome—before someone finally sets it down on the bare chalk, leaving it for the slow mineral dust to claim.

Nothing leaves this chamber, and very little enters it. Even the dead remain.

When a maker grows very old—when its bony brow has grown thick and chalk-rimmed, its knuckles stiff with mineral salt, and its tapping turns frail and uncertain—it does not withdraw into panic or cling to the others. On a cool night, it simply walks to a dry, flat curve near the perimeter where no ceiling-drops fall. There it curls tightly onto its side, knees tucked beneath its ribs, its long five-fingered hands folded neatly in the shadow of its own visor, and grows still.

In the warm, dry air of the hollow, the body does not foul the stillness. Over seasons, it dries clean and light as papery lichen, shrinking gently until the pale skin hugs the delicate ribs and the heavy-browed skull. No one buries it; the stone of the chamber is never broken. For years, the others leave it untouched, letting the fine chalk flour drift into its hollows while the familiar silence along that stretch of wall settles into memory.

Only long afterward, when the sun-bleached stillness has left the bones clean, smooth, and odorless as old stone, will a younger maker walk quietly to that scallop in the pearl-dim dark. Kneeling beside the curled dry frame, it eases loose a single slender forearm-bone or curved rib, tests its heft between thumb and forefinger, and carries it back to its own patch of floor. There, it rubs the tip smooth against the scalloped gypsum wall until it gleams faint ivory.

When morning returns and the ceiling kindles once more into blinding white, the young maker hunches inside its small pool of violet shade, takes a palm-sized flake of flint in one hand and the ancestor’s dry bone in the other, and strikes.

*Tink.*

The sound crosses forty paces of warm, dazzling air, crisp and intimate as a touch at the elbow. All around the bright rim, the pale makers sit far apart in the glare, listening to the dead keep time with the living, while beneath their folded heels the chalk slowly swallows the old bones, the frosted blades, and the salted cords—turning centuries of quiet hands back into the smooth white bell that holds them.
