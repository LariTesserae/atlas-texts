Seen from across half a mile of simmering evaporation pan, the people of the plateau hardly look like people at all. They appear as pale, geometric notations against the glare—white smocks, wide circular brims, the narrow black horizontal cut of slit-goggles—moving with such measured economy along the causeways and anode racks that a stranger might mistake them for counterweighted extensions of the sluice-gates and bronze wheels. Only when the midday sun drives them under the recessed colonnades, out of the silver-white blaze and into the black-violet wedges of shadow, do they loosen their neck-cloths, lift their goggles, and show what the basin has made of them.

They are a lean, salt-cured people, pale as tallow where the linen covers them and weathered to a dry, faint amber at the wrists, knuckles, and throat. No one here has the ruddy burn of lowland field-hands; high-altitude glare reflected off wet sinter does not tan so much as bleach and toughen. By thirty, most pan-tenders carry fine white creases at the corners of their eyes that never quite smooth out, even in sleep, and many of the elders have milky, pearlescent crescents creeping across the whites of their eyes—scar tissue from a lifetime of squinting across mirrors of hot brine. Their fingernails grow thick and slightly chalky from the mineral vapor; their hair, kept clipped close or braided tight beneath reflective caps, takes on a dry, metallic sheen from the ozone that hangs near the electrolytic yards.

More unmistakable than their faces is the way they carry their weight.

A plateau-born worker never drops a heel onto stone. Even indoors, barefoot on the cool flagstones of a residential vault, they place the outside edge of the foot down first and roll the weight gently forward across the ball, knees subtly unlocked, testing the floor before trusting it. It is an unconscious mirror of the tall waders stalking the flats outside—the same deliberate suspension, the same refusal to disturb the surface they stand upon. Watch a woman set a stoneware pitcher onto a slate ledge, and you will see her little finger slip instinctively beneath the base to cushion the last millimeter of descent. Watch two men pass in a narrow stair-shaft, and neither lets a belt-buckle brush the plastered wall. Silence here is not merely an absence of clamor; it is a physical posture, learned in the muscles long before a child understands why the cracked circle is painted on every parapet.

They have lived on the taut mineral shelf for enough generations that their speech itself has thinned and split to fit the terrain.

Out on the bright flats, nobody uses a throat at all. Between causeway gantries and distant condenser yards, they speak in *flash-and-fold*—the sharp, rhythmic code of tilted brass mirrors and matte-black canvas flags that cuts cleanly through the heat-haze. At closer quarters, across a single refining basin or between two flat-bottomed punts poling among the anode racks, they use a brisk, angular hand-language built for visibility in blinding light. The inner cuffs of their white linen smocks are dyed soot-black; a flick of the wrist turns a dark square outward against the glare, then hides it again, spelling out current loads, brine temperatures, or the approach of a loaded rail-bogie in crisp, silent blinks. Any newcomer who watches both a punt-crew and a pair of contesting tall waders soon sees the kinship: the same sudden shutters of matte black against white light, the same rigid economy of posture, as if the city and the birds had arrived at the same grammar from opposite ends of the food web.

Only below, inside the deep vaulted galleries hollowed beneath the pale brick quarters, do they let their voices out—and even there, the language sounds like water slipping over smooth rock. Over generations, the hard percussive stops of their ancestors' lowland tongue have worn soft. Spoken low in the chest, close to the listener's ear, their vault-speech is a breathy, hushed current of vowels and sibilants that carries comfortably beneath the steady sigh of the convection shafts without ever slapping back from the stone. Raised voices are rare enough to be frightening. When two householders quarrel in the vaults, they do not shout or strike a table; they lean close, faces rigid, their whispers thinning to a dry, pressurized hiss like steam escaping a hairline valve, while their hands flash short, cutting signs just below the collarbone. Even laughter is mostly visible—a quick, soundless shaking of the shoulders, eyes crinkling shut, breath released through the teeth in a soft, rhythmic puff.

To enter one of the residential quarters at noon, when the sun stands surgical overhead and the glare shrinks the outside shadows to razor-slits, is to step into another world entirely.

Every doorway has two thick curtains of oiled felt hanging across a stepped vestibule, and the heavy doors themselves have no spring-latches—only counterweighted wooden tongues that turn smoothly in tallow-greased housings so nothing can ever slam. Inside, the air is twenty degrees cooler than the flats and smells cleanly of wet chalk, damp pumice, distilled water, and old beeswax. There are no windows opening onto the blinding white plain. Instead, narrow light-wells angled through the thick refractory roofs bounce daylight off glazed green-white ceramic tiles, filling the vaulted chambers with a calm, shadowless luminosity like the underside of a quiet sea. Overhead, the louvered convection towers draw heat steadily upward through porous stone chimneys, keeping a faint, cool draft brushing along the floors where people rest on low sling-cots of woven rush and canvas.

Because heat demands distance, the city does not have a single center; it is an archipelago of self-contained quarters separated by half-miles of blinding brine and gleaming travertine. Each quarter—whether built around a cluster of electrolytic pans, a solar-drum yard, or the great blackened-bronze lift-wheels—functions almost like an island village, with its own cisterns, its own felt-draped dining halls, its own subtle dialect of cuff-flashes and flag-folds. Families stay within a quarter for generations, marrying across the causeways only when a young wire-tender or punt-master transfers to another yard, arriving silently on an electric bogie with a chest of resin-turned bowls, cork-soled boots, and rolled linen.

Nothing green grows on the plateau, so every scrap of grain, dried root, linen thread, timber, and lamp-oil must be hauled up the worn western escarpment from the lower lands in trade for the city's rare salts and pale crystalline metals. Imported wood is treated almost like ivory—never burned for fuel when geothermal heat can be piped directly into wax-jacketed cooking chests and slow steam-ovens buried in the kitchen masonry. Yet the plateau feeds its own people more generously than a visitor expects.

In the cool midday vaults, families gather around low stone tables inlaid with polished disks of iridescent glass—the shed and harvested shells of the basin's flat grazers. Peeled free of its translucent silica lens and steamed in distilled water with imported vinegar, oil, and crushed dried herbs, the pale meat of the grazer is the city's daily staple, dense and cleanly briny, tasting faintly of mineral broth. Alongside it sit flat unleavened cakes of lowland barley, pale rock-crabs trapped in the dark lower cisterns, and small glazed crocks of a sharp, rust-colored paste made by washing, pressing, and fermenting the warm ochre microbial mats scraped from the cleanest runoff channels. They drink distilled water drawn from the shaded condenser cisterns, always dosed with a measured pinch of cured basin salt so it will not pass through a sweating body too thinly. Even the tableware obeys the old discipline: bowls turned from dense horn or heavy glazed clay footed with rings of vulcanized rubber, spoons carved of smooth bone, cups set onto woven rush coasters so no vessel ever clicks against stone.

The youngest inhabitants of the plateau are born in the deepest, softest rooms of the city—double-walled vaults built directly over beds of loose pumice grit and hung floor-to-ceiling with thick layers of dampened wool felt. A newborn's cry cannot crack the mineral shelf beneath the city, of course; everyone knows the difference between an airy wail and a percussive shock to the stone. Still, sharp sound grates raw on plateau nerves, and a crying infant brings an instinctive, ancestral tightening to every shoulder within earshot. So the nurseries are hushed cocoons where mothers rock infants in leather slings suspended from oak beams, soothing them with slow, rhythmic pressure against the back and a low, steady humming vibrated directly through the collarbone rather than projected into the air.

A plateau child's earliest schooling has nothing to do with letters or sums. It is called *learning weight*.

Before a child is ever fitted with a pair of slit-goggles and allowed to step across the threshold of an arcade into the glare, they are taught to walk across a floor strewn with dry pumice grit without making a whisper, to lift and replace a weighted brass lid without a tap, and—the reflex drilled into every apprentice until it becomes as automatic as blinking—to thrust a cork-shod foot beneath a falling tool so it strikes soft leather and bone rather than bare travertine. A bruised instep heals in a week; a dropped bronze spanner can wake a hairline fissure half a mile down.

Even their play belongs to the basin. In the long shaded arcades overlooking the weeping masonry of the boiler houses, children squat motionless for half an hour at a time, watching the heavy slate-grey wall-baskers cling to the damp ceramic tile. They learn to read the reptiles' moods—the sudden, pulsing waves of violent cinnabar and sulfur-yellow rippling along a rival's flanks—and mimic them with dyed squares of silk tucked into their sleeves. They know never to startle a basker, for the crews value them: the creatures clear wandering brine-vermin from the pipe-galleries, and they are better than any gauge at feeling an unnatural tremor in the masonry. If every basker along a boiler flank flattens abruptly and drains to dull ash, the children know without being told to leave the arcade and fetch a watcher.

Out in the bright air, the relationship between the workers and the wild things of the flats is just as quiet and just as unsentimental.

When the late-afternoon barometric drop eases the air and the weeping tide begins its slow, two-finger rise across the terraces, the punt-crews emerge from the arcades in their reflective linen and dark goggles. Before a single cork sole touches the causeway, each worker lifts their boots for a partner to inspect the treads; a single grain of stray quartz wedged in the cork will grind against travertine, and nobody steps out uninspected. Then they slide their flat-bottomed punts off oiled timber skids and pole smoothly out among the submerged anode racks, their poles padded at the tips with bundled felt and vulcanized sleeves.

Often a tall wader stands ten yards away in the scalding mirror-water, needle-thin horn legs locked motionless beside a calcified pier. Neither species yields the basin to the other, yet neither breaks the truce. Veteran punt-men recognize individual birds across seasons by a chipped ridge on a leg-sheath or a pale notch in the matte-black throat-frill; they know which waders will shadow a harvest punt like pale surveyors, stepping carefully through the gently stirred water behind the anode racks to spear the glass-shelled grazers dislodged from the sinter. A worker never swings a pole near a wader, and a wader rarely flashes its warning frill at a crew that moves at the basin's proper pace. Both are creatures of the same scalding engine, paced to the same slow pulse.

High above them, along the narrow shaded catwalks of the switching stations and condenser towers, live and work the wire-tenders—usually the lightest, nimblest youths of the quarters, with copper-stained palms and insulated resin-soled slippers. Their domain is the shimmering upper air where the copper busbars hum faintly with direct current and the ribbon-winged gliders wheel in vast, silent spirals over the hot updrafts. At dusk, when the bleached sky turns burnished brass and the gliders' trailing silver tail-filaments begin to spark faintly with static gathered from the high-voltage lines, stray threads occasionally snag on the porcelain standoffs. A wire-tender leans out from the catwalk with a long, waxed wooden crook, freeing the translucent creature gently without snapping its paper-thin wing or shorting the line. The shed filaments that catch on the insulators are never discarded; collected carefully in dry horn tubes, they are spun by the quarter's needlewomen into fine metallic thread used to stitch delicate static-tassels onto smock collars—tiny whiskers of silver that lift and fan outward whenever a worker approaches an ungrounded busbar.

Every season, a few outsiders climb the western escarpment drawn by the high wages paid in pure refined salt and copper scrip. They arrive from the lower valleys where wind roars through trees, rain hammers on tin roofs, and men shout across muddy streets. To the plateau-born, these newcomers seem almost unbearably loud—creatures made of loose joints and hard edges. They clear their throats in vaulted corridors; they drop their heels on travertine; they laugh with their mouths wide open, throwing sudden bursts of noise against the tile that make everyone nearby wince and freeze. Worse, they are unnerved by the blank silver sky, the absence of a single green blade, and the sensation of walking across a bright mirror with scalding water beneath their boots.

Half of them break before the solstice and go stumbling back down the escarpment trail, starving for the rustle of leaves and the right to slam a door. The other half—the quiet ones, or the desperate ones—stay long enough for the plateau to cure them. Slowly, their shoulders drop into the glide of the causeway gait. Their hands learn to check the descent of a cup. They stop squinting in confusion at the mirror-flashes across the salt-pans and begin reading them as easily as print. By their third year, if an apprentice from a newer draft drops a spanner on concrete, the former lowlander freezes right beside the tall waders and the wall-baskers, breath held for twenty bright, trembling seconds, listening through their soles to see whether the floor will answer.

Because somebody has to listen all the time, not just when something falls, the city maintains a guild older than any of its refining houses: the *Listeners*, though the pan-crews more often call them the *Foot-Readers* or simply *the Low Watch*.

Most of them are elders whose eyes have finally given out after thirty or forty years on the blinding flats—men and women with silver-clouded vision who can no longer read a distant heliograph or spot a submerged fracture in an anode rack, yet whose remaining senses have sharpened to an almost uncanny stillness. They leave the bright quarters behind and move down into the deepest dry inspection cellars beneath the city's oldest foundations, where the thick refractory masonry sits directly upon the bare, warm bedrock of the mineral shelf.

It is dark down there, and perpetually warm, smelling thickly of dry sulfur and ancient lime. The Listeners go barefoot, their soles as sensitive as palms, sitting for six-hour watches on low woven mats beside the hairline fractures left over from the forgotten, blasphemous age of steam-hammers and blast-charges. Across the oldest cracks, fine hairs of annealed brass are pinned taut between stone pegs; beside them sit shallow slate basins filled with heavy, black mineral oil, levelled to a fraction of a degree.

A Listener does not listen with their ears—the air down in the foundation cellars is dead silent. Instead, they lay the flat of a heel against the warm rock and rest two fingertips lightly against the rim of an oil-bowl, or brush a knuckle across a tension-wire without plucking it. They can feel the aquifer breathing hours before the upper city sees a change: the slow, vast swelling of the weeping tide gathering beneath the basin floor; the faint, subterranean tick of cooling mineral scale detaching inside a deep fissure half a mile away; the subtle tremor of an overburdened electric bogie whose vulcanized wheel-band has worn thin on the northern causeway.

When a Listener feels a wrongness in the stone—a tautness rising too sharply beneath an electrolytic yard, or a hair-fine concentric ripple shivering across the black oil when no tide is due—they do not run or cry out. They pull a weighted linen cord running up through a pumice shaft to the nearest causeway gantry. Above, a watcher smoothly flips a ceramic plaque to show the cracked circle and flashes a single, sustained beam of white sunlight across the flats. Without a word, bogies coast to a stop. Sluice-gates ease open to bleed pressure into the broad eastern evaporation pans. Workers step quietly off the stone and wait, rigid and watchful, until the shelf settles and the oil in the dark bowls goes mirror-smooth again.

And when a life on the plateau finally runs its course—whether a Listener in the warm foundation dark, a punt-master withered by decades of brine, or an elder who has never once seen a tree—the city has no soil to dig a grave and no wood to spare for a pyre.

Instead, they carry their dead at twilight, when the glare has burned down to brass and the heat-haze softens over the terraces, out along a quiet, unused spur-causeway toward the far eastern rim. There, beyond the active refining yards, lie the White Beds: acres of ancient, abandoned settling pans where old iron sluices and retired pump-houses have already been swallowed half-way by decades of steady, silica-rich runoff, their sharp angles rounded into gleaming, pearlescent ledges.

No bell tolls. No dirge is sung. The mourners step off the last low stone pier in weighted boots, wade silently ankle-deep into the warm, clear runnels, and lay the wrapped body—stitched tight in clean white linen weighted with small blocks of porous pumice—into a shallow basin where the mineral water glides only a few millimeters deep. They stand motionless for a span of breaths, black wrist-cuffs turned outward in a final, silent sign of release, then turn and glide back toward the shadowed arcades.

The plateau does the rest.

Over months and years, the hot, silica-heavy brine creeps gently over the linen, laying down coat after translucent coat of glossy pale sinter. Sharp contours soften. Folds of cloth turn to smooth stone. In time, the dead become low, pearlescent swells indistinguishable from the calcified piers and the ancient stepped rock of the tableland itself. Across their bright, mineral-sealed shapes, the glass-shelled grazers creep in slow, rainbow-flashing arcs, rasping away the faint orange bacterial films, while tall waders step delicately among the white mounds on needle-thin legs, pausing motionless in the bright air.

Even in death, the people of the plateau do not break the surface or disturb the quiet. They simply settle into the shelf, layer by mineral layer, becoming part of the thin white stone that holds back the deep water—living and dead alike, keeping the truce of the bright, scalding basin in the breathless silence under the silver sky.
