If you drifted through these sunken lanes for a day, or even a week, you would leave convinced the place was altogether empty of anything with a will of its own. Nothing darts behind a lintel at your approach. No pale shape withdraws into a drainage channel. The kelp fronds hang whole and unbitten; the moss keeps its plush, undisturbed nap; the compressed mats of dead leaves lie flat and unchurned in the corners of the courtyards. Whatever lives here is not animal, has never been animal, and moves at a pace that human eyes mistake for geology.

You have to stop swimming. You have to hang motionless in the doorway of a windowless stone room, wait for the fine dust of your passage to settle, and look closely at the shadowed angle where two dry-stone walls meet the floor.

There, wedged into the corner above a silt-choked trough, is a dark mound that at first seems only another hummock of sodden moss or a slumped heap of decayed sea-wrack. Its upper surface is the same bruised plum and wet charcoal as the fallen blades around it, dull and rubbery, dusted with a faint skin of grey silt. Only as your eyes adjust to the near-absolute dark do you notice the pleats—heavy, deliberate folds layered one over another like a thick woollen cloak dropped in water and left to sink. And beneath the lowest hem, where the dark hide meets the rounded shoulder of a basalt flagstone, there is the barest gleam of something pale and velvety, pressed flush against the stone.

It has no head, no face, no eyes to turn toward the pewter stain far above. It has no mouth, no teeth, no gut, no shell, no bone. It is a mantle—a dense, colonial sheet of cold, leathery membrane, folded back upon itself into a low, hooded bell no larger than a crouched body, or, in the oldest rooms, spread wide enough to carpet a whole threshold. On its upper face, it is thick, dark, and nearly impermeable, built to endure the crushing cold and the slow abrasion of drifting grit. On its sheltered underside, hidden in the permanent night between its own body and the stonework, it is pale and porous, lined with a silvery-white velvet of living biofilms and fine, chalky filaments precipitated directly from the lime-rich water.

It belongs to the same strange chemistry that kept fish and crabs from ever entering this sea. It does not eat the forest. It does not need to.

Beneath its heavy, pleated skirts, the mantle seals a shallow pocket of dead water against the top skin of the underwater peat and the weeping seams of the masonry. There, in the airless cold, millennia of stacked, unburied kelp blades slowly ferment, sweating out faint traces of methane, dark organic acids, and dissolved sulfur, while the ancient limestone blocks beneath release a steady, microscopic trickle of calcium and mineral salts. The dweller simply lies over the seepage and drinks it through its pale underside. Inside the thickness of its dark hide lie dozens of flat, interconnected chambers—bladders lined with pale bacterial webs that turn that frigid chemical breath into slow-growing tissue, fibrous cord, and a thin, cold warmth no sharper than a hundredth of a degree.

Because its feeding leaves the sunken leaves untouched—taking only the ghost of gas and dissolved salt rising out of them—the peat mats remain flat and intact. And because it carries no skeleton, no carapace, and no calcified shell, it leaves no trace when its centuries finally end. A dead mantle simply loses the tension in its pleats, flattens out into a sheet of dark rubbery tissue indistinguishable from the fallen kelp beneath it, and lets its fragile inner veil of chalky lace dissolve quietly back into pale lime-silt.

Yet while it lives, it faces one constant, patient danger: its own breath.

As its internal chambers process the cold seepage from the peat, waste gas collects within them. Left unchecked, the mantle would swell, grow taut, and pull free of the flagstones, rising helplessly through the vaulted kelp canopies toward the fatal thinness and bruised pewter brightness of the upper waters. To remain down here, in the dark where the food is, it must keep itself heavy.

It manages this in two ways.

The first is the sound you took for the landscape itself. Once every few days—or once every few weeks in the leanest seasons—a resting mantle slowly draws tight a muscular ring hidden among its upper folds and lets go a single heavy, pearlescent bead of gas. The bubble slips free from the dark pleats, shoulders through the overhanging kelp, and rises into the gloom with a solitary, muted *bloop*. Across a mile of drowned alleys, a dozen of them may vent in the course of a day, keeping their equilibrium by fractions of an ounce.

The second is why, if you ever peeled back the moss on a sunken threshold or searched the center of a basalt plaza, you would find small, inexplicable clusters of smooth stones resting on top of the peat.

Along the lower margin of its pleated skirt, the mantle ends in a thick, blunt, muscular rim—blind and slow, creeping by the width of a finger over an afternoon, gripping the stonework with the patient strength of a kelp holdfast. With this edge, it gathers ballast. It works loose dull terracotta pebbles ground from ancient pottery beneath a cracked flagstone; it claims fist-sized cobbles from collapsed rubble, weathered chips of basalt, dense knuckles of leached limestone. Slowly, over weeks, it draws them inward and tucks them into deep, fibrous pockets folded along its lower hem. Weighted by ten or twenty pounds of stone, it can lie slack and untroubled in a rich cistern even as its internal bladders swell with gas.

And it is the stones—along with the slow weather of the drowned plain—that let it travel.

It cannot swim. It has no fins to beat, no jet to force water behind it. When the seepage beneath a courtyard grows thin, or a slow bottom-shift buries an alley under suffocating clay, or some older, deeper impulse stirs through its fibrous cords, the mantle prepares to move with a patience that takes weeks to unfold.

First, it stops venting. Day after day, it holds its gas inside its chambers, letting the dark pleats along its back smooth out into a swollen, taut dome. Then, very slowly, it opens the pockets along its hem and releases its heaviest stones—not scattered carelessly, but eased down onto the flagstone in a neat, low ring where it rested. Lightened just enough to hover an inch or two above the moss, holding fast by a single trailing fold hooked around a doorjamb or the lip of a drainage channel, it waits for the water to lean.

When one of the vast, sluggish currents builds across the submerged plain—wheelings of cold water that take days to gather and days to die—the maroon kelp canopies tilt twenty degrees along the axis of an ancient avenue, parting to reveal the straight stone runs beneath. Into that leaning drift, the mantle lets go.

It does not dart. It glides with the solemn, unhurried gravity of a cloud shadow passing over hills. Hovering three or four feet above the flagstones, its dark dome taut and buoyant, its lower skirts trailing just above the moss-furred wall-tops with a handful of smaller pebbles still tucked in its hem to keep it upright, it slides silently down the thirty-foot slab-paved roads alongside the drifting flakes of organic snow. To steer, it contracts one internal bladder and relaxes another, shifting its bubble of gas like a spirit-level to bank gently around a leaning corner, or vents a small bead to sink into the quiet lee of a retaining dike, or trails a blunt margin downward to snag a weed-furred monolith and halt its drift.

In the middle of a three-day current, along the straight avenues that cross the terraced paddocks, you might see five or six of them moving in loose, widely spaced procession through the dim vaulted tunnels of kelp—dark hooded silhouettes barely distinguishable from torn curtains of sea-wrack, traveling miles without spending more energy than it takes to tighten a fold. When one passes over a district whose water tastes sharply of rich peat and raw limestone, it vents a heavy bubble (*bloop*), settles softly down through the swaying fronds like a dropped coat, and begins the week-long work of gathering new stones to anchor itself to a fresh floor.

They know nothing of light, except as an unpleasant, prickling thinning of the water overhead if a freak eddy lifts them too high. Instead, they inhabit a world of extraordinary richness built from chemistry, pressure, contour, and low sound.

Their pale undersides read the ruined plain the way a tongue reads salt and iron. Every district has its own unmistakable savor. The tight warrens of low stone rooms taste of old clay, ground terracotta, and concentrated, bitter peat trapped in narrow cisterns. The open rectilinear paddocks taste clean and sharp, washed by broader currents and dusted with pale lime. The great circular plazas of fitted basalt slabs are austere, cold, almost metallic, swept free of heavy silt by the subtle eddies that curl around their wide rings. Even the ghost of another dweller lingers for years on a flagstone—a faint, sweet trace of altered biofilm and chalky crust telling any newcomer who passes over it how long its predecessor rested there, and what minerals it favored.

More intimately still, they know the ruins by shape.

A mantle has no centralized brain; its awareness is spread through a dense, slow-firing lattice of fibrous conducting cords woven through its dark hide, and—strangely—through the physical memory of its own undersides. When one rests inside a doorway or folds itself into the corner of a sunken trough for twenty or thirty years, its thick, pliable flesh slowly molds itself to every pitted chisel-mark, every dished-out stair tread, every rounded arris of the masonry beneath it. Even after it inflates and drifts away on a current, its lower surface keeps the faint, embossed topography of the last several places it inhabited, smoothing away only over decades. It carries the geometry of the drowned plain pressed directly into its own body.

And because its gas-filled chambers act as taut resonance drums in the dense cold water, it hears the whole plain at once. It feels the rhythmic, twelve-second swell of a distant surface storm rocking the kelp forests as a gentle, cradle-like pulse passing straight through its core. It feels the pressure of a coming current half a day before the fronds begin to bend. And when a stone hollowed by ten thousand years of dissolution gives way by half an inch a mile away, sending that dull, low *thud* rolling softly across the dark water, every mantle within leagues feels the note quiver clearly through its bladders.

Sometimes, that sound is not a collapsing wall.

Now and then, a large drifter arriving at an open basalt plaza—or a settled dweller wedged beside a leaning parapet—will deliberately lift a heavy ballast-stone in its blunt margin and let it drop cleanly onto a bare, resonant slab. The low *thud* travels outward in a slow circle across miles of gloom, followed by a lazy puff of pale silt. There are no words in it, no haste, no urgency—only a sparse, immense telegraphy across the silence. A mark of presence. An announcement that a plaza is occupied, or that a deep channel has opened, or that a vast plume of milky silt is creeping in from fifty miles to the south.

When the silt-fog arrives—turning the already dim alleys into absolute grey blindness for days—the dwellers sense its heavy, gritty approach long before the first cloud curls through the doorways. The loose drifters draw their hems tight and hold enough gas to hover well clear of the suffocating floor. The settled ones, anchored in their rooms, begin a slow, undulating ripple across their pleated backs—a single wave taking hours to pass from rim to crown—shrugging the settling grey dust off into the drainage channels while their undersides drink eagerly from the fresh surge of leached lime and clay minerals carried in the fog. When the water clears days later, leaving a new microscopic skin of dust over the stonework, their hidden undersides are brighter, newly thickened with delicate chalky lace.

In a world stretching across hundreds of miles of drowned plain, encounters between them are rare, unhurried ceremonies.

Often they happen at the circular plazas where the thirty-mile slab-paved roads terminate, or along the sheltered, straight runs of silted aqueducts where slow currents naturally funnel drifting bodies together. Two mantles, arriving days apart from different quarters of the plain, settle side by side on the fitted basalt. For the first few days, they do nothing at all, merely resting while the maroon fronds sway above them and organic snow settles lightly over both their dark domes.

Then, slowly, their trailing hems begin to creep outward across the stone until they touch—and overlap.

Where their pleated margins meet, the dark outer hides soften and part along hundreds of microscopic pores. For weeks, they lie fused along that shared border. Without sound or sudden motion, they exchange fluid, living cultures of pale biofilm, dissolved chemical impressions, and slow, rhythmic pulses of tension along their fibrous cords. One that has spent half a century tucked inside the clay-rich warrens of the east tastes the wide, open, lime-washed paddocks of the west; it takes in strains of bacterial velvet adapted to cold basalt, while passing along a culture tuned to squeeze life out of nearly exhausted terracotta silt. Through those slow contractions, they trade the embossed maps of walls, cisterns, and sheltered channels they carry in their flesh.

When they finally draw apart, they are not always the same two individuals that met.

Sometimes, if the twelve-second swell of a distant storm rocks the dark plain while they lie joined—a rare, rhythmic agitation in a world of near-total rest—a small, taut lobe pinches slowly free from their shared seam. No larger than a folded scarf, holding a single blended memory of both its parents and clutching three or four tiny terracotta pebbles in its newborn rim, the young one inflates quickly and catches the very next current. For its first decades, it is a restless creature by the standards of this sea—drifting every few months, snagging briefly on the tall weed-furred monoliths along the paved roads, tasting dozens of districts before it grows heavy enough to claim a room of its own.

Other times, two old, tattered mantles do not separate at all. They merge completely, pooling their bladders, their fibrous lattices, and their stones into a single immense cloak that slowly creeps off the plaza to seek a deep, sheltered courtyard.

And in those deepest places—the sunken cisterns, the wide, roofless stone enclosures choked yards deep with ancient peat—live the oldest of them all.

These have given up the drift entirely. After centuries of growth, they have become too vast and heavy to ever lift again, spreading fifteen or twenty feet across a courtyard floor, draped seamlessly over low dividing walls and fallen lintels until they look like the very contours of the buried ruins. Long ago, they dropped their ballast-stones for good; instead, they have sent pale, threadlike rootlets of dense biofilm deep into the mortar-leached chinks between the flagstones, anchoring themselves to the masonry as permanently as the stone itself.

Over decades, the forest grows directly onto them. Thick, rubbery holdfasts of giant kelp grip their broad, leathery backs without harming them, raising twenty-foot curtains of dark mahogany and ink-maroon blades from their living hides. When a sluggish current wheels across the plain, the grove on an elder's back leans with the rest of the forest, tugging gently at its dark mantle and telling it, through that quiet pull, which way the water runs and how fiercely the unseen storms are breathing miles overhead. When those fronds finally age and tear free, they sink right along the elder's margins, adding another cold-preserved layer to the peat that feeds it.

These rooted ancients barely stir from one century to the next. Yet they are not asleep. Through the vibrations of the flagstones beneath them, through the pull of the fronds upon their backs, through the occasional young drifter that settles along their edges for a month to press its hem against theirs and drink their vast, layered memory of the plain, they remain quietly tethered to the whole drowned world.

They have no notion that the flagstones beneath them were ever quarried by hands, or that air once filled the windowless rooms, or that anything ever walked upright along the thirty-foot avenues. To them, the fitted stone is simply the generous skeleton of the earth—an ordered landscape of corners that trap good seepage, straight channels that guide the leaning currents, smooth plazas built for meeting, and neat, weathered pebbles waiting to be gathered and carried.

Yet without ever knowing the builders existed, they have inherited the city entirely. They occupy its dark rooms; they travel its broad, monolith-lined roads when the water leans; they gather in its circular squares; they leave small rings of smooth stones on its thresholds like quiet offerings. Beneath the cold pewter ceiling, between the softened dry-stone walls and the swaying, unbitten forest, they live at the exact tempo of the ruins themselves—unhurried, heavy, patient as silt, keeping the long silence of the drowned plain.
