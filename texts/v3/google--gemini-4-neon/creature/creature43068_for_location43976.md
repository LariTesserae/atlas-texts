You can tell one of them before they ever unbuckle their oiled leather mask.

It is in the hands first, and then the shoulders. Nobody who lives in the cleft ever moves with their weight settled flat onto their heels; they travel along the mesh catwalks in a low, fluid crouch, knees soft to absorb the sudden bone-deep flex of a braced beam, one palm always gliding lightly along the copper-wrapped handrail. Even when they stop to rest, they do not lean back against the stone. They hook an elbow through a spreader-bar or clip a short brass frog from their belt-ring to the nearest cable, hanging half-suspended in the amber dusk with the unconscious ease of sailors asleep in rigging.

When a shift ends and a worker ducks into one of the warm transformer bays to peel back their mask and wool hood, the mountain shows plainly in their face.

They are barrel-chested, almost all of them, with deep ribcages built to drag enough oxygen out of air that leaves a lowlander grey-faced and vomiting. Their blood runs thick and dark with iron; it gives their lips, gums, and nail-beds a faint, bruised plum tint that looks alarming in the pale-violet noon blade, though down in the sodium lamplight it merely makes them seem ruddy and carved of old hardwood. Because the air steals moisture from anything left exposed to it, they grease their cheeks, nostrils, and knuckles every morning with pale lanolin whipped with tallow and beeswax. Beneath that faint sheen, their skin is dry as cured parchment, crosshatched around the eyes from a lifetime of squinting past high-voltage corona-glare and razor-bright quartz dust. Their eyelashes and brows are perpetually frosted—not with ice, which cannot linger here, but with talc-fine white mineral grit that never quite combs out.

And everything on them is leashed.

A wrench hangs from the wrist by waxed hemp; spectacles of smoked mica are buckled behind the skull with leather straps; a stub of white slate-chalk, a brass vernier-key, even a dented tin mug dangles from a worker’s belt on short braided cords. Up here, a dropped object is not an inconvenience. A three-pound spanner falling ninety feet onto a stressed phyllite ledge can wake a fracture and shear a whole tier of kilns into the Sump. Among the Cleft-folk—they call themselves *the Wedged*, when they bother to name themselves at all, or simply *High-side*—dropping an unleashed tool carries the quiet, cold horror that arson carries in a timber town. Children have their first wooden spoons tied to their wrists before they can sit upright. To call someone *loose-handed* is the worst insult in the fissure; it means you cannot be trusted with the rock.

---

They live in a state of constant, delicate electrical negotiation.

In waterless air crowded with spinning magnetic rotors and singing rectifiers, every wool-clad body becomes a walking capacitor. Take twenty strides along an insulated gangway and you carry enough charge to throw a half-inch blue arc across a doorway—painful to flesh, and ruinous to the hair-thin alloy filaments and sintered wafers the workshops turn out. So their quilted vests are stitched at the cuffs, collars, and hems with fine annealed copper thread, and every boot has a braided brass ground-tag riveted under the arch to kiss the steel mesh with each step.

Out of that necessity has grown an entire grammar of touch.

Before skin meets skin—before a rigger hands a flask to a partner, or a mother lifts a child out of a canvas sling—they ground themselves. Usually it is a quick, absent-minded brush of the knuckles against the nearest steel strut, or a sharp tap of the heavy brass rings most adults wear on their right thumbs: ring to ring, *snick*, a tiny blue needle of light jumping harmlessly from metal to metal before fingers interlock. To touch someone’s bare neck or cheek without grounding first is an intimate, wicked little shock—used in flirting, in waking a sleeping shift-mate who missed a chalked rotation, or, delivered hard across the back of an ear, as a quiet warning that a quarrel is turning serious.

In the same way, the thin air and the restless mountain have reshaped their speech.

Out on the open tiers, almost no one speaks aloud. Raising your voice is useless against the thin atmosphere—a shout carries barely thirty feet before turning papery and flat—and speaking at length strips the throat raw in minutes. Instead, the Wedged talk with their hands in a crisp, economical cant built to be read across a dim forty-foot chasm through a tangle of busbars and cables. Because the amber lamps leave hands in deep silhouette, workers rub white quartz-chalk into the seams of their glove-knuckles, or wear pale bone-white leather caps on their index and middle fingers so every flick and curl flashes cleanly out of the gloom.

It is a one-handed language by design, because the other hand is almost always on a rail, a pulley, or a tether. Watching two riggers argue across the Winding-Well while winching a live mercury-arc bank away from a creeping seam is like watching two moths fence in the dark—sharp, snapping bursts of pale fingers, punctuated by an occasional flat palm slapped silently against a quilted chest. Only inside the tight corkboard residential cages, where the air is warmer and slightly less fierce, do they use their *cage-voice*: a dry, rhythmic, breath-saving murmur kept close to the collar, full of clipped consonants and soft tongue-clicks that carry clearly across three feet of bunk-space without ever drying the lungs.

Yet for all their quiet, they do not think of their city as silent.

Ask one of the *Bite-Watchers*—the seam-tenders who patrol the narrow brass vernier bridges—and they will hand you their listening-rod. It is a foot-long taper of seasoned ash, shod at one end with a blunt stud of soft copper and notched at the other for the teeth. You press the copper gently against a raw slab of black phyllite, close your lips around the wood, and clamp down with your molars.

Instantly, the silent city explodes inside your skull.

What the thin air refuses to carry, the newly born stone carries with terrifying fidelity. Through the bones of your jaw, you hear the mountain alive and crowded: the high, glassy hum of the magnetic rotors whining down through their upper steel anchor-bolts; the rhythmic double-pulse of the suspended stamping-presses three tiers across; the dry hiss of mineral powder pouring into the Sump. Beneath all of that—slower, deeper, and far more solemn—is the stone itself. You hear the outer cliff ticking like cooling cast iron as a cloud-shadow sweeps past the noon sun-blade. You hear the slow, cello-like groan of deep tectonic pressure miles beneath the peak, and the hairline *tink-tink-tink* of a quartz vein stretching by a tenth of a millimeter somewhere behind the Kiln-Stacks.

The Bite-Watchers spend six hours a shift walking the sheer walls like this, jaws aching, eyes half-closed, reading the city’s health through their teeth. They can tell a stressed turnbuckle from a slipping rock-plate by the pitch of its hum; an old Watcher can press her rod to an iron gantry and tell you which transformer bay forty yards down has a coil-pika chewing the tallow-gasket off its vent. They are the closest thing the cleft has to priests—quiet, slightly hunched people with deep bite-grooves worn into their wooden staves and flat, flattened edges to their front molars, who move through the frantic scaffolding with unhurried authority. When a Bite-Watcher pulls her rod from her mouth and chalks a single downward chevron on a steel truss, nobody asks questions; the rigging crews are already clipping onto the trolley-lines before the chalk dust settles.

Domestic life happens in the warm margins—tucked into cork-lined sheet-metal cages wedged beneath stair-landings and hung from overhead spreader-beams, anywhere the rock is reasonably steady and the waste heat of an induction kiln bleeds gently through a brick baffle.

Nothing in a cage sits loose on a shelf, because there are no true shelves—only netted cubbies, pegged boards, and canvas pockets sewn onto the walls. People sleep in taut, cocoon-like hammocks rigged on rubber-bushed swivels so that when a shear-gust slams the cliff outside and the whole chasm shudders, the sleeper rocks softly instead of slamming against riveted tin. Even in sleep, a Cleft-dweller hooks one calf over the side-rope.

Water is the one true luxury, hauled up from beneath the cloud-deck in sealed iron flasks on the same brutal counterweight winches that lower crates of sintered piezo-wafers and hair-thin alloy spools down into the unseen lowlands. Because open water boils lukewarm and weak at this altitude—barely hot enough to scald—every residential tier shares a communal battery of heavy, bolted brass pressure-kettles set over the kiln-flues. When the brass toggles are lifted, the kettles vent through thick wool mufflers with a damp, muffled sigh, pouring out brick-tea so dark, bitter, and scalding it tastes like roasted bark and iron. They stir in lumps of salted yak-tallow or clarified butter until a golden slick coats the rim, drinking it slowly to soothe throats scraped raw by ozone and grit.

Nobody washes with water; to waste half a pint on dirt is a kind of madness. Instead, after a shift in the graphite and mineral dust, workers sit around the communal heat-grates and scrub their arms and chests with handfuls of warm, dry oat-bran mixed with fine chalk, rubbing the dark grease off in grey rolls before wiping down with tallow-dampened felt. They eat food that cannot spoil, because nothing truly rots up here—it only dries to horn and salt. Pressed cakes of barley, blood, and suet; strips of wind-cured mutton tough as harness-leather; dry salted root-chips; and, as a prized treat when the Kiln-Stacks are swept, a handful of bronze-shelled oil-beetles tossed briefly onto a hot ceramic transformer cover until they pop faintly like tiny, peppery seeds.

They never eat the swifts, and they never eat the pikas.

Between the humans and the cleft’s beasts runs a fierce, unsentimental treaty—less like affection than like the mutual vigilance of crews sharing a leaking hull.

Every residential bay leaves tallow-rinds and wool-scraps along its lower rails for the coil-pikas, and in the cold outer shafts a worker sitting still during a rest-bell will often feel a warm, dense weight nose under the hem of their quilted vest. The pikas curl right against a human belly for the heat, their broad leathery paws tucked under their chins, their thick grey fur crackling softly with tiny blue sparks against the worker’s wool shirt. Nobody brushes them away. The pikas feel the rock shift before even the Bite-Watchers’ rods catch it. If a pika uncurls from your ribs, flattens its stiff whiskers, and pours silently out of your coat to race single-file up the overhead busbars, you do not finish your tea. You seal your flask, clip your harness, and follow the grey stream upward before the crimson shutter drops.

The soot-swifts are treated with a quieter, almost superstitious tenderness. When their mudless nests of lint and copper shavings tumble from a cooling transformer shelf, workers tuck the fallen fledglings inside their shirt-fronts and climb up to wedge them back under a live casing. In return, the city harvests the birds’ molted wing-feathers—those matte-black quills with soft, frayed trailing edges that kill air-noise—and stitches them into the insoles of rigging-boots and the flutter-valves of breathing-masks. A master rigger walks on swift-feathers, silent as smoke.

Higher still, at the brutal, razor-edged throat of the fissure where the pale-violet sky burns dark as a bruise, work the *Web-Combers*. Dressed in double-quilted coats and smoked-quartz goggles, lashed to the rock lips by three redundant chains, they tend the shimmering silver curtains of the drift-weavers. Using long bone needles, they ease down the oldest, heaviest sheets—mats of non-conductive spider silk clogged thick with abrasive quartz grit, meteoric dust, and frozen lowland pollen—and string fresh copper wire looms for the flat, pale spiders to re-weave. The harvested silk is pressed between warm iron plates into supple, featherlight insulating felt that no lowland mill can match. When a Comber accidentally brushes a drift-weaver off a steel strut, they catch it carefully in a cupped wool glove and set it back in the lee of a turnbuckle; the spiders keep the rotors alive, and the rotors keep the town from freezing.

Every few months, when the stratospheric shear drops for half a morning, the long blind cables rising through the cloud-deck bring up a handful of *Low-comers*—desperate debtors, runaway apprentices, or contracted technicians lured by the wages paid in rare-metal scrip.

You can spot a Low-comer across the whole chasm. They cling to the handrails with white-knuckled panic, gasping loud, wet breaths that turn their lungs to fire. Their noses bleed in dark threads that dry almost instantly to black crusts across their upper lips. Worse, they are loud: they drop tin cups, they bump elbows into resonant steel struts, and when a thirty-foot iron gondola shoots silently past their ear on padded rails, they yelp. Whenever a Low-comer cries out, the nearest Wedged worker does not scold them—they simply cross the catwalk in two soundless strides and press a tallow-scented leather palm gently, firmly over the newcomer’s mouth, holding eye contact until the panic recedes, then tapping one chalked knuckle twice against the rock wall to remind them *who is listening*.

Most Low-comers go back down on the next empty counterweight sling, weeping with relief as the tin sea of clouds closes over their heads.

The ones who stay—and the generations born right here inside the crack—become something the lowland world below scarcely understands.

Cleft-born children grow up suspended. They learn to read chalked hand-sign before they can shape a cage-whisper; they learn to tie a figure-eight tether and ground their knuckles before they ever see a tree or a patch of soil. To a child of the Wedged, the stories Low-comers tell about the world beneath the clouds sound like fever-dreams: a place where water falls loose and screaming out of an open roof, where iron rots into red flakes, where the ground lies obscenely flat for miles with nothing overhead to clip onto and no walls to hold the sky apart. The rare Cleft-born envoy who descends below the cloud-deck to negotiate ore-weights or water-quotas almost always falls sick within hours. Down there, the air feels thick and wet as warm porridge; their massive lungs ache from the sheer weight of it; and the constant, deafening roar of wind in leaves, voices in streets, and water running unchecked over mud drives them to huddle inside cramped cellar rooms, plugging their ears and craving the crisp, ozone-clean grip of a steel rail.

---

Even inside the fissure, there is a slow migration across a human life.

The young belong to the high wind and the open gulf—swinging on trolley-lines, wrestling turnbuckles, combing spider-silk in the noon violet blade. Middle age draws them inward to the Winding-Well and the precision bays, where steady fingers sinter mineral wafers and draw alloy filaments finer than a swift’s down.

And when age finally stiffens a worker’s knees and clouds their eyes with decades of mica-glare and high-altitude light, they do not retire to any quiet lowland meadow. They move downward and inward, to the *Warm-Bays* nestled directly against the brick flanks of the great annealing kilns—the steadiest, warmest pockets in the whole restless cleft.

There, on suspended cork platforms padded with old swift-feather quilts, the elders sit cross-legged in the cherry-red glow of the kiln viewports, surrounded by sleeping heaps of static-crackle pikas that treat their laps like live transformers. They are the city’s memory and its finest hands. Because paper turns brittle as dry leaves in the waterless air and crumbles within a few winters, the elders keep the *Chalk-Ledgers*—hundreds of thin, hinged tiles of local black phyllite hung on braided copper rings, etched with fine steel styluses and rubbed with white talc. On those black stone pages lives the true history of the peak: every hairline crack that ever opened since the first anchor-bolt was driven, how many millimeters the east face crept during the great winter frosts, which seams growl before they split, and the names of every child born in the cages. Between entries, by touch alone, their horn-callused fingers twist the hair-thin filament spools and mend the horsehair catch-nets that guard the tiers from falling slate.

When one of them dies—quietly, usually in sleep, their thick blood finally slowing in the warm dry dark—there is no thought of lowering them down through the clouds. The Wedged have a quiet horror of the wet lowland earth, where a body swells, softens, and dissolves into mud.

Instead, they wash the dead one last time with warm bran and cedar oil, braid their copper ground-rings into the cuffs of their children’s vests, and wrap them tightly in layers of pressed drift-weaver silk. At dusk, when the noon heat drains out of the outer ridge and the shear-gale dips into its brief twilight hush, four riggers haul the light, narrow bundle up past the humming Venturi rotors, past the trembling silver webs, and out through the lip onto the knife-edged black blades of the mountain’s spine.

They carry the dead along a shallow quartz ledge invisible from the cleft’s mouth—a place they call the *Dry-Perch*—and wedge the silk-wrapped figure cleanly into a narrow notch of freshly split stone, lashing them securely to the rock with braided copper wire, face turned outward above the bright, flat sea of tin clouds.

Nothing decays up there. Within a season, the dry stratospheric wind and the bruised indigo sky draw out the last trace of moisture, leaving the body light as seasoned cedar, clean, intact, and dusted faintly with white quartz-talc. The copper lashing never rusts. When the night gale rises and screams soundlessly across the blades of the peak, the taut wires hum softly into the black phyllite—one more faint, steady note carried down through the stone to the Bite-Watchers pressing their wooden staves between their teeth three hundred feet below, reminding everyone spinning inside the crack that even the dead keep their tether, stay dry, and hold the mountain together.
