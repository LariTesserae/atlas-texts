The orbital planners were right about the bedrock, and wrong about the valley being empty—though it is easy to see how they missed what was here.

Stand on the western escarpment in the bruised twilight and look down over two miles of leaning evergreen fabric, braided black water, and horizontal sleet, and you will see nothing that looks like a settlement. No straight lines, no cleared fields, no roofs shedding rain, no smoke rising to betray a hearth. Even if a chimney existed, the gale would shred its plume to nothing before it cleared the spruce crowns. The six dull-orange marker lights of the maintenance shelters blink steadily through the driving spray, and between them the valley appears to belong solely to the storm, the shaggy ungulates, and the sudden violence of the anchors.

You only notice the other inhabitants if you climb down off the scree, push into the dripping tangle along an alder bench, and watch a herd of the low-slung beasts when Anchor Three begins its cycle.

The faint, dry hardness runs through the basalt—less a sound than the stone tightening beneath you. Instantly the eight or ten ungulates fold without a noise into the deep moss-hollows between the spruce roots. And ten paces downwind of them, half-buried in a bolster of wet sphagnum, three shapes that looked a moment ago like lichen-draped boulders fold down at the exact same instant.

They do not have hooves. As the half-second stretches taut before the blast, one of them shifts a broad, raw-knuckled hand flat against the wet shale for purchase, the fingers splayed wide, the skin darkened with cold and slick with rendered tallow. Above them, the horizontal shear hits—a white wall of sleet ripping through the willow tops, stripping loose a storm of snapped twigs and wet lichen. The three shapes stay pressed into the peat with their elbows tucked to their ribs, their matted hide cloaks flattened to their backs, letting the canopy bend nearly to their spines and spring violently upright again.

Before the last dislodged beetles have finished pattering down through the needles, they are already rising—low, heavy-shouldered, moving with the same economy as the beasts beside them, gathering the freshly snapped resin-knots from the moss before the wind-eddies can roll them into the creek.

There are perhaps thirty of them across the whole length of the basin, though they are never all in one place, and a decade can pass without everyone standing under the same roof. Some drifted in along the cold coast a generation or two ago, when the anchors were first sunk into the raw rock and the old coastal timber-camps further south were bought out and cleared; a few were people who stepped off the ledgers of the orbital economy and never stepped back on; most of them now were simply born beneath the roar, and know no world where the air does not lean against your chest.

The valley has shaped their bodies the way it shapes the spruces. They are rarely tall, and even the leanest among them carry a dense, low-centered musculature through the thighs, hips, and upper back—the build of people who spend every waking hour outdoors leaning forty degrees into moving mass. They never stand straight on an exposed bench. Their knees stay slightly unlocked, weight balanced over the balls of their feet, ready to drop or pivot when a shifting gust tries to peel them off the scree.

Their outer garments look grown from the valley floor rather than sewn: heavy, hooded cloaks and wrap-leggings made from the rain-matted pelts of the shaggy ungulates, worn fur-out so the sleet runs down the guard-hairs rather than soaking the leather, patched along the shoulders and knees with oiled seal-hide brought up from the valley mouth, and streaked intentionally with cold peat and olive lichen to break their outline against the brush. Beneath the hides are dense layers of felted wool and smoked chamois, smelling fiercely of resin, tallow, and woodsmoke.

Their faces, what little shows inside the deep fur-lined cowls, are weathered to a raw, wind-burned bronze, glistening around the cheekbones and nostrils with fish-oil or tallow rubbed thick into the pores so the near-freezing spray will not split the skin open.

And like the ungulates, they have stopped expecting anything useful from their ears.

Most of the older ones are profoundly deaf, their eardrums scarred early by pressure-cracks before they learned the discipline of keeping their jaws slack when an anchor dumps its load. Even the young, whose hearing still works inside a quiet room, plug their ear canals with tufts of lanolin-heavy wool kneaded with soft spruce gum before they crawl out into the weather. Out in the basin, hearing is not just useless; it is an exhaustion, an endless bruising against the skull.

So the work of listening has migrated downward—into the arches of their feet, the marrow of their shins, the loose hinge of their jaws, and the skin of their bare hands.

Look at their boots and you understand how they survive. An outsider would wear thick, rigid, heavily insulated soles against the freezing basalt and razor-sharp shale. The people of the basin wear soft, multi-layered moccasins of tough ungulate neck-hide soaked in birch-tar and studded along the rim with tiny chips of slate or bone for traction on wet cobbles. The leather beneath the arch is kept deliberately thin, lined only with a single strip of compressed felt. Their feet are always cold—blue-white around the toes when they peel the boots off at night—but through that thin, tarred sole, the bedrock speaks clearly.

They feel the difference between the grinding roll of cobbles shifting in a flooded creek bed and the dry, sharp tremor of a shale overhang beginning to slump a hundred yards away. Most critically, they feel the pre-discharge tick of an anchor a fraction of a second before the water in the moss even shivers. A quarter-second is the margin between dropping cleanly into a root-hollow and taking a wind-driven spruce limb across the ribs.

When they travel together—usually in pairs or threes—they do not waste breath trying to shout into the tearing roar. They link themselves at the waist with a short length of braided sinew and salvaged hemp, slack enough for easy footing on the scree, taut enough that if an eddy or an anchor's back-draft catches one off-balance, the other drops flat and becomes a deadweight.

Through that line, and through direct touch, they carry on a brisk, unspoken conversation.

Two sharp tugs at the waist: *hold, ground is moving.* A knuckle rapped twice against a companion's forearm: *tick coming—east anchor.* A palm laid flat between the shoulder blades and pushed gently left: *the braided creek has cut a new bed ahead, follow the higher root-shelf.* When they meet another pair along a wet alder bench in the gloom, they do not wave from a distance; they close within arm's length, grip each other by the bare wrists inside the sleeve-cuffs, and stand for a moment with their foreheads nearly touching, reading each other's faces and trading small, crisp hand-signs pressed directly against each other's palms so the dark and the sleet cannot swallow them.

Even their hunting is built around silence and borrowed force. Bows are useless here; a crosswind that snaps sleet sideways will toss an arrow into the ferns ten feet from the string. Instead, they carry short, heavy spears of fire-hardened hemlock tipped with freshly cleaved black shale, and weighted throwing-clubs wrapped in rawhide. They do not stalk the ungulates from upwind or downwind—the wind-eddies in the valley twist in three directions at once—they simply move *with* the herds, matching their rhythm, dropping into the moss beside them when the basalt ticks. A hunter may spend half an hour crawling through the wet ferns twenty feet from a browsing cluster, indistinguishable in scent and posture, waiting for the moment an anchor cycles nearby. In the violent, foaming chaos of the back-draft, as the canopy springs back and the beasts scramble up in a shower of broken twigs, the strike is over in two heartbeats, and the rest of the herd barely scatters before the roar swallows the commotion whole.

From the silent coastal birds, they read the wind's gaps. When a hundred dark wings burst together out from beneath an overhanging shale lip like thrown stones, skimming low across a slumped mud-bank, a traveler on the creek's edge does not hesitate—that ragged, unpredictable two-second lull between colliding gusts is the exact window to leap a waist-deep torrent before the next wall of air slams down the trough.

And when the sleet turns solid and the winter gloom thickens into true night, they vanish beneath the forest itself.

They do not build cabins. Anything raised upright in this valley is a sail waiting to be peeled apart. Instead, their dwellings—four or five small, scattered hollows tucked along the bends of the western escarpment—are woven directly into the valley's own layered, bouncing mat.

Find a place where two rotten hemlocks have fallen across a freshly cleaved basalt ledge, catching in the shoulders of their living neighbors and forming a low, wedged vault beneath twenty feet of interlocked timber, shelf-fungi, and creeping moss. Beneath that natural springy roof, the valley's people clear away the angular scree, stack flat slabs of dark shale to brace the rear wall, and close the open front with a thick, curved bulkhead woven from green, whip-flexible dwarf alder and interlaced spruce roots. They chink the weave tight with sour wet peat, brace it with salvaged lengths of rock-bolted wire, and plaster the outer face with live sphagnum moss and creeping liverwort. Within a single wet season, the forest heals straight over the wall. From five paces away, a house looks like nothing more than a mossy root-mound wedged against a cliff.

You enter on hands and knees through a dog-legged crawl-tunnel hung with two heavy, overlapping curtains of oiled hide that break the wind's pressure before it can reach the room.

Inside, the transformation is startling. The deafening, canvas-ripping roar of the basin drops to a deep, muffled surf—a heavy, rhythmic thrum you feel more in the walls than in the air. The ceiling is barely high enough for a grown person to kneel upright, braced with dark, smoke-polished hemlock knees that flex slightly and sigh whenever a heavy gust flattens the canopy overhead. The floor is paved with fitted slabs of dark basalt covered in thick, overlapping layers of dried sphagnum and cured ungulate hides.

There is no open hearth and no chimney. A vertical flue would draw the gale straight down into the room and choke everyone inside in seconds. Instead, nestled against the back bedrock sits a low, squat stove cobbled from mortared shale and flat iron-rich cobbles, fed with tiny, fiercely hot billets of resin-saturated hemlock heartwood and pressed bricks of dried peat. Its exhaust runs out through a narrow stone-lined trench buried horizontally beneath the moss-mat for thirty or forty feet down-slope, letting the smoke cool, condense, and bleed invisibly through a dozen root-holes. Outside in the storm, the only sign of a fire burning inside a hillside is a wandering vein of slightly brighter green lichen where the sleet melts a moment faster than it should.

In the center of the hollow, two or three shallow saucers chipped from black shale hold rendered otter-fat and twisted wicks of bog-cotton, burning with small, steady, smoky orange flames that never flicker. The air inside is intensely warm, close, and thick with the scent of smoked fish, drying wool, pine pitch, wet stone, and human bodies.

Here, with the wool plugs pulled from their ears and the heavy cloaks hung from root-pegs to drip into stone catch-basins, they speak.

Their voices sound strange to an outsider—pitched unusually low, husky, full of resonant hums and long, open vowels formed deep in the chest rather than the mouth. Because half the elders cannot hear a consonant at all, they sit shoulder to shoulder on the hide-draped benches when they talk, or lay a palm lightly against the speaker's collarbone or knee. You do not merely hear a story in the under-mat; you feel it buzzing through the shared timber and the bone of your own ribs. When they laugh, it is a silent, shuddering heave of the chest that passes down the row like a small, warm tremor.

Every household has its own quiet clutter, none of it wasted. Bundles of dried stonefly cakes and smoked dark creek-fish hang from the ceiling roots beside coils of sinew, bundles of peeled willow-bark, and leather pouches packed tight with dry tinder-fungus. Knives and scrapers of razor-edged black shale sit wrapped in oiled chamois alongside a few treasured pieces of metal—a snapped length of braided steel cable, a ground-down alloy bolt, a battered tin cup—scavenged carefully from the outside world.

And always, near the doorway, sits a flat, uncovered slab of the raw bedrock itself, kept swept clean of moss and pelts.

That bare square of stone is their window to the sky.

No one in the valley has ever seen a star clearly, nor watched an orbital freighter burn across the dark above the stacked winter storms. To them, the sky is only an iron-dark ceiling that moves too fast to look at. Yet they know the traffic of the orbital lanes with an intimacy no ground-control clerk could quite understand, because every ship that accelerates or brakes a thousand miles overhead pays its momentum directly into the rock beneath their knees.

They do not think of the hexagonal bleed-anchors as machines, nor as gods. They call them *the weights*, or simply *the six*—and they live inside the rhythm of the weights the way coastal people once lived inside the rhythm of the moon and the tides.

On a long, dark evening inside a hollow, you will often find one of the older women—someone whose knees are too stiff now for hauling wet timber across the scree—sitting cross-legged beside the tallow lamp, her bare heels resting firmly on the uncovered square of basalt, her eyes half-closed, a bundle of notched alder sticks laid across her lap.

A sharp, tight triplet of ticks runs through her soles—barely enough to stir the dust in the shale cracks, followed half a second later by a brief, whipping rattle in the dwarf-alder roof. She does not even open her eyes; her thumb moves one notch along a thin stick. Fast couriers, light-hulled and hurried, skipping down the inner lanes in quick succession.

An hour later, the stone goes hard with a different character—a deep, slow, sickening heave that lifts the stomach and makes the tallow flames dip low as the air outside sucks backward into a sudden void. Far across the basin, shale cliffs pop and rattle; water sloshes high out of the bog-pools. She opens her eyes and lays two thick pebbles beside the stove. A heavy bulk-hauler braking down from the outer dark, dragging ore or ice across the unseen sky, so massive that its ghost bends two miles of evergreens flat to the moss.

They do not know the names of the ports, the companies, or the worlds the ships are bound for. They know them by their *weight*, their *handedness*—which bank the bog-water piles against tells them whether the ship was climbing east or braking west—and their *temper*. There is the steady, predictable thrum of the mid-week supply convoys that shakes down a reliable harvest of tender bark for the ungulates and fat grubs for the birds. There are the jagged, erratic kicks of impatient tugs jockeying around an orbital dock.

And once every few years, there comes the thing they dread more than any gale: a stillness.

A strike on the high docks, a lane-reroute, a distant dispute among governments they have never heard of—whatever the cause, the anchors simply stop cycling. For two days, three days, sometimes a week, only the natural storm howls down the basin. At first it seems a mercy. Then the unease sets in. Without the anchors' sudden shears shaking deadwood and insects from the high canopy, the thick-bodied fish grow lean in the eddies, and the ungulates wander farther up the bare scree, nervous and thin. Inside the hollows, the bare basalt slab feels horribly dead beneath the heels—cold, mute, and heavy, as though the ocean had pulled back from a harbor and forgotten to return. People sleep poorly in the quiet. When the first deep, chest-flattening thump finally returns and shakes the water out of the moss, the households let out a collective breath, pressing their palms to the trembling stone like sailors feeling a deck come alive beneath them again.

Beyond reading the weights, they have learned to put them to work.

No one approaches the flush-set alloy rings while the lanes are busy—inside fifty yards, a heavy transfer can snap a femur with a fistful of gravel or slam a person breathless against a spruce trunk. Yet even there, the people know the exact margins of the physics.

Immediately after a massive discharge, while the anchor's wake is still spinning itself apart against the far ridge, two or three figures emerge quickly from the root-tangles around the fifty-yard line. They are foraging the shear. The anchor does their logging and quarrying in half a second: high up in the hemlocks, brittle dead branches saturated with pitch snap clean and rain down into the ferns; along the newly split shale faces across the creek, fresh, paper-thin flakes of black stone lie scattered in the slush, sharper than any traded steel; in the flooded sedge beside the sloshed bog-pools, stunned dark fish flop briefly in the tangled moss before the water drains back into the channel.

Near the outer skirt of Anchor Two's reach, where the momentum-dump loses its lethal punch and becomes merely a violent, rhythmic shove, they have built quiet, nearly invisible engines out of bent wood and sinew. Long, springy saplings of green spruce, stripped of needles and wedged into basalt cracks, carry netted bundles of freshly scraped hides or thick strips of salted fish at their tips; every time a transfer passes overhead, the saplings whip furiously back and forth through the displaced air, flinging the water out of the pelts faster than any hearth could dry them. In a narrow vertical fissure in the shale directly opposite Anchor Five—a crack that opens half an inch and snaps violently shut with every heavy seismic thump—they wedge leather sacks packed with tough coastal kelp-stalks and bog-roots, letting the mountain itself pound them soft enough to weave or chew.

And on the rare afternoons when a scheduled lane-pause clears the sky for an hour, you may even see a child darting out onto the dark, matte-slate crown of one of the hexagonal vaults itself—not out of irreverence, but out of sheer wonder. In a valley where nothing is ever dry, where water clings to every needle, every stone, every thread of wool, the seamless alloy shell of the anchor is the only place where sleet skates off without leaving a damp skin behind. A child will press a bare cheek or palm against the cold, slick, utterly dry surface for ten seconds, marveling at the strange refusal of water to touch it, before an elder tugs sharply on their waist-line and pulls them back into the dripping ferns.

Growing up in the basin means learning the stone before you learn words.

A toddler's earliest game is played on the bare basalt square beside the tallow lamp. An elder sits behind the child, wrapping broad, scarred hands over the child's small ones, pressing both pairs of palms flat against the cold floor. They wait in the warm, smoky dimness. The instant the faint hardness ticks through the rock—before the hollow's alder roof even begins to flex—the elder pulls the child's shoulders gently down into the pile of cured pelts, curling over them, counting the beat against their ribs until the dull concussion rolls past outside and the hemlock knees creak back into place. Then they sit up, grinning in the orange light, rubbing their foreheads together.

By the time a child has seven winters behind them, they can feel an anchor cycling two miles down the valley floor without taking their boots off. By twelve, they can tell from the angle of a sloshed puddle whether a heavy barge is heading up-well or down-well, and they know how to lace a dwarf-alder roof so it bends with the gale instead of fighting it.

When the very old ones reach the end of their strength—when their joints lock too hard in the wet cold to drop cleanly at the tick—they do not leave the valley. Nobody leaves. When a death comes in one of the hollows, the household waits for a heavy storm to strip an old, rotten hemlock loose from the scree-bed and wedge it deep into its neighbors' shoulders. Beneath that newly fallen trunk, in a bed of thick sphagnum and fragrant crushed spruce needles, they lay the body wrapped in oiled ungulate hide, weighted down with angular dark shale so the creeks cannot shift it. Within a single wet season, liverworts, shelf-fungi, and creeping moss fold straight over the grave, knitting it seamlessly into the living, springy mat that shelters the valley floor. The dead quite literally become part of the roof that holds the weather off the living.

Only once or twice a year does the outside world come down through the clouds to remind them how thin the margin of their secrecy is.

They always know it is coming half a day in advance. The basalt tells them. Every anchor along the trough goes abruptly silent at once—not the ragged, uneasy quiet of an orbital delay, but a clean, crisp, total shutoff right on the hour, preceded by a strange, light flutter of calibration pulses that tick softly like dry rain inside the stone.

Word passes down the miles of braided creeks in an hour—runners moving low and fast along the alder benches, tapping out the sign at each crawl-tunnel's hide curtain. Inside every hollow, the peat-stoves are damped to cold ash so not even a stray thread of warmth rises through the mossy vents. The drying-whips near Anchor Two are unhooked and sunk in a dark root-channel. The live-moss flaps are pulled tight across the crawlways.

Then, from the far mouth of the valley, a sound arrives that does not belong to the wind: a hard, mechanical thrumming that fights the gale instead of bending with it. A stub-winged maintenance skimmer drops out of the iron-dark cloud ceiling, its floodlights cutting pale cones through the horizontal sleet, settling clumsily onto the wet gravel flat below one of the riveted composite shelters.

From the dense, interlocked crowns of the hemlocks a hundred yards up the slope, or from the fern-choked scree along the creek banks, motionless shapes draped in wet pelts and tangled olive lichen watch without blinking.

Two or three figures climb down from the skimmer into the spray. They wear bulky, bright-yellow heated shells and clear visors smeared with rain, their heavy magnetized boots clanking awkwardly against the angular basalt. They trudge up the slope toward the hexagonal vault, leaning too stiffly against the crosswind, staggering whenever a sudden eddy twists up the creek bed. They wave glowing diagnostic wands over the dry slate ring, shout uselessly into throat-mics inside their helmets, and flinch visibly when a rotten spruce bough snaps fifty yards away.

To the watchers hidden in the dripping needles, the visitors look bewilderingly fragile—top-heavy, blind, sealed inside bright shells that keep out the cold at the cost of shutting out everything the valley is trying to tell them. They stand directly over the basalt without feeling a single tremor in it. They shine their white beams straight across a mossy boulder-jam that houses five breathing people and a cold stove, and see only raw stone and wet ferns.

Once, three winters back, a young technician slipped on a freshly shattered sheet of black ice above a swollen creek while the skimmer's engines were idling loud enough to cover the splash. The current rolled him twenty yards down a braided channel and pinned him breathless against a jam of angular cobbles, his yellow visor cracked, his radio dead. His partner never saw him go down in the driving sleet. When the tech came back to himself five minutes later, shivering violently and spitting cold iron-rich mud, he was lying high and dry on the riveted composite doorstep of the maintenance shelter, right beneath the blinking orange marker light. Nobody was in sight. Only the sleet snapping sideways, a tangled tuft of wet, sour-smelling dark fur caught on the latch of his harness, and the faint, impossible impression of broad hands lifting him easily through the dark. He told the medical officer up on the station that the wind must have thrown him clear of the bank, and the medical officer wrote down *hypothermic confusion*.

Usually, though, the visits pass without touch. The technicians seal the shelter, check the bolts on the heavy steel guy-cables, climb back into their roaring craft, and claw back up into the iron-dark overcast.

An hour later, the first crisp calibration tick runs through the stone. A minute after that, the sky's weight drops back onto the basin with a familiar, chest-flattening thump, and the anchors resume their work.

Only then do the valley's inhabitants emerge from the under-mat, moving quickly down to the shelter's lee-side to comb the wet scree before the next torrent shifts the gravel. They never touch the shelter's sealed door, and never damage the blinking amber beacon—those lights belong to the ledger that keeps the valley officially empty. They take only what the storm and the careless visitors leave behind: a sheared length of braided steel wire, a dropped copper washer, a cracked square of bright-orange polymer that a child will tuck beside the tallow lamp inside the hollow like a piece of captured sun.

Then they melt back into the dripping spruce-fabric, linking their waist-lines, pressing their thin-soled boots to the sharp, wet stone.

Far across the two-mile width of the valley floor, Anchor Six cycles. A pale surge of mist leaps above the leaning treetops, followed seconds later by a dull, rolling concussion that joins the endless canvas-ripping roar of the wind. Near the valley mouth, static snaps blue-white across the upper canopy, briefly lighting the jagged black ridges and the rain falling at three angles at once.

And down beneath the bouncing mat of moss and tangled roots, unseen from any ridge and uncounted in any orbit, a bare heel rests calmly on the dark basalt, feeling the weight of a ship braking a thousand miles above the storm—counting the beat, waiting the half-second, utterly at home in the motion.
