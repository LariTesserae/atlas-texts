You rarely see them until you are already close enough to smell the damp wool and cold pine-resin on their sleeves.

In the thirty-pace gloom of a passing cloud-tide, they emerge from the wet alleys and iron catwalks like parts of the masonry that have decided to walk. They wear heavy, layered coats of felted peat-gray, oiled slate, and iron-mordanted black—garments stiffened with tree-sap against the perpetual mist until the cuffs and hems stand away from the body like wet bark. Only across the shoulders, or stitched along the peaked rims of their hoods, do they carry a narrow stripe of boiled liverwort-crimson: a single dull ember of color so a cable-minder or a cart-pusher can pick a neighbor out of the vapor before two shoulders collide on a slippery slate stair.

They are built for the thinness of the air, and the altitude has spent generations carving them to its own measure. Most are broad-ribbed and deep through the chest, with rounded, heavy shoulders and a slow, unhurried carriage that makes even a twenty-year-old move with the settled patience of an elder. Beneath the pewter ceiling, their skin stays pale as pared root-cellar turnip, except across the cheekbones, knuckles, and earlobes, where thick, dark, oxygen-hungry blood rises close to the surface in a permanent plum-copper flush. Their eyes are wide-pupiled and strikingly calm, slow to blink, tuned to the dimness of four-watt amber filaments and the hair-thin meniscus inside a green glass sight-tube.

Nobody hurries here. At three miles above the sea, haste is an affectation that punishes itself in ten paces. Their walk is a flat-footed, economical roll—knees slightly bent, weight kept low, thick boots soled with vulcanized fern-sap rubber gripping the slick stone without a sound. Even their breathing has a different architecture than a lowlander’s: deep, measured draws through the nose that barely stir the woolen scarves tucked beneath their chins, held a fraction longer than you expect before exhaling in small, tight plumes that dissolve immediately into the hanging fog.

Before the copper fog-harps on the northern ridge begin to creak, the people already know the tide is coming.

They carry the barometer inside their own skulls. A dull, familiar fullness gathers behind the cheekbones; old breaks in wrist-bones and finger-joints begin to ache with a mild, informative throb; the skin along the forearms tightens a hair. Across two miles of crowded terraces, you can watch an entire alley of people pause in unison three minutes before the first pale ribbon clears the stone lip. A woman repairing a lead gutter sets down her soldering iron and tilts her chin subtly northward. Two men guiding a low iron ore-cart along the flush cobble rails glance at one another, nod once, and nudge the brake-lever home. Up on a fourth-story sill, an old man pulls his waxed-canvas shutter half-to without once looking at the sky.

They live by no sun—only that cold, occasional brass smear along the eastern underside of the clouds—and scarcely by the clock. Their hours are measured in cloud-tides: *the Rise*, when pressure dips and the bladders swell; *the Soak*, forty minutes of hushed blindness while the bowl drinks; *the Draw*, when the cistern floats clunk beneath the squares and the fog drains south toward the abyss; and *the Thin*, that brief, crisp interval of pewter clarity when you can look across the amphitheater and see the green lamps blinking on the opposite slope.

---

Thin air starves a shout, and the thick pelt of velvet moss swallows whatever escapes. A man yelling across a courtyard here sounds muffled and foolish, as if he were calling into a pile of wet blankets, and the effort leaves him dizzy and gasping against the rail.

Because of this, they are a soft-voiced, close-speaking people. When two neighbors stop to talk on a wet staircase, they step inside one another’s elbow-reach, leaning in until the rims of their hoods nearly brush. Their spoken tongue is pitched low in the chest, built of warm, breath-thrifty murmurs, soft hums, and clipped consonants that waste no air. Half of what passes between them needs no breath at all: a tilt of the chin, a slow turn of the open palm, the measured swing of an amber hand-lamp through the vapor.

When they need to speak across distance, they use the bones of the town.

Every person in the bowl grows up fluent in the pulse of iron. Carry a brass spanner, a knuckle-ring of heavy copper, or simply the hard edge of a boot-heel, and the bundled condensate pipes and overhead catwalks become a single vast nervous system. Three crisp *tinks* tapped against a basement riser warn the third-floor tenements that steam is being bled into the north-wall heating loops. A slow, dragging double-knock along an iron viaduct tells the haulage-station on the next stone knuckle that a leather pulley is slipping wet. At dusk, when the factories shift down to their night-draw, you can press your ear to almost any retaining-wall conduit and hear whole quiet conversations rippling up and down the tiered slopes—a mother tapping out a soft, three-beat trill to call a child up from the wet cobbles; a sluice-keeper signaling a friend three terraces down that the mineral broth is hot.

They listen with their feet as much as their ears. Those thick sap-rubber soles are not merely for grip; they are tuned to the low, subterranean thrum of the buried turbines and the fifteen-foot flywheels turning in their oiled stone pits. A press-hand walking home through a blind fog knows which manufactory he is passing simply by the cadence ticking up through his heels, and if a flywheel’s beat stutters by a tenth of a second, half the people on the alley above will lift their heads from their bowls of soup and frown at the floorboards.

---

Their trades do not divide cleanly into what is built and what is grown. In the bowl, iron and leaf have knitted together so tightly that a mason must know botany and a gardener must carry a rivet-hammer.

Up along the smooth northern lip—beyond the last crowded roofs, where the ancient gneiss curves bare and rounded beneath the leaden sky—work the ridge-minders. They are the quietest and heaviest-dressed of the city’s people, wrapped in matted moss-felt and greased pelts, smelling strongly of cold tallow and wet basalt. They walk the long rows of tilted fog-harps with oil-cans and curved bone needles, easing the iron pivots and mending the taut grids of oxidized copper mesh and coarse, waxed hair. They still call the strands *horsehair*, out of some half-forgotten habit carried up from an older world, though none of them has ever seen a horse; the coarse, dark bristles are gathered each thaw-less season from the sheltered hollows where the shaggy, splayed-hoofed browsers rub their winter coats against the gneiss. When a ridge-minder steps out of the mist with a basket of collected tufts, a browser standing ten yards off in the cushion-moss will barely pause its chewing, recognizing in the slow, heavy-shouldered figure another creature shaped by the same cold pressure.

Down in the crowded tiers of the amphitheater, hanging in wide leather harnesses from the iron viaducts and brick piers, work the sap-tappers and moss-dressers. Their forearms are corded with quiet strength, their palms tough as cured hide, their fingernails permanently rimmed with an iridescent green-black sheen that smells of crushed pine needles and iron filings. They move among the curtains of bladder-ferns with a surgeon’s gentleness. With curved copper knives they pare back the velvety black moss where its soaked weight threatens to bow a slate roof, packing the trimmings into hanging baskets to be pressed into acoustic felt, wall-insulation, or dark peat-briquettes. Beneath the gnarled, tarnished-jade trees, they wedge shallow ceramic trays among the roots, scraping the thick, mineral-heavy sap into stoppered glass carboys before it can crust over.

Nothing is killed casually; nothing is left untended. When a bricklayer repairs a retaining wall, he leaves narrow weep-slots and lime-rich pockets deliberately open behind the stones. If a sap-tapper lifts a cellar grate and spies one of the pale, six-toed wall-creatures flattened motionless against the wet mortar, its slate-gray velvet blending seamlessly into the gneiss, he freezes where he stands and holds his breath until it finishes flicking its dark tongue across the salt-bloom and slips backward into the ferns. They call them *mortar-keepers*, and leave small pinches of coarse boiler-salt on damp sills to encourage them. A house where a mortar-keeper sleeps is a house whose walls drain clean and whose lime will not rot in the frost.

Down on the broad, polished rock pans of the lower bowl, the factory hands work in warm, dim halls beneath saw-toothed roofs of ribbed glass. There is none of the roaring fury of lowland industry here. The air is thick with damp felt, warm machine oil, and the clean, astringent tang of distilling sap. Men and women in heavy gray flannel shirtsleeves move slowly between the squat, felt-insulated retorts and the tall glass-and-copper condensers, reading pressure gauges by green glass lamps and turning counterweighted brass wheels with deliberate, two-handed grace. To check a bearing or a deep stone turbine shaft, a mechanic does not shout over it; he presses one end of a seasoned ash listening-rod against the cast-iron housing and clamps the other end lightly between his back teeth, closing his eyes to hear the smooth, oily song of the machinery ringing straight through the bone of his jaw.

What do they make down in those hushed halls? Everything the bowl needs to keep holding itself together: vulcanized gaskets and waterproof varnishes boiled down from the iron-rich sap; fine copper wire drawn from the dark veins of the eastern ore-yards; delicate blown-glass sight-tubes and filament bulbs; dense peat-bricks; pure, distilled mineral tonics and bitter crystalline salts. Every workshop feeds the next along narrow-gauge rails and silent overhead cables, an endless, slow-turning wheel of craft where waste is treated almost as a superstition.

---

Step out of a dripping alley, duck beneath a low lintel cushioned in moss, and push through the double felt-curtains of a four-story tenement, and the world turns suddenly small, close, and deeply warm.

Ceilings are low so heat cannot drift away. The interior walls are bare dressed basalt or dark brick, warmed from within by thick iron return-pipes that carry spent factory steam through every residential tier before the water cools enough to drop into the lower cisterns. Around these warm pipes, people build deep, curtained sleeping-niches lined with woven fern-matting and quilts stuffed with dried bladder-down. Even indoors, a faint crust of crimson liverwort or bright chartreuse lichen sometimes blooms along a weeping pipe-joint near the floor; households rarely scrape it all away, liking the tiny slash of living brightness against the soot-dark brick.

Light inside is kept deliberately low—a single hooded amber bulb over a scarred slate table, a small oil-lamp burning refined resin on the sill—because bright glare aches in eyes bred for the pewter cloud-ceiling. In that warm, honeyed dimness, families gather after a shift to eat dark, dense fare raised in the glass-louvered rooftop sheds and the steam-warmed cellar beds: pale, knobby tubers steamed over iron kettles; crisp, blanched cellar-shoots; heavy, nutty cakes baked from dried moss-flour and crushed fern-seed; and bowls of rich, salty broth thickened with root-starch and sharpened with a few drops of aged, pine-bright sap-vinegar. Afterward come thick stoneware mugs of hot roasted-lichen infusion, held in both hands so the heat sinks slowly into stiff, cold-numbed fingers while the rain-like *tock-tock-tock* of condensate drums steadily against the copper eaves outside.

Children raised in the bowl are startlingly composed. They learn before they can properly read letters how to gauge the thickness of a cloud-tide by the smell of the air, how to climb a wet iron ladder three stories up without letting their breath go ragged, and how to read the tiny hairlines on a cistern gauge. Their games are quiet, close-watched things: setting flat slivers of slate on the iron gratings above the flywheel pits to watch them shiver in slow circles; floating paper boats made from oil-soaked ledger sheets down the stone water-flutes during the Draw; or crouching in the warm microclimate beside an exposed steam-pipe, betting pebbles on how many seconds it will take a pale wall-creature to flick its tongue across a salt-crust.

Because speed counts for nothing here and patience counts for everything, the old do not vanish to the margins of life. When a man’s lungs grow too stiff for the ridge-paths, or a woman’s knees lock up against the wet stairs, they move inward and downward to the warm stone heart of the city. Wrapped in thick wool beside a humming transformer-house or tucked into a dry alcove beside a bank of glass sight-tubes, an eighty-year-old can still sit for six quiet hours with one spotted hand resting on a brass sluice-wheel, feeling the subtle shudder of the bedrock and metering out the bowl’s borrowed water by the millimeter. They are prized for their joints as much as their memory; a grandmother with bad hips can often tell you five minutes earlier than any brass instrument whether the next cloud-bank off the northern lip will come in heavy with frost or warm with deep-basin moisture.

---

Because the bowl holds everything, it holds its dead as well.

There is no barren graveyard here, and nothing so wasteful as burning good fuel to turn a body to smoke. When someone’s breath finally thins out—what they call *losing the tide*—their neighbors wash them in warm, distilled condensate, dress them in unbleached peat-cloth stitched with no metal clasps, and carry them quietly up the wet slate stairs during a passing cloud-bank so the fog hides the procession from the open sky.

High along the sheltered northern flank, tucked beneath the oldest groves of gnarled, tarnished-jade trees, lie the tiered moss-vaults: shallow, loosely mortared chambers built directly into the ancient gneiss scree. The body is laid there on a bed of damp peat and covered in thick bolsters of living black moss. Within a handful of seasons, the fine roots of the trees thread through the loose stones, drawing the iron, lime, and salt back up into the waxy leaves and the amber stem-bladders. No one finds this grim. When a sap-tapper drinks a spoonful of mineral tonic on a freezing morning, or smells cold pine and iron filings rising from a warm brick flue, there is a quiet, unsentimental comfort in knowing that the grandfathers and grandmothers are still turning the wheels, still insulating the roofs, still helping the bowl drink.

And yet, for all their devotion to keeping, cycling, and holding close, almost every person in the city makes the slow walk down to the southern spillway from time to time.

Sometimes it is after a death, when the body has been tucked into the northern roots and the mourners have nothing left to carry but their own quiet grief. Sometimes it is simply during a long, still interval of the Thin, when the perpetual dimness presses a little too closely against the ribs and a worker feels the strange, nameless urge to see beyond thirty paces.

They walk down past the last packed tenements, past the humming brick transformer-houses wrapped in dark ivy, and out onto that bare, smoothed stone apron where the ancient rock curves gently downward into nothing. They lean their forearms on the rusted, lichen-bearded iron railings, pull their stiff collars up to their noses, and look out across the endless, bruised-gray sea of lower clouds lying motionless a mile below the town’s lowest gutter.

At their feet, the city’s spent water—water that has crossed the copper harps, slid down the waxy leaves, turned three buried turbines, cooled a glass condenser, and washed a cellar floor—finally lets go, spilling off the curved lip in thin silver threads that fray into mist long before they reach the cloud-floor below.

If you ask them what lies beneath that lower sea, you will get half a dozen quiet, contradictory answers, offered with a mild shrug that says it scarcely matters. The old sluice-keepers say the lowlands are a drowned furnace where the air sits thick and heavy as warm grease, and a bare, unshaded sun burns skin raw in an hour. Some of the glassblowers believe the world sank away long ago and left only this one smooth knuckle of ancient stone floating between two skies. A few of the younger moss-dressers, watching that lone, slate-black bird plane silently across the basin on stiff wings every few days, wonder if there are other bowls on other ridges far beyond the northern drift—other quiet corals of brick and iron catching the same slow tides.

Mostly, though, they do not speak at the railing at all. If they have come to mourn, they simply lean out over the curved gneiss lip and exhale one long, deliberate breath into the empty air—giving back to the open sky the one thing the moss-vaults cannot keep.

They never linger long. Out on the bare spillway, with no tiered roofs crowding at your back and no felt of damp foliage to hold your sound, the emptiness feels faintly indecent, like stepping outside without a coat. Soon enough, a dull, familiar pressure begins to press behind their cheekbones. Their fingers tighten once against the cold, rough lichen on the iron rail. Far back up the slope, unseen through the gray dimness, the copper fog-harps are already creaking ten degrees into the northern drift.

One by one, they turn their backs on the vast, motionless floor of clouds, tuck their chins into their peat-dark scarves, and begin the slow, flat-footed climb back up the wet slate stairs—returning gratefully to the warm hum of the hidden flywheels, the glow of the hooded amber lamps, and the quiet, breathing shelter of the bowl, just as the next pale wall of cloud spills silently over the rim.
