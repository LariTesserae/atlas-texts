You do not see them at first—not because they are hiding, and not because they are small, but because the room has taught them to look like itself.

Stand long enough near the mouth of the dark passage, letting the warm spray soak through your collar as you count out the six-heartbeat pulse of the whiteouts, and you begin to notice a single, stubborn flaw in the geometry. Two cycles ago, a low, water-polished fin of pale limestone crouched in the lee of the eastern ribs, tapered clean into the gale like every other wind-carved ridge on the floor. Now the squall snaps away in a whip-crack of pressure, the glare slams back across the glistening stone, and the fin is ten paces closer to the central basin.

You blink the salt and mist from your eyes, certain the whiteout played a trick on your depth perception. The air thins; your eardrums tug inward; the room flashes solid white. Six heartbeats later, clarity breaks open again, and the limestone fin has swung smoothly on its axis to meet a shifting cross-draft. For half a second, before a pale calcified shutter slides shut across its tapered prow, you catch the gleam of something impossibly dark and liquid—an eye the size of a plum, watching the racing water.

Once you know the shape to look for, the vault stops being empty.

There are six of them—or seven, if you count the heavy, motionless hump tucked far back in an erosion flute near the central basin, so thick with pale travertine and bearded with emerald moss that you cannot tell at first whether it is still breathing. The others are scattered across the open apron of the floor and the higher ledges, never clustered in the direct teeth of the gusts, always angled into the eddies with a fluid, unconscious precision.

They are built like keels. Seen head-on, facing into the gale, a grown one is shockingly narrow—hardly wider than a man’s forearm—so the tearing cross-drafts part cleanly across its chest without finding purchase. Only when one turns sideways during a brief lull do you see its real length: two meters of lean, compressed muscle from the blunt wedge of its snout to the trailing, rudder-flat tail, carried low on four long, splayed limbs whose broad feet end in both curved limestone-gripping claws and wide, pale suction pads.

Their upper backs, shoulders, and the long ridges of their brows are plated in actual stone. Generations of living in this warm, lime-heavy deluge have given them a dead outer hide that welcomes mineral scale instead of shedding it. Over the years, the spray lays down smooth, porcelain-white crests of travertine along their spines, and the storm itself polishes those living shells into sweeping, aerodynamic curves identical to the stalactite fins overhead. Beneath the pale mineral armor, along their flanks, bellies, and long flexible necks, their true skin shows through: wet, rubbery, dark as river slate, slick with a thin sheen that lets water race off them without pooling.

Strangest of all is the way they breathe. Watch the pleated, pale-gray membranes stretched between the curved cartilaginous hoops along their flanks, and you realize their chests never heave on their own. When a pressure trough drops across the chamber and pulls at your ears, their flanks balloon effortlessly outward in unison, drawing in the hot, condensing fog and trapping beads of fresh moisture directly inside their throats. Two seconds later, when the pressure wall slams back from the far end of the nave, the room itself compresses them flat, forcing spent air out through narrow slits along their necks in tiny, crisp feathers of mist. They do not fight the violent barometer of this place; they have handed their lungs over to it. Every pulse of the storm is a breath.

In the blinding clarity between squalls, they look blind and carved from rock. Heavy, translucent plates of calcified skin seal tightly over their sockets, shutting out the fierce, shadow-eating glare. They move mostly in the six-heartbeat whiteouts, when the noon softens into diffuse luminous fog—gliding from rib to rib in long, low, rolling strides, claws clicking cleanly into the straight ancient floor-grooves for traction. Only then, or when one ducks its narrow head beneath an overhang, do those pale shutters slide back to reveal a smoky-amber inner membrane over huge, dark, unsettlingly gentle eyes.

Unlike the blind amphibians huddled in the slivers of black shadow, these creatures do not look caught in the wrong world. Spend half an hour watching them through the spray, and you see how thoroughly they have learned the peculiar grammar of this engine.

Near the northern wall, a leaner adult—its travertine crest chipped along one shoulder, exposing raw pink-gray hide underneath—works the wet stone above the waterline with patient, finicky grace. Using a single hooked inner claw, it pries half a dozen of the heavy, pebble-smooth snails free of the warm biofilm, tucking them into an elastic pouch beneath its narrow jaw. When the next squall blooms white, it lopes diagonally across the racing shallows toward the southern floor, stopping right at the rim of the scalding downburst fed by the silent hexagonal plate fifty paces away. It does not touch the scalding column directly; instead, it drops the snails into a deep swirl-pothole directly beneath the steaming cascade and clamps down at the edge, turning knife-thin into the wind. It waits out three cycles of fog and clarity. When the fourth squall clears, it reaches delicately into the hot spinning water, plucks out the steamed shells, and flicks the loosened meat from the stone-thick cups with a long, pale, nimble tongue.

Ten paces behind it, an older one—slower, its hips and elbows stiffened by pale collars of flowstone that have crept too far down its joints—uses the same downburst for a harder purpose. It drags itself straight into the blistering fall, arching its narrow back and bracing its claws into the grit. Even under the cavern’s constant roar, you hear sharp, brittle *ticks* and *snaps* like cooling pottery as the sudden heat expands the mineral crust faster than the supple hide beneath it. Pale, curved shards of travertine flake off its shoulders and wash away down the central channels, leaving fresh, tender, pearlescent skin steaming in the warm wind. The creature shudders, lowers its jaw to the wet stone, and lets out a long, low rattle of relief as the next pressure wall squeezes its ribs.

Closer to the middle of the floor, another adult has clamped itself flat onto the sunlit stone to rest, claws locked into one of the ancient grooves, breathing-pleats fluttering in slow rhythm with the gusts. Because its arched travertine spine casts a rare, sharp pool of darkness directly beneath its belly, a dozen of the tiny, ghost-white amphibians have darted out from under the submerged pebbles to crowd beneath its ribcage. They huddle tight in the cool black shade of the giant’s underside, snapping at scraps of emerald moss and drowned spores shaken loose from its flanks. The resting creature pays them no mind, save for an occasional lazy twitch of a toe when the tiny blind mouths nibble dead skin and biofilm from its soft pads.

Over in the eastern shoals, where the three slender, damaged rods jut at slight angles from the bed of smooth pebbles, you find the youngest of them. It is half-grown, restless, almost untouched by limestone crust—a sleek slip of dark slate-blue with bright emerald creases behind its knees where moss-spores have tried and failed to root. It is not yet practiced at reading the cross-drafts; once, a stray eddy catches it broadside and skids it clumsily across a wet apron of stone before it scrambles upright and flattens itself, looking faintly indignant.

It stays close to the three bent rods, circling just outside the narrow, finger-wide rings of bone-dry gravel where the artifacts meet the shoal. Look closely into those thin dry halos—sheltered from the deluge by the same quiet refusal that keeps the rods themselves dry—and you see why the juvenile lingers. Wedged carefully into the bone-dry pebbles at the base of the leaning shafts sit four translucent, parchment-pale egg-casings no larger than plums, kept miraculously dry while warm surges curve cleanly past them a finger’s breadth away. Between squalls, the juvenile cranes its narrow neck over the tips of the rods, its smoky eyes tracking the hair-thin threads of white light that skate silently across the gap. It never touches them; it only brings its snout close enough that the fine sensory filaments along its jaw stir and lift in the invisible hum, tilting its head back and forth in bright, fidgety curiosity.

And then the storm deepens for a single cycle, and you see what the upper half of the chamber is for.

One of the leanest adults—the one with the chipped stone crest—wades down the straight floor-grooves directly into the central basin beneath the floating arc. Four meters overhead, the dark, mirror-slick half-ring hangs motionless in its own blinding noon, spilling white-gold glare from the empty air inside its curve. The creature settles deep into the spinning runoff, spreads the wide, tough membranes folded between its forelimbs and ribs until its narrow body widens into a taut, veined storm-sail, and anchors its hind claws.

The trough drops harder than usual, yanking painfully at your ears. From the central basin, a violent spiraling column of warm water and air tears straight upward just as the fog flashes blind white. Over the bass thrum of the bedrock, you hear one crisp, hollow *thwack*, like wet canvas catching a gale.

Six heartbeats later, the pressure wall erases the mist.

The central basin is empty. Squinting upward into the fierce glare, shielding your eyes with a wet hand, you find it four meters above the floor—stretched lengthwise along the thick, dark, light-drinking outer spine of the floating arc. As its body crossed that finger-wide boundary around the artifact, the film of water on its hide was stripped instantaneously away in a faint halo of mist. Now it lies completely, miraculously dry—the only dry creature in a flooded world—gripping the impervious, mirror-slick slate with the soft suction pads along its belly and toes where claws can find no scratch. Up on the dark back of the sun, sheltered from the spray and out of the worst of the glare spilling beneath the razor-thin inner lip, its pleated flanks pulse shallow and quiet. Its pale shutters are sealed tight. It is basking. After an hour, another will ride an updraft to take its place, nudging it gently off the spine so it glides down in a short, controlled swoop into the southern pools.

Wait long enough, and the wind shifts to the north.

Instantly, the thin curtains of travertine along the upper walls begin their high, glassy singing—a pure, trembling note that slices straight through the bass roar of the water.

Every creature in the vault goes still. The juvenile freezes beside the bent rods; the snail-gatherer lifts its head from the steaming potholes; even the dry, dark silhouette stretched atop the floating arc raises its narrow chin.

When the next pressure wall slams back across the nave, compressing their pleated ribs, they do not simply exhale. They pinch the narrow vents along their throats and let the storm play them.

Out comes a sound you feel in your teeth before you hear it over the gale: a wet, clear, crystal-bell overtone, like fingers circling the rims of five huge wineglasses at once. Each voice carries a slightly different pitch, shaped by the depth of its chest and the thickness of the limestone armor on its back, tuned just a fraction off from the singing travertine curtains so the notes beat against the stone in a slow, shivering pulse that ripples through the shallow water around your boots.

Last to answer is the heavy, moss-bearded hump tucked deep in the erosion flute near the central basin. Out from beneath its thick, porcelain-white carapace comes a single low, gravelly, resonant hum—slower than the others, hollow as an old cask. Its joints have nearly locked; its travertine ridge has grown so broad and sweeping it can no longer drag itself to the scalding downburst to crack its shell free.

Looking past it now, really looking at the deepest lees behind the curved limestone ribs along the far walls, you feel a quiet coldness settle beneath the bathwater warmth of the spray. Several of the smooth, water-polished boulders tucked tight against the bedrock share that same subtle, tapered keel-shape. Their shutters sealed for the last time years or decades ago; the limestone finished claiming them, and the chamber’s perpetual gale polished their backs down into the seamless curves of the floor. Before the north wind dies away, the juvenile slips over to one of those smooth, humped mounds in the lee of a rib, lays its slender jaw flat against the wet stone, and lets a single quiet breath vibrate against the rock—listening, perhaps, to see whether anything inside still rings hollow.

Then the wind swings back east, the travertine curtains fall silent, and you realize the creature with the chipped crest is no longer by the southern floor.

They knew you were here before your eyes stopped watering. In a sealed vault where every eddy, reflection, and organ-pipe hum has been memorized over a lifetime, your upright, clumsy body broke the airflow wrong and bent the nearest curtain’s note half a hair flat.

During the next six-heartbeat whiteout, two of them cross the wide wet apron of stone toward the mouth of your tunnel, moving silently between the gusts. When the whip-crack clears the room into blinding noon, the nearer one is crouched eight paces in front of you, angled edge-on into the cross-draft, its travertine crest gleaming like wet ivory. Its pale shutters stay half-parted despite the glare, the smoky amber membranes narrowing as those huge, dark eyes take in your boots, your drenched coat, and the empty black throat of the passage behind you—tasting the faint, impossible draft of cool air that carries the scent of darkness, silence, and stillness.

A trough pulls at your ears; its pleated flanks swell in exact time with your own startled breath. For one bright, roaring heartbeat, before the white squall blooms again and folds it back into the storm, it lifts its narrow throat and lets the returning pressure wall squeeze out a single, clean, questioning note—pitched just below the singing rock, hanging in the hot spray, waiting to hear what tone you give back.
