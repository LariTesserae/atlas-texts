You could cross three ridges and never know you had walked within ten paces of one.

They do not lift their heads when a loose stone clatters, and they do not draw back into shadows to watch you pass. Watchfulness belongs to countries with hunters and hunted; up here, nothing stalks anything, and nothing flees except from the ground itself. Folded into the lee of a black basalt rib, knees drawn tight against the chest and forearms crossed over the brow, a body shaped by this highland looks like nothing more than another sheared wedge of slate—matte-dark, beveled along its outer edges, dusted with the same fine iron-gray powder that settles on every ledge before the drafts lift it away again.

Only if you stood very still, long enough for your own breath to stop rasping in the thin warm air, might you notice the slowness of the thing. Not stillness—nothing up here is truly still—but a pace tuned to rock rather than pulse. Perhaps a long, flat-backed hand shifts half a finger’s breadth across the warm shelf it rests on, testing a hairline tremor you could not feel through boot-leather. Perhaps the overlapping dark plates along its ribs expand by the width of a blade’s edge, drawing in a single unhurried sip of dry, sulfurous air through narrow pleated clefts tucked beneath the flanks, and then settle flat again for minutes at a time.

They are not made of stone, though a lifetime of dry heat and mineral dust has left their outer surfaces nearly as hard. What covers their backs, shoulders, outer thighs, and the ridges of their brows is a dense, layered horn-skin—overlapping dark laminae, dry as cured slate, crosshatched with pale hairline scratches where volcanic glass has grazed them and failed to bite through. Beneath the joints, where the limbs fold, the plates give way to tightly pleated charcoal-gray hide, dry and tough as wind-parched kelp, never weeping moisture and never cracking in the oven-flat drafts. They have no fat to speak of, and no soft bulk; unfolded, they are lean, long-limbed, heavy-boned figures built for leverage and low gravity, roughly the reach of a tall person yet carrying the quiet, concentrated density of a wedge of iron-slate.

Their faces hold no wet surfaces at all. In a country where silent dust-devils whirl razor-fine mineral flakes across open terraces, open eyes would be shredded in a single gust. Instead, beneath a heavy beveled brow-plate sit two narrow, slanted recesses sealed behind dry, smoky panes—thin translucent scales, faintly clouded and scored like weathered mica, which they shed and renew when the scratching grows too thick. Through those panes they read only the starkest truths of the dark: the slow wheel of sharp stars overhead, the dull-cherry stain breathing up through rift-soot, the sudden white blink of flint-sparks miles away when a cliff face shears loose.

Their truer sight lives along the pale, unplated grooves that seam their cheekbones, their throats, and the wide flat pads of their palms. Those seams drink temperature. To them, the lightless highland is a shifting, vivid relief map of heat—old benches dim and cool underfoot, fresh fractures glaring bright-warm, and the deeper rifts standing upright like shimmering curtains of hot air long before a ledge gives way. And beneath the heat lies vibration. Their jawbones, wrists, and heels are thick, marrow-dry, and resonant; when one presses its chin flat to a shelf of basalt, the whole terrace opens like a room. Through bone and stone they hear what the thin air refuses to carry: the dry metallic *clink-clink* of an overloaded buttress miles across the gulf, the slow scrape of a slab creeping a finger’s width downslope, the brief high *tink* of a new crack zipping across a plateau.

That is why an outsider swears nothing walks here. They do not walk—not with the rhythmic, upright heel-strike that kicks scree loose and prints trails in drifted powder. When they leave the lee of a rib to cross an open bench, they travel low, almost poured along the stone, distributing their weight across splayed, heel-less feet and broad-knuckled hands. Their elbows angle outward; their weight transfers from three points to three points without a jolt, testing each tilted wedge before committing to it. On bare ridge-spines they leave no mark at all. Across drifted black powder they move in a slow, fluid hitch that scuffs barely deeper than a passing draft, erased within minutes by the next creeping tremor. Only when a broad apron of scree suddenly liquefies beneath them do they show speed—a startling, silent burst of wide-flung limbs hooking four edges at once, scrambling sideways off the dry cataract while dust boils up behind them without a sound.

Their hands are the strangest, most recognizable part of them: five-fingered, long-jointed, built for two opposite tasks. Along the backs and outer edges of the fingers run hard, dark shingles that can grip the surgical corner of freshly broken slate without parting; yet the inner palms and fingertips are pale ash-gray, hairless, and stretched tight as drumskin over dense pads. With the same hand they can pry a heavy basalt plate free of a scree-slope and then set its edge down upon a pointed wedge with the delicacy of a hair settling onto glass.

They need no water, and they hunt nothing. Their nourishment is the highland’s own bitter bloom.

Whenever a distant buttress peels away in a silent flash of flint-sparks, or a narrow rift widens and exhales a fresh plume of sulfur-stained smoke, they feel it through the rock and mark the bearing against the stars. Days later, once the newly torn lip has cooled just past glowing and the pale, brittle fur has begun to creep along its warm seam, they come creeping low along the unstable rim. It is the most perilous ground in their country—stone barely born, still ticking as it settles, ready to shear again at any heartbeat. One lies flat a pace back from the trench, jaw pressed to the shelf, listening until the rock holds steady for a span of breaths. Then it slides forward, sweeping the chalk-colored filaments and papery scales off the raw stone with the cupped edge of its palm—carefully, so the filaments fold together instead of bursting into wasted ash—and presses them into its wide, dry-rimmed mouth.

Inside them runs no wet digestion, only a slow, dry heat-work fed by the warm rock they rest upon. They press their bellies and flanks directly against hearth-warm slabs after feeding, letting the mountain’s own steady warmth bake the bitter sulfurous crust inside their guts, drawing from it the salts and minerals that thicken their outer plates and keep their lean frames tireless. What their bodies discard is neither wet nor foul—only faint chalky flakes and dry mineral pellets shed through pores along their flanks, swept away on the next thermal draft and indistinguishable from the bitter ash of the fissures.

When one has fed well and grows sluggish, or when the dust-devils are stalking thick across the benches, it folds into one of the hunched shelters tucked behind a massive black rib. From the outside the shelter looks crude—overlapping slate plates dry-stacked without chinking, barely wide enough for a single crouching body. Inside, it is exact. The unmortared joints are left open on purpose so the constant warm drafts thread through, scouring grit off the floor and carrying faint wisps of sulfurous air to the sleeper’s breathing clefts. And the roof is wedged just tightly enough to shed falling wedges and razor-dust, yet loosely enough to act as a sentinel. A dweller in deep torpor grows nearly as heavy and unresponsive as the basalt beneath it; if the shelf begins to tilt or split while it sleeps, the shifting tension slips the upper plates first, dropping a sharp slate slab squarely onto its armored shoulder—a dry, sudden clack that jolts it awake in time to crawl free before the terrace peels off into the gulf.

Even rarer than the shelters are the knee-high rings of upright shards set out on older, cooler benches, filled with fine drifted grit. To a passerby they look like collapsed cairns or half-buried hearths. In truth, they are the only soft beds in an unfinished world.

Every surface on the highland is broken sharp; nothing here has ever been tumbled smooth by water. Yet there come stretches in a dweller’s long, unmeasured span when sharp stone is fatal. Once in many seasons, its outer horn-plates grow too thick and mineral-stiffened to flex cleanly at the joints, and the old dark laminate begins to split down the spine. Beneath it lies a pale, chalk-white underhide, thin as dry parchment and easily parted by volcanic glass. Weeks before that splitting begins, the dweller chooses an older shelf—rock quiet enough for pale fur to edge its seams—and stands sharp shards upright in a knee-high ring. Then it waits for the thermal drafts to do their work. As silent dust-devils whirl over the terrace, the ring catches their lee and draws down only the finest, lightest mineral powder, filling the basin over days into a warm, ash-soft cushion. Into that dry basin the dweller lowers itself, burying its limbs and torso up to the cheekbones while the old dark plates peel away and the steady hearth-heat beneath bakes the new hide dark and hard. When it rises, the shed husks are brittle as dead slate; the next tremor breaks them into ordinary scree.

They build the same rings for their young. They are deeply solitary beings—there is never enough fragile fissure-fur on a single ridge to sustain a crowd, and two bodies moving close together cloud the delicate vibration-map of the rock—yet once in a great while, when a massive new rift opens and pours out a long ribbon of warm vapor across half a mile of bench, two will converge from neighboring ridges. They do not rush together. For stretches that might be days, they keep a slab’s width apart along the warm lip, testing each other’s steadiness through light, rhythmic knuckle-taps sent through the continuous stone, until at last they fold together in the lee of a rib while the star-veiled wind hisses past.

Long afterward, one of them builds a grit-ring on the steadier ground of an older bench and lays a single dense, iron-gray casing no wider than two cupped palms, wrapped in dry fibrous scales. Buried in the warm powder, the casing is buffered from the jarring tremors while the rock underneath bakes it slowly toward waking. The parent crouches nearby in a hunched slate shelter, jaw resting on the shelf, listening dayless hour after dayless hour for the hairline crack that threatens the bench. If the slab splits and half the ring slumps outward—as so many do—the parent drags the half-formed young out of the draining grit, clamps it against the warm cleft of its own ribs, and crawls miles across the rifts to stack a new ring on quieter stone. When the young finally splits its dry shell, it consumes the mineral-rich fragments before ever climbing over the rim of upright shards, leaving behind only a shallow dish of warm powder that the next dust-devil blurs flat.

And then there are the balances.

Out on the wide, exposed terraces where the dark opens across three ridges at once, a dweller will spend hours choosing four pieces of rock from the angular debris at its feet: three pointed wedges of hard slate, and one wide, heavy, flat-sheared plate of basalt. It does not hurry. It tests the bedrock beneath with flat palms and jaw, listening for hidden hollows or rising strain. It sets the three wedges upright, spaced like the corners of a narrow triangle, and then—unfolding its long spar-like arms to their full reach—hoists the heavy basalt plate above them and lowers it, millimeter by millimeter, until the flat stone finds its poise on the three sharp tips.

At the simplest level, a standing balance is an instrument. Lifted clear of the muffling scree and touching the bedrock at only three narrow points, the heavy plate turns into a drumhead for the mountain. When a distant rift widens, the plate shivers with a faint, high-strung vibration that runs cleanly down the wedges; lay both palms on its upper face, or press a jawbone against its edge, and you can read the lean of the entire plateau—which of the three facing ridges is rising, which is slipping, and whether the terrace beneath you has shifted by the width of a hair since the last time you passed. In a country without sun, moon, or seasons, a balance is also the closest thing to a clock. Returning to a terrace across the dark, a dweller runs its pale fingertips beneath the poised slab and feels the tiny, ground-out pale dimples where the three sharp points have worried the underside through a hundred minor tremors; it brushes the fine veil of drifted powder on top and knows how long the rock has held steady.

Yet if they were only gauges, they would not be built so often where three ridges come into view, nor tended with such quiet stubbornness.

Because the thin air swallows voices, solid basalt is the only thing up here that carries a message. Now and then, a dweller standing beside a poised plate will draw back one hardened knuckle and strike the rim—a spare, deliberate cadence of dry clicks that shoots down through the three wedges and runs outward through the unbroken terrace, faster than any wind, fading only where a rift cuts the stone apart. Then it lowers its chin onto the slab and waits. Most times, only the mountain answers: the slow ticking of cooling glass, the muffled subterranean shudder of a deep seam breathing far below. Once in a long while, faint and fine as a needle touching bone, a matching cadence taps back from half a mile out in the dark—another dweller on the far edge of the same unbroken shelf, acknowledging that it, too, is still upright on the moving back of the highland.

And even when no answer comes, they read one another in the stones left standing. Coming onto a terrace where another has already hoisted a plate, a dweller lays its flat palms against the basalt and feels the signature of the hand that set it: the daring height of the wedges, the exact weight balanced on so narrow a point, the slight off-center tilt that reveals how much the bench has slumped since the work was done. If the plate is trembling near the edge of collapse, the newcomer does not always walk past. Often it lifts the heavy slab gently off its points, nudges the slipping wedge a fingernail’s width deeper beneath the load, and sets the plate back into quiet poise—a silent repair made for a neighbor it may never meet, across miles of dark and shifting scree.

Most of the balances fall, of course. The highland never stops forcing itself upward and shrugging itself apart; sooner or later a shelf pitches too hard, a hairline crack zips beneath the wedges, and the heavy basalt slides sideways into a flat heap indistinguishable from ordinary rubble. Even the dwellers themselves end the same way. When one grows too ancient and stiff-jointed to climb out of a tilting shelter, or chooses at last not to rise from an old cooling ledge, there is no rot to mark its passing. The dry heat bakes its dense frame brittle; the thermal drafts scour it with flying flakes; the next tremor cracks its dark horn-plates into sharp, iron-gray shards that mingle cleanly with the scree.

Still, on a high terrace under the bruised black sky, one of them crouches beside a newly hoisted plate. Across the lightless gulf, an entire buttress of slate suddenly loses its footing and peels away in a slow cataract of black wedges, flashing once with the brief white sparks of colliding flint before a shroud of dry powder blots out the lowest stars. Seconds later, a papery rustle arrives on the thin warm air, and a deep, dull shudder runs up through the shelf, up through the three pointed wedges, and straight into the dweller’s resting palms.

The heavy basalt plate shivers violently, dances a hair’s breadth on its tips, and holds.

The dweller does not move to steady it, and does not rise. It only keeps its pale palms pressed lightly to the warm stone, feeling the tremor die out into a faint, lingering hum, waiting through the quiet dark—already noting the direction of that distant flash, where in a few days’ time a fresh warm seam will cool just past glowing, and the pale crust will begin to creep along the rawest edge of the mountain.
