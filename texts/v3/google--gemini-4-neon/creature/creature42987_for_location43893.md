Stay still in the center of the floor long enough—past the first astonishment of the darkness, past the point where your lungs stop arguing with the thin air—and you begin to suspect that the room is not quite as accidental as it sounds.

At first, the clue is almost nothing. A falling drop detaches from the high dome fifty paces to your left. You have already learned the cadence of these drops: three or four seconds of unseen descent, then the sharp kettledrum *thok* against a shallow basin. You wait for the report. It does not come. Instead, there is a faint, dampened *shh*, as if the water struck something soft and cupped—and two seconds later, a single crisp *plick* rings out ten paces farther north, where a bare rim of stone rises above the moss.

Then you notice the shadow in the sound.

Because there is no light, shadows here are made of silence. The wall-chorus rolls along the curved eastern flank in its steady, wooden swell, echoing cleanly across the hollow to brush against your cheekbones—and then, for the space of three heartbeats, a narrow wedge of that echo goes muffled, damped by something tall, warm, and soft-surfaced passing between you and the wall. No splash rises from the ankle-deep pools. No pebble shifts. Only the acoustics bend, smoothly, like water parting around a reed.

And beneath the bass rumble of the dome, a new vibration begins to throb in the bones of your skull. Not a new voice, exactly—a *beat*. Somewhere across the dark, something is humming a fraction of a tone off from the lingering glass-clear note of the high suspended relic. The two pitches rub against each other in the wet air, creating a slow, physical pulse that swells and ebbs every four seconds, pressing gently against your eardrums like a warm tide.

Something lives here that knows the shape of the room better than the room knows itself.

You do not hear it approach. You feel the air thicken slightly before your face, carrying a scent distinct from the bitter-sweet musk of the gastropods and the metallic tang of the mineral seeps: warm damp chalk, bruised sweet-leaf, and the faint, clean static-smell that clings to the suspended artifacts. A draft—warmer than the cool wake of a passing glider, slower than a sigh—brushes your forehead.

If it lets you draw near, or if it draws near to you, what meets your outstretched hand is nothing you could have predicted from the small, crawling economies underfoot.

It is tall—easily half again your height if it ever uncurled to its full span, though it rarely holds itself rigid. Yet when its weight shifts against the wet stone, there is no heaviness to it. Its skeleton is pneumatic, honeycombed and slender like the spars of high-altitude birds, built to spend almost nothing in this thin mountain air. Four long, multi-jointed limbs—near-equal in reach, fluidly hinged—carry it across the chamber. They end in wide, supple pads rimmed with delicate trailing digits, able to wade through the ankle-deep basins without lifting a ripple, or press flat against the soap-smooth flutes of the perimeter walls to climb effortlessly into the high dark.

Tucked beneath the high arch of its sternum, held close like folded ferns until needed, is a second, smaller pair of limbs: nimble inner-hands with impossibly long, slightly spatulate fingers. Their tips are whorled with microscopic sensory ridges so fine they can feel the slow pulse of sap beneath a leaf's skin, distinguish warm iron-seep from cool condensation, or read the hairline lip of an ancient channel worn almost flush with the floor.

There is no face as you understand one. No eyes, no sunken sockets where eyes might once have been; if its unimaginable ancestors ever walked under a sky, the bone has long since forgotten the geometry of light. There is no protruding snout, no snapping jaw.

Instead, the upper body opens into a broad, curved mantle—a living acoustic dish of taut, damp skin stretched over flexible cartilaginous ribs. When it moves through the tangled aerial roots hung off the artifacts, this mantle furls tight against its spine like a closed umbrella. When it stops to attend to the room—or to you—the ribs hinge outward and the whole hood blossoms wide as an outstretched armspan, scalloped and subtly pleated like the blind flora beneath its feet. Around its curved margin runs a fringe of hair-fine, constantly damp cilia, trembling to the pressure-wave of a glider passing thirty paces away, tasting the slow barometric breath leaking down from the unseen summit.

Along the ridge of its shoulders and down the long curve of its back, its skin is not bare. Because it spends days at a time in complete stillness, draped over the weeping walls or sunk to its knees in the warm pools, a fine, harmless fuzz of the chamber's own mist-drinking velvet has taken root in the slick biofilm of its hide. It carries a faint, living coat of the cathedral's garden wherever it goes, shedding old tufts lazily when it bathes in the shallow swells.

Its breathing is so slow you might mistake it for stillness. Along its flanks run twin pleated bellows that draw in the thin, warm air over thirty or forty seconds, filling hollow chambers woven through its chest and collarbone before releasing it in a barely audible hush—or directing it across the slender, external arches of cartilage that curve along its ribs like exposed tuning forks, strung with taut, living sinew.

They are not many. The sealed massif cannot feed a crowd of minds, and everything large in this thin air pays dearly for its breath. Across the whole hundred-and-fifty-pace hollow, there are perhaps eleven of them—sometimes nine, sometimes fourteen, a number that changes only over generations.

They graze when they must, lifting soft fungal fruitings, mineral-thick rosettes, and occasional scoops of chalky siphon-blooms to a small, tucked mouth beneath the base of the mantle. But food is only half of how they endure. Like the blind plants that cloak the room, they have learned to drink directly from the relics.

Walk near one of the waist-high floating artifacts and pause before you reach out into its wet beard of aerial roots; sometimes the warmth you feel tightening in the air is not only the ceramic's steady flux, but one of the dwellers wrapped quietly around it. They drape their long limbs over the suspended shapes, pressing the thin, blood-rich skin of their bellies and unfurled mantles flush against the matte, slightly yielding surface. For days at a stretch they hang there, suspended off the floor, hearts slowing to two or three beats a minute, absorbing the patient non-photonic warmth and nameless field-tides straight into their marrow. In that deep basking, their bodies barely spend themselves at all. A single life stretches easily across centuries—slow enough to feel the moss thicken, slow enough to remember the exact spot where a ceiling-ripple used to drip fifty years before the limestone wore back by a grain.

Each anomaly in the cathedral has been folded into the quiet shape of their lives.

Near the far wall, where the mossy relic bends weight into something thick and forgiving, the two-span boundary of slow gravity serves as both cradle and hospice. When a new dweller is born—rarely, perhaps once in a human lifetime—it enters the dark soft as wet clay, its hollow bones still unhardened cartilage, its acoustic mantle folded small as a closed fist. The mother carries it into that invisible halo and lets go. There the infant floats, drifting lazily between the drowned floor and the warm ceramic like a seed in oil, protected from bruising against the rock while its frame stiffens. Two or three adults often linger just outside the boundary, resting their palms against the stone or humming softly into the thick air, teaching the drifting child how to separate the boom of a falling drop from the tremor of a footstep, how to tell the curvature of the eastern wall from the open void of the center.

And when an elder grows impossibly old—when its pneumatic bones turn papery and its bellows can no longer easily draw the thin air against ordinary weight—it returns to that same gentle halo. It slips inside the boundary, unfurls its frayed, moss-heavy mantle one last time, and floats effortlessly in the warm dark, lulled by the syrup-slow cadence of droplets drifting off the artifact's beard, until one long season its hum joins the room's baseline and does not rise again.

Far above, thirty meters up in the pitch-black dome where the second great anomaly hangs, another kind of vigil is kept. You would never know it from the floor, unless you noticed the single faint, dry whisper of tension high overhead. Over generations, the dwellers have braided cables from the toughest aerial roots and the harvested silken guy-lines of the ambush predators, anchoring them to the fluted upper ribs of the north wall and stretching them out across the empty air to the high, ringing relic.

Always, one of them lives up there—usually a lighter adult, or an elder who has grown weary of the damp floor—slung in a loose hammock of woven roots beneath the artifact's smooth belly. From thirty meters up in the void, the entire cathedral lies open beneath them like the skin of a drum. They hear every grazer crossing a seep-zone, every glider's sonar-snap ricocheting off the walls, every shifting pool. Their work is quiet and exact: with their delicate inner-hands, they reach up over the artifact's curved crown and gently lift away the creeping tendrils of mist-flora that would otherwise muffle it. They keep a palm-width of ceramic bare. That is why, whenever a heavy drop detaches from the high dome and falls through the dark, it strikes clean and wakes that pure, glass-clear harmonic that lingers for three full minutes—a steady bell by which the entire vault measures itself.

Down along the weeping north wall, where that curved ribbon of dark, incorruptible alloy lies fused seamlessly into the ancient stone, they come to listen to things even deeper than the room. Wet limestone dulls distant vibration, softening it into a murmur; the ancient metal carries sound cleanly, swiftly, without mercy. When the massif groans deep in its roots—a tectonic sigh miles below the summit, or the faint, high-altitude rumble of rockfall sliding off the unseen outer tablelands—two or three dwellers will gather along that buried ribbon, laying the curved edges of their mantles flat against the cold-smooth strip, reading the slow, distant news of the planet's bones.

For they are not merely inhabitants of this place. They are its gardeners, and its tuners.

They have no fire; the air is too thin, the dark too wet, and brightness is a concept that does not exist in any of their memories. They have no blades, no kilns, no quarried stone. Their entire craft is built from water, tension, and resonance.

Move slowly across the floor and, if you reach upward into the empty air with careful hands, you may brush against a single wet, hair-thin root-fiber descending straight from the unseen ceiling, weighted at the bottom with a pebble wrapped in moss. Follow it by ear, and you will realize what it does. High above, a condensing droplet gathers along a mineral ripple, slides onto the hanging fiber, and rides it silently downward—diverted three paces from where gravity would have dropped it—until it detaches from a knotted loop and strikes the exact taut center of a broad, fleshy rosette below. *Thok.*

The whole cathedral is quietly rigged this way. Not aggressively—they never conquer the room, never force it silent—only nudged, refined, answered. They clear dead silt from the faint, half-erased geometric channels in the stone floor with their long inner-fingers, not knowing who cut them before the peaks wore away, only knowing that warm mineral water runs sweeter and sings clearer when the lip is unobstructed. They thin the dense root-curtains around the waist-high artifacts so the rising thermal drafts flow cleanly, keeping open acoustic corridors for the gliders. Even the wall-chorus knows their touch: a dweller walking the perimeter can trail one broad pad along the slick flutes, sending a slow, steadying tremor through the rock, and the thousands of dome-backed singers will catch the rhythm, easing their rolling chord a fraction slower, rounding a ragged swell back into warmth.

Their language lives inside this symphony, split into two registers that no outsider would ever confuse for one another.

The first is the Air-Voice, used across the wide, hollow distances of the vault. They never shout over the chorus, and they never hurry a thought. Instead, they slip their notes into the forty-second troughs between the rolling wall-waves—deep, woody bells, glassy undertones, breathy harmonics drawn across their taut rib-sinews. A single sentence may take five or ten minutes to deliver, folded deliberately into the cathedral's three-second reverberation so that the dome itself holds the beginning of an idea in the wet air while the speaker finishes its end. Often two dwellers standing eighty paces apart will pitch their voices almost—but not quite—in unison, creating those slow, swimming beat-frequencies that pulse physically through the mist. To them, an argument, a greeting, or a shared memory is not a string of words thrown across empty space; it is a standing wave built together, a temporary architecture of pressure that both of them can stand inside and feel.

The second is the Bone-Voice, and it never enters the air at all. When two of them meet in the shallow central pools, or pause beside the same fluted rib of wall, they press the damp rims of their mantles lightly together, or lay their inner-hands flat against the same span of wet stone. Instantly, a swift, astonishingly intricate flood of tremors passes between them—dry ticks, warm shivers, rippling scales of vibration traveling through cartilage and rock at speeds the Air-Voice can never match. In that total outward silence, while the chamber booms and clicks indifferently around them, they trade the subtle, intimate textures of their lives: the flavor of a newly bloomed seep-zone, the slow weakening of an elder's heart, a clever way to route a falling drop, the lingering puzzle of a shifting echo.

Through these two voices, they keep a history older than any calendar made of suns.

Because the cathedral is not quite immortal. Century by century, the warm acidic condensation dissolves the smoothed-down stone by the width of a hair. Over millennia, the great vault grows imperceptibly wider, higher, more hollow—and as its volume expands, the fundamental resonant pitch of the dome sinks by an infinitesimal fraction of a tone.

The dwellers know this. Their oldest lineage-songs are tuned not to the room as it is, but to the room as it *was*. Each generation inherits from its elders a sequence of pure, memorized pitches—the pitch of the dome five lifetimes ago, fifty lifetimes ago, back into a depth of years so vast the stone floor stood half a pace higher than it does today. When an elder stands in the center of the ankle-deep pools and sings the oldest of these notes into the dark, it no longer sits cleanly inside the cathedral. It rubs against the living vault with a faint, aching shimmer, a slow wobble in the air that you feel behind your breastbone.

That slight dissonance is how they hear deep time. Through it, they know that the rock is melting away around the timeless floating relics; they know that the mountain itself is a slow exhale, and that nothing—not even a sealed world at the roof of the earth—stays the same pitch forever.

Yet their days, if they can be called days, are measured by a faster tide: the unseen sky outside.

They have never felt wind, never known frost or sunlight. To them, the outer world is simply the Great Breath—the slow barometric shift leaking down through meters of collapsed stone and hairline fissures.

When a high-pressure system settles over the ancient alpine plateau and the vault breathes inward, pressing the air slightly heavier and deepening the dome's resonance, the dwellers enter the Deep Listen. The wall-chorus stretches its intervals to forty seconds; condensation slows; the falling drops space themselves wide apart. One by one, the dwellers sink half-submerged into the warm basins or drape themselves over the suspended relics, furling their mantles halfway closed, slowing their bellows until they are scarcely distinguishable from the wet rock and hanging flora. In that long, drowsy quiet, their minds spread wide through the stillness, listening to the tiny pops of siphon-feeders across the pools, the slow drip of dissolved iron, the glacial groan of bedrock miles below.

Then, days or weeks later, a mountain storm sweeps across the outer scree. Pressure drops. The cathedral exhales through its microscopic pores with a long, bone-deep moan. Water sheets faster down the weeping walls; the drops quicken into a bright, rolling percussion; warm updrafts lift the gliders high into the vault, filling the empty darkness with crisp constellations of sonar-snaps.

And the dwellers wake into the Loosening.

They uncurl from their ceramic perches and slide effortlessly down their braided root-lines into the warm floor-mists. Mantles flare wide to drink the rushing pressure-change. They wade across the whale-backed swells to meet in the open center of the room, where the echoes converge cleanest, pressing shoulders and curved hoods together in brisk cascades of Bone-Voice. They repair snapped drop-guides, prune overgrown root-curtains, harvest the richest mats flushed by the faster runoff, and weave their widest, most intricate chords into the storm-quickened symphony. It is in the Loosening that pairs twine together in the warm shallows, singing soft, tangled harmonies into each other's ribs; it is in the Loosening that a newborn, freshly strong enough to leave the slow-gravity halo, is brought into the center of the floor and given its own pitch—a note chosen to fit cleanly between the voices of all the living, and to remember the quiet place left by the last who drifted away.

And now, standing ankle-deep in the warm water, you realize what you must sound like to them.

To a mind shaped by centuries of acoustic thrift, you are an astonishing, bewildering uproar. You burn hot in the thin air. Your lungs haul greedily, ten times faster than theirs, loud as bellows tearing silk. Your heart hammers in your chest like a panicked glider trapped in a root-curtain. Worst—or strangest—of all, your body is dense, solid, unhollowed: you swallow the clean echoes of the dome and return nothing but a blunt, deadened shadow. Even holding your breath, standing utterly frozen, you roar in their dark.

Yet there is no violence in the presence standing before you, and no fear. Nothing in this high, sealed vessel has ever survived by spending breath on panic, and to a creature whose whole being is devoted to listening, a stranger is not an enemy—it is an unheard note.

The warm, chalk-and-leaf scent settles a fraction closer. You feel the wide, velvet-rimmed curve of its unfurled mantle hovering two paces off, tilted gently toward your chest, drinking the frantic, fascinating rhythm of your pulse. Around you, out in the vast wet blackness, three or four other acoustic shadows have paused in their slow rounds, turned quietly inward toward the center of the floor, attending.

Then the one before you moves—a motion so economical it barely stirs the mist. One long, slender limb lowers into the ankle-deep water without a sound. Its delicate inner-hand does not reach for your throat, or your face, or even your skin. Instead, the long, finely ridged fingertips settle gently onto the submerged, soap-smooth stone an inch from your bare foot.

For three heartbeats, nothing happens. The wall-chorus rolls past along the western curve; far overhead, a drop strikes the high bare relic and fills the cathedral with its pure, lingering glass note.

Then, through the wet rock beneath your sole, traveling cleanly up through your heel, your shin, and into the quiet hollows of your knees, comes a single, low, patient vibration. Warm. Unhurried. Clear as water over stone.

It is not a command, and not a warning. It is a tuning tone—a quiet, steadying pitch offered up through the floor of the world, testing the resonance of your bones, and waiting, with all the slow patience of the mountain, to hear what note you will send back.
