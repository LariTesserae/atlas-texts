You do not see them first. You feel the basin lose a fraction of its echo.

Halfway up the north wall, where a shaft of bent green-gray daylight snaps clean off against a shear of hotter water, the roar drops by half a note—not quieter, never quieter, only blunted, as though a heavy slab of basalt had detached from the cliff and hung suspended in the murk instead of falling. Then the dark shape shifts a hand’s breadth to the left, and the braided line of black weed and pale tendon anchored two ledges down goes bar-taut out of rhythm with the current.

Only when a cluster of cavitation voids collapses right beside it, throwing a cold-white pinprick across a bare shoulder and a splayed, scarred hand, does the figure separate from the stone.

There are never many of them in the cleft—four now, perhaps five if the lean, restless one that keeps to the battered froth near the sky-opening has not drifted too close to the arch and been taken by the wind. The chamber is too small and too lean to bear a crowd, and closeness here has a price. Still, they hold the basin the way roots hold a split boulder, spaced out along its height so that no two share the same ledge for long, yet never truly out of one another’s reach.

At a glance, in the smoky dimness, you might mistake them for drowned people who somehow forgot to swell or soften. They have knees, clavicles, five-fingered hands, the compact proportions of climbers. Look closer—wait until one steadies itself on a razor-edged rib of rock while a northward ribbon of silt strips milky dust from the wall an arm’s length behind its back—and you see how thoroughly the basin has remade them.

They are short-limbed and strikingly heavy for their height, thick-ribbed and dense through the trunk, built without the hollow buoyancy of swimmers from gentler waters. There is no air-pocket left inside their chests for a standing wall of pressure to crush; their ribs cage solid muscle and tough, springy cartilage that takes a shockwave the way wet heartwood takes a mallet. They sink when they stop holding on, and they want to sink. Weight is what keeps them moored to the cliff.

Their skin is the matte slate of wet basalt, mottled pale ash across the belly and fever-pink along the flanks where the floor vents bleed their hottest water. Almost none of it is unlined. Forearms, shins, shoulders, and the flats of the back carry a dense crosshatching of fine white scars—hundreds of old, straight hairlines left by glass-edged mineral plates, healed tight and leathery without ever thickening into clumsy welts. Their palms, knees, and the soles of their feet are darker still, plated in deeply grooved callus as hard as horn, able to palm a freshly sheared obsidian corner without flinching or bleeding.

They have no outer ears. Where the shells of ears should sit behind their wide, heavy jaws, the skin lies nearly smooth over tight, whorled bosses of dense cartilage, sealed wholly against the basin’s violence. A delicate eardrum would last three seconds in this cleft. Instead, they listen with their skeletons. Their cheekbones are broad, their jawbones thick, their teeth flat-crowned and slightly worn; when something changes across the nave—a spur splitting loose on the far cliff, a pressure-bar stacking up out of two colliding swells—they part their lips slightly and tilt their bare skulls, taking the shift straight into their lower teeth and collarbones.

Above those broad jaws, their eyes sit deep under heavy brow-ridges, narrow and dark, veiled by cloudy inner lids that snap shut an instant before a spray of boiling bubbles or a cold-white cavitation spark can blind them. They see poorly at distance, and poorly is enough; in this basin, sight is only for catching the bend of light, the pale smear of the open arch, and the flicker of collapsing voids. Along the sides of the neck, tucked neatly into the sheltered hollow beneath the jawline and the collarbone, sit three narrow crescent slits on each side, lipped with thick pale-pink muscle. When a violent current shears past, those lids clamp shut like valves; in the lee of a spur, or curled inside a stone baffle, they part in slow, unhurried pulses, sipping the warm, churned water without ever needing the sky.

They do not swim through the open center of the chamber if they can help it. Fighting this water wastes flesh that takes too long to replace. Instead, they read the unmixed layers like staircases. One of them—a broad-shouldered older one whose left collarbone has healed slightly crooked from an old collision—pushes off a north ledge, tucks its chin hard to its breastbone, folds its forearms over its ribs, and drops straight into a downward eddy of heavy warmth. Thirty feet down, it catches the edge of a furiously rising column with one outstretched heel, pivots without a kick, and lets the surge fling it sideways toward a floor-spur.

Mid-drift, its jaw slackens a hair. It leads with the back of its right wrist, testing the water ahead of its face, and halts abruptly—catching a single protruding basalt tooth with its toes just two feet shy of empty space. A heartbeat later, that empty space detonates inward: an unseen wall of stacked sound collapses into a pale sheet of scalding bubbles, violent enough to bruise bone. Before the foam has even cleared, the swimmer lets go and slips cleanly through the brief, soft hole the collapse leaves behind.

In a chamber where the roar has mass, another dense body casts an acoustic shadow. Even from opposite walls, through dim water thick with spinning silt, each of them knows roughly where the others are by the subtle bluntness they lend to the echo. They have no speech that travels through the water—a voice would be shredded to nothing before it left the lips—yet the basin is not without conversation.

Watch the lean one crouched high on the east cliff, where the basalt ribs run unbroken down toward the floor. It lifts a blunt thumb-knuckle and taps the stone four times, pausing, then twice—not hard, never hard, because a loud strike is swallowed at once, but tightly pitched to the cliff’s own tremor. Twenty feet lower, another one clinging to the same vein of rock stills its fingers against the stone, taking the pattern straight up through its wrists and elbows. Moments later, it replies by plucking once—clean and sharp—at the braided line of wall-weed and tendon stretched across the gap, sending a heavy thrum through the taut cord that cuts clean across the water’s shriek.

Only rarely do two of them draw close enough to touch. When they do—sharing the quiet eddy behind a spur while a large pressure-swell pulls the far wall dizzyingly near, or meeting near the floor when something has fallen from the sky—they do not look long into each other’s cloudy eyes. One simply lays the flat of its forearm against the other’s collarbone, or presses its forehead briefly against the crown of the other’s skull, and clicks its teeth in a quick, uneven cadence, humming low in the solid cartilage of its throat. The vibration crosses bone to bone without ever entering the roaring water: a warning of a newly cracked shelf, the location of a fresh cluster of shells, or merely the plain check of one heavy frame against another before they pull apart and return to their separate heights.

Hunger keeps them moving along the seams.

Most days they work the fresh fractures where the flat, slate-gray shells weld themselves to the rock. Each carries a scavenged wedge—a pale, splintered triangle of mineral plate knocked loose from a ledge, sometimes wrapped at the blunt end with a tight twist of rubbery wall-weed so it will not slice the palm. You cannot pry a shell off by force; hammered at the wrong pitch, it shatters into grit along with its meat. Instead, the hunter lays its cheek or the side of its jaw directly against the basalt beside the shell, listening until the stone’s shiver matches the hum in its teeth. It waits—motionless as a barnacle—for a passing swell to drag the cliff’s vibration a fraction off-key. In that thin instant, while the shell is readjusting its grip to keep from cracking, the mineral wedge slips cleanly beneath the rim. The meat inside—no larger than a thumb, dark and tasting fiercely of iron—goes straight into the mouth before the current can tear it away.

Between shells, they nip the pale stalks near the floor before a passing shockwave can pop their translucent bladders, swallowing the bitter nubs whole. When one of the blind, fist-sized humming sacs drifts into a baffled corner, the youngest of them sometimes cups it between scarred palms without crushing it, letting the creature’s taut internal filaments buzz against its skin like a tiny echo of the chamber before either tearing it open for the warm jelly inside or letting the current carry it off into the murk. The long, pale, unjointed ribbon-things near the floor vents they do not hunt—perhaps because they are nearly impossible to grasp, perhaps because of what follows them. Now and then, when one of those pale ribbons glides effortlessly straight through a violent seam of opposing water, an inhabitant will kick out from a fissure and ride five or six yards in its wake, slipping through the brief, dizzying trough of dropped pressure while the faint thread of iron-and-rain mucus trails across its shoulders.

The real wealth comes from above, and rarely. Once in a long season, something tumbles down through the high froth from the mountain crown—a hawk beaten out of the clouds, a lost kid fallen off the ridge, a heavy mat of frozen highland turf. When that dark shape comes spinning down the bent shafts of light, all four of them move at once, faster than their heavy frames seem built for. They intercept it before the upward currents can hammer it to paste against the shelves, hauling it down into a deep floor-groove. Nothing is wasted. The warm, battered flesh is stripped clean with mineral flakes; the smaller bones are kept as wedges; the pale sinews are painstakingly pulled, soaked in the warmest vent-water, chewed soft between flat teeth, and braided strand by strand with soaked black wall-weed to mend the crossing-lines when a surge snaps them.

Even down by the vents, where scalding mineral silt wells out of the cracked floor, they move with quiet familiarity. Before a fresh fissure tears open in the basin, the floor vents change their rhythm—running hotter, thicker, carrying a sharper metallic tremor. One of them will drop to its knees in the murk and press its bare hands flat into the welling mud, fingers splayed wide to catch the pulse of the stone underneath, holding there until the surge kicks loose. Sometimes the hot mineral flashes hard to sharp sinter in the heartbeat after it lifts its palms away, leaving the hollow print of spread fingers preserved on the rock until the next great swell scours the shelf clean.

Yet even bodies built of dense bone and sealed cartilage wear thin under a noise that never sleeps.

After days of heavy pressure-swells, when the pitch of the roar climbs until the water itself shivers like plucked wire, the high shriek works its way past their jaws and settles deep into their marrow. You can tell when one is worn down: its fingers twitch slightly when it rests on a ledge; its inner eyelids stay half-drawn; it holds its head low between its shoulders as if bracing against a blow that will not finish landing.

That is when they return to the stone hoods—or build new ones where the chamber has torn an old shelter away.

Watching one raise a baffle is like watching a blind mason tune an instrument. It gathers flat, razor-edged shards of slate from the scarred floor, ignoring their shape and testing only their weight and ring. Holding a shard upright in the roaring current at the lip of a ledge, it turns the sharp plate inch by inch, jaw open, wrists loose, waiting for the snarl in its forearms to drop into a deep, steady purr. Only when the stone finds that exact angle against the echo does it wedge the base into a crack, pinning it with smaller chips of basalt, no clay and no mortar—nothing but friction, weight, and an intimate knowledge of how sound breaks against a plane.

When the cramped hood is finished, it backs inside, curling knees tight to chest, heels pressed under its seat, forearms locked around its shins, chin braced firmly onto its knees so its entire skeleton forms one closed, dense ring. Behind those slanted shards, the high tearing edge of the basin shears away overhead, dropping into a slow, heavy, chest-rattling throb. Its gill-slits open wider in the sheltered pocket. Its inner lids draw completely shut. For hours it hangs there in half-waking torpor, rocked inside the stone like a pebble wedged in a millwheel, until the tremor drains out of its teeth and it uncurls back into the murk.

There is only one edge of the chamber that none of them ever grows fully used to: the leeward arch, where the rock ends and the trembling, blistered wall of water holds back the sky.

They keep clear of it when the big swells roll through, for the bulging membrane is treacherous—lean too far when it slaps outward, and you would spill straight out into the freezing void, falling past the cloud-deck into dry, roaring wind where gill-slits glue shut and nothing presses back against your bones. Yet they do not shun it.

Every so often, when the currents slacken slightly along the rim, one of them climbs out to the jagged lip of the arch and hooks a calloused foot securely around a tooth of basalt. It hangs there in the smoky daylight, watching scalding vapor peel off the outer skin of the water and vanish into the pale gray drop beyond. Slowly, deliberately, it lifts one scarred forearm and thrusts its fingers straight through the shimmering membrane.

Inside the basin, the water seals tightly around the wrist. Outside, in the freezing highland air, the fingers go instantly pale and dry.

The body inside the chamber stiffens from crown to heel. Its gill-slits clamp hard; its jaw locks; its shoulders hunch against the sudden, monstrous amputation of sound. To a being whose entire world is continuous vibration, the silence outside the wall does not feel like rest—it feels like a blade laid cleanly across the wrist, severing the hand from the roaring continuity of its own flesh. It holds its fingers out there in the freezing wind for three heartbeats, four, sometimes five, enduring the terrible numbness of an un-ringing world. Then it snatches the arm back inside, pulling the frost-stung hand tight against its warm sternum as the basin’s roar floods back into its knuckles like needles.

Why they do it, they could not tell you even if they had voices. Perhaps to remember that the chamber has an edge. Perhaps because, for one thin second before the hot slate-colored water washes it away, the wet skin of those knuckles carries the sharp, dry, impossible scent of snow and open air.

And down near the center of the floor, in the wide, dim stretch where the currents momentarily twist around a quiet eye, sits the crooked knee-high ring of stacked slate.

None of them claims it alone, yet all of them tend it. Whenever a floor-shift topples a side of the circle, or a wandering shear knocks two shards askew, whoever passes next drops down through the murky volume, gathers the mismatched plates in scarred hands, and wedges them back into a rough ring before drifting on. Inside that circle, resting directly on the raw stone, lie the three small, pale pebbles—utterly smooth, unmoved by the torrents, drawing the roar inward toward their rounded skins until a hand lowered above them can almost catch the cold wind blowing high outside the mountain.

They do not kneel there together. One at a time, between foraging and mending lines, an inhabitant will sink into the quiet eye, rest its shins on the raw basalt outside the ring, and hover one dark-grooved palm a finger’s breadth above the three pale stones. It does not touch them often. It simply lets the sudden, faint pull of stillness gather against its knuckles, gill-slits slowing, jaw unclenching, taking into its bones that brief, miraculous taste of the outside wind without having to pay the freezing cut of the arch.

And if you linger long enough in the dimness—long enough for the sparks of collapsing voids to paint the same scarred ledge twice—you may glimpse the oldest of them curled inside its north-wall hood, knees drawn up, hands cupped loosely in the dark space between its thighs.

Between its thickened palms lies a fourth pale stone.

It is not yet smooth. Broken years ago from a vein of pale mineral high on the east cliff, it still carries one stubbornly flat face and the dulled shadow of an old edge. Slowly, steadily, without hurry, the old one rolls the stone against a pinch of milky rock-dust trapped in the grooves of its skin—thumb over ridge, palm over corner, hour after hour, season after season, while the mountain around it cracks and re-breaks itself to keep every other edge razor-sharp. Now and then, when the swells ease near the leeward arch, the old one climbs out to the jagged rim with that unfinished stone gripped tight in its fist, thrusts its arm through the trembling wall of water, and opens its fingers for five heartbeats so the freezing wind can dry the stone and sing across its half-rounded skin.

Then it closes its hand, draws the cold weight back into the scalding roar, and descends once more toward the crooked ring on the floor—patiently refusing the law of the basin, wearing one small piece of the world down until it can hold the wind.
