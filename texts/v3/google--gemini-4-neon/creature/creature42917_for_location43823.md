You do not notice them at first for the same reason you do not notice the mirror-backed beetles on the upper shields: they have given up every habit of motion that makes a living thing stand out against a still world.

You can pass within six paces of one in the lower galleries and register only another pale, ribbed stem tucked behind a curtain of aerial roots. Only if you stop—really stop, letting your breathing quiet and the heat press back in around your ears—does the shape resolve. A narrow shoulder coated in matte, chalk-white wax. A bare skull angled slightly downward, catching the diffused green glow from the floor rather than the white glare above. Long fingers resting flat against a pressurized stalk, not gripping it, simply matching its stillness.

They are shaped by the gaps between things. Everything in the valley grows shoulder to shoulder, and so the people who live here are narrow—flat-ribbed, lean-hipped, long in the shin and forearm, built to turn sideways and slip between crowded columnar trunks without scraping the pale bloom off the leaves. They are not gaunt; there is a smooth, hydrated fullness to their limbs, almost taut beneath the skin, as though their bodies hold moisture with the same stubborn economy as the stems around them.

Where you can see their bare skin—at the inner wrists, the hollows of the throat, the undersides of their long fingers—it is pale as peeled pith, faintly translucent, with a cool olive cast where slow blood moves beneath. Everywhere else, across their backs, shoulders, forearms, and the smooth, hairless domes of their heads, they are dusted and rubbed with the heavy matte wax harvested from the upper canopy shields. It dries into a chalky, light-bouncing sheen that smells faintly of warm beeswax and keeps the relentless overhead glare from burning them raw. They have almost no body hair—none to trap souring sweat, none to shed and linger forever in a valley where nothing rots. Their eyes sit deep beneath smooth, browless ridges, hooded by heavy lids that blink only once or twice a minute. Their irises are pale and mineral, the color of cloudy selenite or diluted amber, their pupils pinched to tiny dark specks even down in the shade.

They wear almost nothing. Woven cloth would grow clammy in the breathless warmth and never dry. Around a narrow waist or slung diagonally across one flat chest, most carry only a tight cord of braided pith holding three or four quiet tools: a naturally cleaved splinter of raw quartz, never chipped or worked, chosen purely for the factory-clean edge the bedrock broke with; a walnut-sized lump of darkened plant resin; a short length of hollow stem beveled cleanly at both ends for drawing water; and a flat packet of waxy leaves folded neatly against the hip. When they climb along the bare ridges of unrounded basalt, some wrap their shins and palms in tight spirals of supple, stripped bark-skin so the fresh stone cannot slice them. Otherwise they go bare, barefoot, their long toes spreading cleanly across the springy root-mat with barely a whisper of pressure.

To live in a world that neither grows nor decays is to live inside a held breath, and their bodies have slowed down to match it.

If you watch one resting in the clammy shade beneath a cantilevered rosette, you have to look closely to see their ribs lift. Their pulse beats lazily, twenty times a minute or less. They do not sweat as an outsider does; their skin stays dry-matte on top and faintly cool in the creases, conserving every drop. A finger-long wax lizard will sometimes creep right off a stalk onto a motionless dweller’s shin, mistaking them for part of the gallery, and wait there for condensation to bead in the hollow behind their knee. Neither of them flinches.

Because nothing in the valley ever heals over or rots away, every mark a living body makes is permanent. A broken frond stays broken. A spilled handful of crumbs or a dropped scrap of bone would lie exposed on the root-floor forever, unburied by soil, untouched by scavengers. A deep cut into a pressurized stem would weep glossy, unhardened sap until the stalk went slack. So they move through the dense plant-mass with an almost surgical courtesy.

When they eat, they leave scarcely a trace. Deep in the crowded middle tiers, a dweller will press an ear to a thick green stalk, listening for the tight, humming fullness of its sap. With the tip of a quartz sliver, they open a vertical slit no longer than a thumbnail beneath a waxy leaf-joint, insert the beveled hollow stem, and draw out a few slow mouthfuls of lukewarm, faintly sweet liquid—or pare out a pale ribbon of crisp inner pith. Before they step back, they soften their lump of darkened resin between thumb and palm and smear a neat, dark bead directly over the incision, sealing the pressure back inside the plant. That resin is one of the few things they make: sap gathered drop by drop from the lower galleries and carried all the way up to the blinding upper terraces, where it is spread thin on bare basalt ribs beneath the naked white ceiling until the flat, relentless heat cooks it dark and stiff.

Water is taken with the same care. Rather than shaking the foliage or letting the pools stir muddy with pith-sediment, they move slowly along the shaded undersides of the giant horizontal shields with a folded leaf-cup held just below the trembling rows of condensation beads. A feather-light touch of a dry finger breaks the droplet’s grip, letting it slide silently into the cup without ever falling to make a sound. At the shallow warm basins where mineral seams meet the root-floor, they line the raw stone with overlapping waxy leaves folded into clean bowls, separating the clear, lukewarm drip from the sharp-tasting seepage below.

Even their silence is a kind of architecture.

In the padded galleries, sound dies within a few paces, and what does survive—a heavy bead dropping onto a taut leaf, the dry creak of a shifting stem—cuts sharply through the quiet. They almost never speak aloud. When two of them chance to cross paths, they converse through soft, dry clicks made against the roof of the mouth—sounds so close to the click of a mirror-beetle’s leg or a settling shard of rock that an outsider hears only the valley itself—and through touch. They press palm to palm to read each other’s warmth and hydration. They lean their smooth, waxed foreheads together in the dimness beneath a fan-frond, sharing a breath so slow it barely stirs the air between them.

Mostly, though, they keep far apart. There are no villages here, no clusters of dwellings, not even families lingering long under one canopy. Perhaps a hundred of them—perhaps fewer—inhabit the entire several-mile trough, spaced out across wide, unbroken stretches of foliage so their body heat never collects, their footfalls never wear a channel into the springy root-floor, and the air remains undisturbed. A lone dweller may go what an outsider would call months without seeing another face, though out here, under a white mineral ceiling that never dims or angles, no one counts months. Time is not a sequence of days; it is the long, patient stretch of pressure before a droplet falls, and the quiet interval after.

Yet they are never wholly out of touch. Because nothing weathers and nothing overgrows, the valley holds every arrangement they leave in it like a page that never yellows.

Those solitary structures scattered miles apart across the basin are not ruins and not mere shelters; they are a slow, wordless conversation conducted across enormous spans of stillness.

The low, dry-laid crescent walls of cleaved mineral shards perched on the high terraces serve three quiet uses at once. First, they are a refuge for the eyes: crouching inside the curve of dull pale basalt with your back to the blinding sea of waxy leaves, gazing down into the raw cleft of stone, gives the retina the only sharp, muted band of contrast beneath the flat white sky. Second, they are instruments for listening. Down in the spongy botanical floor, vibrations vanish instantly, but up on the bare fractured ribs of bedrock, tremors travel for miles. The dwellers balance thin splinters of freshly split selenite on the razor edges of the stacked shards so lightly that even a faint settling of the deep crust—or the heavy, careless stride of an intruder far down the slope—tips them with a crisp crystalline *tick*. And third, they are messages. A passerby climbing a ridge reads the tilt of the fractures as easily as an open hand: two plates leaned clean edge to clean edge mean the lower basins on this slope are full; a single dark basalt sliver laid crosswise warns of a stalk-wall strained close to splitting; a crescent whose inner hollow holds a single folded waxy leaf means *someone stayed here, and waited, and moved south*.

Half a mile down the slope, the braided root-bowers offer the one thing the blinding subterranean sky refuses to grant: dusk.

They do not grow these domes quickly, because nothing here puts out fresh shoots. Instead, a dweller spends long, unhurried intervals coaxing existing curtains of wrist-thick aerial roots, bending each pressurized green cable a fraction at a time, weaving one over another and binding the tension with knotted, calcified vines until the ribcage locks permanently into a self-supporting shell. Along the sill of the narrow doorway, they smooth a continuous lip of darkened plant resin—holding back the lukewarm seep of the living floor, keeping out the crawling lizards, and marking a dry threshold you step over rather than onto. Inside, the blinding white glare of the ceiling is filtered down through canopy, fan-frond, and tight basketwork until only a soft, deep-jade twilight remains.

Here they take their long rests. With no night to summon sleep, they crawl inside whenever the heat presses heavy behind their eyes, curl on their sides over overlapping layers of cool, stripped waxy leaves, and drop into a shallow torpor that can last for days. Their breathing flattens until it is nearly imperceptible. Peering through the narrow resin-sealed doorway, you would take the pale, folded shape inside for a bundle of peeled stems laid down to dry.

Sometimes, when a balanced shard on a high ridge has gone undisturbed for too long—when a leaf-cupped basin sits brimming until a single drop finally spills over its waxy lip, untouched—wordless knowledge ripples slowly across the basin. One of the dwellers has gone thin and dry.

They do not age by wrinkling or greying; without wind or dust or changing seasons, their skin simply loses its turgid fullness, turning papery and faintly translucent over the knuckles and ribs, their joints giving off a dry, leathery creak when they rise. Even then, they never allow themselves to die in the damp lower galleries, where a motionless body might sour the air or stain the sealed equilibrium of the root-mat.

When their breath grows too shallow to keep up with the warmth, they climb. Slowly, pausing for long stretches on each tier, they ascend the ribbed columnar stalks, slipping past the crowded fan-fronds and out onto the sun-baked upper canopies—or up onto the exposed, knife-edged ridges of raw basalt high along the valley wall, directly beneath the incandescent gypsum vault. There, in the dry, featureless white blaze where no condensation ever gathers, they lie flat on a broad chalky shield or wedge themselves neatly into a dry fissure of stone, palms turned up, eyelids drawn shut.

What soil and worms do in another world, the relentless white heat does here. Gently, without rot, without smell, it draws every drop of moisture out through their skin and lifts it back into the valley’s closed air, where it will eventually bead along the shaded undersides of leaves below. What remains is weightless, chalk-white, and clean as salt—a dry husk that slowly frosts with a faint mineral sheen until you could walk right past it on a high ridge and mistake it for a tangle of calcified vines.

Only rarely, after such a quiet vacancy opens in the valley’s balance, do two dwellers converge deliberately at one of the warm, vine-bridged basins along the ridges. They arrive from opposite ends of the trough, drawn by angled shards left on the high crescents. For a stretch of time that neither measures, they break their solitude—sitting knee to knee in the pale green shade, smoothing fresh matte leaf-wax over the hollows of each other’s backs where solitary hands cannot reach, sharing the same folded cup of warm amber water. Children are rare as rain would be here; when one is born inside a resin-sealed bower, it emerges small, pale, and startlingly silent, uttering only faint dry clicks instead of cries. Carried slung flat against a narrow chest in a harness of braided pith, it learns before it can walk how to hold its breath when slipping past a trembling row of water droplets, and how to read the tension of a stem through the flat of its palm.

If you ever do see one of them clearly, it will not be because you hunted them down. It will be because you stopped moving long enough for the valley to forgive your noise.

To them, an outsider is a shocking, clumsy storm. Your boots scuff the matte wax off the braided floor and press wet bruises into the roots. Your chest heaves, churning the locked heat so droplets shake loose and clatter down the foliage behind you in a loud cascade of *tocks*. You carry surface grit in the seams of your clothes—foreign dust that does not belong in a place where stone has never weathered—and you move with a restless, hungry urgency, as though time were chasing you from behind.

They will not attack you. Violence would spill blood that has no soil to drink it and break stems that will never grow back. Instead, they step sideways into the crowded vertical galleries and wait, watching you with pale, pinprick eyes from six paces away, long fingers resting lightly against the ribbed stalks as if steadying the valley against your wake.

Only if you stand still—truly still, letting your shoulders drop and your breathing flatten to the breathless warmth—might one of them slip silently back into view. Not to greet you, and not to drive you out. They may simply glide past your boots without looking up, reach out a long, wax-dusted hand, and press a small bead of darkened resin over a stem your elbow accidentally nicked on the way down. Then, without a sound, they will slide sideways between two curtains of aerial roots and disappear into the pale green stillness, leaving you alone beneath the blinding white vault while the valley quietly closes back around your footprints and resumes its long, held breath.
