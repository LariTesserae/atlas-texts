If you meet one of the basin’s people close enough to see past the glare, the first thing you notice is that they never quite stand still—not out of restlessness, not like nervous travelers, but the way a boat never quite stands still at anchor. Their knees keep a permanent, almost imperceptible bend. Their weight sits low in the hips, distributed evenly across broad, flat-soled boots cut from the tough belly-hide of the pale wading-beasts. Even when a hauler stops to wait for a mirror-command, they do not lock their joints or plant their heels. To lock your knees is to make yourself a post, and a post drives straight through a tender crust.

Their faces are shaped by light the way sailors’ faces are shaped by wind. Beneath their eyes, the dark mineral soot is never entirely washed away; over decades, powdered bitumen and charcoal work themselves permanently into the pores, leaving broad, smoky smudges like healed bruises across their cheekbones. At the outer corners of their eyes, deep pale fans of creases are etched into chalk-dry skin from years of peering through the narrow slots of carved horn goggles. When they lift those goggles in the shelter of a felt awning, their gaze feels startlingly direct, almost unblinking—the eyes of people who spend their whole lives reading minute shifts in brightness and texture across miles of blank white ground.

They are not mute, though an outsider might spend a week among them and swear they had no tongues. Their throats work well enough. They simply treat sound as a form of weight, and weight is the one thing the basin will not forgive.

Silence is not an instinct here; it is an art taught before a child ever sets foot on the flats. High along the outer rim, where the ancient chalk ramps thicken into dry, slumped whalebacks that never liquefy, lie the loud terraces. There, in shallow stone hollows dug out of the dead marl, the basin’s infants spend their first three or four years. Up on the rim, the old ones whose ankles have stiffened beyond the glide-walk keep the smoldering bitumen pits, chip flint nodules washed down from the plateau, and raise the children who have not yet learned the price of noise. Up there, a baby is allowed to wail at the cold. Toddlers are allowed to laugh aloud, to fall on their backs, to drum their heels against the chalk and bang two limestone pebbles together just to hear the clack.

Then, slowly, the unlearning begins. A child of four is made to walk across a taut, dampened hide stretched over a wide clay dish of milky water; if the surface trembles, they turn back and begin again. They learn to pour their weight from the outer edge of the foot inward, toes tasting the ground before the heel ever arrives. They learn to cough into a folded wad of greasy felt pressed tight against the mouth, and to laugh by shaking their shoulders while expelling a single, silent rush of air through the nose. When a child can carry a brim-full bowl of brine across a freshly blistered pan of marl without spilling a drop or waking a crack, an elder carves their first horn goggles, rubs their cheekbones with soot, and leads them down off the ramps into the moving city. Most of them will not climb back up to the rim until their own knees fail them fifty years later.

Without voices to carry across the white expanses, their language has split into three distinct tongues, each built for a different distance.

The farthest is the flash-talk of the watch-towers—the staccato, blinding grammar of cold-hammered copper and split mica, punctuated by the sharp jerk of ochre, rust, and bitumen pennants. The signalers who hold the towers are a lean, stiff-necked brotherhood and sisterhood, perched three stories up on interlocking dry stone with their legs folded tight under heavy skirts. Their wrists move with a mechanical, twitching speed that looks frantic until you realize how calm their faces are behind the horn slits. Because every mirror-spark can be read by anyone within two miles, flash-talk has no privacy and no ornament. It has no word for *please* and no tense for regret. It speaks only of tilt, dampness, tonnage, herds, and direction.

Closer in, among the hauling teams and drying-yards, they speak with their hands, their chins, and their sleeves. Small discs of polished bone and dark horn are stitched to the cuffs and knuckles of their pale hide tunics so every flick of a finger cuts sharply against the bleached background. Two women prying apart a cistern wall will hold an entire running argument—about a lazy brother-in-law, about the price of condensed water, about whether a slab is cracked—without ever slowing their bone wedges, trading swift, clipped flicks of the left hand and sharp tilts of the brow while their right hands ease fifty pounds of limestone onto a rib-sledge.

And then there is the third tongue, kept for the dark and for grief and for love: skin-speech. Pressed close together under low canopies when the fierce night cold drops onto the basin, they talk by laying fingers against one another’s collarbones, wrists, and shoulder blades. A slow double-press on the sternum means *I am awake*. A quick, dry circle traced into a palm means *the floor is shifting; be ready*. Couples who have shared a sleeping-court for thirty years can conduct long, wry, tender conversations under a single felt blanket while their neighbors, two feet away in the dark, hear nothing but the faint rustle of breathing.

Because nothing in the basin is allowed to stay where it is put, their whole sense of what it means to own something has turned inside out. Nobody owns land; owning a patch of marl would be like claiming a patch of surf. What a family owns is stone.

Every household’s wealth is counted in known blocks—limestone slabs quarried generations ago from the dry outer ledges and worn buttery smooth by ten thousand dismantlings. On the flat, hidden underside of each block, invisible once it is laid in a course, tiny lineage-marks are scratched with a flint point: notched rings, paired crescents, stacked bars. A builder does not have to look at the mark to know her own stones. She knows them by the way they seat against her hip when lifted, by a tiny mineral thumb-hollow in the left corner, by the exact pitch of their weight. When a marriage is made, two households build a shared sleeping-court, alternating courses of both clans’ stone into a single low terrace. If the marriage sours, there is no shouting and no breaking of jars; they simply wait for the ground beneath them to soften, and when the bone wedges bite into the mortarless seams, each family slides its own blocks onto separate sledges and hauls them toward different quarters of the plain.

Even the roofs over their heads are inherited from their own bodies. With no sheep, no flax, no timber, and hairless herds, fiber is rarer than copper. Every clan’s dark, wind-dead felt canopies and thick hooded cloaks are felted across generations from the people’s own shorn hair, harvested steadily from the living and the dead, pounded together with tallow, bits of soft pale down gathered from the rim-nests of skimmers, and the coarse, pale bristles combed from the underbellies of the great wading-beasts. When a child lies down to sleep out of the glare, they pull the dark, grease-smelling hair of their grandmothers and uncles over their eyes.

They bury their dead the same way they build: by letting the basin take what it asks for. You cannot dig a deep grave in the flats without waking the quick-clay, and there is no wood to spare for pyres. So when an elder dies, the family strips away the horn goggles, the copper tally-chips, and the bone wedges, wraps the body tight in an old, frayed sledge-runner of hide, and carries it softly out to a swallow-pan—a stretch of marl that has already begun to lose its nerve, turning glossy and tacky under the glare. They lay the bundle flat on the pale skin of the earth, set two retired, cracked flagstones gently over the chest, and step back in a wide, silent circle.

The extra weight is all the crust needs. There is no splash, only a slow, wet sigh. Hairline cracks bloom outward from the wrapped shoulders; the white pan turns to shivering cream; the bundle tilts, settles, and is drawn straight down into the cold, mineral dark. Within twenty minutes, only a faint, smooth dimple remains. The basin’s salt-heavy mud preserves flesh and bone for centuries, so the living city glides perpetually across a vast, cold vault of its own ancestors, never marking a grave, never forgetting where they sleep.

Between the haulers and the towers move the people who listen to the floor.

They are called the sole-readers, though they listen with more than their feet. Most are women and men of middle years, lean and weather-cured, who walk the damp flats barefoot even when the chilled air makes the nostrils ache, the undersides of their feet toughened to pale, fissured horn. Over one shoulder each carries a long, curved rib from an old wading-beast, scraped smooth and hollowed slightly at the thick end. Every hour, a sole-reader will stop at the edge of an active district, plant the narrow tip of the bone gently into the marl, and press the broad end tight against the hinge of their own jaw.

Closing their eyes against the white glare, they let the basin speak straight into the bones of their skull. Solid chalk rings flat and dead. Dry crust over an empty pocket hums faintly, hollow as a dry gourd. And beneath that, when the cold artesian veins begin to stir, comes a slow, greased crawling—a low, liquid shiver that travels up the rib-bone hours before the first dome blisters upward or the first courtyard turns tacky. It is the sole-readers who catch the eye of a watch-tower and flick the sharp, downward palm-chop that means *unbuild now*, and it is they who venture a mile out across the blinding nothingness, testing the ghosts of ten-year-old plazas until their jaws catch the faint, rising thrum of a new seep about to break the skin.

When that seep finally weeps through the chalk, the people are rarely the first to reach it. They share the basin with an older, quieter citizenship, and their lives are braided tightly into its rhythms.

Down beneath the marl—deeper than any foundation ring, threading through miles of dissolved gypsum and cold water—lives the basin’s true monarch: a vast, ancient, pale web of subterranean fungus. It has no voice, no eyes, and no hurry. For years it sleeps as dry, chalk-colored threads locked in cold silt. The instant artesian water rises toward the light, it wakes, drinking the dissolved minerals and pushing thick, velvety knots and sulfur-yellow skins straight up through the fissures. Every living thing that walks, glides, or crawls across the white saucer is simply a harvester of that hidden orchard.

First to answer it are the flat-shelled grazers, who do not travel far because they never leave. During the dry seasons they tuck themselves beneath abandoned flagstones and inside hairline cracks, sealing the pale, segmented rims of their shells flush against the chalk so tightly they look like flakes of dead limestone. A single hour of damp air breaks their seal. They pour out by the tens of thousands like gravel sliding down a chute, their soft undersides rippling silently as tiny, file-like mouths shave the fresh yellow biofilm down to bare white rock.

Behind them come the great broad-footed beasts—both the wild herds that drift like pale clouds along the far edges of the basin, and the half-tame herds guided by the city’s silent walkers. Up close, they are strange, melancholy, brilliantly adapted things: low-slung and barrel-ribbed, their hairless skin hanging in pale, chalk-dusted folds like wet parchment, smelling sharply of cold lime and sour milk. Their long, spade-shaped snouts are plated in thick, pale horn for plowing fungal knots and buried grubs out of the half-liquid marl. Look down at their feet and you see why they are the lords of the quick-clay. Each foot spreads into a wide, leathery saucer edged with flexible, scalloped flaps; as the beast shifts forward, the rim rolls slightly inward, letting air slip beneath the pad so the foot lifts cleanly without a single wet *pop* of suction.

They have no bellow, no snort, no warning bark. Along the underside of their jaws hang loose, papery throat-sacs that flutter rapidly in silent tremor when a predator nears or a calf strays too far. The herders who live among them barely count as city-folk; they move in a slow, stooping drift beside the beasts, guiding them with flicks of black-dyed hide and gentle touches of long rib-staves against their shoulders. When the brutal twilight freeze descends, a herder does not bother building a stone windbreak. They simply lie down against the wide, salty flank of a resting cow, pull a felt cloak over their head, and let the slow, steady heat of a fermenting gut keep them alive until dawn.

Above the herds wheel the paper-white skimmers, silent as scraps of ash caught in an updraft. Their wings are narrow, almost translucent along the trailing edges, so that when they bank against the bleached dome of the sky they seem to vanish for half a second before reappearing as sharp white cuts. Their windpipes are smooth and voiceless, built purely for thin, efficient breathing during endless glides. The city’s hunters hold a strict taboo against netting them, except to collect molted feathers from their rim-scrapes; a sudden, frantic spiral of skimmers dipping through a rising mist of mute white midges is a signal clearer than any copper mirror, announcing a fresh seep three miles away.

And at the ragged edges of every bloom wait the salt-eyed stalkers.

They are the basin’s darkest mirror of the people themselves—creatures that have turned stillness into a blade. Long-spined and impossibly flat, pale as wet marl, they slide across half-liquefied mud on broad, ribbed bellies that distribute their weight like living sledges. Their flattened skulls carry two pale, glassy eyes set high on the crown, rimmed permanently with tiny crusts of dried salt so that when they bury themselves flush with a damp pan, they look like nothing more than two small, bright puddles of brine. They can lie motionless for a full day while the sun arcs across the white sky, letting mud-snails crawl across their snouts, waiting for a grazing calf, a wading bird, or a careless harvester reaching beneath a ledge for a basket of crustaceans. When they strike, it is a single, soundless uncoiling—jaws lined with fine, inward-curving needles that lock without a snap. Hunters prize them fiercely, setting silent sinew nooses along the damp margins; their tough belly-skin makes the quietest boot-soles in the basin, and their teeth, bound into bone handles, are the finest awls for stitching hide.

By late afternoon, the glare begins to lose its white edge, turning a thin, metallic silver as the sun drops behind the worn western whalebacks. That is when the basin shows its other face.

With no clouds, no wind, and no greenery to hold the day’s warmth, the cold falls into the hollow like a dropped stone. Within an hour of dusk, a fragile rime of salt-frost stiffens the damp marl, and the dry-stacked walls begin to emit tiny, sharp *ticks* as the limestone contracts. Overhead, the bleached dome turns into a vast, pitiless bowl of black, crowded with stars so sharp their reflections glint faintly off the drying brine pans.

Now the moving city folds inward. The hair-felt canopies are drawn down tight over their arched bone hoops and weighted along the hems with smooth stones. Outside every sleeping-court, tilted at a precise angle toward the freezing sky, stand the water-catchers—wide slabs of polished limestone propped on short bone struts. As the chill deepens, cold condensation beads thick along their shaded undersides and runs toward the lower lip. Even here, the people will not allow the sound of a falling drop; from the bottom edge of every slab hangs a braided wick of greased hair, dipping straight into the mouth of a wide clay jar. Drop by silent drop, the night’s drinking water slides down the hair without a single splash.

Inside the awnings, tiny wicks of twisted felt burn in cupped stones filled with rendered tallow and soft bitumen, throwing a low, amber light that smells of fat, cellar-fungus, and warm bodies. Here, at last, the horn goggles come off.

If you were to lift the flap of a canopy at midnight, you would hardly recognize the taut, gliding specters you saw hauling stone across the flats. With their hoods thrown back and their eyes wide and bare in the grease-light, they look intensely, almost startlingly alive. Their faces move constantly, unbound by the glare. A young hauler sits cross-legged, rubbing warm tallow into the bone runners of a sledge while grinning silently at his sister’s rapid finger-jokes. In the corner, an old sole-reader holds the hands of a five-year-old newly brought down from the loud terraces, pressing slow, deliberate shapes into the child’s small palms—teaching her the touch-names for the seven moods of mud: *asleep, tight, sweating, breathing, sour, hungry, gone*.

Beneath them, through the smooth flagstones and the thick felt mats, they can feel the basin settling into its cold night-stillness. Nobody speaks. Nobody sings. Yet the cramped, warm tent hums with an intimacy so dense you can feel it against your skin—the press of shoulders, the quiet slide of flint dressing a bone needle, the soft breath of a dozen people who know that the floor beneath their blankets is only lending them a place to lie until morning.

Long before the sun clears the eastern rim, a faint metallic pulse begins to shiver through the chilled air—not a sound, just a sharp white spark winking from a squat tower half a mile away, bouncing cleanly off the frost-rimed walls. A hand reaches out in the dark and presses two fingers firmly against a sleeper’s collarbone.

*Wake. South yard is sagging. West pan is weeping.*

No one groans. No one curses the cold. One by one, they tie the slotted horn over their eyes, pull the ancestral felt over their heads, and step out into the freezing silver dawn. Their knees bend softly as their soles meet the chalk. Within minutes, the bone wedges are biting silently into the smooth white seams, and the city is already flowing away across the floor, leaving behind nothing but a pale, flat ring in the mud—and the quiet certainty that nothing solid lasts, and that to live here is to be lighter than the ground you walk on.
