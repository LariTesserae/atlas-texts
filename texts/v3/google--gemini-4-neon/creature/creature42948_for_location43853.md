At first, from the upper switchbacks, it is hard to spot the people at all.

The scale of the terraces swallows them, and their oiled canvas coats—the color of wet slate, old tallow, or dark iron—melt straight into the masonry and the backs of the herds. You notice their traces before you notice their bodies: the faint satin polish along the granite stair-rails where generations of palms have slid; the thin blue haze of a brazing torch flickering in the ink-black shadow beneath a viaduct arch; a line of pale, steaming cloth strung deep inside a vaulted arcade. Only when your eye adjusts to the fierce glare off the wet stone do the small, upright shapes separate themselves from the slow tide of grazers—walking unhurriedly between the broad olive-brown flanks, leaning out from crane-cages slung over the mist-choked abyss, or squatting in pairs beside a flooded sluice-grate with long hooks and baskets.

They are human, though a thousand years of glare, warm rain, salt-haze, and chest-vibrating noise have shaped them as surely as the runoff has fluted the brick piers.

Watch how they move across an open plaza. Nobody hurries here. To run on limestone worn satin-smooth by a hundred thousand rasping tongues is to invite a shattered hip, and even the children learn by the age of five to drop their weight into their heels. Everyone walks with the same wide, low-centered, rolling gait—knees soft, soles planted flat in heavy hobnailed or rope-soled boots—shifting effortlessly from slick granite onto the damp, scarred dome of a stalled grazer and back to the curb without breaking stride. Their bodies tend toward the compact and heavy-boned: thick wrists, broad shoulders built for geared winches and iron valve-wheels, calves hardened by endless granite flights. Hands are square-nailed and permanently stained along the knuckles with white lime-dust, black machine oil, or the yellow-brown resin used to seal leather against the salt.

Their faces tell you how bright the plateau is. Even in youth, the skin around their eyes gathers into deep, permanent fans of creases from decades of squinting against the unfiltered reflection off wet rails and wet stone. Out on the open decks during the four-day glare cycles, almost everyone wears either a deep, stiffened hood of boiled hide or narrow slit-goggles carved from pale, translucent grazer-horn and tied behind the ears with gut cord. When they step under an arcade and push the goggles up onto their foreheads, you see eyes that are remarkably still and observant—accustomed to tracking subtle shifts in mist-density, gauging the sag of a percher-laden power cable, or catching a hand-signal from three terraces away.

Because out in the open, nobody wastes their throat on shouting.

The roar of the plateau—spillways, buried turbines, reciprocating pumps, millions of scraping tongues and breathing siphons—does not leave room for casual speech. Shout across a wet street and the sound shears away ten feet from your mouth, swallowed by the stone canyons. Instead, the people of the terraces speak with their arms, shoulders, and open palms. Every child grows up bilingual in voice and a broad, sweeping manual sign-language engineered to be read through drifting vapor, blinding glare, and heavy wet mittens. It uses large, unmistakable shapes—elbow angles, flat sweeps of the forearm, deliberate taps to the breastbone or collar—so a crane-operator suspended over the cliff-edge can tell a skip-driver two hundred yards back on the switchback that a calcareous slab is fracturing along a wet seam, or two friends passing on opposite sides of a moving grazer herd can share the news of a birth, a stuck sluice, and the price of fresh horn-oil without ever pausing.

When they do speak aloud outdoors, they step close—close enough to share the shelter of a rain-hood or lean into the lee of a brick pier—sometimes resting a gloved hand on the other person’s collarbone so the words carry through bone as much as air. They pitch their voices low and steady, instinctively slipping *under* the wet hiss of the grazers and *between* the six-second thud of the water pumps. And they listen with far more than their ears. Standing barefoot in a kitchen three streets back from the retaining cliff, an old woman can feel through the limestone floor if Turbine Six has developed a worn bearing; a tram-conductor knows a herd is shifting around a blind switchback by the faint change in tremor climbing up through the iron brake-lever. Many of the older quarrymen, jaw-millers, and sluice-tenders lose most of their high-frequency hearing by fifty, and nobody treats it as a tragedy. Their hands remain fluent, their feet still read the pumps, and they are sought out as night-watchmen and kiln-watchers because the endless din no longer tires them.

*

To understand how they think, you have to watch them wait.

A freight tram crawling along a contour-hugging terrace comes upon a dense jam of grazers wedged across a flooded intersection. The conductor sounds the low brass horn—two long, mournful notes that roll across the masonry tiers—and slows the electric car to a crawl while its rope-lined iron prow eases gently against the nearest olive-brown flank. It will take twenty-five minutes for the herd to pivot, clacking shell against shell, and yield the rails. Nobody curses. Nobody leans out the cab window waving a fist. The brakeman steps down onto the wet running-board, checks the grease-cups on the axles, and shares a wedge of smoked percher-breast with a passing wall-scaler who is waiting to cross the same track. Two passengers on the open rear bench bring out a slate board gridded with shallow holes and resume a slow game played with cast-lead pegs.

Impatience here is not merely useless; it is regarded as a mild sort of sickness, like a heat-rash or a bad cough. A person who rushes, snaps, or jerks at stuck machinery is called a *skitterer*—after the many-legged curb-scavengers that dart out beneath the chain-rakes—and is gently kept away from crane winches, busbars, and kiln dampers. The plateau rewards only weight, timing, and continuity. A lime-mason does not expect to build a retaining buttress alone; she lays four courses in her decade, beds them true in hot shell-mortar, and stamps her family’s copper mark into the wet joint so the mason thirty years later knows whose work they are standing on. Pride does not belong to what you invented from nothing—almost nothing here is made from nothing—but to what you have kept concentric, uncracked, and running smoothly across four generations.

There are no palaces on the terraces, no gilded spires, no idle classes lounging above the work. The sheer mass of the infrastructure forbids it; a single neglected settling basin or an unscraped transformer house can drown a district or black out twelve miles of freight line. Authority is therefore plain, local, and wedded entirely to load and water. Each tier and drainage sector answers to its Weir-Clerks, Load-Stewards, and Tier-Keepers—men and women chosen usually in middle age from the ranks of the pump-tenders, quarry-foremen, and line-walkers, people whose palms carry the calluses of the systems they balance. They meet in vaulted stone halls tucked directly behind the hydro-intakes, where the low thrum of the buried flywheels vibrates in the inkwells, arguing in quiet, stubborn voices over sluice-schedules, copper rations, the rotation of cliff-quarrying faces, and how many decades a viaduct pier can go before its brickwork must be re-jacketed.

Across the hundreds of miles of stepped stone, subtle tribes of trade and altitude have settled into place without ever hardening into sealed castes.

Out along the windward outer rims live the **Edge-Folk** and **Wall-Cutters**, perpetually dusted white with calcareous grit and smelling sharp of ozone and salt-mist. They work the towering crane-booms that reach down the vertical escarpments, lowering themselves in slatted iron cradles to drill and shear the deep, dead strata laid down by centuries of cliff-siphons. They are famously laconic, with wide-set eyes used to staring into the blinding cloud-floor where the unseen sea breathes upward, and they speak the crispest, widest-sweeping version of plateau hand-sign. It is also the Edge-Folk who tend the deepest winches—immense geared drums that lower heavy iron catch-baskets thousands of feet down into the luminous cloud-bank to lower storm-shelves and unseen mist-line ledges where, once or twice a season, great tangled bales of salt-bleached driftwood, gnarled roots, and coarse sea-soaked fiber are hauled up from the world below.

Timber and plant fiber are the plateau’s quiet luxuries. Nothing green grows on the sunlit stone, so every wooden paddle used by a switchgear-scraper, every ash hammer-haft, every bolt of coarse canvas or true coiled hemp is an heirloom or a precious import dragged up out of the fog. A wooden table is patched with brass splines and oiled like skin for two hundred years; broken tool handles are shaved down into spoons, weaving-shuttles, and hairpins until nothing remains but sawdust, which is mixed with tallow and burned as scented incense. Most daily cloth is not plant-spun at all, nor sheep’s wool—though people still call it *felt-wool* out of ancient linguistic habit—but a dense, grey-white, miraculously water-shedding textile carded and spun from the shed wing-filaments of the perchers, gathered by roof-walkers during the glare-days, boiled in hot lime-lye to strip the chalk, and woven tight on heavy iron looms.

Inland from the quarries, across the broad, sun-glaring middle terraces, live the dense majority: the **Kiln-Men**, **Vat-Boilers**, **Smelters**, **Horn-Carvers**, and **Tram-Crews**. Their lives follow the slow breathing of the lime-kilns and the long, multi-day creep of the freight convoys. The tram-crews form a roving community of their own, sleeping in narrow dry bunks built into the tail-cars, brewing thick marrow-broth on the electric resistance-heaters, carrying mail scratched onto thin slips of oiled horn, marriage-tokens, glazed tiles, and gossip across fifty miles of switchbacks.

And beneath them all, along the lower decks, settling canals, and hollow retaining cliffs, live the **Ditchers** and **Wheel-Keepers**. Pale-skinned from years spent in the ink-dark shadow under the arcades and inside the damp, echoing hydro-halls, they are quiet, fastidious people who dress in oiled leather aprons and carry brass-tipped sounding rods. They press one end of a rod against the bronze journal of a turbine shaft and grip the other between their teeth, eyes closed, reading the health of fifty tons of spinning iron by the hum in their skulls. Above ground, their kin manage the clanking chain-rakes, dredge the filter-worm mats, and tend the warm discharge pools.

*

None of these people think of themselves as masters of the animals around them, nor as guests intruding on a wild place. They share the plateau the way sailors share a ship with its rigging and its ballast—unsentimentally, intimately, with a deep, granular attention to habit and margin.

Take the cliff-siphons. To a stranger, those six-foot mats of ochre-and-plum calcareous chimneys encrusting the windward walls look like a monstrous infestation. To a tenement-dweller or a wall-keeper, they are living masonry, air-scrubbers, and slow ore-mines all at once. The Wall-Cutters never strip a cliff-face clean; they shear only the ancient, chalk-white basal strata while leaving the upper living colonies intact so the wall keeps thickening and pulling copper, zinc, and iron dust out of the mist for the next century’s smelters. In the older residential blocks built directly into the retaining walls, families drill narrow ceramic spigots straight through the masonry into the dead filtration-chambers behind a healthy siphon-colony; turning a brass cock gives them a steady, lukewarm trickle of water already strained free of salt-grit and guano, faintly mineral and sweet on the tongue. And everyone watches the siphons’ breathing. When the slow, rolling wave of dilating apertures across a factory wall falters or doubles its rhythm, householders close their wooden shutters and crane-crews lash down their booms—knowing hours before any barometer drops that a heavy, hot mist-bank is climbing the cliffs from the sea-floor.

With the grazers, the intimacy is almost domestic, though never soft. The herds are the city’s living street-sweepers; without those hundreds of thousands of calcified tongues continuously rasping down the amber-grey microbial film, the warm stone pavements would turn treacherous as greased glass within a week. Children perch unabashedly on the broad, domed backs of old bulls to cross flooded gutters, gripping the worn horn-ridges with their knees while the calf-sized beast plods calmly onward. Shopkeepers leave broken stoneware bowls of cool kiln-ash tucked into curb-corners where grazers like to rub their rubbery legs free of mites. Along the oldest avenues, certain immense, century-old grazers—recognizable by a quarry-chip in their shell or a pale ridge of scar-horn—have been circling the same four-block loop longer than any living person, and tram-drivers know them by plain, dry nicknames: *Notch-Back*, *Slant-Hood*, *Old Sump*. Yet when a grazer’s pads finally wear thin and it can no longer haul its shell up the curb-steps, there is no sentimentality. A renderer comes quietly with a heavy brass bolt-driver, dispatches it cleanly in a single stroke, and within an hour the carcass is on a skip bound for the vats—its dome sawn into translucent horn-plates for windows and lanterns, its fat rendered into thick lamp-tallow and cooking-grease, its tough meat boiled down for days into dark, iron-rich broth.

Even the perchers—whose chalky droppings turn the runoff milky and force every window-sill to be pitched sharply downward—are woven into the domestic calendar. When a strong midday thermal lifts an entire district’s roofs into the sky in a thunder of snapping leathery wings, pairs of agile roof-walkers climb out along the high parapets and insulator-beams with curved horn-knives and woven baskets. They clear clogged gutter-heads, gather the heavy, thick-shelled, intensely yolky eggs laid in the warm flue-angles, and sweep up drifts of shed wing-filaments for the looms. Down in the stone stairwells of the tenements, many families keep a single wing-healed or clipped percher roosting on a granite bracket near the stairwell drain. It is not a pet to be stroked—its slit-nostriled beak can take off a finger—but it keeps the stair-wells completely clear of thumb-thick millipedes and wet-shelled beetles, and its sudden, rattling clatter warns the whole landing whenever a stranger climbs up from the street or a bloated canal-salamander wriggles too far up an overflow pipe.

Down in the settling canals, the Ditchers have their own silent bargains. In the warm, churning discharge basins directly beneath the hydro-halls, blind, pale-skinned salamanders sometimes grow thick as a grown woman’s thigh. The turbine crews toss them trimmings from the rendering aprons and never let anyone spear them, because a pair of old basin-lords will keep the smaller segmented swimmers from matting against the intake bar-screens. Only the insects—the glistening black millipedes and wet-shelled beetles that pack in dark, motionless drifts around the warm steam-pipes and inside the brick transformer houses—are fought without truce. Every evening, as routine as locking a door, someone in every household runs a flat horn-scraper along the flue-joints and dusts the threshold with dry shell-lime. Even then, the apothecaries and boot-makers buy live millipedes by the clay jarful, pressing them into a sharp, blue-black oil that waterproofs stitched leather better than tallow and keeps iron bolts from pitting in the briny damp.

*

Step off the roaring, sun-blinded street, duck under a heavy bolted iron rain-hood, and push open a tenement door, and you enter the other half of the plateau’s soul.

The first room is always the *drip-lock*—a narrow stone vestibule with an iron bar-grate floor over a running drain, stout bronze pegs for dripping canvas coats, a grooved limestone block for scraping boots clean of microbial slime and guano, and a wide glazed crock of dry, warm lime-powder to rub between wet fingers and ankles. Beyond the drip-lock sits a second, thicker door lined around its edges with braided percher-felt. Pull it shut on its heavy brass pivots, and the world drops by half its volume.

Inside the vaulted masonry chambers cut deep into the retaining walls, the fierce glare and the soaking damp are gone. The ceilings are low, sweeping barrel-vaults of lime-washed brick, glowing softly with the amber light of tallow lamps shaded behind paper-thin, oiled sheets of grazer-horn. Because there is no fuel-wood to burn in open hearths, warmth arrives straight from the industrial belly of the city: thick cast-iron exhaust flues and hot return-mains from the pumping stations and kilns run directly through the rear walls of the tenements, jacketed in heavy glazed ceramic tiles that radiate a dry, steady, enveloping heat day and night. Families build their recessed sleeping-bunks, their cooking-niches, and their curved stone benches right up against these warm walls. Laundry hangs outside in the drafty covered arcades above the exhaust vents, where the salt-mist cannot reach it, but fine garments, infant wrappings, and precious horn-tools are kept inside in deep, lime-lined wall-cupboards.

In a world stripped entirely of green, the interiors explode with deliberate, fierce color.

The plateau’s smelters pull trace copper, iron, zinc, cobalt, and manganese out of the quarried siphon-strata, and the people pour those mineral pigments into ceramic glaze. Where a forest-dweller might hang woven tapestries or set cut flowers in a jar, a plateau household covers its interior arches, flue-jackets, and window-reveals in dazzling, fire-hardened tiles: deep peacock verdigris, glowing oxblood red, furnace yellow, midnight cobalt, and iridescent copper-green that catches the lamplight like wet oil. A couple’s wedding gift from their kin is rarely coin; it is a crate of hand-glazed tiles stamped with intertwined work-marks, enough to face the warm wall beside their sleeping alcove, alongside a pair of thin-carved horn lanterns and a seasoned bone-handled ladle.

The air inside smells rich, savory, and wonderfully clean compared to the briny musk outside: warm clay, rendered fat, roasted salt, copper, and slow-simmering pots.

Without grain, fruit, leaf, or root, their cuisine has evolved into an extraordinary art of textures, ferments, broths, and minerals. On the back of the flue-heated stone hob, a heavy glazed pot of *base-stock* simmers perpetually—grazer marrow, charred bone, canal-salamander ribs, and dried filter-worm strips reduced to a dark, glossy jelly that coats a spoon. Bread does not exist; in its place are dense, tangy, golden-grey cakes baked in flat ceramic pans from cultured *stone-curd*—a domesticated strain of the same warm amber-grey microbial film the grazers rasp from the streets, grown cleanly in dark limestone trays fed with strained siphon-water, then pressed, salted, and griddled in tallow until the crust blisters crisp. Beside the curd-cakes sit bowls of pickled siphon-mantle (sliced thin before calcification, chewy, briny, and bright with a sharp sourness that cuts the heavy fat and keeps scurvy at bay), roasted percher eggs cracked open and smeared with dark fermented beetle-paste, and small clay cups of warm, lightly spiced *siphon-liquor* drawn fresh from the inner glands and brewed into a tart, slightly fizzing tonic. Eating is slow, hot, and communal—dipped with horn spoons, wiped clean with griddled curd-cakes, accompanied by quiet talk pitched comfortably against the faint, maternal purr of the distant turbines.

*

If you ask someone born on the terraces what luxury feels like, they will not describe gold, or silk, or idle days. They will describe **dryness and stillness**.

The most cherished spaces in every district are the **Dry-Rooms**—small, thick-walled, double-vaulted chambers built intentionally into the dead core behind the great lime-kilns and condenser stacks. Here, through clever stone baffling and dry radiant heat, the humidity drops away until paper does not curl, salt stays loose in the bowl, and the endless plateau roar softens to a distant, velvet hush. Every neighborhood maintains at least one public Dry-Room, kept spotless by rotation. People slip inside without their boots, dressed in light felted tunics, simply to sit for an hour on the warm glazed benches and feel the perpetual damp evaporate from their joints and hair. Old people go there to soothe aching knees; elders teach children to write cleanly on precious imported parchment without ink bleeding; lovers whisper without having to touch foreheads; musicians bring their most delicate instruments—hollowed horn-resonators strung with twisted gut, tuned slabs of ringing calcareous siphon-stone, and small bronze bowls—to play slow, droning airs structured in cycles of six or eight beats, unconsciously matching the heartbeat of the great water pumps below.

Outdoors, their music is built on the scale of the geology itself. Each district mounts great water-driven brass pipe-chimes along its main spillways, tuned to massive open fifths and octaves that blend with the low hum of the buried hydro-turbines rather than fighting it. Every freight-line carries a distinct two-note horn signature, so even when a three-day bank of lukewarm mist drops over the switchbacks and reduces visibility to six feet, anyone standing under a rain-hood can listen to the mournful bronze echoes rolling off the concrete tiers and tell instantly whether the lime-convoy from Rim Seven is passing, or the copper-skip returning from the central smelters.

They do not have gods of harvest, forest, or storm. When they think about the larger shape of things, their minds move vertically—down into the unseen warm sea breathing endlessly below the luminous cloud-floor, and outward across the centuries of stone and living shell holding them aloft. They call the hidden ocean simply *the Breath*, and speak of it with the quiet, matter-of-fact gratitude you might feel for a tireless engine buried deeper than any blueprint reaches. If a visitor from some other world tried to describe a forest to a plateau-born child—tall green things rooted in soft dark dirt, shedding dead leaves every autumn—the child would listen politely, picturing a towering, strangely fragile colony of cliff-siphons made out of priceless paddle-wood, and ask how the people kept them from blowing off the escarpment, and what they scraped off the streets to keep from slipping.

Even in death, nobody leaves the metabolism of the plateau.

There is no earth to dig a grave into, and on a warm, wet shelf crowded with scavengers, flesh cannot be left idle. Instead, the plateau keeps two ancient customs, chosen quietly by a person while they still have strength in their hands.

Some choose the **Kiln**. After the washing and the slow singing in the Dry-Room, the body is given to the hottest lime-furnace on the terrace. The clean white bone-ash is gathered by the family, mixed with local cobalt or copper-oxide glaze, and fired into a single heavy, luminous ceramic brick stamped with the person’s name, dates, and trade-mark. That bright tile is mortared directly into the heated rear wall of the family’s living vault or the covered neighborhood arcade—so that for generations afterward, descendants brushing past on a wet morning can lay a cold palm against their grandmother’s blue-green glaze and feel the steady industrial warmth of the city radiating right through her.

Others—mostly the Edge-Folk, the crane-workers, and the old wall-scalers—choose the **Quiet-Walls**. Along certain windward outer escarpments, stretches of ancient limestone buttress have been set aside since the first masonry was laid, marked across their crowns with broad bands of glazed oxblood tile so no quarry-crane will ever lower a shear-blade near them. Here, in the fierce white glare and the salt-haze climbing from the sea, the dead are wrapped tightly in oiled filament-cloth weighted with lime-stones and lowered on ropes into shallow niches cut directly into the wet rock face. Within months, the living ochre-and-plum cliff-siphons creep across the shroud; within a decade, the pale calcareous chimneys have fused smoothly over the niche, breathing in the warm mist, exhaling glistening trickles of filtered water, and sealing the bones forever inside living, growing stone.

On quiet evenings, when the high sun sinks toward the bright cloud-floor far beyond the plateau’s rim and turns the rising vapor the color of heated brass, you can see people standing along the granite parapets above those windward walls. Strings of caged incandescent lamps are flickering on behind them along the viaducts and canals, gleaming double in the flooded streets; overhead, the sky-flocks are settling back onto the sagging copper cables in dark, muttering garlands; below their boots, the wet, sandpapery scrape of millions of grazing mouths continues without pause into the night. The watchers lean their broad forearms on the warm, glossy stone, goggles pushed up on their foreheads, hands resting easily beside one another, listening through their soles to the slow, unhurried heartbeat of the pumps—not separate from the stone, the engines, or the crowded living hides around them, simply the upright, attentive layer that keeps the spillways clear and the lamps lit while the whole immense shelf breathes on into the dark.
