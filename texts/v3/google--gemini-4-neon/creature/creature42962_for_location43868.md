If you stay on the floor long enough—longer than the people from the upper country ever stay, past the hour when the chill works into your knuckles and knees—the geometry of the rubble begins to come slightly undone.

At first it is nothing you could point to. The pewter light does not shift. The surf-like roar of the bats and swiftlets keeps its exact, heavy pitch overhead. Across a hundred meters of angular dolostone, the fallen blocks remain as crisp and sharp-edged as the day the vault gave way. Then, in the dark V-shaped trough between two barn-sized slabs, a ridge of damp charcoal stone lifts an inch, pauses, and unfolds. A faint, dry crackle—like eggshell giving way—breaks along its back and vanishes instantly into the din of wings.

Nothing has been built here because the ones who truly belong to this valley have never needed a wall, a roof, or a smoothed path. They do not alter the wreckage of the hall; they fit into the seams it left behind.

Stretched full length, one of them would measure as long as a tall man, though it almost never stands upright. Its body is lean, broad-shouldered, and slung low between four limbs of nearly equal reach, built not for running over flat ground but for bridging the raw gaps between tilted slabs. It moves with an unhurried, unsettling precision. Three points settle before the fourth lifts; each hand—wide, dry-palmed, ending in four long fingers tipped with thick, horn-gray plates rather than claws—tests a loose wedge of scree with a feather-light pressure before committing weight. Where a harvester from the plateau sends sharp clatters skittering across the talus, this creature crosses fifty meters of fractured dolostone without making a single shard speak.

Its hide has the matte, light-drinking dullness of the valley itself: slate along the ribs, umber in the hollows of the flanks, darkening to damp charcoal wherever seepage wets it. Because it spends days at a time motionless in the troughs of the rubble or tucked beneath dripping overhangs, the mineral patience of the cave writes itself onto its skin. Thin, pale rimes of dissolved lime crust the sharp ridge of its spine, the knobby points of its elbows, and the bony shelf above its brow. On older individuals that favor the margins beneath the high ceiling rifts, faint stains of velvet-dark moss or flat liverwort sometimes take root in the rough creases between their shoulder blades—no wider than a thumbprint, no thicker than a smear of damp dust, just enough to erase the last boundary between flesh and fallen stone.

The head is broad, flat, and slightly wedge-shaped, carrying no outer ears. In a hall saturated from floor to vault with the ceaseless pressure of millions of throats and wings, open ears would be a burden. Instead, recessed membranes lie tight beneath the hinges of its jaw, and the real organ of its hearing is its own skeleton. When it wants to read the valley, it flattens its bare sternum, forearms, and chin against a massive shelf of limestone. Through solid rock, the airborne static of the ceiling drops away into a mild, distant fuzz, and the wide floor becomes extraordinarily crisp. It can feel the hollow tick of seepage drops twenty meters off, the skitter of a lean civet crossing a shelf of scree, the dry rustle of harvestmen shifting in a crevice, and—from half a kilometer away—the heavy, clumsy thud of boots descending the natural ledges from the upper world.

Its eyes, set wide beneath the lime-dusted brow, look like the valley's own frigid rock pools: shallow, clear, pewter-colored, rimmed in dark skin and covered at long intervals by a translucent gray membrane that wipes away the fine ammonia dust drifting off the roosts. They do not shine back the dim light. Unless one blinks, you can look straight at its face from ten paces and see only two damp pockets of rainwater caught in a gray slab.

They are cold-blooded—or so slow-burning that their bodies match the cool, unchanging middle ground of the air—and they live at the pace of the cavern rather than the pace of the sun. Where the guano mounds churn in frantic, warm decay, teeming with beetles and pale crickets, these residents keep to the clean, stark edges. They dislike the soft, shifting footing of the mounds. They hunt the margins where naked rubble meets the dark droppings, or where seepage pools pool against the base of a talus slope.

For hours, even days, one may lie folded inside a sharp cleft near a pool, limbs tucked high and angular like broken stone, waiting. When a slender cave snake glides out of a groove, or an exhausted horseshoe bat drops out of the upper gyres and flutters weakly onto the scree, the stillness snaps. A single long arm shoots out with startling, economical speed. The grip is silent and exact. The prey is taken cleanly behind the head before it can cry out or coil, carried back onto bare, dry-topped rock, and eaten with unhurried neatness—often leaving behind nothing more than a tidy pinch of delicate wing-bones that a passing harvester will assume fell straight from the roof. They scoop blind amphipods from the frigid water with cupped palms, pluck pale cave crickets off wet vertical rock, and tear apart the pale mushrooms that sprout around the older, compacted rims of the guano hills. One decent meal—a large cave-nesting bird knocked from a low ledge, or a pair of snakes—will hold them in quiet, mineral stillness for a week or more.

Because no seasons turn down here, and because the slow subterranean breath drifts always in the same direction, their sense of the valley is built along permanent lines of scent and stone rather than calendars. They rest always with their flat nostrils turned upwind into that steady drift. Every kilometer of the hall carries its own unvarying ribbon of odor: the sharp ammonia of the third chimney, the wet chalk of the northern wall, the cold iron of exposed chert beds, the faint fungal sweetness of aging guano. Even the shift from day to night overhead registers to them less as light than as a subtle change in the texture of the overhead roar, as swiftlets settle into the fractures and the bats spill out to replace them in the upper air.

Yet they are not mindless beasts, drifting blindly inside the cavern's equilibrium. In a place where nothing rots quickly, nothing freezes, and nothing grows taller than a handspan, they have found a quiet way of watching time move.

Beneath certain overhangs where seepage water falls at slow, measured intervals, you may sometimes notice a small object cemented to the floor beneath a pale crust of lime: a clean swiftlet skull, the curved rib of a cave snake, a glossy black splinter of chert, or even a notched bone gorge lost years ago by a harvester from above. A human passing by would think it washed there by chance and calcified where it lay. It did not. The residents set these things under the drips deliberately, wedging them into shallow cups of dolostone right where the water strikes. They do not arrange them in circles or rows, nor build cairns beside them; there is no pattern visible to an eye looking for architecture. Each one simply knows where it has left a handful of small markers across its stretch of scree. Years later—ten, twenty, forty years, measured only in the slow shedding of their own lime-crusts—they return, crouch beside the drip, and run the calloused pads of their fingers over the pale mineral skin that has grown around the bone or chert. To feel a razor-sharp black flake softened into a smooth, chalky tongue of stone is to know that the valley is not frozen, that patience is still accumulating, drop by quiet drop.

There are not many of them—perhaps a dozen, perhaps a score, spaced widely across the miles of fractured floor, each holding a broad, invisible tract of scree and overhang. Most of the time they live alone, separated by hundreds of meters of naked rubble. Yet they are rarely out of reach of one another.

Sometimes, when the upper world overhead slips into deep winter and the mist choking the ceiling rifts thickens by a shade, or simply after a long stretch of quiet, one of them will flatten its chest and throat firmly against a broad, unbroken shelf of dolostone and let out its voice. It makes almost no sound in the air—nothing that could compete with the roar of the bats. Instead, a ribbed sac along its throat produces a deep, slow, pulsing thrum that passes straight into the bedrock. The vibration travels underneath the airborne noise for half a kilometer or more, riding the faulted limestone until another of its kind, resting in a crevice across the valley, feels the rhythm wake in its jawbone and ribs.

What passes between them through the stone is neither human language nor simple animal warning. It is a slow, low music of weight and condition: the pulse of presence, the notice that a high wall has begun to seep faster after rain on the plateau, the report of a roost shifting chimneys, the steady rhythm of one long life answering another across the dark.

Every few years, two of them will cross the open bare-stoned expanses between the guano hills and meet. They do not circle or posture. They crouch facing one another in the flat pewter gloom, lift their wide, four-fingered hands, and press them palm to palm, fingers interlocking loosely along the ridges of tough skin. They lean forward until the flat sides of their angular heads touch, sharing a soft, bone-deep hum that neither the owls nor the bats can hear. Then, with calm, meticulous attention, they use the horn-plated tips of their fingers to pry away the over-thick plates of lime crust that have built up along the hollows of each other's spines and shoulders—places a solitary body cannot reach—before parting again across the sharp stone.

Their young are rare, born only once in many years when the cavern's slow abundance leaves room. A young one looks startlingly fragile against the harsh floor: smaller, paler, its hide smooth umber and clean slate without a trace of lime or moss, its limbs thin and spider-long. For its first years it moves with an almost frantic curiosity that looks absurd beside its elder's stillness, slipping on loose chert shards and flinching whenever a large cave-bird booms from the upper ledges. The elder teaches it not by sound, which the roar would swallow, but by weight and touch—pressing a heavy, cool palm between the young one's shoulder blades to flatten it into a crevice until a distant footstep passes, or tapping a dry knuckle lightly against a tilted slab to show the difference between solid bedrock and a loose wedge that will clatter underfoot.

Nothing stirs their quiet attention more than the people who climb down through the upper fissures.

They know the harvesters are coming long before a human boot touches the valley floor. The steady subterranean breath carries the shock of the outside world ahead of them—bright, violent smells that have no place beneath the limestone: sun-dried grass, wind-scoured wool, woodsmoke, resin, sweat, and the fierce, rich iron of warm blood. Then come the vibrations through the rock: heavy, unsteady, rapid, scattering loose dolostone in sharp ticks that ring along the shelves.

The residents never confront them and never flee in panic. They simply lower themselves into the crisp angles of the nearest fallen blocks, pull their elbows in, let their lime-rimmed backs match the broken rock, and watch. From thirty meters away, completely invisible in the shadowless dimness, they follow the two small figures moving along the foot of a talus slope with woven baskets on their backs and slender ash poles over their shoulders. To the cold, slow-burning watchers in the scree, the visitors seem astonishingly loud, dazzlingly hot, and weirdly hurried—creatures that burn through an entire errand in a single fraction of a gloom and scramble back toward the fissures before the cool air has even settled into their joints.

When the harvesters work an exposed seam of black chert, each blow of the hammerstone sends a bright, crystalline shockwave humming straight through the limestone into the watchers' ribs. When they burn a handful of drifted twigs brought from above, the sharp scent of live smoke slices across the entire downwind half of the valley, making every resident within a kilometer lift its head into the drift.

And after the visitors have shouldered their baskets of nests, chert blades, and mushrooms and vanished back up into the damp-walled fissures, the valley's true inhabitants unfold from the rubble and come down to examine what those quick hands left behind.

One will crouch beside the flat-topped boulder where a harvester sat to knap stone, laying its cold, dry palm flat against the rock just to feel the last faint ghost of body heat bleeding away into the cool limestone. It will lower its nostrils to the small smear of cold gray twig-ash, inhaling the strange, bitter trace of fire—a thing that cannot exist here on its own. Farther on, it will pause before the long, fire-hardened ash pole wedged upright in a crack near a guano mound, sliding its long fingers gently up the smooth, straight wood, marveling at a material taller than any fern in the valley and bound in dried sinew that smells of sun-baked hides. It never breaks the pole or drags it off; it leaves it standing precisely as it was left, knowing the warm ones will return for it in another stretch of glooms.

What holds it longest, though, is the battered river cobble resting beside the neat crescent of freshly struck black chert waste flakes.

In a subterranean valley newly broken and untouched by rushing water, where every slab is cleaved crisp and every shard holds a razor edge, a water-worn cobble is an impossible object. The resident lifts the stone in both wide hands, turning it slowly in the flat pewter light, drawing the pads of its thumbs across a surface with no corners, no fracture planes, no sharpness anywhere—a stone shaped by violence and speed it cannot picture. Sometimes it will take a single discarded chert flake from the crescent, drawn by the clean, glassy black edge that catches the seepage like dark water, and carry it off to place beneath a favorite lime-drip. The smooth river cobble it always sets carefully back beside the boulder, right where the harvesters left it.

Perhaps the people of the upper country half-sense them, without ever naming what they sense. It may be why grandparents tell the young never to shout over the roar, never to linger once the chill reaches the knees, never to take every flake they strike, and never—if a dark angle among the barn-sized boulders seems to shift in the corner of an eye—to walk over to look more closely.

When one of the residents grows very old—after a century or more of quiet equilibrium—its movements slow until the pale lime seals across the bends of its knees and spine faster than it cares to crack it free. Its moss stains widen slightly across its damp shoulders. It stops hunting the margins of the guano mounds and stops answering the low stone-borne thrums from across the valley. At the last, it crawls into a deep, cupped hollow beneath a persistent seepage wall, far from the churning beetles and the harvesters' routes, folds its long limbs neatly along the fresh cleavage lines of the dolostone, and presses its chin to the floor.

The cool air keeps decay almost absent. Water glides downward from the bedding planes as a cold black sheen, gathers along the overhang, and falls onto its motionless back at the same measured intervals. Decade after decade, the dissolved lime builds a thin, pale crust over its ribs, its long fingers, its flat angular skull, smoothing them gradually into the shelf beneath. By the time the next generation of harvesters climbs down through the fissures with their woven baskets and ash poles, even its own kind, passing through the dimness, can scarcely tell where the elder ended and the valley floor began.

So if you descend through the damp fissures today and stand scanning the miles of sharp rubble beneath the unbroken roar of wings, your eyes will tell you the same quiet truth they have always told: no one lives down here, and nothing has ever been built. Yet the broken stone beneath your boots carries the tremor of every step you take, and somewhere across the pewter gloom, folded cleanly into the angles of the rock, cold eyes watch your back as you turn toward the ledges—waiting with the patience of dripping water for the last warmth of your hands to fade from the stone.
