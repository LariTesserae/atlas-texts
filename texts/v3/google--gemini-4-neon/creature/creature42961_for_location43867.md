You do not see them until you stop trying to swim.

As long as you kick, turn your head, or reach for a terrace rail to steady yourself, the hamlet stays empty. Your own motion makes too much noise—not sound, which the cold water kills within a few paces, but disturbance: the brief collapse of stillness at your heels, the nervous sweep of your gaze across the violet pinpricks. Only when you let your limbs go slack, hang suspended in the snowmelt dark, and wait long enough for the last shiver of your arrival to vanish does the eye begin to separate one kind of vertical line from another.

Then you notice that the slender shape beside the numbered plinth on the lower terrace is slightly too narrow at the waist to be an anchor-pylon. Across the avenue, seated on a low bench inside an open gallery, a pale crescent turns a fraction of an inch—not swiftly, not in alarm, simply rotating into the bruised amber glow of a threshold marker so that it can regard you straight on.

They have been here the entire time. There are perhaps forty of them in the whole basin, spaced as generously as the towers themselves, rarely gathering more than two or three to a terrace.

At a distance they look like pieces of the architecture that have decided to drift. Each is draped from collarbone to ankle in a single seamless garment of the same matte-dark, light-drinking material as the walls—dry to the touch even submerged, clinging nowhere, falling in stiff, clean planes that refuse to ripple. Because there is no current and they never move fast enough to wake the fluid around them, their robes hang like cleaved slate, holding sharp vertical folds even when the wearer is suspended twenty meters above the basalt crags.

Only their faces, narrow hands, and bare feet are uncovered. Their skin is the color of frost-clouded quartz, smooth and entirely hairless, stripped of flush or blemish. Looking closely, you find no sign of the soft, hurried machinery of warm-blooded life—no pulse visibly beating in the throat, no chest rising and falling, no stream of bubbles ever escaping their lips. Along the hollow beneath each jawbone, fine pale slits barely thicker than pencil lines part and close once every forty or fifty seconds, drinking dissolved oxygen from the motionless cold with an economy that looks almost like abstention. Their eyes are wide, dark, and unblinking, nearly all pupil, rimmed with a faint silver-indigo ring that gathers the fixed star-caustics overhead. When one of them looks at you from the far side of a colonnade, you see only two dim reflections of the nearest amber beacon, steady as the mirror-surfaces of the sunken pools.

They are roughly human in scale, though lengthened and pared down, as if decades of constant neutral pressure had coaxed their spines and fingers into a slightly taller, finer geometry. Their hands are especially telling: long-palmed, tapered, with broad, flat pads at the fingertips and almost no callus. Nothing in their city has a handle, a latch, or a grip. Their hands were not shaped for seizing things; they were shaped for resting flat and waiting.

Watch one approach an archive vault lower on the fractured ridge. It does not swim with its legs. Instead, it tips forward a few degrees from a hovering crouch, shifts some quiet internal ballast deep in its torso, and glides across ten meters of black water without stirring a thread of wake behind its heels. Reaching the flush mineral door, it stops a handspan away, raises both pale palms, and lays them softly against the dark rock. For a long time nothing happens. To a hurried visitor, the figure might seem to be praying, or frozen in place. Then the mineral yields—recognizing a pressure patient enough to belong to it—and slips back around the inhabitant’s narrow shoulders like cold gelatin, sealing behind its matte-dark hem without a scrape or a bubble.

They use the staircases the same way—not because weight demands it, for weight barely exists here, but because a staircase gives measure to an otherwise featureless dark. You may watch one of them descend thirty steps of a free-hanging stair, bare soles touching each crisp tread in an unhurried, exact cadence, counting time through the contact of heel and edge; and then, reaching the flat, rail-less landing at the bottom, simply step out into forty meters of open water and continue on the same level line toward the next tower. Without currents, without weather, without the rise and fall of a sun, a mind left unanchored in uniform cold would blur at the edges. The benches, the numbered plinths, the clean right angles of the colonnades are instruments for keeping a body located—for reminding the self where it begins and where the water takes over.

When you stay long enough to follow their routines, you begin to see what occupies them.

Much of their life turns around the recessed alcoves and the shallow rectangular basins cut into the public plazas. At intervals that seem governed by no clock you can name, an inhabitant enters an open gallery, runs a slow finger along a shelf of the pale, unmarked plates, and lifts one free. Cradling it level in both hands—never tilting it, never hurrying—they drift out into the plaza and kneel at the rim of one of the sunken pools, where the second, heavier liquid rests beneath the water with its own taut, mirror-flat boundary.

Slowly, they lower the plate through that invisible floor until it settles into the denser lens below.

Only there does the plate stop being blank. Through the clear, heavy medium, faint hairline patterns wake inside the pale mineral—not ink, not script, but ghostly filaments of refraction and internal stress, like frost-veins trapped inside clear glass, catching the dim violet light from the corner pylons and throwing delicate inverted webs back up against the water’s dark ceiling. The inhabitant leans close above the boundary—never breaking it with their face, keeping their chin a careful finger’s width above the meniscus—and reads.

What is stored in those plates is never spoken aloud, and perhaps could not be translated into speech even if speech carried here. Some seem to hold exact maps of the frozen star-caustics from decades when the constellations sat a fraction of a degree differently against the mountain crown. Others trace the hairline angles of the freshly cleaved basalt below, recording every crystal facet and conchoidal scar before a single edge can ever be dulled. Still others appear to record nothing material at all—only pressure curves, the infinitesimal drift of the spatial seams, or faint, distilled geometries taken long ago from the world beyond the clouds, stripped of heat, weather, and decay until only their clean proportions remain.

Now and then, an inhabitant carries a small stylus of black volcanic glass to the edge of a pool and rests its tip against a submerged plate for an hour without moving, letting some almost imperceptible warmth or electrostatic charge pass from their body into the mineral, adding a single new hairline to the pale surface before lifting it gently back into the ordinary water and returning it to its numbered alcove.

They tend the seams with the same absorption.

Along the broad avenues where space has been stitched slightly out of true—where a distant amber beacon jumps a hair’s breadth sideways as you cross—you will sometimes find two of them suspended face to face, a meter apart, straddling the invisible join. From the side, their silhouettes appear faintly sheared, a shoulder offset by a millimeter from the ribcage below it. They hold nothing between them, or only a slender filament pinched between thumb and forefinger, adjusting their own stillness until the faint optical snag smooths out—or shifts precisely where they want it to sit. They seem to perceive these seams not as flaws in the district, but as dry mortar, or as shutters. A hallway that appears empty from one angle opens, when approached across the stitch at the right slow pace, onto a quiet inner chamber that does not show itself on any exterior wall. They slip into these folded pockets the way other people step behind curtains, vanishing without a ripple into a thickness of space that refuses to appear on the far side of the tower.

And always, through every task, they are listening.

Not with their ears alone. Deep within their skulls and along the narrow bones of their wrists, fine mineral deposits have grown over their long, cold lives—dense, crystalline structures tuned to the architecture’s quiet rules. When an inhabitant drifts close to a civic anchor-pylon, they do not merely tolerate the faint pressure-hum felt behind the teeth; they lean a shoulder near it and close their eyes, taking the measure of the basin’s charge the way a watchmaker holds a casing to the cheek.

When that single tiny *tick* travels down from high near the roof of the water—sharp and pure as a needle touching quartz, where the containment adjusts against the thin night air outside—every inhabitant in the hamlet pauses at once. A hand lifting a pale plate halts mid-motion. A figure walking a colonnade stops with one heel lightly lifted. Even forty meters apart, separated by dark masonry and empty gulfs of cold sea, they register the tick in the same instant, tilting their heads a fraction of a degree upward toward the fixed silver-indigo webs of starlight, holding still for ten heartbeats until the quiet settles back into its groove. Then, without a glance at one another, they resume.

They do not speak to one another in the water. Vibrations die too quickly here, and anything loud enough to travel across a plaza would feel, to them, like a wound.

Instead, their companionship is built out of alignment and touch. Two inhabitants who wish to share an hour will simply drift into the same gallery and sit at opposite ends of a low bench, angled toward the same dim marker, matching the slow cadence of their hidden breathing. That shared stillness is not indifference; it is the basin’s version of conversation, a quiet acknowledgement that another mind is holding its coordinates beside yours in the dark.

When something exact must pass between them, they draw close enough to lay the backs of their wrists together, or press their bare palms flat against the same numbered plinth, letting solid bone and solid mineral carry what the damped water will not. Through that contact pass minute, crisp pulses—barely audible clicks, faint shifts of pressure, subtle electrostatic tremors that travel straight up the forearm and behind the teeth. An entire exchange of warning, memory, or tenderness might consist of six faint taps spaced across half an hour, leaving the surrounding water wholly undisturbed. Sometimes, when one finishes reading a particularly dense plate at a sunken pool, a second will glide silently behind them and rest two fingertips lightly against the matte-dark cloth between their shoulder blades, staying there until the plate is lifted and the light beneath them goes dark again.

Toward the few other living things in the basin, they show a quiet, unsentimental kinship, recognizing fellow citizens of the cold.

Down along the razor-crested basalt, where the pale, spindly crawlers pick their way on needle-fine legs from crystal facet to crystal facet, an inhabitant inspecting an anchor-tether will pause motionless for twenty minutes to let a finger-long crawler finish scraping trace minerals from a seam, refusing to cast even a shift of pressure across its path. They listen to the dry microscopic clicks rising from the rock below as a reassuring proof that the bedrock remains raw and unsoftened.

Higher up, near the anchor-pylons, the long translucent ribbons of living tissue often drift within a hand’s reach of an inhabitant who has lingered too long in contemplation. Every so often, a ribbon loosens one tapered edge from a filament and lets it trail lightly across an inhabitant’s bare wrist, sending a slow, pale blue pulse down its length that dims into the pale skin. The inhabitant never pulls away. In a world stripped of heat, that brief flicker of borrowed field-energy against the pulse-less wrist is the closest thing to a caress.

Only when one of the chalk-pale mantles passes through the open dark between the upper towers do the inhabitants look genuinely awed. Whenever that two-meter shadow—blind, folded gently at its margins, gliding across the avenues without beating or leaving a wake—enters the fixed webs of starlight, whoever sees it stops completely and turns their pale face upward. They do not approach it or try to study it. They watch until its featureless wings slip back into the perimeter dark, as though witnessing the basin’s own ideal made flesh: motion so pure that the water cannot even remember it passed.

They do not sleep as warm creatures sleep. Instead, for days at a time, an inhabitant will anchor a single fingertip—or the hem of a sleeve—to one of the hair-thin dark filaments beneath a hovering hall and hang suspended in the black water, eyes half-lidded, metabolism pared back to a single slow exchange every few minutes. Seen then, drifting beneath a twenty-meter cathedral of matte stone with nothing holding either of them up, they are almost impossible to distinguish from the architecture that shelters them.

Yet they are not timeless. Even here, where nothing rots and no edge dulls, centuries leave a quiet weight inside a living frame.

As an inhabitant grows very old, their pale skin turns slightly more opaque, taking on the dry, slick sheen of the tower walls. The mineral structures inside their bones thicken; their buoyancy grows harder to lift toward the high colonnades. More and more of their hours are spent on the lower terraces, closer to the newly broken volcanic glass, resting their palms against the archive doors or kneeling for half a day beside the sunken pools without lifting a single plate. They do not grow frail or crooked. They simply grow still—still even by the measure of this place—until the effort of holding a coordinate begins to feel heavier than letting go.

Nothing in the basin is allowed to decay. There is no silt, no pale snow of dead matter, nothing to cloud the water or feed the green things that have never been permitted to begin. The inhabitants keep that law with their own bodies.

When one of them reaches the end of their long equilibrium, there is no ceremony, no gathering in the plazas. One by one, those who cross their path along the terraces may pause and touch the back of a wrist for a single quiet pulse, then drift aside. Alone, the old inhabitant leaves the numbered plinths and the dim amber markers behind, gliding steadily outward through the thinning towers for five or six silent minutes until the hamlet fades into the dark behind them and the water simply ends.

There, at the edge of the world, they hover for a long while facing the darkened, optically dimmed membrane—looking out through that taut skin of nothing at all at the crisp, unblinking stars, the sheer black mountain flanks plunging away on every side, and the distant, unmoving veil of cloud-tops miles below. Sometimes the younger ones come out here too, merely to look, resting a palm lightly against the elastic boundary to feel its faint hum in the bones of their wrists and remember the thin, freezing emptiness they are pinned against.

The old inhabitant does not stop at touching it.

They raise both pale hands, lay their palms flat against the invisible skin of the sea, and press—not sharply, never in haste, but with the same slow, patient steadiness they have used all their lives to pass through granite doors.

For a long minute the membrane holds, bowing half an inch outward into the black night air. Then, recognizing a stillness equal to its own, the boundary yields cleanly around their narrow shoulders. Without a bubble, without a splash, without spilling a single drop of the precious cold behind them, they slip through the surface into the thin freezing sky and let go—falling cleanly away into the miles of unbreathing dark below, leaving the mountain-top sea behind them immaculate, unclouded, and perfectly still.

Back among the hovering towers, high near the roof of the water, a single tiny *tick* travels down as the containment seals the space they occupied.

In the quiet galleries, thirty-nine pale heads tilt a fraction of a degree upward, count the beat in the bones behind their ears, and return to their steady, unhurried watch—keeping the cold unbroken, keeping the rock unweathered, holding their small square of silent ocean intact between the sharp peaks and the stars.
