The people who keep these ledges are built like the masonry they live inside: deep-chested, heavy-boned, and economical with every movement. Generations in the thin, wet air have given them wide ribs and broad, unhurried lungs. They do not rush across a catwalk or shout up a stairwell; running leaves a newcomer gasping with a copper taste in the mouth, and shouting across a gorge only throws your own words back at you wrong, doubled and bruised by the cliffs. Instead they move at a measured, rolling stride that falls naturally into step with the flywheels—two paces to a thump, a breath drawn on the rise, let out in a pale ribbon as the vibration settles back into the stone.

Up close, in the amber spill of a sorting-yard lamp or the damp fug of a boiler-house doorway, they look half-weathered and half-machined. Their skin stays pale beneath the pewter sky, though the damp puts a dull plum flush in their cheeks, and their knuckles are permanently darkened along the creases with graphite, cylinder oil, and the bitter bottle-green sap of the cliff foliage. They wear layered, unbleached wool sheared from the steaming herds—never truly dry, only warm from the body—under stiff coats of vulcanized rubber and tarred canvas that shine like wet basalt. On their feet are thick-soled boots shod in soft copper or lead, partly so a slipped heel will not throw a spark near a substation’s weeping moss, and partly to take the sharper edge off the rock’s perpetual tremor. None of them wants the tremor silenced entirely, though. A silent floor means a turbine has tripped somewhere down the line, and in the high islands, a broken rhythm is the only thing that makes people stop mid-sentence and look toward the door.

Their trades are all variations on patience. The most respected crews are the jackmen and plumb-watchers—men and women who spend their lives walking the viaduct abutments and cellar piers with spirit levels, brass callipers, and sacks of oak and iron shims, keeping the city loose enough to ride the rising stone without snapping its own spine. Below them in the tenements, householders do the same work in miniature. Almost every table, bedframe, and cookstove stands on threaded iron feet or stacks of notched wedges; once a season, when the soup starts pooling toward the eastern rim of the bowl, someone kneels with a mallet and taps the far legs up a quarter-inch. Nothing is bolted down forever. Even the dead are not buried—there is no soil for it, and the rock would only push a coffin back out through the paving stones within a decade. Instead, the families wrap them in oiled canvas weighted with scrap slag and lower them on long hemp lines off the leeward cliffs until the bruised cloud-sea swallows the weight and the line goes slack.

Inside the stacked brick tenements, life presses close to the warmth. Uncovered steam mains run straight through kitchens and parlors, hissing softly in the corners, draped with damp socks, salted strips of grazer flank, and bundles of waxy black-emerald leaves hung to wilt before stewing. The air indoors smells richly of hot wet wool, tallow broth, roasted fern-root, and the faint, sweet stink of coal-tar. On the windowsills and beside the flue-pipes sit little cages woven of copper wire, each holding three or four of the pale, ribbed transformer-climbers. People do not treat them as pets so much as household instruments; fed on stray grease and damp moss, the palm-sized creatures huddle against the warm iron of the stove-pipe and click their shells in ripples of dry applause whenever the electrical load swings across the district. An old boiler-tender can tell, without lifting her eyes from her mending, whether the northern drop-forge has started a heavy run or whether a wet vine has bridged an insulator out on the third pylon.

The rest of the city’s creatures weave through the tenements with the same unsentimental familiarity. Lean, dark-furred scavengers curl atop the warm chimney breasts and ride the dumbwaiters between floors, tolerated so long as they leave the salt-boxes shut; in return, they keep the damp-mites out of the fleeces and alert a sleeping house if a steam-joint starts weeping scalding vapor. Out on the flat tarred roofs, children crouch motionless beside the oil-filmed puddles, dropping slow mist-beetles in front of the flat-headed amphibians to watch their dull bronze eyes blink once, ponderously, before the broad mouths snap shut. Even the great brass-voiced birds have their civic uses. When a cold front tightens the cloud-lid, whole flocks crowd along the exterior water-mains and roost chest-to-chest inside the iron arches, their oily heat and leather-throated rumbling keeping the pipes thawed through the worst of the frost. People speak to them in the same low, chesty cadence they use with one another, tossing offal off the parapets and listening carefully to the pitch of their calls—for the birds are quicker than any barometer to sense when a ridge is about to shift a fraction of an inch.

Yet the longer you watch the people of the ledges—the longer you sit in a cinder-yard canteen drinking dark, boiled chicory and listening to the chalk scratch on the tally-boards—the clearer it becomes that the city’s population does not obey the strict borders you brought up with you from wherever you came.

Start with the counting. Every foreman knows that a shift roster has slack in it, just like the suspension cables and the herds filing across the truss bridges. A crew of eight ironworkers will step into the funicular at the upper eastern peak, descend for twenty minutes through the cold haze, and step out onto the lower switching-yard—looking down across the gulf at the very smokestacks they just left—with nine men stamping the damp off their boots. Nobody reaches for an iron bar or starts demanding papers. The ninth man wears the same tarred canvas, carries the same dented tin of tallow-and-cabbage, and remembers the same cracked flywheel bearing from three winters back. Perhaps his voice sits a shade lower in his chest; perhaps his left thumb lacks the plum-colored scar the others carry from stripping fern-ribs. The tally-keeper simply draws a second column on the slate—one count for going out, one for coming back—and the canteen cook sets out another mug. To argue with the surplus is considered bad manners, and worse luck. You keep your arithmetic directional and let the numbers drift overnight if they want to.

It works the other way, too, in the quiet places where two ridges cross one another like overlapping glass plates on a light table. A woman walking home from the sorting yard along a misty bridge-span may step out of the haze onto her own brick landing and find her husband already sitting at the table—except he is still wearing his dry indoor waistcoat, whereas she passed him ten minutes ago on the lower catwalk in a soaking rubber coat, hauling a coil of copper wire. Neither of them is startled. The city’s families have long since grown used to living slightly out of register with themselves. Sometimes a brother or a neighbor slips sideways onto the other plate for a week or a whole season: his wet coat still hangs on its hook by the tenement door, dripping gently onto the flagstones, and his boots stay warm beside the steam-pipe, though he himself is only visible as a faint, smoky silhouette walking a twin parapet across the basin. You can leave a folded note, a fresh pair of wool socks, or a tin of salted grease on the narrow brick ledge where the shadows lean toward one another under the sodium lamp. By the next shift it will be gone, and in its place will sit a brass washer you needed, or a handful of freshly sheared fleece still steaming faintly in the cold.

That quiet porosity goes deeper than the air between ridges. It reaches down into the foundations, where the riveted iron support columns lose their seams halfway down and grade seamlessly into black, unweathered basalt.

The mountain does not only finish the city’s blueprints. Now and again, along the freshest ribs of newly extruded cliff—usually near one of those matte-gray seams where the stone has not quite finished deciding to be rock—a survey crew or a fern-cutter will come across someone sitting patiently on a raw shelf of greenish slate.

They look, at first glance, like any other resident of the high islands: broad-chested, thick-wristed, dressed in heavy dark wool and slick canvas that smells faintly of wet clay and hot grease rather than coal-smoke. They never look lost, and they never ask where they are. They simply sit with their large, calm hands resting on their knees, watching the mist curl past the bridge-piers until someone walks over and offers them a pry-bar or a mug of chicory.

The crews call them *risers*, or *new-cut*, though rarely to their faces, because there is no unkindness meant and no real barrier between them and anyone born in an upstairs tenement room. Still, if you work beside one on a winch for a winter, you notice the small differences. Their breath makes no pale plume in the freezing air. Their fingernails grow in dark, translucent plates that flake cleanly like mica if they catch on a rivet. When they sit in a canteen, they rarely speak for their first year or two—not from shyness, only as though their throats are still learning the local pitch between the drop-forge and the birds—yet they already know how to pack a stuffing-box with oiled hemp, how to read the sag of a suspension wire, and which bolts on a boiler door need half a turn less than the others. Under the yellow sodium lamps along the freight platforms, their shadows do not behave with the aloofness you might expect of stone; on the contrary, a riser’s shadow leans harder and more eagerly than anyone else’s across the wet flagstones, stretching to pool and mingle with the shadow of whoever walks beside them, as though hungry for the slight, damp warmth of older company.

And at the far end of a long life, the traffic runs back the other way.

People here do not go brittle with age so much as they grow heavy. A winch-operator or a weaver who has spent seventy years feeling the four-second turbine throb through the soles of their boots begins, in their last decade, to slow down until their pulse and their breathing lock permanently into the machinery’s cadence. They stop climbing the steep exterior iron stairs. They move down into the lowest brick rooms of the tenements, or out onto the sheltered stone stoops beside the boiler houses, where the warm mist curls thick around the foundations and the freshly extruded basalt pushes smooth black knuckles up through the cellar flagstones.

There they sit, wrapped in layers of tarred wool that gradually take on a fine velvet coat of bottle-green moss along the shoulders and sleeves. Their skin cools and tightens, taking on the matte, bluish-slate sheen of wet rock in low light; their knuckles broaden and stiffen until their fingers can no longer close around a spanner, though they can still hold a warm tin mug cradled in both palms for an entire afternoon. Nobody hides them away or mourns them prematurely. Neighbors bring them bowls of fern-broth, brush the stray damp-spiders from their collars, and sit beside them on upturned crates to talk through the day’s tallies. The old ones answer slowly—sometimes taking three or four beats of the flywheel to shape a sentence—and when they speak, the jack-crews listen closely. Long before a plumb-line registers a tilt or a steel collar begins to groan, the settled elders can feel through their own heavy heels which slab of mountain is inching upward beneath the district, and by how many fractions of an inch the viaduct will need to be raised come spring. In the oldest cellars of the western ridge, there are shapes seated against the steam-warmed walls whose coats have fused entirely into the dark brickwork and the rising basalt beneath—whether they are still breathing at a tempo of one breath per hour, or whether they finished their casting twenty years ago, nobody presumes to decide. People still set a small saucer of hot grease beside their boots on cold evenings, just in case.

Nor are the ledges shared only by the people, the beasts, and the mountain’s quiet half-children. Look closely at the places where the city’s geometry softens, and you begin to see the other tenants—the ones who have no names on any shift-roster and never step inside a brick doorway.

Along the freight platforms, where the dim sodium bulbs smear yellow oil across the wet stone, the workers’ shadows lean gently together as they walk. Yet if you stand still on an overhead catwalk and look straight down at a pair of dockhands pausing to light their pipes, you will sometimes notice a third shadow curled into the dark puddle where theirs overlap—smaller, rounder, low-shouldered, with no body standing above it to cast it. It moves only when the men shift their weight, creeping sideways along the wet mortar to stay inside the pool of mingled dark, much as the pale climbers crowd onto a warm transformer housing. The crews call them *heel-warmers* or *lamp-strays*. They never snatch, never trip a boot, never follow a worker past the threshold of a brightly lit boiler hall. They seem to want nothing except proximity—a brief share of the faint warmth thrown off by two people standing close together in the cold haze. Experienced railmen simply make a habit of stepping wide around a darkened puddle of mortar when they turn away from a lamp, giving the stray shadow a second to peel itself loose and slip back into the crevices of the parapet.

Further out, where the northern drop-forge strikes once every half-minute and the opposite crag sends back first a sharp ring of iron and then a dull, wooden thud, the watchmen listen to a different neighbor altogether. No trees grow on these freshly broken heights; there is no timber here save the wet, creosote-soaked cribbing hauled in or salvaged from God knows where to jack the bridges. Yet the second echo is unmistakably wood—thick, seasoned, heavy timber struck hard in a closed room. And once or twice a month, after a long night of drifting cloud, a roof-sweeper clearing the drains above a tenement will fish out of an oil-filmed puddle a single splinter of dark, dense wood, smooth as river-drift and smelling strongly of resin and wet bark. Sometimes it bears the clean mark of an adze or a peg-hole bored by a hand-auger.

The tenement women never throw these splinters into the stove. They dry them carefully on the steam-pipes and use them to shim their table legs, because wood from the echo never rots and never swells in the damp. Nobody has ever climbed down beneath the bruised sea of cloud to see what lies at the valley bottom, and nobody has ever walked far enough into a matte-gray rock-seam to come out the other side. Yet the older inhabitants speak comfortably of *the joiners*—whoever it is that works in oak and tar just behind the unfinished stone, or down beneath the vapor-floor, striking their wooden mallets half a beat behind the ironworks. When a heavy crane-chain snaps on the northern peak and drops into the mist, the watchmen sometimes swear they hear, ten seconds later, not a splash or a smash on rock, but the low, polite murmur of unseen workers hauling the iron aside and laying down a timber beam in its place.

And then there are the ones in the wide, empty air between the districts.

Half a mile out from any brick parapet, where no viaduct reaches and only the sagging black curtains of power cable buzz softly through the gloom, the damp air occasionally forgets to drop its weight. Condensed cloud-water halts in midair, hanging motionless in vast, trembling sheets of silver droplets for ten or fifteen minutes at a time. From a suspension car creeping slowly along the single-track rail, passengers press their faces near the wet glass and watch those suspended curtains—because they are rarely empty.

Standing upright inside the still silver water, spaced far apart like solitary lattice pylons, are tall, narrow presences of the exact matte, featureless gray you see along the rawest rock-ledges where geology has not finished making up its mind. They have no faces, no coats, no clear edges where their shoulders end and the bent air around them begins. Sound drops dead in their vicinity; even the blue crackle of a wet fern brushing a high-voltage wire goes mute when one of them lingers near the lines. They do not stride or fly. They simply stand suspended in the halted rain, immense and unhurried, drinking the mist or listening to the long, cello-like groan of the bridge cables as the mountain plates grind beneath them.

Whether the rain stops falling because they have gathered there, or whether they drift out of the matte-gray rock-seams to pasture in the brief stillness of the air, no one on the ledges can say. The great brass-voiced birds show no fear of them at all; whole flocks will bank cleanly into a silver curtain, vanish for a heartbeat behind a tall, sound-eating silhouette, and glide out the far side with their oily dark feathers washed clean of soot, letting out a single low, resonant blast that sounds remarkably like a greeting. When the ten or fifteen minutes are up and the suspended sheet of water finally gives way, sliding sideways in one slow, heavy wall to drench the hanging vines on the next cliff over, the tall gray shapes drift effortlessly with it—folding themselves back into the dripping curtains of moss, or stepping silently into the unfinished lip of a basalt promontory where the air thickens like uneven glass.

Back on the eastern parapet, the shift-horn blows—or perhaps a row of broad-winged birds has merely settled onto a warmer crane-boom; even after forty years on the peaks, it is hard to be certain which. A single-track ore car creeps out of the mist, its amber headlamp smearing dull gold across the wet iron, a dark-furred scavenger perched calmly on its rim. Along the narrow truss bridge below, a line of shaggy grazers files homeward through the haze, fourteen from the lead bull to the last calf, seventeen if your eye wanders back from tail to head. On the cinder platform, a crew of ten rubber-coated workers steps off a car that left the upper works with nine, nodding once to the tally-keeper as she rubs out yesterday’s drifted chalk. One of them leaves no plume of breath in the cold air; another pauses by the boiler-house stoop to set a warm tin mug between the stiff, moss-furred hands of an old man whose boots have not lifted from the basalt in half a season. Beneath the yellow lamps, their shadows lean comfortably together across the wet stone, making room for a small, bodyless dark to curl at their heels, while deep inside the terraced turbine halls the great flywheels turn, sending their slow, steady pulse up through fresh rock, riveted steel, and every quiet thing that has learned to breathe in time with it.
