You can stand in the basin for half a day, eyes aching from the silver-white wash, and swear that whoever stacked the cairns and hammered the copper left a thousand winters ago.

The glare teaches you to look for the wrong things. You look for dark shapes cutting across pale rock, for shadows moving over wet shelves, for the warm disturbance of breath or the snap of a boot on scree. None of those exist here. Even the noise conspires to hide them: the rhythmic *clack-clack-clack* rising from a narrow terrace fifty yards below sounds identical to the glassy cobbles spinning in the flooded potholes, until you notice that the cadence pauses for three heartbeats, shifts pitch by half a step, and begins again.

Follow the sound down to a canted shelf of pale gneiss, and you will still see nothing at first—only overlapping fins of wet slate, a low corbeled overhang, and a hunched rib of stone shining with the same continuous skin of condensation as everything around it.

Then the rib of stone shifts its weight, and a long, flat-jointed hand rises to touch a vibrating strip of copper wedged in the cliff face.

Once your eyes break the habit of expecting shadow, you begin to see them everywhere—not crowded, never in groups larger than three or four, spaced out across the stacked horizons like the cairns themselves.

They are shaped along human lines, yet worn down and re-proportioned by cold, gale, and stone until nothing superfluous remains. Shorter than lowland people and denser through the ribs and shoulders, they seldom stand fully upright; even on level terraces they carry a slight, permanent forward cant, low-centered against the wind. Their bare skin is the exact pewter-white and silver-grey of wet gneiss—smooth, completely hairless, and slick with a thin, cold waxy film that beads the relentless condensation and sheds it in bright, unbroken sheets. Where mineral seepage has dried along their collarbones, knuckles, and shins, their hides take on faint streaks of pale calcite and rust. Crouched motionless with their knees drawn tight to their chests and their forearms folded over their shins, they are simply another wet facet of the glare.

They wear nothing. Hide would rot and hold water; woven cloth would freeze stiff in the updrafts. Whatever warmth they keep lies beneath their slick skin in a firm, heavy layer of dense insulating fat, tended by a heartbeat so slow and frugal that a crouched sleeper inside one of the dry-stone cells seems barely alive at all. Their skulls are smooth and narrow-templed, with heavy, pale brow-ridges overhanging the eyes like natural rock eaves. Beneath those eaves, their eyelids are thick and pearly—drawn almost shut in the hammered glare until only a glinting horizontal seam remains, admitting the light as bands of pure edge and brightness. Their outer ears are reduced to small, hooded folds tucked tight behind the jaw, offering nothing for the shrieking wind to catch or freeze.

They are built for contact with stone. Their hands and feet are broad, splayed, and thick-padded, with blunt, horn-hard nails worn flat at the tips from gripping slate and pecking gneiss. The soles of their feet are pale, finely fissured pads that hold wet rock without slipping and leave no mark behind them—only a faint dry outline that the sloughing film of water erases in a breath.

Watch one cross the southern slope along the pale vein of shattered marble, and you understand the pecked footholds.

It moves at a low, effortless, slightly crouched trot—heels barely touching, fingers brushing wet rock only to read its grain—climbs twenty feet up a water-polished rib, rolls smoothly past vertical without checking its pace, and continues upside-down along the underside of a dripping slab. Its hairless head hangs calmly toward the roaring chasm eighty feet below while the runoff around its ankles creeps sideways across the ceiling in gleaming ropes. To these walkers, downward is not a single loyalty; it is simply whichever way the stone beneath their soles happens to insist. They carry nothing loose that might spill when the pull reverses. A flat hammerstone or a freshly pried strip of copper is held pinched in the hand, tucked tight in the crook of an arm, or cached inside one of the tall interlocking cairns.

Because the cairns sit deliberately astride the spatial seams, a single cache serves more than one ridge. You may watch a figure pause beside a slender slate tower on a high wet terrace, slide a smooth, glassy pothole-cobble beneath its third tier, and walk away empty-handed—then, minutes later, see another walker fifty paces downslope reach into the exact same cairn, leaning now at a sharper angle against a wholly different wall of spires, and draw the cobble out dry-palmed from the other side of the fold.

They travel by these folds the way lowland people travel by roads. Stepping neatly across a hand-wide drainage crack, a walker drops thirty yards of wet cliff in a single unhurried stride, emerging out of the spray with the gale suddenly hammering its back instead of its face. They never stumble at the turn of the horizon; they do not navigate primarily by sight.

They navigate by bone and copper.

In air this wet and loud, spoken language would shred the instant it left the mouth, and sound lies constantly—throwing a distant cascade into your ear while muffling the torrent at your feet. Solid rock does not lie. When two walkers need to reach one another across a roaring slot, one presses a smooth pothole-cobble against a continuous vein of gneiss and taps a dry, crisp sequence—*tock, tock-tock, tock*—that vanishes almost immediately into the wind-roar. Forty yards away, the other lays its bare palm or cheekbone flat against the same pale seam and reads the cadence cleanly through the bones of its skull.

Their copper blades serve a quieter, constant speech. Wherever the valley's geometry creases, the walkers wedge cold-hammered plates, narrow tongues, and slotted discs tightly into the fractures with tiny slate pins. To pass a copper tongue is to pause and listen—not with the hooded ears alone, but by laying a knuckle lightly against the metal or pressing a chin to the stone beside the crack. When the copper sings flat and steady, the air across the seam agrees with itself and the distance ahead will hold a walker's stride. When it shudders in harsh, erratic bursts, the rock beyond it is sliding, doubling, ready to drop a careless foot onto a ridge that is no longer where it was an hour ago.

It is the copper, too, and the pecked runnels, that keep them fed.

Nothing green grows in the saddle, and the sterile condensation sloughing off the outer peaks carries almost nothing of substance. What lives here chemically lives in the deep seepages—the rare, iron- and mineral-laden waters bleeding out of fractures in the gneiss, where the brittle rust-colored crust forms in the warmest splash-zones against quartz. To an outsider it looks like oxidized rock; to the basin's inhabitants, it is pasture.

They farm it with hammerstones and broken space. Wherever a thin thread of mineral-rich seepage emerges from a fault, they peck shallow channels across solid stone to guide it deliberately across a spatial seam, splitting one scarce trickle into two identical threads that run down separate terraces without ever rejoining—doubling the bright, wet quartz faces where the rust-film takes hold. Better still are the quiet quartz seams where time itself disagrees. On the fast, dry side of a temporal fracture, mineral-heavy wash evaporates and crusts in an afternoon that costs the wet side of the stone only minutes, building up dense, brittle wafers of pale salt and layered coppery-rust cake that would take a decade to thicken elsewhere.

You find them kneeling beside these split runnels in the blinding wash, lifting flakes of rust-crust with thin slate knives or skimming the mineral-cloudy catch-basins with slotted copper discs. They chew slowly and patiently, working the gritty cakes with broad, flat back teeth stained dark iron-grey along the edges. Their slow, cold-adapted bodies ask very little; a few palms of mineral cake and iron-crust, ground down with cold water, sustain them across days of patient stillness.

Their lives move to the valley's breath.

In the full hammered glare, when the sky pours shadowless white from every quarter, they are mostly quiet. Some crouch motionless on the drier spurs, eyelids closed to glinting slivers, letting the wind scour the wet sheen on their backs. Others crawl into the low corbeled dry-stone cells wedged beneath canted overhangs. Those cramped chambers—barely tall enough to squat in, chinked with crushed white calcite and mineral clay—hold the nearest thing to dimness the basin allows. True darkness never enters them; silver light still bounces off the wet stone outside and crawls beneath the low threshold to paint their folded knees in pewter. Yet inside, the organ-roar of the gale drops to a steady muffled groan. Two or three will wedge together there, shoulder to hip like stacked slate, sharing their sparse, slow warmth. Sometimes, in the dim hush, they make a closed-mouth hum—a low, vibrating throat-chord tuned to the pitch of the wind and the nearest copper blades—until the cramped stone walls tremble faintly around them.

Then the eastern lip darkens, and the great grey wall of vapor rolls up out of the abyss.

As the blinding glare softens into luminous milky haze, the basin wakes. Figures unfold from beneath the overhangs and emerge from the low cells. With the brutal brilliance blunted, their pearly eyelids finally part wide, revealing large, pale silver-grey irises contracted around pinhole pupils. They move swiftly now—gliding along vertical ribs, slipping through the fog where an outsider could not tell slick slate from open air three paces ahead. They do not hesitate; they read the thickening hum of the copper plates, the sudden swells in the pecked runnels, and the damp air pressing unevenly against their hairless scalps.

They know when the floor is about to settle before the stone moves.

In a world where ripples spread across a pool two seconds before a pebble strikes, a walker standing beside a catch-basin reads the coming fault-crack in the water's skin and the faint foreslip trembling under its soles. Seconds before the deep, resonant *crack* snaps up through the gneiss, they are already in place along the lower chasms and looping cataracts.

The fault shifts by a hair's breadth, or a fraction of an angle. Across the whole amphitheater, the pitch of the water breaks at once.

A looping cascade misses its lower intake by a hand's breadth, flinging wild curtains of spray across normally dry rock. For five, ten, twelve rushing seconds, a flooded pothole slackens or a polished chute bares its throat. Out of the milky haze a pale figure darts, plunges a thick, cold-numb arm deep into the swirling stone bowl, and snatches up a freshly rounded glassy cobble before the water can reclaim it—or pries a green-skinned ribbon of raw native copper from a fault-lip that has yawned open a finger's width—and vaults effortlessly sideways onto an inverted shelf an instant before the seam eases back and the torrent slams home.

Then the cloud-wall tears apart and rushes off over the western drop. The hammered white sky slams back down onto the valley, igniting a million wet facets at once.

For a short, brisk span after the glare returns, the walkers fan out along the ridges to tend what the fault disturbed. They test the interlocking cairns, shifting a disc here or wedging a chip there where a fold has drifted out of plumb. They clear loose slate from the split runnels so the twin threads of mineral water run true again. At the spatial seams, they kneel with glassy cobbles and tiny slate pins—*tink, tink*—re-seating copper tongues that have worked loose, tapping until an erratic metallic rattle drops back into a thin, flat hum. Somewhere beneath an overhang, another sits with a raw twist of newly pried native copper laid flat on a gneiss anvil, beating it cold and thin with a smooth pothole-sphere, the steady *clack-clack-clack* disappearing seamlessly into the chorus of the spinning stones.

If you stay long enough to watch them through several breaths of the valley, a quieter strangeness begins to surface—the same unease you feel when you realize you have passed the exact same chipped cairn twice on different ridges.

You cannot count them. There seem to be very few—a sparse scattering across the wide, crowded saddle—yet details repeat in ways that make the mind slip. The walker you watched re-seating a copper tongue on the northern fin had a crooked little finger bent inward from an old break and a pale crescent scar across its left shoulder blade. Half an hour later, across a roaring slot you could not have crossed in twice that time, you see the same crooked finger and the same crescent scar crouched beside a salt-rimmed basin—except the creases beside its pearly eyes are deeper, or smoother, or its forearm carries a dark stain of fresh green copper that was not there before.

Sometimes two of them meet on a narrow shelf where space folds back on itself—same flat-nailed hands, same faint rust-streaks, same scarred shoulder—and pass one another without startlement, offering only a brief, level tilt of the chin, or handing a flat slate disc across the gap as easily as a left hand passes a stone to a right. Their sense of sequence is worn as thin and smooth as the rock around them. They do not seem troubled by meeting their own tracks ahead of themselves, or by living a life pleated loosely across the saddle's seams.

Their young are rare and kept close. You never see a child alone on the open spurs. The few nursery cells are built into the most stubbornly faithful stretches of gneiss—places where gravity agrees with itself for fifty paces and the copper plates have sung the same flat note for generations. Outside those thresholds you sometimes find tiny, shallow footholds pecked beside larger ones, and caches of small, smooth pothole-marbles left in stone niches. When a young one finally leaves the shelf, an elder keeps a broad hand locked firmly around its wrist or the nape of its smooth neck, guiding it past the hand-wide drainage cracks until it knows by bone and copper which steps fold distance and which ones drop you into another decade's weather.

Once in a long while, when the wind drops for a handful of heartbeats and the blinding sky settles glassy on the stillest catch-basins, you may find one of them kneeling alone at the water's edge.

Its pearly eyelids are drawn wide open despite the glare, staring straight down into the reflection. Down there, beneath the bright surface, the surrounding gneiss fins stand dry and razor-sharp under a darker, lower vault of cloud, as they stood before ten thousand winters rounded their crests. The kneeling figure does not stir. Its wet, pale face hangs close above the water, watching whatever stands along the rim of that older, drier pool—staring down across the thin skin of time at what they were before the peaks climbed above the clouds, or at who is still waiting on the other side of the glass.

And when one of them finally wears out—when the cold-slowed joints stiffen past the upside-down climbs, when the flat teeth grind down to the gums on iron-crust and grit, when the pearly lids cloud entirely white so even the milky haze dims to nothing—it leaves no bones in the valley.

It does not lie down inside a corbeled cell. Alone, or guided by a steady palm at the elbow, it walks to one of the wide, quiet quartz seams where wet stone meets bone-dry salt. It steps across the pale vein onto the dry side, curls itself down with its knees drawn tight to its chest and its brow bowed against the blinding rock, and becomes still.

On the wet side of the quartz, only a few breaths of the cloud-wall pass. On the dry side, where time runs lean and fast, decades sweep quietly across the crouched form. The cold dry wind and pale mineral salt take the water from its flesh, bleach it to white chalk, and grind the chalk to dust indistinguishable from the crusted basin floor. By the time the next grey sheet of rain washes sideways across the ridge, nothing remains but a shallow, smooth concavity in the mineral salt—the faint hollow of folded knees and a bowed spine, already dissolving at its rims—and, just across the quartz seam on the wet stone, a single fresh disc of pale slate balanced cleanly atop the nearest cairn.

Then the cloud-wall tears apart. The white sky slams back down onto the crowded wilderness of stone, every wet facet flares into cold silver brilliance, and if you blink against the glare, the basin looks utterly empty again—only wind, water, grinding cobbles, humming copper, and the pale ribs of gneiss, shining silently together at the roof of the air.
