You do not see them first; you feel where the heat gives way.

In a basin where the warmth presses evenly against the skin from every side, any break in it feels like an intrusion. You are wading along the edge of a sunken reservoir, ankle-deep over slick flagstones, when your forearm brushes a pocket of cellar-cool air. There is no draft behind it, no breeze stirring the heavy vapor. The coolness simply stands there, dense and localized, as if a slab of wet marble had been carried in from some deep, sunless quarry and set upon the bench beside you.

When you turn toward it and wait for your eyes to sort the gloom, there is almost nothing to separate from the stone.

Resting on the worn travertine bench is a pale, slumped form—no taller than a crouched person, though it has no crouch, no head, no shoulders, no limbs tucked beneath it. At a glance it looks like a weathered bollard, or a folded bolt of heavy wet linen left behind by a mason and crusted over by centuries of mineral runoff. Its surface is the same chalk-grey as the sinter along the curbs, matte in some places and slickly gleaming in others. Only when you lean close enough to feel the coolness radiating off it do you see that it is pleated from crown to base in deep, regular vertical folds, and that the condensation beading on its flanks is creeping steadily upward, gathering into a smooth, dark dimple at its top.

If you lay your palm against it, it does not resist like mudstone. It yields—slowly, with the cool, dense, fibrous give of wet parchment packed tightly over cold river-clay. Beneath that chalk-dusted skin moves a faint hydraulic tension, far too slow to be a pulse, more like the gradual settling of water inside a sealed cistern. You draw your hand back and your fingers come away faintly damp and smelled of nothing at all, only clean wet lime.

If you stay in that reservoir through the next silent tide—standing still as the hot water swells half a foot up the steps and sinks back again—you will find that the pale bolster is no longer beside you. It rests six paces farther down the bench, angled slightly more toward the center of the water. Where it sat before, the stone is scrubbed dark and bare, bordered by a thin, dissolving crescent of pale chalk-silt.

They have no legs to stride with, no fins to turn the shallows, no wings to stir the dark.

They move the way the basin itself moves: by pressure, by film, by patient displacement. When the hot tide rises from beneath the flagstones, their pleated bases widen almost imperceptibly, drinking a thin lens of the warm, mineral-heavy water beneath themselves and riding it like a heavy drop sliding across oiled slate. There is no wake, no splash, scarcely a ripple to mar the black mirror of the flats. Across dry stone between tides, they travel more slowly still—minutes to cross the width of a paving slab—sending a microscopic shudder down their vertical folds so that their undersides creep forward by capillary pull, leaving behind a glistening band that dries to fine silt before the hour is out.

Once your eye learns the slight difference between the rounded stone of the city and the cooler, pleated chalk of its tenants, the empty flats begin to populate themselves.

Not in crowds. Never in hurry. Across a drowned plaza hundreds of yards wide, there may be only five or six of them, spaced so far apart in the gloom that you pass between them without knowing you have crossed a gathering. One rests half-submerged on the lower riser of a tiered plinth, only its smooth, dimpled crown showing above the sheen. Another leans its tall, narrow folds against the pier of an arcade, nearly indistinguishable from the fluting of the stone until you notice that the pale stalks growing along the base of the pier have vanished beneath its hem.

Those cold, waxy reeds are their pasture, though they do not bite or chew.

Watch one approach a seam in a causeway where a thin row of pale stalks has sprung up out of the black silt. It glides over them on the slack of the tide and slowly lowers its outer pleats until its base seals against the paving slabs, enclosing a dozen stalks in a cool, lightless chamber beneath its body. It remains there for two tides, perhaps three, utterly motionless while the steam curls past its flanks. When at last it lifts on a thermal swell and drifts a yard onward, the seam in the flagstones is bare. Not snapped or grazed down to stubble—simply gone, drawn upward through its soft underside and converted into cold, pale marrow, leaving the tenant slightly taller, its pleats sharper, its aura of cellar-chill freshly restored against the relentless fever of the air.

Yet they are not mere grazers drifting through a ruin. They are citizens, and the quiet of the basin is full of their business.

Walk into one of the sunken reservoirs during the long slack between tides, and you will sometimes find seven or nine of them arranged along the perimeter benches—spaced at intervals so exact they seem measured with a cord, never touching, every pleated front turned inward toward the black center of the water. They do not speak as creatures with throats speak. Instead, as you stand at the top of the steps, you feel a slight, velvety shift in pressure against your eardrums. Across the reservoir, one of the pale forms has parted its vertical folds by the width of a knife-blade, releasing a dense, cold parcel of air stored inside its chambers. The cool pocket sinks silently into the fever-warm steam, drawing the surrounding vapor inward in a slow curl.

Moments later, from the opposite bench, comes the answer: the water that has been creeping up another tenant’s pleats brims over the lip of its upper dimple and falls into the reservoir with a single, hollow *thup*. A slow ring expands across the oil-black sheet, strikes the submerged bases of the others, and is read there without haste. Another pleat opens; another drop falls; a faint hiss of vapor slips through a sluice gate thirty paces away where a smaller form has wedged itself into the channel to throttle the flow.

What sounded at first like the blind mechanics of dripping stone and cooling vents is, half the time, a conversation—measured in barometric weight, in ripples, in the disciplined release of drops, stretched across hours the way an argument among loud, brief-lived creatures might be stretched across minutes.

Even the isolated rains belong to them.

Step near a roofless courtyard from which that hushed, steady pattering rises in the dark, and look closely along the inner faces of the low walls—just inside the unseen line where the warm vertical rain begins and ends. There they stand, four or six or a dozen of them, drawn up shoulderless along the threshold like listeners around a hearth. They have opened their folds inward toward the court, pouring their collective coolness into the trapped square of saturated air until the heavy steam gives way and falls in straight, strict columns confined entirely within the geometry of their ring. They sit for hours beneath the rain they have made, letting the warm sheets wash excess travertine grit from their pleats, drinking the runoff into their crowns, murmuring back and forth in tiny changes of weight and humidity. When their business is concluded, they close their folds in slow sequence, glide out through the archway onto the flats, and the rain lifts cleanly off the black water as if a lid had been shut, only to begin again an hour later three courtyards down the causeway.

Still, if you watch them only in the dim, warm air, you have seen less than half of what they are.

Wait until the tide reaches its dead slack, when the basin floor lies flat as dark tin, and look down into the water directly beneath one of the pale, modest forms resting on a cistern rim.

Above the surface, it is barely chest-high—a humble, limbless, chalk-folded vessel, stark and pared back, matching the sparse, low emptiness of the plazas.

In the reflection, it is vast.

Hanging upside down into the denser, taller city beneath the sheen, its image does not end at its crown. It rises—downward into the dark mirror—in soaring, perforated tiers, opening out into broad fluted canopies, hanging robes of intricate shadow, long cantilevered folds that bridge the high galleries and span the empty avenues. Where two tenants sit fifty paces apart across a barren plaza of warm mud, their reflections below the surface lean across stacked bridges and touch, intertwining in the crowded upper vaults of a metropolis the hot air refuses to hold. Look across a reservoir where nine of them sit in silent quorum on the benches, and down in the water you see a towering, vaulted chamber packed with immense, motionless presences, their upper folds disappearing into dark galleries stories deep.

It is hard to say whether the pale forms on the flagstones are the whole of them, or merely their feet—the sparse, mineral anchors by which something far larger and more intricate keeps its purchase in the mud.

And it is they who keep the city at work.

Nothing here was built with chisels because the basin precipitates around their habits. When a small cluster of them settles over an empty stretch of simmering silt and holds its position through tide after tide, drawing the heat inward and chilling the mineral-laden water that swells around their bases, the dissolved stone thickens where their coolness pools. Layer by slow layer, dark mudstone and pale travertine cure out of the slurry, taking the smooth, rounded impression of their stillness. By the time they glide away, a low rectangular sill or a stepped bench stands where open water lay before—already velvety, already cornerless, already worn as if it had endured ten thousand years.

In the same quiet way, they unmake what no longer serves their order. Where a hexagonal cistern is to become five-sided, or a standing viaduct is to be parted down the middle, they do not strike it. Some withdraw their cool stewardship entirely, leaving the soft sinter to the scalding vents; others gather tight against the piers and reverse their slow chemistry, exhaling a warm, sour, steam-thick condensate from their lower pleats that coaxes the compacted clay back into soft mud. Between one thermal swell and the next, a pillar thins without rubble or sound, returning with a low granular sigh to the floor of the basin. The city is not an artifact they inherited. It is the slow, shifting sediment of their deliberations—a municipal posture held in stone for as long as they care to hold it.

Not all of them keep the same size or the same errands.

Along the arrow-straight aqueducts and narrow drainage canals glide the slenderest ones—no wider than an arm’s embrace, tightly pleated like closed fans, riding the subtle draw of the tide for miles across the sunken flats. They are couriers of climate. Out near the unseen outer marches of the basin, where dense rows of pale stalks grow undisturbed along half-drowned embankments, these narrow travelers open their inner chambers and seal away sharp pockets of cold, dry air, then carry them silently down the straight waterways to the inner plazas, releasing their cargo into the sunken reservoirs where the larger tenants wait. A single breath of dry chill, carried three miles in the dark without spilling a degree, is enough to begin a new sill or settle a dispute across five courtyards.

At the opposite end of their span lie the oldest ones, anchored along the deepest cisterns and directly above the submerged vents where the tide enters near scalding.

These elders no longer glide across the plazas. Over centuries—or whatever soft measure passes for centuries here—mineral crust has thickened on their outer folds until they are as broad as overturned skiffs, deeply ribbed, scarcely separable from the tiered plinths they rest upon. You could walk across a sunken square and take one for a collapsed dome of dark mudstone, except for the faint, steady coolness hovering just above its fluted ridge, and the imperceptible rising and falling of the water around its flanks. Through vast, labyrinthine pleats hidden inside their bulk, they take in the scalding water surging from below the flagstones, temper it, siphon it, and release it at measured intervals across the basin floor. They are the reason the tide swells without waves, the reason the heat pauses just past fever and never boils the flats dry. Look into the mirror of water beside one of these immense, anchored elders, and the inverted city beneath it is blindingly dense—towers stacked upon towers, narrow alleys and high arcades crowding inward into the dark, an entire vertical archive held intact in the reflection as long as the elder keeps its breath steady.

Even their ending is folded neatly back into the masonry.

When one of them grows too heavy with travertine grit, its pleats too stiffened with precipitated stone to widen on the tide, it does not rot. It makes one final, protracted passage into the shaded concavity beneath an arch or inside a flooded vault, drawing itself tight against the wet curve of the wall. There it settles and ceases to drink the cold stalks. Tide by tide, its outer shell cements itself seamlessly to the mudstone behind it, rounding out the curve of an arcade pier, adding another soft, velvety swell to a cornice. Nothing dies in stink or bone; the body simply becomes municipal fabric.

Yet inside that stiffening shell, before the stone closes altogether, a thin, translucent, leathery inner lining peels slowly away from the hardened chalk and remains clinging to the damp underside of the arch. Flat, pale, lightless, it lies there in the shade as the hot tide rises over it—pulsing faintly, gathering mineral film and tiny reserves of coolness, waiting through countless rises and falls until it has thickened enough to lift its edges, fold itself into a first tentative pleat, and slip quietly down into the black water to find its first row of pale reeds.

If you stay among them long enough, you begin to wonder what you are to them.

To creatures that read the world in surface tension, barometric weight, and the slow grammar of cooling stone, a body with legs and lungs must be an astonishingly crude disturbance. Every step you take sends wild, jagged chop tearing across the mirror of the inverted galleries. Your breath strikes the still air in hot, noisy gusts. You move with absurd, frantic speed through distances they cross in half a week, trailing feverish warmth and mud-clouds in your wake.

Yet they show no fear, and no anger.

Wade too close to a gathering in a sunken reservoir, and the pleats along the nearest forms simply draw shut with a quiet tightening of the air. The upward-creeping threads of condensation stall on their flanks. If you step into one of their courtyards of falling rain, the vertical curtain may shift three paces inward without a sound, drawing itself cleanly between your wet shoulders and their cool, waiting ring like a door gently pulled to.

And if, in curiosity or carelessness, you drag a foot through the reflection of one of them—shattering the towering, vaulted shadow that hangs beneath a modest pale bolster on the steps—the bolster itself will not flinch. Instead, the air directly over your head snaps tight against your ears. A brief, startling gust of drier heat brushes your hair, as if a high ceiling had opened or shifted in the blind dark above you, followed by a single heavy, warm drop falling from nothing onto the crown of your skull.

Stand still after that, and wait.

Do not wade on; simply let the ripples flatten around your calves while the thick air settles. After ten minutes—or twenty—you will notice that the colonnade you meant to reach has grown strangely distant again, while the curb of an unfamiliar canal now presses gently against your heel. You have not been chased. Nothing has touched you. Yet somewhere beneath the dark sheen, across the high inverted bridges, a quiet consensus has passed along the benches, and the soft geometry of the basin has subtly folded itself around your intrusion—nudging you, with the vast, patient, impersonal courtesy of an old city clearing a twig from a sluice, back out onto the wide, steaming flats, where the reflections can close again and the slow conversation can resume unbroken in the dark.
