If you stay in the center of the wide stone plaza long enough for the ripples of your own footsteps to flatten against the curbs—long enough that your breathing slows to match the warm, thin air—the emptiness begins to come undone.

It does not break with a voice or a sudden shape crossing the flagstones. It breaks with a hairline.

Look at the notched basalt weir at the far edge of the cistern. An hour ago, the dark hardwood sluice board sat flush with the emerald moss along the left groove. Now it sits a thumbnail’s width higher, and the bright thread of water slipping over the lip has thinned to a silent glaze. No wind moved it. And further out, across two hundred paces of shin-deep flooded flat where a walled precinct rests inside its own reflection, one of the narrow upright shadows beside a doorway is no longer where it was when the sun stood a finger’s breadth higher in the sky.

They did not depart. They did not withdraw beyond the jagged black ridges. They are here, in the thousands, spread thinly across the miles of terraced highland, and the reason you mistook their city for an abandoned equilibrium is that you were looking for creatures that move against their world rather than inside its grain.

When one of them finally steps out of a narrow rectangular doorway onto a stepped foundation, the shape of the architecture explains itself all at once.

They are tall—taller than a man by a head or more—yet strikingly narrow through the chest, ribs, and hips, built to pass straight through those tight stone slots without turning their shoulders. Their limbs are long, corded, and spare, with heavy, broad-palmed hands and long, flat, un-clawed feet that meet the wet flagstones without a slap or a scrape. They never step off the causeways into the fine dark silt along the margins; silt clogs the fine creases of their soles and clouds the water-panes, so a lifetime of walking leaves no track anywhere on the plateau.

At a distance, against the glazed basalt and shaded lintels, they are nearly impossible to separate from the stone. Their skin is bare of hair, feathers, or scales, and its color shifts with its moisture. After an hour indoors on the dry sedge mats, they carry a pale, matte, ash-grey bloom along their shoulders and forearms. The moment they wade into a shallow basin or draw water over themselves, they darken instantly to the glossy charcoal-black of wet volcanic rock, shading to a paler, almost translucent olive-grey along the inner wrists, throat, and belly.

Look closer, and the surface of their hide is not smooth at all. Fine, deliberate grooves run lengthwise down their backs, flanks, and limbs, like the fluting of sedge-stems or the grain of split rock. Those channels hold water in a thin, clinging film long after they step out onto a warm dry ledge. In the mild highland sun, that moisture transpires steadily off their bodies, cooling them, feeding the faint humidity of the air, and leaving behind, over the course of a day, a whisper-thin crust of dissolved volcanic mineral. They drink through their mouths when they eat, yet half of their thirst is answered through those pale inner seams, drawn directly from the warm sheets of water that glaze the basin.

Their heads are smooth and slightly elongated, with a heavy, clean brow-ridge that shields large dark eyes from the bright double-glare of sky and flooded plaza. They do not blink the way an animal blinks; every minute or two, a pale, wet inner membrane glides slowly across the dark eye from the corner, keeping it bright and washed, so their gaze has a calm, unbroken weight. Where ears would sit, the skin lies flat and taut over dark, narrow slits behind the jaw and along the collarbones.

Those slits are why the city is quiet, and why it is built so wide.

In dry air, their hearing is blunt and distant; sudden air-borne noise—a shout, a clatter, a gust—strikes them as harsh, flat, and disorienting, like staring straight into the sun. Through water and wet stone, however, they hear with an intimacy that reaches across entire districts. The plateau’s seepage is not merely plumbing; it is a single continuous membrane linking every courtyard, cistern, and causeway. When one of them wants to listen to the city, they do not tilt their head to the sky. They kneel beside a carved runnel and lay their pale inner wrists into the flow, or rest a bare shin against the submerged lip of a weir, and wait.

Through the water-table, everything declares itself: the faint tremor of a hardwood sluice being nudged down a fraction of an inch three precincts away; the rhythmic, patient tapping of a river cobble dressing a fresh basalt corner on the upper benches; the heavy, unhurried tread of someone crossing a flagstone causeway half a mile to the east. That is why each walled cluster sits in hundreds of paces of open water and wet bare rock. Those broad flooded flats are not wasted space, nor merely gardens of reflection. They are listening-panes—wide buffers of stillness so that one household’s movements do not blur into another’s.

And that quiet, rhythmic *tick* you heard inside the shaded cistern, which sounded like seepage meeting the surface—it is not accidental.

Watch closely at the mouth of a clay-lined conduit where it enters a courtyard, and you will see a thin wooden peg slotted into a tiny notch beside the weir, tilted just enough to divide the sheet-water into measured drops. By shifting the peg a hair’s breadth, or laying a wet sedge-stem across the lip, someone in an upper precinct alters the interval of the drops: three quick ticks, a long velvety pause, two slow ones. Downstream, inside a dim hall built over a receiving cistern, another listens through the floorboards and replies by nudging a pegged baffle in the outgoing channel, sending a tiny pressure-pulse back up the stone runnel.

Whole conversations travel the plateau this way over the course of an afternoon—unhurried exchanges about a clogged weir, a bundle of cured rushes ready for trade, a shift in the warmth of the southern rim, an elder whose joints have grown too stiff to climb the lower steps. For wider announcements—a gathering on the high ceremonial platforms, a birth, a death, or a fracture in the upper cliff—they use the cistern slabs: flat, carefully selected tongues of dense basalt suspended by knotted plant-fiber cords just beneath the water’s surface in the central basins. Struck gently with a smooth river cobble, a slab makes almost no sound in the air—only a soft, dead *tok* swallowed instantly by the stillness. Beneath the surface, the note rings clear and deep through water and bedrock alike, traveling cleanly across four quadrants until every bare foot on the flagstones feels the hum rise through the ankle.

They have no spoken language in the air. Their mouths are narrow and lined not with teeth but with dark, ridged horn-plates meant for grinding soft, wet food. Only in private—when two of them sit side by side on a sun-warmed ledge, shoulders pressed together, or when an adult leans over a sheltered basin—do they make a sound from the throat: a low, closed-mouth hum, too faint to carry six paces across a plaza, meant solely to pass directly from bone to bone.

Wait long enough, and you begin to see how little of the plateau’s stillness is passive.

Nothing here keeps its balance on its own. The upper escarpments weep steadily, yes, and the highland sun draws up moisture with mild reliability, yet across miles of fractured, stepped volcanic rock, unattended water would quickly betray its symmetry. A single wind-dropped fern frond wedged into a notch would swell one basin until it drowned a raised garden terrace while starving the rushes two tiers below. A cloudy afternoon over the southern rim would raise the tide marks by a finger’s breadth across an entire quadrant.

The tide marks stay fixed and clean to a hairline because the inhabitants are tending them from dawn to dusk.

All through the mild hours, dark figures move across the causeways with a slow, liquid stride, carrying nothing more than a peeled hardwood lever, a coiled length of knotted rush-cord, or a flat wooden paddle. They pause at a weir, crouch, press two fingers to the wet stone just beneath the waterline, and measure the tension of the sheet-water by touch. A millimeter too high, and they ease a water-swollen sluice board upward with a silent twist of the wrist; a millimeter too low, and they tap it home with the heel of the palm. Every district’s water-level is a shared accord, negotiated daily between neighbors. When two precincts fall into a quiet dispute—over the placement of a new retaining wall, or the right to harvest a particularly rich sedge bed—they do not shout or strike one another. One precinct subtly tightens a shared sluice by the width of a reed, starving the other’s reflection of its crispness; the other answers by sending an uneven, discordant chatter of drops down the connecting conduit. Sooner or later, delegates from both walled clusters walk out to the exact midpoint of the linking causeway, kneel face to face with their palms submerged in the runnel between them, and stay there—sometimes for half a day—until the flow is tuned back to agreement.

They live entirely without fire.

In a world of constant mild warmth, perpetual mid-season, and no flesh to roast, fire would be an obscenity—something jagged, dry, and suffocating that would blister their water-grooved skin and lay ash across the clear panes of the cisterns. Every craft they practice relies instead on water, weight, and patience.

To quarry the basalt for a new foundation or boundary wall, they never strike blindly at the living cliff. They search the young volcanic benches for hairline fractures already begun by the cooling of the earth, drive wedges of bone-dry highland hardwood deep into the seam, and then build a tiny clay lip around the crevice to channel a trickle of seepage onto the wood. Then they leave it. Over days, the wood drinks and swells with slow, irresistible power until the lava sheet parts with a single dull crack along its natural plane. To hoist one of those massive uncarved lintels onto the upright jambs of a doorway, they rig timber levers and thick ropes of twisted sedge-fiber anchored to huge, tightly woven reed baskets suspended over the stepped drop-offs. They open a runnel into the baskets. As the clear water pours in, weight gathers silently until the stone lifts free of the bench, inches at a time, guided into place by six pairs of long, steady hands without a single grunt or shouted order.

Their diet is born from the same unhurried alchemy of water and sun. Having no animals beside them—no herds, no fish, no birds, no insects—they draw everything from the disciplined greenery of the terraces and the sunlit film of the shallow flats. They pry up the pale, starchy rhizomes of the tall sedges and peel the dense, sweet inner pith from fallen tree-fern trunks, soaking both in clay-lined troughs until the bitterness washes out, then pounding them with smooth river cobbles into damp, heavy cakes. On the broadest, shallowest basalt pans—where the water lies no deeper than two fingers and warms thoroughly under the afternoon glare—they cultivate vast, translucent carpets of emerald-gold algae and mineral-rich slime. Skimmed off with fine rush frames and laid out on the sun-heated roof slabs to thicken into glossy, pliable sheets, it tastes faintly of sweet rain and dark rock, and provides nearly everything their slow, steady metabolisms require.

At midday, when the sun stands highest and the glare off the flooded flats is sharpest, many of them retreat through the narrow rectangular doorways into the thick dry-stone halls.

Inside, the light drops to a cool, velvet dimness, entering only through the doorway and thin ventilation slots tucked high beneath the overlapping basalt roof-slabs. The floors are laid with thick, tightly plaited mats of dried rushes raised on low stone piers—and beneath every floor runs a shallow, uncovered stone conduit, so that even while resting out of the sun, they remain connected to the gentle pulse of the city’s water. Stacked neatly along the unmortared walls are coils of hand-twisted fiber, curing bundles of peeled timber, river cobbles sorted by weight and smoothness, and dry hardwood wedges waiting for the next quarry.

Here, they let themselves dry to that pale, matte ash-grey, resting stretched out on the mats or sitting in small silent circles, knotting rope with nimble, tough-skinned fingers that work by touch alone. Sometimes one will crouch behind an elder with a bundle of wet emerald moss and a pinch of fine dark silt, gently scrubbing away the faint pale mineral lines that build up along the grooves of an older back—the chalky contour maps left behind by decades of highland sun and evaporating seepage. It is their closest gesture to an embrace: slow, attentive, palm pressing lightly along the fluted ribs until the dark luster returns.

And if you wonder where their young are—why you can peer into the clearest, sun-warmed cisterns and see nothing darting between the glossy reed stems—look again, much closer, beneath the overhanging cushions of emerald moss.

They do not dart because darting belongs to prey and predators, and there are neither here.

In the quietest interior cisterns, deep inside the walled precincts where no heavy footfalls jar the stone, the adults anchor clusters of leathery, dark-olive sacs among the submerged sedge roots. At a glance they look exactly like smooth volcanic cobbles resting in the shade of the weir. When the young finally slip free, they are scarcely the length of a forearm—pale, nearly translucent, with oversized dark eyes and delicate, feathery ridges along the sides of their throats that drink oxygen directly from the still water. For their first long year—measured not in winters, since no winter ever climbs above the cloud floor, but in the slow unfurling of fern fronds—they never leave the cistern. They cling flat and motionless to the shaded undersides of the basalt ledges, creeping imperceptibly across the submerged stone to graze on the velvety green film that coats the rock.

If you stand above the basin and look down, you see only clear water, clean stone, and stillness. Only if you kneel for a long while, resting your fingers quietly against the rim, might a pale, slender shape detach itself by half an inch from the shadow of a reed-stem, curious at the unfamiliar rhythm of your pulse.

When their limbs lengthen, their throat-ridges seal into smooth listening slits, and their hide darkens enough to bear the highland sun, they climb out onto the warm stepped ledges for the first time. For months they move clumsily along the lowest benches, escorted closely by two or three older kin who keep a steady hand near their narrow backs, teaching them the weight of the flagstones, the hairline reading of the weirs, and the quiet discipline of never stepping into the silt.

They live long, unhurried lives, and age comes to them not as frailty so much as mineralization. Over decades, despite all the careful moss-scrubbing, the dissolved silica of the weeping rock settles deeper into their joints and the outer grooves of their skin. Their stride shortens. A builder who once hauled lintels on the upper escarpments gradually narrows their territory to a single quadrant, then to a single courtyard, and finally to one warm, flat basalt bench beside a sunlit weir, where they sit for hours on end, indistinguishable from a weathered stone pillar until a slow dark hand rises to clear a single drifted leaf from the water’s edge.

When they die, they leave no scattered bones to whiten on the lava terraces. Their frames are dense, flexible cartilage and dark, horn-like fiber rather than brittle lime. Their kin wrap them tightly in soaked sedge-mats, carry them silently up the stepped tiers to the raised garden beds beneath the upper cliffs, and lay them deep in the packed wet earth among the roots of the tree ferns, weighted beneath flat slabs of uncarved basalt. In the perpetual warm damp, the moss and fern-roots take them swiftly and cleanly. Within a single cycle of fronds, nothing remains but richer greenness.

It is tempting, watching them glide through their luminous quiet, to imagine that nothing ever troubles them—that they are merely caretakers of an eternal afternoon.

Yet the plateau is young. They remember that better than anyone.

Every few decades—sometimes twice in a long lifetime, sometimes not for a century—the volcanic rock beneath the city settles. It is rarely violent; usually no more than a deep, guttural groan rising through the bedrock, followed by a subtle sideways shudder that lasts three heartbeats. To a creature of the air, it would pass almost unnoticed. To the inhabitants of the plateau, it is the supreme event of existence.

In an instant, every mirror-flat basin shivers into crossed rings of light. Along the upper escarpments, a horizontal seam that has wept steadily for eighty years pinches shut; half a mile away, a fresh crisp fracture opens in the black lava with a bright rush of clear water. Across entire quadrants, the hairline tide marks tilt. One cistern begins to brim darkly over its stepped foundation; another drops, exposing pale, drying rims of emerald moss to the bare sun.

Then, and only then, does the whole city reveal its true numbers.

Out of the narrow dark doorways they come—hundreds, then thousands of tall, slate-black figures pouring silently along the dead-straight causeways. From every central cistern, the submerged basalt slabs ring out in overlapping, deep-water chords that vibrate through the soles of their feet. There is no panic, no shouting, no frantic running—only a vast, breathtaking mobilization of quiet weight. Dry-laid walls are dismantled stone by stone, because dry-laid stone was never meant to be permanent; it was meant to be unbuilt and re-leveled whenever the young earth shifted beneath it. Fresh hardwood wedges are driven into newly fractured benches. Water-baskets fill and swing heavy lintels across new openings. Clay is kneaded wet and pressed into fresh runnels along the altered slope of the rock.

For weeks, the basin hums with the steady cobble-tapping of a whole people reshaping their home to meet the new path of the water. And when the fresh seepage has been guided cleanly through notched basalt weirs, when the tilted basins sit level again and the moss along the lips drinks evenly once more, they withdraw by degrees back into their precincts, easing the sluices home until the surface returns to glass.

Only along the far southern lip of the plateau, where the sharp black teeth of rock drop straight away into the level white floor of cloud, do a few of them keep a different watch.

Sit near that rim toward evening, when the mild sun drops low and the cloud sea turns the pale gold of wet rushes, and you will see them spaced widely apart along the angular ledges, kneeling motionless with their dark backs turned to the city. They face outward over the emptiness, letting the warm, moist updraft rise off the cloud deck and condense in bright silver beads along the sharp ridges of their shoulders and brow. They are listening to the breath of the world below—the unseen lowlands from which nothing ever climbs, except moisture and light.

On the rare occasions—perhaps once in ten years—when a violent storm far beneath the clouds flings something living up over the rim, it is these watchers who find it first: a single exhausted moth, wings tattered and frantically twitching on a flooded flagstone, or a small storm-lost bird gasping in the thin warm air, its heartbeat hammering wildly against the stone.

The watchers do not harm it, nor do they touch it while it still thrashes. They stand back on the dry ledges, two or three of them together, watching with their wide, slow-blinking eyes in something very close to dread—struck by the terrible heat of its breath, the chaotic speed of its wings, the way its small violent motion sends jagged, meaningless ripples racing across a hundred paces of calm water. When the stillness finally claims it, one of them steps carefully forward, lifts the strange, feathered weight on a broad woven rush-frame without letting it touch their bare skin, and carries it inland to bury it deep beneath the furthest fern-beds, where the rock and roots can fold its brief noise back into green silence.

If you remain standing in the center of the wide stone plaza now, you will notice that the city has already noticed you.

No one rushes toward you. No alarm rings in the air. Yet out across the shin-deep flooded flat, at the narrow doorway of the nearest walled precinct, a tall dark figure has stopped moving—half in shadow, half catching the bright sheen of the afternoon sun. Another kneels beside a flagstone runnel fifty paces to your left, palms submerged, reading the unfamiliar, uneven cadence of your weight on the dry stone. Far down the dead-straight causeway, a hardwood sluice board eases downward by the thickness of a blade of sedge, sending a single soft, unmistakable pulse through the conduit beneath your boots.

They are waiting, with the bottomless patience of water and volcanic rock, to learn whether you are a storm-blown disturbance passing briefly across their glass—or whether you know how to stand still enough to hear the plateau answer back.
