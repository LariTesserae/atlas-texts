At first glance, drifting down a terraced alley through the olive gloom, you might think the city has been abandoned. The braided reed runners lie empty over the black ridges. Curtains of olive-weed hang undisturbed from the eaves. Even when a slow *tock-tock-tock* reaches you through the water, the sound arrives so smeared and directionless that you can search three overhanging porches and see nothing but dry-stacked lava, crusty anchor-ropes, and pale travertine.

Then a shadow shifts beside a cracked lintel, and what you took for a mound of wet basalt unfolds an arm.

They do not hurry, and they do not stand out from the rock they quarry. Seen close, in the twenty-foot clarity of the dim wards, they are heavy-built and long-ribbed, standing roughly the height of a tall human when upright, though they seldom hold themselves stiffly straight. Their skin is thick, matte, and dark—the color of wet volcanic slate, warming toward dull umber along the belly and inner limbs—with a faint, slippery sheen of natural wax that keeps the silica dust from settling too fast. Where their palms, knuckles, and the undersides of their splayed, four-toed feet meet the world, the hide thickens into horn-dark pads tough enough to brace against fractured basalt without opening. Even so, volcanic glass cuts deeper than any callus, which is why they lay down the kelp-skin paths and why they move with a grave, unshakable deliberateness, setting each foot and hand down with the calm precision of a creature that has never once been chased.

They are built heavy for the bottom. Their bones are dense and solid, carrying little wasted air, so that on the stepped terraces they walk with a slight forward lean, steadying themselves against the rising plumes of warm water. Around their narrow hips most wear braided slings hung with three or four holed basalt stones—smoothly chosen ballast that keeps their heels planted while they haul lava slabs or drive bone pins. When one wishes to leave the rock and cross to the suspended cane-districts, there is no frantic kicking. She simply slips two stones off her hip-cord, loops them over a basalt peg beside a cane-pillar, pushes off once with her broad feet, and lets the rising thermal draft carry her. Unfolded in mid-water, her limbs stretch out unexpectedly long and spare, her flat-palmed hands steering with tiny, economical tilts as she glides upward through the hanging baskets like a slow, dark leaf caught in a chimney.

Their faces take longer to read in the pewter light. The skull is long and low-browed, tapering slightly toward a broad, blunt jaw. Their eyes are wide-set, large, and dark as wet obsidian, catching no bright glint; every few minutes a pale, smoky inner lid draws lazily across them to wipe away mineral grit. Yet it is not only their eyes that map the alleys. Tracing the line of the jawbone, the heavy ridge above the brow, and the backs of both wrists are neat rows of shallow, velvety-dark pits, tender and flush with blood just beneath the surface. Through those pits, the invisible geometry of the caldera shines clear as noon. They feel the wavering oil of a fresh warm seep from thirty paces off, read the cool drift sinking from the outer cliffs, and sense the subtle thermal shadow cast by a neighbor standing motionless behind a hanging curtain of weed. In a basin where light dies early and distance fades to gray smear, heat is their true daylight.

Because the valley sits so high and its water is bath-warm, breath is a scarce, carefully budgeted coin. Their long torsos house enormous, slow-emptying lungs and hearts that beat with a deep, spaced-out thud—barely a dozen strokes to the minute when they are at work, fewer still when they rest. A builder wedging a bone shim into a tilted sill can hold a single breath for the better part of an hour, moving with such unhurried thrift that scarcely a ripple disturbs the silt beside her knees.

Yet they are bound to two different kinds of breath, and those two breaths divide the city into its working slopes and its sleeping benches.

Near the warm central dome, where the water is thin and stingy, lungs alone must carry them. But along the outer terraces—where water cooled against the frosted cane-crowns sinks slowly back down the raw cliffs, richer from its brush with the mountain air—they need not rise at all. Along the sides of their necks and sweeping down beneath their collarbones lie deep, overlapping pleats of thick dark hide, clamped tightly shut while they climb, haul, or quarry. When a resident settles into a stone alcove along the cool outer downwelling to sleep, or rests after a long shift on the ledges, those heavy pleats slowly relax and part. Out from beneath them unfurl dense, feathery fringes the color of dull crimson moss. They drift limp in the cool descending current, drinking quietly from the water while their owner hangs half-suspended in a reed hammock or sits propped against a basalt wall, eyes veiled by their smoky lids, heart slowing almost to a halt for half a day at a time.

It is the young and lean who make the cold climb to the ceiling.

In pairs, smeared lightly with dark algal grease against the chill, they scale the sixty-foot cane-pillars, hauling collapsed, stitched-hide bladders bundled over their shoulders. As they rise out of the bath-warm bowl, the olive twilight turns to a bruised, flat gray and the water bites harder at their jaw-pits. Up near the trembling skin of the valley, where the hollow cane-tips stiffen into crowns of white rime and slush-ice, they cling to the cold stalks and tilt their flat, upward-set nostrils just through the surface. The mountain air above the steam-layer is painfully thin and freezing; drawing it in feels like swallowing fine glass, stinging their warm throats and misting their brows with quick frost. One steadies the other while they invert the hide-bladders against the churned mist, scooping thin pockets of cold sky-air, cinching the bone valves tight, and hooking heavy basalt weights back onto their belts so they can sink before the cold steals too much heat from their limbs.

To the people of the caldera, that bright, freezing void above the clouds is no heaven. It is the thin lid of the world—a hungry, wind-scoured rim that drinks warmth and gives back almost nothing, save the rare, startling windfalls when some thick-furred mountain beast or great broad-winged bird loses its footing on the unseen black peaks and tumbles through the slush to sink slowly into the cane-groves. When such a carcass drifts down, work across three wards pauses. It is hauled gently to a stone platform and divided with quiet reverence, yielding tough hide, dense terrestrial bone, and long lengths of sinew the basin itself could never grow.

Most of the time, the air hauled down from the ceiling—and the gentler, sweet gas captured directly off non-sulfurous floor vents—goes to feed the inverted hide-bells.

Under water, no one speaks. The basin smothers sharp sounds and blurs deep ones into homeless rumors, so in the alleys and hanging walkways they converse with their bodies: slow, expansive signs shaped with their pale-edged palms, subtle tilts of the chin, and percussive messages tapped out with bone pegs or stone knuckles against the calcified mooring-cables. A worker three cane-decks up can lay her palm flat against a crusted anchor-rope and feel the crisp, humming cadence of a neighbor’s *tock—tock-tock—tock* traveling up the stiff fiber-stone from the ledges below: *Seep shifting. Bring mud. Two hands needed.*

Real voices belong exclusively to the bells.

Climb beneath the weighted rim of a wide hide-dome at the slow turn of the day, and you will find four or five residents gathered there—standing chest-deep on a dry-stacked bench or hooking their long forearms over a lashed cane-rail, their heads and broad shoulders lifted clean through the shimmering horizontal mirror of the water-skin into the dim, warm vapor above. Water sheets slowly off their slate-colored brows and drips back into the dark pool beneath their chins. Above them hang nets of salted olive-weed and strips of curing grazer-mantle; beside their elbows sit hollowed basalt troughs where pale cane-pith mash ferments gently over the rising floor-heat, filling the cramped pocket with a sour, yeasty, mineral tang.

Here, in the thin, damp air where no spark could ever live, they speak. Their voices are startling after the padded hush of the alleys: low, resonant hums threaded with soft throat-clicks and long, breathy vowels that curl around the resin-sealed ceiling without ever growing loud. They trade news of cracking lintels and cooling vents, settle boundaries over a contested cane-grove, hum slow, droning songs whose pitches bend with the gentle rocking of the hanging deck, and teach their young the genealogies of the basin’s seams—which fissure was born in a grandmother’s youth, which lower terrace was sealed when the dome last swelled. When a council runs long and disagreements harden, no one shouts; the trapped air simply grows heavier and damper with shared breath, pressing down until stubbornness fades into quiet, or until someone dips back below the mirror-skin to haul a fresh bladder from the rack outside.

No fire cooks their meals, yet they do not eat wholly raw. Where the central dome cracks open into scalding splits—too hot for moss or bare flesh—they lower tightly woven silica-clay pouches weighted with basalt pebbles on long, stone-crusted cords. Packed inside are cakes of minced crimson heat-moss, pickled olive-weed, sour mash, and sometimes the sweet, oily meat of the blind ivory crawlers scooped patiently off the dome-lips with fine reed nets. Left dangling in the wavering plume for an hour or two, the pouches come up steamed through and steeped in mineral salt.

With the other creatures of the basin, they keep an unhurried, watchful intimacy. They herd the broad, pale scalloped grazers gently from terrace to terrace, nudging them with dull cane-poles toward sluices and basket-walls choked with fresh algae so the slow beasts will rasp the stone clean; only when an old grazer dies do they claim its tough, translucent hide and its dull horn-lip, which makes the finest scraper in the town. The dark, long-bodied swimmers that glide through the cane-forests are another matter—wary, solitary neighbors and the basin’s only true quarry. Hunters do not chase them; nothing in these wards can outswim a startled beast. Instead, two hunters wait motionless for hours near the frosted cane-crowns where the swimmers must rise to breathe, wrapped in hanging curtains of weed until their own slow heartbeats vanish into the hush, striking once with weighted obsidian spears. A single swimmer provides thick hide for a new curing-bell, ribs for house-shims, and long back-tendons that will be braided into mooring-ropes and left to turn to stone.

Even with their waxy skin, no resident escapes the basin’s deepest habit: everything that stays in this warm water thickens.

Over weeks, fine chalky rime gathers wherever their hide flexes least—dusting the backs of their broad shoulders, crusting lightly behind their ears, rimming the outer edges of their forearms and brow-ridges. Left untended, the travertine would slowly stiffen their joints and blind their thermal pits. Scraping it away is not a solitary chore; it is the quiet core of their affection. On almost any terraced courtyard, you may come upon two kin seated knee-to-knee on a kelp-skin runner, one leaning forward with dark eyes half-veiled while the other draws a blunt horn-blade along the curve of her spine in slow, patient strokes, lifting pale curls of mineral frost that drift off into the olive haze. Friends scrape one another’s elbows and shoulders after a long haul; parents gently clear the fine chalk from the jaw-pits of their young; lovers trace the ridges of each other’s collarbones with oiled thumbs, keeping the hidden crimson fringes supple and free.

Yet they do not fight the stone entirely. Like their architecture, they make a quiet pact with it. Many adults dab deliberate lines and whorls of white mineral-ash paste along their outer forearms, their cheekbones, or the bony ridge of their spines and leave those patterns unscraped for years. Slowly, the warm water builds upon the paste, raising pale, knobby ribs of living travertine fused tight into the dead outer grain of their hide. A mason who has worked the volcanic ledges for forty years wears thick, chalk-white guards along both forearms that turn aside fractured basalt better than any stitched sleeve; a respected seam-reader may carry a faint, pale crescent across her brow that marks her stillness and her years.

Children enter this slow world pale, smooth-skinned, and far too buoyant for the footpaths. Born in the warm, sheltered shadows beneath the inverted canopies of the ribbon-fronds—where broad leathery leaves arch downward to hold pockets of bath-warm stillness—infants float upward like pale seeds if left unmoored. For their first years they ride tucked into weighted kelp-slings across a parent’s chest, or drift on short, soft reed tethers anchored to a courtyard stone while their elders work nearby. Their thermal pits are wide and raw; to a young child, the basin is a dazzling, overwhelming tangle of warm currents and cold shadows. They learn to read the drafts of temperature before they learn to sign with their hands, learn to grip volcanic glass without drawing blood before they ever touch a blade, and do not make their first climb to the freezing ceiling until their lungs are broad enough to hold the shock of thin sky-air without panicking.

Because the floor beneath them is never finished being born, their society revolves around patience and forewarning rather than possession. No family owns a house forever; a terrace that sits plumb and pleasantly warm for three generations may tilt within a decade, its dry-stacked walls splitting cleanly upward as the basalt swells, its precious hearth-seep cooling to a dead crack—or worse, opening wider until scalding plumes drive everyone out.

The most quietly honored among them are the seam-readers: older folk, often half-blind in the dim light, whose jaw-pits and wide palms have grown exquisitely tuned to the rock’s slow moods. They spend long, motionless shifts near the cracked central dome, laying both hands and the flat of their cheek directly against the raw, hot basalt, listening through bone and skin for the faint, glassy *tink* of deep stress and tasting the slightest shift in the wavering heat. Months or years before a ward splits beyond patching, a seam-reader will climb its alleys, tap a quiet summons on its anchor-cables, and gather the household beneath its widest hide-bell to tell them where the rock is lifting and which lower vaults will soon need to be emptied, mud-sealed, and surrendered to the crust.

Friction still finds its way into the padded hush of the wards, though it unfolds at the basin’s own glacial pace. When one terrace’s vent cools while a neighbor’s split widens with generous warmth, resentments simmer for seasons. Nobody smashes a wall or severs a mooring-line—to cut a hanging district’s ropes is an unthinkable horror, a crime that brings every surrounding ward down upon the offender in silent, overwhelming unanimity. Instead, grudges are fought with curtains and weight: a slighted household hangs thick mats of cultivated olive-weed along its eaves to deflect a warm rising current away from a rival’s moss-baskets, starving their harvest by a single degree; or refuses to pass a neighbor’s messages up a shared anchor-cable; or leaves a common stair-runner frayed so their rivals must cross the sharp basalt on bruised soles. Such feuds can linger for years, resolved only when a seam shifts again and forces both houses to brace a shared lintel together, or when the elders sit both families neck-deep beneath the same hide-bell until the damp air runs thin and stubbornness gives way to weary, murmured settlement.

As the residents grow truly old, their lives bend back toward the valley’s outer edge and toward the stone itself. Their skin loses its waxy sheen; their hearts space their beats further apart; the climb to the freezing ceiling becomes too costly in warmth. Most elders move down from the hanging cane-districts to the quiet outer benches, where the cool, breath-rich downwelling sweeps gently over their unfurled crimson fringes and lets them live almost without effort. They spend their days watching tethered infants drift beneath the fronds, flaking clean obsidian blades with slow, unerring strikes, or sitting still for hours while younger kin scrape the thickening lime from their elbows and knees.

There comes a season, though, when an elder grows tired of the scraper. She asks her kin to leave the pale rime along her shoulders, then her wrists, then her jaw. Her movements shrink to small, economical gestures; her days lengthen into deep, cool half-sleep along the outer stones. And when the last long beat finally leaves her chest, there is no earth here to dig and no flame to consume her.

Her kin bring her gently inward to the warm water, wrap her long body tight in oiled kelp-skin, and bind her knees to her chest with fresh sinew. Over the bundle they smooth a thick, patient coat of white mineral-ash paste—the same paste that chinks their vaults and hardens beneath the water. Then they carry her down to a newly emptied lower room near a warm seep, or wedge her securely into a niche beside the basalt anchor-ledges of her ward, and seal the doorway with stacked lava slabs.

They do not mourn as though she has been erased. Within months the ash-paste sets hard as bone. Within years the warm, mineral-heavy basin begins its quiet work, coating the wrapped bundle in knobby rinds of pale travertine and delicate crowns of silica needles. As the caldera floor swells upward by a fingernail’s breadth each year, new terraces are dry-stacked atop the sealed lower chambers, new sinew cables are lashed to the thickening anchor-ledges, and the dead become, quite literally, the pale stone footing that holds the living town in place.

Hours pass in the dim olive water with scarcely a frond stirring. Out across the tiered basalt slopes and suspended cane-decks, dark, heavy-boned figures move at the exact speed of the valley’s great turning loop—warming at the cracked center, climbing slowly toward the frosted lid for a sting of cold air, drifting back down the cool outer cliffs to rest with their crimson fringes open to the dark. Beneath a tilted hide-bell, a low hum of voices rises and fades. Three terraces away, a muffled *tock-tock-tock* travels lazily through the hush: someone driving a bone pin into a splitting sill, or scraping pale stone off a neighbor’s back, quietly keeping pace with a mountain that is still rising beneath their feet.
