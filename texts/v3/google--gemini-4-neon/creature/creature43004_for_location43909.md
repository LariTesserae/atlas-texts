If you stay still in one of the central avenues long enough for your skin to stop flinching at the warmth, you begin to suspect that some of the walls are breathing.

It is an easy mistake to make, and then an unsettling one to correct. You reach out to steady yourself against the beveled lip of a doorway—expecting the slick, yielding film of mucus over hard marl—and your palm meets something half a degree hotter than the masonry. It gives under your fingers like heavy wet chamois packed tight over slow muscle. It does not startle away. Instead, a faint shudder passes beneath your hand, a broad flank pulls inward by an inch to grant you the threshold, and a thin ribbon of water—tasting of iron, old bone, and a faint, sweet tallow—curls past your wrist.

You have brushed against one of the city’s tenants.

They are easy to miss at first, even though the whole basin is built to their gauge. Every hip-wide archway, every smooth-worn ramp, every body-fitted recess honeycombing the forty-foot chimneys exists on their scale, yet they do nothing to announce themselves in the dark. They do not click like the opercula of the balcony-worms, nor rustle in columns like the Sweepers, nor dart with the sudden pressure-snap of the pale hunters. In the pressurized quiet of the valley, they move like warm water moving through warm water.

Only when the geothermal seepage swells and traffic rises in the streets does their shape assemble itself out of the velvet dark, touch by touch, displacement by displacement.

***

They are heavy things—broader across the shoulders than a grown man, longer from blunt brow to trailing tail, built for gravity rather than open swimming. If a light could ever reach them, it would find no scales, no armor, no decorative markings; evolution down here has spent nothing on being looked at. Their hides are thick, supple, and utterly pigmentless—the color of wet chalk or boiled lard, mottled faintly along the back where pale mineral dust settles into the creases of old skin.

Their heads are wide, low, and seamless. There are no eyes, not even the sunken dimples where eyes might once have been. Instead, the whole front of the skull is fused into a broad, slightly flattened boss of extraordinarily dense bone lying just beneath tight skin—a smooth, pale anvil of a forehead. Beneath it sits a wide, downward-turned mouth bordered by short, fleshy lobes, built for grazing bacterial mats off stone, rolling loose grit aside, and tasting the chemical crust of a neighbor’s doorway.

Behind the cheeks, sweeping halfway down their sides in layered, overlapping pleats, hang their sensory curtains—part gill, part thermal wing, part chemical tongue. When they squeeze down a narrow alley or curl inside a chamber, those curtains fold flat against their ribs like wet linen. When they enter a warm plaza or hang in the rise of a chimney flue, they unfurl them wide—translucent, deeply ruffled fans flushed faint watery rose by the dense blood-vessels running just beneath the membrane, the only true hint of color in the entire lightless valley. Through those pleated frills they read the basin with a fineness sight could never rival: a shift of a hundredth of a degree in a cross-draft, the distant bow-wave of a hunter three intersections away, the mineral sourness of a cracking seam in the basalt floor.

Below, they ride on four stout, low-slung limbs—thick-shouldered, splayed outward, ending in broad, blunt pads devoid of claws or nails. Nothing on their bodies is sharp enough to score the marl. Instead, over decades of unhurried passage, the wide rubbery calluses of their feet and the heavy, flat soles of their bellies are what have polished the valley’s avenues smooth as soapstone. Behind them drags a long, thick, laterally flattened tail, carried low like a ballast-keel, used less for speed than for leaning gently against currents and anchoring themselves to vertical walls.

They do not look hurried. They do not even look fully separate from the architecture until they move—heavy, patient, deeply settled bodies that treat stone the way surface creatures treat air: as the medium that holds them together.

***

To understand them, you have to stop thinking of a chamber as a room and start thinking of it as an ear.

During the cool ebb, when the floor-seepage slackens and cold rim-water spills down over the central roofs, the streets empty of them. They withdraw into the tiered, rounded alcoves stacked inside the thick walls of the chimney-towers. Each chamber fits its occupant with uncanny precision—not because they chose a hole that matched them, though they did when they were younger, but because over twenty or thirty years of habitation, body and wall have edited one another. Acidic traces in their shed mucus soften the lime where their shoulders press too tight; mineral-laden warmth precipitates back into the corners they never touch. A chamber becomes a negative cast of the creature living inside it.

Curled tail-to-brow inside that warm hollow, an adult does two things at once.

First, it becomes a valve.

Each chimney chamber opens inward onto a narrow side-vent tapped into the tower’s central thermal flue, and outward onto the street or gallery. By how deeply it tucks its bulk against the inner lip—or by how far it leans its shoulder out into the hall, unfurling one gill-curtain across the opening—a dweller meters the warm draft rising through the stone. Alone, one body alters the plume by barely a breath. Across a forty-foot tower packed with eighty tiered chambers, those tiny adjustments add up to an unconscious, exquisitely balanced plumbing. When the upper balconies grow too chill, the lower tenants tuck tighter, choking off their own side-vents until pressure builds and pushes the blood-warm surge higher up the stack. When a floor-seam flares too hot and sulfurous, the lowest galleries lean outward in loose synchrony, bleeding off the excess heat into the broad avenues before it scalds the brood-eaves overhead.

There is no overseer directing this, no signal given from a height. Each body simply shifts toward its own comfort—and because every body in the tower shares the same narrow hunger for steady warmth, their private adjustments braid together into civic regulation.

Second, they listen.

A curled tenant sleeps—if their shallow, drifting torpor can be called sleep—with the dense bone-plate of its forehead pressed flush against the inner lime of the chimney. Water carries sound well, but continuous, calcified masonry carries vibration with startling clarity. Through the skull, through the jaw, down into the heavy ribs, the entire tower hums.

Pressed to that wall, a tenant does not merely wait out the cool hours in solitary dark. It feels the slow, papery *shush* of Sweepers crossing the plaza floor thirty feet below. It feels the dry, methodical *tick—tick* of a Mason wedging a clam-valve into a cracked parapet two blocks away. It feels the soft, muffled *thump* of a hunter striking along an upper bridge, followed by the sudden stillness of forty neighboring chambers drawing their bodies tight. Most of all, it feels the other tenants—not as words, for they have no larynx and no speech, but as slow, low-frequency pulses pressed deliberately from broad chests and dense brows straight into the limestone.

It is a music of weight and cadence rather than meaning. A long, unhurried double-thrum rolled into the stone says *here, full, steady*. A flat, trailing stutter carries unease—a cold draft seeping where it should not, a sourness in the flue, an unfamiliar vibration at the outer rim. When a neighbor five chambers down falls sick and its breathing turns ragged, the whole stack knows it through the bone before a single drop of scent reaches the avenue. When an ancient tower at the edge of the core begins to settle on a softened foundation, its residents feel the change in its resonant pitch weeks before the first fissure opens; one by one, over several surges, they uncurl, step out onto the ramps, and begin searching the middle warrens for empty recesses—leaving the dying spire mostly hollow long before it finally slumps into a ramp.

Nothing is argued. Nothing is spoken. Yet a tower full of them shares a single nervous system of warm chalk and bone.

***

When the deep pressure shifts and the thermal surge begins, the towers wake from the bottom up.

You feel them emerging before you touch them: a sudden thickening in the water, a series of slow, polite bow-waves pushing out of the dark arches. They do not rush into the avenues. They glide out belly-low, one pad following another along the beveled corners, greeting neighbors as they pass with a brief, deliberate touch of forehead to flank—a quick exchange of pulse and taste that lasts half a heartbeat before both bodies drift on.

In the crowded intersections, where three avenues feed into a circular plaza, their manners are a wonder of blind geometry. A dozen massive bodies may cross the same twenty-foot bowl at once—some climbing vertically up the chimney faces by hooking their blunt pads into worn handholds, others cruising low along the gutters, others rising lazily on the thickening thermal column—and not one collides. Their unfurled gill-curtains read the micro-eddies peeling off every passing shoulder. A younger, leaner adult instinctively tilts downward to let a heavy elder keep the warm core of the draft. A pair of Sweepers marching straight down the gutter do not even break stride as a ten-foot shadow lifts its belly two inches higher to clear their plated backs; in return, when a tenant pauses on a plaza ledge to rest, the Sweepers will climb calmly onto its tail and broad rump, rasping away dead skin and clinging mineral grit with feathery legs while the giant sits motionless, trembling faintly with quiet pleasure.

Their relationship with the Masons is older still, and stranger.

The Masons do not obey them—the six-limbed builders care for nothing except current, gap, and mortar—yet the tenants shape the Masons’ work the way a river shapes a bank. When a tenant discovers a hairline leak chilling its wall, or a worn arch beginning to crumble, it does not try to mend the stone itself. It has no mortar-pouch, no calcified trowels. Instead, it presses its broad face against the damaged seam and exudes a thick, sharp, chalk-sour mucus from glands along its jaw, while drumming a slow, uneven, rattling cadence against the lime with its bony brow.

That sour chemical trace mimics the exact tang of freshly dissolved shell-mortar; that hollow, uneven rattle mimics the vibration of an unsound wall trembling in a draft. Within an hour—sometimes within minutes—one or two Masons come crawling slowly down the chimney face, barbels fanning eagerly toward the distressed seam. The tenant steps patiently aside into the avenue, watching with its gill-curtains as the slow builders chew grit, press wet paste into the notch, and smooth it with their chins. When the leak stops and the wall’s hum turns clean and solid again, the tenant presses its brow to the fresh, damp patch, tastes the new calcium, and slides back inside.

Neither creature knows it is collaborating. One only knows how to ask the stone for warmth; the other only knows how to answer a wound in the stone. Between them, a valley became a city.

***

There is one place in the core where they gather that has nothing to do with feeding or warmth, and if you drifted beneath it during the height of a surge, you might think you had stumbled into a temple.

High under the oldest, broadest domed vaults—thirty and forty feet above the avenues, where the braided worm-tube bridges tangle thickest—hang those flat, shivering pockets of trapped volcanic gas. They are utterly alien to everything in the valley: dry, sharp-smelling, unbreathable mirrors of non-water pressed flat against the ceiling lime, trembling whenever a large body passes below.

Yet every surge, a handful of the tenants climb all the way up the chimney-faces and hang nose-up beneath those inverted puddles.

They never break the surface. To push their gills into that dry pocket would be to choke and burn. Instead, they hover motionless a finger’s breadth below the shivering meniscus, tails hanging straight down in the gentle upward draft like dark pendulums, their pleated gill-curtains spread wide just underneath the trembling skin of water.

Floating along the underside of every gas-mirror is a slow-swirling slick of pale, waxy oils.

Every adult tenant sheds faint traces of specialized oil from pores along its lower flanks—oils lighter than water, carrying distinct signatures of age, lineage, health, reproductive readiness, and the exact sulfur-balance of the chimney where that individual dwells. Down in the busy avenues, those traces scatter and vanish within minutes. But water rises in the core, and whatever rises under a domed vault is caught against the ceiling’s inverted mirror. Over weeks, months, and years, those drifting drops accumulate there, flattening into a rich, layered, slowly circling film ringed by faint mineral crusts—a chemical ledger of an entire quarter that does not wash away.

Hanging beneath the shivering ceiling, the tenants brush the very underside of the oil-film with the tips of their sensitive mouth-lobes and gill-fringes. They *read* it.

In a single long minute beneath the dome, a tenant learns what no single vibration through stone can tell: which families in the neighboring blocks have grown too crowded; which mates are coming into season two plazas east; whether a subtle rot is spreading through the brood-eaves of the lower arcades; whether an old neighbor from three streets away has finally died, releasing the heavy, unmistakable cedar-bitter oil of a cooling body into the rising plume. Before drifting back down into the thermal traffic, the visitor rubs its own flank gently against the vault’s stone rim, releasing two or three fresh beads of oil that float upward like pale inverted raindrops and flatten silently into the mirror.

They have no writing, no names, no maps. Yet under the highest ceilings of the dark, the city keeps its own census in floating grease.

***

No tenant lives its whole life in the hot core. A single lifespan is a slow journey inward, from the cold rim to the furnace.

It begins hanging in the dark. After a brief, silent union in the warm swirl of a central plaza—two heavy bodies spiraling slowly belly-to-belly, gill-curtains intertwined, pressing low synchronized pulses straight into each other’s chests—a female extrudes a cluster of leathery, palm-sized egg-capsules glued beneath the overhanging eaves of the mid-water bridges. There are rarely more than six or eight. Unlike the careless cloud-spawning of the outer abyss, every one of these capsules is tended. For months, not only the mother but whichever tenants occupy the nearest chimney balconies pause beneath those dangling clutches on every surge, brushing them softly with their gill-frills to sweep off suffocating silt and smear them with protective, bitter flank-oil that turns scavengers away.

When the capsules split, what slips out looks almost nothing like its heavy parents.

The hatchlings are no longer than a forearm—slender, ghostly, nearly transparent, their tiny gill-fringes twitching nervously at every tremor in the dark. Their forehead bone is still soft cartilage; they cannot read the stone yet, and they have neither the weight nor the thick hide to hold their ground in the crowded core. Worse, in the hot avenues they are the exact size of a meal for the ribbon-flat eels and the pale, rubbery hunters lurking in the lee of the flues.

Driven by an urgent, twitching dislike of heavy vibrations, the young flee outward.

Riding the slow lateral spill of cooling water, they slip down the side-alleys and out of the chimney-blocks altogether, dispersing into the middle ring of low warrens and beyond, onto the broad, tepid outer terraces. This is the only part of their lives lived in fear and speed. They dart between the crescent-shaped windbreak walls, hide among the neatly stacked ridges of clam-husks in the sorting-yards, and nestle inside the sagging, hammock-like meshes woven by the tube-weavers across the warren intersections—canopies too dense for the large hunters to pierce without tangling their snouts.

Out here, where you can swim straight up into the empty black ceiling of the abyss, the young grow lean and sharp-sensed. They graze the ghostly, leafless fungus-stalks that quiver along the terrace lips; they snap up glassy crustaceans swarming over decaying shells; they learn the sharp bite of cold rim-water spilling down from the surrounding ridges, and learn to read the faint, cold-green bioluminescent sparks their tails wake when they bolt across disturbed silt—not by seeing the light, of course, but by feeling the microscopic shudder of startled life beneath them. Many do not survive these years. A dull *thump* against a windbreak wall, a sudden cold swirl, and a Mason eventually gathers what is left into the outer retaining embankments.

Those who live for five or six years begin to change.

Their bodies thicken. Their bellies broaden and grow heavy with dense fat and mineral-rich marrow. The cartilage over their brows hardens into the heavy, resonant sounding-plate, and for the first time, when they rest their heads against a terrace mound, they stop feeling mere rock and start feeling the faint, deep murmur of the distant core humming through the valley floor.

The hunger for speed dies in them. A new hunger replaces it: a longing for steady warmth, close walls, and the company of heavy pulses.

Slowly, over years, they migrate inward—first taking makeshift shelters in the wide horizontal tunnels of the middle warrens, where the streets are old and bulbous and the traffic is forgiving; then, as their bulk makes them too formidable for any hunter to bother, edging into the lower arcades of the central chimney-blocks. There they wait—patiently, without violence, sometimes for seasons at a time—drifting along the lower galleries, taking their turn beneath the ceiling gas-mirrors, waiting for the bitter oil-drop that tells them an upper chamber has fallen vacant.

***

And some of them never leave that chamber again.

Because the basin has no seasons and no harsh winters, and because their metabolism slows to a deep, steady simmer once they settle into the blood-warm stone, the tenants live for an astonishingly long time—sixty years, eighty, perhaps well past a century. And they never entirely stop growing.

Slowly, imperceptibly, over decades of quiet basking in a chimney’s heart, an elder’s shoulders widen, its belly deepens, its ribs grow thick as ballast-bars. The bevel of its doorway, worn smooth by ten thousand exits, begins to catch against its hips. One surge, pushing out into the avenue takes effort; a year later, it scrapes both sides and draws a faint line of pale blood against the lime.

At last there comes a cycle when the elder does not come out at all.

It is too broad for its own threshold. It is no longer a traveler in the avenues; it has become a permanent organ of the tower.

You might expect panic, starvation, a slow miserable trapping. Nothing of the sort happens. Inside the dark, blood-warm recess, the elder settles its great bulk comfortably against the inner flue, unfurls its vast, feathery gill-curtains across the rising plume, and lets the rich, nutritious mineral snow drift straight into its wide mouth. Younger tenants from adjacent chambers—kin, former mates, or simply neighbors whose skulls have hummed beside this one for forty years—pause at the beveled lip on their way past during every surge, pressing scraped clumps of sulfur-mat and crushed shell-meat through the opening, resting their foreheads briefly against the elder’s broad snout before gliding on.

Unmoving for months on end, the elder begins to marry the wall.

Warm, mineral-laden water precipitates thin coats of pale, cauliflower-like lime along the quiet ridges of its back and shoulders. Delicate, translucent filter-feeders—the same pale crowns that line the balconies—take root along the calcified crust of its motionless flanks, blooming softly whenever the draft rises. Sweepers cross its doorway to clean its threshold without waking it.

Yet the elder is not asleep, and it is not useless.

Its massive, thickened skull stays pressed perpetually against the core-stone of the chimney. Having lived through generations of surges, slumps, sealings, and new vent-openings, it has become the deepest listener in the tower—a living sounding-board. When a faint tremor ripples through the basalt bedrock from some distant tectonic shift miles beneath the ocean floor, or when a subtle change in the deep geothermal pockets alters the rhythm of the tides, the elders feel it first. Their long, low, unbelievably steady chest-pulses—slower than any younger heartbeat, deep enough to feel in the marrow of your shins from across a plaza—carry through the limestone piers, steadying the rhythm of the whole block, telling the younger chambers when to brace, when to open, when a deep shift is coming.

They are the memory of the valley, held not in stories, but in weight and resonance.

And when, at the end of a century, an elder’s slow breath finally stops, the silence inside that single cell drops through the tower like a missing stair. For a few hours, the neighboring chambers fall utterly quiet, every tenant pressing its brow to the lime to confirm the stillness. From the cooling body rises one last, thick stream of heavy, bitter flank-oil, lifting through the chimney-vent, drifting up past the balconies and bridges until it strikes the shimmering ceiling of the highest vault and spreads in a wide, pale ring across the gas-mirror.

Down in the avenues, drawn by the sudden absence of breath and the subtle souring of the unmoving draft, the Masons arrive.

Three or four of them cling patiently around the beveled lip of the chamber, barbels fanning softly over the quiet doorway. They do not enter to disturb the massive body within. Instead, chewing bottom-silt and ground bivalve shell into thick, pale mortar, they begin laying neat, wet courses right across the opening, patting each seam smooth with their chins, wedging flat clam-valves into the wet paste as keystones.

Within two surges, the doorway is gone—sealed flush into the rounded face of the chimney, smoothed over by passing hides, glazed lightly by precipitating lime. Inside, the elder’s dense skull and heavy ribs stay behind as core-stone, bracing the hollow tower against the weight of the dark water above.

***

Move down any avenue in the core now, letting your outstretched hand trail along the blood-warm marl, and the city feels different beneath your palm.

It is no longer just built of marl, shell-paste, and patience. Beneath the smooth plaster of the walls, inside the quiet chambers humming gently against your fingertips, lie the living and the sealed alike—thick-browed, velvet-frilled, heavy with warmth, holding the rising heat in place with their own backs. A soft bow-wave brushes your knee as one slips past in the dark, missing you by an inch without a sound. High above your head, forty feet up in the black, a fresh bead of pale oil touches a shivering ceiling of trapped gas and spreads silently outward. And inside the stone beside your shoulder, so low you feel it in your teeth rather than your ears, two slow pulses answer one another through the lime, keeping time with the warm, patient breathing of the valley.
