You could hang motionless in the middle corridors for half a morning and swear the basin holds nothing larger than a bronze snail.

Your eye is trained to hunt for motion—a darting shadow, a flash of flank, the shiver of a reed where something has brushed past—and here nothing brushes past. Only if you give up looking for animals and let your gaze soften into the slow geometry of the canopies does one of the hanging shapes begin to detach itself from the plants.

At first it seems to be a torn strip of upper frond caught on nothing, drifting ten feet below a ceiling of olive and copper leaves. It is tall—roughly the height of a standing person—and nearly as translucent as the water around it, veined in faint rose, smoked jade, and pale amber where the vertical sun strikes through. Yet no stalk anchors it to the valley floor, and no rootlets tether it to the canopy above. It simply hangs there, dead-still, its broad side-flaps spread out flat upon an invisible density plane as if resting on a table of glass.

Wait ten minutes. Wait twenty.

At last, inside the slender central column of its body, something shifts. You see a ladder of clear, nested chambers—like a stack of hand-blown glass ampoules encased in pale, fluted cartilage—and within the uppermost bulb, a bead of silver gas shrinks slowly to the size of a pinhead. A drop of denser, shimmering fluid swells in the chamber below it. There is no flick of a fin, no ripple along the hem of its draped tissues, not even a quiver in the curtain of pollen-dust resting an inch beneath it. It simply grows heavier by a fraction of a grain, yields to the invisible shelf, and sinks with liquid ease into the bright corridor below.

That is how they move through a world too delicate to touch in haste.

They are few—perhaps no more than three or four score across the entire miles-wide basin—and so deeply woven into the valley’s stillness that even their own shadows look like drifting leaves. To swim here with muscle and thrust would be an act of vandalism; a single violent stroke would shred papery fronds grown over decades and smear the sharp water-skins that hold the basin’s food aloft. Instead, they live by buoyancy and geometry. Their bodies are hydrostatic engines, built not for speed or escape, but for exquisite, effortless suspension.

Running the full length of each one’s vertical core is a spine of pale, pearlescent mineral, hollow and segmented around those stacked internal ampoules. By drawing salts across thin inner membranes or releasing microscopic traces of gas from their blood, they adjust their own weight with the same unconscious ease a land creature brings to breathing. When they wish to cross an open expanse between drowned ridges, they do not row. They unfurl their wide, gossamer mantles—broad, ruffled sheets of living tissue that can span ten feet from tip to tip—tilt their bodies by a barely perceptible degree, and shift a droplet of heavy brine from a lower chamber to an upper one. As they sink four feet over the course of half an hour, the slant of their wings carries them fifty yards sideways in absolute silence, leaving neither wake nor bruise behind them.

Those mantles are lungs, sails, and gardens at once. Laced with delicate vessels and seeded with symbiotic green-gold cells, they drink the fierce mountain sunlight directly through the clear water, giving the dwellers a languid, plant-like thrift. They do not hunt, and they rarely need to gather much; when they spread themselves flat across the shimmering boundary between two strata, microscopic fringes along their undersides sweep up the settled dust of pollen, shed spores, and mineral silt like a soft brush passing over polished stone. Sometimes a solitary ribbon-grazer, no longer than a hand, will row right across a resting dweller’s outstretched hem to glean the same settled film. They do not shoo it away. They let it pass, feeling the faint, feathery tickle of its gill-edge as a pleasant small company.

Only when they draw near the razor-edged stone or the sturdy bladder-stalks do their arms emerge from beneath the folds of their mantles.

Each has six limbs, tucked neatly against the upper ribs when drifting, slender as willow switches and multi-jointed, ending in three long fingers. Everything else about them is glass-delicate and tearable, made for an untouched sea; only their fingertips are armored. Grown from the dissolved lime of the warm fissures, every digit is sheathed in a smooth, pale thimble of hard porcelain-white mineral. With these hard tips they can grip the unweathered edges of volcanic glass without bleeding, lift a wedged bronze snail carefully out of a pinching crack, or draw themselves hand over hand up the tough cane-stalks without once grazing the fragile stained-glass canopies above.

At the top of the column, where a head would be, the translucent cartilage swells into a narrow, upright bell—less a skull than a lantern. Inside it, faintly visible through the clear wall, coils a luminous skein of silver-white nerve tissue. Set around the rim of the bell are seven dark, bead-clear eyes of unequal size, arranged to read a layered world. Two broad lenses tilt upward, built to bear the fierce vertical caustics and distinguish the shadow of a passing mountain cloud from the first bruised darkening of a rain squall. Three narrow eyes face horizontally, tuned to polarized light and subtle refraction; they see the invisible water-skins as plainly as panes of ice, and can spot the tiny optical shiver of another dweller adjusting its trim a mile away across the clear expanse. Two smaller eyes look down, adapted to the warm jade twilight of the clefts and the mirror-bright brine below.

Between the eyes sit thin, taut plates of crystalline calcite, no larger than fingernails. These are both their ears and their voices.

In a basin with no surf, no wind, and no current, sound is an astonishingly sharp instrument—and the stratified water makes it stranger still. Because each horizontal layer holds a slightly different warmth and saltiness, a faint tone made inside a given stratum does not scatter evenly into the depths; it bends back off the boundaries above and below, trapped inside its own liquid shelf, sliding outward like a flat sheet of paper for miles.

They speak in the acoustics of the valley. Flexing tiny filaments against their calcite plates, they release sounds so small and clean that an unaccustomed listener might mistake them for the natural noises of the basin: a single dry *tink* like an oxygen bubble striking a leaf; a brief, crystalline click; or a long, trembling harmonic hum, pure as a wet finger circling the rim of a glass. If two dwellers hang thirty yards apart beneath the same canopy and wish for privacy, they aim a faint click upward so the spongy moss-ribbons and layered fronds drink it up. When one wishes to address the whole valley, it sinks to the exact shimmering plane between the second and third strata, presses its bell-crown flush against the invisible ceiling of the denser water, and lets out a slow, two-toned ring. Trapped in the warm channel, the note glides uninterrupted from cliff wall to cliff wall, heard clearly by every dweller resting on that shelf while the upper corridors and the deep troughs remain wrapped in their drowsy hush.

Their conversations unfold over afternoons. A question posed at noon may be answered twenty minutes later, after the listener has drifted a slow yard to follow a slant of light and turned the tone over in its mind. No one speaks over another voice; to overlap would muddy the clean reflections rebounding off the dark slate walls. They discuss what matters in a timeless place: the slow softening of an ancient bladder-stalk whose holdfast is beginning to fail; a faint new warmth seeping from a crack on the western hogback; the shifting height of the third density plane after a dry season; the precise harmonic interval of a rain squall that drummed across the ceiling three winters past.

They do not build towns or carve the stone, yet the valley is gently tended by their hands. Over decades, they act as quiet gardeners of buoyancy. Where a rising cane-stalk grows crooked and threatens to push through an established canopy and shred a neighbor’s forty-foot leaf, a dweller will patiently wedge a flat pebble of dark slate into a fork near the stalk’s base, weighting it by just enough ounces to bend its growth sideways over the course of a year. They untangle curtains of dangling rootlets that have snagged on sharp ledges, gather beads of resinous amber sap from aging stems, and hang slivers of volcanic glass from hair-fine roots along the open corridors—not as wind chimes, for there is no wind, but as delicate touch-bells. A dweller gliding past on a slow afternoon may brush a single porcelain fingertip across a suspended obsidian shard, sending a thin, bright chime skating along the stratum to let a distant neighbor know it is passing by.

The nearest thing they know to weather—and to shared excitement—comes from above the taut skin of the surface. When a sudden high-altitude rain squall crosses the peaks, hammering the ceiling into frantic silver, the upper dwellers do not retreat to the clefts. Many of them brighten their upper bladders and rise deliberately into the topmost corridors just beneath the highest fronds. There they hang in small, loose clusters, tilting their upward eyes toward the shivering sky-skin, letting the rare, muffled hiss and the faint shockwaves run down their mineral spines. For twenty minutes the stillness breaks into bright, dizzying tremor; bubbles shake free from the undersides of leaves in glittering silver storms. Then the squall moves on beyond the jagged peaks, the hammered surface relaxes back into taut glass, and they drift downward again, humming softly to one another about the wild, cold rush of the upper air—a place as unimaginable to them as fire.

Their lives are sorted vertically, just like the water itself.

They reproduce rarely, perhaps once in a decade or two, when a slight surge of geothermal heat enriches the middle shelves with warm minerals. Two or three adults drift together into a quiet jade alcove where the cliffs pinch inward, unfurling their translucent mantles until the ruffled hems overlap. Glowing pearls of living thread pass directly across the shimmering contact while they hold a soft, steady unison hum that sets the water between them trembling in tiny standing rings. Months later, a few buds detach from beneath a parent’s mantle—no bigger than thumb-joints, clear as raindrops save for a bright pinprick of amber oil and a spark of pale silver nerve.

These young belong to the topmost sunlit canopies, where the water is lightest and the light pours straight down in heavy sheets. At first they cannot master their own salt balance; they cling with microscopic tendrils to the dangling rootlets, sipping oxygen pearls and settled pollen, watched over loosely by whichever elder happens to glide through the upper corridors. Their first, hardest lesson is stillness: not to jerk when a mineral seam creaks far below, not to bump clumsily against the fragile fronds, to read the shimmering edge of a water-skin before drifting into it.

As their spines lengthen and their ampoules multiply, the whole basin opens to them. For a century or more—for time in their veins runs as sluggishly as thermal drift—they wander every corridor and ridge, learning the acoustics of each cleft, the temper of every warm fissure, the biographies of the great stacked forests.

Yet the warm, mineral-heavy water leaves a slow record inside them. Decade by decade, dissolved lime, iron, and copper seep into the pale cartilage of their spines, turning the clear bone milky, then striping it with fine, gleaming bands of turquoise, pale ochre, and tarnished bronze. You can read an elder’s spine the way a forester reads the rings of a felled tree: a broad copper band from the hot years when the eastern vent widened and wept rust-colored brine; a thin, pale line from the cold summer when storm-clouds sat low over the peaks for months and dimmed the green light.

With the bands comes weight. Eventually, even with their uppermost chambers filled to bursting with bright silver gas, the oldest dwellers can no longer lift themselves to the sun-drenched upper canopies. They do not mourn the loss. Slowly, over the course of years, they yield to their own accumulated history and settle into the lowest habitable shelf—the deep, warm jade twilight just above the razor troughs where the dense brine pools.

Down here, the light is thick and restful, the geothermal heat rises gently from the split rock beneath them, and the silence is absolute. The elders hang in loose, solitary contemplation over the mirror-like boundary of the brine, their bronzed spines gleaming faintly, their wide mantles half-furled.

They are looking into the valley’s memory.

Viewed from the side, the top of the hyper-saline brine gleams like dark mercury; looked down upon from directly above, it turns as clear as air. The living cannot enter it for long—its fierce salt would parch and shrivel their gossamer mantles—so they hover just above the invisible threshold and gaze straight down into the sharp black troughs.

There, lying intact upon the unweathered scree amid whole fallen thickets of amber leaves and hollow stems, rest every generation of their kind that has ever lived in the basin.

Because decay almost ceases in the warm, still salt, nothing has ever turned to bone or mud. Down in the glassy stillness lie ancestors from centuries before the present canopies even sprouted, preserved whole and unbruised: translucent rose-and-olive mantles fanned gracefully over the black volcanic glass, mineral-ringed spines gleaming softly, clear rib-chambers still cradling a final frozen bead of gas. None have been scattered by current or picked apart by scavengers. Families and companions who chose to descend near one another lie side by side among the unbroken fronds.

And resting beneath the pale porcelain fingers of almost every preserved elder lies a flat sliver of dark slate.

During their long decades in the deep twilight, the old ones take up sharp needles of broken obsidian and score patient, microscopic notations into these slate tablets—nested spirals, fine parallel notches, tiny interlocking rings. It is not a language of spoken words, for their spoken tongue is made of ringing water and fades as soon as the echo dies. It is a geometry of record: the heights of the strata in their youth, the span of a forest that has since drifted down to join them, the deep cracks that shifted in the rock, the precise ratios of a favorite harmonic song. Laid down in the salt beside their makers, those dark flakes remain sharp and legible for as long as the mountains stand. Young dwellers, once strong enough to bear the pressure of the deep clefts, sink down to hover over the brine-skin and study the bright, delicate scratches through the clear salt, learning the shape of the valley as it was five hundred years before they budded.

When an elder feels its inner valves beginning to stiffen for the final time, there is no panic and no haste. A low, sustained tone glides outward along the deepest acoustic shelf—soft, clear, and unmistakably final.

Over the next two or three days, dwellers descend from every level of the basin. Five, twelve, sometimes twenty gather slowly in the warm jade dusk above the chosen trough, hanging at staggered heights like a still constellation of living lanterns. No one crowds the dying one. They keep a wide, respectful ring around the shimmering floor of the brine, humming at intervals so faint the sound feels less heard than held in the chest.

At last, the elder draws its porcelain fingertips across the small, freshly scored slate tablet tucked against its ribs, and releases the final pocket of gas from its uppermost chamber. A single silver pearl escapes the rim of its bell-crown and rises—drifting upward through the jade twilight, slipping through shelf after invisible shelf, sounding a faint, fading *tick* against the underside of each distant canopy until it reaches the bright hammered skin of the valley far above.

Heavy at last, the elder sinks. It pauses for an hour upon the mirror-sharp top of the brine, letting its weight settle evenly; two or three of the living drift close enough to touch their pale fingertips briefly to its outstretched hem. Then the surface yields without a splash, and the elder slips cleanly through into the crystal salt.

Down it drifts, slow as a falling amber leaf, coming to rest unhurriedly among the intact thickets and the sharp dark glass, its mantle spreading wide around it one last time before growing utterly still beside those who came before.

For a long while the living hover above the glassy boundary, watching through the clear brine until the last faint shiver leaves the elder’s fronds. Then, one by one, a tiny silver bead brightens inside each translucent chest. Without a ripple, without a sound, they lift away from the floor of preserved time and rise gently back toward the stacked green light of the canopies, leaving the valley as quiet, and as whole, as it has ever been.
