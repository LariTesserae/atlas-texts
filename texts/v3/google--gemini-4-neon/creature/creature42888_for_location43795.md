If you stand on one of the low whalebacks at noon and sweep your eyes across the shining miles, you will be certain you have the country to yourself. There are no rising roofs, no columns of smoke smudging the pale horizon, no beaten paths cutting through the scattered ribbons of wire-rush. Even the bright distances play tricks on you, lifting the far rock swells into the trembling haze so that everything looks uninhabited, unfinished, or long abandoned.

It is only when you stay still for an hour—long enough for your eyes to stop hunting for verticals and your ears to stop blurring the noise into a single bright hum—that the first small wrongnesses begin to show.

Look down at the rim of a saucer-basin dissolved into the pale gneiss, and you may notice a ring of flattened quartz discs laid edge to edge, far too deliberate to have been tumbled there by slow rain. Look out across a tea-colored braid half a mile off, where no crane is stepping, and a faint plume of milky cloudiness drifts just under the mirror, too wide and steady for a sifting bottom-fish. Look toward a distant rock swell, and what seemed at first to be a bleached outcrop of stone beside a gnarled shrub has a curve too even for geology—a low, pale carapace tucked flush against the bedrock, casting almost no shadow at all.

Then you hear it.

Threaded cleanly beneath the dry rattle of the sedge-crickets and pitched into the quiet spaces between the frogs’ barking relays comes a slow, deep, velvety *thwump*—not sharp like a crustacean’s snap, not hollow like a crane’s trumpet, but round and liquid, skating straight across the warm water from a mile away. A beat later, two crisp, glass-clear clicks follow it, like river cobbles struck together just above a mirror. From somewhere far off to your left, where the haze turns water and sky into the same pale silver, three answering booms roll back across the flats.

They are out there. They have been out there the entire time.

Wait without waving or shouting, and eventually one of them will emerge from the midday shimmer, wading unhurriedly along the contour of an ankle-deep pan. At first you only see a pale, wavering stem moving with the slow mechanical care of a stilt; then the haze lets go, and a person resolves out of the glare.

They are lean and long-shanked, with wide, flat-spread toes that grip the slick gneiss and the velvety algal felt without slipping. From forehead to shin they are coated in a matte, chalky slip of white kaolin clay, smeared on damp each morning to turn back the double sun pouring down from the sky and bouncing straight up off the water. Where the clay dries over their shoulders and knees, it crazes into fine, pale spiderwebs identical to the cracked mud aprons of the flats; around their calves, the warm water slowly dissolves it away, leaving faint milky trails behind their heels. Across the cheekbones and the bridge of the nose, every face wears a broad smear of dark, rusty iron-ochre mixed with fish oil to drink in the upward glare. Under that bright, mineral mask, their eyes are deep-set, steady, and perpetually half-narrowed, crinkled at the corners by a lifetime of open horizons.

They move the way the water moves here: without urgency, and without splashing. Each foot lifts cleanly from the silt and sets down soft. Ahead of them, nudged lightly by a fingertips’ touch, glides a wide, shallow tray woven tightly from bronze-tipped wire-rush and sealed underneath with dried clay and algal gum. It draws scarcely an inch of water, sliding over the warm braids like a water-strider. Inside it ride the quiet necessities of a crossing—rolled sheets of dried green felt, a bundle of smooth bone needles, a gourd-sized skin of clarified water, perhaps a sleeping child curled under a woven shade-hood, unbothered by the broad sun.

Follow them up onto a pale whaleback and you begin to see how completely the country has shaped their hands.

There are no houses that stand taller than a person’s chest, because there is nothing here to build tall with, and nothing to shelter from except light and rain. Their dwellings are low, saucer-backed vaults of green wire-rush bent into overlapping hoops, lashed with braided sedge, and plastered thickly with white kaolin mixed with fine quartz grit. Curved smoothly down to the bedrock, they catch no wind and break no skyline; from three hundred paces off, they vanish straight back into the pale gneiss. During the heavy rains, the outer crust softens and runs in chalky rivulets onto the stone, and afterward someone simply scoops up fresh clay from the nearest margin and smooths the dome back over with wet palms.

What startles an outsider most, standing in the middle of a camp where eight or ten people live, is what is missing.

There is no hearth. There is no ring of blackened stone, no smell of woodsmoke, no smudge of ash anywhere on the pale ground. In a country where the solitary gnarled shrubs clinging to the rock fissures take a century to put on a finger’s length of twisted trunk, nobody cuts wood, and nobody burns it. Those little olive-leaved trees are the oldest standing things in a world that has forgotten mountains. Each swell is known by the twist and name of its shrub. People hang nothing heavy from its branches; at most they knot a single thread of ochre-dyed rush to a lower twig to mark a kinship, or press a newborn’s dried cord into the handful of sand wedged beneath its roots, asking that the child grow as patient and unkillable as the tree.

Without fire, and in a land where even loose stones have been tumbled into smooth discs, almost nothing they make has a sharp edge. Their tools are rounded, worn, and tactile: flattened quartz cobbles for grinding and tapping, broad bottom-fish ribs polished to pale spatulas, hollow crane-bones, stiff rush-stems, and cured skins. Now and then a worker will strike two hard quartz discs together to lift off a thin, glassy flake for gutting fish or splitting sedge, wrapping the back of the flake carefully in clay and fiber so it cannot bite the palm. When the edge dulls, they do not toss it aside on the rock swell where a bare foot might find it; they drop it into a sandy runnel so the slow creep of water and grit can begin smoothing it back down again.

Their kitchen is the sun, the clay, and the warm mineral floor of the pans.

Like everything else that thrives here, they live directly off the quiet sunlit skins coating the submerged mud. In the late morning, when the algal mats are thick with trapped silver beads of oxygen, two or three people wade out into a pan with flat, wide paddles of woven rush and bone. They never strip a basin bare; they lift only narrow, measured ribbons of the olive and golden-brown felt, leaving wide margins intact to spread back over the silt before the moon turns. Carried back to the whaleback, the wet strips are laid out across the sun-baked gneiss. By mid-afternoon the rock is hot enough to draw the moisture straight into the pale sky, shrinking the mats into crisp, papery sheets speckled with natural mineral salt. Rolled tight and tucked under the dry rush-vaults, those sheets keep for months, tasting faintly of warm rain, sedge, and sweet chalk.

They do not cook flesh over flame; they steep and cure it in time and warmth. Bottom-fish and lungfish are filleted with bone blades and laid in shallow stone saucers filled with weak-tea water rich in tannins and crusted mineral salts scraped from the drying aprons; left in the afternoon glare, the pale meat firms and turns gently tart. Larger fish are packed in damp kaolin mixed with crushed rush-seed and buried in the sun-heated quartz sand along the margins until the flesh steams softly in its own juices. Every child learns to read the radiating fans of sorted sand-balls outside the pencil-wide crab burrows, coaxing out the pale-clawed dwellers with a trembling rush-stem, and every adult knows how to tap the cracked dry-season crust with a knuckle to find where a dormant lungfish lies sealed in its damp mud chamber—taking one, never two from the same apron, and patting wet clay smoothly back over the sleepers so the dry air cannot reach them.

Even their drinking water is an art of patience. The wide sheet-water is warm, cloudy with algae, and brackish in the hollows; so each household tends two or three deep saucer-basins in the high gneiss, rimming them with low curbs of hardened kaolin to catch the slow rains and covering them with tightly woven rush-mats so the sun cannot turn the water green. When rain is scarce, they scoop murky tea-water into wide clay-lined pans, stir in a pinch of pure white kaolin slip, and leave it undisturbed overnight. By dawn the fine clay has bound every grain of silt and carried it down to the floor, leaving a hand’s depth of cool, clear, faintly chalky water shining on top.

Because the leached quartz flats give their abundance thinly, people cannot crowd together. A single rock swell can feed and water only one household—a handful of kin, four to a dozen souls—and the next swell with a family on it may lie a mile or two away across the shining braids. From above, they are spaced out across the basin just like the pale cranes and glossy ibises, each keeping a wide perimeter of water to itself.

Yet nobody here is lonely. They live inside a single immense room whose floor is taut water and smooth bedrock.

Every occupied whaleback keeps a cleared sounding-edge—a clean tongue of gneiss sloping gently into an open pan, kept free of wire-rush so that vibrations can skate outward uninterrupted. At dawn and dusk, when the midday glare softens and the air settles heavy and still over the warm flats, the people come down to their edges and talk across the miles.

They do not shout; shouting tears apart in the open air and sounds ugly, like a startled bird. Instead they use the water itself as a mouth. Kneeling in the warm shallows, they plunge cupped palms into the mirror with practiced, hollow strikes—throwing deep, velvety booms straight across the surface, low enough to slip effortlessly underneath the sizzling rattle of the sedge-crickets. Between the water-booms, they lift their chins and send out long, clean, horn-like tones pitched carefully into the quiet spaces between the frogs’ relays, punctuated by the crisp *tink-tink* of two flat quartz discs tapped just above the skin of the pan. For matters that must travel farther still—a birth, a death, a thick run of bottom-fish along the eastern channels—they float thin, wide bowls of rush and cured skin upside down in the water-filled stone basins and strike them with smooth bone mallets. The whole ancient whaleback takes the note and hums it down into the bedrock, so that a person standing barefoot on another swell two miles off feels the message rising through the soles of their feet before their ears even catch it.

In a world that broadcasts every raised voice for leagues, privacy becomes a tender, deliberate craft. Two people who wish to speak only to each other never talk across a rock swell; they sit knee to knee with their foreheads resting together, or wade out into the nearest ribbon of stiff wire-rush where the constant dry hiss of thousands of hoppers wraps around them like a curtain. There they use their close voice—a dry, breathy murmur scarcely louder than the *plip-pop* of rising gas bubbles breaking the surface of the pans.

Living inside such a sounding board makes listeners of them all. Before a child can balance a rush-tray on the water, they are taught to peel the basin’s endless noise apart layer by layer: first the crickets, then the snapping mud-shrimp, then the frog-relays, then the gas-bubbles, then the waders, and finally the human cadences woven through them. An elder whose sight has finally surrendered to sixty years of double-sun glare is scarcely diminished at all. Sitting motionless on the warm stone with eyes closed, they can map five miles of shining flats by ear alone—telling you where a crane just lifted off, how fast a rain-sheet is shushing toward them across the western horizon, whose rush-tray is brushing through a sedge-tussock half a mile out, and whether a granddaughter three swells over is tired or joyful by the lift at the end of her evening call.

When the drier stretch of the year arrives, the whole basin draws a slow breath inward, and the people breathe with it.

Week by week, the bright sheet-water retreats a few dozen paces from the margins of the outer swells, laying bare wide, glistening aprons of cracked white kaolin edged with thin collars of chalky mineral salt. As the outer braids shrink into disconnected puddles, the distant households lift their shade-mats, roll up their crisp algal sheets, load their flat rush-trays, and wade slowly inward toward the broadest central pans—following the retreating mirrors alongside the cranes, the scoopers, and the ibises.

For a few quiet months, people who have spent the wet season singing across two miles of haze live within a stone’s toss of one another along the central swells. There is always a shy, delighted awkwardness in the first days of the gathering—grown cousins pressing their palms together in wonder at the warmth of a body attached to a voice they have heard every dusk since the last rains; children tumbling into the shallows to wrestle and splash, startling the older folk with the sudden unmannerly noise of it.

During the gathering, the flats hum with quiet commerce and repair. People scrape the pure mineral crusts from the drying clay aprons and pack them into reed tubes. They trade lengths of finely braided sedge, cakes of bright iron-ochre dug from the rusty low places, and—most prized of all—rare, perfectly flat discs of iron-veined quartz whose ring carries clear and sweet across the water. Young people who have spent eight months courting in braided call-and-response across miles of bright glare finally walk up to one another across the glistening white clay, grinning bashfully beneath fresh coats of kaolin and broad smears of fresh ochre. And on warm, windless nights, when the central pans lie so glass-flat that the stars shine underfoot as brightly as overhead, dozens of households gather along the water’s rim to drum the shallow mirrors together—a low, rolling pulse that travels clear to the empty horizons, answered only by the distant frogs.

Then, one afternoon, tall warm rain-clouds build along the bleached rim of the sky. You hear the rain long before you feel it: a vast, soft shushing sliding across ten miles of water, hushing the crickets one ribbon at a time as it comes. When it reaches the central pans, it patters gently across the flat mirrors for an hour, lifting the entire expanse by a finger’s width. Over the days that follow, the bright sheets creep invisibly back out along the faintest troughs, dissolving the chalky crusts without a trace and linking the outer basins back into a single shining plain.

Without ceremony or hurry, the households step back into the ankle-deep warmth, nudge their woven trays ahead of them, and drift out toward their separate swells. Every half-mile a departing family stops, turns back toward the hazy center, and slaps the water twice—sending a soft farewell rolling back across the waking flats until the midday shimmer swallows them whole.

Living for uncounted generations in a country that has finished becoming what it was going to be leaves its own quiet mark on the mind.

They have no stories of great climbs, no myths of raging floods or consuming fires, no tales of heroes who carved their names into cliffs. Nothing in their world remembers height, and nothing rewards haste. In their speech, anger is described as *stirred silt*, a temporary cloudiness that only makes it harder to see the floor; wisdom is *settled water*, clear all the way down to the pale quartz sand beneath. When a dispute flares between two swells—sharpened calls snapping back and forth across the mirror—neighboring households do not choose sides or rush over with clubs. They simply wade down to their own sounding-edges and begin a slow, steady, low water-humming until the bright acoustic bowl fills with calm, and the two sharp voices grow embarrassed by their own edges and quiet down.

Even death is folded gently into the flats’ frugal accounting. You cannot dig deep graves in ancient gneiss, and in the clay pans water rises immediately to fill any print of a heel. More than that, in a land leached thin by eons of slow rain, every grain of bone is precious mineral that the world cannot afford to lose.

When an elder’s breathing slows and stops beneath a pale rush-vault, their kin wash the clay from their skin in the warm tea-water, wrap them loosely in a fresh mat of wire-rush, and carry them out to one of the quietest, unpeopled swells at the edge of the basin. There they leave them to the broad sun, the patient waders, and the small pale-clawed crabs. A season later, when the slow rains have washed the bones clean as tumbled quartz, the family returns. They gather the pale bones gently, rinse them in the shallows, and tuck some into the narrow sand-filled fissures around the roots of their swell’s gnarled shrub; the rest they slip quietly into the deepest central pans, where the thick, velvety mats of olive and golden-brown algae slowly creep over them like living felt, drawing their old calcium back into the bright, sunlit engine of the flats.

And for a long stretch afterward, during the dusk relays across the shining water, the surrounding households keep a measured silence at the exact beat where that voice used to answer. Across that mile of warm mirror, only the sizzling crickets, the patient frogs, and the tiny *plip* and *pop* of rising bubbles fill the space—until a season turns, another young household wades out to take up the pale whaleback, and a new hand slaps the warm water to answer into the light.
