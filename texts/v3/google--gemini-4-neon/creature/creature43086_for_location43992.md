You miss them at first for the simplest of reasons: you came down through the mud looking for soft things.

Your eye is trained by the ordinary sea. It hunts for curves, for pale bellies and trailing filaments, for the slight eddy a fin leaves behind when it turns against a current. Nothing in this pocket stirs the water like that. Nothing breathes, nothing drifts, nothing swims. So your mind files the bright, deafening cleft as an abandoned machine and stops looking at the surfaces themselves.

Keep still long enough—let the cold settle into your wrists and the bedrock hum blur into the base of your skull—and your eyes begin to catch small, stubborn errors in the masonry.

Start with the stair-riser three paces to your left. The light from the floating noon-pane above it is clean and motionless, yet a narrow strip of the ceramic step is doubled, its pale grain offset by the width of a fingernail, as if you were peering at it through a thick, flawed lens. While you stare, that doubled patch lifts cleanly off the stone. It does not bend. It does not ripple. It steps up to the next riser, and the next, leaving the water around it completely unbruised—not a swirl, not a tremor, not a single displaced bead of light.

Wait for the sound. Each time that crisp distortion settles onto a higher tread, a tiny, dry *tick* bites into the bones of your jaw. You had thought it was a micro-valve hidden inside the wall.

Once your eye learns to separate that double-edged refraction from the plain glare of the caustics, the empty city vanishes all at once.

The balconies are crowded. The catwalks are busy. The tight, kitchen-table plazas are packed shoulder to razor-thin shoulder with residents who have been moving past your face since the moment you slipped through the basalt slot.

They are not animals, and they are not ghosts, and they are not machines built by some vanished hand. If you had to place them in any kingdom of things that live, you would have to invent a kingdom between crystal, origami, and tension.

Watch one pause at the top of the stairs, turning broadside to the noon light so its edges catch the bright beam. It stands roughly the height of a child, though height is a poor way to measure something built out of hinged planes. Its body is an articulated lattice of thin, translucent plates—clear as Iceland spar, rigid as kiln-fired glass—framed along their sharpest seams by hairline ribs of the same iridescent dark alloy that bands the tenements. Looking straight through its torso, you see the arched lintel behind it split cleanly into two slightly offset images, the way a slab of raw calcite doubles every line beneath it. Where its planes meet at acute angles, narrow pleats fold inward like the bellows of an old camera or the louvers of a shutter, holding deep, dry-looking shadows that never seem entirely wet.

It has no face, no eyes, no mouth, no soft hinge of meat anywhere. Its whole faceted shell is an organ of measure. It turns its uppermost triangle of spar toward a recessed plaque on the corner of the arcade, tilting by two exact degrees, then three, scanning the etched geometric characters by the way the daylight bends through its own clear plates. When it steps forward again, its lower edges do not push the water aside; for the fraction of a second a limb is in motion, that thin mineral blade turns faintly hazy—phase-perforated, just like the gliding housing blocks—letting the water pass straight through its substance without resistance, before snapping back to steel-hard solidity the instant its corner locks onto the ceramic floor.

That is why the pocket looks dead. A thousand citizens can cross an intersection at full stride and leave the water as still as glass.

And they are not all one size. Once you know how to read the double-vision shimmer along the walls, you realize the city’s dizzying shifts in scale are not architectural whimsy. They are a census.

Up inside the finger-sized towers wedged into the window frames, tiny ones dart and cluster—slivers no longer than a thumbnail, bright and needle-sharp, composed of only three or four hinged triangles of spar. They flicker along the sills in brisk, angular bursts, tucking themselves completely flat against the louvered shutters when they rest, indistinguishable from a bevel of pale glaze until one springs open and steps sideways down a vertical wall. Along the middle terraces, larger citizens—knee-high, chest-high, tall as a standing person—move with heavier, more elaborate geometry, their bodies layered with nested pleats, counter-weighted struts, and fine mineral scrollwork that catches the noon light in tight, prismatic hairlines.

Lower still, wedged deep into the fractured corners where the raw black basalt thrusts through the masonry, sit the oldest of them. You had walked right past two of them thinking they were buttresses. They are massive, broad-shouldered assemblages of darkened spar and sintered ceramic, grown so dense with decades of accreted mineral that they barely step at all. Their lower pleats are fused directly into the lintels and piers of the street. Yet they are unmistakably awake: every few minutes a seam along their flanks opens by a millimeter, venting a silent, warmer thread of chalk-scented water, or a whole tier of their upper plates shifts with a slow, resonant groan—and down the alley, a three-story block of housing detaches smoothly from its terrace and glides across the gap in answer.

They do not have mothers and fathers. They grow by accretion, drinking dissolved stone out of the cold mineral water, laying down fresh microscopic laminae along every facet until their geometry grows too heavy, too intricate, or too loud for the narrow streets. When an elder reaches the limit of its symmetry, it cleaves. Along a chosen plane of weakness, a dry acoustic crack rings out, and a sharp, fingernail-sized sliver peels free from its flank—already rigid, already literate in the angles of the light, already hungry for a sill of its own. The sliver climbs straight into the miniature colonnades grafted onto its elder’s eaves, takes up its post beside a micro-valve or a drainage channel, and begins to gather stone of its own.

What you took for the mechanical racket of the pocket is mostly their voices.

Watch two mid-sized residents meet at the lip of a tight plaza where the gravity tilts sideways toward the eastern cliff. They stand perpendicular to the wall, soles clicked squarely onto the vertical undersides of an eave, facing one another across a span of clear water. Neither moves a limb. Instead, a pair of parallel plates along the flank of the taller one flexes outward by a hair’s breadth and snaps shut faster than the water between them can flee.

*CRACK.*

A whip-snap punches through your teeth. Between the two faceted chests, a microscopic pocket of vacuum tears open and implodes in a millionth of a second, throwing off a brilliant pinprick of blue-white light and a razor-thin whiff of ionized ozone. Before the spark fades from your retina, the smaller resident answers with a rapid triple-volley—*crack-crack-crack*—three blue-white stars winking in a neat vertical row, pitched a fifth higher, vibrating straight through the cartilage of your ears.

They speak in cavitation. Every snap is a syllable shaped by the exact spacing of their plates, the angle of the rebound off the nearby ceramic, the harmonic ring of their own alloy ribs. A quiet conversation between neighbors sounds like a distant crackling of ice; an argument in a cramped arcade turns the water into a blinding swarm of blue-white sparks and bone-rattling pops, the acoustic pressure hitting your chest like dry fingertips tapping your sternum. When they want to address an entire neighborhood, they lean a single tension-strut against one of the singing metal chimes hanging upside-down in the low plaza, letting the long, dense metallic chord carry their message into the bedrock itself.

Look upward, and you see how they travel. A narrow, heavily pleated resident steps to the glassy edge of one of the braided street-currents—those crisp, rectangular ribbons of rushing water that shoot down the center of the alleys without spilling into the stillness around them. For a heartbeat it stays phase-porous, sliding cleanly across the violent shear boundary that tried to wrench your wrist. Once centered inside the ribbon, it snaps solid. The current seizes it instantly, hurling it straight down the thoroughfare like a shuttle on a loom, whipping it around a razor-sharp ninety-degree turn at the crossroads and rocketing it vertically up the facade of a five-story terrace. At the fourth-floor arcade, it flickers hazy again, steps effortlessly sideways out of the tearing current, and lands without a wobble on a balcony where gravity pulls toward the ceiling.

Not one drop of water splashes out of place.

If you watch them long enough, you begin to understand the terror that built this city, and why there is not a single worm, minnow, or speck of plankton anywhere within its light.

To beings made of pure edge, refraction, and tension, ordinary life is a horror of blur. Flesh leaks. Soft bodies trail clouds of waste and sloughed skin; they swim by shoving ugly, chaotic turbulence through clean water; they die and soften into silt that coats bright bevels in gray slime. Darkness is worse—darkness is the collapse of angle, the state where a doorway and a wall cannot be told apart. And age, to them, is not wisdom; age is dulling, pitting, rust, the slow rounding-off of every knife-sharp corner until geometry melts back into mud.

That is why the basalt slot you crawled through is still fresh enough to slice a glove. They did not find an ancient ruin and move in. They *are* the reason the shelf split. They travel along the deep, raw fractures of the earth’s crust like a vein of living quartz, seeking newly torn bedrock before the sea and its soft creatures can dirty it. The moment a cleft opens, a vanguard of elders and slivers packs it tight from wall to wall, pinning open their paper-thin daylight panes—windows cut from some high, pale, perpetually overcast sky that has never known mud or tide—and locking the water into crisp, obedient ribbons.

And yet they are not wholly cruel to green things.

Follow a cluster of three finger-sized slivers down to a shadowy seam near the base of a terrace, where a louvered vent bleeds a faint, warmer trickle of chalky exhaust-water into the chill. Tucked right against the black rock are those tiny tufts of wire-thin aquatic grass and fingernail-sized rosettes of silver-green moss, vibrating in their perpetual, blurred hum.

The slivers do not tear them out. They gather around the seam with extraordinary delicacy. One extends a needle-fine plate of spar and gently lifts a single stray grain of flint off a moss rosette, carrying it out into the edge of a braided current to be swept away. Another tilts its uppermost facet to catch a stray beam from a daylight pane and bounce a clean, angled sliver of noon down into the crevice where the grass clings. The third simply stands beside the trembling blades, its own thin ribs vibrating in exact sympathetic pitch with the plant.

To the residents, the grass and moss are the only biological things in the world that have manners. They do not swim; they do not smear; they stay rooted in neat geometric rosettes and straight, wire-thin lines. Better still, their living fibers vibrate at supple, complex frequencies that rigid ceramic and spar cannot quite produce. In a city where sound is constantly hammering off stone, those tiny tufts of moss act as living acoustic dampers—softening a sour echo in a tight corner, sweetening a harsh harmonic along a stairwell. They are kept the way another civilization might keep songbirds in brass cages, or prized strings on an instrument: rare, disciplined luxuries of softness, carefully fed on the warm, mineral-heavy breath of the vents.

Then the deep chime sounds twice beneath the lowest foundations.

Before, when the flush hit, you braced yourself against a balustrade and shut your eyes against the roaring whiteout of bubbles. Keep your eyes open this time, squinted tight against the stinging surge.

A second before the currents reverse, the entire populace stops walking. Across every terrace, arcade, upside-down plaza, and finger-sized cornice tower, the residents lock their lower edges deep into the sintered masonry—anchoring themselves hard against their neighborhood’s gravity—and *unfold*.

It is the closest thing to beauty you will ever witness in this cold cleft. Figures that looked like narrow, folded shutters suddenly bloom outward into intricate, dazzling fans of clear spar, iridescent ribs, and stepped geometric wings, tripling their width until the narrow alleys are choked with overlapping lattices of crystalline lace. They do not hide from the twenty-second scouring storm. They open straight into its teeth.

The braided currents reverse with a deafening roar, and pressurized sheets of mineral-rich cold water slam diagonally through every street in a blinding, sparkling whiteout. Caught in the blast, the unfolded citizens ring like a cathedral of struck glass. You can feel it in your ribs: thousands of tuned plates singing together in one ferocious, glittering chord as the violent shear strips away every microscopic speck of dust from their joints, polishes every hairline scratch back to mirror sharpness, and flushes fresh dissolved lime straight across their hungry, porous inner seams. At the same time, pale ribbons of warmer ozone-and-chalk exhaust tear loose from their pleated bellies and vanish into the scouring tide.

They are drinking, bathing, and being born all over again, every four minutes, day and night.

Twenty seconds later, the roar cuts off like a slammed door. The bubbles vanish. The water snaps instantly back to noon-clear glass. Across every balcony and catwalk, hundreds of razor-bright, freshly whetted residents fold their gleaming pleats shut with a unified, cascading rattle of tiny ticks—and resume their crisp, tireless business, scrubbed clean back to the first hour of existence.

Only then do you realize that the silence in the alley immediately around you is not accidental.

Look down at your own gloved hand braced against the ceramic railing. A faint, lazy curl of pale silt—mud you carried in on your sleeve from the drowned shelf outside—has drifted off your cuff and is hovering in the clear water beside the balustrade. Around your wrists, your body heat is making the crisp water shimmer in messy, unfocused convection plumes. Every time you shift your weight, your bulky, soft limbs shove an awkward, rolling wake into the street, bumping clumsily against the straight edge of the braided current.

Six paces up the catwalk, a tall, heavily pleated resident has stopped moving. It is facing you directly, its clear calcite plates angled so that you see two overlapping, slightly shifted reflections of your own dark silhouette suspended inside its chest.

On the windowsill beside your shoulder, five fingernail-sized slivers have crawled out of their miniature towers and lined up along the ceramic lip, all of their bright triangles tilted at the exact same steep angle toward your glove. Behind you, in the tight arcade, two more mid-sized residents have stepped out from the shadows of the arches, their dark alloy ribs humming at a low, inquisitive pitch that makes your molars ache.

They have known you were here since the first drop of mud crossed the basalt lip.

The tall one on the catwalk takes two measured, wake-less steps closer. It does not look angry. It looks at you the way a master mason looks at a wet thumbprint pressed onto a newly glazed tile—with a cool, meticulous, civic dismay. Slowly, its two broadest chest-plates part by a hair’s breadth and snap shut.

*CRACK.*

A single, deliberate spark of blue-white light flares between you, sharp enough to leave a purple dot swimming in your vision. The acoustic pulse hits the center of your forehead like a flicked coin. You cannot translate the geometry of the wave, yet its intent settles straight into your bones with unmistakable clarity: a measurement, a classification, a polite and absolute verdict that you are out of alignment.

Nothing strikes you. Nothing lunges. Instead, the city quietly adjusts its plumbing around you.

Two paces to your left, a louvered shutter slides open with a crisp click, and a thin, flat sheet of braided current shoots neatly out across the alley, slicing cleanly between your sleeve and the clean pale masonry. Another ribbon peels off from an upper arcade, banking ninety degrees to box in the faint cloud of mud drifting from your knees, lifting every stray mote of outside silt cleanly away from the sintered walls and whisking it upward toward an exhaust vent. You are being quarantined inside a tidy, moving envelope of pressurized water—walled off from the stonework, routed politely, firmly, inexorably back toward the narrow jagged slot in the basalt where the dark, blurred coast waits.

Yet one of the tiny slivers on the windowsill does not retreat. Before the guiding current nudges your shoulder backward, it darts forward across the gap—turning hazy as it slices straight through the shear ribbon—and settles for a single, weightless instant on the knuckle of your glove.

You feel almost no pressure at all, only a sharp, cold prick of razor-thin geometry and a faint, high-frequency buzz, like the vibration of the silver-green moss, running down your finger. It tilts its three clear triangles toward the curve of your hand, checking your impossible, yielding, unrepeatable softness against the bright noon light. Then it snaps one microscopic blue-white spark—so small it feels like a grain of frost popping against your skin—flips effortlessly back into the street, and vanishes into the bright, packed refraction of its endless midday city, leaving you alone in the cold push of the current, suddenly and terribly aware of how blurry, how damp, and how temporary you are.
