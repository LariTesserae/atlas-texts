You think the city is empty because you are looking for things that interrupt it.

You look for a dark shape crossing the bright green of a facade, a sudden flutter under an eave, a leaf twitching out of rhythm, a footprint left behind in wet silt. When none of those appear, your mind closes the ledger and settles on the pale, fingernail-sized limpets clamped inside the quiet cracks behind the viaducts. That feels tidy. That feels like a world stripped down to heat, stone, and noise.

Then you stand still on an open plaza long enough for the roar to stop feeling like an assault and start feeling like a floor, and you realize the bare stone down the center of the avenues is not empty at all.

You catch it first at your feet.

Every plaza is glazed with a thin, clear sheet of hot water gliding steadily toward the outer drainage channels. Look down into that bright moving varnish—past the unfaithful reflection of the four-story block beside you, which stretches in the water into a slender tower pierced with narrow arches—and watch the pale, smooth stone around your boots.

Two paces away, the gliding water dimples.

A neat oval depression forms in the runoff, ringed by a crisp, tight collar of concentric ripples that ride cleanly over the mountain’s sub-audible tremor. A beat later, a second dimple appears a yard ahead of the first, while the first heals smoothly shut. Then another, and another, pacing calmly across the sunlit tiles toward the cliffside colonnade.

Nothing casts a shadow over those hollows. Nothing splashes. Yet if you lift your eyes just above the moving dimples, the lush wall of elephant-ear leaves on the far side of the avenue shifts slightly out of register—warped upward by an inch, sheared cleanly along two vertical lines, as though a tall, clear glass cylinder were drifting through the blazing mid-morning air.

Wait until the warp passes between you and a steaming vent, and the breath catches in your throat.

Inside the bent daylight, for half a second while the steam catches at the right angle, you see ribs. Pale, curved, wetly gleaming struts of porous travertine, stacked vertically like the arched haunches of a miniature aqueduct, open all the way through so that green fern-fronds show clean between them. Wrapped tight around that narrow lattice—clinging to it without dripping, held in a tall, seamless contour by the exact same buzzing acoustic cushion that seals the flooded ground-floor archways—is a thick, glassy mantle of clear scalding water.

It walks down the loudest strip of the street, where not even a moss spore can survive, and it is nearly invisible only because it is made of the same clear boiling water, wet stone, and trembling air as everything around it.

Once your eyes learn the refraction, the empty metropolis crowds in around you all at once.

They are everywhere.

Along the bare center of the avenues, they move in slow, unhurried files, keeping strictly to the roaring lanes between the razor-straight walls of foliage. They stand singly at the edges of cantilevered plazas, their clear outer mantles catching the fixed mid-morning glare like lenses of heat-shimmer as they lean slightly over the cloud-gulf. They ascend the looping cliffside stairways in long, spaced intervals, polishing the travertine treads to that dull, dished satin sheen. What you took for motionless spirals of rising mist along the viaducts are, half the time, simply *them*—pausing where the falling water-curtains pinch into rigid corrugated ribs.

And down in the thin reflective varnish of the plazas, where the waking stone lies about its own height, they do not hide at all. Look at the water-glaze beneath a crowded colonnade and the city suddenly swarms: tall, narrow-shouldered figures, half again the height of a person, gliding through reflected galleries that stand twice as high as the squat limestone blocks above them. In the water, their clear mantles disappear, leaving only their intricate, unmistakable frames—elongated spires of braided mineral-bone and taut, pale membrane, pierced with dark arched openings where the real air shows only blank refraction.

To understand what they are, you have to stop expecting flesh that fights the heat, and look instead at what happens when life stops resisting a place and becomes one of its standing waves.

Step close to one—as close as you can bear—when it pauses at the seam between the roaring avenue and the quiet edge of the ferns.

At arm’s length, the heat coming off it is fierce enough to dry the sweat on your eyelids before it can drip, though its surface is drenched. You feel its boundary before you touch it: that same elastic, furiously vibrating shell of air you meet at the flooded doorways, buzzing so hard against the hairs of your forearm that your knuckles ache. Inside that humming skin, the scalding water-mantle circulates without ever breaking its smooth contour—gallons turning over continuously along its flanks, gleaming like blown glass, held fast against gravity by the pitch of the stone inside.

And that interior frame is astonishingly strange.

It has no bones in the animal sense, no joints fitted with hinges or pulled by cords of muscle. Its structure is grown of the same porous, iron-stained travertine and calcified root-lattice as the city’s balustrades—pale bone-white along the outer ridges, stained pale rust and faint green in the hollows. The torso is a tall, tapered cage of narrow curved arches, completely hollow at the core, lined inside with smooth, wet chambers coiled like the interior of a whelk shell. Steam and hot water well continuously up through its flat, broad feet—wide travertine pads that skim the wet tiles on a cushion of micro-vibrations—pass through those spiral chambers, and vent silently from fine pinhole lattices along its narrow shoulders.

Where a head would sit, there is no face, no mouth, no eyes. There is only a slightly cupped, parabolic dish of smooth pinkish-white sinter, stretched taut across its inner hollow with a glossy, red-veined membrane identical in color to the fleshy creepers hanging across the alleys. It tilts that smooth dish slowly from side to side—not looking, but listening, balancing its internal cavities against the massive sub-audible tremor rising out of the mountain’s bones.

If you could lift one of them out of the roar and carry it down below the white cloud-floor into ordinary quiet air, it would die in three heartbeats. Without the relentless acoustic field driving through its porous lattice, the limestone frame would lock rigid, lose its ultrasonic suppleness, and freeze into a brittle pillar of common rock; its clear water-mantle would collapse into a scalding puddle around its ankles and leave nothing behind but an ornate piece of travertine statuary indistinguishable from a weathered cornice.

Here, inside the chord, the stone is as pliant as wet reed.

They do not walk by flexing tendons; they move by tuning the resonance along their legs, softening one porous strut to fluid elasticity while locking the next firm, gliding forward on the mountain’s own vibration with an unhurried, weightless precision that makes your own clumping stride feel clumsy and obscene.

That is why you never hear them speak, even though your brain keeps trying to carve phantom voices out of the white noise. Nothing can shout over this city. They do not try.

Watch two of them meet on a narrow terrace overlooking a triple-tier aqueduct.

They do not stop at a conversational distance. They glide straight into one another until their clear, boiling water-mantles touch—not splashing, not merging, but pressing flat like two giant soap bubbles meeting in mid-air, forming a razor-sharp, shimmering vertical pane of acoustic tension between their chests. Their smooth, red-veined dishes tilt forward until they nearly graze that shared lens.

If you are unlucky enough to be leaning against the parapet a foot away when they press together, the world drops out from under your ears.

For one dizzying, stomach-churning sliver of a second, the monstrous roar *vanishes* in a narrow wedge right between them. It is not a pause in the city—the chord across the ridges never wavers by a hair—but a localized, razor-thin cancellation, a pocket of dead, breathless silence carved straight out of the noise. Inside that tiny knife-slice of quiet, before the pressure slams back against your eardrums and sends you staggering into the ferns, you hear them: a dry, intricate, heartbreakingly delicate clicking of porous wet stone, like thousands of tiny porcelain cups settling softly into saucers, layered with the faint hiss of steam threading through hairline pores.

To us, speech is noise added to quiet. To them, who live inside a roar older than memory, speech is geometry carved out of sound—small, private rooms of silence built between two bodies, shared for an instant, and closed again without leaving a ripple in the air.

Yet they are not merely walkers on the avenues. To see where they gather, where they begin, and what they do with the endless mid-morning light, you have to press your face up to the flooded ground-floor archways.

Peer through the trembling, vertical wall of water sealing one of those dark openings. Wait for your eyes to adjust past the bright surface sheen into the shadowed, scalding hall behind it.

The flooded rooms are full of them.

Suspended motionless in the still, hot interior—neither sinking to the travertine floor nor floating to the ceiling—hang dozens of figures of every scale. Some are massive, twelve feet tall, their arched ribs thickened with cauliflower crusts of mineral deposit like the lips of the high aqueducts. Others are no longer than your forearm, delicate pale cages of newly precipitated sinter, their red-veined dishes still half-folded like unopened fern-fiddles. Thin, translucent filaments of spun mineral tether them lightly to the drowned columns and recessed alcoves.

They are not sleeping, and they are not swimming. Look closely at the curved struts of their submerged ribs and you will see tiny pearls of silver vapor shivering furiously along every edge, clinging without ever rising toward the ceiling. The drowned halls are tuning chambers. In the scalding, pressurized stillness behind the doorway-skins, the young precipitate millimeter by millimeter out of the mineral-rich flood until their hollow spirals match the exact pitch of the ridges outside; the elders hang there when the constant circulation has worn a chamber a hair too thin, letting the hot lime coat their interiors back to perfect tolerance before they push silently out through the vertical water-wall and step back into the sun.

Push your hand through the elastic threshold if you dare. Your fingertips meet scalding stillness—and then, a fraction of a second later, a slow, courteous, unmistakable *turn* in the water’s pressure. Every tethered figure in the drowned hall has tilted its smooth parabolic dish toward your intruding wrist, feeling the foreign, irregular thud of your pulse through the flood.

And once you see them in the flooded rooms and along the bare avenues, you begin to notice the quiet, relentless work they perform—the reason this whole impossible metropolis never rots, never clogs, never shifts by a single stone.

Follow one as it drifts along the invisible, razor-straight boundary where the dense jungle of elephant-ears and red-veined creepers terminates beside a bare street.

Watch closely. A glossy green shoot, engorged with hot moisture, tries to do what every plant on earth is built to do: it swells a fraction of an inch past the quiet seam, reaching into the roaring daylight of the avenue.

The passing figure does not strike it. It has no hands to break a stem, no teeth to chew a leaf margin. It simply leans its humming flank a fraction closer as it glides by. Its buzzing acoustic skin kisses the protruding green tip—and the excess does not tear, brown, or drop into the gutter. Under that focused pitch, the protruding millimeter of living tissue turns instantly into clear, warm vapor, absorbed cleanly into the figure’s circulating water-mantle.

They do not eat leaves. They eat *forward motion*.

Every living thing in the city is trying, quietly and desperately, to enter time—a frond trying to yellow and fall, a bud trying to burst fully open, a root trying to pry a cornice loose, a grain of mineral trying to break free and tumble down into the cloud-gulf. And these tall, singing citizens patrol the looping streets and terraced roofs, grazing on deviation itself.

Watch a pair of them pause beside a stone sluice where a flake of sinter has begun to loosen under the brisk hot runoff. They kneel—their limestone struts bending with silent, eerie fluidity—and wrap their clear water-mantles over the stone until the water around the crack goes milky with ultrasonic tremor. When they rise and glide on, the flake is welded seamlessly back into the smooth travertine bed, its edge softly rounded as if it had sat untouched for ten thousand years. Watch another drift beneath an overhanging fern just as a bead of water swells on a waxy leaf-tip, timing its passing tremor so cleanly that the droplet detaches at the exact instant, strikes the exact dark wet spot on the stair below, and pulls the next bead into place behind it.

They are not builders, and they are not servants of a builder. They are what grew when a steady-state engine became complex enough to defend its own balance. Every step one takes up a looping cliffside staircase—climbing three tiers only to emerge fifty feet higher on the exact plaza it left, moving up an octave the way a note climbs a scale—is mirrored somewhere across the valley by another descending, keeping the weight and pressure of the ridges summed strictly to zero.

Follow one out along the ridge-path toward the two parallel aqueducts that converge toward the distant needle-peak, and you will see how deeply they belong to the city’s bent geometry.

Where the stone arches actually narrow and compress, squeezing tighter and thinner until a human foot could find no purchase on them, the tall water-mantled figure does not slow down. As the travertine channel pinches down into a humming, paper-thin rib fused into the cliff, the figure compresses right along with it—its arched cage flattening sideways, its clear scalding mantle stretching into a luminous vertical blade no thicker than a wet leaf—until it slips effortlessly into the humming seam of the rock itself, vanishing without a sound while the lush terraced garden between the two bridges remains wide and sunlit beside you.

It will emerge moments later from a flooded ground-floor doorway half a mile back along the central ridge, stepping out into the bright morning as though distance were merely a chord it chose to resolve.

Even the tiny, eyeless limpets clamped inside the shadowed double-walls behind the viaducts begin to look different once you understand the rest of the city.

Pry into one of those dark, damp crevices where the acoustic pressure drops by a fraction, and look closer at those pale fingernail shells drawn tight against the warm wet sinter. Under a hard stare, their ridges are not quite the spirals of sea-creatures. They have the faint, stunted symmetry of tiny parabolic dishes, micro-latticed with sealed pores. Whether they are the last stubborn remnants of ordinary flesh hiding from the roar, or the cast-offs of the choir—young buds that slipped out of the flooded rooms before they were tuned, fallen out of the chord into a quiet crack, drawing down tight against the warm stone to hoard the last vibration they can survive—there is no way to tell. When you touch one, it does not flee. It only clamps harder, terrified of being pried back into the song.

And still, you have not seen the oldest of them.

To see those, you have to walk away from the bright cliff-edges, down narrow switchback stairs buried under curtains of red-veined creepers, until you reach the deep sunken cisterns in the bowls between the ridges—the ones whose still, hot water refuses to mirror the mid-morning sky.

Kneel on the satin-worn travertine lip and stare straight down.

Past your own sweating face, the water opens into that impossible inverted view: the dark, root-fringed underside of the mountain itself, hanging pale and wet above a bottomless black gulf that has no business existing beneath the daylight.

At first you see only the calcified roots dangling like pale stalactites into the cold dark below the clouds. Then your eyes adjust to the scale, and your stomach drops clean through the stone beneath your knees.

Hanging upside down from the porous belly of the peaks are shapes the size of cathedrals.

They are vast, inverted chandeliers of wet white travertine and taut red membrane, anchored by thick root-crowns into the mountain’s under-stone, their mile-long fluted ribs open to the unseen abyss below. They do not walk. They do not step. They hang suspended in the black updraft beneath the cloud-sea, drinking the raw, deafening fury rising from the bottom of the world, filtering it through colossal spiral chambers and driving it upward through the stone throat of the peaks as a single, unbroken, livable chord.

They are the counterweights. They are the lungs and the mothers of the quiet-eyed city above.

Watch the reflection long enough—holding your breath as the steam wets your lashes—and you will see a small, bright swelling gather at the lowest pointed rib of one of those hanging giants, like a bead of water forming on a leaf-tip. Only it does not fall into the dark. It detaches *upward*, rising cleanly into the porous root-tangle of the mountain’s belly, drawn straight through miles of singing limestone to emerge, fully poised and gleaming in a mantle of scalding water, from a buzzing ground-floor doorway in the sunlit streets above.

Do not stay kneeling by the cistern too long.

Because you are the one thing here that does not sum to zero.

You breathe unevenly. Your heart beats in a messy, wandering rhythm that refuses to lock onto the mountain’s tremor. Your boots carry grit from somewhere beyond the clouds; your skin sheds salt; your shadow moves across the wet tiles whenever you shift your weight; your body insists, with every ragged exhale, on dragging the afternoon closer.

At first, they merely part around you on the avenues, leaving your skin prickling and your teeth aching from the brush of their buzzing mantles.

Stay another hour, though—kick a flake of loose mineral into a sluice, pluck a single glossy leaf from a balustrade, or simply linger long enough for the steady state to register the drag of your mortal heat—and their calm, terrible attention begins to gather.

You notice it when the light across the plaza seems to thicken.

Out of the bare roaring streets, out of the flooded archways, stepping smoothly down from the looping stairs, they drift in until six or seven of them stand around you at precise, equidistant intervals—arranged around your body like tuning forks set around a cracked bell. The mist condenses slightly denser along their curved travertine ribs so that, for once, you can see them clearly even in the open daylight: tall, pale, water-sheathed towers of singing stone, every smooth red-veined dish tilted politely, intently toward your chest.

They do not raise a limb against you. They do not strike, or grasp, or drive you toward the mile-deep drop.

They simply step inward by half a pace, aligning their cavities, and begin to tune the air where you stand.

The roar does not grow louder, yet the pressure against your sternum thickens until it feels like wading chest-deep into warm mercury. The breath in your throat starts to catch on a clean, rhythmic beat that is not yours. Your erratic pulse stumbles, skips, and tugs sharply toward the massive sub-audible hum rising through the soles of your boots. The sweat pouring down your temples stops running in jagged tracks and beads evenly across your forehead, trembling in crisp concentric rings.

There is no malice in them—nothing so crude as anger or hunger. They are only doing to you what they do to a yellowing frond, a loosening stone, a cloud trying to rise from the plaster-white valley below. They are trying to smooth out the dissonance. They are trying to bring you gently into phase, so that you will stop hurting the chord with your clumsy forward time, so that the hot lime can settle kindly into your tired joints and the scalding water can hold you upright forever without effort.

If you have any sense left while your knees still obey you, you break out between two of them—gasping as a razor-thin wedge of silence slices across your ears, your wet sleeve scalded by the graze of an acoustic skin—and you run for the outer ridge-path before your heartbeat finishes locking in.

Do not look down into the thin glaze of hot water coating the plaza as you flee.

Because if you stayed even a minute too long, your reflection gliding across the bright wet stone beside your boots is no longer quite yours. It is already a little taller than you are, its shoulders drawn pale and narrow, faint arched openings beginning to pierce cleanly through the solid dark of its ribs—and it is not running at all. It is standing still in the fixed mid-morning light, tilted gently toward the roar, waiting for you to catch up.
