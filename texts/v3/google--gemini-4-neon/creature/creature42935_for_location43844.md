At first, you will not know they are here.

In a valley this thick with flesh and sound, your senses are already overdrawn. Every wall is shingled in scraping shells; every arch drips with blubbery warmth and clicking throats; every gutter stirs with sluggish, pale weight. You walk the wet flagstones assuming the basin belongs entirely to blind appetites and patient stone.

Then you notice a mistake in the geometry.

Along the razor-sharp bevel of a half-finished balustrade, where the newest millimeter of basalt still yields like cold tallow, someone has pinched a neat, thumb-wide spillway into the lip, guiding the sheeting water away from a narrow alcove below. Six inches beneath it, the pale, overlapping armor of the wall-grazers has been cleared in a clean, deliberate band—not scraped ragged by a passing sweeper, but pried up one by one, leaving the slick chemotrophic film exposed in a tidy rectangular patch. And jammed cleanly into the solid, older stone beside the curb is a hollow length of wing-bone, sealed in seamlessly with no gap or crack, as if the city had grown around it on purpose.

Before you can crouch to examine the bone, something strikes the pavement fifty yards away.

You do not hear it in the air—the foghorn groans of the roosting flocks and the endless scrubbing roar of the walls swallow everything airborne. You feel it instead through the soles of your boots: three crisp, dry, ringing knocks, a measured pause, and then a single longer trill that buzzes straight up your shinbones and fades into the deeper hum of the anti-freeze field.

It is too tidy to be basalt settling. Too rhythmic to be a falling shell.

Wait in the shadow of the viaduct, hold your breath against the cold, and you will realize that one of the dark humps wedged into the dry-ish angle of the retaining wall is not a moulting aerialist, nor a bulbous knob of uncured stone.

It shifts. Slowly—obeying the same frugal, glacial calculus as everything else in the basin—it unfolds a long, low-slung frame and turns its face toward the vibration of your weight on the flagstones.

They are built close to the stone, broad through the ribs and dense through the limbs, as though gravity has always pulled a little harder on them than on the gliding flocks above. They wear no woven cloth; in air this cold, thread would turn into brittle, rime-crusted boards within minutes. Instead, they drape themselves in heavy, layered mantles stitched from the dense, water-shedding pelts of dead aerialists, cured in the mineral brine of the lower cisterns and rubbed repeatedly with rendered blubber until the sheeting condensation beads off their backs like mercury. Along the lower hems of these dark coats, they sew overlapping skirts of pale grazer-shell—weighted armor that keeps the sub-zero drafts from lifting their pelts and lets them drop flat against a curb when a towering sweeper plods past.

Where the stitched hood parts, the skin beneath is thick, pale grey-blue, and strangely glossy with oil. There are no eyes—not scarred-over hollows, just a smooth, uninterrupted sweep of bone and blubber-padded flesh from broad brow to flaring cheekbone, sleek as river basalt. Their nostrils are wide and deeply baffled with stiff pale hairs that warm the thin alpine air before it reaches their vast lungs, letting them breathe in long, nearly silent draughts while you pant for oxygen.

The one beneath the arch does not click at you, or bellow, or recoil. It lowers itself gracefully onto one knee, leans forward, and presses the flat, bare pad of its left palm—and then the wide ridge of its lower jaw—directly against the freezing, wet flagstone.

It is listening to you through the street.

Through the seamless, tension-locked masonry, your shivering heels and rapid heartbeat ring out like a panicked drum. The inhabitant holds still for ten seconds, reading your weight, your erratic pace, the strange warmth leaking from your ill-suited clothes. Then it lifts a slender staff made from spliced aerialist wing-bones, tipped at the base with a heavy, polished nodule of sweeper-tooth, and taps the stone twice beside your boot.

*Tock. Tock-tock.*

Again, the air catches none of it. The sound lives entirely inside the basalt, racing cleanly along the monolithic viaducts and stairwells, ringing bright and brittle through finished rock and turning dull and muted wherever a span is still curing. Five seconds later, an answer vibrates back up through your heels from somewhere two tiers higher—a cascading scatter of taps, passing the news of your strange footfalls across the amphitheater without adding a single whisper to the roaring dark.

That is how they keep themselves apart from the shouting multitude: they have abandoned the air altogether.

In the open streets, their voices are useless against a hundred thousand foghorn bellows and the gritty surf of a million feeding shells. So they speak in stone. Every inhabitant carries knuckle-stones strapped to their palms, or bone-and-tooth tapping staves, or thick keratin calluses worn glassy-smooth along their heels and elbows. Because the city precipitates as one unbroken mineral lattice, a crisp knock on an upper terrace can carry for a quarter mile beneath the feet of the beasts, carrying not just words—layered in cadence, weight, and spacing—but the exact health and completeness of the architecture between speaker and listener. By the subtle echo returning through their palms, they know which cantilevered bridges have locked hard enough to bear weight, which drainage channels are buckling, and where a fresh seam is turning soft enough to work.

They reserve their throats only for the dark inside their huddles. When two of them wish to speak privately, they kneel close, draw their oiled hoods together to shut out the freezing drafts, and press their eyeless brows or wide jawbones firmly together. What passes between them then is scarcely a sound at all—a low, warm, gravelly purr that vibrates directly from skull to skull, intimate and entirely unheard even by someone standing an arm’s length away.

If you follow them—stepping carefully, learning to keep your knees bent and your weight low so you do not lose your footing on the frictionless wet steps—you begin to see that the city is not merely growing around them. They are coaxing it.

They do not quarry the finished basalt; it is too hard, too sharp, and breaking it wastes precious calories in a basin where every ounce of heat is hoarded like treasure. Instead, they are gardeners of the curing edge. Moving in slow, patient pairs along the newest extruded tiers where the stone is still soft as chilled tallow, they work the rising millimeter with flat bone spatulas and bare, oil-slicked fingers.

They cannot alter the vast, indifferent geometry that rises from the bedrock—the sweeping viaducts, the deep drainage streets, the crowded canyon-towers belong to whatever silent engineering governs the mountain. Yet wherever the raw mineral paste emerges, the inhabitants leave small mercies behind. They pinch shallow basins into the lips of stairwells to catch mineral silt. They press notched grazer-shells into the edges of blind corners so a passing hand can read a crossroads by touch. They set curved ribs of dead scavengers into rising walls to serve as hooks for drying pelts, and embed hollow wing-bones sideways through thickening lintels to act as acoustic bridges and sheltered handholds. Over months and years, the city locks seamlessly around every insertion, folding their quiet craft permanently into its matte-black flanks.

Even the bare patches you saw on the walls are not vandalism, but husbandry. They tend the grazers the way highland herders tend slow cattle, scraping tiny furrows into the curing stone so the slick chemotrophic film pools thick and rich, then prying up only the oldest, broadest shells with thin bone wedges, leaving the rest spaced cleanly enough to keep the walls evenly harvested. They eat the tough, cold foot-meat raw or steeped in the salty brine drawn from the pumping cisterns below, and nothing of the animal is wasted: shells become plates, scraping knives, rain-shingles, and weighted coat-hems; crushed grit is mixed with aerialist grease to caulk their dwellings.

You find their dwellings tucked into the tightest, strangest pockets of the middle tiers—inside dead-end flues, half-extruded tower stubs, and the narrow sheltered pockets wedged directly beneath the thickest viaduct overhangs.

At first, stepping toward one, you think you have simply walked into an unusually dense aerialist roost. The smell hits you first: thick, blindingly sharp ammonia, wet oily hides, musky animal heat, and the faint metallic tang of curing stone. Then your hands find a low baffle-wall built not by the city, but by them—interlocking mats of pale, coarse sweeper-bristle and overlapping grazer-shells, mortared seamlessly into the edges of the archway with smeared basalt paste harvested from active seams.

Duck beneath the heavy, weighted hide curtain that seals the entrance, and for the first time since entering the mountain bowl, the lethal bite of the air relents.

It is still cold enough to mist your breath, yet it is *livable*—warmed by a deliberate, dense symbiosis. Hanging thickly from the low ceiling overhead are dozens of tolerated, half-tame aerialists, packed shoulder to folded wing like a living, snoring roof. The inhabitants coax them here with bruised gutter-scavengers and scraps of fatty cistern-flesh, trimming away the sharpest hanging ice-crusts from the entrance and keeping the drafts baffled so the heavy, blubbery beasts have no reason to leave. In return, the flocks drip steady, musky warmth downward into the pitch-black chamber.

On the raised, dry-ish ledges of finished stone beneath them—insulated with thick pads of matted sweeper-hair—the inhabitants sleep in tight, braided heaps, twenty or thirty bodies pressed rib to rib, sharing every calorie of warmth while the roosters shift and mutter softly overhead. There is no fire here. There is nothing to burn, and smoke in a sealed flue would kill them as swiftly as the frost outside. Heat is simply flesh kept close to flesh, wrapped in oil and sheltered by bone.

Down on the broad, raw causeways of the basin floor, where the dark retention pools sigh in cavernous unison, you see their quiet daring at its fullest.

When the deep, sub-audible rumble of a sweeper rolls up through the pavement, every gutter-scavenger bolts for the grates and every grazer clamps tight. The inhabitants do not scatter. Instead, three or four of them rise smoothly from the curb as the towering, steaming silhouette looms out of the freezing mist. Walking in dead silence, matching the giant’s agonizingly slow, glacial pace, they slip right under the overhang of its pale, rime-encrusted flanks. While the blind beast drags its massive scoop-jaw along the wet paving stones, unbothered and unhurried, the harvesters reach up with serrated shell-blades and patiently saw away heavy, frost-stiffened mats of outer bristle—never cutting close enough to draw blood or break the animal’s stride. They gather the fallen fleece in slick hide slings, tap a brief *clear* into the causeway behind the retreating hooves, and drift back into the dark before the next sweeper turns the bend.

Others wade calmly into the flooded cisterns, knee-deep in the bitterly cold, stubbornly liquid brine, moving in rhythm with the synchronized *glug-hiss* of the giant siphon-colonies. They feel with bare toes for the plumpest rubbery lobes, slicing off clean wedges that the colonies will slowly regrow, and filling sealed bladders of aerialist stomach with the dense, mineral-rich broth the siphons concentrate.

Now and then, as they work, a deep seam locks somewhere across the mile-wide amphitheater and a dead-cold flicker of blue-grey mineral light flares briefly over the terraced canyons.

They do not flinch at the flash—they have no eyes to be dazzled by it. Yet every one of them freezes for half a heartbeat. You see their smooth brows tilt slightly upward as the sudden static prickles across their wet, oiled skin, followed a breath later by the immense, crystalline *crack* beneath the basin and the long trembling hum rolling through the flagstones. Before the aerialists overhead have even resumed their roaring, the inhabitants’ staves are already tapping rapidly against the wet street—checking on distant kin, calibrating the new resonance of the mountain, updating the vast, shared acoustic map they carry in their skulls.

They have no stories of a sun, nor of open sky above the impenetrable cloud deck. Twenty paces past the outermost half-formed curb, where the stone’s subtle field fades and the mist freezes into lethal rime, the world simply ends. To them, the universe is a slow, growing geometry carved out of silence and frost, expanding millimeter by millimeter into the dead dark—and their whole existence is the art of keeping pace with its rising edges without ever moving fast enough to starve, or slow enough to be swallowed.

Unless, of course, they choose to be.

Walk far enough along the steep, crowded terraces, out toward the active rims where wide viaducts are still extruding over the pitch-black abyss, and you will eventually come upon a quiet gathering where no one is working the stone, and no one is harvesting.

Here, tucked into the broadening brow of a rising parapet where the latest basalt is still soft and yielding, sits an elder.

At first, you think they are merely resting, hunched inside a thick mountain of layered aerialist pelts, hands folded calmly across their lap. Then you notice where their knees meet the floor.

There is no seam.

The matte-black stone has risen smoothly, seamlessly, past their ankles and halfway up their shins, fusing around the old brine-cured wraps with the same sharp, tensionless perfection as a finished stairwell. They have been sitting in this active lip for three years—maybe four. They did not fall asleep here by accident. When their joints grew too stiff for the slick stairs, when their lungs began to labor too hard in the thin alpine air to keep up with the patient harvest, they did not drag themselves out onto the barren talus to turn into useless rime.

They came to the curing edge, chose a junction where three terraces meet, and let the city begin to take them.

Through the bones already locked inside the basalt, an elder wedged into a seam can hear the valley in a way no walking creature ever can. Their trapped shins and pelvis have become part of the monolithic lattice itself. They feel the sluggish pulse of the siphon-cisterns half a mile below, the shifting weight of sweepers crossing distant causeways, the slow creeping tension deep in the bedrock hours before a sudden *crack* splits a rising stairwell. While the living bustle slowly across the tiers, these rooted listeners sit in the freezing dark like living instruments—relays and anchors for the whole district.

With a short bone stylus held in thickened, trembling fingers, the elder taps slow, crystalline messages directly against the smooth black basalt sheathing their own knees. The vibrations carry effortlessly through their fused bones and straight down the length of the viaduct, warning harvesters away from an unstable seam, calling out where a heavy flock has shifted its roost, or passing down old cadences of memory no younger hand has quite mastered.

They are not left alone to the cold. As you stand watching in the roaring dark, two younger inhabitants creep silently up the wet stairs. One crouches beside the elder and presses small, pre-chewed strips of brine-soaked grazer meat and warm blubber carefully between their pale lips. The other rubs fresh, heavy oil across the elder’s smooth brow and cheeks so the drifting mist cannot freeze on their skin, adjusts the thick pelts tucked snugly around their shoulders, and then leans in—pressing forehead gently to forehead, humming a long, low, vibrating bone-song of company and news that shakes quietly between their skulls.

Year by patient year, the matte-black stone climbs higher. Past the hips. Up across the broad ribs. Around the tucked elbows. By the time the basalt reaches the collarbones, the elder scarcely breathes once a minute, conserving the last faint ember of warmth inside a body that is already more architecture than flesh. And when that final breath finally stops, the kin do not take chisels to the rock to pry the body free. They fold the elder’s stiff hands neatly over a small notched shell-token—their distinct, carved signature—smooth the soft tallow of the active seam gently over the curve of the eyeless brow, and let the city finish its slow, seamless embrace.

Ten years later, nothing remains on the finished parapet except a clean, matte-black flank, glistening with sheeting liquid water, utterly indistinguishable from the rest of the sharp, unweathered canyon—save for a subtle, smooth-beveled swell along the wall, just about the height of a seated chest.

Now, when you step back down into the older drainage streets, stepping over the slow grazers and feeling the faint, sharp-edged bumps rising beneath the wet flagstones and along the narrow stairwells, you understand them differently.

They are not all careless beasts that lingered too long in the dark.

Watch closely, and you will see a young inhabitant—barely half-grown, wrapped in a patched hide coat too large for their narrow shoulders—pass along a finished retaining wall where the basalt bulges gently into the faint, unmistakable contour of a tucked shoulder and a curved brow. They do not break stride. They simply trail their bare, thick-padded palm softly across the wet, submerged profile in the freezing dark, tap two quiet, affectionate knocks directly into the stone, and walk slowly on into the roaring city their ancestors have become.
