You do not hear them approach, of course. Even if one of them were to leap from a high basalt fin onto a wet mat of silver-backed ferns six feet from your boots, the impact would reach you only as a soft puff of warm air against your shins and a sudden, slight slackening of the static prickling your forearms—as if something nearby had just drunk the violet haze off the margins of the canopy.

Then you notice the warmth. It is a different heat from the sluggish fever rising out of the rock: narrower, steadier, smelling faintly of heated copper, crushed moss, and clean salt. A yard away, beneath the taut underside of a waxy canopy-mat, two narrow seams of pale amber light brighten slowly along what you had taken for a slicked ridge of wet slate. The ridge shifts. Four long fingers and two opposing thumbs—one set high on the palm, one near the wrist—uncurl from the edge of an extruded cooling-fin, leaving behind a brief, glistening film of dark mineral oil.

They are built for a world that has never offered a flat floor or a quiet edge. Roughly the length of a compact human, they almost never stand upright; they cling, bridge, fold, and suspend themselves across the vertical tangle with an ease that makes gravity look like a local suggestion. Their four limbs are long and subtly flattened, with deeply articulated wrists and ankles that can rotate far enough to hook backwards over a razor-crest or press flush against a sweating sheet of vertical shale. Their palms and soles are padded with dense, grooved, rubbery tissue, dark gray and finely striated—part traction, part sense organ. Through those grooves they hold fast to oil-slick stone while reading the faint electrical hum, the temperature gradient, and the hidden hairline fractures of whatever they touch.

Their skin is the deep, bruised slate-indigo of fresh basalt in rain, upholstered along the belly and inner limbs with a short, damp velvet like that of the grazing herds, muffling every brush against leaf and vine. Along their spines and the outer blades of their forearms, however, the hide grows firmer and rises into neat rows of flexible dermal flanges—living cooling-fins, echoing the rock around them. In the young these fins are soft, dark, and leathery; in adults they stiffen with seams of dull iron-nickel, flaring gently open and flushing ember-orange whenever their bearer has climbed hard or absorbed too much charge from a restless fault, venting excess heat in silent, thread-thin wisps of vapor.

Their heads are smooth and wedge-narrow, with no outer ears—an organ useless in a continent where sound dies at arm’s length—and a small, valved mouth built for drawing in warm, dense air, sipping mineral sap, and shearing tender fern-hearts and soft extruded copper shoots. Their eyes are large, glossy black beneath clear sliding membranes that wipe away the perpetual lukewarm drizzle, tuned to catch heat-flushes in the velvety gullies and faint violet discharges along the wet foliage. Most striking are the pleated vanes folded tightly along their throats and collarbones. When they pause on a ledge to take the measure of a gorge, those vanes fan outward like the delicate, dark-red gills of an abyssal fish. Through them they drink the slow inner-ear pressure of a cliff shearing a mile away, the four-beat rhythm of a distant exhaust fissure, and the tiny electrical intentions of their kin hidden twenty paces deep in the ferns.

Nothing in this high jungle remains purely one substance for long, and neither do they. Their infants are born entirely soft, dark, and warm-fleshed, carried tucked inside the heated ribs of an adult’s folded cooling-fins. As they grow, though, their bodies take on the temper of the ridges they range. A band that spends its seasons among the copper-extruding clefts develops fine, branching filaments of living alloy beneath its slate skin, faintly verdigris along old knuckles and knees; those who haunt the high, fast-rising basalt pistons grow broader, denser-finned, with smooth plates of dark brass knitting naturally across their shoulders where the moving rock rubs them. If one slices a forearm on glassy fresh shale, they do not wait for ordinary meat to scab. They pack the cut with warm coppery mucilage from a severed liana and hold it against a rhythmic vent until it knits shut in a bright, supple seam of conductive flesh that stays alive and sensitive to touch.

***

They have no buildings, no cleared ground, and no names for mountains. Naming a peak that may soften into luminous slurry before a child learns to climb would be like naming a cloud-bank. Instead, they live by cadence and temper.

A ridge is known by the beat of its fissures, the saltiness of its condensation, the rhythm of its rack-and-pinion faults. When one climber tells another where to find a rich ledge of ionized dew or a sheltered hollow beneath a canopy-mat, they do not point or trace a map. They draw close, press their collar-vanes and grooved palms flat against the other’s ribs, and transmit the signature of the place directly: a slow seven-beat pulse of internal pressure, a faint prickly surge of static, a localized flush of warmth along the collarbone. The listener absorbs the rhythm into their own joints, nods once by flattening their fins, and knows which way to climb when the mists thin.

Because the cottony hush isolates anyone who steps ten feet around a basalt spire, their social life is intensely, almost continuously tactile. A band of eight or twelve moves through the vertical jungle like a loose constellation held together by brief touches—a heel brushed across a shoulder in passing, a shared hand laid on the same taut vine, a low, steady flicker of pale amber traveling down their damp flanks so no one is lost in the dim pewter gloom. At rest, they do not lie on stone, which may heave or shear in an hour. They weave wide, elastic hammocks from the rubbery vines that already loop between neighboring spires, slinging themselves beneath the warm underside of a canopy-mat so that as two cliffs grind apart and slide back together, the cradle stretches and slacks smoothly around their sleeping bodies.

Even their quarrels make no noise. When two adults contest a choice warm vent or the right to guide a band across a shifting massif, they meet palm-to-palm and chest-to-chest on a narrow crest. Their dorsal fins flare dull orange; their collar-vanes spread wide; they drive dense, competing tides of heat and static directly into one another’s palms until one’s internal cadence falters, their light dims, and they fold their vanes flat, dropping silently back into the dark tangle below.

For middle distances—across a crowded gully or up a sheer half-mile face—they use the jungle itself as a nervous system. The thick lianas that bridge the chasms already bleed conductive mucilage; a climber with long, brass-veined fingers needs only to pinch a taut stem and send a deliberate series of tiny voltage-tugs and plucking tremors down its length. Three hundred yards away, another climber resting a foot against the far anchor-root feels the message arrive through the grooves of their sole: *the fault is reversing; the fog is thinning; a browser herd is cutting a trail along the lower shelf.*

They are deeply entangled with the silent fauna around them, though they pen nothing and own nothing. They travel often in the wake of the ox-sized, heavy-segmented browsers, letting those cushioned reciprocating jaws and leathery split hooves shear fresh paths through razor-sharp copper shoots and knotted fern-trunks; in return, a climber will crouch beside a resting browser in the ember-lit gloom and patiently peel hardened slag from the creases of its joints, or guide it gently away when a fast-time eddy begins to shimmer near its hooves.

To the hanging colonial nests of braided wire and wet black moss, they show a quiet, attentive reverence. A climber will hang one-handed beneath an overhang, touch two damp fingertips to the rim of a nest, and hold wholly still, matching their own faint bio-electric pulse to the colony’s slow ripples. The colonies feel the whole continental expanse the way a spider feels silk. Through them, the climber drinks a wide, wordless weather report: a terraced massif sinking into slurry twenty miles east, a rotating cell of noiseless sheet-lightning building overhead, a seam ripening along the northern chasm. Before leaving, the climber often slices a nearby liana and drapes a bleeding tendril over the colony’s upper roots so the slow moss-minds can feed on the warm sparks.

And when a band needs to send word across an unbridged abyss, they coax a handful of translucent ribbon-flyers down from an updraft onto their outstretched forearms. Pressing a clear, distinctive pattern of body-heat and static into the creatures’ delicate membranes, they release them back into the warm air-currents. The living ribbons drift across the gorge in total silence, carrying the faint imprint of a greeting, a warning, or a birth for ten minutes before the ambient voltage washes them clean again.

***

For all that they leave no walls or rivets behind, they are not mere scavengers drifting through an indifferent engine. They have a deep, quiet craft—born directly out of living inside a landscape that endlessly manufactures raw, magnificent shapes with no warehouse waiting at the end of the line.

Watch a small band working along a cleft where spontaneous pockets of pressure smelt fresh ore and extrude long, gleaming ribbons of pure copper down slick chutes of wet lichen toward a warm sinkhole. Ordinarily the bars cool, break into segments, and vanish noiselessly down the hole to be remelted. The climbers do not try to hoard the metal—you cannot carry a storehouse on a mountain that moves—nor do they hammer it into permanence. Instead, they *intercept* and *couple*.

One climber catches a warm, still-supple ribbon of copper bare-handed before it slides into the dark. Moving with unhurried precision, they twist it around the bleeding stem of a canopy-vine, bend its far end into a delicate hook, and wedge it gently against the vulcanized rubbery lip of a four-beat steam fissure. Nothing dramatic happens at first. Then the fissure inhales and exhales with its soft *thup*, and each rhythmic pulse now sends a clean, metered trickle of static up the copper, through the bleeding vine, and out across the serrated violet margin of the canopy-mat overhead. Within minutes, the pocket of air beneath the mat warms by several degrees, steadying into a dry, luminous alcove where infants can sleep free of the mineral drizzle, while the heightened glow draws a slow curtain of ribbon-flyers into the gully, brushing the nearby wire-moss colonies awake.

This is the whole of their industry: temporary junctions. They bridge two accidents the massif did not know were neighbors, turning blind geological production into brief pockets of warmth, light, signal, or shelter. When the ridge shifts an hour or a month later, the copper ribbon tears silently free and tumbles into the sinkhole as it always meant to, leaving no trace that hands were ever there.

Their greatest art is made the same way. During the slack hour when a three-mile rack-and-pinion fault finishes grinding a valley floor fifty feet to the east and pauses in breathless equilibrium before reversing, two or three bands will sometimes converge beneath a broad, taut canopy-mat. They sling themselves in overlapping circles of elastic fern-vines, palms touching a shared network of conductive lianas or resting flat against a single warm plate of naturally extruded dark brass.

One among them—usually an older climber whose forearms are heavily ribbed with copper-veined fins—places hands and heels across the vulcanized lips of three nearby exhaust vents and begins to modulate their rhythms, half-choking one breath, letting another swell, tapping tiny counter-beats onto the tensioned vines with their knuckles.

Not a whisper carries past the rim of the gully. Yet through the palms, the soles, and the wide-fanned throat-vanes of thirty hanging bodies, a dense, intricate music rolls outward: a deep four-beat pressure swaying in the inner ear, a wave of fine prickly static climbing the ribs, a warm ember-flush spreading from spine to fingertips, answered by rolling tides of pale amber bioluminescence pulsing across thirty damp hides in shifting harmony. When the distant fault shudders and the stone teeth engage to drag the valley back west, the vines slacken, the brass plate cools and fractures, and the piece dissolves forever—impossible to repeat, because no ridge will ever hold that exact geometry twice.

***

More astonishing than their craft is how calmly they inhabit the places where the massif stops enforcing the borders of time and distance.

Every band learns early to spot the small, shimmering eddies where time loses its steady pace. Beside a hot vent where a cluster of ferns suddenly uncurls, drops its spores, blackens, and calcifies into slotted heat-finned stone in the space of three breaths, a skilled crafter does not recoil. Instead, they twist together raw copper-shoots, damp ore-sand, and fibrous vine-bark, and hold the bundle at the exact edge of the eddy on a long insulated stick of dead shale. In two heartbeats the bundle ages decades—fusing, tempering, knitting into a finely slotted resonance-plate or a supple hooked climbing-spur—and the crafter snatches it back on the third breath, a moment before it can stiffen all the way into brittle slate.

The slow-time pockets are prized even more deeply. Wherever an invisible electrostatic hollow holds a heavy drop of condensation trembling detached in midair for twenty minutes, the climbers recognize a natural keep. They never mark these places with stone; they remember their exact pressure-signature and return to them across whole seasons. Inside a slow hollow tucked beneath a mossy overhang, you might find a clutch of perishable mineral-rich pods suspended fresh for months, a delicate knot of copper-vine holding an intricate charge-memory that would otherwise dissipate in seconds, or—most tenderly—a badly injured climber held motionless in the sluggish air while a swift runner crosses three gorges to fetch a skilled mender with fresh brass-sap.

And then there are the seam-walkers.

Along the active fault lines where distance itself develops seams—where a distant volcanic stack exhaling dark steam snaps close enough for you to count the velvet mites on its mossy intake-rim before snapping back into ten miles of haze—most climbers avert their eyes and grip the rock until the tug behind the eyes passes. A few lean, solitary individuals do the opposite. They study the tension building in the fault with their throat-vanes fanned wide, wait for the exact breath when the seam yawns open, and step calmly across it.

In a single silent stride they cross ten miles of chasm, emerging out of the mist on the distant stack with their own shadow lagging three full paces behind them on the wet basalt, catching up moments later with a faint pop of cold static.

Even their own people regard seam-walkers with a quiet, careful distance. Crossing too many folded miles leaves a person faintly out of register with the continent. Their shadows no longer match the dim pewter twilight, stretching sideways toward heat-sources that have not yet broken the surface. If you look away from a seam-walker to step past a curtain of ribbon-flyers and look back, their dark shale shoulder may have shifted into humming, oil-slick brass, only to soften back into matte hide a heartbeat later. They feel rockslides before the stone shears, and hear the faint thrum of vents that have not yet extruded from the mist. When a seam-walker arrives at a band’s canopy-camp carrying news or rare ore-buds from thirty miles off, the others wait in polite, absolute stillness—not touching palms until the walker’s lagging shadow has fully caught up and settled cleanly beneath their grooved heels.

***

Death here is not a clean departure, any more than stone is cleanly separate from flesh.

When an elder grows very old—when their cooling-fins have thickened into heavy, ridged iron-nickel, their amber light has dimmed to a slow, deep coal-glow, and their joints begin to stiffen against the shifting ledges—they do not wait to lose their grip in a chasm. With the quiet accompaniment of their band, they climb toward a freshly thrust-up spur that still steams gently in the drizzle, or seek out a narrow cleft beside a steady, four-beat vent.

They find a smooth shelf, press their belly and limbs flat against the sweating rock, and spread their throat-vanes open for the last time. Their kin gather close, laying grooved palms along the elder’s back and ribs, sharing a long, slow tide of warmth and static until the elder’s cadence merges seamlessly with the hum of the massif beneath them.

Over the next several days—or, if they have chosen the edge of a rapid eddy, in the span of a few quiet breaths—the boundary finally gives way. Their hide settles into a smooth, warm sheet of dark brass; their dorsal fins fuse into the cliff’s own heat-radiating slots; their conductive veins thread outward into the damp moss as pulsing roots that are half-plant and half-conduit.

They do not vanish cleanly from the lives of the living. For as long as that massif holds together—a year, a decade, half a century—younger bands passing along the ridge will pull back the wet moss to lay their palms and collar-vanes against the humming brass, drinking in the lingering temper and quiet rhythm of the one who settled there. Then, sooner or later, the whole terraced range softens into a boiling bank of mist and luminous slurry, draws the elder down into the deep continental circulation, and pushes them up again miles away as a hot, razor-edged new ridge already sprouting thickets of silver-backed fern.

***

Yet the climbers are not the only presences moving through the warm, cottony hush. The continent is too feverish, and its rules too porous, to stop at a single lineage.

Down in the deep, velvety gullies where the vents glow dull ember-orange and the extruded metal chutes run thickest, you occasionally encounter things that have no parents, no young, and no real place in the jungle’s food web. They are the continent’s own accidental wanderers—half-geology, half-flora, half-engine, assembled by blind momentum along the churning fault lines and shaken loose before they could knit back into the cliffs.

One may lurch softly out of the warm steam looking roughly like a low, six-legged beetle the size of a cart, its carapace built of naturally slotted basalt fins articulated on vulcanized mineral hinges, bound together by aggressive mats of copper-veined liana that took root in its joints and began carrying stray pulses of static from limb to limb. Inside its hollow stone ribs, a trapped pocket of rhythmic steam or a cooling lump of self-smelting ore beats like a slow, stubborn heart.

They do not eat, hunt, or mate. They simply inherit a fragment of the factory-mountain’s forgotten urgency. One will spend three weeks hauling segmented iron-nickel bars out of a lichen chute and arranging them in an immaculate, concentric spiral along a narrow spur that is already tilting into a sinkhole. Another will pace back and forth along a mossy fault line, matching its silent, cushioned piston-gait to the valley’s fifty-foot cycle, venting brief pulses of static that almost—never quite—resolve into the cadence of a greeting.

The climbers neither fear these unfinished creatures nor try to command them. They step politely aside on the knife-edged trails, gently unwind choked mats of wet fern from their interlocking stone teeth, and sometimes lay a curious palm against their hot flanks to feel what half-dreamed task is turning over inside them before their joints seize, moss over, and harden back into the living rock.

***

And finally, there are the ones who live on the other side of the puddles.

After a sullen drizzle falls from the bruised pewter sky and gathers in a sharp basalt hollow, a climber will sometimes crouch beside the dark, lukewarm pool—not to drink, and not to see the clouds, which the water refuses to reflect. Looking down through the mineral-slick surface, they look straight through the stone beneath their knees into the vast, glowing underside of the continental platform: a vaulted ceiling of black rock laced with deep amber ore-veins, tangled with colossal roots that hang downward into miles of empty, lightning-lit sky above the hidden world below.

If you stay still beside a wide hollow long enough, you realize those hanging roots are not empty.

Moving along them—upside-down to any eye on the heights, yet utterly unhurried in their own gravity—are pale, slender figures whose forms echo the upper climbers the way a negative echoes a print. They have no dark shale hides and no heat-venting fins; down beneath the belly of the massif, shielded from the wet jungle’s crowding and bathed in the cool updrafts rising off the lower cloud-decks, they have grown long, pearlescent, and almost translucent, trailing broad, ribbon-like membranes along their flanks and heels to catch the silent wind over the abyss. Where the upper people carry ember-orange warmth and violet static, these underside dwellers glow faintly with a cool, steady mineral blue along their joints, threading hand-over-hand down the luminous amber roots to gather whatever drifts upward from the unseen floor miles below.

No one on the heights knows whether the underside people are distant kin who climbed down a sinkhole ages ago and forgot which way the sky belonged, or the older inhabitants of the continent’s roots, or simply the upper world’s own straying reflections thickened over long time into living flesh. The two peoples cannot climb to one another; the solid miles of churning ore and pressure between them crush anything that tries to pass straight through.

Only the puddles open a window—and only briefly, while the rock holds still.

Now and then, when the mists settle and a dark hollow brims clear, a slate-skinned climber on the upper ridge will bend low over the lukewarm water, extend a single grooved fingertip, and touch the surface tension. Directly beneath, rising up from the glowing underside of the rock, a pale, slender fingertip reaches back to meet it. The water does not break. Instead, a tiny, cool ring of opposite-polarity current passes silently between the two tips—a faint tingle of frost that has no right to exist in this fevered air, a wordless acknowledgment of weight answering weight across the thickness of the world.

Then, half a mile away, a cliff detaches itself along a knife-straight seam and begins to rise with a whisper like a palm drawn over damp wool. The puddle trembles. The glowing roots dissolve into dark oil, the pale figure vanishes back into the inverted sky, and the climber lifts their damp hand, folds their collar-vanes against the drizzle, and swings smoothly back into the warm, crowded, silent green.
