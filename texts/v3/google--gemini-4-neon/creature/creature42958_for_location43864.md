You will not see them coming, even if they are walking straight toward you along the same bow.

What arrives first is a change in the wood beneath your boots: a faint, deliberate *tok—tok—tok* running up through the frost-seamed bark, timed neatly between the deeper booms of the wind. It is not the frantic, metallic knuckle-rap of a clatter-back hunting grubs, nor the irregular drip of thawing rime. It is slow, even, and patiently repeated every twenty paces—the butt of a bone-shod walking pole striking the leeward crown to announce a living weight on the span.

Only when the figure is within a few lengths of you does the dark thicken into a silhouette: a compact, hooded shape moving in a low, fluid crouch, knees bent and springy as a sailor’s, torso leaned effortlessly into a crosswind that would knock a flatlander off the edge. As the bow heaves slowly upward on a twenty-foot swell and settles again, the walker’s body scarcely seems to bob at all; their hips absorb the whole roll of the plant while their gloved hand glides continuously along the shoulder-high guide-cord, iron-hard bone toggles clicking open and shut on the two braided leash-lines clipped to their waist.

They do not lift their head to look at you. Their eyes are nearly hidden inside a stiffened, frost-rimed tunnel of glider-hide and narrow bone slit-goggles rubbed with dark pitch to kill the glare of ice-mist. Instead, they tilt one muffled ear slightly leeward, resting two bare fingertips for a heartbeat against the nearest taut guy-vine to read the rhythm of your breathing and the shift of your boots.

Then, if you answer their tap properly—two flat knocks, then a pause—they will edge around you on the windward side, leaving you the safer inner curve of the bark, and raise a mitted palm in passing. You will catch, for half a second before the gale tears it away, the smell that follows everyone born to the lattice: acrid resin-peat smoke, sour tallow, iron-dark sap, and the clean, sharp cold of frost-dried wool.

### Bodies Built for the Sway

The people of the spans—they call themselves simply **Walkers**, or sometimes **Strand-folk**, reserving *Chime-walker* for those who mend and tune the paths—are shaped from infancy by the fact that nothing beneath them is ever still.

A child here is stitched into a soft leather harness before they can roll over. While their parents sleep or work inside a hollow rib-chamber, the infant hangs in a woven phloem-sling suspended from the ceiling by three springy sinew cords, rocking gently through every tremor and gale like a seed-pod. When they begin to walk, they do so clipped to a taut overhead line strung along the center of the chamber. They learn balance not against dead weight, but against tension—how to let a sudden lurch pass through the ankles without locking the knees, how to drop flat and dig hooked boot-cleats into bark the instant the wind-chord pitches sharply upward.

By adulthood, their bodies carry the signature of the void. They are rarely tall, but broad through the shoulders and ribbed with dense, ropey muscle across the calves, thighs, and forearms. Their hands are wide, calloused along the heel and inside of the thumb from a lifetime of gripping braided fiber and reading bark-notches in the dark; many lose a fingertip or two to frostbite or a snapped vine before middle age and scarcely slow down for it. Even when resting inside a sheltered rib, they never sit completely motionless. Watch a Walker squatting beside a smoldering peat-pan and you will notice a slight, unconscious sway from the hips, forever anticipating the next flex of the arch.

Put one of them down on the bare, unyielding shale of a mountain peak, and within minutes they grow queasy and short of breath. Stillness feels wrong to them—heavy, deaf, and dead, like a bow whose roots have rotted through and is waiting for the gust that will shear it clean.

Their voices, too, belong to the wind. Shouting across the open void is a fool’s habit: the roar swallows words within ten yards, frost burns an open throat, and any ragged human cry carries down the eddies to the ears of a hanging shroud-glider. When Walkers speak face-to-face—pressed hood-to-hood on a ridge, or huddled inside a saddle-hut—they use a low, clipped, hummed register pitched right under the wind’s drone, full of soft tongue-clicks and resonant chest-vowels that travel cleanly through fur and bone.

Across greater distances, they do not use their throats at all. Every Walker carries a pair of notched bone strikers and a curved horn plectrum tucked under their belt. To hail a camp across a chasm or warn a companion a hundred yards back along a bow, they lean against a secondary guy-vine and pluck or strike it in dry, measured intervals. To an outsider, the sound blends straight into the discordant roar of the lattice. To a Walker, it is plain speech—clear enough to carry a joke, a warning of thin ice, or a quiet greeting from a quarter-mile away.

### Inside the Singing Dark

To understand how anyone endures a lifetime here, you have to crawl out of the screaming frost and slip behind the lashed glider-hide baffle of a **rib-chamber**.

Inside a great bow, the gale drops from a physical blow to a deep, steady thrum you feel mostly in your molars. The walls curve overhead in pleated, resin-dark arches weeping slow beads of warm mineral condensation. Every few hundred paces, an oval vent-slit lets in a cold blade of indigo twilight and a constant, low organ-pipe note that makes the whole tunnel hum like the inside of a cello.

This is where the real daily life of the spans unfolds—and where the people are not alone.

In the quietest, warmest stretches of the chambers, far from the biting draft of the vent-slits, live the **chord-sleepers**: slow, blunt-snouted, long-armed creatures the size of badgers, draped in pale, felted gray fur that stays permanently damp with chamber-dew. They never venture out into the raw sky. All their lives they hang belly-up along the dripping inner ceilings, hooking flat, leathery claws into the soft inner pith to dig out pipe-grubs and lick the salty mineral crust around sap-seams. They are nearly blind, with huge, folded leaf-like ears, and they have one uncanny habit: whenever the wind-slit hums, they hum back—a soft, trembling bass purr that matches the chamber’s exact resonance.

Walkers regard chord-sleepers with an affection bordering on reverence. No one harms them; hunters leave strips of dried gristle tucked into high cracks for them during lean dry spells. A chamber with three or four humming sleepers clinging to its roof is a safe chamber. Because the creatures press their soft bellies directly against the plant’s pressurized vascular veins, they feel deep root-tremors and sour sap-pressure days before any human ear can catch them. If you wake from sleep to find the sleepers silent, unhooked, and scrambling frantically toward the vent-slits, you do not pack your bedding. You clip your harness, grab your water-bladder, and get off that bow before it shears.

Sharing the dim warmth beneath the sleepers are tiny, soot-dark **hearth-wrens**—smaller, nearly flightless cousins of the loud vent-birds—that slip through the hide baffles to glean stray crumbs, wool-mites, and the pale, wingless sap-lice that breed in damp fur. Many Walkers keep a half-tamed wren tucked inside the high collar of their coat during long marches; the bird sleeps pressed against the throat like a warm fist of feathers, and if the air inside a sealed chamber ever turns stale with deep root-sulfur or heavy spore-dust, the wren wakes first and begins to peck sharply at the wearer’s collarbone.

Light inside a chamber is always miserly. An open flame is unthinkable inside a hollow tunnel of resinous wood, so the suspended stone pan holds only a thumb-sized nub of slow-burning resin-peat, banking a dull orange ember barely bright enough to silhouette faces around it. When a Walker needs closer light—to stitch a split seam in a hide, splice a frayed belay-line, or pull a thorn of frost-hard bark from a palm—they do not add fuel to the pan. Instead, they reach into a damp moss-pouch for a freshly pared strip of pale inner cambium, clamp it into a small split-bone vise, and slowly twist the wooden peg.

Under sudden strain, the living plant fibers wake with that same cold, sulfur-white and pale-green gleam that winks along the great bows during a gale. Held close to the work, a **strain-lamp** sheds a ghostly, trembling radiance for twenty or thirty minutes as the peg is turned notch by notch, until the fibers finally snap with a soft *tick* and go dark forever. Children love to beg for spent strips, bending them sharply in the blackness between their fingers to make brief green sparks dance across their palms.

### The Trades of the Tension

Nobody owns a house here, and nobody farms an acre. Survival belongs to small, fluid bands—called **strands**, usually six to twenty people bound by kin, debt, or long habit—that move continuously along looping circuits hundreds of miles wide. Within every strand, survival splits into four interlocking crafts, though most adults know enough of all four to keep breathing if they are left alone.

#### The Sap-Tappers
The tappers are the quietest and most patient of the folk, smelling perpetually of boiled resin and wet iron. Working inside the great bows or perched on leeward slings during calm mist-fronts, they read the vascular pulse of the lattice by pressing hollow bone tubes against the bark and listening to the sluggish upward *thump-hiss* of mineral sap.

With hand-turned flint augers, they bore narrow, angled plugs into the thick junctions—never more than two taps per span, and always sealed afterward with a hammered wedge of cured pith and hot pitch. Over-tapping a bow is treated almost as harshly as hanging a false chime: bleed a span too thin and its inner walls turn brittle in the next dry freeze, snapping like dry straw under frost-load.

From the dark, bitter, iron-rich sap they draw, tappers boil down the lifeblood of every camp: heavy black pitch for waterproofing seams and boots; dense, salty **sap-cakes** mixed with ground frost-fungus and dried marrow that keep a walker going for days without cooking; and, fermented in warm hide bladders tucked against the ceiling of a rib-chamber, a sharp, faintly fizzing amber drink called **vein-sour** that clears the stomach of bitter cistern-tannins and staves off the winter-wasting that creeps into bones deprived of sun.

#### The Glider-Trappers
If tappers live by patience, glider-trappers live by nerve and timing. No weapon a human can carry on a swaying cable can kill a shroud-glider in fair flight—the beasts are too fast, too silent, and own three thousand feet of empty black air beneath every arch.

So trappers turn the gliders’ own ears against them.

A trapping pair—never one alone—finds a leeward eddy beneath a broad arch where the updrafts curl predictably. Across the gap below the bow, they string a loose, nearly invisible web of greased sinew-cord weighted along its bottom hem with pebbles of slate and lumps of raw copper, held aloft by bone slip-triggers tied back to their perch. Then they hunker down behind a bark-ridge, pull their hoods tight so not even a pale strip of skin shows, and begin to play the bait.

One trapper draws a rosined bone edge across a taut scrap of dried cistern-bladder, producing a ragged, desperate chittering almost indistinguishable from a clatter-back with a broken paw. The other rhythmically taps a pair of split horn-plates against a guy-vine, sending the trembling vibration of frantic flight down fifty yards of cordage.

Hours can pass in freezing stillness. Frost crusts over their shoulders until they look like two more frozen burls on the bark. Then, without a single wingbeat, the dark beneath the bow thickens; a sudden *shhhk* of wind shears across stretched hide as a fifteen-foot shadow banks up out of the abyss, grappling talons already splayed wide.

The trigger-line gets one yank—not by sight, which is useless, but by the exact pitch of the air tearing across the membranes. If the timing is right, the weighted sinew-web drops over the glider’s broad shoulders, fouling its wing-ribs and pitching it tangled and shrieking onto the leeward flank of the bow, where both trappers leap out on short belays with flint-tipped spears before its thrashing claws can shred the net or drag them over the lip.

A single taken glider clothes and feeds a strand for a season. Its thick, leathery flight-membranes become windproof saddle-hut walls and oiled storm-coats; its coarse fur lines boots and mittens; its hollow wing-bones are scraped thin into flutes, needles, and bright, clear-ringing way-chimes; its fat is rendered into tallow that keeps frostbite off cheeks and hands. And every trapper carries the thin white scars along their forearms where an almost-dead glider still managed to rake through double-layered hide.

#### The Tooth-Scramblers
Most Walkers spend their entire lives thousands of feet above stone and are glad of it. Yet the lattice gives no metal and no cutting edge; plant fiber dulls, and bone chips. Every flint scraper, every slate chime-sliver, and every hammered spike of dull, cold-worked copper has to be torn from the raw newborn mountains far below.

Once every few years, when a strand’s circuit passes above one of the rare **Iron-Peaks**—a razor-edged mountain crest stabbing straight up through the lower lattice like a broken tooth—the youngest, lightest, and least fearful climbers prepare for the **Descent**.

Going down into the root-dark is like entering another world. Half a mile below the singing upper bows, the wind slows into heavy, swirling drafts thick with wet sulfur and iron steam rising from the young fractures. The bows here are colossal, gnarled, ancient cylinders as wide as valleys, crusted in frozen mineral ooze and pale, weeping acid-roots that hiss faintly where they bite into black shale. Light disappears entirely; down here even the bruised indigo sky is vanished behind a dozen overlapping miles of arches overhead.

Scramblers go down roped together in threes, carrying woven reed baskets, horn wedges, and heavy bone mallets, lighting their way with freshly clamped strain-lamps that turn the sweating basalt walls a sickly, flickering green. The rock is never quiet. Frost cracks loose slabs with pistol-shot reports; gravel patters continuously down into the unseen troughs below. They work fast and wordlessly, prying brittle plates of ringing slate from shale seams, grubbing flint nodules out of shattered veins, and hammering free dull, green-crusted nuggets of native copper pushed up by the warm mineral seeps.

Nobody lingers on a Tooth. Even seasoned scramblers admit that after three hours of feeling dead, unyielding rock under their cleats, their legs begin to shake and panic rises in their throats. When the baskets are heavy, they haul themselves back up the root-talons hand over hand, weeping with relief when the first living bow finally sways gently beneath their knees again.

#### The Chime-Walkers
Above all the trades—or threaded right through them—stand the **Chime-walkers**.

Some are lone wanderers who left their birth-strands after grief or restlessness; others travel in bonded pairs, moving faster and lighter than the family camps, carrying scarcely more than a rolled bivouac-hide, a coil of sinew, a bone chisel, and a clattering leather roll of tuned slate and bone slats bound silent until needed.

They are the memory and the nervous system of a shifting continent. Because a single mountain tremor can shear a holdfast and drop a square mile of arches without warning, no map drawn on hide stays true for more than a few seasons. The Chime-walkers roam ahead of the storms and after the quakes, walking the loneliest, highest wind-bows to test the tension of the world.

Watch a Chime-walker work at a junction and it looks almost like medicine. They lay their cheek flat against the frost-crusted bark, close their eyes, and listen to the long groan of a mile-wide arch flexing in a gust. To them, every healthy bow has a clean, steady fundamental tone; a bow with an unseen internal split or a dying root drops a faint, sour shudder into the chord, a flat buzz that lingers half a second too long after a gust passes.

When they find a frayed guy-vine, a frozen cistern, a newly opened detour, or a dying span, they leave the voice of the road behind them: a freshly hung **way-chime**, its slate slivers chipped to exact intervals with a copper point so the wind itself speaks the truth half a mile into the freezing mist. Where bark has grown over old hand-tallies, they re-carve the grooves deep and sharp so a mittened thumb can read them at a dead run. Where a gap has widened, they cast a weighted grapnel-line across the dark and knot a new hand-rope, testing it with their own full weight over three thousand feet of nothing before they trust another soul to it.

Every strand feeds a Chime-walker without asking payment, offers them the warmest spot beside the peat-pan, and mends their harness while they sleep. And if a Chime-walker is ever caught hanging a careless or deceitful chime—say, marking a rotten vine as sound out of spite or haste—the strands have only one punishment: they cut their chimes from their belt, take their walking pole, and turn them out onto an unstrung span with a single leash-line, to listen to the dark alone.

### Memory-Cords and the Great Knots

For months at a time, a strand may meet no one but its own twelve faces, tracing its circuit from fog-belt to dry ridge as the mist-sails open and snap shut across the seasons.

Yet they are not scattered savages lost in the dark. Every senior Walker wears wound tightly around their forearm or waist a **knot-cord**: a long, greased braid of dark sinew studded with tiny notched bone rings, hammered copper beads, dyed wool tufts, and flat slate tabs. Run a bare thumb down a knot-cord in pitch blackness, and a three-hundred-mile landscape unfolds beneath your skin. A triple-knotted loop marks a sheltered Great Knot; a smooth copper bead means a reliable cistern-gall; two notched bone rings spaced a finger-width apart tell you how many hand-tallies separate the windward arch from the leeward crossing; a jagged slate chip warns of a known shroud-glider roost below the span.

When two strands cross paths on a lonely arch, they do not hurry past. They lash their harnesses to the leeward ribs, huddle shoulder-to-shoulder against the bark, pass around a bladder of warm vein-sour, and exchange fingers across each other’s knot-cords—updating news of fallen miles, new mist-fronts, births, deaths, and the sudden appearance of a rich spore-burst two days’ march to the east.

And twice in a long cycle—usually during the quieter, mist-heavy lulls between the fiercest dry gales—the circuits converge at the **Great Knots**.

A Great Knot is the closest thing this world has to a city, though it has no roofs of stone and stands empty for half the year. It forms where four or five colossal primary bows, anchored above an unusually stable mountain saddle, have fused together over millennia into a single sprawling tangle of chambers, ledges, and wind-sheltered alcoves two hundred yards across.

You know a Knot is alive from two miles away. Long before you see the faint orange smudges leaking through baffled vent-slits, the roar of the wind-harp changes: woven into the deep groan of the arches are dozens of personal **strand-chimes**—each band’s distinctive three- or four-note chord hanging from the leeward ribs, telling everyone approaching in the dark exactly who has survived the winter and made it back to the hub.

Inside the warm, dripping galleries of an active Knot, the air is thick with resin-smoke, wet fur, roasted pipe-grubs, and the rare, dizzying luxury of a hundred human voices talking at once. Saddle-huts hang clustered like seed-pods in every sheltered crotch; ropes crisscross the hollow interiors hung with frost-dried strips of rime-grazer meat, rolled glider-hides, coils of braided phloem-cord, and tiny leather pouches of precious native copper hammered thin as fingernails.

Old friends clasp forearms—never hands, which might be stiff with frost or slick with pitch—and press foreheads briefly together. Young Walkers who have walked the same ten faces since childhood trade shy, tapped rhythms across crowded chambers, courting someone from another strand whose circuit runs five hundred miles away. Marriages here are simple and practical: the two unclip their secondary belay-lines and splice them briefly together with a single braided copper toggle, deciding before the gathering whose strand will take the new pair—usually whichever band has lost more climbers to the last hard season.

At the end of a Knot-gathering, before the dry wind climbs and drives everyone back onto the trails, the strands crowd into the largest central chamber—a vaulted, cathedral-dark cavern inside the fused heart of the bows, fifty feet high and resonating deeply with the distant gale passing its high vent-slits. The peat-pans are hooded down to mere red pinpricks. Everyone leans their bare shoulders or the backs of their skulls against the curved, living wood, feeling the low, booming organ-note of the mountain wind vibrate straight through their spines.

Then one elder Chime-walker opens their throat and matches that pitch.

One by one, a hundred voices slide into the same low, trembling chest-drone until the entire chamber begins to ring, the dripping ceiling vibrating so strongly that pale dew shakes loose in a fine mist over their faces. Above that living floor of sound, two or three clear, high voices weave the **Span-Count**—a slow, haunting chant listing every major arch that still holds, every peak-tooth that sheared since the last gathering, every cistern that went sour, and, last of all, the names of every Walker who did not come back to the Knot.

No one cries loudly; tears freeze too easily on the spans, and the habit stays even in warmth. Instead, as each lost name is sung, those who knew them strike a single flat knuckle-beat against the living wall, sending a soft, wooden pulse rippling out along five miles of bows into the black air.

### The Flat Note and the Drifters

Where there is no earth to dig, grief has to make its peace with emptiness.

When a Walker dies quietly inside a chamber—worn thin by age, lung-rot, or a fever that no sap-brew could break—the strand strips every useful thing from them without shame. Boots, coat, harness, copper hooks, flint blade, bone strikers: the void takes enough without burying tools beside the dead.

If the strand is near an old, sealed side-tunnel—a **dry-vault**, where a branch-bow withered long ago and closed off its sap-veins—they may carry the wrapped body inside and wedge it gently into the grooved pith beside generations of ancestors. The cold, dry-moving air preserves the dead into light, papery figures scented faintly of pine and iron, gradually veiled in pale threads of frost-fungus until they look less like corpses than part of the hollow bark itself.

More often, out on the long loops far from any vault, they give the dead to the wind.

The strand waits for a fog-surge or a rising spore-burst, when the void is thick and soft and hides the drop. Wrapped loosely in a single strip of torn, discarded mist-sail, the body is carried to the leeward lip of a high arch. Nobody weights them; they do not want the dead crashing through some unseen lower span where another strand might be sleeping. They simply hold the wrapped bundle out over the rushing mist until the updraft catches the thin membrane and lifts it gently away from their hands, vanishing within three heartbeats into the pale, swirling dark.

Then the dead person’s closest kin takes one thin bone slat from their own belt, chips a tiny nick from its edge so it sings slightly *flat* against the clean chord of the road, and ties it by a single worn sinew-thread to the nearest way-chime.

For weeks, or months, or a whole year, every traveler passing that junction hears the familiar, reassuring three-note clatter of the path—and, fluttering just beneath it, one soft, off-key rattle that does not quite fit. You do not cut it down. You leave it to the frost and the scouring gales until the thin cord finally frays through on its own and drops the slat into the abyss. Only when the chord rings clean again is the mourning done.

And yet the Walkers are not entirely unsentimental—and in the long, swaying dark of a dry gale, even the steadiest Chime-walker has ghosts.

They tell quiet stories, hunched around the cupped orange glow of the peat-pan, about the **Unmoored**: the broken saddle-huts you sometimes hear knocking unevenly far below a ridge, hanging askew from a single frayed guy-vine after some forgotten tremor tore their home arch loose. No sane Walker ever climbs down to loot a hanging hut. The cable might snap at a touch—and besides, everyone knows the old superstition that a severed hut keeps swaying long after its owners are gone because something of their balance is still tucked inside the hides, leaning endlessly into gusts that no longer matter.

Stranger still is the whispered tale they tell children who wake screaming from the **Falling Dream**—the dream every soul born on the spans has at least once a year, of unclipping a hook by mistake and dropping forever through the black, listening to the great bows sing fainter and fainter overhead until there is only wind.

No one truly believes in the ground—only in the jagged, cruel Teeth that stab upward through the dark. So to calm a trembling child, a parent will pull them close inside their coat, feel the gentle sway of the bow rock them both, and whisper that the void below the lowest arches is not empty at all. Down there, they say, miles beneath the coldest gales, the air grows thick and warm from the earth’s hidden cracks, so heavy with rising steam and pale spores that nothing can ever truly hit the bottom. And those who fell with a torn glider-hide wrapped around their arms, or clinging to a splintered mist-sail, are still down there—riding the warm dark updrafts in slow, effortless circles, listening to the distant hum of the continent overhead, never needing a harness again.

Then the parent taps three slow beats against the curved wall of the chamber—*safe, steady, here*—and waits for the wind-slit to hum its answer back, while outside in the freezing indigo night, ten thousand miles of living strings pull taut and sing on into the dark.
