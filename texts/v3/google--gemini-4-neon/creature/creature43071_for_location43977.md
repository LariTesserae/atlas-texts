You could cross a full kilometer of the gray avenues, your ears stuffed solid with the roar, and swear that nothing down here has hands.

Nothing darts away at your approach. No smoke threads upward toward the sky-rifts; no laundry snaps in the steady barometric draft; no distant shout cuts across the flat hiss of the cicadas. The plazas lie wide and pale beneath the slate dusk, marked only by the dry tracks of beetles and the quick flicker of grey lizards along the worn slabs.

Then you stop moving—stop long enough for your breath to quiet and your eyes to lose their surface impatience—and the small, deliberate things begin to separate themselves from the stone.

High up on one of the plunging root-cables, twenty feet above an old granary roof, a band of braided sinew has been wrapped tightly around a thick side-shoot, holding a hollowed nodule of greased limestone beneath a hairline notch in the pale bark. Every few minutes, a single bead of clear, bitter sap swells at the cut and drops into the cup. Along the lip of a sunken cistern, three flat slabs of chalky stone have been wedged upright across the windward mouth—not fallen there by chance, for their undersides still show the pale, unweathered scrape where rock was rubbed against rock within the last few decades. In the shadow of a dry aqueduct, a heavy mat of woven olive vines hangs straight and square across an oval doorway, its lower edge weighted with polished riverless cobbles so the steady draft cannot lift it.

And twenty paces away, seated on the rounded parapet of a stepped terrace, someone has been watching you since you entered the plaza.

They did not hide. They simply did not move fast enough to register as alive.

In a world filled wall-to-wall with an unchanging physical roar, an outsider is blindingly loud—not because of the noise they make, which vanishes instantly beneath the cavern-hum and the insect choir, but because of the silence they carve out around themselves. Walk briskly across a flagstone apron and the dry-earth crickets beneath the slabs hush in a traveling ring around your boots; the lizards freeze; the stout rock-dwellers on the upper ledges break into a rattling chain of alarm-barks that races half a mile down the terraces. To anything born in the basin, a hurried stride looks like a tear ripped straight through the fabric of the air.

The people who live here do not tear the air.

When the figure on the parapet finally rises, they do so at the pace of shifting midday light—a slow, unbroken unfolding of limbs that never once interrupts the metallic hiss rising from the crevices beneath their seat. Their feet are bare, broad, and calloused to a pale, horn-like thickness; as they step down onto the scooped troughs of the stair treads, they roll their weight smoothly from outer heel to toe so no grit grates, no pebble shifts, and no dust plumes up around their ankles.

Up close, they look as though the basin has been working on them with the same dry, patient erosion that rounded the limestone arches.

They are spare and long-fingered, with narrow ribs and strong, low-set shoulders shaped by generations of climbing braided roots and wedging dry stone. Their skin is matte and dry, carrying the muted slate-olive tone of the dusty evergreen shrubs, always faintly powdered with fine limestone silt in the hollows of their collarbones and the creases of their knuckles. Because the cool, chalky air drinks moisture from anything left exposed, they rub their faces, arms, and hands with a thin balm of rendered bat-fat and crushed waxy leaf-resin, leaving a quiet, dull sheen across their cheekbones. Their hair is coarse, dust-pale or dark as cured timber, kept tightly bound in braided root-fiber or tucked beneath close-fitting caps stitched from the velvety pelts of rock-dwellers so the drifting grit will not mat it into felt.

It is their eyes that hold you longest. Beneath heavy, shadowed brows, the irises are wide and pale—pewter, chalk-gray, washed-out amber—opening huge in the permanent twilight. They blink slowly, deliberately, with a thick, moisture-saving closure of the lids, and they almost never tilt their faces straight up toward the sky-rifts. Even the cold, indirect midday wash filtering down from the plateau above is harsh to them.

Their clothes belong wholly to the valley’s cold, fireless craft: sleeveless tunics and knee-length wraps of scraped rock-dweller hide and supple, pounded root-bark, dyed nothing brighter than dust, olive, and dried blood. Across their shoulders, many wear light, rustling mantles sewn from dozens of oiled bat-wing membranes stitched edge to edge with bird-sinew—translucent as old parchment, wind-proof against the steady barometric draft, holding the body’s modest warmth close against the chill of deep stone. Along the hems and cuffs, tiny iridescent shards of black beetle-carapace are knotted into the thread. In the dim gray air of the colonnades, those pale glints are often the first sign that another person is moving through the shadows.

***

They do not speak into the open air.

To shout in the basin is an act of violence and waste: it strains the throat, drains precious moisture from the lungs, and still scarcely carries past an arm’s length before the cicadas and the swirling swiftlets swallow it whole. Instead, they carry their language inside their bones.

Watch two of them meet in the middle of an empty avenue. They approach slowly, giving each other generous room across the flagstones until both have stopped and let the wall-crickets settle back into their steady pitch. Only then do they step close. One raises a hand and lays three chalk-dusted fingertips lightly against the hollow of the other’s collarbone; or, if they are kin or old companions, they lean in until the flat of their foreheads touch, eyes drifting half-shut.

Their mouths remain closed. Deep in the throat and the back of the nasal vault, they produce a tight, dry stream of hums, clicks, and modulated pulses—vibrations that never touch the roaring air at all, passing straight from skull to skull or through the resonant cartilage of the chest. They can stand like that for ten minutes in the center of a deafening plaza, utterly motionless as two weathered statues, carrying on an intricate, nuanced exchange about a shifting dove-colony or the health of a root-curtain half a mile away, losing scarcely a breath of water to the chalky draft.

It makes every conversation an act of trust. You cannot argue from across a room here; you cannot insult someone while walking away. To have words with another person, you must offer them your bone.

Across the vast, uncrowded distances between districts, where the eye can travel a quarter of a mile down an avenue of laid limestone, they rely on two older instruments: light and percussion. Every adult carries at their waist a pair of hand-length strikers carved from fallen splinters of the city’s ancient cured lintels—wood so old and bone-hard that when tapped together it rings with a clear, dry ceramic note. Struck inside the acoustic focus of a recessed doorway or a curved amphitheater wall, that sharp *tok—tok-tok* rides cleanly over both the low cavern-flute and the high insect hiss, bouncing cleanly down the limestone cliffs. It is not speech, but a sparse, basin-wide grammar of intervals: *a root has begun to dry; a roof-block has shifted; gather at the western cisterns; something has come down from the fissures.*

When finer meaning is needed at a distance, a watcher climbs to a rounded parapet and unrolls a wide length of pelts rubbed bright white with pure chalk, or holds up a dark mat of woven vine against the pale masonry. Slowly, with the patience of a sundial, they shift the cloth from shoulder to knee, from arch to ledge—a stately, visible semaphore easily read through the slate dusk three avenues away.

***

In a city without a single stream, seep, or puddle, the whole spine of their lives hangs from the unseen world overhead.

They have never seen the surface trees. Few among them have ever even climbed high enough into the shadowed overhangs to glimpse the thin jagged strip of sky through the rifts. To them, those colossal curtains of braided roots plunging hundreds of feet out of the upper darkness are simply the living rivers of the earth—vertical aqueducts carrying water down from an unimaginable tableland where rain still falls.

They treat the roots with an unsentimental, generational devotion. No one would ever hack a major cable or strip its bark; to kill a root-curtain is to condemn an entire quarter of the city to slow thirst. Instead, they climb the thick woody ropes with smooth, practiced grips, tending them like orchards. They scrape away choking dust-mats, bind split fibers tightly with dampened tendon, and bleed only the slender outer tendrils—nicking the skin with thin flakes of chipped chert no larger than a fingernail, tying greased limestone cups or stitched bladder-skins beneath the cut to catch the slow, patient weeping.

A single tapped tendril yields only a few mouthfuls over a full shift of light—a pale, cool liquid faintly tannic and sharp with green sap, tasting of a rain no living person down here has ever felt on their face. Every drop is rationed, carried down in sealed bat-membrane pouches stopped with plugs of resinous wood, shared first with nursing mothers and the very old.

Even more extraordinary is what they do when a new root appears.

Once or twice in a lifetime, high in the dim air beneath a sky-rift, a fresh tendril from some unseen sapling above pushes through a limestone crack and begins dangling downward into the void—pale as a thread, swaying imperceptibly in the barometric draft, two hundred feet short of the ground. Left alone, it might dry out in the cool air or take two centuries to reach the terraces.

So the people coax it.

Across decades, households gather beneath the dangling thread and dry-stack a slender, stepped cairn of limestone blocks atop the nearest granary roof or aqueduct arch, packing its summit with a dark, dampened mound of bat-guano and crushed waxy leaves. When the tendril grows close enough to brush the air above the highest terrace, a climber lashes a slender guiding-cord of braided sinew to its pale tip, weighted with a tiny pebble—never pulling hard enough to snap it, only offering it a steady downward gravity, inch by millimeter across the years, until at last its pale fingers strike the guano-packed stone, grip, thicken, and begin to anchor into the flagstones. The person who ties the first stone to a new root rarely lives to drink from it. Their grandchildren will.

Water also comes from everything green and everything breathing. They are quiet gardeners of the dry shadow. Inside the ancient sunken cisterns and leeward courtyards where dry droppings collect in deep, nutrient-rich drifts, they tend dense beds of the gnarled, slow-growing evergreen shrubs and silver-green rock-ferns, stacking low limestone windbreaks to hold the trapped coolness around the roots. Before eating the tough, waxy leaves, they wipe the fine limestone silt away with a thumb and chew them slowly until the bitter, moisture-bearing pulp yields its juice, or press heaps of them between heavy rubbed slabs to collect a dark, syrupy extract.

Beside the flora, the city’s vast animal chorus feeds them without ever seeming to diminish. They do not hunt with chases or clatter—a rushing pursuit would panic half a kilometer of galleries and leave the cliffs shrieking for hours. Instead, they harvest inside the slowness of the valley.

A hunter will sit beside a rock-dweller terrace for half a gray afternoon, completely dust-still, until the stout furry creatures resume their drowsy grooming a body-length away; then a weighted sinew noose settles with glacial smoothness, or a smooth stone drops once, cleanly, without triggering the alarm-chain. They climb into the vaulted storehouses at midday to gather swiftlet eggs and pluck slow, torpid bats from the edges of the dense ceiling carpets, never taking so many from one chamber that the colony abandons it. In fact, they maintain the colonies with careful craft: clearing fallen rubble from clogged archways so the flocks can wheel cleanly, leaving certain entire quarters untouched for years at a time so the birds and rock-dwellers breed thick and fearless.

Because there is no fire—no charcoal, no flame, no smoke to foul the dry trapped air—everything is eaten raw, fermented in sealed stone jars packed with crushed evergreen berries, or cured by the city itself. High in the cliff-cut galleries, where the cool barometric draft pulls strongest and driest through the oval doorways, they hang thin ribbons of rock-dove and rock-dweller flesh on strings of braided root-fiber. Within days, the bone-dry wind turns the meat dark, brittle, and dusted with fine chalk—light as dried bark, capable of keeping for years inside the domed storehouses without ever spoiling. Fresh blood and rendered fat are never wasted; they are drunk warm at the kill, supplying the salt and wet richness the bitter root-sap cannot give.

***

They occupy only a fraction of the vast stone shell—perhaps a few hundred souls, or a thousand, scattered in small, widely spaced households along the sheltered middle terraces—leaving whole avenues and stepped plazas to the beetles, the green thickets, and the wind.

They do not map this enormous city by sun-direction. Without a visible arc of daylight, east and west are vague notions, shifting gently as pewter brightness fades to charcoal. Instead, they know the basin by its acoustics.

To an outsider, the valley’s roar is a single, flat wall of sound. To someone whose bones grew inside it, the noise is an immense, three-dimensional geography with a thousand textures, pitches, and borders. Every district has its own chord. There is the low, sternum-bruising drone down by the eastern sunken basins, where the subterranean draft pours across twenty rectangular cisterns in a row and makes the teeth hum; there is the dry, sizzling treble of the upper aqueducts, where the cicadas pack three deep in the root-bound cracks; there is the hollow, fluttering echo beneath the collapsed northern vaults, where the rock-doves eddy in thousands.

A blindfolded child born in the basin could tell you within fifty paces where they were standing simply by listening to the balance between the cavern-flute beneath their soles and the cricket-hiss against their ears.

More than that, they read the roar the way surface people read the sky. Because the barometric breath connects the basin to the unseen plateau above and the deeper dry caverns below, every distant storm or cold front passing over the tableland miles away shifts the pressure of the draft—raising or lowering the city’s colossal flute-note by a hairline fraction. Long before the indirect light at the sky-rifts darkens to storm-pewter, the people feel the pitch drop in their ribs.

And in the deepest, most inward rooms carved directly into the cliff-ribs, they build what the open city never offers: **the hush-cells**.

By hanging three and four overlapping curtains of heavy woven olive-vine, braided root-mat, and stuffed rock-dweller pelts across the narrow oval mouths of deep rock-cut chambers, they baffle the exterior roar down to a distant, surf-like murmur. Inside a hush-cell, the air is cool, dry, and astonishingly soft. For the first time, you can hear the dry rasp of cloth moving over skin, the faint click of knuckles, the steady pace of your own breath.

They do not like to stay in the hush-cells too long. Too much quiet feels precarious to them, like the moment before a roof-slab cracks or a whole hillside of crickets goes silent before an unseen threat. They trust the roar; when a newborn is three days old, its parents carry it out of the padded dark and stand with it on an open flagstone apron, holding its small back against their chests so the full, steady vibration of the basin can settle into its ribs and teach it the permanent pulse of the world.

Yet the hush-cells are sacred to them, because they are the only places where a spoken voice can survive in the open air.

To speak aloud—to let words travel freely on breath rather than locked inside pressed bone—is something extraordinarily rare, tender, and private. Mothers go into the hush-cells to whisper low, rhythmic melodies to infants who are still too fragile for bone-speech. Lovers go there to hear the actual timbre of each other’s breath. And when an elder grows dry and light with age, nearing the end of their decades, kin gather inside the vine-hung dark so the dying person can spend their final moisture speaking aloud—telling slow, unhurried memories into the quiet air while the city’s muffled hum holds steady outside the curtains.

***

They have no memory of who built the city.

If you pressed your forehead to an elder’s and asked who hammered these miles of limestone block against block, who cut the broad colonnades and set the waterless conduits on their heavy arches, they would answer with a slow, puzzled vibration—as if you had asked who stacked the limestone cliffs themselves, or who dug the sky-rifts. To them, the city is not a ruin left behind by vanished people; it is simply the finished floor of the world, older than the roots, older than the bats, shaped once and for all into its final posture.

Yet they are not merely squatters in it. In their own glacial way, they are its tuners.

Across generations, they have learned how the city sings. Wedge a row of dry-fitted slabs halfway across the mouth of an upper gallery, drape a curtain of leathery vines across an empty cistern, clear decades of drifted limestone flakes from a deep stairwell—and the moving air shifts its voice across an entire quarter. Sometimes the adjustment is practical: a slight change in resonance keeps the restless bats from roosting inside a household’s sleeping-vaults, or coaxes the cool draft to linger inside a shadow-garden where the waxy ferns grow thickest.

Other times, it is purely for the chord itself.

At long, unhurried intervals—not set by seasons, for the valley has none, but by the slow consensus of clacked timber and chalk-white cloths—dozens of them gather inside one of the ancient stepped amphitheaters carved into the terraced flanks. They arrive gradually over half a day, drifting in from the distant quarters along the wide avenues, climbing the scooped limestone stairs without hurrying, taking their seats in quiet rows across the rounded stone tiers.

No one stands at the bottom to perform. No one speaks.

They simply sit together in the slate-colored dusk, hands resting flat on their knees or pressed lightly to the worn stone beside them, eyes closed, chests open to the valley. Above them, flocks of pale swiftlets wheel in vast, silent-seeming eddies beneath the overhangs, their cries folded seamlessly into the insect hiss and the deep, barometric hum rising out of the avenues. For hours they sit like that, feeling the entire basin vibrate through the limestone beneath them, listening to the slow, subtle work of their own hands—the faint new warmth in the low register where a western arch was narrowed ten years ago, the clear, dry ring of a newly cleared cistern, the imperceptible thickening of a century-old root humming taut as a bowstring in the steady draft.

If you asked them why they never climb the root-cables all the way up through the sky-rifts to see what lies on the tableland above, they would look at you with quiet, genuine dread.

Now and then, across the generations, a young climber goes too high into the pale glare of the fissures, or a storm-washed bird tumbles down from the rim, bruised and frantic, smelling of wet mud and sharp ozone. From those rare encounters, they know enough of the upper world to want no part of it. Up there, the light burns raw and unshaded; the wind does not breathe steadily, it tears and drops away into terrifying, broken silences; water falls violently out of nothing and rots everything it touches; nothing settles, nothing keeps its shape, and the air holds no constant note to anchor the ribs.

Down here, nothing is lost quickly.

Even their dead do not leave them in haste. In the bone-dry chill of the basin, with no dampness to soften flesh and no earth deep enough to dig without iron, bodies do not decay as they do on the surface. When someone’s breath stops inside a hush-cell, their kin wash them with dry limestone powder, rub their skin thoroughly with evergreen wax and bitter root-resin, and wrap them tightly in woven mats of olive vine.

Then they carry them up the polished, scooped stairways to the highest rock-cut galleries—the ancient upper courses whose oval mouths blend seamlessly into the natural overhang of the cliffs. There, far above the avenues, they seat the dead upright against the smoothed limestone walls, knees drawn up, faces turned outward toward the vast, dim horizon of the plazas.

Over the years, the cool, dry draft cures them as completely as it cured the ancient timber lintels. They grow light as dried gourds, matte and gray-brown, dusted grain by grain with falling chalk until they look scarcely different from the rounded parapets beside them. Rock-doves nest peacefully in the alcoves at their shoulders; silver-green ferns trail across their wrapped knees. Now and then, a living descendant climbs up to the high galleries, sits beside a cured great-grandmother in the dim air, and rests a palm gently against the dry, vine-wrapped shoulder—not expecting an answer, only sharing the vibration of the stone for an hour while the valley holds its note.

***

Leave the basin, climb back up through the narrow wind-scoured fissures to the open plateau, and the roar fades behind you until the surface world seems shockingly thin, bright, and half-empty.

You might look back down at the dark rift in the limestone and remember only the vastness of the dry stone city, the hanging curtains of roots, and the deafening, unchanging chorus of millions of bats, birds, and crickets.

Unless you remember the slowness. Unless you remember that moment in the middle of a wide, slate-gray plaza when you finally stood still long enough for the crickets beneath your boots to begin singing again—and realized that the pale, dust-matte figure sitting on the rounded parapet twenty paces away had never stopped watching you, one long-fingered hand resting lightly on the warm-smooth limestone, listening to the exact same note humming in your sternum, patient as the dust, holding the valley’s quiet from inside the roar.
