At first, in the bruised amber gloom and the tearing noise, you might think the machines are running themselves—some abandoned deep-earth engine left locked in gear while the roots and the swarming dark closed in around it. 

Then you notice the grease on the eccentric rods is fresh. You notice the carbon brushes on the humming dynamos have been shimmed with tight wedges of folded brass. And then, high on a swaying catwalk between two towers, a silhouette shifts that has neither the jagged, six-ribbed twitch of a climber nor the leathery slump of a roosting bellows-wing. It pauses by a caged filament bulb, reaches up with a bare forearm to tap a loose wire-guard back into place, and moves on, unhurried, stepping neatly over a surging braid of high-voltage cable as a farmer steps over a ditch.

There are people here. Not many—perhaps four hundred souls tucked into the shuttered cantilevered boxes of the six or seven towers, thinned out across the tall ironwork like birds nesting in the ribs of a bridge. Generations of living inside a sealed, deafening, shirt-sleeve engine room have pared them down to fit its seams.

They are compact, quick-wristed, and heavy through the shoulders and grip, with the pale, faintly ochre skin of things that never see a sun, permanently weathered around the wrists, necks, and knuckles with a dark patina of machine oil, sweet root-sap, and faint verdigris stains tucked beneath the fingernails. Because the cavern never cools, they go lightly dressed: sweat-darkened shifts woven from retted peat-fiber, heavy boiled-leather aprons tied high over the chest to shed hot tallow and spitting sparks, and thick-soled boots pegged with brass that let them feel the floor’s tremors while guarding their feet from the biting scum around the boiler footings. Almost every adult wears a tight, greased leather cap or keeps plugs of tallow-soaked moss tucked deep in their ears. Their eyes are wide, glossy, and slow to blink, built for amber dusk and bruised-green moss-glow; when a worn commutator throws a sudden blue arc, they flinch and turn their faces aside before the flash even fades.

In the open cavern, you will almost never hear them speak. Shouting into the grinding chord of the flywheels and the wet paper-roar of the wall-pelts is a child’s mistake that buys you nothing but a raw throat. Instead, they talk with iron and with light. Every worker carries a short brass-headed wrench or a tapping-pin at the belt, and the steam-mains and catwalk railings carry their arguments, warnings, and gossip clear across the open plazas in sharp, clipped bursts of metallic Morse—*clack-clack, pause, clack*—pitched high enough to knife straight through the bass drone of the transformers. Up close, across a loud mill floor, they sign with broad, crisp gestures, keeping their fingertips smeared with pale grease-chalk or crushed grazer-shell so their hands flash clearly against the dim ironwork.

Inside the shuttered tenements, where heavy timber boards and peat-caulked seams muffle the outside world to a steady, vibrating rush, they do use their voices—though even there, generations of competing with the machines have tuned their speech into a strange, tight, rapid register, flat and slightly metallic, sitting precisely in the narrow acoustic slot between the deep *thup* of the bellows-wings and the dry hiss of the walls.

The oldest among them do not hear air-sound at all. Forty years of quarter-hour steam shrieks and plunging echolocators take a person’s ears cleanly by middle age. Yet these deaf elders are the most prized folk in the settlement. They walk the lower turbine halls barefoot or press their bare palms, forearms, and jawbones straight against a hot boiler casing or a root-truss, closing their eyes to read the ward by vibration alone. A deaf listener can feel a dry main bearing shivering three floors down, or catch the faint, uneven hitch of a cracking flywheel hub weeks before it tears loose and sends jagged iron spinning through the tenements. When an elder lays a cheek against a riveted stanchion and frowns, the younger mechanics reach for their spanners without asking twice.

They are not mining anything, nor forging goods for some distant surface none of them has ever seen. Words like *sky* and *quarry* and *raw stone* linger in their speech only as dead metaphors—things dry, cold, and impossibly quiet, hard to imagine and unpleasant to think about. The machinery runs because the machinery is the climate, and the climate is the only thing keeping the hollow alive.

Without the reciprocating presses stamping dense cakes of harvested peat-hide, root-fiber, and tallow, the boilers would go cold. Without the boiler heat and the sour-ozone warmth of the dynamos rising to the arched vault eighty yards up, the ceiling bladders would stop sweating condensate. Without the sudden warm dumps of water, the spongy root-floor would desiccate, the wall-pelts of dermal grazers would flake off in choking gray dust, the burrowers would starve, and the sealed air would turn dead and stale. The settlement is not a factory; it is an organ. The people are simply the stubborn, greasy nervous system keeping its pulse from skipping.

Because the living vault would swallow their iron towers in a decade if left untended—and because the towers would topple in a year without the roots gripping their rusted masonry—a quiet, endless negotiation goes on around every foundation. The footing-wardens spend their shifts hunched in the warm, sap-weeping crawlspaces beneath the mills with curved drawknives, hot irons, and wedges of salvaged steel. They never kill a root-buttress; they train it. By scoring a pale woody knuckle on one side and packing the wound with bitter grease-sludge, they persuade a thickening root to bend *around* a flywheel pit rather than through it, coaxing the cavern itself to cradle their crumbling brick piers. The tough, fibrous shavings they pare away from the encroaching floor-borders are bundled straight into the press hoppers, compressed under twenty tons of iron stroke, and fed back to the fireboxes.

Above them, in the humming switch-houses and transformer bays, move the winders—the quietest, most careful people in the ward, their forearms striped with pale scars from long-ago burns and their aprons dusted green with verdigris. Copper is precious here; nothing new ever enters the hollow, so a burned-out coil is unwound by hand, scraped clean, varnished with boiled root-sap, and wound back onto its core with reverent patience. The winders have made a strange truce with the violet-veined polyps that cement themselves to live windings. They scrape the colonies back only when a crusty verdigris shell threatens to bridge two terminals and throw a short; the rest they leave alone, regarding a thick, evenly pulsing bloom as the sign of a healthy circuit. Every winder wears a damp, hollowed knuckle of root-wood on a leather thong around the neck, inside which lives a single plump polyp kept wet with sap-water. Before touching a doubtful busbar or a dark junction box, they press the damp cup to the metal. If the little violet creature tightens and shivers at sixty cycles a second, the line is hot; if it stays slack, they go to work bare-handed.

Higher still, clinging to the top gantries where the air smells sharpest of yeast and musk from the hanging curtains of bellows-wings, sit the bladder-riggers. They are lean, steady-nerved watchers who spend their shifts staring upward into the bruised shadows of the ceiling ribs, reading the pendulous, melon-skinned water sacs the way sailors read squall lines. A forty-gallon drop from eighty yards will cave in a corrugated shed roof or drown an open dynamo deck in a heartbeat. The riggers know the exact sheen of a bladder whose stem is about to tear—the tight, pale translucence when the condensate turns it the color of boiled tallow. Long before it tips over vital ironwork, a rigger leans out over the abyss with a twenty-foot lance of hardened root-wood tipped with a barbed iron hook, snags the taut stem, and hauls it sideways on a block-and-tackle until it hangs clear over an empty stretch of springy root-plaza. A twist of the wrist, a sudden tear, and the deluge plunges harmlessly onto the thirsty floor while the rigger wipes warm spray from their eyebrows and scans the next rib over.

Everything in the ward—sleep, meals, courtship, repair—is cut into fifteen-minute slices by the main accumulator stack. Nobody owns a pocket watch; the hollow itself is the clock. They speak of time in *Vents* or *Blows*. A short nap is four Vents. A mill-watch is twenty-four. A stew needs eight Vents on the steam-pipe jacket to soften the tough fruiting-knuckles.

And when the shift-lamps along the tenements dip to ember-red and the heavy presses begin to cycle up, a sudden, fierce stillness falls over the lower catwalks. People step back under the overhanging eaves of the workshops, pulling broad, sap-lacquered leather cowls down over their skulls and shoulders like beetles drawing in their wings. 

Everyone except the runners.

Mostly young, light on their feet, and half-drunk on the rush of it, the flush-runners crouch at the open mouths of the lower mills with woven wicker creels strapped tight across their backs and long hooked gaffs in their hands. The instant the accumulator valve shrieks open and the three-minute storm breaks loose, they vault out into the wide, dim plazas. Overhead, two or three ceiling bladders tip in ragged thunderclaps; curtains of blind bellows-wings erupt through the swirling steam, booming *thup-thup-thup* so loudly it rattles the teeth in the runners’ heads; clouds of pale dermal grazers wash off the corrugated roofs in glistening sheets.

The runners sprint straight into the churning dark, weaving between the boiling puddles and ducking beneath the banking, coat-wide shadows of diving fliers. They have three minutes to glean what the cavern only surrenders during the Flush: heavy, nut-rich fruiting-knuckles snapped loose from the high canopy by the bursting sacs; thick mats of clean, tender wall-grazers sluiced down off the upper ironwork before they can crawl back up; and, best of all, the occasional stunned bellows-wing that misjudged its dive in the steam-mist and clipped a braided power cable. A runner will scoop a thrashing flier by its leathery wing-joints, snap its neck with a practiced twist, heave it into the creel, and fend off a surging, blunt-snouted floor-burrower with the butt of a gaff—all while dodging the sudden forty-gallon hammer of a late-tipping bladder. When the relief valve clangs shut and the amber bulbs brighten back up, the runners come stumbling back onto the mill decks drenched, steaming, scraped raw by stray climber-claws, grinning breathless white grins through the oil on their faces, their baskets heavy with warm meat and fresh sap-cake.

Not even the hog-sized burrowers go to waste. The ward-folk treat them as semi-wild livestock, tossing spoiled mash and bruised root-cuttings into the warm, sludge-rimmed hollows beneath the boiler piers to keep the beasts from gnawing through the mortar of the pump-houses. Every few shifts, a crew corners an older boar with iron-shod boar-spears and heavy nooses. Its pale, lichen-furred hide makes tough aprons; its thick layer of yellow fat is rendered down in iron cauldrons—half for the cook-pots, half mixed with graphite to grease the stamping presses. And when the butchers split the beast’s muscular, stone-hard gizzard, the foundry-hands stand waiting with a tin pan. Inside, tumbled bright and smooth as river-pebbles by the burrower’s grinding jaws, sit ounces of swallowed scrap: corroded valve-nuts, sheared rivets, stray windings, and antique fittings the beast has rooted out of a century’s worth of floor-peat. They call it *crop-brass*. Melted down in tiny coal-fired crucibles, it comes back to life as fresh bushings, switch-contacts, and grease-cups.

Pull back the heavy timber shutter of a tenement box high on the flank of Block Four, and the roar drops to a warm, cradling hum you feel mostly in the soles of your feet and the trembling concentric rings inside a tin cup of sap-tea. 

The rooms are tiny, ship-like, stacked three and four deep on groaning timber braces, smelling richly of rendered lard, roasted fruiting-knuckle, clean boiled linen steaming over an insulated radiator pipe, and the faint sweet-sour tang of the sap weeping through the floor-joints. Because light is scarce and filament bulbs are guarded jealously for the gantries and dynamos, most families keep shallow clay saucers of damp floor-moss on the table and beside the narrow bunk-frames. Whenever someone wakes in the dark to tend a coughing child or find their boots before a watch, they press a thumb gently across the spongy moss; it answers with a slow, bruised-green glow, bright enough to read a face by, fading softly away a minute later as the moss settles back to sleep.

Children here grow up with astonishing balance and an almost eerie calm inside the noise. Infants sleep strapped in canvas slings suspended by leather springs from the ceiling rafters, so the harsh jolts of the stamping presses soften into a gentle, rhythmic sway. By the time a child is five, they know better than to leave a shutter unlatched against the chattering, six-ribbed climbers that prowl the outside braces; they can tell by the pitch of the humming walls whether Block Two’s dynamo is pulling heavy load; and they know how to scrape the salty, translucent wall-grazers off the outer walkway without pinching their fingers, pressing the catch between warm iron plates to make crisp, paper-thin wafers chewed with bitter moss-salt.

They have their small, quiet beauties, too. In the few Vents between shifts, people carve intricate knots and tiny mechanical puzzles out of seasoned root-wood, or polish the dead verdigris shells of transformer polyps and set them into clear beads of hardened amber sap, stringing them into dull green-and-gold necklaces that catch the dim lamp-pools. Courtships happen on the high, quiet catwalks during the lulls between bladders—two people leaning side by side on a warm steam-main, sharing a lump of sweet sap-toffee, tapping soft, private jokes into the iron pipe between their elbows where nobody else can read them.

When someone finally wears out—their lungs thickened by decades of damp ozone, their knees stiffened by the springy floors, their hands too curled to hold a wrench—they are not burned, for smoke has nowhere to go in a sealed vault, and they are not left to the floor-hogs. Instead, the footing-wardens carve a deep, clean cradle straight into the side of one of the massive pale root-buttresses gripping the base of the dead person’s own tower. The body is washed in warm condensate, wrapped in a single unbleached peat-cloth, and laid inside the living wood, packed snugly around with sweet amber sap and damp moss-bark. 

The cavern is hungry and patient. Within twenty shifts, the pale root-flesh swells and knits smoothly across the hollow, sealing the seam with a thick, calloused bulge of fresh ochre bark. The dead do not vanish into earth; they thicken the knuckles that hold up their grandchildren’s floors. Passing out of Block Six on the way to the turbine pits, workers still reach out without thinking to lay a greasy palm against a particular smooth rib of root clamping an iron stanchion, feeling the steady vibration of the presses thrumming right through it, greeting an aunt or a grandfather who still helps keep the walls upright.

They have only one true fear, and it is not the bursting bladders, nor the slashing swoop of a bellows-wing, nor the slow, patient squeeze of the living ceiling overhead. It is the Cold Stop. 

Every few years, a main belt snaps with a gunshot crack, or a bridged polyp-colony trips a heavy breaker, and for ten terrible seconds one whole wing of the hollow drops out of the chord. The lamps die. The presses hang mid-stroke. And in that sudden, ragged hole in the sound, the roaring wall-pelts falter, the floor seems to lose its warmth beneath your boots, and you can almost feel the miles of silent, dead stone waiting patiently beyond the roots.

In every shuttered tenement across the six towers, conversations break off mid-word. Sleepers bolt upright in the bruised-green dark, palms flat against the floorboards, holding their breath—until the backup switch slams home, the dynamos catch with a rising whine, the blue arc spits across the copper, and the heavy iron heartbeat picks up its groove once more. Only then do the people of the hollow let out their breath, turn back to their benches and bunks, and go on humming, mending, harvesting, and leaning into the dark alongside the vast living machine that holds them.
