If you walk slowly down from the corbelled bridges and follow one of the mossy avenues into a residential quarter, the people do not vanish or hurry indoors. They simply come into focus the way the salamanders on the runnel-lips do—first as shapes of stone and wool, then as hands moving with a patience so practiced it looks, from a distance, like stillness.

Up close, they look made of the same wool, slate, and cool light as the basin itself. They are spare, deep-chested people, built for thin air and long stairs, with wide shoulders and strong, unhurried legs. Their faces carry the particular weathering of high altitude without wind: skin pale and fine-grained where the eaves keep them shaded, faintly burnished along the bridge of the nose and the tops of the cheekbones where the unchanging midmorning strikes. Because the sun never shifts its angle, they know every path in the city by the touch of light on their faces—warmth on the left temple walking east, cold air grazing the jaw turning down into a shaded trough. Fine creases fan from the corners of their eyes from a lifetime of stepping across the three-inch boundary between high glare and velvety dimness.

Their garments are built for that exact boundary. Every person wears layers of undyed wool, felted dense and heavy, the color of the shaggy grazers that wander the green intervals—oat-cream, wet slate, dark charcoal, and the mottled grays where both fibers have been carded together. The outer wraps are thick enough to bead with mist on the shoulders without soaking through, fastened at the collarbone with a single large pin of cold-hammered copper. Beneath the wool, they wear softer shifts of beaten plant-fiber woven from the stalks of the marsh sedges, and on their feet, thick-soled boots of layered felt and cured hide stitched with bone needles. Like the spongy hooves of the mountain herds, their soles grip the wet, glass-sharp rock without slipping and without making a sound. A dozen people can cross a flagged plaza behind you, and you will hear nothing more than the faint, dry brush of wool against wool.

Look at their hands and you can read what part of the basin they tend. Those who work the high dew-combs have pale, clean-scoured knuckles and small calluses along the insides of their fingers from drawing horsehair and split reeds tight across weathered cedar frames. The gardeners who keep the terraced plots have soil darkened permanently into the fine lines of their palms and faint green stains beneath their nails from pinching back the silver-veined greens and feather-moss. The stone-dressers carry a silvery sheen of ground slate-dust in the creases of their wrists, and almost everyone has one thumb slightly smoothed and polished from resting absentmindedly on their copper pin while they pause to think.

They do not speak the way people speak in cities with crowds, storms, or markets. When sound sinks into the moss and never echoes back, a raised voice feels less like communication and more like a crack opening in a sound cistern. Their speech is low, brief, and pitched just beneath the faint trickle of the runnels, full of soft consonants and long, breathy pauses. Much of what needs to be said passes without words at all: a slight dip of the chin on a narrow stair; two fingers laid briefly against a companion’s copper pin to acknowledge a task completed; a palm turned upward toward the sun-side of a wall to show that a neighbor is resting within. Across half a mile of fern-slope, they read one another as easily as the tailless rock-mammals read the flick of an ear—knowing by the set of a walker’s shoulders whether a hamper is heavy with wet tubers or light with folded cloth, knowing by the angle of a kneeling figure whether a comb-post needs bracing or merely brushing clean.

When they do speak at length, their language reveals how strangely time lives inside them. Having no nights, no seasons, and no moving sun, they have no words for hours, days, or years. There are no calendars carved into their plazas and no rings inside the dark timber of their roofs, for the dwarf pines and highland cedars grow in an even, uninterrupted grain. Instead, the people measure what has passed by growth, by wear, and by water. A neighbor will say that a stone slab was reset *three trimmings ago*, meaning the emerald moss has crept outward from its shadow-line and been pared back to its carved groove three times since. An elder remembers her childhood as *two trowels back*, measuring her life by the slow vanishing of short bronze blades sharpened down to the tang on wet slate. Between two people who live in the same quarter, a short wait is *a basin’s fill*, or *while the peat turns gray*.

Because there is no night to send the city to bed at once, nobody sleeps on the same turn. Life inside the wide stone halls moves in a soft, staggered drift, never wholly awake and never wholly hushed.

Step through the woven rush-screen of a dwelling—every one of them built squarely across a permanent knife-edge of light and shade—and you discover the three weathers the people inhabit. On the bright front porch, where the dressed slate stays faintly warm beneath the bare hand, an older man might sit cross-legged in the glare, carding loose tufts of cream wool combed from a dozing grazer, while rows of pale highland grain and sliced tubers dry beside him on clean slabs. Pass straight through the doorway into the back of the hall, where the roof eaves cast a fixed, cold darkness, and the air drops ten degrees in three paces. Here the stone walls glisten faintly with condensation; wide clay crocks hold dark berries, salted tubers, and curdled milk from the mountain herds, kept perpetually cold as well-water without ice.

Between those two zones—buried deep inside the thick, dry-laid boulder foundations where neither the dry glare nor the dripping draft can reach—lies the only weather the basin cannot make on its own: warm darkness.

These inner rooms are low-ceilinged, lined with woven rush-mats and draped in thick felted hangings. At the center of each sits a broad stone dish holding a nest of compressed peat that burns with a dull, ember-red heart and almost no smoke, filling the chamber with the faint, earthy scent of bog-moss and dry warmth. This is the *made night*. Whenever a person’s eyelids grow heavy—whether their household-mates are out tending the ridges or kneading flatbread on a heated slab—they unpin their outer wrap, step behind the heavy wool curtain into the peat-warmed dark, and lie down on piled fleeces. One person may rise from the inner room, blinking slowly as they step onto the bright porch to rinse their face in a brimming basin, just as a sister or a husband comes in off the causeway, shoulders beaded with mist, ready to turn in. They pass in the dim stone threshold and lay a hand lightly on each other’s forearm—one palm warm from the brazier, the other crisp and cold from the open ridge—without needing to wake the sleepers behind the curtain.

This unsynchronized rhythm makes them deeply companionable and deeply solitary at once. Crowds almost never form. Even in the largest flagged plazas, where people from distant quarters meet to exchange what their slopes produce—bundles of tight-grained cedar staves from the upper rims for baskets of marsh-sedge fiber from the low troughs, or blocks of cut peat for freshly cold-hammered copper cramps—the gathering looks less like a market than a slow choreography. Someone sets a woven hamper along a sun-lit border stone, sits quietly on the parapet watching the crane-owls spear shrimp in the cistern, and waits until a figure in gray wool walks up from the opposite causeway. They examine each other’s goods by touch, lifting the cloth to test its weight, smelling the dried herbs, murmuring four or five words so low they vanish before reaching the far lip of the pool. Nothing is haggled over; scarcity is almost unknown here, and excess is merely weight to be carried up stone stairs.

More than anything else, the people of the basin are defined by a devotion that outsiders might mistake for habit, and that they themselves scarcely bother to name: the craft of *holding*.

To live here for generations is to learn that the city is not something placed on top of the valley, but a delicate skin stretched inside its breathing. Every mason, gardener, and comb-tender knows in their bones what a single careless change can do. Build a wall a hand’s-breadth higher, and its permanent shadow reaches three paces further across the terrace behind it. Within a few turnings, the pale golden lichens in that newly shadowed strip die back, sphagnum moss swells up in their place, the dampness creeps into a dry cellar-wall, and the overflow notch at the bottom of the quarter begins to weep faster than the soil can drink it. Tear down a timber gallery and leave bare rock to the bright midmorning, and the heat rising off the sunlit slab nudges the slow drift of cool air off the eastern ridge, starving the horsehair combs directly downwind.

So they do not expand, and they do not innovate for the sake of novelty. Their highest pride lies in making their labor disappear into the balance that already exists.

Watch a stone-worker replacing a fractured slate shingle on a hall roof. She does not fetch stone from whatever scree lies closest; she walks half a mile to the exact dark seam from which the old roof was split generations ago, drives wet wooden pins into the natural cleavage with a stone maul, and waits with quiet patience for the swelling timber to part the slab cleanly. She dresses its edges with wet river-sand until its weight, thickness, and dull gleam match the weathered slates around it. When she pins it into place with bone pegs and slips the old copper cramp back over its rim, not even the shadow beneath the eave shifts by the width of a hair. If a passerby can tell which slate is new, she considers the work unfinished.

The same quiet artistry governs the water. Along the edges of the terraces, certain elders spend their waking spells listening to the cisterns. They kneel beside the carved stone spouts with tiny bronze chisels and bone scrapers, shaving a nearly invisible burr of mineral scale from a lip or deepening a flute by a fraction of a grain. They do this not only to keep the basins brimming evenly to their glass-taut rims, but for the sound. Because the basin holds no wind and no thunder, the faint hollow *tock* of falling dew-drops is the only pulse the air carries. Every courtyard has its own water-tempo—one deep, slow note from a broad stone snout answered six breaths later by a brighter, quicker drop from a narrow overflow notch. A person born in the western halls can close their eyes anywhere in their quarter and know exactly which terrace they stand on by the unhurried rhythm of its cisterns. It is the closest thing the city has to music, and the closest thing to a bell.

Among the valley’s animals, the people move without dominance or ceremony, treated less as masters than as another slow-moving highland species. They do not pen the shaggy mountain grazers or drive them with dogs; instead, when a herd wanders beneath an arched causeway and beds down in the quiet corner of an outlying plaza, two or three wool-gatherers walk out with wide-toothed bone combs and sit cross-legged in the damp turf beside them. The beasts scarcely lift their broad, curved horns as the people gently work loose the shed clumps of tangled cream and charcoal wool from their flanks, leaving the dense undercoat intact against the cold mist. When a grazer grows very old and dies on one of the wild fern-slopes, the people take its horns for spoons and lamp-scoops, its long bones for roof-pegs and rakes, and its hide for boot-soles, leaving the rest for the quiet scavengers of the ridges.

They step around the flat-bodied salamanders lining the aqueduct lips as automatically as they step over threshold stones, and they never shoo the slate-gray crane-owls from the public cisterns, even when a dozen of the solemn birds crowd a parapet and watch them draw water with wide, unblinking eyes. When a pale velvet moth settles on a weaver’s sleeve, she simply slows her shuttle until it lifts away on its own silent wings.

It is easy, watching all this, to imagine the people as serene as the stone they dress—touched by the same eerie equilibrium that holds the lone cloud pinned against the eastern teeth. Yet the basin is timeless, and the people are not. Inside the unmoving midmorning, they are still warm-blooded, still frail, still born into bodies that grow, ache, desire, and eventually fail.

You see that tender friction most clearly in their children.

A newborn’s cry is the sharpest sound the valley ever hears, startling the crane-owls from the eaves before the moss and open air drink it down. For their earliest turnings, infants are kept close to the peat-warmed dark and carried inside the folds of their parents’ wool cloaks, peering out with bright, dark eyes at the gleaming stone lips and drifting copper beetles. Once they find their feet, though, children break every rhythm the city keeps. They run loud and breathless along the mossy ramps; they slap their flat palms into the mirror-still cisterns just to watch the bulging surface tension spill over the carved rims in bright sheets; they invent games along the knife-edge of the light, leaping back and forth from warm stone to dripping emerald velvet until their cheeks flush scarlet.

The adults do not scold them into stillness. An elder kneeling beside a garden-bed will simply catch a running child by the hem of their wool tunic, sit them down on the dry stone edge, and place a pale, glistening frog on the back of their hand, waiting quietly until the child holds their breath so the creature will not leap away. Over the turnings, the valley itself teaches them its pace. You can watch a youth’s stride gradually lengthen, their voice drop an octave into the soft register of the runnels, their fingers learn the patient weight of a copper pin or a bone rake, until one day they pause on a corbelled bridge and hear, for the first time, the exact six-breath interval between two dripping spouts below.

Even then, the restlessness does not vanish altogether from human blood.

Every household knows of a quiet, unspoken affliction they call *carrying too much light*, or simply *the hurry*. It can touch a young person whose bones are still stretching, or an artisan who has dressed the same seam of dark slate for half a lifetime. It begins subtly: a person starts speaking in full, quick sentences; they walk too fast down the shallow stairs, scuffing the wet moss; they stand beside a cistern and find themselves wishing a storm-wind would tear the white ribbon of cloud free from the eastern teeth and scatter rain across the dry plazas. Sometimes they chip a sound shingle just to feel a fresh edge break, or stare out at the unshifting shadows until their eyes water with a nameless ache for dusk.

No one treats this as a fault or tries to argue them back into calm. The people understand that a mortal mind is not made of vitreous stone; sometimes the held morning presses too close against the ribs. When someone shows the signs of the hurry, a neighbor may quietly set a small hamper of flatbread, dark berries, and smoked tubers beside their threshold. Often, the restless one takes it and walks out past the last stone quarters, climbing the wild, unbuilt boulder fields toward the jagged eastern rim where the cold breath slips down from the cloud-pinned peaks. They sit for several sleeps among the raw triangular shards of rock, wrapped tight in wool, watching the empty pale cobalt sky and feeling the damp air move endlessly across their face, until the tightness loosens in their chest and they walk slowly back down to the terraced gardens.

A very few do not come back down. Now and then, over the long span of generations, a figure climbs the knife-edged crest beneath the cloud, pauses once to look back across the wide net of stone causeways and green velvet troughs, and steps over the ridge into whatever lies beyond the basin. No one climbs after them, and no one speaks of them with grief or anger. Someone simply takes up their bone rake, trims their stretch of moss back to the carved line, and keeps the terrace held.

For those who stay—and almost all of them stay, bound by a quiet, bone-deep love for this clear air and soft green silence—growing old is a slow folding back into the city’s warmth. Elders whose joints stiffen in the dripping shade spend more of their waking hours on the dry sun-struck porches, backs pressed against faintly warm ashlar, spinning fine sedge-thread or mending the woven rush-screens while younger hands climb the high ridge-combs. Their stories are the only history the basin possesses, told in low voices inside the peat-lit dark of the inner rooms: tales not of wars or kings, for there are none, but of the great repair of the northern aqueduct, of a shaggy grazer born entirely silver-white, of the long-ago artisan who tuned the seven cisterns of the low plaza so cleanly that their drops fell in a braided pattern that took half a waking to repeat.

When at last an elder’s breath thins and stops inside the warm darkness of the inner room, there are no bells, no funeral pyres, and no carved monuments left to cast unwanted shadows across the plazas. The household washes the body with cool dew-water from the courtyard basin, combs the hair smooth, and wraps them in their oldest, softest sheet of undyed wool. At the shoulder, the nearest kin unfastens the heavy copper pin that has held their cloak for a lifetime and keeps it in their palm.

Then four or five people walk single-file along an outlying causeway, carrying the light bundle out into one of the broad, unbuilt intervals between quarters—into a deep, permanently shaded trough where the waist-high bracken and dwarf junipers hang draped in beard-lichen, and emerald sphagnum upholstery covers every sharp edge of stone. There they lay the dead gently into the plush, dripping velvet of the moss, uncovered by earth or rock, and stand for a long space in the quiet air, listening to the distant *tock* of a terrace spout. They do not need to dig a grave. In the cool, perpetual damp of the shaded hollows, the feather-moss begins to climb within a few turnings, folding slowly over the pale wool until the form softens into one more dark green pillow among the ferns, returning without hurry to the cold peat that holds the valley’s water.

Back in the hall, the copper pin is laid for a short while in the red heart of the peat brazier until the metal softens. Then it is lifted onto a flat stone anvil and cold-hammered slowly—tapped with a smooth stone maul in a soft, rhythmic ringing that carries barely ten paces past the doorway—until the old bends flatten, the verdigris rubs clean, and the copper takes a fresh gleam. In time, it will be shaped anew and pressed through the collar of a child who has just grown tall enough to walk the high ridge-combs alone, carrying the quiet warmth of twenty pairs of hands forward into the next turning.

If you linger on the corbelled bridge long enough, you will see one of them pause directly below you beside a brim-full plaza basin. A woman in mottled gray wool kneels to skim a single fallen juniper needle from the glass-taut surface with two fingers, careful not to break the mirror. Her copper pin catches the pale midmorning light—one small, warm spark against dark slate and emerald moss. She rests one palm on the dry, faintly sun-warmed parapet and the other, three inches away, on the cold wet stone of the shadow-side, feeling both halves of her world at once. Above her, the lone ribbon of cloud holds its exact station against the jagged eastern teeth; beside her, a slate-gray bird settles onto the stone lip without a whisper of air; and after a quiet breath, she rises, lifts her woven hamper, and walks on down the long avenue—unhurried, mortal, and wholly at home inside the held morning.
