If you come down into the basin and walk its flooded lanes for ten minutes, you will leave convinced the place is empty. Nothing darts out of your path. Nothing watches you from the fanlights or rustles behind the paddle-leaved ferns. Even if you stand directly beside one of the residents, leaning against a damp brick corner to catch your breath in the warm, booming haze, you are likely to mistake it for a calcified bollard, a slumped bundle of oiled hemp wedged beneath a lintel, or an old lime-encrusted cistern valve that someone abandoned to the moss generations ago.

You only see them if you stop moving at your own speed and wait for the pace of the ward to catch up with you.

Stay on the central footbridge through two liftings of the quarter-hour steam valve—long enough for your ears to flatten the fifty-cycle bass into silence and your eyes to adjust to the dull amber globes—and the geometry of the narrow streets below begins to shift. A hunched, pale-grey column standing half inside a glazed brick doorway is no longer flush with the jamb; it has moved a pace out into the shallow runoff. Beneath an overhead cooling rack, what looked like a bloated cushion of verdigris-stained sponge perched on a junction box has tilted twenty degrees toward the falling curtain of water. Down the east alley, two squat, barrel-bellied shapes that seemed bolted to the flagstones are slowly, almost imperceptibly, drawing apart.

They live here because they do not possess the thing the basin rejects. They have no nerves—no fragile, hair-thin filaments firing swift sparks across wet flesh, nothing for the roaring bass and stray voltage to scramble or drive mad. They are made of the precinct’s own stubborn grammar: mineral salt, braided vine-fiber, pressurized water, and slow electrical charge.

Up close, in the dim, tea-colored glow of a caged walkway lamp, one of the walkers looks less like an animal than a piece of wet masonry that grew tired of standing still. Most are roughly the height of a stooped man, though narrow-shouldered from generations of threading two-man alleys choked with pipe-risers. Their outer hide is a thick, felted rind of dark olive sponge-moss stitched through with overlapping plates of chalky lime and dense hematite crust—the same smooth, protective rust that seals the iron girders. Where their bodies bend, along deep horizontal pleats at the waist or the thick, unjointed bellows of their limbs, the mineral crust breaks into tight seams that grind softly against one another, kept slick by a clear, slightly oily condensate weeping from within.

They have no legs in the ordinary sense, only two or three heavy, splayed anchoring trunks that stay submerged in the warm street-runoff. When they move, they scarcely lift these bases clear of the flagstones; instead, they ease them forward with a slow, suctioning glide that barely disturbs the crisp standing ripples on the water’s surface. Higher up, two—sometimes three—ribbed, club-ended appendages hang from their sloped shoulders, wrapped tightly in pale, copper-veined climbing shoots. They have no fingers, only broad, pleated pads of dark, sponge-like tissue inlaid with metallic grit, soft enough to mold themselves flush around the curve of a pipe, a rusted iron wheel, or the porcelain ribs of a transformer standoff.

And they have no faces. Where a head might sit, each body ends in a wide, shallow dish of bone-pale calcified lime, thick-lipped as an old stone basin and fringed around the rim with stiff, glassy fern-bristles. That dish is always brim-full of clear mineral water. Because the whole sunken ward vibrates with the deep hum of the submerged turbines, the water carried in every crown is held in a razor-sharp geometric mandala—a motionless silver lattice of cross-hatched ripples and concentric rings.

That small carried pond is how they know the world.

They do not see the bruised grey light above the skylights, nor do they hear the deafening roar of falling water as sound. Instead, every event in the basin writes itself directly onto the surface of their water. When a hydraulic ram two blocks away bottoms out its minute-long stroke, the cross-hatching in a walker’s crown tightens by a hair’s breadth. When a heavy brass flywheel catches its pawl, a brief hexagonal ring blooms at the center of the dish and fades. A drop in water pressure along the north risers, a fat droplet bridging two damp ceramic insulators high on a transformer balcony, a choked floor grate softening the runoff’s hum three lanes away—all of it arrives as fine shifts in interference, tilted planes of vibration, and minute changes in electrical resistance rising through the wet flagstones beneath their soles.

Inside the thick felt-and-lime hide, there are no bones, no muscles, no lungs or heart. They are built of nested, tough-walled bladders filled with warm brine, separated by porous mineral partitions laced with the same copper-drawing fibers that wind around the ward’s busbars. They move the way the sight-gauges move. To lift a heavy limb or shift their weight forward, they let a slow trickle of low-voltage current pass across an internal wall, drawing water from one chamber into the next until the receiving sac swells taut as a pressurized hose and its twin slackens. It takes half a minute to raise an arm, ten minutes to cross a narrow street. And the fifty-cycle bass trembling up through the stone is what keeps them alive: without that constant, bone-deep vibration stirring their internal brines, their salts would settle into dead sediment, their porous walls would choke, and they would stiffen into dry rubble. Silence would kill them faster than drought.

Because they grow rather than being cast from a single mold, their shapes answer to the corners of the ward they inhabit.

Down in the narrow flooded lanes live the upright walkers—tall enough to reach the lower valve-wheels and brick-mounted switchboxes, their calcified crusts streaked pale grey and brick-brown from brushing against wet mortar. Deeper inside the pump-houses, wedged beneath the great rocking walking beams where the air is hottest and thick with boiled lime, squat huge, barrel-chested elders whose hides have gone nearly black with oil and iron scale, scarcely moving a yard in the course of a day.

Look upward, though—past the caged amber lamps, into the three and four tiers of overhead pipes, iron catwalks, and rotting cedar cooling racks—and you will find another kind altogether. Up there, where there are no flagstones to stand upon, they have grown broad, low, and slung like heavy wet saddlebags draped across the copper-jacketed steam risers. They haul themselves along the overhead conduits with slow, patient reaches of four ribbed arms, their mineral dishes sunk deep into the center of their flat, mossy backs so that they stare straight up through the wire glass, catching fat drops of condensate straight from the trusses. Now and then, one draped beneath a leaky aqueduct will swell too full, tip its flank with glacial composure, and spill a long, shining sheet of excess water down into the alley below—indistinguishable from any other spout in the rain-soaked roar.

For all their slowness, they are not idle. Watch the precinct over the course of a long afternoon, and you begin to understand why this drowned, rusted clockwork has neither burst its pipes nor choked to death on its own lushness in the generations since it was left alone.

When a storm-drain in a back lane begins to clog with shed umbrella-ferns and lime-sludge, the runoff slows and the crisp standing ripples around the grate blur into a dull, dead smear. To the creatures of the basin, that dead spot is an itch, a sour note in the ground’s steady chord. Over the next hour, two or three of the hunched walkers will drift out of the surrounding alleys, drawn by the distortion in their crown-pools. They gather around the flooded grate, sink down on their splayed bases until their lower hides press into the warm water, and reach blunt, bristle-rimmed pads into the muck. Without hurry, taking half an afternoon over the work, they tear the heavy metallic fronds loose, folding the iron-rich pulp directly into the damp moss-seams of their own flanks to digest over the coming weeks, until the grate clears, the water resumes its sluggish pull, and the sharp geometric grid snaps back across the flagstones.

They do the same with the machinery—not as obedient caretakers following some forgotten duty, nor out of reverence, but with the unconscious, stubborn precision of a sleeper smoothing a twisted sheet. When a brass packing leaks too fast and throws off the patient quarter-hour rhythm of a steam valve, a tall, verdigris-crusted walker will wedge its broad back against the opposing brick wall, press its soft metallic pads around the iron wheel, and lean its entire water-heavy bulk into a slow, creaking quarter-turn. When a porcelain insulator grows furred with too much damp moss and starts bleeding voltage erratically into the brickwork, one of the overhead drapers will haul itself along the sagging cables, scrape the excess growth cleanly away with the sharp calcified rim of its flank, and then rest its copper-gritted pads against the bare warm ceramic for hours—drinking the stray current straight from the line until the damp fuzz along its shoulders bristles with static and the water in its back-basin hums into tight, razor-fine hexagons.

Their equilibrium and the ward’s equilibrium are the same thing. The machines keep the water warm, stirred, and charged; the residents keep the water flowing and the charge even.

Sometimes, beneath the dim orange-brown glow of a streetlamp where two alleys cross, you will witness two of the walkers meet.

They do not acknowledge each other from a distance. They simply halt face to face in two inches of trembling water, separated by less than a foot, and begin to lean forward. It takes several minutes. Their heavy, pleated midriffs compress; their anchoring bases widen against the slick cobbles for balance; and at last the bone-pale calcified rims of their crown-basins touch with a faint, stony *clink*, barely audible beneath the drumming rain from the cooling racks.

Where the two lips meet, surface tension breaks. Water bridges the narrow seam of lime, and for an hour—sometimes for half the slow drift of an unmarked dial—their two separate ponds become a single shining figure-eight.

No sound passes between them, yet the whole sunken town moves across that narrow bridge of water. Ripples born in one basin cross into the other and return altered; slight differences in internal voltage equalize in faint, silent currents through the brine, occasionally raising a tiny spark of pale blue light along their damp, bristled fringes that dies instantly in the mist. In that quiet interference of waves, whatever one knows of the eastern transformer banks, the falling level in a south cistern, or a hairline crack weeping hot oil in a boiler shed settles into the other. It is greeting, memory, tuning, and mating all at once.

Days or weeks after such a meeting, pale, translucent sacs begin to swell along the damp pleats beneath a walker’s lower ribs—nearly identical at first to the condensate bladders hanging from the overhead steam pipes, except that hair-fine filaments of drawn copper already branch through their jellied skins. When they grow heavy enough, they tear free with a dull wet splash and drop into the runoff.

You can find these young ones if you look closely in the quiet eddies behind worn kerbstones or tucked inside the recessed brick doorways where the current slows. They look like ribbed, fist-sized sponges or waterlogged puffballs, rolling a few inches an hour in the sluggish drain-flow, trembling faintly in time with the submerged turbines. Most stay low for years, absorbing dissolved iron and lime from the warm streets until a pale crust forms along their undersides and stubby, suctioning pads bud from their bellies. Only when they have grown heavy enough to resist the runoff do they press themselves against the damp brickwork, gripping the sagging cable conduits and low pipe flanges to haul themselves, millimeter by millimeter over the seasons, upright into the dim light.

And when one grows very old—after decades, perhaps centuries, of drinking mineral-heavy runoff and scraping lime-crusts from leaking joints—its shell thickens past the point where internal pressure can bend it. The pleats along its bellows stiffen with pale travertine; turquoise copper salt seals its joints into permanent ridges. It does not panic, and it does not wither. Before the last flexibility leaves its trunks, an elder chooses a place where the basin’s hum runs cleanest—a quiet landing on a spiral iron stair, the crook beneath a transformer balcony, the flooded corner beside a roaring cistern riser—and settles down to stay.

Over the next several years, its splayed base calcifies permanently to the flagstones or iron grating. The pale climbing shoots wrapped around its forelimbs grow outward, creeping across the wet brick to splice themselves directly into the nearest insulated cables and hanging vines. Its shoulders broaden and slump into the masonry until it looks like nothing more than a strange, bulbous stanchion or a mossy decorative font built into the wall.

Yet it is not dead. Look down from a catwalk into the wide, thickened stone dish at the top of one of these settled elders, and you will see the water inside still brim-full, clear as glass, and etched with the crispest, steadier lattice in the whole precinct. Long after they lose the ability to walk the lanes, they remain as living tuning-forks bolted to the walls—holding the deep acoustic memory of the ward, steadying the stray currents in the brick, and offering their broad, shimmering rims to younger walkers who pause beside them in the dim amber gloom to lean in and touch water.

If you stay in the basin long enough, standing quite still on the central footbridge with your hands resting on the sponge-moss padding the iron rail, you realize at last that you have not gone unnoticed.

At first, when you splashed through the alleys below, you moved far too quickly for them to grasp—a brief, clumsy disruption, no different from a ruptured moss-bladder spilling into a gutter. By the time the nearest walker had begun to tilt its crown toward the disturbance, you were already two lanes away and climbing the iron stairs.

Standing still changes that. Your boots press steadily into the vibrating catwalk; your warm, erratic pulse throws a tiny, irregular flutter into the iron rail beneath your palms; the slow warmth of your body alters the damp air rising toward the skylights.

Down in the flooded street below the bridge, the dim orange-brown globes smear long reflections across the trembling water. Beneath the nearest archway, a pale-rimmed crown tilts a fraction of an inch upward. On the opposite brick corner, the silver lattice inside a calcified elder’s stone basin shifts its geometry, tightening around a faint, unfamiliar tremor. And from the mouth of the west alley, sliding over the flagstones with glacial, unhurried grace, one of the broad, olive-and-lime walkers emerges into the open lane and stops directly beneath your span.

There is no hostility in it, and no curiosity as a creature with eyes or a mind would know it. Only the basin’s deep, patient intolerance for anything out of tune. If you remain there—leaning on the rail through another hiss of the quarter-hour valve, letting the warm mist bead on your coat and the fifty-cycle bass rattle softly in your ribs—the heavy shape below will slowly raise one ribbed, copper-gritted forelimb, press its wet, spongy pad flush against the iron pier supporting your bridge, and hold it there, waiting with inexhaustible patience for your frantic, ragged rhythm to quiet down and settle into the hum.
