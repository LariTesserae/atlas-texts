If you stand on one of the wide heartwood avenues long enough—long enough for your eyes to stop squinting against the unsoftened light, and for your bones to settle into the low vibration coming up through the turf—you begin to notice small things the machines could not have left behind.

Beside an ordinary expansion joint in the wooden paving sits a neat crescent of dark, papery lichen, sliced cleanly off at the curve, still faintly damp along its cut edge. Further down, tucked out of the wind beneath the flared root-buttress of a transformer-bole, someone has stacked six hexagonal plates of stamped leaf-felt and weighted them down with a lump of pale, half-cured amber. And if you catch the flicker of movement three hundred yards up the boulevard—not a bird, not a grazing beast, just a thin vertical shape detaching itself from the glare beside a louvered dynamo mast—you realize the city is not abandoned at all. You have simply been looking along the straight lines, and listening to the first sound instead of the second.

They are taller than you expect, and narrow through the shoulders, with deep, barrel-curved ribcages built to drink the thin high-altitude air without hurrying. Even when one stands completely still on a dead-flat slab of heartwood paving, their posture carries a faint, permanent cant: one hip set a fraction higher, knees subtly angled, weight shifted forward onto the balls of their feet as though bracing against a steep scree-slope that wore away ten million years ago. Their skin has the cool, matte texture of cured inner bark, pale grey-olive and dry in the wind, though if they grip a wooden rail hard enough to crease a palm, a clear bead of cold water wells up along the fold and withdraws the instant they let go. Their eyes are wide-set and pale, the color of bleached resin, equipped with a second translucent lid that flicks sideways whenever a mist-bank brushes the overhead vascular cables and sets them snapping with static.

They dress in long, stiffly tailored coats assembled from the hexagonal felt plates stamped out by the reciprocating presses—overlapping down their backs and thighs like dull green slate, sewn together with dark, copper-rich vascular twine that sheds condensation cleanly. Strapped neatly beneath each heel is a small, curved blade of hardened thorn-wood. When one walks across an open avenue in bright sun, you hear a faint, rhythmic *snick-snick* behind every stride: the blades slicing their creeping shadow-mat cleanly free of the paving slabs before the dark lichen can thicken, take root in the wood seams, and drag at their ankles.

You rarely hear them speak to one another directly. In the continuous roar of wind-dynamos and sap-pumps, a human voice pitched across a forty-yard boulevard is torn to shreds in ten paces. Instead, they wear hollow resonators—curved shells of polished pale burl, some grown directly onto wooden clasps, some fitted snug behind the jaw and along the collarbone—to sift the sub-bass thrum from the cleaner air above it. Watch two of them confer across a switching-yard, and you will see neither turn toward the other. The first tilts their chin toward the empty northern sky and throws out a sharp, dry volley of clicks and whistled syllables. Four seconds later, the vanished granite cliff face bounces the phrase straight back—crisp, amplified, stripped of the turbine-din—landing cleanly at the listener’s ear as though whispered from an arm’s length away. Every family, every work-crew, knows the exact angle of the invisible crags above its own district; an outsider standing between them hears only meaningless bird-like cries scattered into the wind, followed by an echo that seems to come from nowhere.

They do not sleep inside the great xylem factory halls, where the machinery never rests and the air smells sharply of hot sap, nor do they camp exposed on the wind-scoured boulevards. They live in the *hushes*.

Because the eroded mountain range left behind its entire acoustic skeleton—valleys, chimneys, overhanging ridges—there are scattered pockets across the open turf where the reflected roar of the city collides with itself and cancels out. From fifty yards away, a hush looks like any other bare patch of olive cushion-plant half a mile from a refinery. Step across the faint boundary ring of crushed white resin-char that marks its edge, and the shrieking wind-dynamos drop instantly to a soft, submerged murmur, as if heavy granite walls had closed smoothly around your ears. Inside those invisible rooms sit their settlements: low, domed lodges ribbed with bent ironwood and shingled in thick layers of leaf-felt, sunk four or five feet down into the living turf directly above the shallower subterranean sap-mains so the warm mineral current heats their floors from beneath. Children play here without raising their voices, rolling dry char-pebbles toward the edge of the hush to test how close they can get before the clatter suddenly catches an echo and flies back at them from the sky.

They do not pretend to own the city, nor do they try to steer it. Whatever purpose originally set the root-turbines spinning and gridded the tableland has drifted too far past legibility to mend. Instead, they live as deft, patient gleaners along the fractures in its machinery—harvesters of the plateau’s slipped rules.

Go to the unfenced yards around the reciprocating presses at mid-morning, and you may see a young gatherer at work beside a towering ironwood stamp. They stand loose-shouldered, eyes half-closed, a long hooked pole of cured burl balanced lightly in both palms. The thunderous boom of the impact strikes their ribs—and in the razor-thin half-second *before* the massive counterweight actually drops, their arms flash forward. The hook darts onto the anvil, snatches out the pristine hexagonal plate—or lays a strip of their own braided leather across the die to be pressed waterproof—and clears the jaws with a hair’s breadth to spare just as the block slams down in silence. To an outsider it looks like sleight of hand performed inside a guillotine, yet their breathing never quickens. Born into a world where effect often arrives a heartbeat ahead of cause, they inhabit that half-second lag as comfortably as a hallway.

Down by the windowless kiln-sheds, where the tall chimneys draw cold grey smoke steadily downward out of the clear blue air, older gatherers wait beside the far sorting trestles with sealed tuber-flasks. When the wooden hopper-cars rattle back out into the daylight, no longer heaped with pale resin blocks but mounded with raw, sopping bundles of unpressed moss, they scoop it gently off the top layers before the conveyors haul it away. This is *back-moss*—tissue that has already been compressed, dried, and burned, and wound backward through the kiln into damp green youth. Packed against a split shin or a bruised joint, it does not merely soothe; it unravels the damage, knitting torn fiber cleanly in a single afternoon. Rubbed along a splintered ironwood tool-handle, it coaxes the ragged grain back into smooth heartwood. Still, they handle it with strict caution, timing every poultice by the creep of the sun. Leave back-moss strapped to living skin overnight, and you may wake with an old scar vanished—along with the quiet, useful memory of whatever taught you to avoid it.

Even the scarce animal life of the plateau is woven into their narrow economy. Every traveler carries a tiny, perforated burl-box tucked inside their felt coat, housing two or three of the glossy-black beetles pried from the warm seams of the transformer-boles. Fed on drops of sweet mineral sap, the beetles stay warm against the ribs and hum in exact sympathetic pitch with the nearest high-voltage trunk. If the box suddenly goes silent and cold against a walker’s chest, they drop flat to the paving slabs without looking up—knowing that a copper-sap cable overhead is about to discharge, or a steam-vent half a mile east is seconds from blowing its crown.

As for the pale, long-legged grazers that crop the green seams between flagstones, no one wastes time chasing them across the open intersections. You cannot corner a creature that steps calmly behind a narrow signal-post and does not emerge on the other side. Instead, the herders have spent generations mapping which posts are paired across the tableland. A youth walks unhurriedly toward a grazing buck in the southern distribution yard, gently crowding it toward a marked wooden post smeared with resin-salt—while three miles away, in an empty switching-lane to the northwest, their cousin waits quietly with a shallow trough of warm sap-water and a pair of horn-shears, catching the beast as it steps out from behind a pylon far too thin to conceal its shoulders. From its shed, hollow horns they carve the clearest ear-cups; from its wet-birch fleece they spin the only soft thread on the plateau.

When they travel between districts, they rarely walk the full, punishing length of the boulevards. They carry folded maps inked in dark char-stain on the translucent, pressed wings of the rare white moths scooped from the condensation gutters at the base of the cloud-combs. These maps show no miles and no compass roses; they are charts of *expansion joints*. Every few seasons the folds drift slightly, and surveyors have to re-pace the avenues, marking the helpful seams with tiny notches cut into the pale burl curbs. A skilled walker sets out along a three-mile causeway, counts fourteen humming pylons, steps deliberately with their left heel onto a notched wooden seam—and feels that brief, stomach-dropping dip of air pressure as the distant refinery district snaps straight into looming presence overhead.

Because the daylight turns their own shadows into real, creeping tissue, they have learned to read and keep them as well. If two families settle a trade, or a surveyor marks the drift of an important seam, or a traveler wishes to leave word at a lonely intersection, they unstrap their heel-blades and stand motionless in the harsh noon sun. For twenty minutes they hold a deliberate posture—arms folded, a hand raised with two fingers extended, a staff slanted toward a particular district—while the dark photosynthetic mat thickens beneath them and grips the wood. The instant a stray wisp of cloud passes across the sun, the shadow curls sharply at its rim with a dry rustle; they step neatly free, peel the dark silhouette up whole before it can vanish, and press it flat between boards of pale amber. Around certain quiet crossroads you can find low, tidy stacks of these dried black silhouettes anchored beneath resin weights—a mute archive of who passed, what they carried, and which way they faced when the sun stood high.

Stranger still is their traffic with the horizon. Because the saucer-tilted plateau cannot agree on its own calendar, the duplicated districts—such as the triad of swollen fermentation domes standing both northwest and southeast, one a shade paler with its cooling-louvers angled three degrees further open—are treated less as illusions than as neighboring shift-rooms separated by twelve miles of turf. When a main sap-conduit ruptures in the northwestern complex, flooding a storage yard or shattering a vital wooden impeller, a runner folds across the seams toward the southeastern twin, where the louvers still stand three degrees wider and the failure has not yet occurred. They do not enter the work-yard itself; custom forbids meeting one’s own earlier footsteps, which leaves both versions dizzy and half-deaf for days. Instead, the runner scratches a terse warning into a slab of soft resin, leaves it balanced on the outer curb where the earlier shift will find it, or fetches an intact replacement gear back across the upland.

Yet no one makes that crossing lightly, and the elders keep strict count of who has gone. The plateau collects a quiet tax of erosion from anyone who borrows against its doubled days. A runner who crosses too often begins to bleach, their bark-grey skin fading to the color of dry lichen-ash; their hair thins into brittle white filaments; small, ordinary nouns wear smooth and slip out of their vocabulary, until they stand facing the northern sky, shaping their mouth around a whistle that refuses to carry far enough to echo back.

All of this happens in the margins of the glaring day, small and scattered across twenty miles of roaring emptiness. To see them in their full numbers, you have to wait for the weather-front.

When the broad, low belly of bright grey cloud drags its underside onto the tableland, and the horizon ruffles with the vast, dry clatter of ten thousand wooden louvers cranking open, the plateau changes hands. Visibility collapses to fifty yards; the vascular cables hum a full tone higher, spitting sharp blue sparks that smell of ozone and crushed pine needles. With the fierce sun blotted out, the shadow-mats curl away to nothing. Across miles of open turf, hatch-lids lift in the quiet hushes, and figures pour out onto the wet heartwood avenues by the hundreds.

Unburdened by their heel-blades, coats buttoned high against the cold mist, they form loose columns along the broad empty boulevards and begin to walk in brisk, locked unison. These are the crowds you hear tramping invisibly through the whiteout. They do not march out of discipline or habit; they are pressing the city. Hundreds of flat-soled boots striking the wet planks together drive a steady, rhythmic compression straight down through the paving into the living sponge of root-felt beneath, answering the deep sub-bass of the root-turbines. Somewhere miles below, the cadence coaxes the subterranean trunks and eases the tension along the expansion joints, allowing whole households—laden with sledges of back-moss, bundles of hexagonal felt, and cages of humming beetles—to fold smoothly across fifteen miles of tableland before the mist lifts.

And while the columns move along the avenues, a smaller, quieter company turns away from the paved roads altogether, stepping out onto the sopping open meadows where the fog is darkest.

These are the high-gleaners. They wear light, close-laced harnesses of braided copper-vine, carry empty felt satchels slung high between their shoulder blades, and strap jagged spurs of hardened ironwood to their shins and forearms. They move fast, heads tilted up, waiting for that brief loosening of the world when the vertical ribs of the vanished mountains darken the mist a hundred yards ahead.

Where flat olive turf ought to stretch unbroken to the horizon, a sheer flank of wet, phantom granite briefly thickens inside the whiteout—steep, ribbed, impossible, holding just enough weight to take an edge. Without hesitation, the high-gleaners step off the living ground and begin to climb.

They ascend straight upward into empty fog, kicking ironwood spurs into ledges that eroded away before the first root-mat ever knit this plateau together. Fifty feet up, eighty feet up, vanished completely from anyone standing below on the turf, they work with ferocious, silent speed. They scrape crusts of bitter mineral salt and rare frost-lichen from invisible crevices; they wedge short pry-bars of thorn-wood into cold, half-real fissures to break loose the only commodity the immense botanical city can never manufacture: true stone. If you stand near the edge of a switching-yard during a thick front, you may hear a faint, bright *tink-tink-tink* ringing down out of the empty grey sky—the crisp strike of wood on quartz, utterly foreign to a world made entirely of fiber and sap.

It is the most dangerous work on the tableland. A climber must keep one ear tuned constantly to the singing power-lines below and the shifting pressure against their ribs. They have only minutes. When the wind begins to tear the underside of the cloud apart, the pitch of the vascular cables drops by a half-step, and the climbers drop everything—sliding, scrambling, kicking recklessly down the dissolving granite as its weight thins beneath their boots into cool wet air. Most spill breathless onto the damp turf seconds before the sun slams back down onto the open plain, clutching a cold, heavy splinter of grey granite or pale quartz inside their satchels—dead, unyielding matter, prized beyond amber, to be hung as true plumb-bobs inside their lodges or ground slowly into whetstones for their thorn-wood blades. Those who misjudge the wind by ten seconds leave no trace on the paving below, save sometimes a small, dry clatter that echoes down from the clear blue sky four seconds after the sky has emptied.

The cloud tears past. The louvers clatter shut in a rolling wave across the horizon, and the boulevards stand bare, glistening, and immaculate once more, as though no foot had touched them in a century.

Yet even in the fiercest noon sun, the oldest of the inhabitants never leave the avenues at all.

Walk closely along the base of a humming transformer-bole, or pause beside the ribbed xylem buttress of a refinery hall where the living wall thickens by fractions of a millimeter each decade, and you may notice a shape you first took for a weathered burl or a cluster of swallowed thorn-pegs. Look closer, past the slow orange lichen creeping over the curves, and you see the clean, symmetrical arch of a narrow ribcage rising once a minute; the dry, folded hands resting palms-down against the paving; the pale, half-lidded eyes tracking the slow drift of a cloud-bank along the tilted horizon.

There is no dirt here to dig a grave, and no stone to pile above the dead. When a dweller grows too stiff with mineral sap to time the half-second lag of the presses, when their knees can no longer catch the cant of the vanished slopes and their shadow grows too stubborn to shear cleanly at the heel, they walk out to a quiet intersection or a warm substation wall, sit down in the full light of midday, and unstrap their heel-blades for the last time.

Over a few bright seasons, their dark shadow-mat roots deeply into the heartwood seams and holds them fast. The fibrous cushion-turf creeps calmly over their ankles; the thickening xylem buttress folds gently around their shoulder blades, gripping them the way it grips its own ironwood pegs. They linger there for years, perhaps decades, fed faintly by the cool moisture welling through the sponge beneath them and warmed by the copper-sap current thrumming behind their spines. Their polished burl ear-cups remain angled cleanly toward the empty northern sky.

When the deep root-turbines miles beneath the turf shift their hum by a minor third—marking some unreadable quota met, or some ancient shift changed—the rooted elders feel it first. If a younger seam-walker kneels beside the buttress and presses an open palm against the living wood, a slow, dry tapping answers them straight through the grain: telling them which joint has stretched overnight, which kiln is drawing cold, and where, out past the bright, roaring emptiness of the city, the next belly of grey cloud is already gathering along the saucer-rim of the world.
