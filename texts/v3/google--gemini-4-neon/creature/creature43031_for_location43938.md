You could walk half a circuit of the perimeter, feeling your way along the waist-high parapets and stepping over the wet curb-runnels, and remain certain that nothing here has a mind. The hollow is too big, too loud, and too thoroughly occupied by its own machinery to feel inhabited. Even the wild things keep to the seams.

It is only when you stop moving—when you lean your weight against a cantilevered rail to catch your breath in the warm dark—that the first small wrongnesses begin to register.

Your palm rests on a stretch of composite parapet where the mineral crust should be thick as a thumb, yet the surface beneath your fingers is rubbed clean and satin-smooth, faintly oily. Under the lip of the rail, out of reach of the blowing grit, your fingertips find a row of narrow, deliberate notches filed into the stone at uneven intervals. A few paces further along, the worn tactile striping at the threshold of a recessed transit bay has been worn *differently*—not across its whole width by vanished crowds, but down to two narrow, pale tracks right at the inner edge, where bare heels have turned into the dark doorway thousands of times.

And then, through the soles of your boots and the bones of your wrist, you feel a sound that does not belong to the floor-plates or the wind.

*Tock. Tock-shhh. Tock.*

It does not come through the air. If you lift your hand from the rail, it vanishes completely beneath the steady mechanical roar. Press your palm back to the cantilevered slab—that impossible, pillarless shelf held rigid by invisible internal tension—and there it is again: a crisp, ringing vibration traveling out of the wall from somewhere three tiers up, clean as a plucked wire.

A moment later, from a gallery far across the northern void, another tension-slab answers.

---

They are human, or as close to human as enough centuries without sky, fire, or quiet will leave anyone. Nobody among them remembers whether their first ancestors built the precinct, serviced its buried works, or simply climbed down an access stair like yours when whatever lies above the bedrock stopped being livable. To them, the question makes no more sense than asking how the floor-plates learned to be hot. The precinct was already ancient, already scarred with healed glassy ridges, before the oldest cadence in their memory began.

If you ever glimpse one—and you will only ever see them in fragments, passing across the cherry haze leaking from a sunken floor-grille or silhouetted for half a second against a hairline of dull violet along a cracked cornice—your first impression is of stillness and economy. They are lean, narrow-hipped, with broad, toughened feet and long fingers whose pads are calloused thick as horn. Their skin is the pale, matte gray-ochre of lime-dust and dry stone, lightly greased with rendered grazer-fat to keep the rising heat from cracking it and the static along the cantilevers from raising dry sores. Their hair is cropped close to the skull or plastered flat with the same pale oil; loose hair catches the static charge near the tension-rails and whips painfully across the face when the louvers open.

They are not wholly blind, not like the gliders. Beneath heavy, grit-lashed lids, their eyes are pale and wide-pupiled, kept half-shut most of the time against blowing flakes of travertine. They do not use sight to read a face or judge a step; in this dark, trying to look for ledges is a fast way to die. Instead, they keep their eyes for the precinct’s few accidental leaks of light—using the dull violet cornice-crack to the west, the sunken cherry grille below, and the blue static-snap along the eastern conduit as fixed compass points, the way sailors once used three dim stars through cloud.

Everything else they see through bone.

Almost every adult wears fitted plugs of waxed soot-moss and smooth, ground lime-stone twisted into their ear canals—loosened slightly during the quiet baseline if they want to catch the thin airborne ring of the floating squares, pushed deep and tight the moment the pressure begins to build behind the high louvers. Without them, a lifetime in the precinct would leave you deaf before your twentieth winter—if they had winters. With the chaotic air-roar damped down, the body becomes an instrument. They walk barefoot or in thin-soled wraps so their heels can read the sub-bass pulse of the buried works, feeling where the floor-plates run hottest or where a hidden seam leaks vibration.

And every one of them carries a *rod*—a finger-length baton of dense, fused dark ceramic salvaged from old broken rail-fasteners, worn on a cord of twisted tendril-fiber around the neck. When they want to listen deeply, they put one end of the ceramic rod between their back teeth and touch the other to a wall, a curb, or a cantilevered rail. Through the jaw and skull, the roar of wind disappears entirely, and the precinct opens up around them with terrifying clarity. They can feel the slow, heavy scrape of a fist-sized grazer working a wet gutter six bays away. They can feel the subtle, dry shift of a flat-bodied alcove hunter uncoiling inside a dark doorway three minutes before the wind-surge hits. They can tell, from the pitch of a cantilevered walkway, whether the person walking out onto its tip is a child, a laden water-carrier, or a stranger wearing hard-soled boots.

---

Because the air belongs to the vents, they have almost forgotten how to speak through it. Shouting is exhausting, imprecise, and useless for six minutes out of every forty.

Instead, they have two languages: one for distance, and one for the dark between two bodies.

At a distance, they speak through the architecture itself. The cantilevered walkways jutting thirty and forty feet into the void are held taut by invisible internal tension; struck properly, each one rings into the perimeter wall like a massive stone tuning-fork. Over generations, the inhabitants have filed small, tuned notches along the undersides of the parapets and learned which ceramic seams carry vibrations cleanly from Tier Four down to Tier Ten. Using their ceramic rods or smooth knuckles of calcified lime-crust tied to their wrists, they send patterns of sharp taps, damped chords, and scraped harmonics through the stone.

To an outsider, it is indistinguishable from the precinct’s own settling—a faint tick, a dry rasp, swallowed at once by the ambient roar. To those whose bare heels or fingertips rest against the walls, it is a constant, quiet neighborhood murmur running around the hollow: *The northern drip-curtain shifted two bays east. The fourth mezzanine is clear of hunters. A floor-plate near the west corner cracked dry and is hissing. Come eat.*

When they are close—huddled inside a shuttered transit bay while the louvers roar outside—they use touch-speech. Two people sit knee to knee in the pitch dark, pressing forehead to forehead, or temple to cheekbone, or one laying an open palm gently against the other’s throat and collarbone. Then they hum low, in a narrow, gravelly middle register that threads cleanly through the acoustic gap between the floor’s sub-bass thrum and the thin high ring of the floating squares.

Words pass straight from sternum to palm, from jaw to skull. You cannot eavesdrop on it from three feet away. You cannot easily lie in it, either, because your listener feels the pace of your heartbeat, the tightness in your throat, and the small catch of breath before a hard sentence. Children learn to recognize their mothers not by face or voice, but by the distinct, warm buzz of a familiar breastbone pressed against their ear while the precinct breathes around them.

---

There are perhaps three or four hundred of them in all, never crowded, spread thinly across the twelve tiers of the perimeter walls—and out in a stranger place beyond them.

On the walls, they keep mostly to the middle tiers, Four through Nine, where the floor’s dry fever has mellowed into a steady, bearable warmth and the ceiling’s cold seepage has not turned every ledge slick. They make their homes inside the recessed transit bays and shuttered civic alcoves—favoring the few whose ancient composite shutters can still be coaxed partway closed in their corroded tracks, or building low, curved windbreaks across open thresholds by packing wet mineral silt and crushed travertine crust into place until the rising dry heat bakes it hard as mortar.

Inside these dry pockets, the roaring draft drops to a heavy, muffled hum. There are no lamps and no fires—there is nothing to burn, and no need for either. A household looks sparse to an outside eye: thick pallets woven from dried soot-moss and stuffed with cured glider-down; glazed ceramic bowls ground smooth from old floor-shards; hanging bundles of pale ochre-stalks and strips of salted grazer-flesh suspended near the warm outer lip of the doorway where the rising air cures them slowly without rot; loops of braided tendril-cord coiled neatly over stone pegs.

Everything they do is governed by the *breath*—the forty-minute cycle of the high northern and eastern louvers. They have no days or nights, no weeks or years; an infant is counted in hundreds of breaths until it can walk, and older spans of time are measured by the rare, massive groans of the buried works below, when the deep load shifts and the floating squares drop half an inch for a season before rising again.

During the thirty-four minutes of steady baseline, the walls come quietly alive.

Small parties move along the damp perimeter gutters, scraping soot-colored velvet moss into woven slings—never taking all of a patch, always leaving a strip rooted along the vibrating seam so the stone’s drone will feed it back to thickness within thirty breaths. Others pry matte-gray grazers off the ceramic before the creatures can clamp down with their muscular feet, sliding a flat sliver of scavenged conduit-metal under the pitted rim. The meat is dense, rubbery, and intensely mineral; eaten raw or heat-dried with chewed moss, it is the backbone of their diet, and the pale fat rendered from it in shallow hot-stone pans oils their skin, their moss ear-plugs, and their cords.

Beneath the cantilevered walkways, the boldest harvesters—usually young, light-boned, and fearless of the drop—knot braided tendril-ropes around their waists, anchor themselves to a parapet stanchion, and lower themselves over the hand-thick edge into the black void. Hanging upside down in the rising warm air, their hair prickling with static, they hook the dangling skeins of translucent, rootless tendrils right after a wind-surge, when the humidity peaks and the filaments swell into pale amber beads. They pinch the beads carefully into cured glider-bladders. The liquid inside is faintly sweet, metallic, and clean—prized above the salty, steaming water of the floor-basins.

A few, the *floor-walkers*, descend all the way to the sunken heat-plates during the baseline lull. Before stepping off the lowest stair, they bind thick pads of soaked soot-moss around their soles and ankles; without them, a single step onto an exposed bare plate will blister skin to the bone, and a stray overhead drop landing beside you snaps into scalding steam with a *hiss-crack* loud enough to make you flinch. Down there, bathed in sweat and the faint cherry glow of the sunken grille, they scrape pale salt-crust from the rims of the shallow steaming pools and harvest the waxy, bleached-ochre stalks that grow around them. They know the exact minute before the vents open, when the stalks’ bulbous bases finish drinking the lingering mist and clamp their pores shut; cut cleanly right at that moment, a stalk stays swollen with trapped, potable moisture for dozens of breaths.

Then, through the stone beneath their feet, comes the warning: a faint, tight tremor creeping down from the northern upper tiers as pressure builds behind the recessed louvers.

Across twelve tiers, the rail-taps quicken and fall silent. Harvesters haul themselves back over the cantilevers. Floor-walkers climb rapidly off the hot plates. Households pull their shutters narrow or crouch behind their baked-silt windbreaks, tying damp strips of moss-cloth over nose and mouth and twisting their ear-plugs home.

When the louvers groan wide and the five-minute gale spirals down through the hollow—whipping the drip-curtains into horizontal mist and tearing flakes of dry lime off the upper arches—the wall-folk do not sleep. They sit with their backs to the warm interior ceramic, one bare palm pressed to the floor, a short stabbing-pin ground from old rail-bolts held ready across their knees.

Because the wind-surge belongs to the alcove hunters.

Those flat-bodied, long-limbed things with ear-plates fused to their skulls wait out the quiet baseline pressed motionless in abandoned doorways, and uncoil only when the deafening rush of air masks every scrape of claw on wet stone. The wall-folk cannot hear them coming during a surge, not through air. Instead, they hang loose chains of thin, brittle lime-flakes and tiny ceramic shards across the mouths of their recessed bays, tied by single hairs of tendril-fiber to a peg beside the sleeper’s wrist. The wind rattles the chains in a steady, familiar patter; a hunter brushing past catches the cord with a sharp, dead tug that does not match the gust. More than a few elders wear three pale, parallel scars across a forearm or ribs from a quiet grapple in the roaring dark, ended only when a ceramic pin found the soft joint beneath an ear-plate.

When the louvers groan shut and the chord drops back to its heavy, warm baseline, a slow ripple of rail-taps travels tier by tier around the perimeter—each household checking in, confirming who is still whole.

---

Yet the perimeter walls are only half of the habitation.

If you stand near the tip of a high northern cantilever just as the warning tremor begins—just before the louvers open—you may hear a faint, dry *creak* behind you, and feel the warm air displace as two or three figures step quietly past you toward the black drop.

They are carrying frames taller than themselves: wide, ribless, paper-thin sheets of oily pale membrane cured from the wings of three or four blind gliders, stitched together with fine sinew and stretched across flexible spars of split, heat-hardened ochre-stalk.

The gliders themselves are caught with patience, never with haste. From the highest cantilevered tips, snaring-crews cast long, weightless lines of tendril-twine tipped with sticky amber-sap and small scraps of warm, freshly killed grazer—letting the rising thermal columns carry the bait out into the void where the blind wings spiral. A caught glider makes no sound at all; you know you have one only when the line snaps taut with a silent, heavy pull against the updraft, and you haul six feet of thrashing, drum-tight parchment back through the dark.

Even dead, oiled, and stitched into a sail, a glider’s membrane remembers how to read the roar.

The *crossers*—there are never more than a dozen in the whole precinct, lean and calm, with broken noses and old bruises along their shins—do not launch blindly. They step to the very lip of the northern cantilever on Tier Eleven, spreading their wide cured wings behind their shoulders. They pull their ear-plugs halfway loose, letting the precinct’s full chord wash over them: the sub-bass floor, the rising groan of the louvers, and out there in the empty black, a hundred yards away and three tiers down, the thin, clean harmonic ring of the suspended hexagonal platforms.

When the northern louvers slam wide and the humid gale pours out over their backs, curling downward into its vast central spiral, the crossers lean forward and drop off the edge into pitch darkness.

You do not steer a glider-wing in this place with your eyes. As the wind catches the cured parchment, the whole membrane goes drum-tight, trembling violently against the crosser’s palms and forearms. Every acoustic reflection in the hollow speaks straight into their hands: the dull, flat roar of the distant walls; the sharp, sudden *absence* of wind-echo as a rising thermal shear passes beneath them; and then, rising out of the dark below, a crisp, bright pressure against the fabric as the acoustic shadow of a floating town square sweeps under their feet. Guided by that vibration in their wrists—and by the clean harmonic hum growing louder until it buzzes in their teeth—they bank through the blinding mist, spill the wind at the last heartbeat, and drop hard onto worn flagstones suspended over nothing.

---

The three dark hexagonal platforms hanging in the empty core are not uninhabited ruins. Each has been folded into the precinct’s quiet life, shaped by its elevation and its heat.

The **highest square**, hanging closest to the cold bedrock ceiling and catching the full brunt of the northern louvers, is too wet and wind-scoured for permanent sleep. Instead, it is a cistern and a landing-deck. Along its worn flagstones, between the ancient low benches, the inhabitants have mortared shallow catch-basins out of lime-paste and stretched wide, weighted mats of soot-moss to drink the whipping mist during every wind-surge. Beneath its dark belly, where cold seepage beads heaviest before falling a hundred feet to the floor, crossers hang in harnesses to harvest the fattest, cleanest amber tendrils in the hollow, packing skin-bladders full before launching off the southern rim on the next breath.

The **lowest square**, hanging deep in the rising dry fever above the floor-plates and brushed on its underside by the faintest upward smear of cherry haze from the sunken grille, belongs to the dead.

In a sealed, warm world with no soil to dig and no wood to burn, you cannot leave a body in the damp wall-gutters to foul the air or draw alcove hunters. So when someone’s heartbeat slows and stops beneath a loved one’s palm, they are wrapped tightly in dry moss-cloth, lashed to the back of a crosser, and carried out on the spiraling wind to the lowest hexagon. Laid gently along the ancient stone benches or propped upright with their backs against the low parapets, they do not rot. The relentless, bone-dry thermal plume rising off the floor-plates beneath them draws every drop of moisture away within fifty breaths. They become light, taut, papery, rimed with a whisper of pale salt—dozens of quiet elders sitting in the faint cherry mist like the vanished citizens who first wore these flagstones smooth, humming imperceptibly in tune with the slab’s invisible field. Only occasionally does a crosser drop down there between burials, stepping softly among the dry benches to unlace a polished ceramic rod from a grandmother’s wrist and bring its familiar weight back to the living.

And between the wet cistern above and the hot quiet dead below hangs the **middle square**—wide as a town plaza, temperate, suspended in the true heart of the roaring void.

Roughly sixty people live out here, and many of them have never touched a perimeter wall in their lives.

They are subtler, stranger cousins to the wall-folk—lighter on their feet, wind-scoured, with an uncanny, effortless balance born of living every moment on a massive stone slab that drifts half an inch upward or downward whenever the deep load beneath the precinct shifts. There are no alcove hunters out here; nothing without wings can cross the empty air. In exchange, there are no shuttered bays either. When the five-minute gale sweeps the open plaza, the middle-square folk retreat into the narrow lee of the ancient stone benches, sliding beneath heavy, weighted awnings stitched from old glider-hides and pegged deep into the flagstone seams.

They do not know the heavy sub-bass heartbeat of the floor the way the wall-folk do; separated from the masonry by vast gulfs of air, their whole world instead vibrates with the thin, clean harmonic ring of the platform holding its mass against gravity. Because of that, their touch-speech sits a fraction higher in the throat—a clear, singing hum rather than the wall-folk’s gravelly rumble. They sleep with a loose loop of tendril-cord knotted around an ankle and pegged to a bench-leg, utterly untroubled by the sheer hundred-foot drop ten feet from their heads. They farm soot-moss in the damp leeward cracks, lower weighted hooks off the edges on long thermal-drifting lines to fish the dark air for passing gliders, and trade cured wing-skins, clean ceiling-water, and braided cordage with the wall-folk whenever a crosser drops out of the storm—or launches back off the windward edge during the dying minute of a gust to catch the rope-nets hung out from the lower southern transit bays.

Because their floating square does not touch the walls, they cannot use rail-speech to call across the void. Yet they are not cut off.

A suspended hexagonal slab held in empty air is, in its own quiet way, a bell. Near the center of the middle square, the residents keep three heavy, rounded drums of dense floor-ceramic resting directly on a central seam. When they have news for the walls—a birth, a death, a shift in the thermal currents, a request for a crosser—three or four of them lift the heavy stones and strike the flagstones in slow, practiced unison.

Out across the roaring dark, the thin harmonic ring of the middle platform shudders. It does not grow louder; it *wobbles*, pulsing in a slow, clean swell—*wah… wah… wah…*—that rides right over the mechanical baseline. Along twelve tiers of perimeter walls, people pause, twist their moss ear-plugs loose for three heartbeats, and listen to the floating square sing.

---

They do not worship the precinct, not with prayers or altars. Worship implies a listener who might answer, or who might be bargained with. They know better. The buried thermal sinks below do not know they are warm; the northern louvers do not breathe for their lungs; the fused ceramic walls do not slowly flow and heal their ancient fractures to keep the moss-harvesters safe.

Yet there is a deep, unsentimental devotion in how carefully they attend to it.

Every few hundred breaths, a pair of the oldest wall-folk—and sometimes an elder lowered down from the middle square—will descend to the broad, feverish floor, wrapping their feet in thick wet moss and walking out across the drifts of mineral silt to the sunken grille leaking its barely-there cherry haze. They kneel right at the rim of the hot metal, lay their palms flat against the sizzling edge until their calloused skin can barely bear it, and press their ceramic rods between their teeth and down onto the grille’s bars.

They are listening to the buried works underneath.

For generations, they have kept a strict, tapped memory—passed from elder to apprentice through forehead-to-forehead humming and struck rhythms on the stone—of the precinct’s exact heartbeat: the spacing of the sub-bass pulse, the pitch of the three floating squares, the length of the forty-minute breath, the weight of the deep load shifts. They cannot fix a single wire or turn a single valve in whatever immense buried system still runs below the floor. They can only bear witness to its stubborn, weathered balance, measuring each generation against the last, listening quietly in the dark to see whether the beat ever slows, or skips, or changes its mind.

---

Stand still by the narrow access stair for another minute, letting the warm, ozone-scented roar press against your ribs.

You have not seen a soul. Across the void, the hairline of dull violet along the far cornice flickers once and holds. Somewhere down on the hot floor, a stray drop lands on bare ceramic and snaps into vapor with a bright *hiss-crack*.

Yet your boots struck the threshold differently from bare, moss-wrapped feet. The rusted access door behind you groaned with a sound that has not entered the walls in longer than any living cadence remembers.

Beneath your hand, if you rest your fingers lightly against the nearest parapet, the stone is no longer quiet. A slow, measured chain of tiny rings—*tock… tock-tock… shhh*—is already moving softly from cantilever to cantilever along Tier Six, down toward the mid-tier bays, up toward the northern ledges. Nobody is rushing toward you. Nobody is shouting in the dark. They are patient people, shaped by a patient room.

High above, behind twelve tiers of shuttered urban stone, the louvers begin their faint warning tremor, building pressure for the next breath. And far out in the roaring black void, just before the wind tears loose, the thin, clean harmonic ring of the middle floating square wavers—just once, slow and deliberate—to let the walls know it has heard.
