You walk past them without knowing it. That is the first thing to understand about whoever still lives in the flats: they do not stand up to meet the sky, because there is nothing in the sky worth meeting, and anything that rises higher than a crouching shoulder is only asking to be sheared off by a sideways squall or folded over when the shingle buckles.

If you trudge through the ochre gloom looking for villages, upright silhouettes, or smoke rising against a horizon, the basin will happily confirm that it is empty. You will find a ring of seven fused cobbles, a low clay mound with a worn floor, a faint spiral beneath the silt, and assume the hands that shaped them dissolved into grease a million years ago.

Then, on the sixth or seventh day, when the wet thunder has beaten your ears numb and you stop to lean your weight against a smooth, domed knob of grey mudstone, you feel it shift half an inch beneath your palm—not slipping with the boiling slurry, not shuddering along a spatial seam, but *bracing* against you. A slow, heavy counter-pressure, warm as a banked hearth.

If you pull your hand back and wait without speaking—speech is useless here anyway, shredded to wet pressure before it clears your lips—the knob of stone uncurls. Not quickly. Nothing that belongs to this floor moves in a hurry. What looked like a slab of river-worn basalt lifts slightly on two broad, flat-heeled legs, shedding curtains of lukewarm brine from its curved back, and you see that the smooth mineral dome is a spine, a pair of wide shoulders, and a low-slung skull tucked tightly between them.

They are built the way the basin permits things to be built: wide at the hip, deep in the barrel of the chest, low to the mud, with no sharp angles left anywhere on them. Even standing as straight as their curved spines allow, the tallest among them barely reaches a human collarbone, yet they carry the dense, settled weight of something twice their height. Their arms are long and heavy-boned, hanging near their knees so that when the ground drops out or tilts into a sudden wall of groaning pebbles, they can drop onto their broad, calloused knuckles and anchor themselves like four-legged boulders.

Their hides tell the story of how long they have endured here. Across the belly, the throat, and the tucked insides of their limbs, the skin is dark, supple leather—the bruised pewter of the cloud-deck—constantly glistening with a faint, bitter oil their pores weep to keep the warm brine from rotting them through. Across the exposed upper planes—the crown of the head, the ridge of the shoulders, the broad sweep of the back, the outer faces of the forearms—that hide has thickened over decades into smooth, fused plates of horn-hard callus, embedded with microscopic grains of local silt and pale streaks of lime. Rain, grit, and sliding clay polish these plates until they gleam like wet soapstone. Sometimes thin ribbons of dark olive biofilm even take root in the shallow creases behind their shoulders, doing no harm, letting the tumbling silt slide cleanly off them just as it slides off bedrock.

They have no hair to tangle in the slurry, no projecting ears to catch the deafening wet blasts. Behind each heavy jaw sits only a tight, muscular slit that can clamp shut before the gunshot slap of a collapsing gravity-dome. Their eyes are buried deep beneath thick, overhanging ridges of bone, veiled by double translucent lids that sweep sideways to clear away grease and grit. Those eyes are wide, milky ochre, nearly blind to distance—there is no distance here to see—but finely tuned to wrongness in the light: the sluggish amber shimmer along the mud that warns of an approaching tear, or the sudden, intolerable flash of clear blue when a puddle forgets which epoch it belongs to.

Mostly, though, they do not look at the world. They feel it through their bones.

Their shins, collarbones, cheekbones, and the broad flats of their palms are dense, mineral-heavy, tuned like sounding-boards. What hits an outsider as an undifferentiated wall of wet grinding noise breaks apart inside their skeletons into a vast, intricate grammar of weight and tremor. Standing ankle-deep in vibrating pebbles, one of them can sort a dozen overlapping pressures at once: the slow, oily heave of a steam-pocket forty paces down; the hollow, bell-like hum of a basalt ridge letting rainwater pass straight through it; the grinding sled-track of a bottom-cruiser wedging east through the strata half a mile off; and, fainter than all of these, the deliberate, weighted *tap-pause-lean* of another of their kind pressing a cobble against a continuous slab of bedrock far out in the mist.

They move through the overcrowded flats with a rolling, low-hipped wade, never lifting a foot wholly free of the mud when the ground is trembling, letting their wide, blunt toes feel for the clay beneath the rolling shingle. They do not fight the basin’s churn; they read its rhythm and slip between its collisions, crouching flat when a curtain of warm brine folds inward, wedging their heels behind stubborn nubs of stone until a squall tears itself to pieces overhead.

They eat whatever the exhausted floor still yields, and they waste nothing. With their flattened, shell-hard thumbnails, they peel thick strips of rubbery olive biofilm from the lee sides of ancient domes, folding the greasy mats into wide, thick-lipped mouths to be chewed slowly between teeth worn as flat as millstones. They drink by tipping their broad faces into the sweeter run-off beneath mist-curtains, or by pressing their mouths to the cool seams where rainwater weeps back out of old stone.

With the sparse animals of the flats, they keep a quiet, unsentimental truce. A living bottom-cruiser is too vast and armored to harm, even if they wanted to; instead, when an inhabitant meets one wedging slowly through a deep basin of slurry, they will sometimes climb onto the creature’s leeward rim, hook their broad fingers into the barnacle-pocked seams of its calcified plates, and ride it for miles, resting their legs while the tectonic sled carries them through the upheaval. In return, they scrape away the thick mineral blisters that sometimes jam the cruiser’s wide siphon-mouth. Only when a bottom-cruiser dies—a slow, weeks-long unhinging as the sliding mud pulls its plates apart—do they gather to carve away its dense, fatty cartilage with sharp-edged broken shells, and haul away its great curved ribs, the only rigid beams in the entire world.

And wherever they crouch for more than a few minutes, the tiny, pale, six-legged things come to find them. Those translucent bone-crabs, forever fleeing the crushing clamor of the flats, sense the pocket of dampened vibration formed beneath a heavy, anchored body. They scuttle out of the clicking pebbles and pack themselves by the dozens into the hollows behind an inhabitant’s knees, beneath their folded wrists, under the shelter of their chin. The people never crush them and never eat them; a crab has scarcely a drop of meat, and its nervous, tickling legs are a better instrument than bone alone. Where the pale things linger without darting away, the ground’s competing tremors have canceled each other out. That is a quiet place. That is where you can build.

Even the lone, leafless canes—dark as wet iron, rooted impossibly deep in the clay—have a place in their days. To an outsider they are only strange stalks bending in the gusts and whistling when the roar dips. To the inhabitants, they are sounding-rods. Because a cane’s roots reach past the worn, frayed skin of the mudflat down into the deep crust where the planet’s rules have not yet thinned out, the stalks do not snap when space bunches like wet cloth. An inhabitant will kneel beside a cane, press a broad cheekbone gently against its wet iron-dark stem, and listen *downward*—reading the slow groan of buried plates miles below, sensing a great regional slump days before the surface clay begins to boil.

***

Yet the rain, the roar, and the shifting mud are not the hardest things they live with. They are made for the mud. What wears them down—what shapes every habit of their lives, every stone they lift, every mark they leave behind—is the crowding underneath.

In a world whose crust has been ground so thin that several versions of the same acre are forever trying to occupy the same space, flesh does not remain exempt. To walk across the flats is to walk through the frayed seams of deep time, and if you do not guard yourself, you begin to fray along with the ground.

It starts subtly. You wade across a stretch of slick clay and feel a sudden, sickening bass hum rise in your shinbone—the same sound a basalt ridge makes when rainwater passes through it—because for half a heartbeat your heel did not strike the stone it was standing on, passing half an inch *through* it instead. You reach into the folds of your oiled hide for a strip of biofilm and your fingers close on warm, dry air. Worse is what happens in the skull: you remember walking east around a seepage-pit yesterday, and at the exact same moment, with the exact same weight of certainty, you remember walking west across dry gravel where no pit existed. Two mornings jostle inside one sternum. Two versions of your own shoulder try to lean against the same wind.

If one of them wanders too long without anchoring, this doubling thickens into a quiet ruin. Their edges blur. They lose track of which breath is the one filling their chest, which hand belongs to the arm currently braced against the mud. In the end, they come unseamed—not cleanly dead, not properly alive, just thinned out across half a dozen overlapping flats, leaving behind nothing more than a brief, wandering patch of wrong pressure that makes a passing kinsman’s teeth ache without knowing why.

That is why they are never truly without stones.

Every inhabitant carries three or four palm-sized cobbles tucked into braided slings of tough olive biofilm belted tight around their wide hips—stones they have carried for years, worn slick against their own thighs, heavy and dense and unmistakably singular. When the hum begins to gather in their knuckles, when their memories start crowding shoulder to shoulder in the dark behind their eyes, they stop. They kneel in the warm slurry, gather enough matching cobbles from the rolling shingle to make seven, and begin to wedge them into a tight, inward-canted ring.

Always seven. Three or four stones can be folded flat by a passing ripple of space; six can split into two competing triangles; eight divides too easily into rival halves. Seven rounded stones, driven tight against one another in a slight inward lean, lock into a single stubborn pressure-arch that has only one geometric way to hold. The inhabitant leans the full weight of their chest and shoulders onto the final stone until it seats with a wet, solid *clack*. Under that sustained press, the damp mineral edges begin to bite and cold-weld together.

At once, the boiling silt around the ring quiets and parts cleanly. For three feet in every direction, the mudflat stops arguing with itself and remembers how to be one piece of ground.

Inside that small, hard-won island, the inhabitant folds their legs, tucks their chin against their sternum, locks their broad palms over their knees, and stays still. They may sit there for an hour; they may sit there through three sluggish cycles of amber lightning. Slowly, the phantom warmth of drier epochs drains out of their joints. The competing memories thin and settle into a single line. Their skin remembers where it stops and where the rain begins. When they rise and wade onward, they leave the fused ring behind—not as a ruin, not as a boundary marker, simply because seven stones that have remembered how to stay solid should not be pried apart. Someone else, forty miles off and twenty years later, may stumble into its three-foot shelter when their own bones start to hum.

***

Because too many bodies gathered in one acre weigh on the frayed crust and tempt the ground to fold, they cannot live in villages. They are sparse—scattered across thousands of miles of identical upheaval, wandering slow, wide circuits measured not by maps, which the mud would erase in a week, but by the enduring slabs of ancient bedrock that still poke up through the grease.

On the flattest, hardest of these slabs, they leave the spirals.

A traveler who scrapes away the silt and traces one of those worn grooves with a fingertip sees only a weathered carving. To the people who make them, a spiral is the only shape that survives a world where straight lines snap. When space bunches like wet cloth, a straight channel kinks into nonsense; a spiral can stretch, bow, or warp and still lead a searching finger reliably inward, turn after turn, asserting one simple, defiant truth: *this came before that; this turn lies inside the next.*

When a wanderer reaches a familiar bedrock slab, they kneel and sweep the warm grease clear with the edge of their hand, then lay their broad fingertips into the faint, water-worn groove. They read it slowly. The spacing between turns tells them how the slab has behaved across generations—which edge tends to slip, where the deep root holds fast. Tiny, deliberate widenings ground into the curve mark who passed here before them, each person leaving their own subtle signature of weight and rhythm: three short scoops and a long drag; a paired hollow at the third turn. Some of those marks are so worn they are barely a sigh beneath the calloused pads of the fingers, left by elders whose names have been outlived by the bedrock.

Then the wanderer takes a hard river-cobble, dips it in wet grit, and begins to grind. For hours they trace the old channel, deepening what the rain has tried to erase, letting the steady, gritty vibration climb up their wrists and settle their thoughts, adding at the spiral’s outer lip a small new turn of their own before moving on. They do not believe stone lasts forever. They have seen mountains chewed flat into soup. They grind the spirals because remembering is not a monument here; it is a muscle you keep flexing, pass after pass, so the flat does not smooth you out before your time.

And yet they are not solitary creatures. Beneath the mineral plates and the oiled hides, beneath the slow, boulder-like patience, they carry a fierce, quiet need to be held in the same *now* by someone else.

When two wanderers feel each other’s stone-taps trembling across a shared bedrock vein, they do not hurry—hurrying splits your footing—but both turn their heavy wades toward the nearest clay dome.

The domes are built only where the pale quiet-crabs linger, over a buried seven-stone ring or a stubborn hump of ancient rock. Over a low cage of sun-bleached bottom-cruiser ribs, generations of hands have packed layer upon layer of sunless, iron-rich clay, shaping a roof no taller than a crouching shoulder, with a single narrow crawl-hole turned away from the prevailing squalls. Time and warm rain slowly slump them; each visitor who uses a dome slaps fresh mud into its thinning flanks before leaving, keeping the shell thick.

Inside, the floor is worn smooth as polished horn. The deafening, shoulder-to-shoulder roar of the flats drops away—never to true silence, which does not exist on this world, but to a heavy, muffled, womb-like thrum that stops rattling your teeth.

Two, sometimes three of them crawl in out of the rain, pull a flat cobble partially across the opening, and wedge themselves shoulder-to-shoulder on the smooth clay. They do not speak into the air. Air is too thin a vessel for what they have to say. Instead, they lean forward until their broad, mineral-dense foreheads meet—or press their deep sternums tight together—and begin to hum.

It is a sound an outsider standing three feet outside the dome would never hear above the squalls, yet inside the bone it is rich as a choir. Deep, slow vibrations rise from the barrel of the chest, shaped by the heavy tongue and wide jaw, passing directly from skull to skull, rib to rib. There is no shouting in rib-speech, and almost no way to disguise yourself; the pace of your heartbeat, the slight tremor of an overworked shoulder, the faint hollow ring of an unhealed ontological blur all travel straight inside the cadence.

First, always, they perform the checking. Broad thumbs trace the smooth planes of the other’s brow and shoulders; palms press flat against palms, leaning weight into weight, humming a low, steady, confirming tone: *You are solid. Your left hand ends where your fingers end. We are in the same rain.* Only after both bodies have settled into a single shared floor do the hums widen into news—where a vast strip of olive biofilm has thickened on a lee dome; where a bottom-cruiser has begun to unhinge in the eastern flats; where a familiar bedrock slab vanished silently into a foaming crater two seasons back; who has not added a turn to their spirals in too many years.

It is inside these domes, too, that children are born. A newborn arrives soft-skulled and unanchored, its edges still wet and thin; if it drew its first breath out in the open mud, a passing fold could peel it sideways into another version of the flat before it ever learned how to cry. So a mother and father—or two kin who have met for the season—pack the dome’s walls double-thick, lock a fresh seven-stone ring beneath the smooth clay floor, and wait out the birth in the muted thrum.

For its first several years, the child scarcely touches the ground at all. It rides clamped tight against an adult’s chest, tucked inside a sling of tough olive film in the warm hollow between collarbone and bent knees, its small skull pressed perpetually against the parent’s humming ribs. That constant, weighted vibration teaches the child’s growing bones what it means to remain one thing. Later, when its legs thicken and its hide begins to shed the rain, the parent kneels beside a slab of bedrock, sets the child’s tiny palm flat against the stone, and lays their own broad, calloused hand over it—holding it there for hours, humming softly into the child’s crown, sorting the planet’s endless roar into pieces a mind can carry: *That is brine bursting from clay. That is rain passing through basalt. That is a cruiser sledding north. That is someone calling from beyond the mist.*

***

There is only one thing in the basin that breaks their heavy calm, and for it they have a distinct note in their rib-hums—a high, thin, breathy vibration that sits uneasily in their deep chests, like a pebble caught under a tongue.

It is the note for the dry-sky domes.

When a puddle pulls itself upward into a trembling, waist-high blister of water, reflecting a clear, bone-dry blue sky the world has not seen in a hundred million years, every animal in the flats flees or clamps down tight. The people do not flee. They stop where they are, deep-set milky eyes widening beneath their brow-ridges, drawn despite themselves toward that impossible glare.

Most of them approach with rigid discipline. They know how treacherous the margins of a gravity-dome are—how the rules fray sharpest around its trembling lip, how its collapse strikes with the force of a boulder dropped from the clouds. Kneeling just outside the shuddering meniscus, one of them will lock their knees into the mud, brace their left hand on a keystone, and plunge their right forearm quickly into the taut curve of water.

When they snatch the arm back before the dome loses its grip and slaps down with a gunshot crack, their wet, oiled skin comes out coated in hot, dry, pale dust—powder from a sun-baked world buried a hundred million years beneath their feet.

They scrape that dust off before the falling rain can turn it instantly to paste, folding every precious grain into a tightly knotted pouch of double-layered olive biofilm tucked beneath their collarbone. In tiny pinches, it is the strangest medicine they possess. Worked into a cracked clay dome before it fully wets through, it drinks the moisture so fiercely that the mud sets almost as hard as baked stone. Pressed onto a deep abrasion where the warm brine refuses to let flesh close, it burns fiercely and seals the wound shut. And when they find the body of a kinsman who died too suddenly in a rock-fold to settle themselves properly, they smear a single pale streak of that dry dust across the dead one’s brow—an offering of the one thing the flats can never make on their own.

Yet not everyone stops at a single forearm.

Now and then, one of them—usually someone who has walked the basin for a very long time, or someone whose kin vanished into a seam without leaving a body behind—stays kneeling beside a gravity-dome a beat too long. They do not just reach in for dust. They lean close and stare through the trembling water into that clear, bone-dry blue.

To a creature born beneath a low, feverish ceiling of ochre cloud, who has never seen an edge further than ten paces away, *distance* is a terrifying narcotic. A sky with no lid on it. Air with no weight. Light that casts a clean, sharp shadow.

Those who look too long—the ones the rib-hums mourn with a slow, descending groan—begin to come loose from the flats. They stop wedging seven-stone rings when their knuckles hum. They forget to drop low when a sideways squall sweeps in, standing awkwardly upright with their broad, flat faces tilted toward the dim belly of the clouds, mouths slightly open, waiting for the ochre ceiling to split and let dry heat pour down. Their memories of the mudflat thin out, replaced by a fierce, hollow hunger for a place they have never stood. Some wander straight into the next trembling dome they find, burying their whole face and shoulders inside the glare, breathing in a searing lungful of ancient dust until the dome breaks over them and the mud boils around their knees. Others simply blur faster and faster, shedding their weight until a kinsman reaches out to steady their shoulder and feels only a faint, dry warmth and a hollow bass ring in the fingers.

***

Most of them, though, do not lose themselves to the blue sky. They stay faithful to the mud.

They live a very long time—measured not in years, for there are no clean seasons under the smothered afternoon of the clouds, but in the slow wearing-down of bedrock spirals and the thickness of the mineral crust across their backs. As an inhabitant grows ancient, the smooth keratin plates over their shoulders and spine grow heavier, thicker, fused ever deeper with lime and fine silt. Their broad knees stiffen; their wade slows to a deliberate, unhurried crawl.

And gradually, inevitably, their singularity grows heavy to hold. Not because they are broken, not because they have forgotten the craft of the seven stones, but because a life lived long enough on an overcrowded floor gathers too many versions of itself. The memories of a hundred circuits, a hundred clay domes, dozens of kin whose ribs once hummed against theirs—all of it begins to press shoulder to shoulder behind their milky eyes, just as the mudflat presses against itself outside.

When an elder feels that the effort of staying one has finally grown heavier than the world around them, they do not crawl into a clay dome to die. A dome belongs to the living; an unseaming body inside those packed walls can leave the clay shivering and sour for generations.

Instead, they wade out onto the open flats one last time. If they have the strength, they seek an old bedrock slab whose spirals they helped deepen in their youth, or a quiet hollow where the pale bone-crabs gather. There, with slow, heavy patience, they unbelt the smooth keystones from their hips, gather enough cobbles from the clicking shingle to make seven, and wedge the ring tight around themselves.

They draw their wide knees up to their barrel chest. They tuck their chin down, fold their broad, flat palms over their shins, and curve their thick, stone-polished back into a low, seamless dome facing the warm rain.

And then they stop holding themselves apart.

It does not happen in an hour. Over days, their slow, deep breaths stretch wider and wider apart until their ribs rise no faster than the sluggish heave of the seepage-pits around them. The warm rain sheets cleanly off their curved, lime-streaked back. The dark olive biofilm, which always clung lightly to the creases of their shoulders, creeps slowly down over their hips and heels, sealing them to the rock. The vibrating pebbles click against their shins, piling in a neat, wet apron around the seven fused stones.

In time, no eye could tell where the elder ends and the ancient bedrock begins.

So when a traveler wanders across this endless, roaring floor—ears ringing, boots sinking into warm slurry, convinced that nothing human or mindful could ever endure this slow unmaking—and pauses to rest a hand upon a soap-smooth, shoulder-high nub of grey stone around which the boiling silt parts cleanly for three feet in every direction, they think they have only found a stubborn lump of the planet’s oldest bone. They do not notice the seven fused cobbles half-buried beneath the grease at its base. They do not know why, when they sit with their back against it for a moment to catch their breath, the wet clamor of the basin softens by a fraction, or why the smooth stone behind their ribs feels faintly, stubbornly warm—holding them steady, just for a little while, in a single version of the world.
