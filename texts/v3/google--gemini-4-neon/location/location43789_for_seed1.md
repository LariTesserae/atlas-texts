The air here flickers. Not like fire, and not like lightning, though there is lightning enough close to the ground—it flickers at a flat, mechanical sixty cycles a second, a dull grey twilight trembling across an open basin so wide and so worn down that the horizon looks less like a boundary than a seam where the sky was imperfectly pressed to the mud.

It is bitterly cold. Everything lies just on the shivering side of freezing, held liquid only by salt, vibration, and the sheer press of living bodies. Beneath the shallow, iron-stained water—ankle-deep across the broad pans, knee-deep in the braided channels—the bedrock has been ground smooth over ages too long to measure. There are no cliffs, no spires, no sharp stones left anywhere in the valley. Whatever mountains once ringed this low plain were chewed down into low, wet swells of black gneiss and oil-slick shale, weeping constant cold seepage into the flats. Even the stones underfoot have been tumbled and abraded until they lie flush with the silt like worn molars.

Nothing green grows. There are no reeds, no moss, no roots gripping the banks. Where the icy water laps against the slick rock shelves, only a thin, rust-colored grease of iron-eating slime clings to the wet line, along with brittle crusts of pale mineral rime that flake away like dead skin whenever the ground shudders.

And the ground is always shuddering.

You hear the basin miles before you understand what you are looking at: a heavy, layered roar that rattles in the jawbone and sits against the sternum like a hand. Beneath the wind and the wet slap of water runs a deep, continuous mains hum—not coming from any buried engine, for if you dig through the freezing silt you find nothing but ancient, featureless stone, yet the stone itself drones as though a turbine the size of a continent were turning inside the mantle. Over that drone rides the percussion: a vast, rhythmic hammering, the screech of bone grinding against wet quartz, the sharp *hiss-clack* of pressurized steam venting by the tens of thousands, and the constant, snapping hiss of blue-white static discharging through the fog.

There is not a wall, a beam, a bolt, or a brick anywhere across the entire expanse. Nothing here was ever built. No hands laid a foundation; no mind drew a plan. Yet the valley is a factory, packed wall-to-wall with no walls to hold it.

Because the plain is completely full.

From one low, fog-drowned rim to the other, the cold shallows are saturated with fauna—pressed flank to flank, shell to shell, loop to loop, leaving barely a hand's breadth of open slush between presences. At first glance in the dim, stuttering light, the valley looks like a single shifting slate-grey floor. Then your eyes adjust to the wet gleam of hides, calcified plates, and pale, hairless meat, and you realize the whole landscape is breathing, cycling, indexing.

Nearest the wide silt-pans stand the bellows-herds: massive, low-slung beasts the color of wet ash, their backs glazed with hoarfrost on the upwind side. They stand shoulder-deep in each other's warmth—though it is a strange, localized warmth, harsh and dry beneath freezing skin. Every four seconds, across half a mile of flats, three thousand of them inhale together, their ribbed flanks swelling with a wet, creaking drag, and then lock rigid. A dull orange glow blooms briefly behind the translucent membrane between their ribs, hot enough to hiss where the freezing drizzle strikes their sides. Then, on the exact same millisecond, they drop their heavy, flat-hoofed forelimbs into the icy mire—*THUMP*—and blow twin jets of scalding, grease-scented steam out of cartilaginous vents behind their shoulders.

The synchronized impact shakes the slush into standing waves. From their undersides, pressed straight out into the brine, fall warm, dark cylinders of smelted iron-silt, stamped along their flanks with crisp, right-angled grooves and shallow notches that look impossibly like serial marks.

No one collects them. Within ten minutes, the cylinders cool, lose their sharp edges—the right angles seeming to confuse the cold water around them until the geometry slumps—and dissolve back into gritty black mud, or get scooped up by the next tier of life.

Everything here is tied into scaled, relentless production with no customer and no end. Between the legs of the stamping herds run the conveyor-worms: pale, segmented loops of cold flesh, ten yards long, with their blunt mouths permanently clamped onto their own tail-ends. They lie half-submerged in the freezing channels, rotating continuously in place with a wet ratcheting click, their ridged backs dragging silt, shed mineral plates, and smaller, frantic soft-bodied things up from the riverbed. Stationary clusters of barnacle-like feeders—hard, hexagonal, buzzing faintly with current—crowd the waterlines and strip what passes along those rotating backs, spitting out pale pellets of refined white salt and thin ribbons of copper-sheathed tendon that tangle in the slush like discarded wire.

Overhead, low under the flickering cloud ceiling, dense clouds of flat, metallic-winged gnats drift like iron filings in a magnetic field. They don't swarm in natural eddies; they snap into rigid grids and long, humming ribbons, aligning themselves with invisible lines of force that twist through the cold air. Whenever a voltage squall rolls down off the low hills, the air thickens with the sharp smell of ozone and wet iron, the swarms lock motionless in mid-air for a beat, and ragged blue arcs leap from wing to wing, jumping down into the wet backs of the herds below. A thousand creatures convulse in unison, their vents slamming shut, their internal rhythms surging a notch faster as the charge runs to ground through the flooded plain.

And that is when you begin to notice that the valley does not merely imitate industry—reality itself has frayed around the edges here, jammed in some cosmic press.

Look down into a puddle of clear, supercooled water between two shifting hides, and the reflection does not match the sky. Above you, there is only open air, cold drizzle, and that flat, grey, buzzing cloud deck. Down in the dark water, mirrored cleanly among the floating flecks of rust-slime, rise soaring girders of rusted iron, high catwalks, rows of hooded yellow lamps swaying slightly in an unfelt draft. Step into the puddle and the phantom architecture shatters into cold ripples; let the water settle, and the ceiling is back, staring up from beneath your boots.

The animals themselves slip out of clean singularity. Out in the deeper channels stand flocks of tall, raw-boned waders, picking through the circulating worm-loops with long, caliper-shaped beaks. Watch them closely and the nausea hits: they are not flocking. When one snaps its head down to spear a clicking, copper-shelled crawler, four hundred waders spread across a quarter-mile of water make the exact same strike—not a fraction of a second apart, not mirrored, but *identical*, down to the tremor of a single wet featherless crest. They are instances of one creature stamped poorly across the marsh, and the registration is failing. One wader stands with its beak floating half an inch ahead of its skull, biting down on empty mist while water splashes from its throat anyway. Another flickers with a second, slightly offset ghost of its own ribcage whenever the light dips.

For the light does dip. Every few minutes, a broad, dark band rolls silently across the entire valley from west to east, like a shadow cast by nothing, or a brownout sagging across a grid. The dim grey world drops half a shade darker; the deep bedrock hum falls a half-step in pitch; the great pounding herds slow from a hammer to a labored thud. In those brief dim seconds, small causal knots slip loose. A splash thrown up by a lunging predator hangs mid-air in cold, glassy droplets while the concentric rings beneath it have already widened and vanished. A bellows-beast exhales its plume of white steam *before* its ribs draw in air. A blue spark snaps across the damp air between two rocky shelves and leaves behind a black, razor-straight line of scorch-marks hanging in empty space, which slowly breaks apart and drifts downwind like soot.

Then the voltage catches again. The grey sky brightens with a faint, high whine. The suspended water drops smash back into the channel. The roar climbs back to its full, chest-vibrating pitch.

Even in the madness, an ecology grinds on. Predator and prey are tightly packed, brutal, and cold-blooded. Huge, flat benthic pressers—wide as a small pond, armored in ancient plates of stone-hard callus so weathered they look like slabs of the valley floor—slide slowly across the silt on thousands of hydraulic cilia. When one moves over a dense patch of burrowers or stalls beneath a weary, lagging bellows-beast, it simply stops and brings its immense weight down in slow, rhythmic pulses, crushing shell, bone, and extruded iron-slag together into a grey slurry it drinks through pores in its belly. Above the flat backs of the herds, pale, leathery gliders with slotted, louver-like wings ride the rising columns of hot vented steam; they drop without a sound onto the smaller ratcheting scavengers, clamp shut with a sound like metal shears, and lift away trailing thin threads of pale blue spark.

Weather moves across the basin in heavy, industrial shifts. Freezes try to seal the water; thin sheets of hexagonal ice skin over the quieter eddies, etched with uncanny right-angled stress lines, only to be shattered minutes later by the vibrating stone and the trampling, synchronized shifts of the herds migrating toward fresher brine. Sleet falls sideways in grey curtains, turning immediately to gritty rime on every protruding spine and horn, then melting in sudden, violent bursts wherever a cluster of beasts hits its heat cycle together. Fog condenses not just from the sky, but directly off the cold-warm boundary of a million packed bodies, hanging thick and greasy at shoulder height so that you look out over a sea of wet, humped backs disappearing into a roaring, sparking murk.

Far across the open expanse, miles away where the basin tilts subtly toward some unseen, frozen sea, the geometry begins to fail altogether. Distant herds appear too tall for the horizon, stacked faintly above themselves in shivering vertical mirages that hum in time with the ground beneath your feet. Steam plumes rise straight up into the dim, flickering sky and flatten out against an invisible roof two hundred feet up, spreading horizontally as if trapped under rafters that aren't there.

Stand still long enough in the freezing, deafening press—pressed between a rattling, frost-rimed flank that smells of hot oil and wet marrow, and a smooth, ancient boulder vibrating like a live wire—and you feel the rhythm trying to take your own bones. Your pulse begins to drift toward the sixty-cycle drone. Your breath wants to wait for the four-stroke drop of the herd. Down in the dark slush between your feet, the reflection of a high, empty gantry sways silently in the water, waiting for a drop of cold rain to break it apart.
