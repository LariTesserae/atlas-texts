At this altitude, the sky is not blue and never has been. It is the hard, depthless black of the upper air, thinned to almost nothing, pressing down over a wide country of broad basalt shoulders and hollow, yawning valleys. Miles below the lowest ledges, a bruised violet haze marks the roof of the lower clouds—a buried sea that never rises high enough to drown these ridges, only sends cold tongues of vapor creeping up the sheer rock flanks before the thin air shreds them.

Between one weathered mesa and the next, there is mostly nothing: gulfs of frigid, near-empty space a mile across, open and cold enough to turn breath immediately into pale grit. Yet the silence is the first thing that disorients you. The wind here moves fast, combing steadily across the high saddles and bending heavy thickets flat, yet it makes scarcely a whisper. The air is simply too thin to carry a roar. Everything happens behind a pane of invisible glass. You can watch a ridge half a mile away shivering in a violent gust, watch clouds of white vapor erupt from the slopes and shear away into the dark, and hear nothing at all except the faint, low tremor coming up through the stone into the soles of your boots.

The rock itself is ancient and settled, long past the raw jaggedness of young mountains. Ages of frost, scouring wind, and slow chemical seepage have ground the high plateaus into sweeping amphitheaters, smooth-lipped terraces, and shallow catchment basins. There is not a single cut stone anywhere, not a wall, a bolt, a ruin, or a laid track. Nothing here was ever built. Yet looking down across a broad valley floor in the dim, starless gloom, the weathered basalt looks disturbingly deliberate: veined with branching, angular channels etched deep into the stone, gleaming faintly with metallic sulfides and slicks of dark, half-frozen moisture.

Those channels are the work of ten million years of current and dew.

The bedrock is folded through with thick seams of iron, copper, and piezoelectric quartz, constantly stressed by the mountain’s weight and brushed by the charged, freezing mists rising from the abyssal clouds below. Voltage builds silently across the ridges—vast, slow telluric tides running through the stone like groundwater. And wherever the cold, intermittent vapor condenses against the rock—slicking the ledges with heavy dew, pooling in glassy, ankle-deep pans that freeze and thaw by the hour—life has erupted in a dark, startlingly lush profusion.

It does not look like green growth from the lowlands, though it is every bit as thick and tangled. Along every damp terrace, fissure, and sheltered saddle, the flora crowds together in dense, heavy belts of matte black, oiled graphite, and bruised verdigris. There are sprawling brakes of condenser-ferns whose broad, ribbed fronds feel like vulcanized rubber and gleam with thin films of metallic mica; they angle themselves slowly into the freezing drafts, combing moisture and static charge out of the passing mist at once. Droplets of cold, remarkably pure water bead along their serrated margins and run down grooved stems into the dark, braided root-mats below—roots plated in bio-deposited copper that wedge deep into the mineralized fractures of the rock to tap the mountain’s current directly.

Rising above the ferns in crowded, swaying stands are the bellows-reeds: tall, segmented stalks sheathed in dull, corrugated casings that look like weathered ceramic or ribbed industrial hosing. In the sub-freezing gloom, they stay alive by brute thermodynamic labor. Every few minutes, a slow, rolling wave passes across a hillside of them. Their upper bladders swell, draw in pockets of the thin, frigid air, and then contract with a slow hydraulic shudder, compressing the gas inside until adiabatic heat warms their dark sap. When their apical valves release, each reed exhales a tiny, silent plume of warm white vapor that instantly crystallizes into glittering diamond dust and drifts out over the empty chasm. You can watch whole valley walls breathing like this in staggered, rhythmic intervals—vast, unhurried cycles of intake, compression, and exhaust, all in near-total silence.

Underfoot, filling every hollow between the weathered boulders, lies a deep, plush carpet of resistor-moss. On the surface it is coarse, wooly, and black as soot, dusted with brittle rime-frost that crunches faintly under weight. Push a hand down through that insulating pelt, though, and the core of the mat is astonishingly warm—dry, humming faintly with a minute vibration, smelling sharply of ozone, wet slate, and warm paraffin. Millions of carbon-rich filaments run steady, low-voltage currents through their own tangled mass, turning the hillside into a living, self-regulating heating element. Part the dense black wool in the dark and you can catch a fleeting glimpse of the underside: a dull, buried cherry-red glow, too dim to light the ground above, instantly hidden again as the cold fibers spring back.

Intertwined through the moss and ferns are heavy, gnarled distillate-shrubs hung with clusters of thick, translucent resin sacs—some cloudy amber, some milky grey. Inside these pressurized bulbs, the plants run a slow, continuous fractional refinement of atmospheric nitrogen, leached minerals, and cold brine. They weep dense, dark oils and pale, waxy polymers that coat the surrounding basalt in smooth, weatherproof lacquers, sealing the damp crevices against the killing dryness of the open air.

Because the ridges run hot with refined oils, compressed warmth, and stored charge, this cold, empty height is quietly teeming with animals. They are rarely crowded together—the scale of the place is too wide, the voids between the lush ledges too vast—so at first glance a valley can seem completely deserted. Stand still on a weathered promontory for ten minutes, however, and the dark resolves into constant, disciplined motion.

Far out in the hollow air between two opposing cliffs, a loose procession of gyro-gliders drifts across the gulf. Each one spans twenty feet from tip to tip: paper-thin wings of matte-black membrane braced by conductive silver-grey spars, gliding effortlessly on the shearing cold currents without ever flapping. Inside each creature’s narrow thorax spins a heavy, calcified ring—a biological flywheel driven by pulsed muscle contractions—holding the animal dead-steady in gusts that would tumble a bird into the abyss. As they bank low over a lush terrace of condenser-ferns, trailing fine pale filaments beneath their bellies, they skim static charge straight off the canopy. For a fraction of a second, a faint, violet veil of ionized air traces their wake, then vanishes into the black.

Closer in, moving in slow single file along a damp, oil-lacquered ledge, a herd of valve-backs browses the distillate-shrubs. Heavy, six-limbed, and armored in dull, overlapping plates the color of cold cast iron, they look almost too dense for the thin air. Along their spines run twin rows of pale, hinged intake-slits that open and shut in a quiet, staggered cadence, mechanically pressurizing the thin atmosphere inside thick-walled lung chambers so their blood can drink enough oxygen to keep moving. Faint, rhythmic curls of condensation drift silently from their flank-vents as they push their rigid siphons into the amber resin bulbs, drinking the warm, high-energy oils.

Where the herds graze, the moss is never safe.

Low in the dark, warm matting of the resistor-beds, an arc-hunter lies completely motionless. Its long, narrow body is wrapped in shaggy, oil-shedding fur the color of wet ash, insulating it from both the freezing wind and the live ground beneath it. Only two parallel combs of smooth, bone-white mineralized keratin protrude along its shoulders and forelimbs—biological capacitors charged slowly from the living moss it sleeps in. When a young valve-back steps away from the herd toward a shallow, ice-rimmed pan of brine, the hunter uncoils with startling, weightless speed. Its padded feet make no sound on the dark wool. There is no snarl, no cry of impact—only a single, blindingly brief fork of blue-white light snapping between its forelimbs and the grazer’s throat, accompanied by a flat, dry *tick* smaller than a fingernail breaking. The grazer drops heavy and silent onto the frosted moss; the brief blue afterimage fades, leaving the valley darker than before.

Nothing goes to waste on the ledges. Wherever a stand of bellows-reeds collapses or a copper-plated root-mat dies back, swarms of armored slag-beetles emerge from the damp cracks. Slowly, methodically, they chew through the mineralized wood and conductive tissue, running acidic reduction chambers in their guts that strip the metals cleanly from the carbon. Behind them, they leave neat trails of tiny, smooth, dull-brass and iron-grey pellets that wash with the next condensation slick back into the etched rock channels, replenishing the conductive veins for the next generation of ferns.

Every few hours, the whole region shifts gears at once.

Down in the unseen depths, the lower cloud-deck swells, sending a wide, cold bank of heavily charged vapor rolling silently over the weathered southern rims. As the mist spills into the open valleys, the ambient light barely changes—only the distant indigo horizon disappears—but the landscape responds with an eerie, coordinated surge. Across three miles of dark terraces, the lush fronds of the condenser-ferns stiffen and lift simultaneously, aligning with the climbing electric field. Pale, ghostly blue corona-fire blooms along the tips of the highest basalt spires and flickers soundlessly across the wet edges of the leaves.

The mist condenses rapidly on every chilled surface. Shallow basins brim with dark, glassy water; thin needles of ice shoot across the surfaces with tiny, vitreous *pings* barely audible at boot-length. Fueled by the sudden surge of moisture and voltage, the bellows-reeds double their pace, sending dense, silent billows of freezing white exhaust rolling upward into the black sky. Below the moss, the hidden dull-red heat intensifies, melting the rime-frost into dark, steaming dampness that smells sharply of hot oil, ozone, and cold iron. Out in the hollow void above the valley, dozens of gyro-gliders wheel back through the mist-front to harvest the sudden abundance of charge, their faint violet trails crisscrossing the gloom like dying sparks.

Then the vapor bank moves on, torn apart by the upper-altitude wind.

Within twenty minutes, the blue corona fades from the rock tips. The wet slicks on the basalt glaze over into hard, matte ice. The bellows-reeds slow back down to their long, hypnotic cadence, the herds pull tighter into the warm, dark hollows of the moss, and the vast gulfs of thin black air between the ridges empty out once more. Across the whole sweeping country of stone and dark jungle, the silent, unbuilt engine keeps turning in the cold dark—refining, compressing, hunting, and conducting—entirely self-contained, miles above the world, answering to nothing but gravity, frost, and current.
