At first glance, the country looks like a high, sun-bleached desert of pale sandstone—an immense tableland rolled out flat and smooth beneath an ordinary, clear-blue sky. The light is plain and honest, the clean daylight of high altitude, throwing crisp shadows behind every low ridge and shallow gully. You can see for forty miles in any direction before the curved, undulating horizon dissolves into silver heat-shimmer. Almost nothing rises to break that sweep: here and there, miles apart, a solitary fluted chimney juts forty feet into the air, or a bleached rib-arch leans over a dry wash, or a lone wind-planed mound squats in the middle of a broad, empty basin.

Then the sound hits you—or rather, you realize it has been hitting you the whole time, so thick and continuous that your mind tried to treat it as pressure instead of noise.

It is deafening. Not a single roar, like a waterfall, but an interlocking architecture of sound that fills the thin, hot air from horizon to horizon and vibrates straight up through the soles of your boots into your teeth. There is a dry, relentless rasp at the top of the register, like billions of fingernails dragged across parchment; beneath it, a pulsing, brassy throb that rises and falls in slow, staggered waves across the plains; beneath that, a deep, resonant booming, dull and hollow, as though massive mallets were striking the inside of a buried drum miles away. If you shout at someone standing an arm’s length off, they see your mouth move and feel the warm breath, and hear almost nothing.

And when you look down to steady yourself against the vibration, the illusion of stone collapses.

There is no rock anywhere. There is no dirt. The entire continent—every broad valley, every terraced mesa, every smooth, wind-scoured ridge—is a single, ancient body of living plant.

Over ages too long to measure, the surface has settled into a weathered, sun-cured crust. Wind and brief, violent rains have planed it down the way weather shapes limestone, carving wide shallow bowls, rounded ledges, and stepped flats out of dense, fibrous rind. To the touch it is scorching hot and dry as old cork, bleached to pale ochre, ash-gray, and faded bone. Its skin is split by fine polygonal cracks and veined with thick, fused scars where old growth buckled and knitted back together. Walk across it and it feels almost as solid as timber, yet with a faint, uncanny elasticity—and everywhere, under the dry crust, the ground is faintly warm with its own internal heat, distinct from the furnace-glare of the sun.

Drive a copper blade through six inches of that dead, papery rind and the whole truth of the plateau bleeds out. Beneath the armor, the ground is damp, pale-green pith, dense as wet gourd-flesh and threaded with tough, pale vessels thick as a wrist. Sap wells into the cut almost at once—warm, slightly sweet, smelling sharply of crushed green stems and resin. No rivers run on top of this world. Water belongs to the interior. It moves invisibly through a continental lattice of fibers, drawn from deep root-beds far below the high tableland and held tight against the fierce, dry heat.

Above ground, moisture is scarce and fleeting. In the hour before dawn, when the high elevation briefly wins out over the heat and the air drops cool, dew beads across the waxy ridges and pools in the wind-hollowed basins. The plains gleam faintly for an hour; the dry rind darkens a shade and softens. By mid-morning the sun burns it all back into a faint, steaming haze that smells of warm hay and wet bark. Only in the deep growth-fissures—long, narrow cracks where the living floor has split under its own slow expansion—does dampness linger all day. Down in those shaded clefts, the exposed pith sweats continuously, dripping dark, tannin-stained water into narrow, stagnant pockets before the heat can take it.

Yet for all its wide-open emptiness—for all the miles of bare, rolling horizon between one landmark and the next—the plateau is choked with animal life. You do not notice the scale of it until your eyes adjust from the vast distances to the square yard in front of you.

Nothing on the surface is still. What looked like wind-scattered flakes of peeled bark are actually flat, wafer-thin grazers pressed flush against the rind to keep out of the noon wind, their pale, mottled carapaces indistinguishable from the terrain. There are millions of them to an acre, billions across a single valley, slowly inching along on hidden legs and rasping away the dead outer skin with tiny, serrated mouthparts. That is the high, papery hiss that never stops: the sound of a continent being steadily filed down by its own tenants.

And because the living floor is full of hollow air-bladders, dry vascular tubes, and ancient, abandoned chambers just beneath the skin, the entire landscape acts as a soundboard. Every footstep, every burrowing jaw, every vibrating wing is amplified by the ground itself.

Scattered across the open flats at wide, regular intervals—sometimes a hundred yards apart, sometimes half a mile, spaced out in strict, invisible territories—stand the drummers. They are gaunt, long-legged creatures the height of a horse, built almost entirely of heat-shedding geometry: spindly, dry-jointed limbs, narrow flanks, and wide, translucent sail-ears veined with dark blood that fan outward to bleed off the sun’s heat. Beneath their jaws hang inflated, ribbed throat-gourds nearly the size of barrels. They stand motionless in the open glare, facing into the wind, and pump air through those gourds in long, shuddering, brassy blasts. Each one answers its neighbors across the empty miles, turning the sparse plain into a staggering grid of interlocking sound. They are not merely calling; the pitch is tuned to the hollow chambers underfoot. When a dozen drummers along a ridge lock into the same frequency, the ground beneath them shudders violently enough to drive deep-burrowing creatures out of the damp pith and up through the cracks, straight into the waiting jaws of the drummers’ long, hooked beaks.

Down in those same crevices and tunnels, out of the noon blaze, lives an entire second world. Pale, segmented siphon-worms and eyeless, slick-skinned pith-eels pack the wet arteries of the plant-mass, chewing tunnels that the plateau slowly heals behind them. You hear them more often than you see them—a wet, muffled clicking and tearing underfoot—until a seam splits open or a brief rain sweeps the basin. Then they boil upward in tangled, glistening knots, desperate for fresh oxygen or spilled sap, and the wide, seemingly barren plateau erupts.

Creatures you took for weathered knots of dead wood unfold twelve stiff, bark-plated legs, vent a sharp hiss of superheated steam from dorsal vents, and scuttle sideways at terrifying speed to snatch the surfacing burrowers. Tall, needle-limbed ambushers that spend the hottest hours folded rigid like splintered snags snap open and strike. From the high air, the sky-flocks drop.

The sky-flocks rarely touch the ground at all. Because the plateau is vast enough to generate its own weather, immense columns of superheated air rise steadily off the sunlit basins all afternoon, and whole populations ride those invisible pillars miles up. They are light, papery, multi-winged things with broad, translucent membranes that rattle against one another in the dry gusts like thousands of dry seedpods shaking at once. Most of the day they are only high, drifting smudges against the bright blue, audible as a dry, incessant clatter whenever the wind shifts downward. When food erupts on the crust below, spirals of them peel off the thermals and descend in roaring, shadow-casting funnels, stripping a wet fissure bare in minutes before catching the next hot gust and climbing back into the glare.

Even the towering chimneys scattered sparsely across the plains are part of this vast, noisy machinery. They are the plateau’s breathing-flues—tall, pale, fluted stalks of hardened fiber rising out of the living floor where internal pressure builds too high. Every few hours, with a deep, organ-pipe groan that carries for ten miles, a chimney vents a plume of warm, spore-heavy vapor into the clear sky. Around the damp rims of these vents cling dense clusters of bloated, sticky-footed filterers and metallic-shelled wasps, shimmering in the mist, feeding on the rich cloud before the dry air shreds it into nothing.

Because of the scale of this high world, weather does not merely pass over it; the plateau makes its own. By late afternoon, the heat rising off hundreds of miles of pale rind builds towering, flat-bottomed storm anvils along the distant horizons. Most of them stay far off, muttering in low thunder that blends indistinguishably into the deep booming of the ground. Now and then, one breaks loose and drags a narrow curtain of warm, heavy rain across a valley.

When the rain hits the baked crust, steam rises instantly in thick, blinding white sheets. The sound changes completely: the dry rasp of the surface-grazers vanishes as they clamp shut to drink, replaced by a deafening, frantic chorus of clicking, piping, and splashing as every crevice floods with warm runoff and thousands of hidden creatures surge into the sudden damp. The storm passes in twenty minutes. The sun returns, hot and stark, and within an hour the flat rind is dry again, leaving only a faint, sweet smell of soaked wood and the frantic, cooling groan of the living floor contracting as evening approaches.

Fire, when dry lightning strikes from those same high storms, obeys a stranger rule than on stone-and-soil worlds. The plateau cannot burn deep; six inches down, it is too wet, too dense with sap. Instead, lightning ignites only the papery, sun-cured dead outer skin. A fire here is a thin, fast, almost smokeless sheet of pale orange flame that races across miles of open flats in an afternoon, peeling the old gray crust cleanly off the living tissue beneath. In its wake lies a vast, steaming plain of raw, scorched green pith, weeping thick rivers of cooked, caramelized sap. Within hours, the smell draws overwhelming waves of fauna from twenty miles around—clattering clouds of sky-flocks, miles-long ribbons of migrating drummers, and surging hordes of burrowers—turning the blackened scar into a writhing, deafening feast until the plateau slowly seals itself with a fresh, tough, waxy callus over the following seasons.

In a world this vast, this hot, and this loud, the marks of people are tiny and easily missed.

There are no towns, no roads, no walls against the horizon. You might walk for three days across the rolling ochre flats and see nothing made by hands. Then, tucked into the lee of a wind-carved ridge, you stumble across a low, rounded blister of packed fiber-turf and stretched hide, half-sunken into a carved-out pocket of the dry rind. There is no door—only a narrow, curved crawl-tunnel lined with thick layers of braided plant-wool and dried pith-batting.

Crawl inside, pull the heavy felt flap shut behind you, and the world drops away.

It is the first and most precious invention of whoever wanders this plateau: a baffled shelter, built not merely against the heat, but against the noise. Inside the dark, cramped hollow, the deafening roar of the plains softens to a dull, bearable murmur, like distant surf heard from behind a heavy wall. Your ears ring in the sudden relief. The air smells of dry tallow, cured hide, and old woodsmoke.

Around these scattered shelters, or standing alone at the lip of a damp growth-fissure miles from anywhere, lie the other sparse traces of their craft. They work with what can be carried, shaped by hand, or hammered cold and warm over small anvils: simple copper and bronze blades, riveted bone, waxed sinew, fired clay, and the toughest cured parts of the plateau itself.

At the edge of a shallow condensation-bowl stands a lone dew-frame—three poles of fire-hardened rind-wood pegged together with heavy bronze pins, holding a wide, taut sheet of translucent, oiled membrane angled into the dawn wind. A grooved spout carved from a drummer’s leg-bone leads from its lower corner into a half-buried clay cistern, tightly stoppered with a wooden plug to keep out the billions of rasping surface-mites and the heat. Nearby, a set of notched bronze taps have been driven carefully through the dry crust into a thick vascular ridge, each fitted with a woven grass filter and a hanging gourd to collect slow, steady drips of sap.

Not far from an old camp-hollow sits a hearth—never dug into the ground, for a fire built directly on the living crust will cook the wet pith beneath, sending up clouds of foul, blistered steam and drawing the massive subterranean borers straight up through the floor. Instead, the fire-bed is a raised cradle of lashed rib-bones packed tight with white, calcined shells and hardened ash, lifting the coals a foot clear of the skin. Beside it lie a pair of worn copper flensing-spades, a broken clay pot mended with bronze wire, and a thick band of braided fiber matted with ear-wax—dropped and left behind when the camp moved on.

Most telling of all are the way-posts. Because the flat horizons shimmer into nothing by midday and the plains look identical for fifty miles in every direction, travelers mark the safe water-fissures and firmest ridges with solitary stakes driven deep into the rind, spaced miles apart. From the top of each stake hangs a thin, hammered plate of copper beside a hollow, notched horn. Even through the overwhelming, chaotic roar of the plateau—through the rasp of billions of mouths, the rattling sky-flocks, and the shuddering chorus of the drummers—the wind catches those hung plates and draws out a single, sharp, metallic ringing, utterly unnatural in its pure pitch.

Stand on a high ridge at dusk, as the sun drops cleanly toward the vast, pale rim of the living continent and the air begins to cool just enough to make the whole plateau creak and sigh beneath your boots. The drummers begin their long evening shift, their deep chords rolling across the empty miles. High overhead, the paper-winged flocks bank downward into the fading light. And somewhere far off across the shimmering valley, faint but unmistakable above the wall of living noise, a single beaten-copper plate rings in the hot wind—thin, steady, and small against an endless, roaring world.
