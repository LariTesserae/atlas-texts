The flats lie barely above high water, a broad, sunken shelf of concrete, silt, and gridded canals tucked beneath a low lid of gray vapor. No hill interrupts the horizon, only the long horizontal line of the outer sea-levee and, beyond it, the pale blur of the coast. The sky never quite clears. Warm marine haze rises to meet the exhaust steam from the power and brine works further inland, forming a thick, diffuse ceiling that strips the daylight down to a dull, even pewter. Shadows barely exist. Even at midday, the photocells on the tallest yard lamps are fooled by the gloom, leaving solitary pools of pale amber hanging over empty asphalt half a kilometer apart.

The heat sits on everything with a wet, pressing weight. It is not the dry sting of a desert but the close, suffocating warmth of a basement behind running boilers. Sweat does not dry; it collects and cools nothing. Every iron handrail and concrete wall feels feverish to the touch, and the air carries a flat, familiar mixture of tidal mud, warm saltwater, hot lubricating oil, and rust.

Across this whole low basin—several kilometers from the outer sluices to the rail sidings at the upper edge—the built world is laid out on the clean, practical geometry of heavy industry. Paved service roads cross on right angles over concrete culverts. Wide, flat aprons of tarmac surround old pump-houses, transformer yards, and settling lagoons. Elevated pipe racks run straight as rulers on weathered piers, carrying insulated hot-water lines beside bare, sweating intake mains. High-voltage pylons march out of the haze and vanish back into it.

Yet for all this urban infrastructure, the volume of the place is mostly empty space. The yards are vast and unpeopled. Between one low brick substation and the next lies a quarter-kilometer of cracked pavement, shallow evaporation pans, or open brackish water. Many of the older warehouses have lost their corrugated cladding to decades of salt air, leaving hollow steel skeletons that frame nothing but gray distance. Concrete drydocks sit open to the sky, half-filled with murky tidewater. Nothing is crowded together; the structures stand wide apart across the flats, weathered down into a long-settled truce with the climate. Concrete curbs have spalled along their edges, exposing dark knots of rusted rebar. Brick walls are salt-whitened up to chest height, their mortar recessed by years of damp. Silt has banked in smooth, permanent wedges against the pilings and floodwalls, shaped by thousands of identical tides.

Water is everywhere, though it rarely moves fast. It lies in wide, tepid sheets across the settling basins, creeps through concrete drainage channels, and stands in broad, mirror-flat puddles across the sagging asphalt. The retaining walls weep continuously from hairline seams, dark vertical streaks running down into the mud. Overhead, the cooler supply pipes pull moisture straight out of the thick air, forming beads that gather along the flanges and drop onto the pavement in slow, irregular taps. Where the warm industrial discharge meets the brackish canals, thin ribbons of steam lift a meter off the surface and dissolve into the gray light.

Almost nothing green survives here. Between the salt, the relentless heat, the chemically scoured runoff, and the sheer expanse of stone and tarmac, land plants have been ruled out. There are no reeds along the canals, no grass splitting the cracks in the road, no scrub on the levee slopes. Only below the high-water mark does a slick, olive-brown film of diatoms and fine algae coat the wet masonry, giving the drowned concrete a greasy sheen. Here and there, in the lee of a dry retaining wall, a single stunted twig of dead saltbush sticks out of a gravel seam, bleached white as bone.

At first glance, walking out across a wide wet apron between hollow sheds, the basin feels lifeless and hushed. Sound travels poorly in the damp, heavy air and dissipates quickly over the flat distances. What noise there is stays low and steady: the faint, permanent electrical drone of a distant transformer yard; the slow, wet slap of tidewater inside a hollow quay; the occasional mechanical clunk of a motorized sluice gate adjusting its height by a few centimeters on a timer. A rotating drum screen at the intake channel turns at two revolutions a minute, its damp steel chain ticking quietly against the sprockets.

Then your eyes adjust from the empty distances to the surfaces, and the silence reveals itself as full.

Take a step toward the edge of a drainage canal and the mudflat beside the concrete shifts in a single pale ripple. What looked like wet gravel is thousands of fiddler and shore crabs packed across the silt, retreating sideways into their burrows in a wave that moves ahead of your shadow. The sound they make is barely louder than dry sand pouring through a sieve—a soft, papery clicking of shells brushing shells. Along the slick mud banks, mudskippers prop themselves on bent pectoral fins, blinking bulging eyes in the humid air before flopping down into the tepid film of water.

Every hard surface touched by the tide has grown heavy with life. The square concrete pilings beneath the pipe bridges are no longer square; they are swollen into gnarled, gray-white trunks by layered crusts of barnacles, sharp-edged oysters, and crowded clusters of dark mussels, bound together by chalky worm tubes. Between the shells, tiny bristle worms and amphipods thread through the wet grit.

Look down into the warm outflow canals and the water itself is crowded. Because the plantless flats produce almost no terrestrial food, the whole basin feeds off the sea and the heat—tidal plankton, suspended detritus, and the rapid microbial blooms thriving in the bath-warm discharge. Schools of mullet and warm-water tilapia pack the culverts by the thousands, stacked side by side in the sluggish current. The oxygen in the hot water runs thin, and every few seconds the oily surface dimples across a fifty-meter stretch as hundreds of broad mouths break the film together, taking air with a muted, wet pop before sinking back into the green-brown murk.

Above the waterlines, the built frame belongs to the animals that eat what comes out of the canals. Look up at the pipe racks, the gantry cranes, the corroded guardrails, and the crossarms of the power poles: nearly every horizontal span carries a spaced row of motionless birds. Herons, egrets, marabou-like storks, cormorants, and heavy gray gulls stand shoulder-width apart along the ironwork, hundreds of them stretching away into the haze until they become small dark knots on distant wires. In this heat they do not call or quarrel. Many stand with their wings drooped slightly away from their ribs, bills parted, the pale skin of their throats fluttering silently to dump warmth into the damp air. Their yellow eyes stay fixed on the canals below.

On the hot concrete aprons, small brown geckos and flat-bodied lizards cling motionless to every shaded flange and breaker box, darting only when one of the low clouds of brine flies drifts within reach. The flies themselves hang in dark, shifting mats a hand's breadth above the shallow evaporation pans—completely silent, millions of tiny bodies rising and settling with every faint eddy of warm air. Above them, red-bodied dragonflies patrol back and forth in crisp, soundless turns.

Heavier scavengers work the embankments. Thick-tailed water monitors, gray-brown and nearly the color of wet slag, walk slowly along the edges of the concrete quays with swaying shoulders, pausing to flick dark forked tongues over the crab burrows or slip without a splash into the drainage troughs. Near the automated intake screens—where a slow metal rake periodically lifts mats of stunned fish, shed crab carapaces, and broken mussel shells into concrete dump-bins—a loose crowd of monitors, feral cats, and black kites gathers on schedule. Even here there is little commotion. Food arrives with every turn of the tide and every cycle of the pumps; a kite drops silently out of the gray mist, snatches a mullet off the concrete lip, and beats away with heavy, unhurried strokes.

Inside the hollow structures, away from even the dim daylight, the density of living things tightens further. Beneath the concrete undersides of the wide road bridges, and high up in the rafters of the gutted pump-houses and idle cooling towers, tens of thousands of bats hang packed in dark, velvety sheets. Through the hot hours they make only a faint, high rustling, a dry scratch of claws shifting on damp cement, while dark trails of droppings stain the empty floors below and support entire carpets of shiny black beetles.

Nothing here is frozen in place. The basin runs on two interlocking clocks: the twelve-hour push of the tide and the steady, automated hum of the surviving works. When the flood tide presses in through the sea-gates, cooler, saltier ocean water drives up the channels beneath the warm outflow. Along the mixing lines, small fish surge toward the surface, and the quiet rows of herons along the pipes unfold all at once, dropping down to the wet ledges to strike with sharp, clean plunges. When the ebb draws the water back out, acres of slick silt reappear between the concrete slabs, and the crabs emerge from their holes before the mud has even finished draining, turning the gray flats restless again.

Late in the afternoon, the heat barely breaks, though the thick vapor overhead deepens from pale pewter to slate. The automated yard lamps lose their washed-out look and begin to throw sharper cones of amber onto the wet tarmac, each bulb instantly ringed by silent swarms of midges and small moths. Under the bridge spans and inside the hollow steel warehouses, the dark ceilings come loose in quiet ribbons. Stream after stream of bats pours out through the missing roof panels and open doorways, fanning low over the warm lagoons and concrete streets, skimming the insect clouds without a sound above the soft rush of their wings. Far off across the flats, a pump switches cycles with a dull metallic thud, a sluice eases shut against the turning tide, and the warm water settles once more against the crusted walls.
