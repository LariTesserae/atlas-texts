The air up here is thin enough to make your teeth ache, and hot enough to curl dry paper. You stand on a broad, pale tableland miles above whatever lower world once held it up—an expanse of wind-planed gypsum, baked ochre dolomite, and flat shelves of matte black basalt that stretch out in shallow, stepped basins toward horizons too far away to resolve cleanly. There is no water anywhere. Not a salt pan that remembers a lake, not a dry arroyo cut by forgotten rain, not a film of dew on the underside of a stone. The rock has been worn smooth solely by heat, abrasive mineral grit, and time—long, settled, patient time that has sanded every ridge down to a dull, resting contour. Nothing grows. There is no green, no moss, no root-crack, no lichen clinging to the shaded faces of boulders. The light is plain, clear, high-altitude daylight: an ordinary white sun hanging in a bleached sky, laying down sharp, unremarkable shadows across miles of empty stone.

And yet the silence you expect from such emptiness does not exist. The valley is deafening.

It is a dry, layered, industrial roar that fills the thin air from horizon to horizon—a bass hum like a subterranean powerhouse, braided with the agonizingly slow shriek of unlubricated shafts turning, the rhythmic *thump-hiss* of immense bellows, and a constant, dry crackle of high-voltage static snapping across miles of open space. You scan the basins for smokestacks, for refineries, for power lines, for a single straight wall or poured foundation. There are none. Not a brick, not a riveted plate, not a road, not an artifact left by any hand. Nothing here was ever built.

Then one of the distant mineral ridges lifts a leg.

It takes nearly three minutes for the limb to clear the flatstone. What looked like a weathered ironstone outcrop miles across the basin is a grazing colossus, and once your eye adjusts to its scale and its impossible sparsity, you realize the plateau is crowded with them. Yet "crowded" is the wrong word, and "empty" is also the wrong word. Even a titan two hundred yards tall is mostly vacant air: a vast, hollow cathedral of bone-colored struts, oxidized copper ribs, and widely spaced rings of calcified iron, with clean blue-white daylight shining straight through its chest. Between one beast and the next lie miles of bare, shimmering flatstone. Per cubic volume, this landscape is almost nothing—thin hot gas and vast stretches of nothingness. Yet the fauna are overwhelming. They dominate every angle of the horizon, every vibration in the soles of your boots, every frequency of the scalding air.

Move closer to one—if you can bear the radiant heat coming off its flanks—and you see that industry here is not an invention. It is a kingdom of life.

The creature dragging itself across the gypsum flat at twenty yards an hour has no bolts, no welds, no tool marks. Its massive reduction gears grew from dense, fibrous bone plated in naturally precipitated nickel; the teeth are slightly asymmetrical, worn like old molars, yet they mesh with a clean, roaring precision that generates a steady current along its spine. There is no oil anywhere in its joints—water and grease would boil off or cake in this heat. Instead, its axles ride on thin cushions of pulverized graphite shed by specialized glands, and where the friction climbs too high, the joint simply floats a half-inch clear of its socket, held apart by a crackling halo of electrostatic repulsion.

At its rear, a broad, ribbed kiln-belly glows a dull cherry-red beneath translucent plates of mica. It has no mouth in the usual sense; it drags a wide, serrated hopper-jaw along the bare basalt, scraping up raw mineral dust at a glacial pace. Inside its hollow ribs, you can watch the whole digestive line working in plain daylight: a slow helix of calcified bone lifts the dust into a roaring combustion chamber fueled not by food, but by concentrated ambient heat and static pulled from the dry wind. What comes out the far end is not dung, but long, slow ribbons of white-hot slag and humming copper filament that cool on the stone behind it—and then, over the course of an afternoon, quietly thin out and vanish, as if the world forgets it ever owed them mass.

That is where you begin to notice that the physics up here have come unglued—not in a sudden panic, not with flashing lights or tearing skies, but slowly, ages ago, until the brokenness weathered into a habit.

Watch the beast's dorsal turbine. It is a spoked wheel of blackened, fibrous bronze forty feet wide, turning once every four minutes with a groan that shakes your jawbone. Look between the hub of the wheel and the beast's spine. There is nothing there. A clean four-foot gap of empty, scorching daylight separates the wheel from the body that drives it. You can see a distant mesa straight through the gap, though the mesa appears faintly inverted in the shimmering ozone between them. There is no strut, no shaft, no magnetic tether that obeys any sensible law; the wheel simply continues to belong to the animal across the void, transmitting torque through sheer local stubbornness.

Look down at the creature's shadow on the pale dolomite. The shadow is sharper than the sun justifies, and near the beast's hindquarters, the shadow of a smaller flywheel has detached itself from the rest and is turning on its own across the flat rock, grinding loose pebbles into fine dust with an audible gritty hiss, though nothing solid is touching them. When the colossus eventually migrates over the eastern shelf—a journey that will take it four days—that loose shadow may stay behind for a season, spinning slower and slower until it wears a shallow saucer into the stone and fades.

Nor are the colossi alone. Once you stop looking only for dense, solid flesh, you realize the empty air around you is saturated with smaller industrial life.

Drifting through the valleys in vast, sparse clouds are the filament-swarms—creatures no thicker than a hair and three feet long, built of hollow, spun-silica tubes wound with live threads of natural copper. Between any two of them hangs twenty feet of vacant air, so you can see clear through a swarm to the mountains thirty miles off; yet a single cloud spans half a basin, and as it drifts lazily on the thermal updrafts, its collective capacitance turns the whole valley into a live circuit. Your hair stands straight up. Every dry pebble around your feet begins to tick softly as static bleeds from the ground into the sky. Now and then, two filaments drift close enough to mate—or discharge—and a blinding white arc jumps fifty yards across nothing at all, cracking like a cannon shot.

Because sound here has abandoned the courtesy of distance, that crack lands directly against your eardrums even if the arc happened three miles down-basin. The air carries noise without thinning it properly. You stand on a bare ledge of ironstone with nothing visible within a mile, and yet you hear the dry, rhythmic *clack—pause—clack* of a herd of stamp-beetles hammering bedrock on the far side of the valley, loud as foundry presses inside a closed room. They are the size of freight cars, low-slung, matte-white porcelain shells veined with dark copper ore; they lift their heavy, flat abdomens six inches over the course of ninety seconds and drop them flat against the plateau, shattering quartz veins underneath to lick up the sudden flash of piezoelectric voltage that the crushed crystal releases. Sometimes, on this ridge, you feel the tremor of their impact through the stone a full minute before the sound arrives—or a full minute before you see their bodies drop through your binoculars. Cause and effect drag their feet here, drifting out of step like tired gears.

Closer to the ground, nestled in the long grooves worn ten feet deep into the basalt by millennia of migrating titans, live the scavengers and parasites. There are low, sprawling condenser-crabs whose backs are rows of thin, papery aluminum fins grown directly out of their carapaces; they sit motionless for days, soaking up the blistering thermal gradient between the sun-baked stone and the thin air six inches above it, humming in a high, piercing monotone that sets the dust vibrating into crisp geometric ripples around them. When a colossus passes overhead, smaller things—like barbed, three-legged transformer coils with clicking glass mandibles—detach from the hot shadows of boulders and winch themselves slowly up the giant's trailing voltage-fields, climbing empty air as if it were rope, until they latch onto a humming tendon and siphon off raw current. They swell softly as they feed, growing heavier without growing larger, until the rock beneath the colossus groans under a weight that does not match the creature's size.

Everything moves with a vast, unhurried patience. There are no sudden chases, no frantic flights, no darting wings. A predatory smelter—a long, articulated spine of dark iron segments enclosing an open, roaring core of white-hot plasma that hangs suspended in empty ribs—stalks a crippled bellows-grazer over the span of a week. You can watch the hunt for an entire afternoon and barely perceive the gap closing: a millimeter of piston-crawl, a long, whistling intake of scorched air that lasts twenty minutes, a slow realignment of disconnected bone-lenses that focus ordinary sunlight into a thin, pale line of impossible heat. When the line touches the grazer's flank, the metal does not even melt at first; it simply softens into a shimmering blur that smells of hot dust and burnt wool, while the grazer continues its four-minute breathing cycle as if it has not yet received the news.

Even the geography participates in this slow, quiet unmaking. At the far rim of the plateau, where the tableland breaks off into a hazy drop of terraced stone and bright empty air, the same jagged ridge appears twice—once at the actual edge, and once hovering faintly thirty yards past it, slightly more eroded, its shadows leaning at a different hour's angle. Nobody built a mirror there. The high heat and the endless, screaming work of ten thousand living engines have simply rubbed the local space thin, the way wind rubs sandstone, until the plateau begins to echo itself.

When the sun finally dips toward the western shelves—taking its time, bleached and pitiless right down to the rim—the temperature barely drops. The stone gives back everything it swallowed, radiating heat upward in slow, invisible rivers that flow sideways and uphill toward the nearest feeding beasts, bending the laws of thermodynamics so neatly that a dry, scalding breeze blows straight down into the hollows where the largest kilns have settled for the night. The light turns ordinary, dusty gold. Across the vast, hollow miles of the tableland, nothing sleeps. The colossal hollow frames keep turning their disconnected wheels in the empty air; the swarms drift like bright, sparse sparks across the basins; the porcelain stampers lift and drop, lift and drop, hammering voltage out of dead stone.

The roar does not soften. It settles into the bones of the plateau—an ancient, unbuilt, blistering equilibrium, loud as a world-sized factory, empty as a dry well, grinding through its slow, impossible shift under a plain and indifferent sky.
