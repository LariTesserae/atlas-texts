The first thing you notice is not the drop, though the drop is miles deep, nor the city itself, though it covers every ridge in sight. It is the weight of the air pressing into your ears—a solid, unvarying roar that hits like standing directly beneath a spillway, except there is no spillway close enough to blame. It comes from everywhere at once: up from the white abyss below, out of the porous walls beside you, through the soles of your feet from the hot stone underfoot.

It is staggeringly high up here. The ground exists only as a sprawling spine of knife-edged peaks, buttressed crags, and broad stepped saddles jutting clean above a floor of cloud so far down it reads less like weather than like salt flats at the bottom of a canyon. Yet the altitude brings no chill. The air is fiercely, suffocatingly hot—the wet, clinging heat of a greenhouse left sealed in high summer. The rock itself radiates warmth, feverish to the palm, and everywhere water weeps out of it in hot, clear films that steam as soon as they meet the open air.

From a distance across the valley, the ridges look merely terraced and overgrown, draped in a dense, saturated green. Only as the eye settles into the bright, ordinary daylight do the right angles assert themselves.

It is a city—vast, layered, and continuous across three or four jagged ridges and the deep bowls between them. Multi-story blocks cling to sheer faces, stacked so tightly that the roof of one forms the street-terrace of the next. Colonnades run for half a mile along cliff edges. Open plazas cantilever over the cloud-gulf on thick arched haunches. Aqueducts span the chasms between peaks in triple tiers, their upper channels brimming and spilling slender curtains of steaming water down into the haze. Narrow stairways switchback up every incline, branching into courtyards, recessed galleries, and sunken cisterns.

Yet if you walk up to a wall and press your face close to it, the urban certainty curdles.

There is no masonry. No bricks, no mortar beds, no chisel marks, no lintels laid across posts. There is no metal anywhere—not a nail, not a hinge, not a drain grate, not a rod of iron bracing an arch. No glass sits in the rows of tall rectangular openings; no cloth hangs from the balconies; no timber beams cross the ceilings. The entire metropolis is seamless, formed of a single continuous porous travertine and iron-stained sinter, as though the limestone had simply *grown* into streets, cornices, window-grids, and drainage channels the way salt grows into cubes. Where a balustrade rims a bridge, it is braided from thick woody roots that have slowly calcified into stone-hard pale bone, still sprouting glossy green shoots from their undersides. The aqueduct troughs are hollowed smoothly along their spines, lipped with cauliflower-like crusts of mineral deposit where hot water has overflowed for ages.

Nothing here was built with tools. Nothing looks ruined, either. The edges of the steps are softly dished and rounded, the eaves thickened with travertine stalactites, the plazas worn to a dull satin sheen—everything weathered into a mature, settled balance that looks older than any civilization could account for.

And the vegetation owns every surface the stone leaves damp, which is almost all of it. Broad, waxy leaves the size of tabletops crowd out of the window openings, drooping under beads of condensed vapor. Mattresses of emerald moss and liverwort upholster the undersides of arches, dripping steadily into the streets below. Giant tree-ferns erupt from the corners of plazas, their fronds brushing the balconies of the third stories above them. Curtains of fleshy, red-veined creepers hang between facing blocks across narrow alleys, so dense that whole avenues become humid green tunnels lit by fractured daylight. Along the sunlit parapets, thickets of dark reeds lean out over the abyss, rooted in shallow troughs of hot, clear standing water.

Despite the jungle density and the stifling, bath-warm wet, the place is stripped almost clean of animal life.

No birds cross the open valley between the peaks. No swifts nest under the thousands of overhanging eaves. No flies hover above the warm street-gutters, no mosquitoes rise from the brimming cisterns, no lizards dart along the hot stone ledges. The vegetation itself is eerily unbitten—not a single chewed leaf margin, not a spiderweb strung across a shaded archway, not a track in the silt-traps of the drainage channels. Only if you pry deeply into the shadowed double-walls behind a viaduct, where the acoustic pressure drops by a fraction, might you find pale, eyeless limpets no larger than a fingernail clamped to the wet sinter, barely distinguishable from mineral droplets. Even when touched, they do not crawl away; they only draw down tighter against the warm rock.

It is the sound that explains part of the emptiness, though it explains nothing about itself.

The roar is deafening—not merely loud, but physical, thick enough to blur the edges of your vision when you step into the open avenues. Listening closely reveals it is layered like the architecture. At the bottom sits a massive, sub-audible tremor rising through the mountain’s bones, rattling the sternum and turning the water in every stone basin into a field of crisp, concentric rings. Above that runs a broad, rushing cataract-sound—the combined voice of thousands of hot springs weeping through hollow limestone shafts, gutters pouring into cisterns, and sheets of water plummeting miles off the outer parapets. Crowning it all is a fierce, steady hiss, almost metallic in pitch despite the utter absence of metal, forced out of countless hairline vents where steam presses upward through the hot stone.

Your own voice vanishes before it leaves your throat. Clapping your hands makes no audible dent. Worse, the sound does not fluctuate. It never swells with a gust of wind, never lulls, never breaks into thunder or splashes or irregular drips. It holds a single, monstrous, unchanging chord across the whole visible range of peaks, until your brain begins trying to carve phantom voices or crowds out of the white noise just to find relief from its uniformity.

Then you begin to notice that the sound is holding things up.

In the ground floors of the dense terraced blocks, tall dark archways open directly onto the streets. Peer into one and you expect a shadowed interior room. Instead, the opening is filled flush from threshold to lintel with clear, steaming water—a vertical wall of liquid standing motionless inside the stone frame without spilling a single drop onto the pavement. There is no glass retaining it. If you bring your palm near the surface, you meet a buzzing, elastic resistance in the air itself, a cushion of acoustic pressure vibrating so violently it numbs your fingertips. Push harder and your hand breaks cleanly into scalding, still water; draw it back and the vertical face heals immediately into a trembling, glassy skin, holding back tons of flood as if gravity had forgotten to look inside the doorway.

Other laws seem equally tired, worn thin by the height and the heat and the relentless steady state.

The water that sheets off the high viaducts does not break naturally into spray. As it falls across the open chasms, the acoustic field catches it in standing nodes, pinching the falling curtains into rigid-looking horizontal ribs like corrugated tin—bright, braided bands of water that appear utterly frozen in mid-drop even though gallons are hurtling through them every second. The mist rising from below curls upward in smooth, motionless spirals that never dissipate and never thicken.

Walk the streets long enough and the city’s layout stops agreeing with the ground it sits on.

A broad stone staircase climbs three tiers up a cliff-hugging tenement block, passing beneath dripping root-arches and fern-choked balconies, and brings you out onto the exact paved plaza you just left—except now the plaza sits fifty feet higher than the parapet you stood beside a minute ago, looking down onto the crown of the tree-fern whose roots you just stepped over. Across the central bowl of the valley, two parallel aqueducts stretch toward a distant needle-peak, converging neatly with perspective—yet if you follow the ridge-path out toward their far end, they actually *do* narrow, their stone arches compressing thinner and tighter until the whole structure pinches down into a humming, paper-thin rib of wet travertine fused into the cliff, while the space between the two bridges remains wide enough to hold a full terraced garden.

Look down into the thin, gliding sheet of hot water that coats every plaza like polished varnish. The reflections do not quite keep faith with what stands above them. A squat, four-story travertine block reflects as a slender tower twice its height, pierced with arched openings where solid rock stands in the waking air. Sometimes, in the deeper cisterns, the bright normal sky does not appear at all; instead the water mirrors an inverted shadow of the mountain’s underside, hanging pale and root-fringed above an endless dark drop that has no business being visible from the daylight above.

Even the foliage obeys the fractures rather than ordinary botanical habit. Plants do not merely grow toward the light; they grow along the quiet seams of the roaring air. Walk down a wide sunlit avenue and you see lush, impenetrable walls of elephant-ear leaves and tangled epiphytes pressed tight against the facades, terminating along razor-straight invisible planes a few feet out from the stonework. Beyond that plane, in the center of the street where the standing pressure-waves focus hardest, not a single moss spore or grass blade survives—only bare, steaming, wet stone shuddering beneath your boots.

And yet nothing moves forward.

That is the deepest strangeness of the place, slower to register than the heat or the impossible water-walls, and far harder to shake once seen. Everything here is loud, steaming, dripping, and fiercely alive—and utterly locked in equilibrium.

The daylight never shifts its angle. It stays fixed in a clear, slightly humid mid-morning brightness, casting soft slate-blue shadows beneath every cornice and frond that never lengthen or swing across the tiles. No clouds drift across the sun; the vast white cloud-sea far below sits at the exact same altitude along the distant peaks, its valleys and crests sculpted like plaster, never boiling up to swallow the city and never thinning to show what lies beneath.

Look closely at the plants. Every frond is at peak saturation—heavy, glossy, engorged with hot moisture—yet there is no leaf litter clogging the gutters. Nothing rots. Nothing buds into a flower that wasn't already half-open. No dead stem sags into brown mush in the humid corners of the courtyards. A bead of water gathers at the pointed tip of a waxen leaf hanging over a stair, swells until it gleams, detaches, and strikes the stone below—and in the next instant another bead of the exact same size is already swelling in the exact same curve of the leaf, landing on the exact same dark wet spot that never widens and never dries.

In the narrow drainage sluices, clear hot water runs briskly over smooth green algae, carrying no silt, cutting no deeper into the mineral bed, never surging and never slackening. On the sheer outer cliffs, no loose stone ever clatters into the abyss. No root ever pries a cornice free.

The whole vast, vertical metropolis—from its highest sun-baked roof-marshes down to the cloud-licked viaducts miles across the gulf—operates like a single immense steady-state engine built without gears, without builders, and without purpose. Hot water wells up, steams, condenses on a million broad leaves, drips back into stone channels, pours through acoustic walls, and feeds the howling stone throat of the peaks, balanced to the last drop and the last degree of heat. You can walk its looping stairs for hours, drenched in sweat and condensed mist, your skull ringing from the unbroken roar, watching the bright daylight hold the wet green towers in place—and leave with the unsettling certainty that if you returned a thousand years later, the same droplet would be hanging from the same leaf, trembling in the exact same chord of noise.
