The land here has forgotten what a mountain is. Whatever upheavals once lifted this continent were ground down so long ago that even their roots lie flush with the water table, planed to a gentle, barely undulating shelf that stretches to every horizon. From any rise—and a rise here is nothing more than a broad, pale swell of bedrock no higher than a person’s shoulder—you can see for miles across a shimmering patchwork of flat stone, bleached silt, and vast, shallow sheets of warm water.

Nothing has a sharp edge anymore. The exposed rock consists of ancient, pale gneiss and iron-stained quartzites worn as smooth as river cobbles, their surfaces marked by faint, swirling bands of older stone fused inside them. Where rain has pooled on these low whalebacks over tens of thousands of years, the stone has dissolved into shallow, saucer-shaped basins no deeper than a forearm, their rims polished smooth. Between the rock swells lie wide flats of washed quartz sand and pale kaolin clay, tinted here and there with rusty ochre where iron has bled slowly out of the ancient ground and settled in the low places. Even the loose stones scattered across the flats are small, flattened discs, tumbled and leached until only the hardest quartz remains.

Water is everywhere, yet almost nowhere deep. Because the ground slopes imperceptibly—dropping perhaps a hand’s breadth over a mile—rain and groundwater cannot gather into rushing rivers or cut steep banks. Instead, water spreads out and lingers. It lies in immense, ankle-deep pans and braided, mirror-bright channels that wind around the low rock swells with no clear beginning or end. If you wade out into one of the wider sheets, the water is lukewarm, bath-warm in the afternoons, clear enough near the margins to show every ripple in the pale sand beneath, turning the color of weak tea in the deeper pockets where dissolved minerals and old plant tannins collect. You have to drop a dry rush-stem onto the surface and watch it for a long minute to tell which way the water is creeping. Even the dry-looking clay shelves between the pools are only dry at their crust; press a heel down hard, and water immediately seeps up to fill the print, slow and cloudy.

Above all this lies a bright, generous sky, bleached pale along the rim where warm moisture rises steadily from the flats. The daylight is broad and steady, pouring down unimpeded and bouncing straight back off the wet stone and endless water mirrors. In the middle of the day, a soft, trembling haze blurs the far distance so completely that the distant rock swells seem detached from the earth, hovering like pale rafts between two identical skies. The air is warm and lightly heavy, carrying a clean, mineral smell: wet chalk, sun-baked clay, warm algae, and the faint, sweet sharpness of crushed sedge.

At first glance, the country looks strangely bare for a place so warm and wet. There are no forests, no thick marsh walls, no tangled canopies. The ground is simply too old and too thoroughly washed out; eons of slow rain and seepage have carried away almost every rich mineral that might support dense growth, leaving behind sterile quartz grit, hard clay, and bare stone. What grows here is tough, scattered, and frugal, spaced out with wide openings of water and pale earth between every living cluster. Along the subtle contour lines where a pool’s depth changes by an inch, isolated tussocks of stiff, bronze-tipped wire-rush stand in thin, interrupted ribbons. On the higher rock swells, wherever a hair-thin fissure has trapped a handful of sand, solitary gnarled shrubs cling to the stone—shoulder-high at most, with twisted, pale-barked trunks and small, leathery olive leaves that look as though they have weathered a century to grow a finger’s length.

Most of the green in this world does not stand upright at all. Look down through a few inches of warm, clear water and the flooded pans are lined with thick, velvety mats of olive, rust, and golden-brown algae and benthic film. They coat the submerged clay and the bottoms of the rock-basins like living felt, speckled everywhere with tiny silver beads of oxygen trapped against their nap. Those quiet, sunlit skins are the true engine of the flats.

And because of them, the emptiness is an illusion—one that shatters the moment you listen.

For a landscape so visually open and sparse, the place is astonishingly loud. There is nothing here to muffle sound: no steep valleys, no dense timber, no heavy soil banks, only miles of taut water and smooth bedrock acting together as a single immense sounding board. Into that bright acoustic bowl pours the relentless, layered noise of creatures tucked into every seam of mud, reed-tuft, and shallow pool.

Closest to the ground runs a constant, dry, sizzling rattle—tens of thousands of tiny sedge-crickets and pale hoppers clinging to the scattered wire-rushes, rubbing their wings in overlapping waves that swell and dip with the warmth of the sun. Beneath and through that hiss comes the crisp, erratic snapping of countless burrowing crustaceans hidden in the mud-margins and brackish pockets, a popping crackle across the shallows like hot fat in a pan. From the deeper water-filled pits in the ancient rock and the damp collars around every tussock rises a steady, throaty chorus of frogs—clicking, buzzing, and barking in patient relays, answered from half a mile away with startling clarity. Even the submerged algal mats contribute a soft, ceaseless undertone, a tiny *plip* and *pop* everywhere at once as swollen gas bubbles pull free from the warm floor and break the mirror surface.

Over all of this ring the cries of birds. Long-legged waders—slender stilts, pale cranes, dark glossy ibises, and broad-billed scoopers—are scattered across the bright distances, never crowded together, each keeping a wide perimeter of water to itself. They step with slow, mechanical precision through the warm shallows, lifting their feet cleanly so scarcely a splash disturbs the surface, pausing to probe the silt or sweep their bills side to side. Every few moments one will throw back its head and loose a sharp, metallic pipe or a hollow, carrying trumpeting call that bounces clear across two miles of water and stone, instantly answered by three more dots on the far side of the basin.

Look closer at the flats and the abundance reveals itself everywhere in quiet, unhurried motion. Pale, broad-headed bottom-fish cruise lazily through the warm braids, puffing little clouds of ochre silt as they sift the microbial mats. In the shallowest sheets, small air-gulping fish rest half-exposed on wet stone, wriggling calmly from one warm puddle to the next when the sun warms their backs too much. Around the margins of every clay pan, the ground is perforated with neat, pencil-wide burrows; watch without moving for a minute and small, pale-clawed crabs emerge in cautious intervals, methodically rolling tiny balls of sorted quartz sand out of their mouths and laying them in radiating fans around their doors. Overhead, thick-bodied dragonflies patrol the borders of the rush-clumps, their wings rustling audibly as they turn sharply in the bright air to snatch rising midges, while flat, mottled lizards cling motionless to the warm granite domes, their throats pulsing rapidly in the heat.

Nothing in this landscape hurries, because the land itself has lost all urgency. There are no flash floods here, for there are no hills to gather a torrent; when tall, warm rain-clouds build on the horizon and drift across the basin, the rain simply patters across the flat mirrors for an hour, lifting the water level by a finger’s width across the whole expanse. Over the weeks that follow, that extra skin of water creeps invisibly outward, slips along the faintest troughs, and slowly lifts back into the sky. There are no rockslides, because no cliffs remain to fall. There are no wildfires, because no plant grows close enough to its neighbor to pass a flame along.

The cycles here are measured in gentle breathings of depth and dryness. During the drier stretch of the year, the sheet-water retreats a few dozen paces from the edges of the pale rock swells, leaving wide, glistening aprons of cracked kaolin clay rimmed with thin collars of chalky mineral crust. The frogs burrow deep into the cool, wet mud beneath the crust; the lung-breathing fish seal themselves into damp chambers; the crabs plug their holes; the waders follow the retreating mirrors toward the broadest central pans. Then the slow rains return, the pans link back together into a single bright sheet, the crusts dissolve without a trace, and the water warms again under the wide sun.

It is a place that has finished becoming what it was going to be. The rock has surrendered its height, the water has surrendered its speed, and life has spread out thinly and brilliantly into the flat warmth between them—patient, loud, untouched, and older than anything that remembers hills.
