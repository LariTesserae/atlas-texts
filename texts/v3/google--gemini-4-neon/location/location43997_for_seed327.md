It is no larger than a tall chapel, yet almost the whole of it is air.

At the very apex of a grey-blue needle of rock, miles above the flat white floor of the clouds, the mountain stops being solid. Over ages of frost, wind-grit, and rising vapor, the summit has weathered into a hollow crown: six slender ribs of pitted schist curving upward to meet in broken, open-topped arches, enclosing a single vertical chamber roughly fifty paces across. There is no true floor. Between the inner ledges, the shaft drops away cleanly—a dark throat narrowing out of sight into the mountain’s core, where warm, wet air from some deep sunlit basin or geothermal cleft far below funnels upward without pause.

Step out onto the narrow rim and the sheer emptiness of the volume hits you before the height does. You can see every boundary of the place in a single turn of the head, yet almost nothing occupies the space between them. Across fifty yards of bare sky, the opposite stone rib looks delicate as bleached bone. What little solid matter exists here is pressed tight to the margins: weathered shelves no wider than a tabletop, wind-carved flutings, and thin natural buttresses that have long since settled into an ancient balance against the gale. No fresh rock slides here; the loose stone fell centuries ago. What remains is sleek, dark, and stubborn, polished into long vertical grooves by ten thousand years of mist.

Because of the rising breath from the throat below, the chamber never freezes, despite the thinness of the sky around it. The air stays sharply cool—bracing, raw, tasting of wet slate and crushed green stems. Clouds do not drift peacefully past this peak; they are sucked up through the mountain’s hollow core and blown straight through the pierced arches on the sides. As the vapor strikes the chilled stone ribs, it condenses instantly. Every surface weeps. Silver beads run down the flutes of the schist, gather on the undersides of overhangs, and let go—only to behave strangely once they leave the rock. A droplet falls two or three feet into the open center, shudders, hangs motionless as a bead of glass against the rising thermal, and then drifts slowly upward, spinning into fine cold spray before vanishing out the open crown into the bright sky.

Everywhere that cold stone drinks this upward mist, life erupts in a thick, wet rind. The inner walls are hung with heavy tapestries of emerald and ochre moss, deep enough to sink a wrist into, saturated like wrung sponges. Long curtains of ladder-fern droop from the overhangs, thrashing sideways whenever a gust clips the rim. Pale cloud-orchids and swollen bladder-mosses crowd the recessed crevices, their translucent sacs taut with condensed water; every few minutes one splits with a faint wet pop, firing a puff of brown spores straight into the updraft. Trailing beards of grey-green lichen stream horizontally off the arches like ragged pennants, while stunted, gnarled shrubs anchor themselves in the deeper cracks, their woody roots woven so tightly into the ancient seams that it is impossible to tell whether the roots hold the rock together or the rock holds the roots. Yet step two feet back from any ledge, and all that lushness ends abruptly at nothing. The center remains sheer, swept void.

And it is deafening.

The hollow summit behaves like the mouth of a gigantic stone bottle. The high-altitude wind shearing across the open top sets the whole chamber thrumming with a deep, chest-rattling bass note that rises and falls with the squalls. Through the narrow lancet gaps between the ribs, thinner streams of air whistle at higher pitches, layering over the roar in shifting, hollow chords. Against this constant organ-swell breaks the slap of wet fern-curtains, the hiss of upward-driven drizzle, and the sharp, ricocheting clatter of hundreds of birds.

The fauna here are everywhere and nowhere at once—never crowded on a surface, always flickering through the empty middle. The warm chimney acts as a siphon for the lowlands far beneath the clouds, hoisting endless ribbons of pale, long-winged midges, glassy moths, and wind-borne spiders right up through the throat. Behind them come the hunters. Flocks of slate-blue swifts and long-tailed mountain choughs pour through the side arches in tight, screaming gyres, banking centimeters from the mossy ribs, their wingtips snapping like wet cloth as they shear through insect clouds. High at the sunlit lip of the open crown, broad-winged cloud-hawks hang motionless on the rising air, tilting their heads to track the whirlpool below before tucking their wings and plunging straight down the hollow center like dropped stones, pulling out in a violent roar of feathers just before the shaft narrows.

Along the dripping margins, smaller lives cling to the damp skin of the stone. Plump, slate-spotted frogs no larger than thumbnails hunch inside the folded bracts of the ferns, calling in quick metallic clicks that carry cleanly through the mist. Iridescent orb-spiders string heavy, dew-laden webs across the sheltered notches behind the ribs, rebuilding them twice a day as gusts tear them loose. On the outer ledges, small pale-eyed cliff-beasts—shaggy, compact creatures with splayed rubbery hooves—pick their way along inches-wide cornices to graze on the mineral-rich lichen, freezing only when a hawk’s shadow crosses the wall. Even the water leaves strange, fragile architecture for them: where mineral-heavy drips hang from the same root-tip season after season, delicate hollow straws of white calcite grow downward like pale icicles, chiming faintly against one another in the gusts until a bird’s wing or a sudden wind-shift snaps one loose to spin away into the sky.

Amid all this motion sit the sparse, weathered marks of human hands—never enough to tame the place, only enough to prove that someone climbed here, listened, and left tools behind.

Tucked beneath the broadest overhang on the leeward rib crouches a single dry-stone shelter, barely tall enough to sit upright inside. Its flat slate slabs are fitted without mortar and carpeted now with pale moss; inside sits a cold ring of blackened hearth-stones and a stack of split cedar kindling turned silver with damp. From this shelter, a narrow catwalk of adze-hewn planks—lashed with braided sinew and bound at the joints with green-oxidized hammered copper wire—hugs the curve of the inner wall for twenty paces before ending at a splintered gap where the rest rotted away years ago. Notched wooden pegs, worn glossy by rain and palms, are driven into the vertical cracks above it, marking an old climbing route up toward the crown.

Stranger than the walkways are the things wedged into the stone itself. Set carefully into dozens of narrow wind-fissures around the ribs are hollowed femurs of large birds and fire-hardened clay tubes, each notched near the tip and packed in place with pitch and twisted hair-cord. Whoever set them knew the exact throat of every draft. As the barometric pressure shifts and the rising wind changes angle, different tubes wake and fall silent—a sudden, breathy wooden moan from the eastern arch, then a clear, eerie harmonic fifth from high overhead, answering the deep hum of the mountain itself. A small cairn of river-rounded pebbles—carried up from impossibly far below—sits on a protruding spur beside a frayed hemp line that still dangles an empty woven basket out over the abyss, swinging in slow, erratic arcs.

Daylight here is clean, bright, and restless. With no roof to block the sky, ordinary crisp mountain sun floods straight down into the hollow chamber, turning the wet moss electric green and lighting every upward-drifting droplet like blown sparks. Then, without warning, a denser boil of cloud surges up from the throat or tears across the summit from the west. In three heartbeats the far stone ribs dissolve into luminous silver-grey; the light cools and flattens; the bone-pipes drop a half-tone as the heavy mist chokes their bores; the swifts vanish downward into the dark neck of the shaft while the frogs break out in a sudden chorus along the walls. Five minutes later, the squall shears away cleanly. The sun snaps back onto the steaming stone, the calcite straws glitter, a hawk folds its wings at the bright upper lip and drops, and the whole small, empty, roaring cathedral spins back into full motion.
