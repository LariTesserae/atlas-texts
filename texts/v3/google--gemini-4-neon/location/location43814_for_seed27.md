The first thing you notice is that the ground is not stone, and the second is that it will not stop singing.

It sits high enough in the atmosphere that the sky overhead has thinned to a bruised, dry indigo, with faint bands of dust and the paler stars still readable beside a small, exhausted sun. The light is dim—a perpetual, low-contrast afternoon that never quite warms the air. It is cool up here, crisp and parched, with a chill that has no dampness in it to cling to the skin. There is no water anywhere on the plateau. Not in pools, not in clouds, not as frost on a leaf, not even as moisture in a living cell. If you kneel and press your palm to the pale, grey-ochre floor, it feels like cool pumice blended with tightly woven felt and centuries-old cedar. It gives a fraction of a millimeter beneath your weight, faintly springy, and when you lift your hand your skin is dusted with dry chalk-spores and prickles with static.

Every square meter of this high, wide valley—downward for kilometers, all the way to the buried mineral roots of the old mountains—is alive.

Millions of years ago, before the peaks were ground down into this broad, gently rolling tableland, the original granite was colonized by dry-chemistry flora that needed no water to live. Instead of liquid sap, their vascular networks move ions along superconducting carbon-silicate filaments, holding their shape through electrostatic tension and braided, glassy lignin. Over deep time, wind and thermal creep wore the mountains flat while the plant-mass digested the rock from beneath, replacing stone with compacted root-felt, laminated pith, and hollow, resonant chambers of living wood. Now the plateau is in its oldest, most settled form: a vast, smooth-curved continent of fused vegetation, weathered until every ridge and basin has the soft, final profile of driftwood left out for an epoch.

And because it is dry, hollow-chambered, and pulled taut across miles, the whole plateau behaves like the soundboard of an immense wooden instrument.

It is startlingly loud here. You look out across a wide, sparse landscape—kilometers of open, pale-ribbed plain stretching toward low, worn horizon ridges—and your eyes expect silence. Instead, the air is packed with a heavy, layered roar. High-altitude wind drives endlessly across the open flats, thin and fast, tearing through scattered stands of stiff, hollow quill-reeds and whistling across the fluted flanks of distant structures. Beneath the wind sits a deep, chest-rattling hum that travels up through the soles of your feet: the sound of the plateau itself creaking under thermal shift, amplified by subterranean vascular vaults and the thrum of buried civic machinery.

Because this is not wilderness. It is a city, though one so ancient and thinly spread that it breathes like a steppe.

Across the valley—visible from rim to rim in the dim, flat light—urban districts lie scattered like low archipelagos, separated by wide expanses of open living plain. There is no crowding here, no vertical clutter. A neighborhood might consist of a dozen slender, wind-smoothed spires grown directly from the substrate, terraced into a shallow bowl alongside a sunken plaza and a few arched, braided-root arcades. Then comes three kilometers of open, windswept root-floor before the next terrace rises gently out of the dimness.

Everything built has been grown and weathered into the same matte, bone-pale and graphite-bark palette as the plateau itself. You have to look twice to recognize the scale of the engineering embedded in the wood, because the technologies running this valley do not resemble machines so much as settled laws of nature.

At the heart of each sparse district lies a wide, circular basin paved in smooth, dark cambium. Floating effortlessly at its center, half a meter above nothing, is a matte disc of engineered bark the size of a town square. There are no engines, no cables, no visible intakes; the disc simply leans against the geometry of empty space, siphoning usable work straight from vacuum shear and planetary spin. It has been running longer than continents last, and its only exhaust is a cold, dry 40-hertz bass note that vibrates in your teeth and feeds electrical charge directly into the living root-grid beneath the plazas.

Linking the scattered districts are the transit ribbons—broad, smooth strips of silver-grey living bark laid straight across the open plains, bordered every hundred meters by low, weathered pylons that look like worn boundary stones. They appear empty until a traveler steps onto the strip. Then there is a sharp, resounding *crack*—like a heavy steel hawser snapping across the valley—and the air along the ribbon shimmers. The distance between pylons briefly folds inward. From a kilometer away, you watch a lone figure take four unhurried strides and cross half the basin, their silhouette fanning out into translucent, overlapping afterimages before settling calmly onto a distant terrace. Freight sleds glide along adjacent tracks on repulsive field-skis, hovering a hand's-breadth over the dusty bark, humming in a shrill metallic counterpoint as they haul compressed resin-cells, dry-spun textiles, and acoustic baffles from one district to the next.

Even the spires themselves refuse simple geometry. Seen from outside across the open plain, they are narrow, wind-fluted monoliths of braided silica-wood, pierced by small apertures leaking muted amber and pale cyan light. Their weathered shells are tapered to split the gale, singing in low, shifting woodwind chords that rise and fall as the wind veers. Step through one of those narrow doorways, however, and the interior opens effortlessly into broad, hushed, multi-tiered halls, workshops, and residential galleries folded calmly into spaces far wider than the tower's outer rind. Above the plazas, barely visible as faint optical ripples against the slate sky, anchored field-lenses catch the worst of the scouring high-altitude wind and bend it cleanly upward over the walkways. You can stand in a cool, still market alcove under a woven-root eave while ten meters above your head, the deflected jet stream screams across the invisible canopy like a continuous waterfall of dry air.

Nothing in this city looks new. The impossible physics has been worn down by dry wind and millions of biological seasons until it carries the quiet patina of old furniture. Inhabitants—slender, muffled figures draped in layered, static-shedding bark-cloth—move calmly through the terraced nodes, pausing occasionally to crank wooden tuning-vanes set along the plaza rims, shifting the pitch of a whistling arcade so the district's wind-chord stays bearable.

Between the sparse urban islands, the open plateau is thick with dry, noisy life.

None of the animals here have wet blood or soft skin. They are creatures of parchment, ceramic, braided fiber, and electrostatic muscle—mobile cousins of the same dry biosphere that makes up the floor. Across the wide flats between transit ribbons, herds of drum-backs graze in loose, scattered constellations. They are heavy, six-limbed herbivores with broad, bone-dry membranes stretched taut over hollow silica ribs. They creep slowly across the undulating living floor, scraping conductive spore-crust and mineral dust from the bark with comb-like jaws. Whenever a shift in the wind carries a threat, a lead bull tightens its dorsal skin and strikes it with twin spurs on its hind legs. A booming, kettle-drum report rolls across the valley, immediately answered by dozens more, turning the open flat into a percussive wall of sound meant to organize the herd and scramble the senses of anything hunting them.

They have good reason to keep watch. Along the sparse margins of the transit avenues, motionless among the real weathered pylons, stand the crack-stalkers. Lean, tall, three-legged ambush predators with matte, bark-grey hides and fine silica bristles running down their spines, they spend hours standing perfectly still in the gale, harvesting enormous electrical potential from the dry wind. When a stray drum-back or a scattering of smaller chaff-skimmers drifts within ten meters, the stalker unlocks its posture and discharges. A blinding, jagged arc of pale blue lightning leaps horizontally through the dim air with a dry thunderclap loud enough to rattle windows two districts away, cooking the prey's neural lattice on the spot. The smell of ozone and scorched cloves drifts downwind for minutes afterward.

Above them, riding the howling shear-currents where the city's invisible wind-deflectors bend the gale skyward, glide flocks of glass-kites. Their wings are vast, translucent sheets like dried onion-skin veined with pale copper filaments, never flapping, banking effortlessly through the dim twilight air. They hunt by screaming—long, fluted, harmonic whistles that echo off the wooden plateau floor and the fluted towers, letting them map the vibrations of what moves underground.

And much moves underground. Every few minutes, as you walk across an open expanse of plateau or even across the edge of a plaza, a frantic rattling surges up beneath your boots—like bushels of dry gravel shaken inside a wooden barrel. That is a swarm of cambium-borers, pale, ceramic-plated tunnelers chewing fast veins through the spent, discharged vascular layers just beneath the surface crust. Their passage sounds destructive, a furious mechanical clatter racing beneath the ground, yet the plateau depends on them. Within hours of a swarm passing through, the living substrate seals the hollow galleries with fresh, high-conductivity xylem, keeping the whole valley's vascular lattice supple and alive. Where borers surface to shed their husks, glass-kites drop from the dim sky like falling knives, screaming as they stoop.

The whole landscape turns on a grand, dry diurnal cycle known across the districts as the Charge-Tide.

As the small, pale sun slides along its low arc toward the worn western rim, the temperature drops a few degrees cooler—still crisp, never freezing, just sharper. The dry air loses what little conductivity it held at midday. Across the entire valley, static begins to pile up. The fine dust-spores lift off the ground and hover in faint, ghostly horizontal sheets a foot above the plain. The hollow quill-reeds stiffen and vibrate with a rising, metallic hiss. Commuters hurry off the open transit ribbons; herds of drum-backs pull their legs beneath their hollow shells and hunker down in the shallow depressions; the muffled tenders along the plaza eaves close the wooden louvers over the glowing amber alcoves.

Then comes the Discharge Hour.

It begins on the highest worn ridge at the valley's edge as a stuttering chain of cold blue sparks, and within minutes it is sweeping across the plateau in a rolling, deafening storm of dry light. Arcs leap from spire to spire, from reed-clump to transit pylon, from the hovering vacuum-discs out into the waiting vascular seams of the floor. There is no rain to wash it away, no cloudbank overhead—just a clear, dark-indigo sky of faint stars above a basin erupting in crackling, percussive blue fire. The sound is overwhelming: a continuous, tearing roar of snapping fibers, booming floor-resonance, and rhythmic thunderclaps echoing back and forth between the ancient rims.

The plateau drinks it. Every living filament in the ground flares dimly beneath the translucent dust, storing the voltage, flexing imperceptibly across miles. At the height of the crackling roar, millions of tiny pores across the open plains pop open in unison, exhaling a buoyant silver-grey cloud of dry spores that catches the blue flashes and turns the dim air briefly luminous.

Gradually, the charge bleeds down into the deep roots. The arcs thin to scattered blue ticks, then fade. The spore-mist settles back onto the smooth, ancient curves of the valley like cold ash. The drum-backs uncurl and begin their booming, percussive calls across the open dark; the glass-kites lift back onto the screaming updrafts above the plaza shields; a lone commuter steps out onto a grey ribbon of living bark, and with a sharp, clean crack, folds away into the dimness toward the distant amber glow of the next sparse cluster of towers.

Underfoot, cool and bone-dry, the endless wood hums on.
