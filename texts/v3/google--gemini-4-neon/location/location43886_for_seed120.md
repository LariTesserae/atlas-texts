The whole place is scarcely wider than a potter’s cellar, and there is nowhere inside it to stand straight.

You are immediately pressed on all sides by warm, weeping surfaces—hand-coiled clay walls still slick with slip, slung mats of damp braided sedge, low timber struts lashed together with hemp so tight the green bark weeps white sap. Every cubic inch is spoken for. Clay jars are wedged into mud alcoves; shallow terracotta troughs hang from the ceiling on knotted leather thongs; stacks of flat river-stones sit jammed beside smoking earthenware braziers. To move even a pace requires turning sideways, brushing your ribs against hot, wet pottery and your cheek against the broad, trembling paddle of a freshly unfurled leaf.

And yet the floor shudders beneath your heels with the hollow vibration of something suspended over nothing at all.

Your ears pop. Then they pop again, and again, a sickening, rhythmic pressure-flutter that never settles. The air is scalding—thick with steam, heavy with the smell of hot clay, bruised foliage, charcoal, and raw wet loam—yet every breath feels strangely starved, thin as a whistle, leaving your chest heaving as if you had sprinted up a mountain. Because you are higher than mountains. Outside the thin, mud-daubed skin of this pocket, the wind screams with the flat, dry violence of the upper stratosphere, battering the stitched hides and wattle exterior like fists against a kettle-drum.

Here and there, where a woven reed mat has shifted a hair’s breadth against a fresh bead of mortar, a pinhole no wider than a needle lets the outside show through. There is no sky-blue in those slits—only a brutal, ink-violet dark and, impossibly far below, the pale, curved backs of thunderheads lying like frozen curd on the floor of the world. Wherever that cold void touches the boiling interior, a sliver of blue-white frost flashes into existence for a fraction of a second, screaming as it boils instantly into wet vapor an inch inside the room.

No light floods in through those punctures—only bruised slivers of altitude-gloom that die almost immediately in the steam. Most of what you can see is lit from below and within, by the dull, sultry orange of charcoal embers buried deep in clay fire-pots tucked beneath shelves and into the crooks of lashed joists. Everything stays dim, murk-red and glistening, a world seen through fever and wet smoke.

There is no natural ground anywhere here. Even the floor is a tight patchwork of hand-pressed tiles laid over cross-lashed poles, slightly domed at the center as though built atop the knuckle of a needle too high for stone to sit flat. Every surface has been touched, shaped, pounded, tied, or fired by primitive craft—stone pestles, bone awls driven into damp mud walls like pegs, hammered copper pins holding mortised beams, hollowed gourds bound in sinew. Yet nothing in this packed, sweltering room shows the faintest mark of use or time.

Not a single clay rim has been softened by the water boiling constantly over it; every lip keeps the sharp, clean burr of the bone scraper that trimmed it. The wet mud plastering the rafters holds crisp, glistening thumbprints, their ridges unblurred by the steady rain of condensation. The split bamboo gutters bleed fresh sap along razor-sharp edges that never brown or fray. Even the hammered copper pins, bathed continuously in hot mist, show no green crust of tarnish—only the raw, greasy gleam of soft metal freshly beaten on a cobble. Nothing rots, nothing rusts, nothing settles into age. The pocket is trapped in the violent first second of its own making, perpetually embryonic, perpetually unfinished.

And out of every made seam, the lushness pushes.

There is no soil except what has been carried or conjured into woven silt-baskets, felt slings, cracked clay basins, and the wet mortar joints between cobbles. From those tight pockets erupt thick, glossy taro-like fronds, rank hot-house mosses, fat pale aerial roots dripping with warm condensate, and heavy creeping gourds that wrap around the structural beams. Every plant is caught in the same furious emergence as the masonry: leaves uncurl visibly from tight, glistening spirals; pale shoots thrust out of mortar cracks with tiny audible snaps. Dead foliage never accumulates. Before a lower leaf can yellow or droop, its edges curl inward, soften, and fold neatly back into a fresh wet bud—or press flat against the nearest wall and disappear beneath a fresh smear of river-mud.

Up here, where the air is too thin to hold normal pressure, the boundaries between things have thinned out too.

Follow a thick braid of hemp cord down from the ceiling, and halfway along its length the coarse fibers flush pale green and burst into tender serrated leaves; trace a dark gourd-vine across a damp crossbeam, and watch its curling tendril suddenly stiffen, loop over itself twice, and cinch down into a tight, expert clove hitch around a wobbling strut. The craft and the undergrowth have forgotten which of them is alive and which was made.

They have also forgotten how to stay out of one another’s way.

To your left, a tightly woven wicker partition passes cleanly through the belly of a terracotta vat filled to the brim with simmering water. The clay is unbroken; the reeds are dry where they enter and dry where they exit; no water leaks from the seam. If you press your palm against the intersection, your skin feels the scalding smooth curve of fired earth and the prickly ridge of woven cane occupying the exact same inch of space, and for a dizzying moment your fingers cannot decide which side of the basket they are attached to. Further back—though "further back" is barely two arm-lengths away—a clay shelf juts straight through the stem of a fern without crushing it, bearing three stone mortars that sit slightly tilted, yet the pooled water inside them stays parallel to the crooked slate instead of the floor.

The noise is relentless, physical, impossible to tune out.

Above the deep, roaring shear of the outer wind sits the room’s own frantic percussion: water boiling violently in a dozen clay troughs at the warped, low boiling point of extreme height, surging into thick steam, striking the cooler outer skin of the ceiling mats, and clinging there in heavy, trembling liquid sheets that refuse to fall until they grow too thick—and then slap down onto the glowing hearthstones with sharp, spitting cracks. The green timber struts groan continuously as the wind flexes the whole suspended nest. Somewhere just out of sight behind a curtain of broad wet leaves, a stone pestle grinds rhythmically in a clay bowl, *shhhk-clack, shhhk-clack*, though when you push the wet fronds aside there is only a freshly mortared wall, still warm, with the sharp imprint of a bowl pressed deep into the mud.

Even the echoes have lost their footing. A fat drop of scalding condensation swells on the tip of a fern above your shoulder—and you hear the wet *plip* of it hitting the floor tile a heartbeat before it lets go. Clap your hands inside this cramped mud womb and the sound rebounds not off the wall two feet from your nose, but with the vast, metallic ring of a gorge miles across, before snapping abruptly back into the muffled closeness of wet wool and steam.

No builders walk among the crowded shelves, yet the building never stops.

While you stand crouched between a smoking brazier and a dripping sling of moss, the smooth wet clay beside your elbow dimples—five warm, neat thumb-impressions sinking cleanly into the slip, pulling a fresh ridge upward to brace a leaking seam, with no hand attached to the pressure. A bone needle hangs half-threaded in a split strip of hide overhead, jerks twice as if tugged by an impatient wrist, pulls the sinew taut, and stills. Another narrow ledge of cobbles and ash-mortar extrudes itself slowly from the rear wall, though there is already barely room to turn your shoulders. Because nothing ever wears away and nothing is ever cleared out, the constant making has nowhere to go except inward. Reach your arm into a narrow gap between two stacked water-jars, and your hand finds no back wall—only a tight, scalding tunnel of identical freshly mortared niches and dangling roots, stretching deeper than the tiny exterior shell could possibly hold.

Living animals are rare in this roaring, overcrowded kiln, and when one appears it feels less like an inhabitant than a stray spark struck off the chaos.

Once, while watching scalding runoff trickle along a carved wooden trough, you might glimpse a finger-length amphibian—pale, blind, its slick skin ridged with faint parallel grooves like unfired wheel-thrown pottery. It slides easily through water hot enough to blister skin, pauses on a bone peg with its milky throat pulsing fast, and slips backward into the seam where a clay jar overlaps a reed mat. Behind it lies a shed skin, thin and dry, woven in a tiny neat herringbone pattern.

Rarer still, high in the dim, ember-lit tangle of the ceiling rafters, comes a dry, rapid clicking like bone needles striking together. If you crane your neck through the steam, you may catch a fleeting silhouette—long, brittle legs the color of scorched ash, a flat papery body dusted with charcoal—moving with frantic precision along one of those screaming pinholes where the black-violet stratosphere leaks in. With quick, jerking strokes it draws translucent gray thread out of nothing and stitches the tear in the air itself, pulling the raw edge of the sky closed like wet leather. Then a wave of boiling vapor rolls up from the floor pots, and when it thins, the creature is gone, leaving only a neat row of tiny stitches hanging in empty dim air, beading with hot water.

Down below your feet, the whole pocket creaks and leans slightly into another colossal gust from the thin dark outside. A fresh coil of clay slaps onto the rim of a jar behind you. A bud splits open beside your ear with a sharp wet pop. The water hisses on the stones, the embers flare dull orange through the mist, and the tiny, impossibly high room packs itself one fresh thumbprint tighter—forever boiling, forever unfinished, stubbornly trying to build an entire lush, roaring world inside a single cramped fist of mud lashed to the roof of the sky.
