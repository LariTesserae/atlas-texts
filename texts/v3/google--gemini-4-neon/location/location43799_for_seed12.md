High above the timberline, held inside a raw collar of black volcanic peaks, a valley lies drowned to its brim in warm water.

From within the basin, the sky is only a bruised, trembling lid forty to ninety feet overhead. Because the caldera sits so high in the thin mountain air, the boundary between water and atmosphere behaves strangely: the heat rising from the submerged floor constantly steams off into the freezing air above, mantling the whole bowl in a permanent roof of heavy, low cloud. Whatever sunlight survives the cloud-deck and the churned mist enters the water spent and flat. The basin lives in a permanent olive-and-pewter twilight, dim enough that the far cliffs across the two-mile width of the valley appear only as faint, tiered smears of darker gray, yet clear enough in the middle distance to reveal a world packed tight with raw rock, hanging foliage, and stacked stone.

Nothing down here has had time to grow old or smooth. The floor of the basin is young—still rising, in fact, pushed upward at the pace of a fingernail growing by heat trapped just beneath the crust. There is no beach sand, no water-worn shingle, no rounded river cobbles. Every exposed ledge is freshly fractured basalt and ribbed sheets of black volcanic glass, sharp enough along its ridges to score skin or sever an unwary mooring line. Near the center of the bowl, where the rock is youngest and hottest, the floor swells into a broad, cracked dome crisscrossed by pale, new splits. Thin plumes of bath-warm and scalding water seep out of these seams, wavering upward like transparent oil and warping the view of everything behind them.

Because the mountain air pressing down on the surface far above is thin, dissolved gases and water vapor break free from the warm stone with unusual ease. They do not fizz in tiny streams; instead, wide, flat-bottomed sheets of silver gas peel lazily off the cracked basalt, wobble upward like translucent bells, split slowly in two, and drift for minutes before vanishing into the dim ceiling. As the water carries dissolved silica and lime up from the hot seams and cools by a degree or two against the upper dark, those minerals settle out onto everything stationary. In a wetter, older valley, water eats stone away. Here, water thickens it. The basin is not eroding—it is accreting, coating its own jagged bones in pale, knobby rinds of chalky travertine and delicate, needle-like crusts.

Lush, crowded vegetation takes hold wherever the rock is merely warm rather than scalding, turning the stark volcanic bowl into a layered, heavy-curtained forest. Much of it ignores the weak sun above in favor of the steady heat below. Thick mats of velvet-black and dull crimson moss swell along the edges of the fissures, and broad, leathery ribbon-fronds grow in inverted canopies—arching outward and curving *downward* over the warm vents to trap pockets of heat beneath their undersides. Along the cooler outer benches of the caldera, vast stands of hollow, segmented water-cane shoot straight up from the broken talus in dense, near-touching groves. Filled with buoyant gas, these pale green pillars rise sixty or seventy feet until they press against the underside of the water's skin. There, exposed to the thin, freezing mountain wind just above the steam-layer, their tips stiffen into fragile crowns of white rime and slush-ice. Each stalk is a single continuous bridge between heat and cold: bath-warm at its black-rock root, frosted at its unseen crown.

Woven directly into this dense, dim growth—clinging to the stepped basalt shelves and slung across the open water between cane-groves—sits a sprawling, layered city.

It occupies nearly half the basin's curved slopes, arranged in tight, stepped wards that follow the raw contours of the emerging rock. Everything in it is made by hand, without metal, smelted glass, or kiln-fired brick. Houses, store-vaults, and terraced courtyards are dry-stacked from angular slabs of quarried lava, wedged tight with shaved bone shims and chinked with a paste of white mineral mud and crushed ash that sets hard in the warm water. Where footpaths cross the razor-sharp basalt ridges, the builders have laid down thick, braided runners of cured reed and rubbery kelp-skin, pinned to the rock with obsidian stakes. Narrow vertical alleys and switchback stairways cut between the compounds, so crowded with overhanging eaves, drying-racks, and trailing curtains of cultivated olive-weed that a swimmer or climber moving through a ward rarely sees more than thirty feet ahead before the view breaks into overlapping layers of stone, cord, and frond.

Higher off the slopes, where the valley opens out, the city detaches from the rock altogether. Whole mid-water districts hang suspended in the dim olive gloom, built on tiered lattices of lashed cane-trunks anchored downward to the basalt crags by heavy nets of stone. The ropes holding these floating terraces are braided from pale plant-sinew and tendon, yet the older ones are no longer flexible: years of warm, mineral-heavy water have turned them into stiff, chalk-crusted cables as thick as a forearm, half-fiber and half-stone. Between the suspended walkways, dark woven baskets plastered in silica-clay dangle like fat gourds, serving as storage cellars, nurseries for edible heat-moss, and workshops.

The strangest structures in the town are its inverted roofs. Over many of the stone chambers and beneath the hanging cane-decks, the builders peg down wide, upturned domes woven of tight reed and stitched hide, sealed with dark, pitchy algal resin and weighted around their rims with holed basalt stones. Positioned above the gentler, non-sulfurous gas seeps—or patiently filled from bladders hauled down from the misty surface—these upturned bells hold broad, trembling mirrors of trapped warm vapor beneath their ceilings. Inside those dim, low-pressure pockets, where no fire could ever be coaxed to burn, the water ends in a shimmering horizontal ceiling-skin just above the lintel. Suspended nets hang inside the trapped air to cure salted weed, dry bone-glue, and ferment mash in hollowed stone troughs heated gently from the basalt beneath. From the alleys outside, when a slow current rocks a hanging district, the rims of these bells occasionally tilt and spill a single fat, silver coin of gas that glides silently up through the overhanging fronds.

Nothing in this city stays plumb for long. Because the caldera floor is still swelling beneath the wards, neat, pale-edged fractures creep steadily up through stacked basalt walls and crack the plastered baskets. The settlement does not fight the rising ground; it accommodates it with slow, patient patchwork. Split lintels are bound together with fresh sinew that will calcify into stone over the next decade. Tilting courtyards are leveled with new wedges of dark rock and woven mats. Abandoned lower rooms near newly opened hot seams are simply sealed with mud and left to encrust, becoming pale, knobby foundations for fresh terraces stacked above them.

Even with so much dense construction and tangled growth, the basin is startlingly quiet. Water smothers sharp sound and stretches the deep ones into low, distant rumors. Most of the time, the alleys hold only a thick, padded hush, broken at long intervals by the faint, glassy *tink* of fresh basalt settling somewhere under the central dome, or the wet, hollow *thump* of a giant bubble detaching from a fissure and bumping against the underside of a hide-bell. Now and then, a slow, rhythmic *tock-tock-tock* travels from three terraces away—a stone hammer driving a bone pin into a cracked sill, or an obsidian blade scraping white mineral rime off a clogged sluice—muffled so thoroughly by water and hanging moss that it is impossible to tell whether the worker is twenty yards off or two hundred.

Animal life is sparse and unhurried, moving like afterthoughts through the crowded stillness. There are no darting shoals or chattering reefs. Once in a long while, a broad, pale, scalloped grazer—nearly translucent, with a dull horn-like lip—ripples slowly across a crusty terrace wall, Rasping off fresh mineral algae and leaving a clean dark ribbon behind it. On the hot lips of the central dome, motionless clusters of blind, six-legged ivory crawlers sit for hours with their feathery forelimbs spread into the warm seep, indistinguishable from the jagged silica needles beside them until one shifts a single joint. Near the terraced roofs, a dark, long-bodied swimmer occasionally rises out of an alley shadow, gliding without haste up through the cane-pillars toward the steaming gray lid of the world to draw a thin, cold breath before sinking back into the warm murk.

Everything in the valley obeys a single, vast, unhurried loop. Warm water lifts gently off the cracked central dome, carrying faint trails of sulfur, mineral dust, and pale shredded moss up toward the dim, steaming surface. Pressed against the freezing high-altitude ceiling, it sheds a fraction of its heat, drifts outward through the frosted crowns of the cane-forests, and sinks slowly back down along the raw, unweathered outer cliffs, rolling at last through the terraced streets and hanging basket-wards before returning to the hot seams at the center. Hours pass with scarcely a frond stirring more than a hand's breadth. Seasons pass with little more change than a slightly thicker gray cast to the overhead slush and a new millimeter of pale stone added to every mooring line—a whole warm, unfinished town perched near the roof of the mountains, quietly thickening in the dim water as the ground beneath it finishes being born.
