The first thing that hits you is the sound, and the second is the light—both of them flat, unsoftened, and far too large for the body to hold.

There is no cloud anywhere in the sky. At this altitude the atmosphere is thin and cold, stripped of moisture down to the last trace, so the sun arrives as a hard white glare that cuts razor-edged shadows into every ridge and hollow. The wind coming across the highland plateau is dry enough to draw the salt out of your lips in minutes, sharp with cold and smelling faintly of ozone, crushed chalk, and hot resin. And through that clear, freezing air pours a wall of noise so thick you feel it in your sternum before your ears can sort it into parts: a continuous, screaming whistle of wind across millions of hollow apertures; beneath it, a deep, staggered *thump—hiss—thump* vibrating up through the soles of your feet like distant stamp mills; and cutting across both, the dry crack of static discharges snapping in broad daylight.

Look down, and there is no stone beneath you, nor dirt, nor sand. The ground itself is plant—a continental crust of living, compressed, bone-dry vegetation hundreds of meters thick, weathered by ages of wind into the likeness of badlands.

You stand on a pale, fluted pavement of tightly braided root-fiber and silica-hardened bark, scoured smooth in wide sweeping terraces and cut by deep, sinuous canyons. No river ever carved those gorges. For millennia, dry gales loaded with pulverized pollen and quartz dust have planed down the living shelf, exposing strata in the canyon walls: bands of ancient, petrified pith at the bottom, layered beneath thick beds of corky dead tissue, honeycombed in the upper hundred feet with active, air-filled vascular conduits the width of a human torso. Every surface is dry as paper and hard as seasoned oak—bleached bone-white where the sun hits full, tarnished copper and oxidized verdigris in the sheltered grooves where the flora concentrates metals leached from the deep bedrock far below.

Across this vast, undulating floor of living crust, the horizon lies impossibly far away, uncluttered and bright. The vertical world here is sparse. You can look out across ten kilometers of open, wind-scoured air before your eye catches the next upright presence: a lone rotor-spire standing eighty meters high on a raised swell of the plateau, its crown spinning so fast in the jet stream that its stiff, bronze-edged fronds blur into a shimmering disc. Farther off, spaced out across the immense pale expanse like lone derricks on a dry sea, stand four or five more, each tiny against the curvature of the highland, each trailing a faint plume of yellow chaff downwind.

Up close, a spire makes the physics of this place unmistakable. Without a drop of water to drive sap or swell a cell, the vegetation runs on air pressure, friction, and charge.

The spire’s trunk is not solid wood, but a clustered bundle of rigid, hollow chimneys banded with dark, iron-rich lignin. At its crown, a collar of smooth, naturally secreted graphite-waxy scales forms a dry bearing around which the vast ribbed canopy rotates continuously in the gale. You hear it before you reach its shadow—a high, keening mechanical whine, punctuated by a rhythmic clatter as internal ratchets engage and slip. That torque drives a thick central tap-shaft straight down into the plateau. Beneath the crust, unseen but felt in every bone, great leathery bellows-chambers expand and contract with each revolution, compressing thin highland air and forcing it through miles of subterranean root-pipes.

Every few seconds, somewhere out on the pale flats, a pressure-valve in the living floor blows open with a deafening pneumatic *shriek*, venting a geyser of fine mineral dust and spent gas twenty feet into the bright cold air before snapping shut with a wooden clap. Deep underground, those air-blasts do the work that water does elsewhere: they drive fine abrasive grit against the buried stone basement of the continent, milling raw rock into dust; they lift that mineral flour up through the vascular pipes in pressurized pulses; and they force dry, viscous resins—warmed by the friction of the pumps—out to the growing tips.

The friction of dry wind, dry fiber, and rushing dust across a continent-sized plateau generates static electricity on a scale that warps the air. The hairs on your forearms stand permanently upright. Running along the ridges of the eroded gullies are low, sparse hedges of copper-veined filaments that act like natural collector arrays. You watch violet-white arcs leap ten feet from a spinning spire’s tip to a grounded patch of metallic scrub with the report of a rifle shot, leaving a brief, stinging whiff of ozone. The flora does not merely survive the charge; it harvests it. Inside darkened nodular chambers buried just under the crust, steady electrical currents crack mineral salts and fix atmospheric nitrogen in tiny, humming furnaces of living tissue, releasing heat that keeps the deep root-engines from freezing brittle in the highland chill.

Nothing about this landscape is calm, even though its canyons and terraces are ancient. The whole plateau exists in a state of roaring, high-speed turmoil.

The winds here do not blow steady for long; they shear, drop, and slam sideways as continental weather systems—made not of rainclouds, but of towering, sunlit walls of golden pollen, ash-fine silica, and crackling static—sweep across the expanse. When a sudden cross-gust strikes a spire spinning near its limit, the sound changes to a terrifying, tortured groan. Thick internal brake-pads of fibrous cork clamp down, throwing off trails of smoking dust. Sometimes they hold; sometimes a whole crown shears away in an explosion of splinters and whistling fronds, tumbling end over end for a mile across the empty flats while the decapitated trunk screams like a broken steam pipe, blasting pressurized chaff skyward until emergency resin-glands foam over the wound and seal it hard in minutes.

Farther out, where a dust-front rolls in, the clear blue sky turns a blinding, metallic amber. Hundreds of static arcs begin firing at once across the advancing wall, stitching the air with jagged white light. Here and there, a discharge hits a pocket of vented resin-vapor and ignites a furious, brief flare—bright orange against the pale bark—only for the ferocious gale to tear the flame flat and snuff it out within heartbeats. Already, before the blackened ring has even cooled, coiled pale tendrils are unspooling from the neighboring crust, racing across the scorched patch to claim the liberated ash and copper salts. Destruction and assembly happen at the same frantic tempo, an endless, howling churn that never breaks the underlying balance of the highland.

Through all this bright emptiness and noise, animal life moves in surprising abundance—never crowded, always spread along the wind-lines and tucked into the roaring margins, built to match the hardness of the substrate.

Down in the wind-carved gullies, where the worst of the shear passes overhead, wide-spaced herds of heavy, low-slung grazers pick their way across the pale crust. They are broad-backed, armored in overlapping plates of dull, insulating silica-horn that shed static without flinching, their wide hooves heavily ballasted with dense bone so no sudden downdraft can lift them off their feet. They walk nose-down against the roar, using chisel-like front jaws to crack open the pressurized resin-blisters that bulge along the canyon walls. Whenever one punctures a blister, a pressurized spray of pale waxy sap hisses out, and the animal clamps its broad snout tight over the vent to swallow the rich dry emulsion before it hardens.

Above them, riding the violent eddies that spill off the spinning spires, wheel flocks of narrow-winged hunters—sleek, leathery gliders whose long, stiff pinions vibrate with a metallic hum in the thin air. They never flap; they tilt and knife through the turbulence at startling speeds, snatching up heavy, winged seed-pods fired from the spires’ crowns with a sharp *pop*, or stooping on the swarms of tiny, silver-dusted pollinators that ride the electrostatic currents. Those insects gather in glittering drifts along charged copper-stalks until the voltage peaks, then launch all at once on the repulsive pulse, carried hundreds of yards downwind in shimmering, crackling clouds.

And inside the ground itself—you see them whenever a pressure-valve snaps open or a canyon wall sloughs away—live whole populations of sleek, segmented runners with polished black shells. They use the hollow pneumatic root-conduits as a continental transit network, tucking their limbs tight to let the sudden air-blasts shoot them down the dark vascular tunnels at dizzying speeds, braking with hooked spines when they sense the warmth of a nitrogen-furnace or a fresh fracture rich in ground mineral meal.

Stand still on a high lip of one of the wind-planed terraces and look out as the sun angles lower, turning the bright glare to a fierce, horizontal white-gold. There is no wall anywhere, no road, no carved mark of any hand—yet the whole vast, cool highland thrums and spins and hammers like an unroofed engine the size of a hemisphere. Dust-devils of bright chaff twist and vanish across kilometers of open air. Distant spires blur and sing in the cold wind. Beneath your feet, the ancient living crust shudders with the steady, tireless beat of subterranean bellows, grinding dry rock in the dark, trading wind for spark, spark for metal, metal for bone-dry leaf—loud, clear, cold, and utterly awake under an empty sky.
