Up here, the air is thin enough to make every breath feel unfinished, yet it arrives warm in the throat—dry, oven-flat, smelling faintly of struck flint and bitter sulfur. Darkness holds the highland almost completely. Above, the sky is a vast, bruised black where only the sharpest stars pierce a drifting veil of high grit; below, the land drops away in sheer, unseen falls, terrace after terrace descending into a gulf that gives back no light and no echo.

It is an unfinished country, still forcing itself upward. Nothing here has ever been smoothed by rain or softened by frost. Walk ten paces in any direction and every surface underfoot has the raw, surgical edge of rock broken this morning: black basalt sheared along flat planes, wedges of dull volcanic glass, stepped ledges of iron-gray slate whose corners can part leather. There is no soil, no sand rounded by tumbling, no riverbeds or gullies—only angular scree and immense tilted slabs thrusting out of the dark like the blades of a rising spine. The scale of it stretches wide across miles of open, stepped benches and parallel ridges, separated by long, narrow rifts that split the plateaus like drawn seams.

The warmth comes from underneath. Press a palm to a flat shelf of stone and it holds the steady, dry heat of a hearth hours after the coals are raked out. Along the edges of the deeper rifts, the rock is warmer still, radiating invisible curtains of air that shudder upward into the night. Lean over one of those black trenches and wait for the eyes to widen; far down, unreachable and muffled by rising dust, a sullen dull-cherry glow sometimes breathes along the bottom seam—never enough to cast a shadow up on the rims, only enough to stain the rising plumes of soot a faint, bruised rust before the dark swallows them whole.

The strange thing—the thing that takes longest to understand—is how furiously the place is moving, and how little noise it makes doing it.

Across the wide valley, an entire buttress of newly lifted slate can lose its footing and shear away in a slow-seeming cataract of black wedges. A shroud of fine, dry powder boils hundreds of feet into the dark sky, lit for a blink by the sparks of colliding flint. Yet because the air up at this height is too thin to carry thunder, no roar follows. Seconds later, only a thin, papery rustle arrives across the gulf, accompanied by a dull shudder traveling through the soles of the feet and up into the teeth. The highland is in constant upheaval—slabs tilting over the course of an afternoon, broad aprons of sharp scree suddenly liquefying into dry avalanches, hairline cracks zipping across an open terrace with a muted *tink* like cooling glass—and almost all of it unfolds in a vast, pressurized hush.

Between the tremors, the thermal drafts do their own restless work. Columns of hot air tear upward out of the rifts and collide with colder currents skimming the high ridges, twisting into lean, silent dust-devils that stalk across the open benches. They lift sheets of razor-fine mineral flakes, whirl them into dark funnels that briefly blot out the stars, and drop them miles away with the soft hiss of dry salt hitting slate. The ground never settles long enough to keep a smooth skin; footprints in the drifted black powder are blurred out within the hour by creeping tremors and passing drafts, or swallowed altogether when a bench shifts half a hand's width downslope.

Nothing walks here, and nothing flies. There are no burrows tucked beneath the overhangs, no droppings, no feathers caught in the jagged teeth of the scree, no small scuttling sounds when a stone is overturned. Even the quietness has no watchfulness in it. Yet the rock is not entirely sterile. Along the warm lips of certain fissures, where faint wisps of mineral vapor rise steadily out of the crust, a pale, brittle fur sometimes takes hold—dry, chalk-colored filaments and papery scales that cling directly to the rawest stone. It has no green in it, no sap, no roots to grip with; it drinks heat and sulfurous air instead of water or light. Brush it with a knuckle and it flakes into bitter ash, yet whenever a fresh seam splits open and cools just past glowing, those faint pale crusts begin creeping along its edges within days, only to be torn apart the next time the rock heaves.

Scattered sparsely across these open expanses, miles apart from one another, are the marks of hands.

They are nothing grand—no carved blocks, no mortared walls, no sign that metal, timber, or rope ever existed here. Everything is dry-stacked from the sharp debris lying immediately to hand. Tucked into the lee of a massive black rib stands a hunched shelter barely wide enough for a single crouching body, built of overlapping slate plates wedged tight without chinking. Inside, the floor is bare, warm rock, swept clean of powder by the constant drafts threading the joints; there is no soot on the ceiling, for no one here would ever need a fire. Half a mile down the same tilted bench sits a knee-high ring of upright shards filled with drifted grit, half its circumference already slumped outward where a recent tremor cracked the slab beneath it.

Most common, and strangest, are the balances. Out on the wide, exposed terraces where the view opens across three ridges at once, single heavy plates of flat basalt have been hoisted and propped carefully atop three pointed wedges. Dozens of them lie collapsed in flat heaps where the ground pitched too hard, indistinguishable at a glance from ordinary rubble; a few still stand, impossibly poised, shivering faintly whenever a distant rift widens. Whether they were left as markers of footing that once held still, or gauges to measure how fast a shelf is tilting, or simply small stubborn arguments against a world that refuses to finish settling, there is no one in sight to say. Whoever stacks these stones leaves no tools behind and wears no lasting roads between their cairns.

To move through this place is to read heat and tension instead of landmarks. A ridge that looks permanent against the stars may already be ticking softly along its base, the dry metallic *clink-clink* of rock under strain warning that its face is about to peel off into the dark. Safer ground feels slightly cooler underfoot, older by a season or two, its seams already edged with that fragile, chalky crust. Yet even the older shelves are barely born, sharp-angled and temporary, waiting to be lifted higher or broken back down into scree.

Night holds without thinning, the dry heat keeps rising from the dark floors, and across the immense quiet distances, the mountain keeps building itself and shrugging itself apart—hushed, raw, and utterly unbothered by the small balanced stones left trembling on its back.
