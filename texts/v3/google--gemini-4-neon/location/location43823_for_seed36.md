You reach the valley by going down, not out. Far beneath the crust, where you would expect cold stone and blackness, the earth opens into a sunken trough several miles wide, and the first thing that strikes you is the glare.

There is no sky here, only a low, vaulted ceiling of raw, newly fractured mineral—vast interlocking plates of clear gypsum and fibrous selenite that have never known rain or wind. Their facets are razor-clean, looking as though the bedrock split apart ten minutes ago and then stopped. Through miles of vertical crystal grain, daylight from some unseen surface is piped straight down and scattered across the roof until the whole overhead vault burns a flat, featureless, blinding white. It never dims, never angles, never reddens. There are no clouds to pass across it and no dusk to relieve it. Down in the basin, the air sits at a heavy, breathless heat, the kind of heat you feel inside a closed glasshouse at noon, pressed gently against your eyelids and the backs of your hands.

Below that incandescent ceiling lies a world made almost entirely of plant.

There is no soil anywhere in the valley—no silt, no humus, no loose grit, because nothing here has ever weathered down into dust. Where the underlying geology shows through, it juts up in fresh, knife-edged ribs of pale basalt and unrounded quartz, completely bare of talus. Everywhere else, living botanical mass has poured directly over the sharp stone and become the ground itself. You do not walk on earth; you walk on a dense, springy lattice of braided pale-green roots, overlapping waxy plates, and pressurized hollow stems that have fused into thick terraces, ramps, and tiered ridges stretching away into the bright haze of the far wall.

The vegetation fills the volume in tight, suffocatingly lush layers, yet it has none of the dripping, ragged softness of a jungle. Under the relentless white heat, everything has grown thick-skinned, turgid, and strangely pale. The upper canopies—which climb halfway toward the luminous mineral roof on ribbed, columnar stalks—are bleached to chalky jade, chartreuse, and silver-olive. Their leaves are broad, leather-stiff shields coated in a heavy, matte wax that reflects the glare back upward. Beneath them, packed shoulder to shoulder so closely that you have to turn sideways to pass between the trunks, stand secondary tiers of folded fan-fronds, hexagonal rosette-towers, and dense curtains of aerial roots as thick as wrists, all pressed nearly touching in the crowded warmth.

Water exists here only as a secret held close to the body. There is no rain, no stream, no mist rolling across the valley floor. Instead, the dampness is trapped inside the tight spaces between the layers. Push past the sun-baked outer shields into the lower galleries and the air turns warm and clammy, smelling faintly of bruised pith, warm beeswax, and clean mineral stone. Along the cool, shaded undersides of the giant horizontal leaves, rows of clear condensation beads hang suspended—heavy, bright, and tremblingly still. Shallow pockets of warm, pale-amber water collect in the cupped joints where broad stalks meet, and in the narrow, razor-edged seams where fresh stone pokes up through the root-mat. If you press your palm down into the fibrous floor, it yields slightly and dampens your skin with a slow, lukewarm seep before springing right back into shape.

What makes the valley quietly unsettling is not its heat or its buried brightness, but its absolute, locked stillness.

Nothing sways. There is no draft moving down the length of the trough, not even the faintest thermal rising from the bleached crowns. More than that, nothing seems to be becoming anything else. There is no fallen litter rotting in the crevices between the roots, no yellowing fronds, no fresh shoots uncurling from the tips of the stems. Across the entire mile-wide expanse, half-opened pale buds remain cupped at the exact same angle; bruised sap-beads sit glossy and unhardened on bark; a row of water droplets clings to the lip of a waxy leaf without growing large enough to fall. The geology below is raw and unfinished, as if caught in the instant of its emergence, and the vegetation that rushed in to swallow it has hit a state of pure, airtight equilibrium—living, turgid, humming with stored heat, yet frozen at the peak of its own fullness.

In this dense, padded silence, sounds are rare and oddly sharp. The spongy walls of living fiber swallow echoes before they can travel more than a few paces. Mostly there is only the warm, pressurized quiet of your own breathing, and a faint, almost imagined hum from the illuminated crystal roof high above. Then, every few minutes, somewhere out across the terraces, a single heavy bead of condensation finally loses its grip and drops onto a taut leaf below with a hollow, wooden *tock*. Occasionally a thick stem shifts a fraction of a millimeter under its own internal pressure, giving off a dry, leathery creak.

Animal life is scarce enough that you could cross half the basin without spotting a moving thing. When you do see it, it is small, pale, and economized to the stillness. Pressed flat against the damp undersides of the ribbed stalks are translucent, wax-skinned lizards no longer than a finger, their ribs barely rising, waiting motionless beside the cupped leaf-pools. High up on the sunlit shields, solitary beetles with carapaces polished like tiny fragments of mirror sit motionless in the glare, reflecting the white ceiling so cleanly that they disappear until one shifts a leg with a faint, dry click. Across the narrow gaps between stacked stems, hair-thin spiders string single, unbranching silk threads—not webs for catching prey, only taut lines along which microscopic droplets of dew gather, which the spiders drink slowly, leg by leg, without ever leaving the shade.

Scattered through the wide expanse of the valley—never clustered into anything like a village, always separated by long stretches of unbroken plant-mass—are the marks of hands.

None of them show the bite of metal or the dust of a chisel. On a high terrace where the root-floor bridges a sharp cleft of raw stone, someone has stacked flat, naturally cleaved mineral shards into a low, dry-laid crescent wall, using only the clean geometric fractures the rock already had. Half a mile down the slope, tucked beneath the cantilevered overhang of a massive rosette, stands a solitary bower made by braiding living aerial roots together and coaxing them into a tight, domed ribcage, its narrow doorway sealed along the sill with darkened plant resin. Elsewhere, a narrow footpath of knotted, calcified vines runs straight along the spine of a jagged ridge before ending abruptly at a shallow, warm basin lined with overlapping waxy leaves folded neatly into cups.

You cannot tell whether these structures were left behind yesterday or ten thousand years ago. Nothing has overgrown them; nothing has weathered them down. The braided vines have neither rotted nor thickened past the knots that bind them. The stacked mineral plates keep their razor edges without a grain of dust gathered in their joints. Looking out from one of those quiet, hand-woven alcoves across the vast, crowded sea of pale-green architecture—mile after mile of dense, terraced foliage gleaming under a white subterranean ceiling that never shifts—the valley feels less like a ruin and less like a wilderness than a single, enormous breath drawn in long ago and held, hot and bright and damp, forever.
