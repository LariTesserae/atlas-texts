The heat arrives before anything can be seen. It is not the dry blast of a desert, nor the sharp radiate of fire, but a heavy, saturated warmth—just past fever—that presses evenly against the skin from every side. The air holds all the water it can carry and then a little more, shedding it continuously onto every surface in a slow, oily sweat.

Underfoot, the ground is low, flat, and submerged. Warm water lies across the basin in a sheet that shifts between ankle-deep and knee-deep, slick over packed black silt and broad, worn paving slabs. The basin stretches out for miles—wide as a valley floor, sunk below whatever rim might enclose it—yet nothing can be made out at distance. Darkness sits low and thick here, matte and sourceless. There is no sky to speak of, no moon, no stars, no horizon line separating earth from air. After an hour, the eyes adjust only enough to catch the faintest differences in gloom: the dull, bruised sheen of standing water, the paler chalk-grey of mineral crusts along a submerged curb, the darker bulk of a wall rising out of the shallows twenty paces away.

It is a city, though spread so thin across the steaming flats that most of it is emptiness.

Between the structures lie enormous open plazas of shallow water and mud, hundreds of yards across, unbroken except for long, low embankments and straight drainage channels cut into the basin floor. Where the built forms do appear, they are unmistakable in their intent: stepped cisterns, wide arcades, tiered plinths, causeways laid on strict perpendicular axes, sunken reservoirs ringed with worn benches, aqueducts running arrow-straight into the dark until their piers vanish in the haze. Yet nothing here was made with a tool.

Run a hand along a pillar or a stair-riser and there are no chisel marks, no mortar lines, no fitted blocks, no timber, no iron. Every lintel, cornice, and gutter is continuous with the slab beneath it, formed from a single dense body of dark mudstone, compacted clay, and pale travertine sinter. The corners are rounded and velvety with age, deeply weathered by millennia of warm runoff, settled into the floor of the basin like boulders in a riverbed. It looks less like a settlement that was constructed and abandoned than one that the low ground itself precipitated out of hot mineral water, layer by slow layer, obeying an old and stubborn memory of municipal order.

The basin is never still, though it makes almost no sound.

Every few hours, a silent tide swells from beneath the flagstones. There are no waves and no current you can easily name; the water simply deepens by half a foot, rising with a barely audible lip-smack against the steps of the plazas and filling the dry upper tiers of the sluices. This fresh water is hotter than the surface layer—near scalding around the submerged vents—and sends slow ribbons of thicker vapor curling upward into the dark. After a while, the level sinks back, leaving the low walls gleaming and weeping.

On those weeping walls, gravity loses its certainty. Condensation gathers in fat beads along the weathered arcades and, instead of running down, often creeps upward in slow, glistening threads, collecting along the flat parapets before spilling silently off the other side in sheets. Elsewhere, rain falls in strict geometric columns—warm, heavy, vertical—confined entirely to the interior of a roofless courtyard while the open flats a step beyond the threshold remain dry-aired and still. You hear these isolated rains before you reach them: a hushed, steady pattering on black water, muffled quickly by the thick air, vanishing the moment you walk past the unseen line of the wall.

Nothing lives here that moves on legs or wings or fins. There are no insects whining in the steam, no frogs in the canals, no fish turning in the reservoirs, no birds roosting in the dark arches. The quiet is deep and biological-empty, broken only by the slow mechanics of water and stone: a heavy drop striking a flooded vault with a hollow *thup*; the soft hiss of hot vapor slipping through a narrow sluice; occasionally, far off across the drowned plains, the low granular sigh of a weathered ledge softening and slumping back into warm mud.

Life is not entirely absent, only pared back to sparse, lightless stubbornness. Along the seams of the causeways and the warm rims of the cisterns grow thin rows of pale, leafless stalks—waxy and hollow, cold to the touch despite the heat, as if they drink warmth out of the air and convert it into flesh. In the shaded concavities under arches, flat mats of translucent, leathery moss cling to the wet mineral crust, pulsing faintly when the hot tide rises over them. Between these scattered colonies lie vast stretches of bare silt, bare stone, and barren water, uncluttered and stark.

The longer one moves through the basin, the less dependable its geometry becomes.

Distance does not hold steady across the wide, dark avenues. A colonnade dimly perceived across an open sheet of water may take twenty minutes of slow wading to reach, its pillars refusing to grow any larger until, with a sudden tightening of the air, the nearest stone is an arm’s length from your face. Walk a straight canal embankment with your hand trailing the curb and you will arrive back at the same hexagonal cistern you left—except now it has five sides, the steps descend from the opposite edge, and the pale reeds along its lip are thicker than before.

Even the water refuses to reflect what stands above it. Look down into the mirror-flat shallows during the slack between tides, and the faint grey-dark sheen holds more architecture than the air does. You can make out stacked galleries, high bridges spanning the empty plazas, tiered roofs crowding inward over the avenues—a denser, taller city hanging upside down beneath the surface. Look up, and there is only the blind, hot dark. Yet if you drag a foot across the reflection, breaking the glass of the water, the air overhead responds: a sudden shift in pressure against the ears, a brief draft of drier heat as though a ceiling opened or closed just above your head, followed by a single warm drop falling from nothing.

The city is not frozen in this strangeness; it is quietly at work. Over days, or perhaps over hours—time feels as soft here as the wet clay—the basin revises itself. Where a broad plaza lay empty, a low rectangular sill begins to harden out of the simmering silt, its edges already worn smooth as if it had stood for centuries. An ancient viaduct standing alone in the shallows slowly loses its middle piers, not collapsing in rubble, simply thinning into slurry between one thermal swell and the next until the two ends face each other across a gap of unbroken dark water, still weeping upward into the empty air.

No light ever breaks over the rim of the basin. The heat does not fade, nor does it boil over. Across the wide, sunken miles, the water rises and falls, the isolated rains shift from courtyard to courtyard, the sparse pale reeds drink the steam, and the unbuilt city continues its slow, hushed conversation with itself—endlessly weathered, endlessly remade, stretched thin beneath a darkness that feels as warm and close as breath.
