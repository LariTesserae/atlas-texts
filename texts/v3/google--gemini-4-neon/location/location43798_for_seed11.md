The noise arrives before anything else has time to become a shape. It is not a steady roar, though at first the ear tries to flatten it into one; it is a violent, layered hammering of warm air against ancient stone, loud enough to blur vision and rattle the back teeth. From deep below comes a continuous, gut-heavy thrumming, as if an immense hollow drum were being rubbed by a wet thumb. Above that sits the tearing, canvas-rip scream of shear-winds colliding in open space, punctuated every few seconds by dry, concussive slaps where opposing currents collapse into one another and reverse.

Around the noise, there is almost nothing.

For miles in every direction, the world is made mostly of bright, ordinary daylight and empty air. Across a gulf too wide to easily measure, the last bones of an unimaginably old range of rock stand isolated in the void—not jagged peaks, not young cliffs, but the ultimate survivors of millions of years of grinding and dissolution. Only the hardest quartz-veined cores and iron-bound sills remain, worn smooth as river-tumbled femur bones. They rise out of a pale, sun-washed haze thousands of feet below, lifting as thin vertical sails, leaning pillars, cantilevered shelves, and colossal perforated arches separated by vast, dizzying reaches of nothingness. Almost the entire volume of this valley is hollow sky.

The stone itself has reached its final, settled geometry. Every sharp corner was sanded away ages ago. The faces of the spires are pale buff and rusted ochre, fluted with long, silky channels and bored clean through by smooth-rimmed eyelets and vertical chimneys. Those chimneys are the throats of the noise. Warm air, heated in the unseen depths beneath the haze, boils upward in chaotic, surging columns, slamming through the hollow rock and shrieking out of the wind-bored holes. Touch a stone fin with an open palm and it is distinctly warm—sun-baked on its broad faces, faintly humming throughout its whole mass, vibrating like the body of a struck bell.

And yet, clinging to these ancient, howling skeletons, everything is riotously, furiously green.

Wherever the stone widens into a ledge, a saddle, a cracked crown, or even a pitted leeward crease, thick knots of vegetation erupt in dense, tangled abundance. There is no stillness in any of it. Every plant here is built for perpetual battery. Broad, deeply ribbed fan-leaves of glossy dark olive whip back and forth on thick, rubbery stems, snapping loudly when a gust flips direction. Behind them crouch dense, springy thickets of copper-stemmed brush, bloated rosettes with waxy, spoon-shaped leaves, and sprawling mats of coarse, bright-emerald moss that feels heavy and fibrous, like wet wool. Great curtains of pale air-roots and braided vines spill off the edges of the stone shelves—except they rarely hang down. Caught in the upward surge of the warm thermals, whole beards of tangled root and fern stream straight outward into the empty sky, or stand upright like thrashing green flames, twisting wildly as invisible eddies tear past.

It is not a wet place, not truly. There are no rivers, no waterfalls, no standing lakes anywhere in the bright expanse. Yet the leaves glisten, and the air smells sharply of crushed sap, warm stone, and faint mineral damp. The moisture arrives in violent, erratic deliveries. Down in the haze-choked abyss, warm vapor gathers and gets sheared off in ragged, spinning plumes that shoot upward at dizzying speed. A torn ribbon of cloud will slam headlong into a spire out of a clear blue sky. Instantly, the stone darkens and sweats; the furry moss swells; the waxy rosettes brim with tepid, frothy water; droplets fly horizontally off the thrashing fronds like spray off a ship’s bow. Thirty seconds later, a dry cross-draft slices through, tearing the mist to shreds and carrying it away across the chasm. The stone steams faintly in the normal, bright afternoon light and dries again, leaving only the water trapped inside cupped stone hollows, spongy bark, and swollen botanical bladders.

Because the air is never stable for more than a few heartbeats, the geography of this lushness is constantly rewriting itself.

Nothing here stays rooted forever. As the moss-mats and braided fern-thickets grow thick and water-heavy on the narrow rock shelves, they begin to catch too much wind. When a sudden vertical shear hits from underneath, you can hear—even over the deafening roar—the wet, fibrous screech of an entire hillside tearing loose. A raft of intertwined roots, damp peat, bright lichen, and thrashing brush the size of a cottage peels cleanly off the satin-smooth stone, flips end over end, and is flung out into the miles of sunlit emptiness. It tumbles wildly across the chasm, trailing a green blizzard of shredded leaves and spiraling droplets of water, lifted high by a rising funnel, then slammed downward by a sudden pocket of sinking air. Minutes later, half a mile away, it collides with the flank of another stone sail. Half of it bursts apart and rains down toward the haze; the rest snags on a weathered rib, its barbed, spring-loaded tendrils already hooking into micro-fissures, its broken stems bleeding sticky white latex that hardens in the warm breeze and glues the raft to its new cliff.

Everywhere across the open valley, if you watch the bright space between the distant spires, you can see these drifting green wrecks—some small as baskets, some vast and trailing long tails of vine—spinning lazily in distant vortices or whipping horizontally through rock arches like debris in a flood. The spires trade their forests back and forth endlessly. A bare, sun-bleached pillar one week is draped in a sprawling, blossoming mat of crimson-tipped creepers the next; a lush shelf is stripped back to bare, gleaming ochre stone in a single afternoon’s upheaval.

Even the stones are not wholly exempt. Pockets of ancient, pea-sized gravel collect in the wind-hollowed bowls, and when a tight whirlwind drops into a notch, the pebbles lift in a rattling cloud, stinging like hail against the leathery foliage before clattering down into the void. Sometimes the upward blasts carry smooth, hollow mineral nodules out of the deep haze—pale, wind-pitted stones no bigger than eggs that emit a brief, startling descending whistle as they arc past a ledge and vanish again.

In all this bright, furious, deafening emptiness, animals are rare, and the ones that exist look carved out of the same stubbornness as the rock.

Hours can pass without a sign of one. Then, out in the middle of the chasm where two thermals grind against each other, a solitary glider appears. It is wide-winged and strangely angular—not sleek like a swallow, but heavily built, with ragged, deeply slotted pinions of dark leathery membrane stretched over thick, hinged wing-bones. It never beats its wings. Instead, it folds and snaps them into sharp, asymmetrical geometries, riding the violent boundaries where rising air meets falling air, rolling effortlessly through turbulence that would snap an ordinary bird’s spine. When a fresh root-raft tears free from a distant needle, the glider folds into a heavy dart, plunges through the howling shear, and strikes the tumbling greenery in midair, tearing open the damp peat with hooked feet to snatch pale, bloated grubs before banking violently away from the cliff face.

Closer in, tucked into the narrow seams where two ancient rock strata meet, live flat, coin-shaped grazers with ridged, dust-colored shells clamped tight against the stone. You could run a hand over a dozen of them and mistake them for mineral nodules. Only when a cloud-shred bursts against the rock do their shells lift a fraction of an inch, flicking out pale, bristled tongues to rake condensed moisture and wind-blown pollen off the wet stone before clamping shut again the instant the gust shifts. Occasionally, in the brief half-second lull before a pressure-slap hits, a pale, long-toed lizard darts out from under a fern mat, gulps a trembling bead of water from a leaf-cup—the droplet literally hovering and buzzing on the vibrating surface of the leaf—and vanishes back into the roots before the foliage slams flat.

And there are other things, rarer still than the lizards, tucked into the quietest cracks of the ancient rock: marks that were not made by wind or roots.

You have to look closely to spot them, because the scale of the place swallows small work completely. On the leeward hip of a broad stone bridge—where an overhang of eroded quartzite deflects the worst of the upward blast into a spinning, tolerable eddy—sits a low half-circle of stacked flat stones, no higher than a crouching shoulder. No mortar holds it; the stones are simply wedged tightly together, their gaps stuffed with dried, resin-soaked moss. Behind that windbreak, a shallow hollow has been chipped slightly deeper by hand, the tool-marks old and half-softened by grit.

Driven deep into a fissure beside the hollow are three pegs of hammered soft copper, green with oxidation, their heads mushroomed from stone mallets. Around them are knotted thick, frayed braids of dark plant-fiber rope, sticky with boiled sap to keep them from unravelling in the endless shaking. A bundle of fire-hardened wooden wedges rests beneath a heavy flat boulder, alongside a few chipped flakes of dark flint and a cracked gourd-like bladder scraped clean to hold mist-water.

Beside the pegs, pinned firmly to the bare bedrock with rawhide lashing and stone shims, lies a curved slab of dense, polished hardwood, about the length of a forearm and hollowed along its underside like a shallow trough. Deep notches of varying widths are cut into its upper rim. It looks at first like an abandoned tool, or a broken piece of a cradle. Yet if you kneel behind the low wall and press your bare palm—or your jawbone—against the wood, the chaos of the deafening valley suddenly sorts itself out. The wood drinks the vibration directly out of the ancient stone spine of the bridge, stripping away the screaming surface wind and translating the deep, sub-bass groaning of the chimneys below into crisp, distinct pulses. You can feel a massive thermal building miles down in the haze half a minute before it arrives; you can feel the slow, heavy shudder of a distant vortex crossing the gulf; you can tell, through your teeth and knuckles, when the shear across the next gap is about to slacken for ten breaths—just long enough for someone lashed to a braided vine-line to scramble across a knife-edge rib toward the next spire.

Half a mile away, on the crown of a leaning ochre needle that looks wholly unreachable, a single thin pole of stripped wood stands jammed into a crevice. Three tattered strips of cured hide tied to its tip snap so violently in the warm wind that they blur into a pale horizontal smudge against the clear sky. There is no path visible leading to it, no bridge unbroken, no smoke, no second figure anywhere across the ten miles of sunlit chasm—only the solitary marker whipping furiously in the bright air, the pale bones of the ancient rock humming beneath a sea of thrashing green, and the endless, warm, deafening riot of the sky.
