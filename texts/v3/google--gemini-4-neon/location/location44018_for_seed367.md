Up on the crown of the highland, there is almost nothing left for time to take. The rock—an ancient, iron-dark gneiss—has been ground down over ages until even the ridges have forgotten they were ever sharp. It rolls out in wide, bald swells, satin-smooth under the scouring wind, bleached to rust and dry ochre beneath a thin, pale sky. There are no creeks here, no snowbanks, not even a dry streambed worth the name. A traveler crossing the plateau hears only the high, dry hiss of air moving over bare stone and sees nothing taller than an ankle-high blister of lichen.

Yet where one of those worn domes breaks on its leeward flank, an overhang lips outward like a heavy eyebrow, shielding a deep, wind-carved pocket no wider than a courtyard and scarcely half as deep. A low apron of fallen slabs screens the entrance from the plateau above. You do not see the place until you squeeze past the baffle of rock, drop six paces down a slick, heel-worn trough, and step out of the wind.

The shock is immediate: warmth, dimness, and a wall of noise.

The pocket is a natural oven. All afternoon the sun hammers the bald stone roof overhead, and the ancient rock holds that heat deep in its grain, radiating it gently downward long after the plateau outside goes bitter cold. Trapped beneath the curving ceiling, buffered from the thin highland gale, the air sits heavy and close—dry as chaff, smelling of warm clay, crushed herbs, dry dung, woodsmoke, and the faintly sweet musk of thousands of living bodies packed into a single room of stone.

The light drops to an amber dusk. The overhanging brow cuts the sky down to a narrow horizontal slit along the front edge, so direct sun never reaches the back half of the hollow. What illumination enters arrives sideways and secondhand, bounced off the pale stone lip outside and filtered through hanging curtains of dusty foliage. Near the mouth, motes of grit and drifting seed-fluff burn bright in slanted shafts; twenty paces inward, the cavern deepens into warm umber, punctuated by the tiny orange sparks of tallow lamps set into wall niches.

Inside this single pocket sits an entire town.

It rises in five and six cramped tiers from the uneven rock floor almost to the scalloped ceiling—a honeycomb of stacked chambers, overhanging lofts, cantilevered balconies, and narrow switchback stairs, all wedged so tightly together that the walls lean on one another and on the living stone for support. Nothing here is cut by saw or squared by iron. The walls are hand-packed from dry highland silt bound with chopped stems, ash, and the chalky mineral crust of bird droppings, patted smooth with bare palms until every corner rounds off like dried dough. Roofs are mats of bound scrub-twigs plastered over with clay; the roof of one house serves as the porch and drying floor of the one above it. Ladders of knotty, bone-dry juniper—polished to a greasy sheen by generations of bare feet and climbing paws—link the levels. Between the stacks run alleys so narrow a person must turn sideways to pass a basket, ducking beneath low woven-reed lintels and slung bundles of dried tubers.

Even the craft of the place belongs to flint, bone, fiber, and fire. Down on the lower ledges, women grind hard seeds in hollows worn palm-deep into the bedrock, the steady *shuck-shuck* of river-smooth cobbles vibrating up through the floor. Men split dry gourd-shells with chipped stone knives, twist tough leaf-fibers into cordage, or mend cracked water-jars with heated plant resin. Here and there a hammered copper awl or a thumb-length bronze blade passes from hand to hand, ground down by decades of sharpening and treasured like a tooth.

There is no running water anywhere in the pocket, nor any pool, well, or muddy edge. A handful of clay cisterns tucked into the coolest back crevices hold what little liquid the town owns—harvested at dawn from wide nets of finely knotted hair and plant fiber stretched across the rock mouth to comb the brief, thin highland mist that slips over the rim before sunrise. By the time the sun clears the horizon, the mist is gone, the nets hang dry and rattling, and the air returns to its parched, static crackle.

And yet the hollow is startlingly, riotously green.

Without wind to strip moisture from the leaves, and fed by centuries of rich, dry droppings packed into every ledge and mortar crack, vegetation takes hold of every surface that catches a slant of bounced light. It is not the dripping, glossy lushness of a wet forest, but a thick, dusty, muscular tangle of dry-land flora. Great bearded curtains of silver-grey air-plants hang from the stone brow and drape the rafters, swelling faintly during the dawn mist and spending the day papery and cool. Thorny caper-vines and gnarled rock-figs wedge their roots into hair-thin seams in the masonry, sending cascades of waxy, dark-olive leaves across the clay facades. Every roof-edge and windowsill is crowded with earthenware troughs and woven baskets overflowing with trailing bitter-melons, padded stonecrops, hairy aromatic thyme, and climbing dry-beans. Even the shadowed mortar joints are furred with cushion-mosses that crunch dryly under a fingernail at noon and turn velvety green for a single hour at daybreak. The town looks half-swallowed by its own hung gardens; you push aside curtains of thorny stems just to climb from one landing to the next.

And everything inside those curtains is moving.

In the vast, scoured emptiness of the surrounding plateau, this warm, sheltered pocket is a magnet for every creature that can fly, crawl, or cling. Wildlife does not live *around* the settlement; it saturates it, outnumbering the human inhabitants by thousands to one, occupying every cubic inch the people leave unused—and most of the inches they use as well.

Look up toward the smooth, smoke-darkened stone vault: it is crusted shoulder-to-shoulder with thousands of gourd-shaped mud nests, plastered onto the rock, onto the juniper rafters, even onto the undersides of the upper balconies. Cliff-swifts and rock-martins pour continuously through the sunlit slit at the mouth, banking hard in the dimness, chittering in a sharp, metallic chorus that bounces off the concave ceiling until the air hums with it. Deeper in the dark rear recesses of the overhang, where the swifts will not go, clusters of small bats hang in trembling, leathery grapes, shifting and squeaking in their sleep.

Look at the walls: the warm clay is alive with reptiles. Flat-bodied agamas with rusty throats bob their heads along every sunlit lintel; pale, translucent geckos cling upside-down beneath the eaves, barking softly from the shadows; tiny bronze skinks pour like spilled beads through the dry chaff on the stairs. Behind the woven wall-mats and inside the hollow spaces beneath the floors, colonies of plump, blunt-eared cliff-rodents—half-wild, half-tolerated—whistle and gnaw, darting out at dusk to steal spilled grain before vanishing back into holes worn smooth as keyholes. Underfoot in the narrow central court, small speckled fowl and tame, short-eared rabbits weave through people's ankles, scratching through the dust alongside armies of matte-black beetles that strip any dropped scrap, dead nestling, or shed skin down to clean white bone before it can ever sour.

The sound of the pocket never drops below a roar. It is an acoustic bowl, smooth-walled and tight, so every noise layers on top of the last: the slicing rush of hundreds of wings overhead, the dry rattle of papery vines brushed by a stray eddy, the clicking of geckos, the high trilling of swifts, the sharp alarm-barks of cliff-rodents, the rhythmic thump of stone pestles, children shouting from an upper loft, clay pots clinking, and—just beyond the baffle—the faint, constant hollow whine of the highland wind that cannot get inside.

Nothing here stays still for ten seconds. The pocket runs on a ferocious, compressed pulse of eating and being eaten, woven straight through the human workday.

At mid-morning, when the thermal currents rise off the cliffs outside, slender kestrels stall in the bright slit of sky at the mouth and stoop straight into the dimness, plucking swifts out of the air not ten feet above a potter's head, scattering feathers that drift slowly down into the courtyard. Along the high thatch and inside the tangled caper-vines, pencil-thin rock-snakes slide silently from clay nest to clay nest, raiding eggs until a mob of screaming birds drives them back into the stonework. Papery wasp-nests hang beneath the dry lintels; hunting wasps drag paralyzed caterpillars across the mortar while jumping spiders stalk the midges rising from the compost bins. A lean, long-eared cliff-fox—known to everyone by the notch in its ear—pads casually along the highest roof-parapet at twilight, pausing to watch a boy crack dry nuts before leaping cleanly into a tangle of vines after a slow-moving rodent.

The people neither clear the animals out nor worship them; the pocket is too small for separation. They hang thorn-bundles over their grain-jars to thwart the rodents, smear slick ash around the legs of sleeping-platforms to turn away scorpions and ants, and keep small, bright-eyed lizards tethered or encouraged near the storage baskets to trim down the moths. When a swift's mud nest breaks off a beam, the fallen clay is swept up, crushed, and re-kneaded into wall-plaster. When a hen lays in a crevice, a child reaches in carefully, tapping first with a stick to make sure a coiled viper has not claimed the warmth.

The day turns on two violent shifts of traffic.

At dawn, while the air holds its brief, cool dampness, the people crowd the outer ledges to wring the dew-nets into narrow-necked gourds. At the exact same moment, the nocturnal bats stream inward through the mouth just as the waking swifts explode outward, turning the narrow opening into a five-minute cyclone of colliding wings, sharp cries, and falling dust. The dry mosses swell; the aromatic herbs release a brief, piercing wave of bitter scent; the sluggish lizards drag themselves onto the first slivers of pale orange light.

By afternoon, the stone brow is hot to the touch from above, and the pocket settles into its warm, dusty hum—motes hanging suspended in dim golden bars, flies buzzing over drying strips of squash, the steady murmur of voices and grinding stone beneath the endless avian chatter.

Then dusk drops fast at this height. The slit of sky turns violet, then black. The swifts pack themselves back into their mud jugs, the last late stragglers skimming inches over the people's hair. Oil lamps blink awake up and down the five tiers, turning the hanging vines into huge, trembling shadows on the smooth primordial rock. The air cools just enough for the bats to pour out into the dark highland air, trading places with clouds of pale moths emerging from the foliage. Geckos step out into the lamplight to snap them off the clay walls. Beneath the floors, the whistling of small mammals rises to a chorus, and somewhere in the deepest dry crevice at the back of the pocket, the ancient gneiss gives a faint, settling tick as its sun-baked spine cools by a fraction of a degree.

Step back out through the narrow baffle of boulders, climb six paces up the worn slot, and the sound cuts off as cleanly as shutting a heavy door. The wind hits your face, cold and thin. Ahead stretches the empty, flattened roof of the world—bone-dry, timeless, scrubbed clean of almost every mark—giving no hint that, a stone's throw beneath your boots, a whole packed, chattering, leaf-choked city is breathing warmly in the dark.
