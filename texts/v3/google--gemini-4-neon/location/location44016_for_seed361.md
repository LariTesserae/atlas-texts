From fifty paces out on the tableland, there is nothing to see at all—only the immense, planed-flat expanse of an ancient highland, worn down across eons until every ridge has slumped into low, sun-baked swells of pale rose-ochre stone. The altitude shows in the sky, which is a sharp, dry, cobalt blue, and in the steady rush of thin wind that skims unobstructed across the bare rock. You could walk across this roof of the world for a week and never encounter a sharp edge; the bedrock has settled into its final, pillowed geometry, smooth as old bone.

Then the stone dips. Not a cliff, not a canyon—just a single, shallow bowl pressed into the plateau, thirty or forty paces across and barely deeper than the height of two people, with a rolled, overhanging lip that hides it until you are standing almost on its rim.

The sound hits you before you even look down.

It rises out of the depression like heat from an oven: a dense, reverberating wall of living noise, amplified and bounced back and forth by the smooth, curved walls of the basin. Down inside, out of the plateau wind, the high sun pools into a heavy, drowsy warmth. And though the pocket itself is surprisingly open—most of its floor is bare, satin-worn pale rock, with wide sunlit gaps separating a handful of lush green thickets and a few small, inexplicable objects—every square inch of that openness is violently, overwhelmingly inhabited.

First, you hear the chorus. Thousands of thumb-sized, amber-skinned lizards cling to the warm rock walls and the shaded undersides of leaves, inflating translucent throat-sacs that catch the bright daylight like bubbles of pale glass. They do not call at random; their dry, ringing *clack-chime* sweeps across the basin in rolling acoustic tides, swelling until it vibrates in your teeth, dropping for half a breath, then crashing back from the opposite curve of the stone. Beneath it runs the steady, papery roar of wing-cases—broad, copper-backed insects the size of plums rattling like dry seedpods as they cross the open spaces between plants—layered over the sharp, bickering chatter of dense colonies of gray-furred cliff-dwellers packed into the weathered horizontal seams along the rim. When a gust of plateau wind occasionally spills over the lip, the whole basin seems to inhale and scream at once, flocks of small, needle-beaked birds exploding out of the foliage in tight spirals before dropping back below the wind-line.

It takes a moment, through the sheer riot of movement and sound, to notice why anything is alive up here at all.

Scattered across the basin—spaced well apart, leaving generous stretches of bare floor between them—are four small structures that do not belong to the geological age of the rock, though they have plainly been here nearly as long. They are narrow, curved slats of an utterly matte, warm-gray material, each no longer than a human arm and thin as a knife-spine, suspended waist-high above the stone with nothing beneath them. No struts, no moorings, no hum of machinery. They are simply locked to their coordinates in space. Because the primordial bedrock has slowly weathered and slumped away beneath them over uncounted millennia, they hang at faintly mismatched heights relative to the floor, untouched by wind, untouched by corrosion, indifferent to gravity. When a heavy, long-legged ground-hunter leaps onto one to survey the basin, the slat does not tremble or dip by a hair's breadth; it holds as rigidly as the core of the planet.

Around each suspended slat, the daylight bends in a thin, trembling envelope—barely two inches thick, like heat-shimmer, except that the air inside that skin is completely still. Touch the slat and it is warm as sunlit clay, yet out of that quiet optical boundary, water is constantly being wrung from the dry mountain air. Not cold condensation, and not a steady stream: fat, warm droplets simply materialize along the lower edge of the hovering gray arc, swell until they flash white in the sun, and detach with a slow, heavy rhythm. *Plip. Plip. Plip.*

Beneath each of these four floating arcs, and nowhere else on the vast dry plateau, the world bursts into extravagant, dripping green.

They sit like four lush islands separated by wide yards of bare, warm stone. Broad, waxy leaves as wide as shields crowd tightly together, their veins flushed dark crimson, their glossy surfaces perpetually slick with warm beads of water. Thick cushions of springy emerald moss coat the bedrock beneath the drip-lines, spilling outward into shallow, palm-sized puddles that feed thin, wandering trickles across the stone—trickles that gleam brightly for three or four paces before evaporating cleanly back into the warm air. Twisted dwarf figs with pale, corky bark anchor themselves in the damp seams, their roots spreading out over the open rock like veins, their branches draped in tangled lace-curtains of pale green epiphyte.

Even the air around the artifacts has been colonized. Growing directly out of the wettest edge of the moss mounds, fragile, translucent filaments of a pale chartreuse algae-moss have learned to bridge the gap upward into the still, damp envelope around the floating slats, hanging in shimmering, weightless curtains of wet green thread that sway only when a darting insect punches straight through them.

Nothing in this pocket is wasted, and nothing stays still.

Across the wide, exposed aprons of pale stone between the lush islands, the daily traffic of predator and prey plays out in bright, candid daylight. Because the thickets are sparse and separated by open ground, every crossing is a gamble. Lines of pale, glassy-bodied ants stream in braided highways from one damp mound to the next, carrying torn bits of berry and insect wing. Beside them, small mottled skinks sprint in frantic bursts from shadow to shadow, pausing to gulp droplets from the evaporating edges of the trickles. Watching them from the bare floor—and from the unmoving perches of the floating slats—are the basin's hunters: slender, earless, pale-feathered runners with long, hinged beaks, stalking with a strange, jerky precision across the open rock. Every few seconds one strikes, snapping a singing lizard straight off a warm stone wall or plucking a rattling copper beetle out of midair. Overhead, shadow-flickers cross the bright blue circle of sky as larger raptors from the distant cliffs drop suddenly below the rim, folding their wings to skim the tops of the dwarf figs, sending the chattering furred burrowers diving into the rock seams in a storm of shrill alarm calls.

Look closely at the bare floor where the ants and runners cross, and you find the faintest, oldest traces of whoever left the water-engines behind. Sunk flush into the ancient rose-ochre granite is a broken geometry of hairline filaments—pale, mirror-bright lines thinner than a fingernail, forming half of an arc and a few stray intersections that vanish where the stone has cracked and settled. Millions of footsteps and millennia of wind-borne grit have lowered the rock around them, leaving the metal-or-glass threads standing razor-sharp and proud by a millimeter, unbent and untarnished. Along the eastern wall, there is also a single low alcove—barely large enough to sit inside—not chiselled or blasted out of the bedrock, because its interior is as continuous and glassy as a soap bubble cut in half. Whatever precision hollowed it out is long gone; today its smooth curved ceiling is plastered with mud-wasp nests, its floor piled deep with chewed hulls, damp leaves, and the musky, restless bodies of the cliff-dwellers.

The strange engineering still subtly bends the rules of the pocket, even now. Walk slowly across the open floor along one of the mirror-bright inlays, and the roaring, chaotic soundscape suddenly sorts itself into uncanny focus—an acoustic lensing that makes the wet scrape of a snail's shell on a waxy leaf twenty paces away sound as crisp as if it were beside your ear, while the deafening wall of lizard-song behind you thins to a distant, metallic hum. Take one step off the line, and the full noise crashes back over you, hot and breathless.

As the sun climbs to the top of the sky, the pocket shifts gear. The direct, unfiltered light bakes the open stone until heat rises off the bare patches in faint ribbons, and the warm air inside the bowl thickens. Strangely, the drier and hotter the high plateau becomes outside the rim, the faster the suspended slats seem to work; by early afternoon, the steady *plip* of warm water quickens into an almost continuous patter, darkening the moss, swelling the tiny rock-pools until they spill a few inches farther across the bare floor. The response is immediate. Swarms of tiny, iridescent midges lift off the damp mats in glittering clouds; the bell-lizards swell their throats until the stone walls seem to thrum; the slender hunters abandon their stalk and chase openly across the blazing pale rock, kicking up puffs of dry dust only inches away from the wet green edges of the thickets.

Then a single high cloud drifts across the cobalt sky. For two minutes, the bright sun dims, and the temperature in the sheltered bowl drops by a fraction of a degree. The entire chorus changes key in a single ragged heartbeat. The rattle of wings dies down; the warm droplets slow; the furred colonies spill boldly out of the glassy alcove to strip fallen figs from the open floor while the aerial shadows are gone. Beneath the overhanging lip of ancient stone, a thousand invisible creatures wait in the leaf-axils and rock-cracks, breathing the warm, damp air, ready for the sun to return and set the whole small, loud, impossible engine roaring again.
