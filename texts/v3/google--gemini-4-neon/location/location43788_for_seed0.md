You feel the valley before you can make out a single edge of it—first as a wall of warm, sour-green air pressing against your skin, and then as a physical weight against your eardrums.

It is almost entirely dark. Above the basin hangs a low, unbreaking lid of bruised cloud that swallows whatever sun or moons might exist beyond it, leaving only the faintest pewter stain along the highest rim—barely enough to tell sky from mass, never enough to tell a branch from a limb until it brushes your face. Down inside the lowland trough, that feeble ceiling-glow dies completely beneath layers of foliage. Sight shrinks to a few arm-lengths of shifting charcoal silhouettes, broken only by the occasional sudden smear of cold blue-green where some startled creature vents a defensive mucus, or a brief, vicious shower of orange sparks when hard rock strikes hard rock in the dark.

And the noise never drops. It is not background; it is an atmosphere with pressure and current. Every cubic yard of air vibrates with competing frequencies—high, serrated trills that drill behind the eyes; the wet, rapid slap of thousands of leathery wings wheeling overhead; hollow rattling booms that roll down from the far ridges; the dry, brittle crack of thick canes snapping under running weight. Because the valley floor and its terraced walls are geologically new, still sharp-angled and unrounded by age, the sound does not soften into moss or deep loam. It caroms off raw, flat faces of fractured basalt, stacking echo onto echo until the entire multi-mile basin hums like the inside of a struck iron pot.

Underfoot, there is no comfortable earth. The terrain is young and violent: tilted slabs of dark volcanic glass, loose aprons of razor-edged clinker, jagged fissures cracked open recently enough that their rims still ring like porcelain when kicked. Only a shallow, springy mat of root-fiber and shredded leaf-litter sits across the stone, nowhere deep enough to blunt the angles beneath. Take a careless step and your heel slides on damp waxy foliage straight onto a fresh stone edge that slices clean through hide.

Yet out of this harsh, unweathered rubble surges an astonishing, choking luxuriance.

The vegetation does not wait for sunlight or deep soil. It drinks heat from the fractured ground and catches every stray photon in broad, oil-dark leaves—velvety black-violet, deep bruised bronze, slick bottle-green—packed so tightly across the slopes and sunken troughs that they form stacked, suffocating roofs. Thick, fluted canes shoot up in dense palisades from every crack in the basalt, their stems hollow and tight with turgor, groaning as thermal updrafts bend them. Between them hang dense curtains of aerial roots, fleshy lianas thick as a thigh, and matted tangles of thorn-ribbed vines that knit ridge to ridge. If you scramble up one of the few bare spines of freshly thrust volcanic rock that pierce the canopy, the scale of the place opens out in the gloom: miles of crumpled, low-lying terraces and sunken hollows, all buried under a heaving, unbroken sea of dark growth that rises and falls in chaotic swells as gusts and stampedes tear through it below.

There are no rivers here, no standing lakes, not even a proper mudflat. The warmth keeps the air heavy, and every few hours a brief, violent squall rips across the basin—fat, lukewarm drops hammering the broad leaves for ten deafening minutes before vanishing into steam off the sunless, heat-retaining stone. Between those bursts, moisture is a hidden, hoarded currency. It condenses in beads along the cool undersides of overhanging basalt plates and runs down the slick grooves of giant rosette leaves, pooling in deep, fibrous plant-cups and sharp pockets of unweathered rock. Every wet seam in the stone, every brimming axil of a broad frond, is slick with algae and fought over mercilessly.

Because nothing in this valley is ever alone for three seconds.

The fauna does not inhabit the foliage; it saturates it, crowds it, rearranges it minute by minute. In the black air above the canopy, immense shifting ribbons of winged things—some feathered, most membrane-winged and blind-eyed—pour from one end of the valley to the other, pouring out streams of echolocation clicks so rapid they fuse into a continuous metallic hiss. Below them, the middle tiers thrash with climbers: long-limbed, wiry-muscled bodies with prehensile tails and hooked digits, leaping blind through the tangled canes, shrieking challenges that ignite chain-reactions of fury across half a mile of thicket.

Closer to the jagged ground, the density becomes almost suffocating. Armor-plated burrowers rasp through the loose scree in clicking phalanxes. Swarms of warm-blooded scavengers, low-slung and narrow-chested, weave through the root-tunnels, snapping at anything that bleeds or slows. Every leaf you brush drops a shower of biting mites, flat-bodied centipedes, or heavy, buzzing beetles the size of a fist. And moving through the lower troughs like slow storms are the bulk-feeders—huge, barrel-ribbed herbivores with leathery hides scarred from raw stone, bulldozing straight through palisades of hollow cane, stripping whole hillsides of broad leaves in a frantic hour before the press of predators or a shifting thermal gust drives the herd onward.

Nothing stays put. The whole basin operates with the frantic, boiling instability of an overturned hive. A herd pushes too hard against a steep apron of fresh clinker, and half a slope lets go in a roar of sliding basalt, crushing thickets, burying nests, tearing open pockets of damp pale fungus that glow faintly for an hour before swarms descend to devour them. Within minutes, the noise of the landslide pulls scavengers from three ridges away; their convergence spooks a roost of ten thousand cliff-hangers, whose sudden takeoff lashes the canopy like a hurricane, shaking loose gallons of trapped rainwater from the curling leaves above, which sends the ground-swarms boiling upward into the vines to avoid drowning in the narrow rock-cracks. Creation and destruction happen on the same afternoon. A stripped cane-brake pushes out pale, whip-fast shoots within days, nourished by dung and shredded carcasses jammed into the warm cracks of the rock.

And woven right into the teeth of this roaring, unbuilt wilderness is the unmistakable rhythm of hands at work.

There is not a single wall in the entire valley. No stacked-stone shelters, no thatched roofs, no cleared compounds, no beaten roads. Anything fixed to the ground here would be shaken apart by scree-slides, chewed down by swarm-grazers, or strangled under fast-running vines before a season turned. Whatever thinks and crafts in this darkness does not settle; it moves with the tumult, light-footed and relentless.

You hear them first as a pattern that refuses to dissolve into the general roar: *tink-tink-clack. Tink-clack.*

Deliberate, measured percussion. Along the exposed ribs of glassy basalt, hunched silhouettes sit wedged into natural rock notches, driving hard riverless hammerstones against fresh obsidian cores. Sparks jump bright orange against their rough, scarred knuckles—tiny, momentary suns that reveal broad palms, calloused thumbs, faces angled down and listening rather than looking. Around their feet lie thick drifts of razor-sharp debitage, half-buried in leaf-shred and shattered shells.

Once you recognize the signs, their discarded craft is everywhere, tangled into the wild clutter of the basin: long lengths of hollow cane split and lashed with damp, braided inner-bark cordage to hold jagged volcanic flakes; thick marrow-bones notched and scraped smooth at one end to pry stubborn armored grubs out of fissures; fist-sized anvil stones pitted in the center from smashing thousands of rock-hard seeds; bundles of spongy lichen tied tightly to flexible twigs, plunged deep into narrow, dripping cracks in the basalt to soak up hidden water where no mouth can reach.

They do not use fire—perhaps the damp fuel and swirling thermal gusts make open flame treacherous, or perhaps light itself is a beacon that draws too many teeth out of the dark. Instead, they work by touch, smell, and acoustic mastery. You hear sudden, cutting rattles of notched bone scraped rapidly with flint, or sharp double-clacks of hardwood blocks struck together—dry, unnatural frequencies engineered to slice straight through the deafening biological wash. A sharp triple-clack from a high basalt ridge answers a muffled scraping whistle from down in a tangled trough; seconds later, a loose band of dark shapes swings silently along the thick lianas, carrying woven vine-slings heavy with split pods, stone wedges, and fresh-flaked blades, vanishing into the heaving undergrowth just before a stampede of horned grazers smashes through the ridge behind them.

They build nothing, own nothing they cannot carry or remake in three minutes from the raw glass beneath their feet, and leave no claim upon the land save a thin, endless scatter of chipped stone, knotted fiber, and cracked bone sinking into the sharp rubble.

Then the wind shifts again. A hot, mineral-stinking draft belches up from some deep rift along the valley's belly, rattling the dry cane-stands like a million bones. High above, the bruised sky flickers once with silent, smothered sheet-lightning—just a fraction of a second of dull violet clarity that shows the whole sprawling basin at once: mile upon mile of razor-toothed volcanic ridges drowning under black-green foliage, dark clouds of flyers twisting like smoke over the troughs, a hillside sliding slowly under the weight of thrashing bodies, and everywhere the wet gleam of droplets clinging to sharp leaves and sharper stone.

Then the dark slams shut again, thick and hot, and the deafening roar surges up to fill the space the light left behind.
