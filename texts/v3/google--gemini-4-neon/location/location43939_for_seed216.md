There is no ground here, and there is no stone. What holds your weight is a narrow deck of split cane, lashed tight with retted bast fiber and pegged with fire-hardened thornwood dowels, all of it bound along the spine of three colossal living trunks that were braided together when they were young and have spent centuries fusing into a single fluted beam. The wood underfoot is worn into a shallow, glistening trough—not by iron or wheel, for there is no metal anywhere in this world, only by soft soles and the long, patient rubbing of water. Between the lashed slats, a thin sliver of warm air rises from below, smelling of wet cedar, crushed fern, and a faint, feverish sweetness like rising bread.

If you kneel and peer through the gap between two poles, you see nothing to fall onto. Only miles of dim, tea-colored vapor, and far beneath it—half a league down, perhaps more—the ghostly curve of another braided span, thin as a wet thread, crossing the haze at an oblique angle. Below that, another, paler still, until the mist thickens into a bottomless murk of grey and dull ochre. Look up, and the sky is equally absent: towering arches of pleached timber, thick as castle keeps and ribbed with ancient scar-tissue where old bindings rotted away, vault out of sight into a low, warm ceiling of hanging cloud.

This is a world made entirely by hand, and entirely of living plant, stretched across an abyss so sparse that almost all of it is empty air.

Walk a hundred paces along the causeway and the craftsmanship reveals its age. Nothing here is raw wilderness, yet nothing is fresh construction either; the wild growth and the hand-tool have long since settled into a quiet truce. Where an ancient builder once drove wooden wedges to bend a sapling into a parabolic strut, the cambium has swelled cleanly over the wedge, swallowing it whole. Knots tied in hemp and nettle-cord a thousand years ago have petrified into woody knuckles, their twist still embossed in the dark bark like the imprint of twine in dried clay. Every seam between decking poles has been chinked with living cushion-moss, pressed home by thumb and palm until it took root and knitted the walkway into a spongy, uneven carpet. Along the outer rails, split-bamboo gutters channel beads of condensation into hanging cisterns grown from enormous cultivated pitcher-pods, their mouths propped open with notched bone struts and draped in braided moss-wicks that drink straight from the humid air.

Now and then, tucked into a dry hollow beneath a joint, you come across the tools of the place: a horn fid polished bright by handling, a flint scraper bound to a forked willow handle with blackened resin, a wooden mallet whose face has been beaten concave by ten thousand soft taps. Yet you can travel for days along these high, trembling spans without meeting a living hand. Only the evidence persists—a newly pared shoot weeping milky sap beside a black, centuries-old splice; a length of pale green cordage still tight and smelling of fresh-cut reed. Once in a long while, when the air is stillest, a single muted *thock* of wood striking wood drifts across two miles of empty vapor. If you look toward it, the haze may part just enough to show a distant limb slowly bending into an arch under no visible weight at all.

The scale of the lattice refuses to sit comfortably in the mind. It is not a forest canopy or a city of bridges; it is a continental filigree suspended at an altitude where the air ought to be thin and frozen. Instead, it is persistently warm—a close, greenhouse heat that seems to radiate from the beams themselves. Billions of tons of damp cambium, fermenting moss-mats, and slow-breathing phloem generate a steady metabolic fever that pools inside the hollow miles of the structure. Because there is no sun—only a low, bruised twilight that seems to leak sideways out of the vapor and gather weakly in damp depressions—the warmth never breaks into noon and never drops into frost.

Across leagues of open void, that heat drives weather as slow as tides. There are weeping belts fifty miles wide where warm, sodden air rises steadily from the unseen depths and condenses against the upper spans. Rain does not fall here in sheets or storms; it simply gathers on the undersides of waxed leaves and braided boughs until the droplets grow too heavy to cling, then detaches in plump, tepid beads that slip downward through the hollow air without ever striking a floor. Far off, separated by days of walking across dizzyingly sparse tension-cables, lie the dry belts, where the convection sinks and the great wooden ribs shrink a fraction of an inch, emitting a slow, deep creak that travels along the grain for miles before fading out.

What strikes you most, after the height and the emptiness, is the hush. Even in a weeping belt, the silence is nearly absolute. There are no cliffs to throw back an echo, no ground for falling water to drum against, and every solid surface is wrapped in wet moss, felted lichen, and spongy bark that swallows vibration the way a rag drinks spilled oil. Your own footsteps make no more noise than a finger pressed into damp bread. When a warm gust rolls slowly through the arches, it moves past your ears as a pressure rather than a rush of wind, carrying only the faintest wet *tick* of a droplet sliding from a gourd-lip into a cistern.

Yet the emptiness is crowded with life. You learn to see it only once your eyes adjust to the dimness and stop looking for movement on the walkways.

Out in the wide, hazy gulfs between the arches, hundreds of pale gliders wheel in slow, silent spirals. Their bodies are thin as soaked vellum stretched over curved ribs of cartilage, wings translucent enough that the mist glows faintly through them. They never flap; they ride the tepid updrafts rising from the lower tiers, tilting a wingtip to slip through a ribbon of cooler vapor, hunting by tasting the shifting humidity with fringed snouts. Closer to the spans, long boneless filter-feeders—pale, pleated ribbons forty yards from blunt head to frayed tail—drift horizontally from one distant rib-arch to the next like stray banners of silk, grazing on floating spores and airborne water-fleas. They pass within arm’s reach without disturbing the air, so quiet that you only notice them when the dim light shifts behind their translucent flanks.

Along the beams themselves, the traffic is denser and more furtive. Inside the hollow pith of pegged cane-joints live clusters of small, many-fingered amphibians with skin the color of wet bark. When the mist thickens, they ease out of the dowel-holes to lick resin from fresh grafts and nibble away soft pockets of rot, keeping the structural knots clean as they feed. Below the walkways, clinging upside down to the hanging moss-wicks, pale arboreal crustaceans comb droplets from the fibers with feathered legs, dropping into the abyss only when startled—and even then, they unfurl membranous skirts and sail calmly away into the grey below.

And hunting all of them are the stalkers: long-limbed, reed-thin creatures with matte olive hides that match the weathered cane so closely you can stare directly at one and see only a braced strut. It is when one moves that you first realize something deeper than distance is wrong with this place.

A flock of bladder-winged drift-moths settles along a damp bamboo rail, pulsing softly as they drink condensation. A stalker ten feet away unfolds its forelimb to strike—and the nearest moth is already torn cleanly in half before the predator’s mandibles finish closing. The ragged wing flutters off the rail into the mist a heartbeat *before* the stalker’s jaws snap shut on empty air. Then the creature swallows, calmly, as if the sequence of events had never needed to line up in the first place.

Stay long enough on the high spans and that slackness begins to show everywhere, like wet hemp rope that has lost its tension.

Water pools in a hollowed pitcher-cistern, warm and brimming, twenty minutes before the mist-bank drifts in to wet the collector-leaves above it. You reach out to steady yourself against a slick braided rail, and your palm grows damp and cool a fraction of a second before your fingers meet the bark. Shadows fail to hurry after the things that cast them: a great ribbon-grazer glides silently past the walkway, and its murky, grey-green blotch remains sprawled across the mossy cane for nearly a minute afterward, slowly fading as though soaking into the wood.

Even the space between the arches no longer obeys a straight line. Sight along a perfectly pegged bamboo spar stretching into the fog, and you will see it bow gently upward until it seems to curve back over your own head—yet if you walk it, your feet feel only a dead-level path. Two immense pleached spans may cross in the open haze, one fifty paces above the other; climb the knotted vine-ladder hanging between them to reach the upper bridge, and when you pull yourself over its rail and look back, the walkway you left hangs *above* you, swaying gently in the vapor while the ladder drops downward into nothingness. Gravity does not belong to a distant earth here; it clings locally to the living timber, pulling gently toward whichever braided trunk lies nearest your boots, so that on a neighboring arch a quarter-mile across the void, droplets of condensation fall sideways, streaming toward a giant junction-knot as if toward a small, damp moon.

If you pry a loose hardwood peg from a rotted joint and toss it over the edge—there are no pebbles to drop, only wood and bone—it falls silently into the dim, warm hollow, shrinking to a speck in the tea-colored mist. Watch patiently, and it does not vanish forever. Somewhere far below, its fall slows and bends sideways, lost for a few breaths in the murk. Then, minutes later, a dark speck appears high above your head, tumbling gently down out of the ceiling-cloud to land with a soft *thump* on the moss beside your knee. Pick it up, and the dowel is swollen with tepid water, furred along one side with velvety black liverwort and the tiny bite-marks of knot-dwellers, as though in its forty-second loop through the emptiness it passed through three wet seasons somewhere else.

In the oldest junctions, where four or five continental trunks were spliced together into vast hollow chambers the size of cathedrals, the weaving itself begins to fray at the edges of sense. The bindings are ancient, impossibly complex—braids within braids, looped through living wood that grew shut around them millennia ago. Try to follow a single strand of cordage with your eye and it doubles back without turning, passing through solid timber as if the beam briefly forgot its own hardness. Press your ear to one of those fused knots, warm and damp against your cheek, and beneath the deep silence of the void you can sometimes catch a faint, trapped rustle: the sound of a wind that is not blowing today, or the distant murmur of rain falling on leaves that have not sprouted yet, grafted into the cambium along with the sap.

Out beyond the junction, the causeway narrows again, a single hand-lashed ribbon of living cane stepping out into ten miles of empty, dim, breathable warmth. Ahead, the mist shifts, parting for a moment to reveal a far-off arch climbing patiently into the low haze, its pale bark faintly luminous with damp pith, a few hundred silent gliders turning lazily in the gulf beneath it—the whole immense, weathered scaffold holding itself together by knot and graft and habit, suspended in a warm hush where neither earth nor time has anywhere left to anchor.
