The plateau sits high enough that the lowlands are gone beneath a level floor of white cloud, yet the air here is warm. Sunlight drops cleanly through the thin altitude without harshness, heating the dark volcanic rock until a faint, steady warmth rises off every ledge and slab. Far across the basin, sharp ridges of black basalt frame the horizon—jagged, newly broken profiles that wind and weather have barely begun to round. The geology here is young. Where lava sheets cooled in successive steps, the rock fractured into crisp vertical faces and flat benches, leaving crisp edges, clean angular rubble, and shallow fractures that look as though they split apart only a few lifetimes ago.

Everywhere, the stone is wet.

Water does not fall here in storms or thunder down gorges; it wells quietly out of the rock itself. Along the upper escarpments, horizontal seams between lava beds weep continuously, glazing the dark cliff faces in a bright film. Thin sheets of clear water slip down the angular steps and spread across the broad benches below, collecting in shallow panes no deeper than an ankle or a shin. Where soil has gathered in the creases of the rock, water sits right beneath the surface, so that even firm ground shines dark and yields a bead of moisture when pressed. At the far southern lip of the plateau, where the rim drops away into the cloud deck, warm moist air lifts just enough to condense in fine droplets along the sharpest rock teeth, feeding the basin back in an endless, quiet loop.

At a distance, it takes a moment to tell where the natural lava terraces end and the city begins. The builders used the exact stone they stood on, splitting the fractured basalt along its natural planes and laying it dry in long, level courses. Only when the eye follows the straight lines does the full scale of the place declare itself: an organized, multi-tiered settlement stretched across miles of open highland, laid out with generous emptiness between its parts.

Broad causeways of fitted flagstones run dead-straight across the shallow flooded flats, linking widely separated quarters. Between one walled precinct and the next lie hundreds of paces of open water, wet bare stone, and low beds of greenery—enough room that each cluster of buildings sits within its own reflection. The architecture is low, heavy, and layered. Stepped foundations raise courtyards and halls clear of the creeping sheet-water; thick dry-stone walls support flat roofs of overlapping slate-like basalt slabs or tightly bundled reeds laid over hand-peeled timber beams. Doorways are narrow and rectangular, framed by massive uncarved lintels hoisted into place by leverage and rope rather than cranes or iron. Nowhere is there metalwork, mortar of any sophistication, or the marks of saws. Corners are pounded square with river cobbles; wedges of soaked wood split the slabs; wooden pegs and knotted plant fiber hold the rafters and eaves.

Yet the urban order is unmistakable. Running alongside every causeway and through every courtyard are carved stone runnels and clay-lined conduits, guiding the plateau’s constant seepage with quiet precision. Rectangular cisterns—some the size of a room, others broad as plazas—catch the overflow from upper terraces and pass it down through notched basalt weirs. Thick boards of dark, water-swollen hardwood sit slotted into grooves in the stone, serving as simple sluices that hold the water level steady across entire districts. Terraced steps connect lower basins to high ceremonial platforms, and long boundary walls divide the open basin into deliberate, airy quadrants.

In the spaces where the builders packed earth behind retaining walls, or wherever wet basalt cracks have caught grit, vegetation grows with a dense, vivid greenness. Lush cushions of deep emerald moss upholster the lips of the weirs and climb the shaded north faces of walls. Neat stands of tall sedge and glossy-leaved rushes rise out of the shallow rectangular basins, their stems reflected cleanly in the motionless water. On the raised garden terraces and along the damp margins of the upper cliffs, tree ferns spread wide, feathery fronds above thickets of broad-leaved highland shrubs and trailing wet vines. Because the rock is young and hard, the greenery stays disciplined without anyone needing to hack it back; bare, unweathered basalt plazas and deep stone channels remain cleanly open, keeping the lush growth balanced against vast expanses of wet stone and pale sky.

What strikes you last—and longest—is the stillness.

There are no birds crossing the open basin, no swallows tucked beneath the timber eaves, no high specks circling above the sharp black peaks. In the clear, sun-warmed cisterns, nothing darts between the reed stems; no frogs rest on the mossy lips of the weirs; no water-striders dimple the mirror-flat surface. Even among the damp fern fronds and warm shaded lintels, no insects hum or drift through the shafts of light. No tracks press into the fine dark silt at the edges of the paths. Whether the builders departed recently or withdrew beyond the outer ridges, no livestock pens, dung heaps, or scattered bones remain to mark an animal presence alongside theirs.

Without wind or wildlife, sound narrows to almost nothing. The air stays hushed, carrying neither the roar of distant rivers nor the rustle of high gusts. If you stand in the center of one of the wide stone plazas and listen, you hear only the smallest, most patient sounds of water in balance: a single drop detaching from a fern tip into a brimming trough; the faint, velvety slip of a millimeter-thin sheet of water gliding across a moss-coated weir; a quiet, rhythmic tick somewhere inside a shaded cistern where seepage meets the surface.

Nothing here seems to be moving toward change. The sun moves cleanly across a bright, slightly humid sky, warming the dark stone to the same mild pitch every afternoon while the cloud floor below the plateau’s rim neither climbs to drown the terraces nor breaks apart to reveal the valleys beneath. The water inflow from the weeping upper scarps matches evaporation down to a hairline, leaving the tide marks on the basalt walls fixed and clean—neither flooded nor dry. The ferns hold their fronds in perpetual mid-season; the sharp volcanic rock resists wearing down; the dry-laid walls sit square and settled on their bedrock ledges. Across the whole open breadth of the basin, stone, water, greenery, and sky hold one another in a warm, luminous, nearly silent equilibrium.
