The first thing that hits you—before the heat, before the glare, before you even register the impossible horizon stretching beneath the crust of the earth—is the noise.

It is not a single roar like a waterfall or an engine. There is no water here to roar, and no iron machinery to grind. It is the sound of a billion dry things striking, scraping, splitting, and vibrating all at once inside a closed stone shell. Hollow reeds clacking together like frantic castanets; sheets of stiff, papery foliage shuddering in hot drafts with the volume of snapping canvas; pressurized seed-husks detonating with flat, concussive cracks; and beneath it all, a vast, sawing, stridulating hiss that rises from every crevice and settles into the bones of your jaw until your teeth ache. You cannot hear yourself shout. You cannot even hear your own footsteps over the percussion of the place tearing itself upward.

You are a kilometer or more below the surface, inside a newly split subterranean valley that runs for leagues before vanishing into a pale haze of floating chaff and mineral dust. The cavern is young—terribly, violently young. Nothing in its geology has had time to learn how to be smooth. The walls and the low ridges that divide the basin are raw tectonic fractures of slate, basalt, and pale gypsum, their edges sharp enough to slice leather. Flakes of newly stressed rock still ping loose from the vaulted ceiling high overhead and tumble down into the green-ochre riot below. No river has ever carved a bed here. There are no stalactites, no damp seeps, no mud, no moss slick with condensation. The air is as dry as the inside of a kiln, warm as fresh bread and prickly with static electricity that lifts the fine hairs on your forearms and snaps softly against your knuckles whenever you brush a stem.

Light fills the vault with the flat, steady brightness of an overcast noon. It comes from above, where the raw stone ceiling is entirely plastered in vast, papery curtains of luminous lichen-frond—pale, dry sheets that feed on the rising warmth and static charge of the valley, glowing a crisp, dust-white that leaves almost no deep shadows anywhere across the basin. Where a giant fracture in the vault still groans and widens, thin shafts of amber dust drift down like dry waterfalls, catching the flat ceiling-glare.

And everything—every shelf of raw shale, every shattered boulder, every kilometer of the hollow floor—is drowned in vegetation that has never known a drop of water.

It is not soft or succulent. Nothing here bends gently. The plants are built of silica-stiff cellulose, brittle keratinous filaments, hollow pith, and volatile dry resins. They drink the furnace-warm mineral gases seeping from the fresh floor-fissures, harvest fine rock-flour from the turbulent air through sticky electrostatic hairs, and pump themselves upright not with sap, but with hot internal gas and tension. Snap a stalk the thickness of your wrist and no moisture beads on the break; instead, it hisses sharply, venting a warm puff of sulfur-sweet air and pale ochre spores that immediately cling to your sleeves like charged ash.

There is no real ground to walk on—only the substrate of living wicker. Kilometer after kilometer of interwoven, hollow canes, ribbed root-baskets, and braided fibrous mats heave and groan over the raw rock beneath. Because the cavity is still opening and the flora is in its first furious wave of colonization, nothing is settled. You can actually watch sections of the canopy grow. A coiled pale shoot thrusts out from a knot of dark stems, ratcheting upward in audible clicks—*tk-tk-tk*—unfolding stiff, serrated leaves thin as hammered mica. Nearby, a twenty-foot tangle of dry cane grows too tall for its own brittle anchors, groans loudly, and collapses in a sudden, deafening avalanche of splintering hollow wood, kicking up a choking white plume of pollen. Before the dust has even drifted away, dozens of pale, coiled runners are already thrusting through the wreckage to claim the gap.

The air in the open middle of the vault is never still. Superheated plumes of dry gas rise off the jagged floor-cracks, slamming into cooler drafts sliding down the vaulted walls. Tall, frantic dust-devils—whirling columns of shredded husks, shimmering gypsum powder, and luminous spores—spin out across the canopy, shredding brittle leaves and hurling hard, burr-covered seeds like hail. Whole hillsides of the living cane-mat lift and settle by several feet as huge gas-bladders hidden beneath the roots inflate, strain, and burst with dull subterranean thuds.

Packed into every layer of this dry, roaring jungle—and pouring through the turbulent air above it—is an overwhelming, frantic abundance of animal life.

Nothing here is wet or slimy; there are no slugs, no frogs, no slick hides. The fauna is all dry plate, hollow bone, parchment wing, bristle, and clicking hinge. Clouds of brassy, dry-winged insects—millions upon millions of them—billow like rolling smoke between the shaking fronds, their collective hum adding a razor-sharp whine to the din. Along the flexing cane-bridges sprint long-limbed, six-legged grazers with tufted, brush-like tails and articulated shells the color of burnt clay. As they tear through the stiff foliage at dizzying speeds, their dry bristles build up a fierce static charge; when two collide over a freshly burst seed-pod, blue sparks jump between their snouts with crisp snaps, and the loser darts sideways down a vertical stalk without slowing.

Deeper in the dark, warm weave of the root-substrate, heavy segmented borers the length of a man’s arm chew relentlessly through both tangled dry timber and raw, unweathered gypsum. Their overlapping dorsal plates grate against the rock like millstones, throwing up miniature volcanoes of warm, dry mineral dust that smaller, spider-legged scavengers immediately comb through for dislodged larvae and crystalline grit.

Overhead, the open gulf between the undulating canopy and the glowing ceiling is crowded with vast, overlapping flocks of gliders. They look less like birds than like furious, living kites—broad, pleated membranes of thin, dry leather stretched across ridiculously light, hollow lattice-bones. They have no need to flap; they ride the violent thermal columns by the hundreds, tilting their ribbed wings to catch the hot updrafts, veering inches apart with dry, leathery *thwacks*. When they hunt, they open vertical slits along their chests, turning their hollow bodies into screaming whistles as they dive straight into the rising swarms of insects or snatch exploding seed-pods mid-air. Even from half a kilometer below, their shrill, discordant diving-calls slice straight through the lower roar.

Worse—or stranger—are the things that sit somewhere between the plants and the beasts. Suspended in the narrow canyons between rising cane-ridges hang pale, basket-shaped nets of dry, sinewy fiber, anchored to surrounding stems by taut vibration-lines. They look like dead root-clumps until a sprinting grazer or a low-dipping glider clips a single hair-thin filament. Instantly, the whole basket snaps shut with the force and sound of a sprung deadfall, its dry spines interlocking as it reels itself rapidly upward into the canopy, quivering violently while dry powdery digestive dust begins to sift from its center.

You would think nothing with hands and a fragile eardrum could endure a place like this for an hour. Yet look closely along the raw basalt ribs where the rock juts just above the heaving cane-sea, and you begin to spot the faint, scattered marks of people.

They are never large, and never permanent. A hundred yards up a fractured gypsum ridge sits a small, dome-shaped shelter no bigger than a bakehouse, woven from scorched cane and plastered on the outside with thick, amber-colored dry resin mixed with crushed shell to turn aside the constant hail of wind-flung seeds. It is anchored directly into cracks in the bare rock with crude, hammered copper spikes and braided hide loops that creak under the strain of the updrafts. A narrow wooden walkway—just three lashed hollow trunks with rough notches chopped into them by a stone or flint adze—leads out from the blind to a single small platform perched over a boiling thermal vent.

On the platform hangs a scattering of simple, heavy-handed gear: curved saws edged with knapped black obsidian; bundles of dried, resin-sealed gourd-like canisters used to collect the volatile oils and glowing ceiling-dust; stiff aprons made from overlapping plates of the giant borers’ shed shells, laced together with sinew to deflect stray sparks and snapping shoots. Tucked into a woven basket beside a chipped clay hearth are three pairs of bulky ear-guards—hollowed knuckle-bones packed tight with rolled bast-fiber and bound to leather straps. Without them, a single shift in the valley leaves a person dizzy and bleeding from the ears.

There are no roads connecting these scattered stations, because a path cut through the dry tangle in the morning is braided shut by evening, and a stone cairn stacked on the cane-floor will be tilted forty degrees or swallowed whole by the next root-surge. Instead, the harvesters and watchers mark their ways on the raw rock walls with scraped chalk-white gashes and hang tattered strips of charcoal-dyed hide from high, flexible poles. The flags snap furiously in the hot gusts—straight out, then whipped backward, then spinning—carrying simple visual codes across the valley floor where no voice has ever carried more than a hand’s breadth. Occasionally, a harvester kneels beside an exposed vein of solid basalt and strikes it in a slow, deliberate pattern with a heavy antler mallet, sending a low, clean thrum through the bedrock itself—the only quiet, reliable channel left in a world that screams through the air.

Down in a hollow where a fresh surge of growth has recently torn through an older shelter, you can see how thin their hold is. Half a dry-stone windbreak lies tumbled on its side, its cracked mud-and-ash mortar already split by pale, clicking shoots. A crooked drying-rack, still strung with brittle strips of cured glider-membrane and bundles of hollow whistling-reeds, leans drunkenly out over the churning sea of fronds as a dozen bristled, sparking scavengers scramble over its crossbeams, fighting over a dropped leather thong.

Stand back on a raw basalt ledge and take the whole subterranean valley in at once, and it feels less like a finished landscape than like an engine caught mid-ignition. Above, the endless white glare of the ceiling-curtains flickers faintly as flocks of pleated gliders sweep beneath them in restless spirals. Below, miles of bone-dry, snapping, surging vegetation rise, buckle, collapse, and leap upward again in hot clouds of static-charged dust. Nothing rots here—there is no water for rot. Dead things simply shatter into dry chaff, get ground into flour by armored jaws, blow upward on the furnace drafts, and feed the next frantic burst of growth. It is warm, blazingly lit, suffocatingly crowded, and deafening beyond thought—an embryonic under-world hammering itself into existence out of dust, gas, friction, and noise, without a single moment of silence to catch its breath.
