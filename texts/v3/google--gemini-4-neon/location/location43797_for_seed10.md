Nothing up here has an edge left to it. Whatever mountains once lifted this tableland into the thin air were planed away so long ago that even the rubble is gone—dissolved, digested, and buried under a continent of cold, wet plant flesh. You stand miles above an unseen drop, on a flat that runs out to a bruised pewter horizon in every direction, and there is no stone beneath your boots. Only a trembling, miles-deep quilt of living and half-decayed vegetation: black sphagnum, iron-stained sedge, rubbery kelp-like mats, hollow-stemmed reeds, and pale fibrous lichens woven so tightly together that they form the ground itself.

Press a heel down and dark, acidic water wells up immediately around the sole. It never fully freezes, though the air has a raw, knuckle-numbing bite just shy of frost. Freezing drizzle drifts sideways across the expanse, condensing out of a low sky that drags its belly right over the moss. The light is dim and sourceless, the pale iron-gray of an afternoon that forgot to brighten after dawn.

Because the plateau is flat and nearly empty above the knee, you can see a long way whenever the mist parts—miles of low, undulating green-black turf and pewter puddles stretching out with huge intervals of emptiness between anything that rises above the grass. There are no walls here, no roads, no pilings, no sign that hands ever shaped a single inch of the place.

And yet the air shakes with the roar of heavy industry.

It hits you in the ribs before your ears sort it out: a deep, wet, hammering throb—*thump-hiss, thump-hiss, thump-hiss*—coming from half a mile to the west, answered by a high, nasal whine of straining current somewhere out to the east, and underneath it all a continuous rushing hiss, like a hundred steam-lines bleeding into the rain at once. The wind tears unimpeded across the high flat, carrying the smell of bog-rot, cold rain, ozone, and hot mineral grease.

If you walk toward the hammering—carefully, because the living mat heaves in slow, sickening swells underfoot—you find the source rising naked out of the unbroken peat. No foundation, no bolts, no brickwork. The moss itself has twisted upward into a forty-foot column of compressed, iron-crusted root-fiber and wet lignite, ribbed like a cast-iron cylinder. Inside its slick, dark throat, a solid plug of calcified sedge-pulp slams up and down three times a second, driven by a furious hydraulic pulse from deep below the bog. On every upstroke, slits along its fibrous collar flare open and blast scalding gray vapor into the freezing mist; on every downstroke, thick, dark water and bright red iron-slurry belch across the surrounding cushions of moss, steaming in the cold. Long, braided tendrils of copper-accumulating moss droop from its flanks like cables, humming audibly as pale blue sparks jump between their dripping tips.

Nobody built it. Nothing designed it. Up here, where the world has worn down past rock and soil into the bare, frayed lining of physical law, industrial process occurs the way weather does. Compression, distillation, torque, electrolysis, stamping—they precipitate spontaneously out of the saturated plant-mass and the thin, charged air, run furiously on nothing but pressure, salt, and blind momentum, and then fail.

Watch the hammering column long enough and its rhythm begins to slip. Not slowing down, but stuttering in time: for three or four heartbeats you see the fibrous piston at the top and bottom of its stroke simultaneously, blurred by a sick, double-exposed shimmer in the dim air. The sound breaks into a ragged, overlapping clatter, like an echo arriving ahead of its own noise. A hairline rift opens in the steam where the rain is falling upward for two seconds. Then the internal pressure simply ceases to make sense. The entire forty-foot cylinder slumps sideways without a crash, unraveling mid-fall into wet ropes of ordinary cold sedge and dark peat. Within minutes, the bog has swallowed it back down, leaving only a quivering sinkhole, a warm iridescent slick of refined hydrocarbons spreading over the puddles, and a mound of damp, metallic grit.

Across the vast, dim tableland, this happens constantly. The surface is never at rest. Though the bones of the plateau are unimaginably old, settled into their final flat exhaustion, the skin of it is in a permanent storm-state—a cold, chaotic tide of sudden assemblies and collapses.

Far out across the sparse distances, a low ridge of tangled heath suddenly coils into a horizontal screw, churning half an acre of wet turf into a frothing trench of dark water and glowing mineral paste before tearing itself apart in a shower of wet peat. Elsewhere, a thicket of tall, hollow reeds locks rigidly straight, vibrating so hard their skins split; an arc of blinding white electricity snaps from their tips to a low-hanging belly of cloud, lighting the dim expanse for a fraction of a second in harsh, dead silver, leaving behind the bitter metallic taste of fried air and the wet crackle of singing moss.

Even the water refuses to obey ordinary rules for long. Shallow, glassy pools sit scattered everywhere across the flat, dark as steeped tea, but their reflections are unreliable. Look down into a puddle during a squall and you might see the sky clear and brassy, or see the underside of a huge, rotating wheel of interlaced roots turning slowly beneath a surface that isn't there, or watch your own shadow lag half a second behind when you raise your hand. In the lee of a sudden pressure-vent, freezing sleet hangs suspended in trembling vertical curtains, sorted by size along invisible magnetic lines, before dropping all at once in a cold slap when the current cuts out.

Because the open stretches between these eruptions are so wide, the plateau looks desolate at a glance—a lonely, roaring waste of wet moss and low mist. Look closer, or wait ten minutes after an extrusion collapses, and you realize the place is swarming with life.

The moment that iron-crusted column dissolves into warm grit and oily slurry, the bog around the sinkhole begins to crawl. Millions of pale, flat-bodied scavengers—half-translucent, articulated like tiny tin plates—boil up out of the submerged moss-pores to graze on the freshly precipitated iron-crust before it cools and oxidizes. Beneath the surface of the dark, vibrating ditches, thick ribbons of blind, muscular eels thread through the root-tangles; they feed directly off the stray currents leaking through the wet peat, their skins faintly bruised with blue luminescence whenever a nearby dynamo-tussock surges, snapping loudly like breaking twigs as they discharge to stun smaller darting crustaceans.

The air above the flat is loud with animals too, though they rarely linger in one spot. Heavy, grease-feathered birds with ragged, broad wings patrol the dim overcast in loose, widely scattered flocks. They are built for the cold and the turbulence—low-slung, blunt-beaked, smelling faintly of tar and wet wool. Whenever a new steam-vent punches through the moss, three dozen of them bank sharply out of the mist, screaming in a harsh, brassy chorus that blends straight into the shriek of escaping vapor. They circle tightly through the scalding plume, soaking warmth into their sodden down and snatching the clouds of bloated, heat-stunned bog-flies blown upward from the churning turf, diving out of the way just as the vent begins to shudder and misalign.

Out in the deeper open, separated by miles of empty, quivering mat, move the big things. You catch them first as tall, pale smudges in the gray fog—long-legged grazers standing twelve feet at the shoulder, their narrow bodies hung with shaggy curtains of water-repellent hair that drips constantly. Their hooves are broad, slatted paddles of dark horn that spread wide with every step so they can stride briskly over the softest, most unstable peat-skins without breaking through. Trailing from their chins and flanks are long, quivering sensory filaments tipped with tiny nodules of magnetite; they don't wander blindly, they read the shifting electrical gradients of the plateau. You watch a small group of four or five turn in unison, head into the driving drizzle, and lope silently toward a patch of trembling bog half a mile away—arriving right as a new extrusion begins to swell, tearing off mouthfuls of hot, newly-pressurized cellulose and lapping at the mineral froth before the mechanism reaches full speed and becomes too violent to approach.

And everywhere, tucked inside the hollow reeds and the wet pockets under overhanging mats of liverwort, live thousands of palm-sized, cold-blooded amphibians whose inflated throat-sacs don't croak so much as *chuff* and *whir*. They have tuned their mating calls to the resonant frequencies of the plateau's unbuilt machinery. When a distant piston-geyser kicks into rhythm, half a square mile of unseen reeds picks up the beat a fraction of a second off-phase, building a huge, wet, rhythmic roar that seems to come from the ground itself, rolling away across the flat until the wind shreds it.

Standing out here for an hour leaves you ringing inside your skull. The cold creeps through wool and leather, carried straight into the bone by the relentless damp, yet pockets of the air blow suddenly warm and acrid with scorched vegetation and hot wet metal. The light never changes—just that dim, bruised twilight clamped down close to the turf—except when an electrical squall rips across the flat and turns every droplet of hanging mist briefly fluorescent.

There is no center to this place, and no shelter. Walk ten miles in any direction and the horizon stays flat, ancient, and open; the ground stays soft, living, and soaked; the air stays thin, freezing, and loud with work being done for nobody. Somewhere miles down beneath the dead layers of peat, the worn-out stump of an old mountain range sits cold and silent, finished with its history. Up here on its back, the moss keeps drinking the rain, the fauna keeps eating the sparks and the slag, and the frayed laws of an exhausted world keep grinding away in the fog—building engines out of weeds, running them hard until time skips a tooth, and letting them melt back into dark water.
