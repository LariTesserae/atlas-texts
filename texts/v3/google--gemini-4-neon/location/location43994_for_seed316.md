The noise arrives before the ground does—a wall of wet, grinding pressure so thick you feel it in the roots of your teeth and the hollow of your sternum. It is not the sharp crack of a young storm, nor the clean crash of an ocean shore. It is the sound of a continent rubbing itself raw.

Everywhere, as far as the curvature of the world will carry, the land has been worn down to its last, lowest knuckle. There are no mountains here, no cliffs, not even a sharp edge left on a pebble. Whatever rose up in the youth of this planet has been chewed flat by billions of years of warm rain and slow abrasion, reduced to a vast, low-slung basin of greasy silt, soap-smooth bedrock nubs, and waterlogged clay. By all rights, it should be a dead, quiet plain—an open mudflat under a dull sky.

Instead, there is no room to breathe.

The flats are packed to bursting. Even though nothing rises higher than a low hillock, the space from the mud up into the dim clouds is completely occupied—crammed with jostling mineral blisters, tumbling sheets of warm brine, boiling seepage-pits, curtains of sideways rain, and the relentless, shoulder-to-shoulder press of matter that refuses to stay in its place. You cannot look ten paces ahead without your gaze snagging on a dozen competing movements: a mound of wet slate-clay heaving itself up like a surfacing back, a gout of lukewarm grey water bursting from a smooth seam in the stone, a dense veil of mist folding inward on itself with a wet clap. The horizon is never a line; it is a crowded, boiling smear of dim copper and bruised pewter.

The light is always the light of a late, smothered afternoon. Overhead, the sky is a low ceiling of churning condensation, so heavy and close it feels less like atmosphere and more like the damp belly of something resting on the lowlands. It glows dimly from within—a flat, ochre-grey luminescence that casts no clean shadows, only muddy pools of darker grey around the crowded hummocks. Every few minutes, a sluggish flash of pale amber crawls horizontally through the cloud deck, or leaks upward from a crack in the mud, briefly showing the sheer, terrifying scale of the basin: thousands of miles of identical, packed upheaval, generating its own wandering pressure-fronts and warm, circular squalls that collide and tear one another apart.

The air is fever-warm, saturated to the point of drowning. Water does not merely fall here; it weeps out of the smoothed stones, sweats from the undersides of mud-shelves, condenses instantly on bare skin in heavy, tepid beads. Beneath your feet, the water table sits half an inch below the surface—or half an inch above it, depending on the second. Every step sinks into warm, fine-grained slurry packed with millions of rounded, clicking pebbles no bigger than thumbnails, all of them constantly vibrating against one another in the tremor of the ground.

And the ground is forgetting how to stay singular.

Because the crust here is so ancient, worn so thin by deep time, the rules underneath have started to fray and leak through the mud. The landscape is not merely eroded; it is ontologically overcrowded, as if several versions of the same mudflat are trying to occupy the same acre at once. You watch a wide, shallow sheet of warm rainwater rushing east across a slick of clay, and halfway through its run it passes directly *through* a low ridge of solid, river-worn basalt—neither stopping the other, the stone merely shivering and throwing off a deafening, bass-heavy hum like a struck bell submerged in syrup. Ten feet away, a puddle pulls itself upward into a trembling, waist-high dome of water, held taut by some local collapse of gravity, its surface reflecting a sky that is clear and bone-dry and blue—a sky this world has not seen in a hundred million years. If you plunge a hand into that dome, your fingers come out coated in hot, dry dust, even as warm rain hammers your shoulders. Then the dome loses its grip and slaps down with the sound of a gunshot, and the mud boils around your ankles.

These slips happen constantly, packed edge to edge across the flats. Space bunches up like wet cloth pushed across a table. A strip of worn shingle will suddenly fold upward into a vertical wall of waterlogged pebbles that holds together for twelve seconds, groaning under impossible friction, before slumping back into flat ooze. Patches of ground trade places silently inside the roar, leaving behind sudden circular craters that immediately fill with warm, foaming seepage. Everything is ancient, smooth, and exhausted—yet it churns with the frantic, suffocating energy of an anthill kicked open in the dark.

To listen to it for any length of time is to lose the sense that sound comes from outside your body. There is the low, endless rumble of buried strata grinding over one another; the wet *shhhh-clack* of billions of rolling stones; the bass percussion of steam-pockets and spatial seams snapping shut; the dense, hissing roar of warm squalls colliding in the dim air. Nothing echoes cleanly, because there is no open air to carry an echo—only soft mud, thick vapor, and crowded water swallowing the sound and vomiting it back as a flat, deafening pressure.

Almost nothing grows in this churning soup. Vegetation is a rare, desperate rumor. Here and there, clinging to the lee side of a particularly stubborn, house-sized dome of ancient bedrock, you find tough, rubbery mats of dark olive biofilm—thick as leather, slick with a greasy sheen that lets the tumbling silt slide right off them. When the rock beneath them stretches or splits along a reality-seam, the mats stretch too, thinning to translucent, veined membranes before snapping back. Stranger still are the lone stalks of what might be a kind of cane: leafless, dark as wet iron, rooted deep in the shifting clay, bending almost double in the warm blasts without breaking. They stand far apart, sparse and solitary, whistling faintly whenever the ambient roar dips for half a heartbeat.

Animals are rarer still, and the ones that endure here have long since given up on eyes and ears. You can walk for days through the dim, packed clamor without seeing a living thing move of its own accord. Then, in a brief slackening of the mist, you realize that the broad, flat, barnacle-pocked slab of grey stone you were about to step onto is breathing. It is a bottom-cruiser—a vast, saucer-flat creature of thick, layered cartilage and calcified plates worn as smooth as the surrounding cobbles. It has no face to speak of, only a wide, downturned siphon-mouth that vacuums the warm silt for mineral films and the occasional shred of rubbery lichen. It moves by letting the ground carry it, wedging its heavy, beveled edges into the sliding strata and riding the land’s turbulence like a tectonic sled. When a spatial fold opens beneath it, the creature simply flattens harder, locking its plates together until the world straightens out again.

Even rarer are the tiny, pale, six-legged things that look like peeled crabs made of translucent bone. They live exclusively in the momentary, palm-sized pockets of quiet that form where two competing tremors cancel each other out. They scuttle furiously from dead-zone to dead-zone, reading the vibrations of the mud through sensitive hairs along their undersides, forever fleeing the crushing noise of the world they inhabit.

And yet, scattered across this deafening, uninhabitable immensity—miles apart, half-drowned, almost erased—are the faint marks of hands.

There is no metal here, no glass, no wire, nothing forged or cut by a tool harder than river-stone. Whatever made these things worked only with what the basin offered: rounded cobbles, heavy clay, the sun-bleached ribs of dead bottom-cruisers. Sometimes, half-buried in a churned-up ridge of mud, you come across a ring of seven smooth stones wedged so tightly together that they have fused along their damp edges, forming a tiny, stubborn island around which the boiling silt parts cleanly—as if the arrangement itself reminds the ground, for three feet in every direction, of how to remain solid. Sometimes you find a low, domed mound of sunless, hand-packed clay, no taller than a crouching shoulder, with a narrow entrance facing away from the prevailing squalls. Inside, the floor is worn smooth by something that sat there a very long time ago, and the roar of the flats outside drops by a fraction, muted into a heavy, womb-like thrum.

On the flattest, hardest surviving slabs of ancient bedrock, if you scrape away the warm grease of the silt, you can sometimes feel shallow spirals ground into the stone—worn so deeply by the water and tumbling grit that they are barely more than a memory under your fingertips. No one is here to tend them. There are no roads between the cairns, no villages, no tools left lying in the mud. Whether these scattered marks were left by beings who lived here before the world’s skin wore through, or by travelers who wandered in from somewhere sturdier and stacked stones just to keep a patch of ground from folding under their knees, the basin does not say. Most of them are already half-swallowed, tilted at drunken angles, slowly being ground back into the same warm, featureless paste as everything else.

If you climb to the top of the highest thing for fifty miles—a slumped, grease-slick hummock of ancient mudstone no more than thirty feet high—you expect relief, a glimpse of clean distance. You get none. From that meager height, the horror and grandeur of the scale finally settle into your bones. There is no edge to the flats. There is no highland where the water drains from, and no clean ocean where it empties. In every direction under the dim, feverish lid of the sky, the entire world is this: an endless, low, overcrowded floor of worn-out matter, sweating warm rain, roaring with the friction of its own unmaking, folding over itself again and again in the twilight, far too old to rise and far too restless to finally lie still.
