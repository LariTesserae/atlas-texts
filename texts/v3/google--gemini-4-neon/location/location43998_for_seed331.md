From the low rim, the whole basin fits inside a single turn of the head—a shallow saucer no wider than a small village, worn down over ages until every ridge has slumped into a soft knee of pale stone. Nothing here has an edge left. The surrounding lip rises barely a few meters above the flooded floor, smoothed by eons of warm rain and patient seepage into broad, soap-worn curves that look less like rock than the final, settled posture of matter that has stopped trying to be tall.

The light is fierce and white, pouring from a bleached sky and bouncing right back up off the water. Nearly the entire floor of the basin lies beneath a warm, ankle-deep sheet that holds the glare like polished tin. Set wide apart across that bright expanse are maybe a dozen low hummocks—clumps of tangled, gleaming vegetation and a handful of pale, rounded artifacts that protrude like knuckles from the bath-warm shallows. Between them lies open, shining distance. At first glance, the basin seems sparse, almost empty: a quiet, flooded courtyard left out in the sun too long.

Then your eyes adjust to the brightness, and the stillness breaks apart into movement.

The basin is drowning in animals.

It is hard to register at first because there is almost no sound. No chorus of frogs, no insect drone, no slap of wings or shrieking of hunters. A thick, sunlit hush lies over the water, heavy and strangely dry to the ears despite the humid heat. If a branch drops from a hummock, the splash rises in a clean, slow crown of droplets—and makes only a faint, muted *thup*, as though heard through a wall of water, while a tiny puff of warm mist blooms off the impact ring. Something buried under the silt drinks vibration and exhales it as heat. Even your own breath seems to travel no farther than your collar.

Inside that silence, life operates at a frantic, continuous pitch.

Look down into the clear, tepid water near your boots and the silt itself begins to crawl. The bottom isn't mud; it is a shifting tapestry of flat, pale colonial grazers sliding over one another like overlapping plates of wet porcelain, grazing the microscopic films that coat the ancient floor. Above them, the six inches of water are packed with ribbon-thin swimmers so transparent you only catch them when their silver nerve-cords flash against the sunlight. Thousands move in braided currents from hummock to hummock, parting around slow, fist-sized mollusks whose shells are grown in smooth, pearlescent spirals that seem slightly too symmetrical to be purely biological.

On the skin of the water itself, the surface tension behaves oddly—thicker than it should be, almost elastic, dimpling deeply before it breaks. Across that warm trampoline skate legions of pale, needle-legged hunters, their bodies tipped with tiny amber mirrors. They do not chirp or buzz to find one another; they angle their abdomens to catch the blinding glare and throw sharp sparks of reflected light across the flats. Every second, the water's surface winks with thousands of tiny semaphore flashes—territorial warnings, mating offers, alarm relays rippling from one side of the basin to the other in under a heartbeat.

The few widely spaced hummocks are choked with lush, heavy growth that spills over their own edges into the shallows. Broad, waxy leaves the color of bruised jade hang slick with condensation, heavy enough to bend their thick stems toward the water. Between them, pale aerial roots dangle in wet curtains, encrusted with tiny translucent polyps that open and close in silent unison whenever a breeze moves the humid air. Every square inch of bark and leaf-underside is claimed. Soft-skinned, six-limbed climbers the size of a spread hand cling to the stems, their hides shifting smoothly from leaf-green to chalk-white as they slide out of the small, tight shadows into the glare. Clusters of velvet-backed herbivores hang motionless from the lower branches, drinking the warm dew that gathers in constant beads along the leaf tips, while long, boneless ambushers—colored like wet lichen—drape themselves across the crowns, waiting for something to step too close.

And the air, which looked empty against the bleached sky, is layered with silent flight.

Drifting between the hummocks are ragged clouds of pale, papery gliders—creatures with wide, translucent membranes frayed at the trailing edges so they cut the air without a whisper. They ride the warm thermals rising off the sunlit water, catching the light like scraps of mica. Through them slice larger hunters: lithe, four-winged shapes with long stabilizing tails and sleek, oily hides that scatter the sunlight into brief rainbow smears. A strike happens ten paces away in total silence—a sudden banking curve, a flash of silver belly, a puff of torn wing-scales drifting lazily down toward the water where waiting skaters already converge on the dimples before the scraps even land.

Here and there, emerging from the open flats between the green islands, sit the remnants of whatever built this quiet.

There are only a few of them left, spaced with wide, deliberate geometry that erosion has nearly erased. One is a low, seamless dome the color of old bone, half-submerged, worn so smooth that water refuses to run off it normally; instead, a thin film climbs lazily up its flanks against gravity, pooling at its crown in a trembling, convex lens before spilling back down in silent, rhythmic pulses. Another is a pair of curved ribs rising from the center of the basin—matte-grey, unrusted, their surfaces somehow cooler than the hot air around them, constantly weeping fresh condensation that sheets down into the basin and keeps the water level fed. Where the material has aged for unimaginable spans, it hasn't crumbled into grit or shards; it has thinned, turning glassy at the margins, dissolving slowly back into light and warmth.

Beneath the shallow water, you can still trace faint, straight channels—the ghosts of conduits worn down nearly flush with the ancient stone—running between the sunken nodes. They don't hum or glow. Yet near them, the world loosens its grip by a fraction of an inch. Water beads into perfect spheres that skitter across the surface without merging. Shadows fall at slightly the wrong angle, lagging a sliver of a second behind the creatures that cast them.

The fauna has lived inside these worn-out rules for so long that it treats impossible physics as just another kind of weather.

Around the weeping ribs, schools of finger-length browsers hover directly in the faint distortion fields, their scales grown from strange, non-metallic lattices that harvest stray energy straight from the node. On the low bone-colored dome, a congregation of spindly, long-necked waders stands knee-deep in the upward-creeping film of water, dipping their beaks into the hovering lens at its peak. When a sleek, low-slung predator pads silently across the flats toward them—its wide, padded feet barely dimpling the water's skin—it does not rely on speed alone. Ten feet out, near one of the buried conduits, its outline smears sideways like grease on glass; for a blink it seems to occupy two dry inches of air at once, and then its jaws snap shut on a wader that never heard a footstep or saw the lunge close the gap. Even the kill is quiet: a brief, furious thrashing that throws arcs of bright water into the air, the sound eaten immediately by the basin floor, leaving only a sudden bloom of steam and the widening rings of fleeing swimmers.

Because the basin is so small and warm, it runs on its own tight, restless clock.

As the sun climbs higher, the glare intensifies until the water nearly vanishes into white light, and the weeping artifacts sweat harder to dump the accumulated energy of all that silenced motion. Vapor rises off the flats in slow, ghostly curtains that never quite cloud the sun—just enough to make the far rim shimmer and bend. Every hour or two, the humid air reaches its limit. A brief, localized shower forms directly overhead in the bright sky and falls straight down into the bowl: fat, warm drops that dimple the water into a million silver craters, raising a muted, velvety sigh—the loudest sound the place ever makes.

The rain transforms the traffic instantly. The papery gliders vanish beneath the waxy canopies of the hummocks, clinging in dense, trembling curtains to the undersides of leaves. From the silt, larger things rise—flat, mottled swimmers wide as tabletops that spend the brightest hours buried under the porcelain grazers, lifting now to cruise the churned shallows with their wide, toothless mouths open. Across the open flats, tiny iridescent amphibians erupt from the wet root-tangles, hopping across the rain-stippled water in frenzied waves to spawn in the warm, agitated pools around the sunken nodes before the shower ends.

Ten minutes later, the rain cuts off cleanly. The white glare slams back down. Steam lifts off the lush, dripping hummocks, the velvety sigh dies back into the deep, warm hush, and the water flattens into a mirror once more.

Nothing here is resting, and nothing is new. The basin has reached the bottom of its geology—worn flat, settled into its final shallow cup, its ancient machinery weathered down to quiet reflexes that nobody remembers designing. All that remains is the heat, the blinding sky, the scattered islands of dripping green, and an endless, soundless crowd of living things chasing, flashing, feeding, and multiplying across the warm glass floor.
