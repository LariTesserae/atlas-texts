From inside, it is easy to forget how high up you are, until you tilt your head back and look through the ceiling.

The ceiling is a low, ribbed dome of thick, ancient glass, pitted on its outer face by millennia of windblown ice and polished smooth on its underside by water. Straight, ordinary daylight pours through it—neither blinding nor dim, just the clean, unremarkable brightness of an open afternoon—and beyond the glass there is nothing at all. No trees, no neighboring roofs, no rock face. Only a pale, almost colorless violet-blue sky and, once in a long while, the slow white smudge of a cirrus cloud passing *beneath* the horizon line of the dome's brass rim. When the upper-atmosphere winds lean hard against the tower outside, the whole chamber tilts by a fraction of a degree, and the clear, bath-warm water shifts its weight with a quiet, heavy sigh against the walls.

This entire world is no wider than a courtyard—perhaps thirty paces from one curved bulkhead to the opposite—sealed away inside the hollow crown of an engine-tower so tall its base is lost miles below the cloud deck. It is a pocket of water suspended at the edge of the breathable sky: an old ballast-and-condenser tank whose service hatches seized shut so long ago that the metal of the door and the metal of the frame have slowly grown into a single continuous skin.

There is no stone here, no mud, no sand. Every inch of the floor and curved walls is made—riveted iron plating, flanged cast-iron headers, banks of looped copper heat-exchangers, rows of fluted porcelain insulators bolted along the ribs. Yet nothing looks sharp, or recently ruined, or even particularly metallic anymore. Ages of warm, mineral-rich circulation and faint stray currents have carried the ironwork past rust and into its final, geologically calm form. Every bolt-head has been worn down to a smooth, dark pebble. The overlapping hull plates have lost their crisp seams, softened into gentle undulations coated in velvety magnetite and pale, river-smoothed travertine. The faces of the pressure gauges mounted along the wall are opaque, milky moons of lime-scale, their brass bezels melted into soft, rounded lippings. Through the exact center of the room runs a thick bronze drive-shaft, turning endlessly, slowly, descending through a collar in the floor to whatever vast, forgotten machinery still grinds miles below. Its surface is burnished to a dull, oily satin, worn slightly waisted where the water has rubbed against it for centuries.

For all its intimacy, the chamber feels surprisingly spacious. The machinery is widely set—a cluster of copper coils along the southern curve, a tall manifold pillar to the north, the central turning shaft—leaving wide, generous lanes of open, sun-filled water between them. You can see clearly from one curved wall to the other across broad stretches of empty, amber-lit volume, broken only by drifting motes of white mineral flake and rising chains of tiny bubbles.

And it is loud. Water does not soften sound; it hands every vibration directly into your chest. The central shaft produces a deep, rhythmic *thrum-drag* that turns twice every three seconds, a bass heartbeat felt in the teeth. From a brass bleed-valve near the top of the dome comes a continuous, thin, piercing harmonic whistle as trapped gas forces its way out into the thin air above. Along the lower bulkheads, hidden hydraulic relays tick and snap with crisp, metallic percussions, and every few minutes the tower's steel skeleton far below lets out a slow, resonant groan as the sun warms one side of the distant hull, ringing the flooded room like a sunken bell. Beneath it all lies an incessant, dry crackling—like grease in a hot pan—made by thousands of tiny hard-shelled creatures moving across the smooth iron.

In the wide spaces between the pipes, the water is open and clear; wherever the warm copper coils or electrified conduits run, life erupts in sudden, extravagant abundance.

The vegetation does not choke the room, but where it takes hold, it is intensely lush. Thick, buoyant cushions of bright emerald velvet-moss swell over the rounded heat-exchangers, soaking in the steady bath-like warmth radiating from the pipes. From the upper rivets and the rim of the glass skylight hang long, sweeping curtains of translucent water-fern, their broad, ruffled fronds swaying slowly in the ambient current like green silk draped in a cathedral. Strangest of all are the wire-vines—dense, glossy vascular plants that root directly into the calcified cracks around the porcelain insulators. Responding to the faint alternating currents still humming through the old insulated cables, their pale-green stems grow in strict, tight left-handed helices around the copper lines, sprouting clusters of gas-filled bladders that swell like glass berries until they tear loose and chime faintly as they bounce upward against the domed ceiling.

The fauna moves through this sparse, sunlit architecture with the brisk, practiced efficiency of a bustling tide-pool.

Tight schools of translucent filament-fish—slender as sewing needles, their hair-thin spines glinting with an odd, coppery sheen—hover in neat geometric ribbons just inches from the humming cables. They graze constantly on the rich bacterial velvet coating the warm metal, their gill-covers pulsing in unison with the electrical hum. Down along the smooth, pebble-riveted floor scuttle hundreds of valve-crabs, no larger than walnuts. Over generations without natural stone or shell-grit, they have learned to bind flakes of shed brass, verdigris, and pale scale into their carapaces; they look like tarnished clockwork mechanisms come alive, waving delicate feathered antennae as they pick over the manifolds. Beside them, slow pale snails creep across the iron curves, laying down tiny, mathematically exact hexagonal shells aligned to the magnetic pull of the spinning central shaft.

Up in the wide, empty middle water—suspended motionless beneath the hanging fern curtains where the daylight breaks into dancing ribbons across the walls—hang the predators. They are sleek, pale-bodied ambush hunters, half-newt and half-skate, with broad ribbon-fins undulating slowly along their flanks and delicate, feather-like electro-receptors fanning outward from their blunt snouts. Nearly invisible against the bright glare of the skylight, they wait with total patience, reading the tiny electrical disturbances of the schools below.

They do not have to wait long. The room breathes on a strict mechanical schedule.

Roughly every four minutes, something deep in the tower's ancient ballast-governor decides the chamber has grown too warm, or too heavy. There is a sudden, deafening *clang* that shakes loose a snowstorm of pale mineral dust from the rafters. High on the northern curve, a heavy bronze intake louvre grinds open against its smooth-worn tracks.

Instantly, a violent jet of frigid, oxygen-rich meltwater—harvested from the frost-collectors clinging to the freezing outer skin of the tower—tears across the warm room.

For thirty seconds, the calm pocket-world turns electric with motion. Where the icy jet collides with the bath-warm water rising off the copper coils, the room fills with shimmering, glass-like sheets of optical distortion—writhing thermoclines that twist the daylight into bending lenses. The lush curtains of fern flatten horizontally in the rush. Blizzards of trapped oxygen bubbles rip free from the moss pillows and spiral crazily toward the skylight. The schools of copper-spined filament-fish break their tidy formations and scatter wildly into the open lanes, their delicate balance upset by the surging cold.

That is the signal. The pale predators drop out of the bright upper water in sudden, fluid flashes, twisting through the shivering curtains of mixed water to snap at the fleeing schools. Below, the brass-plated crabs surge out from the bolt-recesses in a furious, clicking tide, hoisting their clawed fans directly into the freezing jet to strain the dislodged moss-spores and microscopic flecks swept off the far walls. Even the wire-vines whip and thrum, releasing dozens of tiny chiming bladders at once.

Then the governor finds its balance. With a heavy, shuddering boom that rattles every plate in the hull, the bronze louvre slides shut.

The violent jet dies back into a broad, lazy clockwise eddy circling the turning bronze shaft. The sharp sheets of cold water dissolve slowly into the steady, comforting warmth rising from the floor coils. The fern curtains lift and drape themselves downward once more; the scattered filament-fish reassemble their shining, orderly ribbons along the humming wires; the sleek predators drift back up into the sparse, sunlit emptiness beneath the dome, vanishing into the bright ripples.

Outside the pitted glass, miles above an unseen earth, the stratospheric wind keeps pressing against the ancient iron skin of the tower, unseen and freezing. Inside, loud with the tireless turning of its axle and bright with ordinary afternoon light, the little drowned room settles back into its warm, buzzing, self-sufficient rhythm—an old machine that forgot its purpose so long ago it has become a world instead.
