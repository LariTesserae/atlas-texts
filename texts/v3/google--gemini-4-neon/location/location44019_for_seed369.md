The approach gives no warning of the volume. High on the shoulder of the massif, the outer wind is thin and dry, scouring pale scree and flattened lichen. Then the ridge splits into a narrow, curved cleft of worn stone, and five paces inside, the wind cuts off cleanly. Two things rush in to take its place: a thick, bathhouse warmth, and a sound so large and steady it feels pressed against the sternum.

The hollow beyond the cleft is not big—no wider than the nave of an old cathedral, perhaps sixty paces across at its broadest—yet it feels curiously vast because almost nothing crowds its floor. It is a natural stone cradle perched near the upper lip of the mountain, walled on three sides by curved, overhanging ribs of pale ochre and tarnished-silver rock, and open cleanly to a bright, high-altitude sky. On the fourth side, the rim sinks into a smooth, rounded spillway. Beyond that glistening lip there is nothing at all: only sheer air, bright cloud-tops drifting far below, and the blue knuckles of distant ridges.

The rock is old, porous travertine and layered sinter, smoothed by millennia of seepage until not a single sharp fracture remains. Every wall weeps. Water does not cascade here so much as it sweats from ten thousand pinholes, gathering along bulging stone bellies, sliding down verdigris-stained flutes, and pooling in broad, shallow pans rimmed by scalloped mineral lips. The floor is a patchwork of these wide, ankle-deep mirrors and slick, warm stone aprons. Even in the full glare of midday, the air hangs damp and faintly sweet, shimmering where the heat rising out of the rock meets the crisp blue chill overhead.

It is the stone itself that begins the noise. Somewhere deep beneath the basin, warm groundwater and trapped air move through an ancient warren of flooded chimneys, and the floor vibrates with a continuous, low bronchial hum—not a tremor you stumble over, just a steady acoustic pressure that travels up through the soles of your feet. Across every shallow pool, the vibration holds the water in crisp, stationary patterns: fine geometric lattices and tight concentric rings that shiver in place without ever rolling to the edges.

Because the overhanging walls curve inward like the shell of an ear, nothing inside the hollow is quiet. High up near the rim, swollen drops of condensation gather on overhanging beards of sinter, cling until they are heavy as plums, and let go. When they fall forty feet onto the thin, hard-bottomed mineral pans below, they do not splash softly. They strike with a sharp, ceramic *clack* that ricochets twice across the bowl before dissolving into the undertone.

Set apart across this wide, glistening floor are the plants—and at first glance, the strangeness of the place is how sparsely they are arranged. There are only nine or ten great botanical presences in the whole basin, separated by generous expanses of bare, wet, reflecting stone. You can walk clear around each one without brushing a leaf. Yet wherever a plant takes hold, it is extravagantly, almost impossibly lush.

They grow in isolated islands anchored to low stone hummocks: towering, waist-thick stalks of glossy jade and deep emerald, rising into dense, overlapping canopies of massive, heavy-veined fronds that drip without ceasing. Around their bases sit thick bolsters of velvety moss saturated like sponges, pale green at the tips and almost black where they drink from the pools. Curtain-like tangles of pale aerial roots hang straight down into the warm air, beaded along their entire length with clear droplets that catch the bright high-altitude sun.

Rising straight through the crowns of these broad leaves are the flutes—clusters of tall, smooth, hollow reeds and taut, pale-skinned bladders that the plants grow as condensation traps. Each reed is open at the top with a bevelled, papery lip. As the steady upward draft of warm air brushes past them, and as the floor hums beneath their roots, the stalks sing. Not melodically, and not gently: together they produce a loud, thick, organ-like chord that fills the entire sunlit room, loud enough that a person speaking here would have to raise their voice to be heard across ten paces.

Everything in this choir moves with extreme patience. Over the course of a morning, warm dew condenses inside the hollow reeds, bead by bead, slowly raising the water column inside each stalk. As the air-space shrinks, the pitch of a stalk creeps upward by tiny, almost imperceptible fractions over hours. When a tall chamber finally fills past its balance point, its supple lower joint yields. Over the space of three or four slow minutes, the stalk bows gracefully sideways, pours a bright, heavy ribbon of water onto the mineral pan below—raising a sudden burst of echoing clatter—and then slowly rights itself, hollow again, dropping back to its lowest resonant note to begin the half-day cycle anew.

Nothing decays cleanly here. When one of the huge fronds yellows and tears free, or when a cushion of moss slumps into the edge of a warm pool, the mineral-laden water goes to work before rot can finish. Over seasons, dissolved silica and pale lime crust over the veins and ribs, replacing soft green tissue with a thin, glassy porcelain shell. Along the margins of the terraces lie clusters of these pale-ivory ghost leaves, brittle as eggshells. When the slow spill from an emptying stalk washes through them, they clink together like fine pottery before slowly fusing into the next layer of the floor.

For all its warmth and lushness, the basin feels curiously empty of creatures. There are no clouds of midges hanging above the water, no frogs, no birds nesting in the deep green umbrellas. Perhaps the constant low booming in the stone unsettles anything with delicate bones or nesting habits, or perhaps the sheer altitude keeps most lowland life from ever finding the warm pocket. Only if you kneel beside a wet rim and wait long enough might you notice a thumb-sized snail with a thick, chalk-white shell, creeping along the mineral lip at a pace indistinguishable from stillness. Once in an afternoon, the dark crescent of a mountain swift may slash across the bright open sky above the rim, never dipping down into the column of warm, singing air.

There are no walls laid by hand here, no roofs, no cairns, no paved paths. The basin remains unbuilt. Yet if you move slowly between the shining pools, you begin to see the faint, respectful marks of hands that come here rarely, and carry only simple tools.

Along the edge of one warm terrace, someone has patiently pecked a shallow, finger-wide channel into the soft wet sinter using a pointed stone, coaxing a thread of mineral water away from a moss bed and into a clean, cupped depression where the silica settles thick and pale as cream. On a broad, sun-baked slab near the center, three long hanks of stripped plant-fiber have been laid out flat to bleach and dry, pinned at their corners by smooth, palm-sized cobbles.

Tucked deep into a dry pocket beneath the eastern overhang—the only place in the whole bowl where the stone never weeps—rests a small, orderly cache: two flaked blades of dark glassy basalt, a stubby wooden mallet whose striking face has been beaten soft and fibrous, a bone awl polished bright by skin oil, and a coil of tight, dark cordage twisted from the aerial roots. Nearby, one of the great jade stalks bears a clean, shallow notch cut into its side. A wedge of pale seasoned wood has been tapped gently into the incision, holding it open just enough for a slow tear of thick amber resin to bleed down into a stiff, folded leaf-cup pinned beneath it with a thorn. The resin smells sharply of wet cedar, hot stone, and green sap.

Whoever visits this high room never alters its acoustics and never stays long enough to stack one rock upon another. They take only what the slow wet chemistry gives up—cured fiber, brittle calcified leaves, resin, mineral paste—and slip back out through the narrow cleft into the cold wind.

At the open lip on the fourth side, the shallow pools all drain toward one smooth, algae-slick sill. The warm water spreads thin as glass across the curved stone, slips over the edge without a sound, and shreds immediately into bright mist as the mountain wind catches it outside. Standing a few paces back from the drop, bathed in clear daylight, wet to the ankles, surrounded by wide bare stone and a handful of immense dripping greens, you hear the whole small world holding its single, shifting, deafeningly patient note against the empty sky.
