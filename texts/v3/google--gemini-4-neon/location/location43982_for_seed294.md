The water here is never still, and it is almost entirely silent.

At first there is only the cold—a clean, mineral, near-freezing cold that numbs the skin without the greasy heaviness of the deep sea—and the dim, bruised pewter light filtering down from a surface too high and wind-torn to resolve. Shadows move across the depths in broad, erratic sweeps, cast by ragged plates of thin ice and thick cloud-banks passing far overhead. Between them, the water holds a pale slate glow that dies out fifty paces away into a shifting veil of suspended stone-flour.

Then the current hits. It does not come as a steady river. A dense, bitter plume of colder water drops almost vertically out of the dimness above, strikes the floor, and explodes outward in every direction at once. A cloud of fine pale grit erupts from the seams below, spins into tight, voiceless corkscrews, and tears sideways across the open expanse. Loose cobbles—smoothed like eggs, pale grey and dark basalt—lift an inch off the floor and skitter across the flagstones with a dry, muffled knocking, the loudest sound the place ever makes. Seconds later the down-surge collapses, sucked backward toward an unseen gap, and the grit-cloud folds in on itself and vanishes.

There is no earth beneath the grit. Every surface within reach, and every surface beyond it, is cut stone.

Wide, unmortared flagstones stretch out across a flooded plateau the size of a mountain valley, laid in sweeping concentric courses that have been scoured by water for so long that no right angle survives anywhere. The joints between blocks have been widened into undulating gutters. Where stairways once climbed from one broad terrace to the next, the treads have been ground down into wavy, slickened ramps, their outer edges thinned to curved lips. Low retaining walls, stout enough to hold back a glacier, run in broken, sparse lines across the open plain; most have lost their upper courses, reduced to rounded spines of masonry half-buried in drifting bars of water-worn rubble.

Nothing is crowded. Between a drowned colonnade of squat, waist-thick pillars and the next stepped basin lies a hundred yards of bare, scalloped paving and empty churning water. The scale is civic, highland, and vast—a plateau of terraced cisterns, sunken arenas, broad causeways, and immense breached sluices perched high between mountain ridges that never show themselves through the gloom. Along the outer rims, the paving stops abruptly at immense curved parapet walls, worn smooth as soap, over which the coldest currents spill and vanish downward into darker, deeper troughs below. You feel the altitude in that drop, and in the sharp, meltwater bite of the water itself, constantly renewed from higher basins unseen above the murk.

No green thing grows here. Not a frond of weed, not a tuft of moss, not even the slick fur of algae that ordinarily coats wet stone. The light is too meager, the cold too constant, and the scouring sand-laden surges too relentless for any root or holdfast to survive. Where a sheltered nook stays out of the worst vortices, the stone is coated only with a faint, chalky patina of settled dust, wiped clean again the next time the current reverses.

Yet the water is full of life.

As soon as a surge slackens for ten heartbeats, the lee-sides of the ruined walls come unstuck. What looked like overlapping grey shingles packed into the mortarless crevices begin to slide and separate—hundreds of flattened, armored crawlers with carapaces worn matte and pale as wet slate. They move out in sparse, purposeful scatterings across the open slabs, scraping microscopic film and trapped organic specks from the scalloped stone with fine, combing forelimbs. Before the next vortex arrives, some pressure-change warns them; they flatten simultaneously, clamping their beveled rims flush against the paving until they are once again indistinguishable from the masonry, letting a violent sheet of grit and tumbling pebbles roar harmlessly over their backs.

Above the flagstones, entire ribbons of pale, boneless swimmers ride the chaos rather than fighting it. They are long, translucent, silver-grey things with broad, undulating fin-margins and tiny, dark, forward-set eyes. They move in loose, twisting skeins through the breached archways and over the ruined viaducts, turning cleanly as a single sheet of cloth whenever a cross-eddy slams into them, snapping sideways into the calmer eye of a vortex to pluck invisible specks of glacial detritus swept down from the upper watersheds. Between the ribbons drift translucent bell-creatures no larger than a cupped hand, milky and faint, trailing delicate pale threads. The currents whip them wildly across the broad open terraces—tumbling them end-over-end, dragging them through sunken stairwells, flinging them upward into the dimness—yet their soft bodies never tear; they pulse lazily once every few seconds, letting the storm carry them where it will.

In the deeper drainage trenches and collapsed vaults beneath the plazas live heavier things: broad-headed, whiskered bottom-dwellers with pale pebbled skin, wedged tight between fallen lintels, and long-legged, stone-colored crabs whose shells are pitted with the same small scour-marks as the blocks around them. They do not roam far. They wait in the quiet pockets behind ancient baffles, reaching out into the roaring lip of the current to snatch whatever stunned swimmer or loose morsel gets spun out of the main flow.

Everything here is made, and everything made is unimaginably old, yet there is no trace of metal, glass, wood, or engine. No iron clamps hold the blocks together; no bronze grates bar the sluices; no wheel or lever ever sat in these channels. The builders worked only in quarried stone—heavy, interlocking slabs shaped to lock by weight and gravity alone—and they built without leaving a single clear sign of what they meant to house or measure. There are broad circular courts whose floors slope gently toward central perforated plinths, stairways that rise fifteen paces to a flat crown and descend identically down the far side, and long rows of chest-high stone troughs cut with narrow vertical slots through which the turbulent water now threads in silent, pressurized jets.

When a particularly fierce down-surge scours out a collapsed courtyard, peeling away a yard of rounded rubble-cobbles in a minute, it never exposes bedrock. Beneath the broken paving are older, smoother paving slabs; beneath those, the buried crowns of lower arches choked with smaller, even rounder stones. Layer sinks beneath layer, masonry resting on older masonry, as if the mountain ridge itself were replaced long ago by stacked stone and then abandoned to the cold water.

Every hour the geography of the loose ground shifts. A long, curving bar of pale, fist-sized stone fragments—once the carved cornices of some vanished wall—migrates steadily across an open plaza, driven half a pace at a time by the slamming cross-currents, burying a shallow channel here and uncovering an ancient grooved threshold there. The crawlers relocate with it. The ribbon-swimmers bend their routes around the new obstructions without hesitation.

If you hang suspended in midwater above one of the wide central terraces, holding still against the constant, unpredictable tugs at your limbs, the place reveals its strange equilibrium. Visually, it is a tempest: clouds of pale silt blooming and shredding, translucent bells whipping past like blown snow, whole shoals flicking sideways in silver flashes, loose stones rolling silently down worn stairs, shafts of dull grey light breaking and reforming overhead as the wind-beaten surface heaves. Yet to the ears, there is almost nothing. Only the heavy, velvet pressure of the cold water, a faint hydraulic thrum felt more in the ribs than heard when a surge funnels through a drowned archway, the rare blunt *tock* of cobble striking cobble, and the soft, scattered ticking of hundreds of plated shells sealing themselves back against the ancient stone before the next voiceless wall of water arrives.
