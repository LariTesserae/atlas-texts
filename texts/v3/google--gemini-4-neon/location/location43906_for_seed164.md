From any vantage, most of the world is air.

It is high, thin, bright air, cold enough to turn breath into instant plumes and clear enough to show the teeth of ridges sixty kilometers away. Below, so far down that perspective flattens it into a pale, motionless floor, lies a continental deck of cloud. Thrusting straight up through that cloud-deck are the blades: parallel, razor-edged shards of dark rock, tilted at near-vertical angles like the shattered plates of a crust that cracked open and never settled back down. Some are tens of kilometers long and barely three hundred meters thick. Between one blade and the next lie sheer, sunlit gulfs of emptiness—two miles, five miles, twelve miles of open sky before the next sheer cliff face rises.

The rock is brutally young. It has the fresh, conchoidal sharpness of fractured glass or newly split hornfels, dark slate-blue where it is dry and black-lacquered where it is wet. There are no soft foothills, no rounded boulders, no scree slopes softening the bases of the cliffs. Every ridge-line looks as though a chisel split it an hour ago. Yet nothing is crumbling. No pebbles clatter into the abyss. Whatever tectonic violence lifted these jagged fins out of the planet stopped in mid-gesture, leaving the stone locked in an unweathered crispness that the cold water running across it never seems to dull.

And across the upper edges of these knife-blades, bolted straight into the sheer faces and cantilevered out over miles of bright void, sit the cities.

They are built like heavy industrial towns stretched onto tightropes. Along the narrow crests run paved viaducts, terraced brick-and-iron avenues, rail-yards with three parallel tracks, and stacked blocks of riveted factory halls. Where the rock narrows too sharply to hold a street, steel trusses shoot sideways out over the drop, supporting entire civic squares, switchyards, and multi-story tenements on webs of cross-braced girders. Between distant ridges—spanning those vast, empty chasms—hang colossal catenary bridges, sagging pipelines wrapped in frost-beaded lagging, and banks of high-tension cables slung from porcelain insulators the size of grain silos. Taken as a whole, the built infrastructure spans a continent; measured by volume, the world is almost entirely hollow. A city here is a thin, intricate crust of iron, concrete, and glass clinging to a splinter of stone suspended in nothing.

The first thing that hits anyone standing on one of these wet esplanades is the noise.

It is colossal, steady, and inescapable—not the chaotic clamor of a crowd or a storm, but a vast, permanent industrial chord that vibrates up through the soles of your boots and hums in the fillings of your teeth. A laminar planetary wind rushes forever across the parallel rock blades, howling through the steel latticework of the bridges and setting the multi-kilometer power spans thrumming like the lowest strings of an impossible harp. Beneath that sits the dense, bone-deep bass of induction motors, reciprocating pumps, and vast turbine halls running at constant load. Overhead, the damp air makes the high-voltage lines hiss and crackle with continuous blue-white corona discharge. Down every cliffside, stone spillways pour steady sheets of water straight off the terraces into the empty air, their thunder echoing back and forth between the parallel rock walls until the chasms themselves become resonant chambers.

Because the ridges stand parallel like the plates of a gigantic instrument, sound behaves strangely here. Walk fifty paces down a cobblestone avenue and the roar of a substation two miles away may suddenly double in volume, focused by an invisible acoustic lens in the cold air; step past a streetlamp and the pitch drops an octave into a pressure node where the transformer hum feels thick as water against your eardrums.

Everything is cold, hovering just a few degrees above freezing, and everything is wet.

It does not rain in the ordinary sense—the sky overhead is a clear, steady, high-altitude blue, bright with an even, crisp daylight that casts clean shadows and glares off wet galvanized roofs. Instead, the moisture comes out of the air itself. Cold vapor condenses continuously on every sharp stone facet, every riveted girder, every pane of wired factory glass. Water beads, merges, and runs in constant glittering films down the masonry. Gutters run permanently brim-full. Cisterns overflow in smooth glass ribbons. Beneath the miles-long power lines, the electrostatic field is so strong that falling curtains of runoff from the aqueducts refuse to drop straight; they bend slowly sideways in midair, curving back toward the cliffs in translucent, shimmering sails before breaking into mist and vanishing into the bright void below. On the windward corners of the highest steel towers, thin needles of rime-ice project horizontally into the draft, neither growing nor melting.

Out of this freezing, electrified wetness grows a startling lushness.

There are no animals here—not a bird riding the updrafts, not a beetle under a damp paving stone, not a spiderweb in the switchgear, not a fish in the concrete retention basins. The stillness of animal absence is total. Yet the flora thrives with a thick, sodden, uncanny abundance. Wherever cold water weeps over rock or drips from copper busbars, dense carpets of deep teal, bruised indigo, and emerald bryophytes swell over the edges of the gutters and coat the underside of the viaducts. Thick curtains of rubbery, frost-pale ferns and hollow-stemmed hanging reeds drop twenty meters down from the cantilevered girders, trembling constantly in the wind and the acoustic roar.

Without insects or birds to carry pollen or spores, the plants have tuned themselves to the machines. Their fruiting bodies are taut, waxy bladders and stiff resonant stalks that vibrate at the exact frequencies of the nearby turbines and transformers, shaking out continuous pale clouds of spores into the wet draft. They drink water laced with trace copper, iron, and ozone from the industrial runoff; their foliage carries a faint metallic sheen, and bruised stems smell sharply of cold pine, wet pennies, and crushed flint. Great velvety bolsters of pitcher-moss swallow the abandoned rail spurs and pillow the bases of the floodlight towers, creating lush hanging gardens suspended directly over three-mile abysses.

Inside the towns, the urban logic is immaculate and profoundly strange.

The streets are laid out with unmistakable civic organization: numbered sectors stamped into enamel plaques, pedestrian overpasses with painted handrails, glass-roofed transit sheds, iron streetlamps burning with steady white arc-light right through the bright daytime. Yet no one walks them, and nothing moves along the rails. The doors of the brick tenement blocks stand slightly ajar onto cold, damp, spotless rooms that contain no beds, no tables, no signs of domestic life—only rows of heavy cast-iron radiators hissing softly at constant pressure and tiled floor drains carrying away the condensation.

Walk down a broad avenue lined with lush mossy planters and you will find it terminates cleanly at a sheer cliff drop, complete with painted crosswalk stripes, a steel guardrail dripping with ferns, and an electric signal box frozen permanently on amber. Above a freight yard jutting out over the chasm, a colossal gantry crane holds a twenty-ton block of dark, wet, freshly cut stone suspended forty feet above the tracks. Its hoist motor is running; you can hear the fierce, high whine of its coils and feel the heat radiating off its housing, holding the slab at exact stall-torque, neither lifting it an inch nor letting it fall.

Follow the pipes and conveyors through the cavernous, hollow factory halls—rooms so vast and empty that their far walls are hazy with indoor mist—and the purpose of the continent-spanning industry reveals itself as a closed circle.

Massive aero-turbines mounted in the cliff notches harness the permanent wind to drive banks of generators. That power feeds colossal electrolytic vats where condensed atmospheric water and dissolved mineral runoff from the mossy cliffs are refined under fierce voltage into dense, luminous brine and dark, vitreous filaments. Automated overhead trolleys—silent except for the hum of their magnetic tracks—do not move; instead, pneumatic tubes and high-pressure conduits roar continuously, forcing the refined mineral slurry through glowing ceramic kilns and out to immense, cliff-edge condensers. There, the product is atomized back into a cold, electrically charged vapor and vented directly into the wind, where it blows across the five-mile chasm, condenses onto the next razor-blade ridge, nourishes the metallic ferns, runs down the weeping black rock into the next city's cisterns, and enters the intake pipes all over again.

Every brass pressure gauge along the catwalks is pegged to a fixed mark. None of the needles tremble.

This is the strangest rule of the place, the one that only settles into your bones after hours of walking its wet, roaring walkways: nothing here is progressing. The light never shifts toward evening or rises from dawn; the shadows of the steel pylons stay pinned to the exact same cobblestones. Far below, the vast scalloped deck of clouds holds the identical crests and troughs it held an hour ago, a year ago. The moss neither dies back into winter nor overgrows the running shafts of the turbines; its lowest layers simply petrify slowly into the dark stone at the exact rate new green fronds push out into the mist. No bolt rusts through. No girder fatigues and snaps. The vast industrial cities roar at peak output, burning megawatts into wet iron and bright air, solely to keep the cold, sharp, screaming balance from ever tipping by a fraction of a degree.

Stand at the railing of an upper viaduct and look outward. Across four miles of empty, bright, freezing air, the next knife-edge of young black rock rises sheer from the cloud-floor, draped in lush emerald moss and crowned with another humming skyline of iron towers, glowing daylight lamps, and foaming spillways. Beyond it rises another, and another, repeating pale into the curvature of the world—a planetary-scale engine built out of cliff, mist, fern, and roaring wire, humming forever at a single unbroken note.
