The whole precinct sits inside a sunken, rectangular bowl no more than half a mile across, held in by high earthen embankments that long ago ceased to be earth. Seen from the iron inspection-walk along the northern rim, the perimeter reads like a clipped, continuous rampart of ancient hornbeam, woven willow-root, and dense grey-green cushion moss—a living retaining wall forty feet high, sheared flat along its crest by generations of cool wind. Within those walls, the town is laid out with the severe, practical clarity of an old municipal works: three straight avenues, a sunken central yard, elevated gantry-walks, and half a dozen widely spaced industrial houses. Yet nowhere is there bare stone, asphalt, or dead mud. Everything sits in, and grows out of, an unbroken floor of living plant.

Step down the wet iron stairs onto the main avenue and the ground yields slightly beneath your boots, firm as packed cork. It is not a lawn in any ordinary sense, nor a tangled thicket, but a deep, anciently felted substrate—yards thick—of dwarf sedge, braided rhizomes, tough peat-moss, and low woody groundcover compressed by centuries of foot traffic and iron wheels into a springy, load-bearing mat. The avenues are marked not by curbs of granite, but by parallel channels of narrow-gauge steel rail sunk flush into the green-brown turf, and by double rows of heavy, flat-topped timber-lichen cushions grown into neat paving blocks that drain instantly and never turn to mire.

Between the structures, there is an almost startling amount of open room. You can stand in the middle of the western yard and look cleanly past three low, widely separated transformer-sheds all the way to the southern embankment. The air is cool—the crisp, steady chill of late autumn that makes iron railings sting the palm—and filled with even, ordinary daylight from a high, pale sky streaked with moving cloud. In that clear light, the emptiness between the buildings feels deliberate, balanced, like the quiet floor of a cathedral where the columns have been set far apart so the eye can travel.

What passes for architecture here is neither wholly built nor wholly grown, and it is old enough that the distinction has weathered away. The millhouses and substations were framed long ago in riveted cast iron, weathered red brick, and heavy slate, then packed and clad in living flora that has since become structural. Their walls are thick, double-skinned lattices stuffed with damp bryophytes and threaded through with grafted, tightly espaliered alder whose pale grey bark has swelled into smooth, collar-like folds around every bolt-head and flanged pipe. Where brick corners remain visible, centuries of cool mist and crustose lichen have softened their edges into gentle curves. The pitched roofs are heavy with low stonecrop and clipped heather, broken only by clean rows of north-facing clerestory glass and copper ventilation louvers oxidized to a dull, stable pewter.

Even the overhead infrastructure has been absorbed into this patient equilibrium. Heavy braided power lines and bare copper busbars run across the open spaces between buildings, strung not on dead timber poles but between tall, pollarded iron-strapped trees and rusted lattice pylons wrapped halfway up in tight, woody ivy. White porcelain insulators sit half-swallowed in smooth grey bark, clean and dry beneath little flared lead rain-hoods. Everything has the dark, oiled patina of a place that has been running in the same posture for a very long time—nothing gleaming and new, nothing collapsing into ruin.

The first thing you notice, once you stop moving, is how quiet it is. For an electrified, mechanized district, it makes almost no noise at all. There is no roar of furnaces, no clatter of crowds, no hammering. Instead, the silence is fine-grained and busy, built out of small, widely spaced sounds: the faint, sixty-cycle murmur of an oil-cooled transformer behind a mossy brick wall; the dry whisper of a tallow-greased haulage cable gliding over an overhead sheave; the soft, irregular ticking of condensation falling from a cold copper gutter into a stone-lipped catch-basin below. Now and then, from somewhere across the yard, comes the dull, heavy *clunk* of an oil-damped brass relay throwing itself, followed by ten seconds of a low electric hum that fades back into the rustle of wind across the clipped turf.

Yet the place is never still. If you watch any single corner for five minutes, you begin to see how many processes are turning over at once, layered across the basin at different speeds.

Water governs the earliest shift. The precinct is not wet in the way of a marsh—there are no streams, and rain here tends toward brief, fine drizzles rather than downpours—but the low elevation traps cool air overnight, and by dawn every cold metallic surface in the bowl is beaded with heavy condensation. Along the eastern terraces stand four wide, skeletal condenser-towers: tall frames of weathered iron strung with fine vertical meshes of waxed linen and copper wire, angled into the pale morning drift. As the cool mist gathers and clears, water beads along the meshes, sheets silently down into moss-lined troughs, and feeds a recessed network of shallow, dark cisterns sunk flush with the plant-floor. Their surfaces sit black and motionless like polished obsidian mirrors, holding pockets of chilled water in the otherwise merely damp ground. Below them, hidden root-channels wick the moisture outward, drawing it laterally through the fibrous substrate until the entire floor of the town holds an even, cool dampness without ever pooling underfoot.

At the same time, the machinery answers the moisture directly. Look closely at the junction boxes bolted to the flanks of the substations and you will see no human hands at the levers. Many of the outdoor switches are tripped by thick, seasoned blocks of living stem-wood clamped into brass yokes: overnight, as the cool mist saturates the outer bark, the wood swells by two or three millimeters, slowly leaning against a weighted copper arm until, sometime after sunrise, the arm tips past center and closes a circuit with a muffled snap. High on the drying-house roofs, rows of hinged glass-and-slate louvers tilt open in staggered sequence as the morning air warms by a few degrees, releasing pale, brief plumes of warm vapor from the interiors into the cool sky.

Down at street level, the narrow-gauge rails carry the town's quiet circulation. Every few minutes, a low, unmanned iron trolley glides out of an arched hedge-tunnel on the northern flank, moving at an unhurried walking pace along the mossy tracks. Its electric motor makes only a faint, high singing sound beneath the chassis as its brass shoe slides along a sunken third rail insulated by dry, waxed peat-blocks. Each trolley carries slatted crates of dark, compressed pith-cakes or coils of pale fibrous cordage bound for the pressing-sheds at the southern end. Mounted beneath the front bumper of every cart is a pair of small, spinning wire-brushes: overnight, the aggressive sedge and creeping thyme along the avenue edges push pale new shoots across the steel rails, and each passing trolley quietly shears them back to a neat, wet green line, scattering fine clippings that the damp substrate reabsorbs before evening.

Overhead, the aerial cableways move even more slowly. Slatted wooden baskets hang from iron grips on taut steel wires, creeping across the wide empty sky between the condenser-yard and the upper lofts of the central millhouse. Because the structures are set so far apart, you can track a single basket for minutes against the pale clouds, watching it swing gently once or twice as a cool breeze crosses the basin.

The buildings themselves participate in the work. Through the clean glass of the long millhouse windows, you can make out slow-turning wooden drums, rows of brass bobbins, and heavy iron screw-presses descending fraction by fraction onto thick mats of harvested root-fiber, squeezing out a clear, slightly viscous sap that runs down open porcelain channels into cool glass carboys. The heat generated by the electric motors and induction coils inside is never vented wastefully into the air; copper heat-exchangers run straight into the thick, mossy double walls, warming the damp bryophytes from within. In response, the walls breathe—drawing cool moisture up from the buried cisterns and transpiring it gently from their outer surfaces, so that on a brisk midday you can see a faint, ghostly shimmer rising off the green flank of the main powerhouse, smelling cleanly of wet bark, ozone, and crushed fern.

By midday, when the pale sun reaches its highest arc and lays crisp, slanting shadows across the avenues, the color of the whole precinct shifts. Across the wide open expanses between the buildings, millions of small, tough leaves on the groundcover and terraced walls subtly turn their pale undersides toward the light, muting the basin from deep damp olive to a cool, silvery sage. Sap pressure rises in the tall pollarded cable-trees; along their iron-collared trunks, small copper taps weep slow, amber beads into glass drip-cups wired into the low-voltage signaling lines. Everything is growing, being measured, being trimmed, conducting a trickle of current or a slow pulse of water, and settling back into balance.

Animal life is scarce enough that any flicker of it catches the eye immediately. There are no flocks circling the chimneys, no stray dogs trotting the avenues, no hum of summer flies. Once in an hour, a solitary grey wagtail might alight on the oxidized rim of a catch-basin, bob its tail twice against the dark water, snatch a tiny cold-numbed midge from the damp moss, and flick away over the hedge-rampart. Down near the warm stone base of a transformer-shed, a slow, pale-shelled beetle picks its way across a copper grounding-strap; along the shaded underside of a porcelain insulator, a single silver snail-track dries in the midday breeze. Of the people who must once have laid out these grids—or who still, perhaps, arrive on some long, quiet schedule to top off the oil baths and tighten a loose shackle—there is almost no sign, save for the disciplined geometry of the clipped walls, a clean rag draped neatly over a brass valve-wheel, and the unmistakable sense that none of these gears have ever been allowed to run dry.

Late in the afternoon, as the sun drops toward the western rim of the embankment, shadow creeps quickly across the sunken floor of the basin. The temperature dips a few degrees in minutes. One by one, along the elevated walkways and at the rail junctions, caged filament bulbs and pale blue-green mercury lamps flicker awake—not all at once, but singly, as the cooling air contracts the wooden switch-blocks and drops the weighted relays back into their evening positions. The overhead louvers groan softly shut along the millhouse ridges. Mist begins to pool again around the low iron rails and the dark cistern lips, curling lazily around the ankles of the mossy pylons. Beneath the gathering chill, the trolleys keep gliding, the cables keep creeping across the pale dusk sky, and the thick, living floor of the precinct drinks in the first cool beads of the night.
