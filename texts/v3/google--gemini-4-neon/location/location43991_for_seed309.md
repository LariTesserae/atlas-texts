You hear it long before your eyes can make sense of the dark: a broad, grinding chord built out of iron flywheels, slipping leather belts, hissing pressure reliefs, and underneath—and somehow louder than all the steel—a wet, chitinous roar that has no single source.

The place sits deep underground, sealed beneath a weight of stone you never actually see. Every surface of the hollow—floor, curving walls, and the ribbed arch eighty yards overhead—is plant. Not plants growing *on* rock, not vines draping a cavern, but centuries of fused, pale-ochre root-wood, spongy peat-hide, and felted moss-bark knitted so tightly together that it serves as geology. The floor gives slightly beneath a boot like thick wet saddle-leather. Where old drainage channels have been cut into the ground, their sides are fibrous and calloused, weeping a thin, sweet amber sap that dries into tacky ridges. No raw mineral shows anywhere.

The whole cavern is no larger than a compact river-ward or a grand railway hall—perhaps three hundred paces end to end, curving gently shut at its borders so that even in the gloom you can always sense the far walls holding the air in. Inside that bounded volume, the built city is surprisingly sparse. Only six or seven tall, narrow industrial blocks rise from the undulating root-floor, set well apart from one another with wide, dim plazas of springy vegetable ground and broad gulfs of open air between them. They are old, layered things: brick-and-riveted-iron turbine halls at the base, topped by stacked corrugated workshops, switch-houses, and cramped, shuttered tenements cantilevered out on timber braces. Stenciled district numbers peel on the ironwork. High above the open spaces, sagging catwalks, burlap-wrapped steam mains, and taut braids of insulated power cable span the gaps from tower to tower, holding the scattered settlement together like a web strung across a cellar.

Nothing looks newly made, and nothing looks ruined. The settlement and the living vault have been leaning on each other so long that neither could stand alone. Massive pale root-buttresses have grown right through the lower masonry of the pump-houses, clamping steel stanchions in smooth woody knuckles. Iron bolts have weathered to a stable, greasy plum rust that matches the surrounding bark. Inside the open-sided lower mills, heavy reciprocating presses stamp and thud on beds of compressed fiber-wood, their copper-wound dynamos throwing off a steady, sour-ozone warmth and the occasional spitting blue flash where worn carbon brushes skip across spinning commutators.

Light is scarce. There is no sky-shaft, only plugged ventilation chimneys choked with ancient roots. A few dozen low-voltage filament bulbs, caged in sweating wire-glass along the gantries and boiler fronts, throw pools of dim amber that barely reach the ground. Between those pools, the cavern swims in bruised shadow, broken only by the flicker of electrical arcs and faint, bruised-green smudges of bioluminescence wherever something heavy brushes the damp moss-skin of the floor.

The air stays stubbornly warm—the close, shirt-sleeve warmth of an engine room after a long shift—and intermittently damp. There is no stream here, no standing lake, no steady rain. Instead, the waste heat from the engines and the breathing mass of the hollow rises into the slightly cooler ribs of the ceiling-canopy, where huge, pendulous, melon-skinned bladders grow out of the overhead roots. They swell slowly with collected condensate until their stems can no longer bear the weight. Every ten or fifteen minutes, somewhere across the dim expanse, one of those sacs tips and tears open, dumping forty gallons of warm, mineral-tasting water straight down onto a corrugated roof or a wide patch of empty root-plaza. It hits with a sudden violent drumroll, sends a brief plume of white steam curling off the hot iron cylinder-heads, and pools in shallow, oil-filmed puddles that the thirsty plant-floor drinks away within minutes.

And then you look closer at the shadows, and realize the cavern is not merely vegetated and mechanized—it is crawling, packed, saturated with animal life to the point that stillness itself barely exists.

What looked from twenty paces like glistening damp bark on the distant walls is actually a continuous living pelt of millions of flat, coin-sized dermal grazers—pale, translucent, gill-fringed things piled three deep, rasping the sap-sweat off the root-walls with a collective sound like dry paper rubbed endlessly between palms. Whenever a flywheel shifts pitch or a heavy stamping press hits bottom stroke, the vibration travels through the root-substrate and whole acres of the wall-pelt ripple in a single pale shiver.

Along the overhead cables and moving leather drive-belts run the spindled climbers: lean, six-ribbed, hairless predators the length of a forearm, with splayed cartilaginous pads that let them sprint along spinning shafts without losing purchase. They travel in chattering, jagged packs, leaping across gaps between insulated conduits, snapping up smaller pale hoppers shaken loose from the machinery housings. Their calls are sharp, dry clicks—like copper relays firing too fast—cutting straight through the bass drone of the transformers.

The wide, sparse air between the factory towers belongs to the bellows-wings. They roost in dense, heavy curtains along the ceiling ribs, hanging upside down in swaying clusters that smell strongly of musk and warm yeast. Blind, leathery, and broad as a coat, they navigate the dim gaps by inflating ribbed, accordion-like chests and snapping them shut, throwing out deep, wet *thup-thup-thup* booms that rattle your ribcage as they dive. A sudden water-dump from a ceiling bladder will flush fifty of them at once, sending them banking wildly through the amber lamp-pools, snatching climbers right off the catwalk railings while smaller, moth-winged scavengers swirl in their wake like ash.

Lower still, in the warm depressions around the factory footings where grease-sludge mixes with brief condensate puddles, heavy, blunt-snouted burrowers the size of hogs root noisily through the soft floor-fibers. Their plated backs are furred with the same pale lichen as the walls; they crack fallen fruiting-knuckles, dead fliers, and corroded brass scraps alike with wet, stone-on-stone jaws, grunting in low rhythmic bursts that answer the thump of the water-pumps.

Stranger than the free-roaming swarms are the things that have made peace with the electricity itself. Open any warm junction box or peer into the vents of a humming transformer and you find colonial clusters of soft, violet-veined polyps cemented directly to the copper windings, their outer shells stiffened with a dull verdigris crust leached from the damp metal. They pulse visibly at sixty cycles a second, feeding on the heat and the tiny winged gnats drawn to the ozone, thriving so cleanly inside the live circuitry that the mechanics no longer bother scraping them out unless they bridge a terminal.

Everything in the hollow runs on overlapping, locked-in clocks. Every quarter-hour, the main accumulator stack at the center of the settlement vents excess steam with a brazen, ear-splitting shriek that briefly drowns even the roaring walls. The shift-lamps along the tenements dip to a dull ember-red as the heavy presses cycle up; the sudden surge of heat triggers two or three overhead water-bladders to tip in ragged succession; the localized downpour flushes fresh clouds of grazers off the iron roofs, sending the bellows-wings plunging in loud, snapping arcs through the mist while the ground-foragers surge out from under the boiler piers to fight over whatever falls.

For three minutes the dim, wide spaces between the towers are a churning storm of wet wings, clicking feet, steam, and flashing blue commutator-sparks. Then the relief valve clangs shut. The lamps brighten back to their tired amber. The floor drinks the puddles dry. The engines settle back into their heavy, oiled groove, the wall-pelts close over the bare damp streaks left by the water, and the whole buried ward goes on humming, chewing, pressing, and devouring itself in the dark—a small, complete, deafening world sealed tightly under miles of silent earth.
