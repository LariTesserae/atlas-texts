The first thing you notice is that the dark has weight, and the dark is shouting.

You stand on a high shelf of ground where the air is thin enough to make your chest work for every breath, and cold enough to turn the inside of your nostrils crisp within seconds. Above and around you is an almost complete absence of light—not the tidy black of a closed room, but the bruised, depthless pitch of a high mountain basin sealed beneath an impenetrable cloud deck. If you stare upward long enough, you might convince yourself that the jagged notches where surrounding peaks cut the sky are a shade blacker than the clouds behind them, though it could just as easily be your eyes inventing borders to keep you from losing your balance.

Yet the darkness is nowhere near empty. It roars.

Sound hits you from inches away and from miles across the valley all at once: a wet, percussive storm of throat-clicks, hollow booming calls that drag on for ten or fifteen seconds at a stretch, the relentless hiss of water sheeting off stone, and beneath it all a dense, abrasive scrubbing noise, as if ten thousand stiff brushes were being dragged slowly across wet slate. Everything echoes with a brutal, brittle clarity. Nothing here has been softened by age or moss.

When you crouch to steady yourself and lay a palm on the ground, your hand meets two impossibilities at once.

First, the surface is soaking wet, even though the air is well below freezing. A thin, glassy film of liquid water slides steadily across the stone, chilling your fingers to the bone without ever stiffening into ice. It feels subtly wrong beneath your skin—almost frictionless, faintly vibrating at a pitch too fine to hear, as if the stone itself is agitating the water molecules just enough to forbid them from locking into crystals. Only where droplets splash off onto your sleeve do they freeze instantly into gritty pearls of rime.

Second, the ground is not natural rock, or not entirely. Under your left hand lies a broad, seamless flagstone with an edge sharp enough to nick leather, beveled at a precise geometric angle. Follow that bevel a foot to the right and it softens, blurs, and loses its discipline, dissolving directly into raw, fractured alpine basalt.

This is a city in the act of being born—and it is taking its time.

Across the vast, mile-wide amphitheater of the high mountain saddle, an urban lattice is slowly precipitating out of the bedrock. There are no cranes, no rivets, no mortar lines, no lamps. Whatever engineering governs this basin works silently and darkly from beneath, coaxing raw mountain stone and condensed vapor into layered tiers of matte-black architecture. Walk fifty paces along a newly formed street and you can feel the gradient of its completion under your boots: crisp, finished drainage channels and razor-edged stairwells giving way to half-extruded balustrades that end in bulbous, unformed knobs of dark mineral paste, still soft as cold tallow where the latest millimeter is curing.

Out over the steep drop of the basin, broad viaducts and terraced promenades jut thirty, fifty, a hundred yards into the black air without a single supporting pillar beneath them. They do not groan or sway. They hold their weight with an eerie, tensionless indifference, as though gravity itself has been quietly negotiated into compliance along their undersides. Even the unfinished stubs of towers—clustered thickly along the stepped slopes, crowding close together in dense, narrow canyons—stand with sharp, unweathered flanks that have never known a century of wind.

Nothing green grows on them. There are no roots prying at the seams, no lichen tufts, no alpine grass. The only non-animal life is a faint, greasy grey smear that weeps out of the curing masonry wherever the agitated water runs slowest: a slick chemotrophic film fed by the catalytic salts and heavy mineral compounds leached from the growing stone.

And that film is enough to anchor a staggering, suffocating abundance of flesh.

Move your hand six inches from the flagstone bevel and you touch something hard, wet, and faintly yielding. It shifts—barely a fraction of an inch—and gives a dry, irritated *clack*. What you took for cobbled masonry along the curb is not stone at all. Every vertical wall, every retaining buttress, every newly risen pillar in the city is shingled from base to lintel in living armor.

They are broad, flattened grazers, plated in overlapping bands of pale, gritty shell, packed edge-to-edge so tightly that you can scarcely fit a fingertip between them. Blind and patient, they creep across the freezing wet architecture at a rate of inches per hour, scraping the mineral slime from the walls with serrated rasps. One of them makes scarcely a whisper; millions of them, coating every square yard of a layered mountain city, produce that ceaseless, roaring grit-sound that underlies the valley.

Above your head, the space beneath a cantilevered walkway is packed even tighter. Warmth—real, musky, ammonia-sharp animal warmth—drifts down from the darkness overhead, thick with the smell of wet hides and oily excrement. You hear the slow, heavy shuffle of clawed limbs adjusting their grip, the wet *thwack* of folded leathery wings shifting against slick stone, and then a sudden, deafening volley of sharp clicks fired straight down into the street as dozens of roosting bodies take your acoustic measure.

Hanging in dense, overlapping curtains from every archway, conduit, and underside of every viaduct are immense flocks of cold-adapted aerialists. They are heavy-bodied, thick-blubbered things, draped in dark, water-shedding pelts and long, pleated sails of membrane they wrap around themselves like damp coats. In the freezing dark, flight is too expensive to waste on speed. When they move from one tier of the city to another, they simply unhook, drop into the sluggish updrafts rising from the lower terraces, and glide in wide, silent-winged arcs—until they bellow. Their calls are long, low, foghorn-like groans meant to claim a handspan of dry-ish overhang before they land, and because the valley holds hundreds of thousands of them, the bellows overlap into a continuous, rolling surf of sound that rebounds off the hard, raw walls of the basin.

Once every few minutes, a fleeting, dead-cold flicker of blue-grey light blinks somewhere out across the dark city—not a signal, not a lamp, only a brief flare of mineral discharge where a deep seam of bedrock locks into its new geometric lattice. For half a heartbeat, the flash catches the sheer density of the place: a breathtaking, dizzying vista of stacked terraces plunging downward for half a mile, narrow canyon-streets choked with hanging conduits and crowded arches, every horizontal ledge lumpy with clustered backs, every vertical face crusted with pale shells, and the whole dripping edifice shimmering faintly with sheeting water before the pitch dark slams shut again.

Follow the water downward—careful on the slick, razor-edged stairs, stepping over the slow bodies blocking every second riser—and you find the drainage streets.

Because the city’s surfaces refuse to let water freeze, the perpetual condensation from the cold mountain fog gathers quickly into brisk, ankle-deep black channels that run down the center of every sloped lane. Away from the stone’s subtle field, the air bites mercilessly; icicles of grey slush form on the outer fringes of the hanging flocks’ tails, snapping off occasionally to shatter like glass on the pavement below. Inside the channels, though, the water stays bitterly, stubbornly fluid, thick with shed shells, mineral silt, and the steady rain of droppings from the viaducts overhead.

The gutters are alive. Long, sinuous scavengers—pale, nearly translucent beasts with flat heads and fans of twitching sensory barbels—wade sluggishly through the cold runoff, bumping against your boots without panic before sliding past. They are slow, conserving every calorie against the alpine chill, waiting patiently for a wall-grazer to lose its grip or an aging rooster to slip from its perch above. When one finds a meal, the squabble is not a frantic blur, but a heavy, grunting tug-of-war in the dark, full of wet snaps and low, coughing hisses that echo down the narrow street.

Further down, where the terraced streets converge on the wide floor of the mountain bowl, the city opens into a network of broad, sunken retention basins and unfinished plazas. Here the air is loud with a different rhythm: a slow, cavernous *glug-hiss, glug-hiss* pulsing across the dark water. The flooded cisterns are choked with vast, rubbery colonies of siphon-animals anchored to the newly poured bottoms, endlessly pumping the mineral-rich brine through their pale tubular bodies and expelling it in synchronized, lung-like sighs.

And along the broad, raw causeways that bridge these dark pools, the largest inhabitants move.

You hear them long before they reach you—not footsteps, exactly, but a deep, sub-audible rumble that vibrates in the soles of your feet and rattles the loose basalt scree along the unfinished road edges. Then comes the smell: heavy wet wool, iron, and frost. Out of the darkness loom immense, long-legged silhouettes, taller than the low archway behind you, draped in dense, matted mats of pale bristles encrusted with frozen fog along their outer tips. They are blind, broad-muzzled sweepers that pace the wide urban spans at a glacial walk, dragging wide, scoop-like lower jaws along the wet paving stones to harvest the fallen detritus and slow-moving grazers alike. They do not hurry, and they do not yield. Smaller creatures simply flatten themselves against the curbs or slip into the drainage grates as the great, steaming legs step past with deliberate, agonizingly slow precision.

Everything in this valley obeys the same cold, patient calculus: move slowly, stay close to the stone, and listen.

Far from the city’s edges, where the emerging tiers finally peter out into raw mountain talus, the field fades. Out there, the wet mist freezes solid onto the barren rock in thick, windless crusts of rime, and the silence is abrupt and lethal. Nothing lives twenty paces past the outermost half-formed curb. So the entire ecology presses inward, stacking itself deeper and denser into every new stairwell, flue, and gallery as soon as the stone solidifies enough to bear weight.

Now and then, deep beneath the basin, a sound rises that momentarily silences the nearest flocks: a sharp, immense, crystalline *crack*, like a frozen lake splitting under a mile of snow, followed by a long, trembling hum through the flagstones. Another millimeter of viaduct has locked into being. Another raw seam of basalt has surrendered to geometry.

The silence lasts barely two heartbeats. Then a hanging beast somewhere overhead lets out a long, wet groan; a wall-grazer shifts its shell with a gritty scrape; a drop of freezing water hits a brimming gutter; and the roaring, blind multitude settles back into its slow work, crowding eagerly into the brand-new dark.

If a grazer lingers too long on a curing wall, or a sick animal curls into an active seam to sleep, the city does not push it aside. Over months and years, the matte-black stone simply rises around it, sealing shell and bone seamlessly into the fresh masonry—leaving the emerging streets subtly bumpy with the faint, sharp-edged contours of things that waited just a little too patiently in the dark.
