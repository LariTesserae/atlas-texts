The light arrives before anything else—white, unshaded, and thin with altitude, pouring down onto a valley-sized expanse where not a single square inch of earth has ever existed.

Everything beneath the glare is freshly made. Step onto any walkway and the diamond-plate grating bites cleanly into the soles of your boots, its edges sharp enough to shave fingernails, the zinc spangle still bright and unweathered. Concrete parapets show the crisp wood-grain imprint of formwork that might have been stripped an hour ago; mortar squeeze-outs remain brittle and unbroken along precast seams; brass valves gleam with a faint, factory-applied film of pale grease. Yet there is no crew, no scaffolding, no pile of scrap. The entire basin—stretching several kilometers from rim to rim across stepped terraces, sunken courts, and elevated trusses—sits packed shoulder-to-shoulder with complete, electrified infrastructure: banks of finned transformers, louvered turbine halls, riveted cisterns, gantry cranes, catwalk lattices, porcelain-hung substations, and dense bundles of conduit bolted ten deep against every vertical face.

Beneath the lowest grated decks you can reach, there is still no soil or bedrock. Looking straight down between steel slats reveals another tier of pitched corrugated roofs, pipe racks, and narrow service alleys, and beneath those, pale concrete light-wells flooded with an inch of clear water, and beneath those, deeper grids of bolted trusses dropping away into a bright, chalky haze. Light seems to leak faintly upward from the sub-levels, as if the floor of the world were simply another sky turned face-up behind layers of steel.

The air is cool and thin, carrying the clean, metallic scent of wet aluminum, ozone, and cold masonry. No rain falls for hours at a time, yet the place stays stubbornly damp. Because the altitude keeps the steel and ceramic cold even under the punishing brightness, moisture pulls quietly out of the air and skins every shaded surface. It beads along the undersides of overhead pipes, gathers in the recessed dimples of bolt heads, and collects along the bevels of angled glass-block walls. Every few minutes, a drop grows heavy enough to let go.

That drop is often the loudest thing you will hear.

Despite the staggering density of moving iron and living flesh, the basin is held in an eerie, almost clinical hush. Massive spoked flywheels rotate in open housings along the central causeways, turning at a glacial pace—one revolution every twenty minutes—and their bearings make no sound at all. Hydraulic rams lower gleaming pistons into dark oil-sumps by millimeters an hour without a groan. Overhead, a heavy gantry carriage drifts along its I-beam rail, its steel wheels grinding solidly across the flange in complete silence. Friction here has partially slipped loose from acoustics. A pressure-relief valve on a boiler dome snaps open and vents a stiff white plume of vapor into the bright sky, entirely mute; eight seconds later, from somewhere inside a brick transformer shed forty yards behind you, comes a single soft metallic *tick*, as though the sound had been misrouted through the wiring and arrived late.

Only the smallest, wettest noises stay loyal to their causes: water plinking into a galvanized drip-tray, the dry click of claws on grating, the whisper of a slow breeze threading a louver bank.

Even at a glance across the open terraced bowl, the geometry refuses to settle. Stand on a high crossover bridge and sight along a straight row of cooling stacks: the perspective pulls tight too early, then bows outward, so that a warehouse half a kilometer away shows its north, west, and south walls simultaneously. Walk the perimeter of a square switchgear yard and you count five ninety-degree corners before returning to the gate. The rules of light fracture along similar fault lines. Although the sky is a uniform, blinding white dome with no visible sun, objects cast razor-sharp shadows—and they do not agree on where the light comes from. Galvanized iron throws its shadows strictly eastward; poured concrete drops pale grey shadows straight beneath its overhangs; glass and glazed porcelain cast bright, bleached patches brighter than the sunlit deck around them. Over the course of many hours, these shadows creep slowly across the walkways on separate schedules, sliding over one another like overlapping plates of tinted glass.

Power runs everywhere, patient and unconcerned with continuity. High-tension lines sag heavily between tiered racks of white porcelain insulators, humming not to the ears but to the fillings in your teeth and the fine hairs on your forearms. Follow a copper busbar along a wall and you will find places where a two-foot section is cleanly absent, the cut ends bright and mill-square, yet the charge crosses the empty air without arcing, warming the dampness between the severed ends into a faint, invisible shimmer. Conveyor belts inch forward along elevated trusses, carrying nothing for hours, dive straight into solid windowless concrete walls with no aperture to receive them, and emerge smoothly from the side of an elevated water tank two blocks over. Inside a glass-walled press hall, an enormous die descends over forty minutes onto an empty steel bed; when it lifts, a thin rectangular sheet of cold fresh water lies where stamped metal ought to be, holding its square edges for three impossible seconds before collapsing and draining through the bolt holes.

Nothing here is barren, though the plants keep to the margins. Wherever damp mineral dust settles in an expansion joint, or condensation feeds a shallow concrete catch-basin, sparse vegetation takes hold with austere precision. Stiff, wire-thin rushes sprout in neat lines along drainage troughs, their tips rimed with tiny beads of moisture. Grey-green crusts of lichen grow across the fresh steel, refusing the flat plates and tracing only the weld seams and rivet circles in strict geometric patterns, like botanical blueprints. In the wide shallow hoppers of idle ore-chutes, where cool rain-mists and condensate have pooled into pale silt, occasional silver-leaved rosettes and stunted, wind-clipped tufts of pale sedge cling tightly to the corners, never growing tall enough to soften the sharp lines of the metal around them.

The animals, by contrast, are everywhere—dense, unhurried, and completely at home in the bright, fractured machinery.

Every high railing, gantry boom, and ventilation lip is lined shoulder-to-shoulder with chalk-white, flat-faced birds the size of gulls. They never cry out. They sit packed tightly together against the cool air, their feathers slick with fine mist, and at long intervals a slow wave of blinking ripples from one end of a rooftop to the other, hundreds of translucent eyelids closing and opening in solemn sequence. When a flock lifts off, their wings stir the air without a flap or rustle, a silent white blizzard shifting three rooftops east to settle along another sharp cornice.

Down among the pipe-racks and stacked decks, slender, long-necked browsers pick their way along wet corrugated roofs and narrow catwalks. They have pale, dense wool clipped close to their ribs, pale eyes set vertically rather than horizontally, and wide, splayed feet padded with dark, ribbed tissue like vulcanized rubber that grips slick zinc at steep angles. They move in small, patient herds, stepping over live conduits and ducking under slowly turning drive-shafts without flinching, using hard narrow lips to scrape the geometric weld-lichens and sip from the bright rectangular drip-troughs. Their joints seem to accommodate the local kinks in space: watching one turn a corner around a boiler casing, its hindquarters briefly appear a half-pace higher than its shoulders before rejoining the rest of its body smoothly on the far side of the pipe.

Closer to the warm hum of the substations, life grows smaller and more densely packed. Thousands of slow, heavy-bodied insects with matte, porcelain-white wing-cases cling to the cooling fins of transformers, drinking the thin sweat of condensation that forms where warm metal meets high-altitude chill. When disturbed, they do not scatter; they merely shift sideways in unison by an inch, producing a faint dry sound like dry rice sliding inside a tin. Threading through the shadows beneath the decks are sleek, velvet-furred creatures with too many ribs and long, flexible tails—silent scavengers that live inside the wiring conduits and hollow box-girders. They slip headfirst into a three-inch junction pipe on a lower walkway and, without hurrying, poke their whiskered snouts out of a sealed brass pressure-gauge on a tower sixty meters away, sniffing the cool air before pulling their whole bodies cleanly through the solid glass face, which leaves neither crack nor ripple behind them.

Water, scarce as it is, holds a strange authority over the broken rules. In the wide concrete sumps and rectangular catch-basins where condensate collects a few inches deep, the bright sky reflects cleanly—except the reflections do not quite match the buildings overhead. Peer into a still, cold pool beside a substation and you may see a footbridge arching between two silos that does not yet exist in the air above you, or notice that a bank of louvered vents is closed in the water while standing open in the flesh. The animals seem to read these pools carefully. The rubber-footed browsers will pause at the brink of a shallow basin and step around the reflected shadow of a stanchion that isn't there; the white birds roost along the concrete lip facing inward, watching the slow, unhurried drift of machinery reflected from some adjacent version of the terrace.

Wait long enough—hours, or days—and you realize the basin is not static; it is merely operating on a pulse far slower than human impatience. Over the span of an afternoon, the thin mist thickens along the far rim where the dense grid of roofs tilts impossibly upward into the white glare, repeating the same cluster of silos at slightly rotated angles until distance dissolves into bright vertical static. Slowly, the air cools a few degrees further. Fresh beads of condensation swell across millions of square meters of zinc and porcelain, darkening the concrete joints from chalk-white to slate. Far down in the sub-decks, some immense, unheard relay closes, and across an entire quadrant of the valley, thousands of tiny indicator lamps behind thick green glass lenses brighten from dull olive to steady emerald.

Nothing roars to life. A row of giant steel louvers tilts three degrees wider over the course of an hour. Along an upper gantry, a newly minted bay of walkway—sharp-edged, unrusted, still faintly warm—extends quietly into empty space where nothing hung the day before, already slick with dew, already bearing a neat line of silent white birds watching the bright, cold, impossible horizon.
