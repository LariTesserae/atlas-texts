High in the karst shoulder of the mountain, where the cloud-bank presses against the limestone without ever lifting, the ground opens into a narrow throat no wider than a fallen trunk. Below that throat the rock bells outward into a single sunken chamber—roughly the height and breadth of a small stone chapel, and entirely closed off from the wind.

Almost no light gets down here. The opening far overhead is woven shut by the buttress-roots of cloud-forest trees standing in the permanent mist outside, and from those roots hang curtains of sodden peat-moss twenty feet long. What little greyness filters past the tangle is already thinned by altitude and cloud. On the floor of the hollow it registers less as illumination than as a faint difference between matte black and wet black. Only after long stillness do the shapes detach themselves: the fluted, inward-curving walls, the tiered shelves of calcite, the thick vertical cables of descending root, and the dull silver line where cold water meets the rims of shallow stone basins.

The rock is old beyond any rough edges. Tens of thousands of years of slow dissolution have smoothed every fracture into rounded ribs, scalloped ledges, and lipped pools of travertine. Nothing shifts or flakes away anymore; there is no scree at the foot of the walls, no fresh scar of a rockfall. The pit has finished wearing itself into shape. Water seeps continuously through the limestone pores—not in streams or cascades, only as a universal, patient sweating. Every surface carries a film of moisture. Beads gather at the tips of hanging fronds, swell until they are heavy, and let go at steady intervals into the soaked sponge below, where they disappear without a splash.

The air holds a steady mountain chill, cool enough to stiffen unaccustomed fingers, never cold enough for frost. More unusual than the chill is its flatness. There is no draft between the floor and the choked throat above, no warming at midday, no deepening cold at night. Because the cloud outside never breaks and the surrounding bedrock is hundreds of feet thick, the seasons do not reach this far down. The humidity hangs at permanent saturation. Even the smell never shifts: cold wet chalk, dark peat, bruised fern-stem, and the dense, clean musk of damp fur and amphibian skin.

In this near-darkness, the plant life is improbably thick. No bare stone shows anywhere. The walls are upholstered in two or three inches of deep-pile cushion moss and black-green liverwort that yield like wet felt under pressure. Out of the moss grow filmy ferns whose fronds are only a single cell thick, dark as oiled slate and faintly translucent when a stray glint catches them. Where the massive roots of the rim-trees plunge down through the ceiling to drink from the mineral pools below, they bring down sugars forged in the dim daylight far above; entire gardens of pale, leafless stalks and swollen waxy tubers tap directly into those living cables, flourishing without ever needing sun. Every ledge is jammed with low, dark-leaved shade shrubs and lichen mats layered over older mats, growing at the exact snail-pace at which the lowest layers dissolve back into peat.

And yet the plants are only the backing. Once the eye adapts to the dark, the hollow reveals itself to be packed solid with animals—not moving in swarms or frenzies, simply occupying every square inch of available surface.

There is nowhere to set a hand without touching something alive. Under every overhanging lip of travertine, fist-sized snails with heavy, moss-furred shells cling rim to rim in pale clusters. Inside the wet cups of the fern crowns, glossy black-and-ochre salamanders lie coiled two and three deep, their throats pulsing slowly. Flat, broad-winged moths the color of wet bark press flush against the vertical bryophytes in overlapping shingles; harvestmen with thread-thin legs hang motionless in tangled curtains beneath the root-arches. In the ankle-deep rimstone pools that cover most of the floor, small dark crabs and blind, pale amphipods sift the submerged peat, while high up in the dryish flutes just beneath the root-plug, hundreds of damp-furred bats hang pressed flank to flank like dark fruit.

Among all of them, occupying every broad calcite gallery and every forked cradle of root from floor to mid-wall, sit the hollow’s tool-users.

They are compact, heavy-shouldered creatures, roughly the size of curled children, clothed in thick, oily charcoal pelts that shed the constant seepage. Their faces are short and wide, dominated by enormous, pale, wet-gleaming eyes that gather every stray particle of grey light from the throat above; their hands are broad, hairless, and deeply calloused, with long, spatulate fingers. They sit shoulder-to-shoulder, back-to-back, knees drawn up against the mossy rock, filling the tiered ledges of the pit like a silent congregation in a dark gallery.

They have built nothing. There is not a stacked stone, a woven screen, a bridge, a hearth, or a scraped path anywhere in the hollow. There is no weather here to shut out, and no spare ground left to clear; the limestone itself already provides every shelf and basin they could use. Yet almost every pair of hands holds something made.

Their craft is ancient, intimate, and entirely handheld. One creature perched on a travertine lip holds a thick, calcified snail in its left palm and works the seam of the operculum with a small wedge of flaked black chert, applying steady leverage until the seal yields with a soft, damp *pop*. Beside it, another uses a fist-sized river pebble—tumbled smooth long before it was ever brought down into this pit, and since polished nearly greasy by generations of wet skin—to bruise a fibrous root-tuber against a flat knee of limestone until the starch softens into paste. Along a higher root-bridge, two sit grooming each other’s pelts with slender needles ground from bat-bone, pausing to lift a sluggish salamander from a bent knee with the flat of the tool and set it gently onto the moss beside them.

Nothing is discarded. A few of the older hands hold thin, dark awls and scrapers hammered cold from rare nodules of bog-iron sifted out of the floor-peat centuries ago, their edges kept keen by rubbing them quietly against the fine grit of the travertine. Nearly every adult wears a braided cord of tough hair-moss or split fern-stem looped tightly around a wrist or throat, strung with a single chipped quartz flake, a notched bone pick, or the empty, polished shell of a giant snail used to dip clear seepage from narrow wall-fissures. When one dies, the tools are lifted from its cold fingers by whoever sits nearest and slipped onto another cord. The stones and bones have been handled so long that they look less like artifacts than worn-down parts of the bodies that carry them.

What is most startling about so much crowded life is the quiet.

The thick upholstery of soaked moss and dense pelts swallows sound almost at the point of origin. There are no calls, no territorial shrieks, no rustling wind, no rushing water. The dwellers do not speak, or if they communicate it is through shifts of weight against a neighbor’s ribs and the softest clicks at the back of the tongue. What fills the air is an intricate, muted hush: the slow *thup* of water beads sinking into bryophyte cushions; the fine wet rasp of flint on shell; the papery shift of a hundred bats resettling in the upper flutes; the faint, synchronized sigh of dozens of slow lungs breathing cool mist in the dark. Every few minutes, a single dull *tock* of stone against root carries across the twenty-pace span of the chamber and vanishes into the wet green walls.

Between the different species there is an odd, settled tolerance—not gentleness so much as the complete absence of panic or surplus energy. A giant snail glides across the braided wrist-cord of a sleeping tool-user, rasping microscopic algae from the fiber without waking its host. Salamanders thread lazily over wet furred ankles; moths settle directly on the backs of motionless hands. Everything has its narrow ration. The bats drop guano into the pools; the amphipods and crabs break it down; the mosses and roots drink the mineralized water; the snails graze the moss; the ledge-dwellers pry loose only as many snails and dig only as many root-nodules as keep their slow metabolisms turning in the cool air.

Nothing swells, and nothing collapses. Because the hollow sits outside the reach of drought, storm, fire, and seasonal plenty, the population has long since pressed up against the exact ceiling of what the seepage and the overhead roots can feed, and stayed there. A frond decays at the same pace a new one uncurls. A pebble loses an invisible grain of stone each year to the root-paste it crushes. High above, the cloud-forest endures its winds and winters, but down inside the smooth wet throat of the limestone, the water beads at the same rhythm, the dark holds the same cool weight, and the crowded shelves breathe together in an equilibrium so complete it feels as though time itself has pooled here and stopped flowing.
